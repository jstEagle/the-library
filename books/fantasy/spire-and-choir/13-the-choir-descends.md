## 13. The Choir Descends

### Ilse

Cantor Ilse Voss had waited eleven years for the mist to open, and when it finally did—when the first scout's death tore a ragged seam in that gray curtain and the wind came through smelling of pine and woodsmoke instead of nothing at all—she did not weep, though her throat ached with the effort of not doing so.

She stood on the ridge above Ashford with two hundred voices behind her and watched the seam widen like a wound being deliberately kept from healing.

"Sing," she said.

They sang. The hymn was old—the oldest one, *Vael Who Holds*, in notation no living ear had been taught to read until she had taught them, note by corrupted note, from the water-stained hymnal she had carried out of a burning rectory as a girl of nine. The melody was wrong. She knew it was wrong; the intervals sat crooked in the ear, like a stair with one step missing. But wrongness had power too. Wrongness was a key filed down to fit a lock it was never made for, and it turned, and the mist flinched.

The valley below them lay white under late snow, deceptively gentle. Crooked rooftops. A thread of smoke from a chimney or two. Fields gone fallow and gray. Somewhere down there, a child who spoke the First Tongue without knowing what it cost to speak it.

"Vael who holds," the Choir sang, two hundred voices braided into one rope, "Vael who keeps, wake from the silence of your sleep."

Ilse had been nine years old the winter her family starved. She remembered it in colors: the gray of her mother's gums, the yellow of tallow they could no longer afford to burn, the red—no, there had been no red. Red required blood still warm enough to bleed. Her father had died quietly, which was the cruelest way, because quiet meant he had given up arguing with God about it. Her mother had lasted until spring and then simply stopped getting up, and Ilse, nine years old and already fluent in hunger, had walked to the rectory and asked the priest why.

The priest had said: *Because the god is silent.*

And then he had said: *Because someone made him silent.* And he had looked at her with eyes full of terrible knowledge before the mob came for him, and she had run with his hymnal pressed against her chest like a second heart, and she had spent every year since learning to read what was written in it.

Not vengeance. She wanted to be clear about that, even now, even here. Not vengeance. *Restoration.* There had been a time—her grandmother swore it, and grandmothers did not lie about bread—when the granaries never emptied, when the sick were healed by a touch and a word, when every soul in the land knew its place in the great ordered chord and no child ever, ever starved. That time had ended not by accident but by choice. By oath. By someone's treachery dressed up as mercy.

Mercy. Ilse spat the word silently into the snow. Mercy was what the godless called their abandonment. *We freed you,* they said. *We unchained you.* Yes—and a man thrown overboard is also freed.

The seam in the mist gaped wide enough now to drive a cart through. Beyond it, the valley's air moved differently—thicker, slower, like walking through water that had forgotten how to be anything else. Two hundred choristers would enter it. Some would die in it. She had already sung the requiem for them in her head, privately, at dawn, where no one could hear doubt in her voice.

"Cantor." Deacon Sarel, at her shoulder, young and hungry-eyed. "The scouts report resistance at the tree line. One armed defender. Possibly two."

One. Ilse almost laughed. One sword against two hundred voices.

"Then we will be gentle with them," she said, "until they stop being an obstacle."

She began to descend, and the Choir descended with her, and behind them the mist closed over the seam like skin over a wound that had never been meant to heal—and this time, it did not close all the way.

### Astrid

Astrid felt the mist break the way a man feels a rib crack—not at the site of the injury but everywhere, a wrongness radiating outward, a structural truth suddenly negotiable.

They were on the roof of the longhouse with a spyglass older than the valley itself, and through it Astrid watched the ridge line come alive. Not soldiers first. *Singers.* Ranks of them, dark-robed, moving in step, mouths open, and though the distance ate the words, Astrid's body remembered the rhythm anyway. Eleven years of engineered forgetting, and still the spine knew that cadence. Still the marrow hummed along.

"They're singing it wrong," Maren said beside her. She was propped against the chimney, bandaged, gray-faced, her sword across her knees because sitting up straight was beyond her. "The hymnal notation. It's degraded. Every copy of a copy drifts."

"Does it matter?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked softly against the shingles. Below them, the valley was waking—doors opening, faces turning toward the ridge, toward the sound. In the Valley of Hush, no one had names spoken aloud, but everyone had faces, and Astrid knew every one of them: the cooper whose hands shook; the twin sisters who kept bees; old Tam, who had come here forty years ago fleeing a debt and had grown old enough to forget what he owed and to whom.

"It matters," Maren said, "because wrong is louder than right. Right would have taken them another decade to gather enough power to crack the mist. Wrong works faster. Sloppy keys turn locks too, if you file them hard enough." She coughed, wetly. "How many?"

"Two hundred. Maybe more behind the ridge."

"And Wren?"

"Wren is inside. With the door barred and the standing stone between them and everything." Astrid lowered the spyglass. Their hands were steady. That was the horror of it—they had done this so many times, in so many other lives, that steadiness came without being asked for. "Maren. If I go down there and hold the tree line, how long can I buy?"

Maren looked at them for a long moment, and something passed across her face that was not quite pity and not quite anger but lived in the country between.

"You know how long. You've always known. Long enough to lose well."

"I'm not trying to win."

"No." Maren's hand found theirs, briefly, callused palm against callused palm. Eleven years ago they had sworn oaths side by side in a language that ate liars, and only one of them remembered it, and that was its own kind of wound. "You're trying to spend yourself somewhere it counts. Fine. But Astrid—" her grip tightened, "—the Choir doesn't want you dead. Dead mercenaries are cheap. They want you *broken*. Cantor Voss has been preaching your name for years. The Traitor of the Silence. The one who unmade paradise. When they take you, they'll want the valley to watch."

"Then the valley should look away."

"The valley won't look away. Neither will I." Maren released their hand and reached for her sword. "Give me the left flank. I'm slow, but I'm mean, and I owe a debt to someone I can't remember."

Astrid climbed down from the roof into the smoke-smell of the morning, and behind them, faint and growing, the wrong hymn came down off the ridge like weather.

### Wren

Wren heard the singing through the floorboards and thought it sounded like the mountain had sounded, the night the voice first spoke.

That was the thing nobody understood, Wren thought, pressing their ear to the cold planks. Everyone talked about the Choir like they were frightening, and they were, probably, the way a landslide was frightening—but the *singing* wasn't frightening. The singing was lonely. Two hundred people singing a song that didn't fit together right, and every one of them hoping that if they sang loud enough, the crooked parts would straighten themselves.

Wren knew about crooked songs. Wren had been humming one since the mountains.

*Little bird,* said the voice, soft as breath on a window. *They have come for you.*

"I know."

*You are afraid.*

Wren considered this honestly, the way the voice liked—honesty was the toll for its company. "I'm afraid for Astrid. And for the bee sisters. And for Tam, even though he smells like onions. I'm not afraid for me." Wren picked at a knot in the floorboard. "Is that strange?"

*It is rare,* the voice said, after a pause that felt like a held breath lasting a year. *It is the rarest thing I have heard in a generation of silence.*

Outside, the singing swelled, and beneath the floorboards Wren felt the house tremble—not from boots, not yet, but from the song itself, from the pressure of two hundred wrong notes pushing against a valley built to keep such things out. A cup rattled on the shelf. The fire leaned sideways in the hearth as if listening.

"If I sang the waking-words," Wren said quietly, "would you make them stop? All of them? Even the ones who aren't bad, just lost?"

Silence. Then, carefully, the way a large animal steps through a room full of glass:

*I would make everything stop, little bird. That is what I am. That is what I was. You must understand what you are asking before you ask it.*

"I do understand." And Wren found that they did, suddenly and completely, the way children sometimes understood things whole, without the long road adults needed. "That's why I won't. Astrid says a promise spoken perfect is a cage. So I'd sing it crooked. I'd sing it human. And you'd have to stay small enough to fit inside the song."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came again, it sounded—Wren would remember this later, would carry it like a stone in the pocket of their heart—it sounded *afraid*.

*No one has ever asked me to be small.*

"Well," said Wren, as the first boot struck the door below, "get used to it."

### Ilse

The valley fought back the only way it could: by being difficult to believe in.

Ilse's column entered the tree line in good order and immediately began to come apart. The paths doubled back on themselves. Trees that had been pines became birches between one glance and the next. Men reported seeing their own faces in the snow ahead of them, walking away. Three choristers knelt down mid-march and began weeping without knowing why, and had to be carried.

But the mist was torn now, and torn things leak, and through the leak poured two hundred voices' worth of certainty, and certainty is a solvent stronger than any magic.

By midmorning they reached the first homestead. It was empty—its owners fled toward the valley's heart—and Ilse stood in its doorway and felt something in her chest crack sideways, because the house was *poor*. Thin soup still on the table. A single blanket on the bed, patched three times. A child's carving on the sill, a lopsided bird.

This was what freedom had built. This was the paradise the godless had purchased with a god's silence: soup going cold, one blanket, a carved bird by a window facing nothing.

"Burn it," she said, and hated how steady her voice was, and burned it anyway, because hesitation was a luxury paid for by the dead, and she had buried enough of the dead to last several lifetimes.

Behind her, the fires began to bloom across the valley like terrible flowers, and the Choir sang louder, and somewhere ahead, down the white thread of the road, a single figure stood at the tree line with a drawn sword, waiting for them.

Ilse raised her hand, and the column halted, and for a moment the only sounds were fire and snowmelt dripping from the branches.

"Cantor?" Sarel whispered.

"That's them," Ilse said. "The Traitor of the Silence." She studied the distant figure—at this range just a shape, dark against the snow, patient as winter. Eleven years of sermons, of hymnals, of starving children's names recited like psalms, and it all came down to one person standing alone in a field, guarding a child and a grave and a lie.

"Archers," she said. "On my mark. We will not dishonor ourselves with cruelty. But we will not stop, either. Whatever stands in the road today—" she thought of her mother, who had stopped getting up; of her father, who had died quietly; of the priest with his terrible knowing eyes, "—whatever stands in the road today was standing there eleven years ago too, and chose silence."

She dropped her hand.

The arrows rose like a flock of black birds, and the figure in the field did not move, and the war that had ended a generation ago in a whisper began again, at last, in earnest.

* * *

### Astrid

The first arrow took Astrid high in the shoulder, and the pain was almost a relief—proof that this was real, that the valley's long anesthesia was over, that the bill had finally come due.

They pulled it out, snapped the shaft, and kept standing, because the Choir's front rank had faltered at the sight of a lone mercenary refusing to fall, and faltering ranks were a currency Astrid had spent a lifetime learning to spend.

"MAREN!" they roared across the field. "LEFT FLANK!"

And from the trees, hoarse and furious and gloriously alive, Maren's answer: "I REMEMBER WHY I OWE YOU NOW!"

Astrid smiled with blood in their mouth, set their feet in the snow, and watched two hundred singers come down the hill like a tide with a hymn in it—and thought, with the strange clarity of the doomed:

*Hold the line until spring. Hold it until the equinox. Hold it until the child is ready.*

*Hold it, and let everything else burn.*

The tide broke over them, and Astrid Blackwood, who had once silenced a god, began to remind the world why.
