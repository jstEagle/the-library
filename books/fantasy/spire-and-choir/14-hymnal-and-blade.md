## 14. Hymnal and Blade

The valley burned the way a hymn burns—verse by verse, each line of flame given its own measure of time to be sung.

Astrid ran through it with a sword in one hand and a name in their mouth.

*Corvin.* That was the first one. Corvin Ash-hand, who used to carve little wooden birds for the children in the company's baggage train and pretend he hadn't, leaving them on bedrolls where no one would see him do it. He stood at the edge of the burning barley field now in white-and-grey vestments, a censer swinging from his fist, and when he saw Astrid coming he did not reach for a weapon. He opened his arms.

"Brother," he said—or sister, or sibling; the First Tongue had a word for it that meant *sworn-of-my-blood*, and that was what came out of his mouth, in the old language, perfect and terrible. "You've come home."

Astrid killed him anyway.

It took three strokes. The first he caught on his forearm because he would not stop reaching out. The second went through the meat of his shoulder and he was still singing—the Choir sang while they fought, that was their discipline, low sustained notes that wove together into something like a wall—and the third went up under his ribs and into the heart that had beaten beside Astrid's heart through eleven years of contracts and cold camps and the long grey peace after.

He fell against them. His blood was hot through Astrid's sleeve. His mouth kept moving.

"You'll see," he whispered, in trade-tongue now, human and small. "When Vael wakes, there'll be no more hunger. No more winters like this one. You'll see, and you'll weep, and he'll forgive you."

"I know your wife's name," Astrid said, because it was true, because the memories had come back like floodwater through a broken door and every one of them had a person attached, and because they wanted him to hear it from someone who remembered. "Sefa. She made plum brandy and watered it so the young ones wouldn't get sick. She's dead, Corvin. She's been dead thirty years, and no god came."

His eyes went wide. Not with fear—with recognition, the awful kind, the kind that finds a face across decades. "Astrid?"

The name hit harder than any blade had all night. Astrid stepped back and let him fall.

* * *

They had not planned to fight. The plan—if Maren's coughing, bleeding sketch of a plan deserved the word—had been to go around: down the goat track behind the mill, along the frozen creek, to the standing stone where Wren had been taken. But the Choir had come over the mistline in their hundreds, and the mist had torn like wet paper, and the goat track was full of white robes moving uphill with torches, singing.

So Astrid went through them instead.

It should have been impossible. One mercenary, even a good one, does not cut through two hundred faithful. But the Choir did not want to kill Astrid. That was the horror of it, the thing that made every stroke cost double—they wanted to *convert*. They fought with staffs and hooked poles, weapons meant to bind and drag, and they called out as they pressed in, calling Astrid by rank, by name, by the old titles of a company that had been dust before most of them were born.

*Sword-Captain.* *Oath-bearer.* *The one who carried it.*

And every name they spoke, Astrid remembered more.

That was the cruelty underneath everything, Astrid understood now, running, cutting, breathing smoke. The silence-oath had not erased the past. It had only locked it, and the lock was keyed to the First Tongue, and the Choir sang nothing else. Every hymn was a key. Every verse turned another tumbler, and behind the tumblers were faces.

*Tamsin,* Astrid thought, driving a hooked pole aside and putting a knee into its owner's chest. *Tamsin of the Red Ford, who taught me to sleep sitting up in a saddle.*

The woman under the pole had Tamsin's jaw. Her daughter's jaw, maybe. Granddaughter's. She looked up at Astrid with no recognition at all, only radiant certainty, and she smiled as the point went in, and Astrid felt the smile go through them deeper than steel ever could.

* * *

The village was lost. Astrid had known it since the first torch arced over the mistline, but knowing and seeing were different animals, and seeing nearly stopped them in the middle of the lane.

The Hollis farm gone. The meeting-house gone, its bell still tolling though half the tower had fallen, a mad bronze heartbeat under the roar. Old Petra's dye-works, where Wren had spent a whole rainbound week staining their hands blue and laughing—gone, the vats split open, the snow around them running indigo like a wound that bled color. People knelt in the lanes with their hands on their heads while white-robed cantors walked between them singing the submission-verses, gentle as lullabies, and the kneeling villagers' mouths moved along without their permission, because the First Tongue did not ask.

Astrid killed two cantors in the lane. Neither fought well. Neither was a soldier. One of them could not have been older than nineteen, and as the light left her she said, wonderingly, "I heard him. I heard Vael in my sleep. He's real, he's *real*—" and Astrid, who had heard Vael too, thirty years ago, in a voice like a mountain deciding, could not argue.

That was the worst of it. Ilse's fanatics were not liars. They were believers, and their belief was built on a foundation Astrid had laid with their own oath-sworn hands, and every one of them dying on Astrid's blade had a name, and Astrid knew them all now, knew them the way you know a scar's history, and grief came off them in waves that smelled of copper and burnt barley.

*Keep moving.* Maren's voice in memory, rough as gravel: *Grief is a country, Blackwood. March through it. Don't build a house.*

Astrid marched.

* * *

The standing stone rose above the orchard on the hill, black against the firelight, older than the valley, older than the god perhaps. The Choir had ringed it. Of course they had. Cantor Ilse was no fool; whatever ritual she intended, she would perform it where the original words had been spoken, on ground soaked in the First Tongue until the stones themselves probably dreamed in it.

Wren was there. Astrid saw the small figure at the base of the stone—a scrap of blue-dyed wool Astrid had mended themselves, a shock of dark hair—and something in Astrid's chest tore loose from its moorings and drove them up the hill faster than sense allowed.

Three guards barred the orchard gate. Veterans, these—ex-company, the ones Ilse had gathered from the old families, the children and grandchildren of sworn soldiers raised on stories of the silenced god. They fought in formation, shields high, singing the binding-canticle, and the song wrapped around Astrid's sword arm like wet rope.

And Astrid knew the counter-song.

It rose without permission, out of the recovered depths, out of thirty years of sealed memory: the company's marching cadence, the one Sefa used to beat out on a pot with a wooden spoon, the rhythm that broke a canticle apart the way a shout breaks glass. Astrid sang it through gritted teeth, ugly and off-key and human, and felt the binding loosen, and laughed—actually laughed, a horrible sound—because this was the answer, wasn't it? This had always been the answer. Imperfection. Noise. The graceless, unbindable mess of being people.

The guards faltered. Astrid did not.

The first died quickly and Astrid knew his grandfather's face, knew the man had once shared a tent with Astrid through a winter in the Karst passes, and Astrid said the grandfather's name aloud as the grandson fell, an apology in the only currency left. The second threw down her shield when she saw how it would go and Astrid, shaking, spared her—knocked her cold with the pommel and stepped over her body, because if the world was ending tonight it would not end with Astrid executing a surrendering child of old friends.

The third was Maren's problem. Maren came limping out of the smoke with a borrowed axe and no left boot, took one look, and said, "Go. I've got Dain's boy. We'll talk." And there was something in the old sergeant's face that said *we will not talk, one of us will not walk away from this*, and Astrid chose—chose, deliberately, the way you choose to keep walking past a beggar whose eyes you can't meet—to believe the lie.

"Wren!" Astrid shouted, and the child's head came up, and hope has never had a sound so small and so enormous.

* * *

Then the crowd between parted, and Cantor Ilse stood in the gap.

She was not what the fires had promised. Astrid had imagined a monster, a zealot carved from ice and iron, and instead here was a thin woman in her fifties with wind-chapped cheeks and ink stains on her fingers, holding a hymnal the way other people hold grandchildren. Behind her, the last of her inner circle formed a crescent, censers swinging, and the smoke they poured out smelled of myrrh and something older, something that made the hair on Astrid's arms stand up—the smell of the standing stone itself waking.

"Sword-Captain Blackwood." Her voice was quiet. It carried anyway; the First Tongue gave it bones. "Thirty years I prayed for a sign. Do you know what he told me, in the dream? He said: *my sword still lives, in the valley that forgets.* He has been waiting for you."

"He's been waiting for a vessel," Astrid said. "The child is not a door you get to walk through, Ilse. I've seen what comes through doors like that. I helped shut one."

"And look what the shutting bought us." For the first time her voice cracked, and what showed underneath was not madness but arithmetic. "My daughter ate pine bark the winter she was six. My husband froze hauling charcoal because there was no blessing to warm the kilns, no order, no *purpose*—just a world of scattered people scraping at dirt under an empty sky. You did that. Your oath did that. And you hid in your pretty fog while the rest of us starved quietly."

Every word landed. That was the unforgivable thing about her; every word was true, or true enough, and Astrid's recovered memories supplied the rest—the famine years, the gray generations, the prayers that rose like smoke from a fire that wasn't there.

"I know," Astrid said. "God help me, Ilse, I know what it cost. I paid it too. I paid it *twice*."

"Then pay once more." She lifted the hymnal. "Give us the vessel. Wake him whole, wake him as he was, and by harvest there will be bread in every granary from here to the sea. Or stand there, and we take the child, and you die forgotten in a burning orchard, and nothing changes except the speed."

Behind her, at the stone, Wren had begun to speak.

Small voice. First Tongue. The waking-words, half-remembered, stumbling—and the air above the standing stone began, very softly, to glow.

Ilse turned toward it like a flower toward sun, arms spreading, hymnal falling open, and her choir drew breath for the great canticle that would carry the child the rest of the way—

Astrid put their head down and ran at all of them, screaming the company's marching cadence at the top of a ruined voice, sword low, grief and love and fury braided into one long note, and the last thing they saw before the white robes closed over them was Wren turning, finding them through the smoke, and mouthing one word in the old tongue:

*Astrid.*

Their own name. Spoken perfectly. It burned all the way down.
