## 15. Cantor Ilse

The valley burned behind them in long ribbons of smoke, and Ilse found that she could not look away from it.

Not from horror. She had seen enough burning to be past horror—barns in the Godless Years, granaries put to the torch by men who had nothing left to take, a whole winter's village of desperate faces lit orange against the snow. No, what held her was the beauty of it, the terrible rightness: the mist that had hidden this place for a generation was tearing like old linen, and through every rip poured light. Morning light. Honest light, unfiltered by enchantment, falling on soil that had been hoarded by cowards while children starved three valleys east.

*This is what hoarding looks like when it is finally opened*, she thought. *It looks like dawn.*

"Cantor." Brother Anselm limped up beside her, his hymnal clutched to his chest with both hands as though the smoke might steal it. His face was gray with ash and something worse than ash. "The scouts we sent ahead. The ones who crossed first."

"I know."

"They didn't scream. That's the part I—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They just came apart. Like a chord resolving wrong."

Ilse kept walking. Her boots crunched on frost-burned grass, and beneath the crunch she could hear, faint and far, the sound of water moving somewhere in the valley's heart—the sound of a land that had never known drought, never known rationing, never known a mother boiling leather for soup because there was nothing else. She had heard such water described in the old testimonies, back when the Choir was nine ragged people meeting in a cellar in Ashford, whispering hymns they only half remembered. Water that moved like it had somewhere generous to go.

She had hated that sound before she had ever heard it.

* * *

The winter Ilse turned eleven, the snow came in October and did not properly leave until April, and her father sold their second ox in November and their last hope in December, though he called it something else when he said it aloud. Her mother boiled pine bark. Ilse remembered the taste of it still—bitter, resinous, a flavor like punishment—and she remembered how her mother smiled while serving it, because mothers in those years had learned that despair was a food you could not afford to serve alongside the bark.

There were four of them, then. Ilse; her father, a wheelwright with hands too honest for the times; her mother; and Tomas, who was six and believed everything he was told, which in the Godless Years was the most dangerous condition a child could suffer.

Tomas believed the reeve when he said the relief wagons were coming. Tomas believed the priest when the priest said the gods had abandoned them for good reason, that some sin of the old wars had earned this silence, and that penance meant waiting. Tomas believed her father when he said, in the voice fathers use, that everything would be all right.

Tomas died in February, on a Tuesday, quietly, in the space between one breath and the next, as if even his breathing had finally accepted that no one was coming.

Her mother lasted until April. She did not die of hunger exactly. She died of the look on her husband's face, Ilse thought later—the look of a man doing arithmetic every waking hour and arriving at the same answer. Her mother walked out into the last of the snow one morning wearing her good shawl, the blue one, the one she'd been saving, and they found her where the river ice had taken her, and Ilse understood even at eleven that her mother had chosen the river over the arithmetic, and that she could not entirely blame her.

Her father lived two more years. He became a quiet man who worked wood for bread and spoke of the old god Vael only once, the night before he died, when fever had loosened whatever clamp he kept on himself.

"He wasn't cruel," her father had said, staring at the ceiling beams. "Everyone says he was cruel. But I stood in his temple once, girl, when I was young. Before. There were granaries behind it that fed forty villages. Forty. Not because we begged. Because order demanded it. You've never known order. You think it's just being told what to do." His hand had found hers, fever-hot and shaking. "Order is a world where no one has to choose between their children. He took that away from us when he went quiet? No. We took it from ourselves when we sealed him. Remember that, Ilse. Whatever they tell you. Remember which side did the sealing."

She had remembered. Through the orphan house, through the years of scrounging work in Ashford, through the day a ragged cantor named Odile had found her singing an old fragment of hymn over her father's grave—not because she believed it but because the melody was the only beautiful thing she owned—through all of it, she had remembered.

*Which side did the sealing.*

* * *

Now she walked through a green valley in the last week of winter, and everywhere she looked she saw abundance, and everywhere she looked she saw theft.

Orchards in careful rows, untended and yet heavy-limbed, as if the trees themselves had been spared the despair that stunts growth. A millstream running full and unhurried. Fields lying fallow not from exhaustion but from choice—there was rich black soil under that frost, soil that could feed a hundred families, and it lay here sleeping behind a wall of forgetting-mist while the eastern valleys scraped lichen off stones.

"Godless," Anselm muttered beside her, meaning the valley's inhabitants, and Ilse did not correct him, because it was true in the way that mattered. These people had not merely lost their god. They had *chosen* the losing. They had come here to forget him, the way a debtor flees a town where his debts are known.

And one of them, she reminded herself, looking down the column of her faithful—two hundred strong now, armed with pikes and hymnals and the particular ferocity of the vindicated—one of them had done the sealing itself. The mercenary. Sterling. The name had surfaced in Maren Hale's interrogation like a bone from shallow soil, and when Ilse had spoken it aloud in the war-council, half her singers had wept. Everyone knew the story in its corrupted form: the traitor who silenced heaven. Almost no one knew it truly.

But Ilse had spent twenty years learning to read corrupted texts. That was her gift, her office, her burden. Give her a hymnal mangled by a century of frightened copyists and she would find the original line underneath, the way a stream finds its old bed. And so when the fragments about the silence-oath had come to her—fragments gathered at hideous cost, from archives and graves and one dying apostate in Vellmark—she had read them the way she read everything.

And she had understood: the seal required a volunteer. Someone had to carry the silence in their own body, pay for it out of their own life. The histories called Sterling a betrayer. Ilse called them what the text called them: *the vessel of forgetting*. The lock, walking around in human shape, hiding in the one place where its own nature could not torment it.

Which meant the lock could be opened. Which meant the key existed too—a child, the scouts' reports said, a child who spoke the First Tongue without being taught, who had walked out of the mountains alone in dead winter and survived.

A child who had been *sent*.

"You're certain," Anselm said now, for perhaps the tenth time since the breach. "Certain the equinox—"

"The thirteenth turn completes at sundown on the equinox." Ilse did not break stride. "The moon the old calendars refused to count. The texts are clear: the seal thins when the count goes past twelve. It has thinned every year for thirteen years—we simply weren't strong enough to act on it before. This year, with the vessel awake and the key arrived, the seal can be broken cleanly. Spoken open. Or—" she glanced at the smoke, at the hole the dying scouts had torn in the mist, "—torn open, if it comes to that. Tearing costs more. I would prefer to spare the valley further grief."

Anselm made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Spare them grief. Cantor, we burned their sanctuary this morning."

"We opened their granaries," Ilse said. "That is what we did. Everything else is accounting."

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a shrine—an old one, pre-silence, its stone Vael worn smooth by centuries of foreheads. The Choir's singers cleared the debris and set up the great drum, and soon the evening hymn rose over the valley, two hundred voices in the modal lines that Ilse had spent two decades restoring from corruption.

She did not sing. That was her custom; the cantor listened, always, because a cantor who could no longer hear her singers as they truly were was already corrupt. She sat apart on the shrine's worn steps with her own hymnal open on her knees—the first one, the cellar hymnal, nine voices' worth of shaky notation in brown ink—and she let the restored music wash over her and searched it, as always, for the flaws.

There. Third phrase, the ascending line: the Ashford tradition flattened the fourth degree, had always flattened it, because the copyist who survived the purges had learned the melody from a woman with a damaged throat. Two hundred voices sang the flaw nightly, beautifully, in perfect unison, and it was wrong, and Ilse loved them for it anyway.

That was the thing no one understood about her, she thought. They thought conviction was certainty. It was not. Conviction was standing in the wreckage of every certainty you had ever been handed—gods silent, priests corrupt, harvests failed, parents dead—and choosing, deliberately, with open eyes, to build one true thing out of the rubble and hold it. She did not know that Vael would return gentle. She did not know that abundance would follow him like a tide follows the moon. She knew only that the world without him had starved her family, and the world with him had fed forty villages, and that she had watched both with her own eyes, and that a person had to choose what their eyes had seen.

Below her, the hymn ended. In the silence after, she heard one of the younger singers—a girl named Petra, seventeen, hollow-cheeked, rescued from a debt-farm two winters ago—saying to another, low and wondering:

"Do you think they have children here? In the valley? Do you think the children here have ever been hungry?"

And the other singer laughed softly, bitterly. "Not once. Look at them. Look at this place. Not once."

Petra was quiet a moment. Then: "Good," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "I hope it hurts them. I hope—when we wake Him—I hope they learn what it tastes like. Just once. Just so they know what they kept from us."

Ilse closed her eyes.

There it was. The other current, the one she could never fully dam: not faith but fury, wearing faith's robes. Half her Choir sang for restoration. The other half sang for the bill coming due. She could not sort them apart, could not tell which voices carried which, and she had stopped trying, because the truth was that both currents ran in her own chest, braided together past any hope of separation. She wanted Vael back because order was mercy. She wanted Vael back because she wanted the comfortable world to hurt, just once, just enough to understand.

Both things were true. Both things had marched her over the mistline.

*Father*, she thought, to the cold stars above the torn mist, *you said remember which side did the sealing. I remembered. I built my whole life on remembering. But tell me—if the seal breaks and He wakes, will He be the granary-keeper you stood before? Or will He be the fever that took you, the silence that took Tomas, the arithmetic that took Mother? What if we sealed Him because He had already become the silence?*

The stars gave no answer. Stars never did. That was the whole sickness of the Godless Years, distilled: everything above you, silent.

Tomorrow night the moon completed its turn. Tomorrow night the standing stone in the valley's heart would stand at the center of a thinning world, and either the child would speak the waking-words willingly, or—

Ilse turned the thought over carefully, the way she turned flawed phrases in a hymnal, examining it for damage.

—or the mercenary would do what mercenaries did, and stand between the Choir and the key, and there would be violence at the stone, and the seal would tear instead of opening, and the texts were very unclear about what woke when the seal tore. She had read those passages a hundred times. *What comes through a torn door does not knock,* one fragment said. She had chosen to believe it was copyist's embellishment.

She had to believe it. The alternative was that she was leading two hundred starving believers into breaking the one thing keeping something terrible asleep, and she had watched enough parents make that arithmetic to know how it ended.

No. It would open cleanly. The child would speak. Children wanted to please; children wanted to belong; the Choir had bread and songs and two hundred warm bodies in a cold season, and the mercenary had only secrets and a fading valley. It would be simple. It would be almost kind.

"Cantor." Anselm again, at the bottom of the steps, and there was something in his voice that pulled her upright before he finished. "Riders. From the vanguard. You need to hear this."

They brought the news to her by firelight: the vanguard had reached the valley's heart at dusk and found the standing stone—and found it guarded. One figure, alone, standing in the stone's long shadow with a blade already drawn. And above, on the ridge beyond, a small shape watching them, a child's silhouette against the last light, motionless, patient, as though it had been waiting for them all along.

As though it had been *led* there.

"And the mercenary?" Ilse asked, though she knew.

"That's them. The one at the stone." The scout hesitated. "Cantor—they didn't run. When they saw us, they just... planted their feet. And I swear on the hymnal, I heard them say something in the old tongue. One word. And the ground answered."

Around the fire, the singers had gone still. Two hundred faces turned toward her, ash-gray and firelit, hungry and holy and afraid.

Ilse looked down at her cellar hymnal, at the brown ink, at the ninth voice's wobbly notes, at everything she had built from rubble. Then she closed it, and stood, and felt the thirteenth moon rising somewhere behind the clouds, counting down.

"Rest," she told them. "Eat. Tomorrow at dusk we sing the waking-hymn at the stone." She paused, and heard herself add, softly, in her father's voice without meaning to: "Whatever answers, we will meet it together."

Above the valley, the clouds parted, and the thirteenth moon showed its face—full one night early, swollen and strange, as if it too had been waiting, and had grown impatient of calendars.
