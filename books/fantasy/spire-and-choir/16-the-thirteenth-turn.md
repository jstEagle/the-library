## 16. The Thirteenth Turn

The valley burned behind us, and the snow did not care.

It fell the way it had fallen all winter—softly, indifferently, each flake a small white lie that the world was still gentle. It hissed where it met fire. The orchard on the southern slope was gone; I could see the glow of it through the trees like a wound in the dark, orange light pulsing under the smoke. Somewhere down there, Maren was holding the mill bridge with six of the valley folk and whatever weapons we'd been able to press into their hands. Somewhere down there, people who had never sworn anything to anyone were dying for my silence.

I did not look back. Looking back is how you fall.

Wren's hand was small and cold in mine, and they ran when I ran and stumbled when I stumbled, and they did not ask where we were going because Wren had never once asked me a question I could not bear to answer, and this one I could not have borne.

*The stone,* Maren had said, pressing her knife into my hand with her blood still wet on it. *Take them to the stone. If it has to end, let it end where it began.*

The mountain path was a scar of switchbacks above the tree line, and the wind up there had teeth. It came off the peaks in long white gusts that scoured the breath from my lungs and drove ice needles into every seam of my coat. I pulled Wren ahead of me by the hand, then behind me when the gusts turned, keeping my body between them and the open air the way I had once kept it between myself and arrows. Some habits are older than memory. That is the cruelty of what I am: I forgot the faces of everyone I ever loved, but my body remembers how to stand in front of someone.

"Almost there," I said, and my voice came out shredded.

"I know." Wren's voice carried strangely in the wind—not loud, just *present*, the way their voice always was now, since the lessons, since the words had begun to live in them like seeds in warm soil. "I can feel it."

Of course they could. The stone had been calling them since the night they walked out of the mountains half-frozen and speaking a dead language. I had spent a season pretending not to understand what that meant. There was no more pretending left. The valley had taught me the value of not-knowing, and the Choir had burned the lesson out of me in a single night.

We climbed.

* * *

The standing stone stood at the crown of the world, or close enough to it—a single grey monolith taller than three men, thrust up from the bare granite of the summit plateau like a finger pointing at nothing. Snow did not settle on it. I noticed that as we came up over the last rise, gasping, and found the circle around its base swept clean as a hearthstone, black rock gleaming wet in the moonlight. Thirteen turns of the moon since the oath, Maren had said. Thirteen turns, and the thirteenth ended tonight, on the equinox, when the hours balanced on a knife's edge between dark and light.

The old faith had called nights like this *the listening hours*. When the world holds its breath, everything spoken is heard.

I knew this place. Not with my mind—with something deeper, some sediment of self that the silence-oath had not reached. My feet knew the way around the stone, clockwise, the way you approach a thing you must ask forgiveness of. My knees knew the hollow worn into the rock at its base, a shallow bowl polished smooth by centuries of kneeling. When I set my palm against the stone's flank, it was warm.

Stones are not warm in winter. Stones are not warm at all.

"Astrid." Wren tugged my sleeve. Their face was pinched with cold, lips gone pale, but their eyes were wide and steady and terribly awake. "It's humming."

I hadn't heard it yet. Then I stopped breathing, and I did: a note below hearing, felt in the bones of my hand and the roots of my teeth, a vibration like a struck bell that has been ringing for thirteen years without permission to stop. The god Vael, sealed beneath silence, and the seal was me, and the seal was cracking.

"How long," I said, and didn't know I'd said it aloud until Wren answered.

"Since the fires started." They hugged their arms around themselves. "It got louder when the mist broke. Like—" They frowned, searching for the word the way they always did, carefully, refusing to guess. Wren never guessed. Guessing was lying's little cousin, and Wren did not lie. "Like someone calling through a wall. And now we're on the other side of the wall."

Below us, the valley was a map of ruin. The mistline—the border that had hidden us for thirteen years, that had dissolved scouts and kept out gods and kings alike—was torn open along its whole eastern length, a ragged gap glowing with the light of burning homesteads. Tiny figures moved in the torchlight. Even from here I could pick out the Choir's formation: not a mob, not a raiding party, but ranks, ordered and unhurried, advancing up the valley floor like a hymn being sung very slowly. Cantor Ilse did not rush. Rushing was doubt. Ilse had never doubted anything in her life except, perhaps, whether mercy was a virtue, and she had concluded that it was not.

They would reach the mountain path by dawn. They would climb. Nothing would stop them now—the mist was dead, the valley's invisibility spent like a candle burned to the socket, and the only things between the Choir and this stone were Maren and her six, and distance, and the last hours of an oath I no longer understood how to keep.

"Wren." I crouched to their level. My knees cracked. I was older than I remembered being—that was the joke of it, the bitter punchline of my whole existence: I had traded my memories for survival, and survival had made me a stranger to myself. "Look at me."

They looked. They always looked. Trust like that should be illegal.

"In a little while, someone is going to come up this path, and they will be kind to you. That's important—you need to know that. Ilse will kneel. She'll speak softly. She might cry. She lost children to the famine years, and she believes with her whole broken heart that the god in this stone could have saved them, and she is not lying to you. She believes every word."

"But she's wrong."

"She's wrong the way a fever is wrong. It burns because the body is trying so hard to live." I tucked a strand of hair behind their ear, frost-stiff, and my fingers shook and I let them see it shake. "She wants to put the waking-words in your mouth like a sword into a soldier's hand. She wants Vael back the way he was—the voice that commands, the hunger that eats whole countries and calls it feeding them. And you are the door she thinks she can kick open."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Snow gathered on their shoulders and they didn't brush it off. "But you said the words don't work like that. You said a language isn't a weapon, it's a *handshake*. You said—" they paused, and recited, in my own cadence, my own lesson coming back to me: "'Words bind the speaker first. Always. Whoever speaks truest, serves deepest.'"

"I did say that." My throat closed. "Yes."

"So if I speak the waking-words, I don't free Vael for Ilse." Their brow furrowed, working it through, this child of maybe ten years doing theology on a mountaintop in a snowstorm while the world burned below. "I *bind myself* to him. Whatever he wakes up as. Whatever I make him, by how I say it."

"That's why she needs you alive and willing. A coerced vessel speaks crooked. She knows it. She's been patient for thirteen turns of the moon because she needed you to *choose* it." I laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked sound the wind stole. "Patience. From fanatics. Who would have thought."

"You're scared," Wren said.

"Yes."

"Not of dying."

The wind screamed past the stone. Below, a second fire bloomed—Maren's position, or near it—and I made myself not count the seconds, not do the arithmetic of how long six people could hold a bridge against forty. "No," I said. "Not of dying."

"Of what happens after. To me. Or—" and here Wren's voice went careful, the way it did when they were approaching something fragile, "—to whoever you were. Before."

I sat down in the snow. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply ended their contract with standing. The stone hummed against my spine through my coat, that buried bell-note, patient as geology. Above us the sky was tearing open, clouds shredding off the peaks, and the stars came out hard and cold and numberless, and among them the equinox moon rode fat and white and one sliver short of full—the thirteenth turn, ending tonight, exactly as the old reckoning promised.

Thirteen moons ago I had knelt in this spot—I knew it the way you know a room you've woken screaming in—and I had spoken the silence-oath, and I had paid for it with every face I ever loved, and I had walked down into a valley designed to help me forget that I had forgotten. Thirteen moons of the moon's own turning. A year and a day times twelve, plus the odd hours. Long enough for a child to be born, grow, walk out of the mountains, and find me. Long enough for a god's patience to run out.

Or long enough for something else. That was the thought I kept circling, the splinter in my mind: Maren's story, told in the lamplight weeks ago, of the last war. Vael demanding total obedience. Vael's faithful feeding the granaries of cities that starved anyway. Vael's voice in ten thousand mouths at once until no one could hear their own thoughts. And a mercenary company—the Ashen Company, my company, though I remembered none of their faces, only Maren's account of them, only grief borrowed secondhand—deciding that a silent god was better than a hungry one, and paying the price that decision cost.

But Wren had changed the equation. Wren, who refused to guess. Wren, who had been offered protection in exchange for obedience and had chosen instead a tired mercenary with holes where a life used to be. Wren, whose imperfect mouth was the only imperfect thing in a system built on perfection, and whose imperfection might be the only mercy left in the world.

If Vael woke through Wren, he woke through a child who said *please* before *thank you* and got them mixed up sometimes. Who pronounced the First Tongue slightly wrong in ways that made Astrid bleed from the nose but also made the words sound—there was no other word for it—*human*. Who had already refused the god once, alone, in the dark, choosing me.

What would wake, if Wren spoke? Not the tyrant. Something else. Something asked-for rather than imposed.

And what would it cost?

I knew that too. I had known it since the lessons began, since each true word cracked another seal inside me and gave me back another piece of the person I had been—fragments at first, then floods: a woman named Odile who laughed with her whole body. A boy called Tam who collected river stones. A lover whose name arrived in my dreams two weeks ago and sat on my chest all night like a bird I couldn't shoo away. Every word Wren learned correctly, I remembered more, and bled more, and the ledger was explicit: the oath would take it all back. Every recovered memory, reclaimed. Every face, erased again—or worse than erased, because this time I would watch them go. This time I would know what I was losing while I lost it.

Unless.

Unless the waking unmade the oath entirely. Unless a god reborn through an imperfect mouth released the seal that bound him, and the price was forgiven, and I got to keep the faces. Or unless the price was only deferred, and larger, and due at dawn.

I didn't know. That was the truth of this stillness on the mountaintop: I did not know, and there was no one left to ask, and the only oracle available was a standing stone and a child, and both of them were waiting on me.

* * *

"They're climbing," Wren said.

I looked down. Torches on the mountain path—first one, then five, then a constellation of them, winding up the switchbacks with the unhurried discipline of a procession. Behind them, the valley glowed. Ahead of them, the path crested the tree line and became bare rock and wind, and there was nothing on it, nothing at all, between the Choir and this summit except the miles of cold and the time it took to climb them.

An hour. Maybe less. Ilse's people were hardened pilgrims; they'd marched through worse than this to reach shrines far less real.

I got up. My body obeyed the way bodies do when the mind has run out of alternatives—it simply continued, because stopping was not among its options. I drew the knife Maren had given me and then, deliberately, drove it point-down into the frozen ground beside the stone, within reach but not in hand. A statement. To whom, I wasn't sure. Perhaps to myself.

Then I took Wren's shoulders and turned them to face me, and I knelt again in the worn hollow at the stone's base, the hollow my knees apparently already knew, and I looked at the child the god had chosen and tried to find the words for what I had to say next.

Here is what I had: fragments. A language I'd been sworn never to teach, reconstructed from a dying comrade's whispers and a child's half-memory and my own bleeding reconstructions. Enough to wake a god, maybe. Certainly enough to die of.

Here is what I had to decide: whether to stop Wren from speaking at all—to take them over the mountain's back side, north into the passes, running forever, the war following us from valley to valley until one of us fell. Whether to let Ilse have them, and trust that a child's stubborn heart could out-bend a fanatic's certainty. Or whether to stand here, in the listening hours, at the place where the oath was sworn, and do the maddest thing available: teach the waking-words myself. Speak them alongside. Break the language's perfect power with my imperfect human mouth, and wake Vael changed—or unmake the world trying.

"Wren," I said. "Do you remember what I told you about oaths? The first lesson?"

"Words bind the speaker first."

"And the second lesson?"

Their eyes flicked to the stone, to the humming dark, to the torches winding up the mountain. When they spoke again, their voice was very small and very clear. "That some words, once spoken, can't be taken back. Only answered."

The wind dropped. All at once, the way a held breath releases. The snow fell straight down, slow as ash, and the stone's hum rose through my knees and my ribs and settled behind my sternum like a second heartbeat, and somewhere below us, faint and growing, I heard it: singing. The Choir, climbing, singing their corrupted hymnal in their corrupted tongue, each verse a key testing a lock.

The moon touched the peak of the western ridge. The equinox hour had begun.

I took Wren's hands. They were freezing. So were mine. Between us, the god waited, and the enemy climbed, and the choice stood in front of us like the stone itself—vast, ancient, and utterly indifferent to whether we were ready.

"Then listen carefully, little bird," I said, "because I'm going to teach you the words your voice was made for. And this time, we're going to say them *wrong*."

Wren's eyes went wide. Below us, the first torches cleared the tree line.

And the stone began, very softly, to sing back.
