## 17. Speak, Little Bird

The standing stone was older than the valley. Older than the mist that hid it, older than the road that had once led pilgrims up from Ashford before the road forgot itself and sank into moss. It rose out of the frozen earth like a black tooth, taller than three men, its surface carved with letters so deep that snow could not fill them—letters Wren had known since before knowing, the way you know your own heartbeat.

*Here,* said the voice inside them, gentle as ever, patient as falling snow. *Here is where it began. Here is where it ends.*

Wren's bare feet ached against the stone's threshold. They had left their boots at the treeline because Astrid had told them once—back when Astrid still remembered telling things—that oaths were sworn skin to earth or not at all. The cold climbed up through their soles like water finding cracks in clay. Above them the sky was doing something wrong: the stars were moving, sliding sideways in slow spirals, as if the whole bowl of night had been lifted by its rim and tilted. The thirteenth moon hung low and swollen over the mountains, too close, the color of an old scar.

Behind them, down the slope, the valley was burning.

Wren did not look. If they looked, they would see the orchard where they'd learned to climb go up in light. They would see the meetinghouse, the smokehouse, Maren's little house with the blue door she'd painted herself because *a door should tell you something good before you open it.* They would hear the singing—the Choir's hymn rolling down off the ridgeline in a hundred voices, that beautiful terrible music that was almost the First Tongue but not, corrupted the way a mirror corrupts a face—and they would want to stop. They would want to run back down into the fire and do something small and useless and human.

So they didn't look. They looked at the stone instead, and at the words cut into it, and at the place where Astrid knelt in the snow ten paces away, breathing hard, one hand pressed to their ribs where a Choir blade had found the gap in their armor on the way up the hill. The blood was very dark in the starlight. It steamed.

"Astrid," Wren said. "You're hurt."

"I'm old." Astrid's voice came out rough, half a laugh. "There's a difference. Mostly." They tried to stand, failed, settled for kneeling straighter. Snow gathered on their shoulders like a second mantle. "Listen to me, little bird. Before you start. There are things I need—" Their voice caught, and for a moment Wren saw something move behind their eyes that was not pain but grief, grief arriving all at once after twenty years of arriving nowhere. "I remember now. All of it. I remember swearing this oath. I remember choosing it. And I need you to know that whatever happens when you speak—if the god comes back wrong, if the world breaks open, if I forget your face before the sun rises—it was worth it. You were worth it. Do you understand me?"

"You keep saying things like goodbye," Wren said. "I don't like it."

Astrid smiled. It was a real smile, tired and crooked, the kind Wren had been collecting all winter like river stones. "I've earned my goodbyes. Let me have them." Then the smile faded, and Astrid looked past Wren, up the slope, toward the sound of the singing growing louder, and their jaw set the way it did before a fight. "But first—wake him. Wake him better than he was. That's the only order I'll ever give you, and I'm giving it in Common, so it doesn't bind. Just love. Choose it."

The voice inside Wren stirred, and Wren felt it the way you feel a bird turning under your ribs—not painful, never painful, but full, so full it ached. *Now,* the voice said. *Speak me now, vessel. Speak me whole.*

Wren turned to the stone.

The waking-words were written there in the First Tongue, and the First Tongue was not like other languages. Other languages described things. The First Tongue *did* them. Every word was a small act of creation; every sentence, a hinge in the architecture of the world. Wren had known this since the mountain, since the voice had first begun teaching them syllable by syllable in the dark of the passes, and they had understood it the way children understand things—completely, without fear. Words made. Words unmade. Words woke what slept.

They put both palms flat against the stone. The cold bit through their skin, and beneath the cold they felt something else: a pulse. Slow. Enormous. Once every thirteen heartbeats, as if the stone itself had been counting the turns of the moon, waiting.

Wren opened their mouth and began.

"*Vael-annu. Vael-annu sereth-kai.*"

The words left them like heat leaving a wound. The first phrase struck the stone and the stone *rang*—not with sound but with meaning, a vibration that went up through Wren's arms and teeth and the soft bones behind their eyes. Down in the valley, the singing faltered. On the ridgeline, torches swung wildly. Somewhere far away, in a country Wren had never seen, rivers changed course by a finger's width.

"*Vael-annu. Torath il-maren. Torath il—*"

They faltered.

It happened between one word and the next, the way stumbling happens—you are walking and then you are falling and there is no moment in between where you decide. Wren knew the word. *Il-sorath.* Arise. It was carved right there under their fingers, deep and clean. But their mouth would not make it, because their mind had finally done what it had refused to do all winter: it looked down the hill. It saw the fire. It saw the small dark shapes running between the burning houses, and it understood—not with the voice's certainty but with its own child's certainty—that when the god woke, those shapes would either kneel or be made to kneel, and that the making would be called mercy, and that no one would be allowed to say otherwise, ever again, anywhere, forever.

Because that was what the First Tongue was. Absolute. A word spoken perfectly could not be taken back. Could not be argued with. Could not be *human.*

"I can't," Wren whispered. The word died in the cold air, small and gray. "*Il-sorath* means arise and I—I can't say it right, I can't say it if saying it right means everyone has to—"

*Say it anyway,* the voice said, and for the first time in all the turns of the moon, Wren heard something underneath the gentleness. Something vast and hungry and old, the thing the gentleness was wrapped around, the way a bandage wraps a blade. *Say it and I will save them all. Say it and there will be no more burning, no more starving, no more choosing. Only order. Only peace. Speak, vessel. SPEAK—*

And the voice stopped being gentle, and Wren felt it seize the words in their own throat like a hand closing around a bird, and their mouth began to shape *il-sorath* without them—

"No."

Astrid's voice. Not loud. Astrid never needed to be loud. But it cut through the seizing pressure like a knife through rope, and Wren's throat unlocked, and they turned.

Astrid had gotten to their feet. Blood ran freely down their side now, dripping from their fingertips into the snow, each drop hissing faintly where it fell—as if even the ground remembered what Astrid's blood had sworn here, twenty years ago, and flinched. They were looking at the stone, and their face was terrible and calm.

"It wants perfect," Astrid said. "That's the trap. That was always the trap. Perfect words, perfect obedience, a world with no gaps in it." They took a step forward, then another, dragging their wounded leg. "But listen to me, Wren. Listen. Nothing alive is perfect. Your heartbeat isn't perfect. The seasons aren't perfect. My memory of your face won't be perfect tomorrow, because I won't *have* it, and someone will have to tell it to me like a story, and stories change every time they're told, and that's not a flaw. That's how anything stays *alive.*"

"Wake him better than he was," Wren said. "Your order."

"My request." Astrid reached them, dropped to one knee in the snow so their faces were level, and took Wren's small cold hands in their scarred warm ones. Up close, Wren could see everything the last day had written on them: the burn along the jaw, the blood, the exhaustion deep as well-water. And underneath all of it, steady as bedrock, something Wren had no word for in any language except maybe this: *I am here. I am not leaving.*

"So we do it wrong," Astrid said. "On purpose. We wake him the way a mother wakes a child—with mistakes, with love, with our voices cracking. Imperfect words for an imperfect world. And if the language demands perfection, then we deny it the one thing it cannot survive without." Their grip tightened. "We deny it absoluteness. Are you with me, little bird?"

Above them the sky shuddered. The thirteenth moon pulsed like a heart. Down the hill, the Choir's hymn swelled toward its final verse, and Cantor Ilse's voice rose above the rest, clear and desperate and sincere, and the firelight turned the snow the color of roses.

Wren looked at Astrid's hands around theirs. Scarred. Steady. Human.

"Yes," Wren said.

They turned back to the stone together, side by side, and Wren began again—but differently now. Slowly. Deliberately imperfectly.

"*Vael-annu,*" Wren said, and let the vowel bend, soften, the way a note bends when sung by someone who is crying. "*Vael-annu... sereth-kai.*"

The stone rang—but the ring wavered this time, caught, confused. The pulse beneath Wren's palms stuttered like a sleeper turning over.

"*Torath il-maren,*" Wren continued, and stumbled on purpose, and the stumble was a kind of music. "*Torath—torath il—*" They shook their head, laughed wetly, started the phrase over. "I always get this part wrong. *Torath il-sorath.* Arise. Please. If you want to."

And then Astrid joined in.

Their voice was wrecked—smoke-damaged, grief-cracked, twenty years rusted—and their accent in the First Tongue was atrocious, vowels flattened by two decades of silence, consonants blurred by blood loss and cold. But they spoke it anyway, half a beat behind Wren, echoing and bending and getting it wrong:

"*Vael-annu,*" Astrid rasped. "*Sereth-kai. Torath il-sorath... vael-annu... torath...*" They lost the thread, swore softly in Common, found it again. "*Torath il-sorath. Come back. Come back different.*"

And the language broke.

Wren felt it happen—a soundless snap, like ice releasing a trapped branch, like a chain whose weakest link finally gives. The absolute power of the First Tongue, the power that had silenced a god and hollowed a mercenary and built a valley out of forgetting, required perfection to bind—and two voices speaking it badly, lovingly, *humanly*, wove imperfection into every syllable. The waking-words became not a command but a plea. Not a key turning in a lock but hands knocking on a door. *Please. If you want to. Come back different.*

The stone split with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Light came up out of the ground—not the hard white glare of the Choir's torches but something warmer, older, dawn-colored, rising through the cracks in the black stone and through the snow and through Wren's own chest, where the voice inside them cried out—not in triumph, Wren realized, but in something like terror, like a prisoner hearing footsteps that might be rescue or might be judgment. The thirteenth moon flared. The spiraling stars stilled. And everywhere across the broken valley, in the burning houses and the fleeing ranks of the Choir and the frozen orchards, every living thing felt the same impossible sensation:

Someone enormous, opening their eyes.

"*Vael-annu,*" Wren whispered, tears freezing on their cheeks, still holding the stone, still holding Astrid's hand. "*Come back. Please come back different.*"

The light gathered. The presence rose. And the first thing the waking god did—the very first thing, before speaking, before judging, before anything—was pause.

As if asking permission.

Down the hill, the hymn collapsed into silence, and through that silence came the sound of Cantor Ilse screaming—not in victory, Wren thought. In something else entirely.

Then the light took them all.
