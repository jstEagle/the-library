## 18. Vael Wakes

### Astrid

The standing stone was warm.

That was the first wrongness. It had been cold for a generation—cold as the valley's forgetting, cold as the silence Astrid had paid for with everything they had ever been. Now heat pulsed up through the frost-rimed granite like blood through skin, and the snow around its base had melted into a ring of black, steaming earth. Wren stood inside that ring with their small hands pressed flat against the stone, and the words coming out of them were not quite the waking-words.

Astrid heard the difference immediately. Every syllable Wren spoke bent slightly—a vowel too open here, a consonant softened there, the way a child says *aminal* or *psghetti* and the world does not correct them because correction would be cruelty. The First Tongue was meant to be spoken as mountains speak it: absolute, uninflected, each word a law. Wren spoke it the way children speak everything—imperfectly, lovingly, with their whole crooked human mouth.

And the world was listening anyway.

Light came up out of the ground. Not the white surgical glare of Vael's old miracles—Astrid remembered that light, remembered how it had shown every flaw in every soul until flaws became sins and sins became fuel—but something warmer, amber and uneven, flickering like a hearth-fire learning what it was. It rose through the melted snow, through the roots of the dead pines, through the bones of the valley itself, and it gathered above the standing stone in a shape that could not decide what it wanted to be: a hand, a wing, a face half-remembered from a dream.

Astrid's knees hit the frozen mud. Not from reverence. From recognition.

*You,* said the presence—not in sound, not in the First Tongue's binding grammar, but in something older and gentler, a pressure against the mind like a forehead resting against a forehead. *I know you.*

"You don't," Astrid managed. Their voice came out shredded. "You knew who I was. That person is dying right now, word by word, because of what we did together. What I did."

The presence was silent for a long moment. In that silence Astrid felt it turning something over, the way Wren turned over smooth stones by the creek, looking at all sides before deciding what a thing was.

*I am being born,* it said at last, wonderingly. *Do you understand? I am not returning. I am arriving. And I find that I do not wish to command.*

"Then what do you wish?"

Another silence. Then, so softly Astrid almost missed it:

*May I?*

It was asking permission. A god, asking permission to exist in the world it had once ruled by decree.

Behind Astrid, down the ruined slope of the valley, the Choir's fires still burned and the screams of the fighting still carried on the wind—and yet here, in this ring of warm stone, there was only a child speaking broken holy words, and a god learning humility one mispronounced syllable at a time.

Astrid began to laugh, or weep, or both. They couldn't tell the difference anymore.

### Ilse

Cantor Ilse Veyra had waited thirty years for this moment, and when it came, it broke her.

She had climbed the last stretch of the valley on bleeding feet, past her dead scouts dissolving into mist, past the burning homesteads, past Maren Kestrel kneeling in the snow with her sword laid flat before her like an offering. Ilse had sung the Ascension Hymn under her breath the whole way—the true version, the version from before the Silence, the notation she'd spent three decades reconstructing from corrupted hymnals and half-burned manuscripts. She knew every note. She had taught those notes to two hundred faithful who were even now dying in the snow below.

She reached the standing stone in time to hear the vessel finish the waking-words.

Except the child did not finish them correctly.

Ilse heard it instantly—the flattened vowels, the swallowed final consonants, the tender childish lisp laid over language that was supposed to be the architecture of creation. Her first thought was *failure*. Her second thought was *heresy*. Her third thought, arriving unbidden and unwelcome, was of her daughter Liss, aged six, singing the hymns back to her with exactly that same sweet imperfection, before the famine year, before the godless winters, before—

The light took shape above the stone, and Ilse fell to her knees because her legs simply stopped agreeing to hold her.

It was not Vael.

She knew Vael. She had been a choirgirl in the last temple at Ashford; she had felt his presence settle over congregations like a yoke settling over oxen, magnificent and terrible, the certainty of it, the *rightness*, the blessed relief of never having to choose again. This presence in the amber light carried none of that weight. It moved the way smoke moves. It hesitated. When it touched the edges of her mind she felt it *pause*—felt it consider her, the way one considers a wounded bird, before speaking.

*You have carried me a long way,* it said to her. *Through hunger. Through grief. Through thirty years of ash.*

"I carried your *true* word," Ilse gasped. Blood ran from her nose; the proximity of divinity was crushing her like deep water. "They corrupted it, they let it rot, but I kept it, I kept every note—"

*You kept my name in a cage,* the presence said, not unkindly. *And now the cage is open, and look what flew out instead. Something new. Something that asks.*

"That's not—you're supposed to *lead* us." Her voice cracked down the middle. "You're supposed to make it *right*. My family starved while the world forgot you. My daughter—" The word wouldn't come. It never came. "If you had been there, if you had *spoken*—"

*I was silenced,* the presence said. *By people who loved the world more than they loved me. And they may have been right, little singer. Look at me now. I am made of a child's mistakes. I am built from mercy they taught me without knowing it. If I had returned as I was, I would have demanded what I always demanded.* A pause, vast and gentle. *Everything.*

"No." Ilse shook her head, and tears froze on her cheeks. "No, you're broken, this is wrong, the vessel pronounced it wrong, if we say it again, properly, the true notation—"

*Would you like me to show you what I was?*

And he did—or she, or it, the grammar of gods being beyond her—instant, total, merciless in its gentleness. Ilse saw the old Vael entire: the congregations kneeling until their knees deformed, the doubters made into pillars of salt-light, the abundance that flowed only to the obedient and the obedient who were never allowed to stop obeying. She saw her own faith as it would have been fulfilled: fed, clothed, safe, and hollowed out like a gourd, singing hymns forever in a paradise that was also a fist.

She saw Liss, grown old in that paradise, never once making a choice of her own.

Ilse Veyra screamed. It was not a battle cry. It was the sound of a woman watching the thing she had built her whole survival upon dissolve into something she could neither fight nor follow. She clawed at the frozen mud. She sang the Ascension Hymn, desperately, perfectly, every note true—and the presence listened politely, the way one listens to a beloved song from childhood, and did not come.

When the hymn ended, she knelt in the melting snow, empty-handed, thirty years gone, whispering: *"What am I supposed to do now?"*

And the new-made god, in its uncertain amber voice, answered: *I don't know. Perhaps we could find out together?*

That, somehow, was worse than any judgment. That, somehow, was the beginning of mercy.

### Maren

Maren Kestrel had made her peace with dying in this valley. She'd done the arithmetic on the march down from the pass: two hundred Choir faithful, herself, Astrid, and a child. The numbers didn't work. They never had. She'd fought beside Astrid Blackwood for eleven years and the numbers had never worked, and they'd survived anyway, mostly, except for all the ones who hadn't, and Maren had learned that survival wasn't arithmetic. It was stubbornness with a blade.

So when the light came up out of the standing stone, Maren was already kneeling—not in worship, just because her legs had finally filed their formal complaint—and she watched the god arrive like a traveler arriving late to their own funeral.

She felt the old Vael stir in her memory. She'd been a soldier in the last war; she remembered what divine command felt like, the iron taste of it, orders arriving in your skull with the authority of your own heartbeat. Every mercenary in the company had complained about it and every one of them had obeyed, because disobedience felt less like rebellion and more like tearing off your own arm.

This was nothing like that.

This was warmth on a cold morning. This was someone knocking at a door instead of breaking it down.

*Maren Kestrel,* the presence said, and her name in that voice sounded like her mother saying it, like Astrid saying it across a campfire, like every good memory of her name rolled into one. *You bled for my silence. You guarded the ones who sealed me away. You have been faithful to a god who was not there.*

"Somebody had to keep the watch," Maren muttered. Her eyes were wet and she didn't care who saw. "Even the empty posts."

*Will you serve me now?*

And here was the thing—the thing she'd carry to her grave—was that the god *waited*. Actually waited. Left the question hanging in the air between them like a held breath, with no compulsion in it at all, no iron, no heartbeat-authority. Just a question, asked by a power that could unmake her with a thought, genuinely wanting to know what she would choose.

Maren laughed, rough and cracked, and bowed her head all the way to the snow.

"Aye," she said. "Freely given. Ask me again tomorrow, though. I want to see if you still mean it."

*I will ask you every day,* the presence said, and she felt it smile, if a newborn god could be said to smile. *I find that I like asking.*

Behind her, the fighting was guttering out. Choir faithful dropping their weapons mid-swing as the presence touched them—not seizing them, just *introducing itself*, and finding, apparently, that most people set down their swords when introduced to something that asked rather than commanded. Some fought it. Some fled. One young cantor stood in the burning ruins of a hay barn and wept without sound, and Maren understood that one best of all: the grief of discovering you'd been ready to die for a lie, and then the stranger, harder grief of discovering it wasn't a lie after all—it was just smaller, and kinder, and newer than anything you'd been promised.

Maren got to her feet, retrieved her sword, and did not sheathe it. Old habits. Somewhere up the slope, at the standing stone, her oldest friend was paying a bill that had come due, and Maren intended to be standing nearby when the collector arrived.

### Wren

Wren was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

The voice was everywhere now. It wasn't inside their head anymore—that had been like holding a bird in cupped hands, wings fluttering against the fingers. Now the hands had opened, and the bird filled the sky, and the sky was warm, and Wren stood in the middle of it feeling very small and very proud, the way you feel when a cake comes out of the oven and everyone says it's wonderful and only you know about the eggshell in the batter.

Because Wren *knew* they'd said some of the words wrong. Astrid had taught them carefully, and Wren had practiced, but the First Tongue was hard—it was like trying to hold water in a net—and some of the sounds had come out soft and round instead of sharp and mountain-shaped. Wren had worried the whole time that the god wouldn't wake up at all, or worse, would wake up *crooked*.

But the god had woken up crooked, and the crookedness was the best part.

*Little bird,* the presence said, and its attention settled over Wren like a blanket pulled up to the chin. *You made me. Do you know that? Out of your imperfect mouth, you made me into something I could never have made myself.*

"I'm sorry about the hard words," Wren said. "Astrid says everybody gets them wrong the first hundred times."

*I am glad you got them wrong.* The warmth deepened. *Every mistake you made was a door where a wall used to be. Your people will be able to walk through those doors. Do you understand? Because you spoke me gently, I can be gentle. Because you spoke me small, I can fit inside small lives. You did not break the language, little bird. You broke its cruelty.*

Wren considered this with the seriousness it deserved.

"Astrid is crying," Wren reported. "Over there. By the rock. And Astrid keeps forgetting things—I can see it happening, the words are going out of them like breath on a window. Can you fix that?"

A long pause. The longest yet. In it, Wren felt the god weigh something enormous, and felt it find the scale tipped against fixing anything at all.

*No,* the presence said quietly. *That price was sworn before I woke, and even I must honor what love has already paid. But I can witness it. I can remember Astrid remembering, even after Astrid cannot. Would that help?*

Wren looked over at Astrid—on their knees in the mud, hands pressed to their temples, mouthing names of people Wren had never met, faces flickering and dissolving behind their eyes like fish surfacing and sinking. Maren was climbing the slope toward them. The fires below were dying down. Snow was falling again, soft and clean, putting out the burns the Choir had left on the valley.

"It might," Wren said. "But I think maybe it should be me. I'm going to write it all down. Every story. Astrid's going to tell me who they loved, and I'm going to keep it, so when the forgetting takes it, it won't really be gone. It'll just be... mine to give back."

The god was silent. Then, with something that felt astonishingly like awe:

*You are teaching *me*, little bird.*

"Everybody teaches everybody," Wren said, which was something Astrid had said once, and which Wren suspected was the truest sentence in any language, First or otherwise.

Below them, the last of the Choir's fires went out. Above them, the thirteenth moon completed its turn and began, imperceptibly, to wane. And at the base of the standing stone, Astrid Blackwood lifted their head and looked at Wren with an expression Wren had never seen before—the look of someone greeting a beloved stranger—and said, in a voice gone strange and light:

"I'm sorry. Have we met?"
