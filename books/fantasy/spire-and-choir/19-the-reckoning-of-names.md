## 19. The Reckoning of Names

The god's waking did not end the way a battle ends. There was no last cry, no falling silence. Instead there was a sound like a bell struck underwater—felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the old scar tissue of Astrid's left hand—and then the oath, broken and bleeding, came to collect.

Astrid knew it first as a smell. Woodsmoke and rosemary, the scent of a kitchen they could not place, and beneath it a name rising like a bubble through dark water: *Sera.* Their mother. They had not thought that name in twenty years, and now it arrived whole, with her chapped hands and her habit of humming when she kneaded bread, and Astrid stood in the trampled snow outside the standing stone and wept without understanding why.

"Mercenary." Maren's voice, hoarse from the hymn-smoke and the fighting. "Astrid. Look at me."

Astrid looked. And looking at Maren was suddenly unbearable, because behind her face stood another face—a young woman with a crooked braid and a laugh like a door swinging wide—and Astrid knew her name was also Maren, had always been Maren, and that for twenty years they had walked past this person every day in the valley and called her nothing at all.

"We lived here together," Astrid said. It was not a question. The words came out cracked down the middle.

"Eleven years." Maren's jaw worked. She was holding her sword arm against her ribs; the Choir's retreat from the stone circle had cost her something Astrid couldn't see yet. "You used to complain about my snoring. You said it sounded like a mill grinding stones."

"I don't remember."

"No," Maren said softly. "I know. I've known since the night you took me in and looked at me like a stranger asking directions to my own house. Ilse told us what you'd done, back before she went mad with wanting him back. Carried the whole company in your skull so the rest of us could live free of him. Gods, Astrid. All these years."

The bell-sound came again, deeper now, and with it the second name went.

It did not fade. That was the cruelty of it—it *left*, cleanly, the way a tooth leaves the gum: a moment of pressure, a wrongness, an absence shaped exactly like itself. Sera. The kitchen. The humming. Gone, and Astrid felt the hole where it had been, smooth-edged and permanent, and understood that they would spend the rest of their life running their tongue over that hollow.

*Everyone you ever loved,* the oath had said, in the First Tongue, in Wren's small imperfect voice as the god rose out of the stone like heat off summer rock. *The price is the remembering.*

They had said yes anyway. They would say yes again. But knowing a thing and living it were different countries, and Astrid was only now crossing the border.

* * *

They made camp in the ruins of the eastern farmhouse, because the valley was no longer a valley that forgot but a valley that had been remembered violently—the mist torn open along its southern seam, the Choir's dead scattered across the fields like dropped hymnals, the spire on the hill above Ashford still burning with the green fire Cantor Ilse had carried in her hands. Wren slept by the hearth, curled around a charred stick they refused to put down, because on it they had scratched the first letters of the first story Astrid had ever told them.

Astrid sat across the fire from Maren and began, deliberately, to remember.

It seemed the only decent thing to do. If the names were going to be taken, they would be taken witnessed. Astrid closed their eyes and pulled, the way you pull a splinter—fast, all at once, hating the hesitation more than the pain.

*Corvin.* The company's cook, who had once fought off three bandits with a soup ladle and then apologized to the ladle. His bald head shining in the rain at the siege of Durenholt. The way he sang off-key on purpose to make the younger soldiers laugh. Gone. A hollow where a man had been.

*Tamsin.* Who had taught Astrid to read the wind off the mountains. Whose grave Astrid had dug with their own hands outside a town whose name—

The town's name went too, taking Tamsin's face with it, and Astrid gasped aloud.

"You don't have to do it this way," Maren said. She had moved around the fire. Her hand hovered near Astrid's shoulder, not touching. Even now, after everything, she asked permission with her body. That, at least, Astrid remembered—not the eleven years, but the quality of her, the grain of her kindness, which apparently the oath did not count as a memory of love or else could not reach.

"Yes, I do." Astrid wiped their face with the heel of their hand. "If I let it take them one at a time, in the dark, whenever it wants—I'll spend the rest of my life being ambushed by ghosts. I'd rather walk out to meet them."

"That's the stupidest, bravest—" Maren stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. When she spoke again her voice was steady the way ice is steady. "Tell me about them. While you still have them. Tell me, and I'll keep them."

So Astrid told her. Corvin and his ladle. Tamsin and the wind. Old Hesk, who had carried a whetstone for thirty years until it was worn to a sliver he kept in a locket like a saint's fingerbone. The twins, Aldous and Aldith, who finished each other's sentences and each other's kills. Each name spoken aloud glowed briefly in Astrid's mind like a coal breathed upon—and then guttered, and went out, and the hollow remained.

Maren repeated every name back. She had a soldier's memory, iron-banded, and she said them like roll call, like a prayer, like a muster of the dead: *Corvin. Tamsin. Hesk. Aldous. Aldith.*

By the time the fire burned down to orange lace, Astrid could not have said who any of them were. They knew only that five people had been loved and lost twice—once to the war, once to the wage—and that the second loss was somehow worse, because the first at least left grief, and grief is a kind of keeping.

* * *

Near dawn, Wren woke and crawled into the space between Astrid and the fire without asking, the way children do, the way the god in them had never learned to do. Astrid felt the weight of the small body against their side and braced themselves, because there was one name left that mattered, and they knew whose it was.

"Wren," the child said, sleep-thick. "Say it. Maren says you're giving your names away. Don't give mine."

"I'm not giving anything away, little bird."

"You are." Wren's eyes were open now, dark and ancient and six years old, both at once. "I can feel it. Inside me, where he is. He says the debt isn't finished. He says—" The child's brow furrowed, listening to a voice Astrid could not hear, and their face went strange with sorrow too large for it. "He says he's sorry. He didn't know the price either. The old words, the perfect ones—they always take more than they say. That's why you broke them. That's why he's different now."

"Different how?" Maren asked from across the ashes.

Wren considered. "Before, when I heard him, it was like being held so tight you can't breathe. Now it's like—he knocks. He waits at the door." The child looked up at Astrid. "Like you did. When I knocked on yours."

Astrid's throat closed. Because yes—that was the shape of it. A knock in dead winter. A door opened against every instinct. And now the god who had commanded armies and devoured faiths stood knocking at the world's door, waiting, changed by a child's mispronunciations the way a river is changed by the stones it cannot move.

And the price of that change was this: Astrid's own heart, being emptied room by room.

"Wren," Astrid said. "Listen to me. Today, tomorrow, next year—there will come a morning when I look at you and don't know your name. It might already be close. I can feel it circling. When that happens, I need you to understand something." They took the child's small cold hands in theirs. "Not knowing your name will not be the same as not loving you. Do you hear me? The oath takes what I remember. It does not touch what I am. Whatever is left of me when it's finished—it will still be the thing that opened the door."

Wren nodded, solemn as a small judge. Then, practical: "Will you still like honey in your tea?"

Astrid laughed—a wet, broken sound. "I don't know. Ask me tomorrow."

"I'll ask you every day," Wren said. "Until you get tired of it."

* * *

The third name came at midmorning, and it was the worst, because Astrid saw it coming and could do nothing but stand in its path.

They were walking up toward the spire—because Ilse was up there, had been up there since the equinox dawn, singing alone among the green fire, and sooner or later someone would have to climb and answer her—but halfway up the hill Astrid stopped as if they'd hit glass.

There had been someone. Before the valley. After the company. Someone Astrid had loved not with a soldier's rough camaraderie but with the other thing, the soft terrible thing, the thing Astrid had sworn off twice and fallen into a third time anyway. A face surfaced: gray eyes, a scar through one eyebrow, hands good with horses. A name—

The name held. *Joss.* Joss, who had waited two winters. Joss, whom Astrid had promised to return to, and had returned to, and had left a third time because the silence-oath required a life with no anchors, no loves, nothing the god's remnant faithful could use as leverage. Joss, who had stood in the rain at some crossroads Astrid could no longer picture and said, *Go, then. But go knowing I'll be here,* and Astrid had gone, and had spent twenty years not knowing why the crossroads hurt.

"Joss," Astrid said aloud, tasting it, holding it on the tongue like the last sip of something rare.

And then it was gone.

Not faded—*taken*, the way the tax collector takes, the way the tide takes, with an impersonal completeness that left the shore smooth. Astrid stood on the hillside with the word still warm in their mouth and nothing behind it. No face. No scar. No rain. Only the knowledge that a person had existed who had been loved, and that Astrid had loved them, and that even the ache was already going soft at the edges, eroding into a general sadness with no address, a grief for no one, mourning with an empty urn.

"Astrid." Maren caught their elbow. "What was it?"

"Someone I loved." The sentence was true and hollow, a shell with the animal gone out of it. "Their name was—" Nothing came. Astrid laughed, and the laugh frightened them more than any scream would have. "An hour ago I could have drawn their face in the dirt. An hour ago I could have found their house blindfolded. Maren, write it down—you have to—"

"I don't know their name," Maren said gently. "I wasn't there. That part of your life, you kept even from us. From me." Something moved through her face—hurt, old and patient, the hurt of eleven years of being looked at like a stranger. "Whatever it was, whoever it was—you're the only book it was ever written in. And the book is burning."

They stood together on the hillside, the two of them, above the wounded valley, below the burning spire. Below them Wren was scratching letters into a plank with a knife, laborious and fierce, building the ark that would carry what the flood took. Above them Ilse's song wound thin and stubborn through the smoke.

"Maren," Astrid said slowly. "When it's finished—when there's nothing left—what will I be?"

Maren was quiet a long time. Wind moved the ash on the slope; somewhere downslope, survivors of the Choir were kneeling in the ruined barley, weeping or praying or both, because their god had woken and the world had not ended and they did not yet know what to do with a miracle that asked instead of commanded.

"The same thing you were the night I knocked on your door," Maren said at last. "Someone who opens it."

Astrid turned toward the spire. The green fire had changed color—cooler now, bluer, almost gentle—and through the smoke came a sound that was not singing anymore but *waiting*, a held breath at a door.

Ilse was up there with nothing left but her conviction and her grief, and grief with nowhere to go becomes a weapon, and Astrid had just watched twenty years of love dissolve into weather. They knew better than anyone alive what a person became when the ones they loved were taken and no one wrote the names down.

"Climb with me," Astrid said. "Before I forget why mercy matters."

Maren picked up her sword. Together they started up through the ash toward the spire, and behind them, faint on the wind, a child's voice was reading names aloud to the empty air—keeping them, keeping them, keeping them.
