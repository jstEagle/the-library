## 20. What Remains When Memory Fails

Wren found the first story in the smell of bread.

It was three days after the god woke—three days of the valley holding its breath, of mist thinning at the edges like old cloth, of Astrid moving through the ruined house with a stillness that wasn't peace—and Wren had asked for breakfast the way children ask for things, without ceremony, and Astrid had stood in front of the cold hearth for a long time before saying, *I don't remember how you like it.*

"You never made it for me," Wren said. "Maren did."

"I know." Astrid's voice was flat as a frozen pond. "I know that now because Maren told me this morning. But I don't remember it. I don't remember her bringing you in from the snow. I don't remember—" A pause, and then, quieter, the way a person speaks when they are reading their own face off a stranger: "I don't remember finding you."

The words went into Wren like splinters going in sideways. Because Wren remembered it. Wren remembered everything about that night—the storm with its teeth, the door opening a hand's width, the firelight falling across the floorboards in a long gold tongue, the way Astrid's shadow had leaned down over a half-frozen child and hesitated, and hesitated, and then hadn't.

*You remember,* said the voice inside Wren, soft as ash settling. *You can give it back.*

"No," Wren whispered, and Astrid looked up sharply, thinking the word was meant for them.

But it wasn't. It was meant for Vael, who lived now in the standing stone and the spring water and the space behind Wren's ribs, who woke changed but not powerless, who could pour memory back into a hollowed person the way water fills a cup. The god had offered once already, in that first hour after waking, when the whole valley rang like a struck bell: *Let me restore what was spent.* And Wren had felt how easy it would be. One word in the First Tongue. One perfect word.

And one perfect word would make Vael whole again too. That was the trap folded inside the kindness. Every flawless syllable fed the old shape of the god, the demanding one, the one whose order had been abundance for some and ash for others. Cantor Ilse was somewhere beyond the mistline even now, gathering what remained of the Choir around that hope.

So Wren had said no. And now Astrid stood in a kitchen they had lived in for years and could not remember a child's breakfast, and the no felt very small against all that emptiness.

"Sit down," Wren said, climbing onto the stool by the hearth. "I'll show you how Maren does it. She puts the fat in first, when the pan's too hot, so it spits at you. She says if it doesn't spit, it isn't honest cooking."

Astrid sat. Astrid watched Wren's small hands work, and something moved behind their eyes—not memory, nothing so kind, but the place where memory used to live, aching like a healed bone before rain.

"Tell me again," Astrid said. "About the night you came. You've told me twice since—the god woke, I mean. Since I forgot."

"Three times," Wren said. "This is the third."

"Then tell me the fourth time tomorrow, and the fifth the day after." Astrid's jaw tightened. "Until I can't forget it anymore than I already have."

And that was when Wren understood what they were going to do.

* * *

The book came from Maren's pack—a ledger, half-filled with supply tallies from a war nobody alive had won, its columns marching down pages yellowed to the color of old teeth. Maren surrendered it without argument when Wren explained, though her hands lingered on it a moment longer than they needed to.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. She'd taken to sitting near the window these days, where she could see the standing stone through the broken shutter slats. She didn't kneel to it. But she looked at it the way sailors look at weather. "You're offering to be a library for a person who is being emptied. Books burn, little bird. People lose books."

"People lose memories faster," Wren said. "Nobody has to carry those in a pack."

Maren laughed—one short, cracked note, surprise escaping before grief could catch it. Then she ruffled Wren's hair with a hand that smelled of pitch and old leather, and gave up the ledger, and found a stub of charcoal pencil besides, and said, "Start with the company. Start with the names. If we're going to build her back, we start with the foundation stones."

That first evening, Wren sat cross-legged on the floor beside Astrid's chair while the fire ate through a chair leg salvaged from the burned part of the house, and asked the first question.

"Tell me about the company."

Astrid's hands went still in their lap. Outside, the equinox moon was two nights gone past full, waning now, and its light came through the shutters in thin silver bars that lay across the floor like the rungs of a fallen ladder.

"There were forty-one of us," Astrid said slowly, feeling along each word like a woman walking a bridge she couldn't see the end of. "No. Forty-two. I'm the forty-second. I know that the way you know your own fingers. I can't see them, but I can count them."

"Say the names."

And Astrid said them. Some came easily—*Davor, who snored like a landslide; Sefa, who could gut a fish in four seconds and taught me dice; Old Bram, who wasn't old at all, just gray at twenty-five from the things he'd seen*—and some came hard, dragged up from depths where the oath's dissolution had left them half-dissolved, names that surfaced and slipped and had to be chased. When a name escaped entirely, Astrid's face did something terrible and quiet: not crying, exactly. The expression of someone watching a boat go out past the point where they can swim.

Wren wrote every name. Their letters were crooked, child-crooked, the *a*s too round and the *s*es like little snakes mid-strike, but they held. Charcoal on paper holds better than anything else in the world. Wren had decided this. Charcoal on paper does not need a god, does not need a perfect word, does not demand anything in return. It just stays.

When the last name was written—or the last name that came, which might not be the last there was—Astrid exhaled like a diver surfacing.

"Where did you learn to write?" they asked.

"Maren. In the valley. Before." Before the Choir, before the burning, before the stone and the waking. The valley had a school of sorts, two winters' worth of it, held in the millhouse. Wren remembered the millhouse. Someone had to.

"Good," Astrid said. "Keep writing. Everything I say. Even the stupid parts."

"Especially the stupid parts," Wren agreed solemnly, and was rewarded with the ghost of a smile—the first one in three days, thin as the crescent moon, but real.

* * *

The stories came in fragments, and Wren learned to fish for them.

Some rose on their own, unbidden. Astrid would be mending a strap or splitting wood—work chosen, Wren suspected, precisely because it required no remembering—and suddenly stop, staring at their own hands, and say, *Sefa showed me this stitch. On a wall in Corvane, in the rain, because my kit had torn and we were losing blood we couldn't spare.* And Wren would scramble for the ledger and write it down, date unknown, place Corvane, Sefa, stitching, rain.

Others had to be coaxed. Wren learned the coaxing the way they'd learned everything—by trust, by patience, by listening harder than seemed possible for such a small body. They learned that questions beginning with *do you remember* slammed doors, but questions beginning with *what was it like* opened windows. They learned that food unlocked more than anything: the taste of a certain smoked fish sent Astrid into a ten-minute recitation about a harbor town and a one-eyed cook and a bet involving an eel that Wren wrote down laughing until their sides hurt. They learned that Astrid's body remembered what Astrid's mind had lost—that the hands knew sword-drills, knew how to dress a wound, knew how to braid hair, and that the third one stopped Astrid cold the afternoon Wren's braid came loose and Astrid fixed it without thinking.

"Who taught me this?" Astrid whispered, fingers still in Wren's hair.

"You did," Wren said. "On me. Last autumn. You said I looked like a scarecrow and scarecrows deserve better."

Astrid's hand trembled. Wren wrote it down anyway, later, by firelight: *She braided my hair before she remembered braiding it. Her hands are older than her head. I think that means something. I think that means the forgetting doesn't get everything.*

Because that was the discovery, the one Wren guarded like a coal carried in cupped hands: the oath had taken Astrid's memories, but it had not taken Astrid. Underneath the emptied rooms, the walls still stood. Kindness was still there, reflexive as flinching. Stubbornness was there, vast and load-bearing. The particular dry humor that surfaced when Maren complained about her knee. The way Astrid always, always slept facing the door.

*You could have me restore it,* Vael murmured sometimes, in the hour before sleep, patient as erosion. *One word. I would ask nothing else. I only want to give.*

"That's how it starts," Wren told the dark. "Asking to give."

The god was silent then, and Wren chose to believe the silence was shame.

* * *

On the sixth day, Ilse's scouts were seen on the far ridge.

Maren brought the news in at dusk, breath steaming, cheeks scraped raw by wind. Three figures, maybe four, moving along the ridgeline where the mist no longer reached. Not entering. Watching. Counting.

"They know the mist is dying," Maren said. "They're waiting for it to finish."

"How long?" Astrid asked. All the softness of the storytelling hours was gone, packed away like winter clothes; the mercenary stood in its place, and Wren hated how familiar both faces were becoming.

"A week. Maybe less. The hole the scouts tore hasn't closed—it's widening. Whatever the god is doing now, whatever new shape it's taking, it can't hold the borders the way the silence-oath did." Maren's mouth twisted. "Freedom has a cost too. Who knew."

That night the stories went differently. Astrid sat by the fire with a whetstone and a blade that had drunk enough Choir blood in the last weeks to fill a hymnal, and instead of the harbor towns and the eel bets, the memories that surfaced were the hard ones—the ones Wren had privately been hoping would stay sunk.

"The night we sealed it," Astrid said abruptly. The whetstone had stopped moving. "I keep getting pieces. Dying men around a fire. Snow. Someone singing—Ilse? No. Someone before Ilse. A cantor with a burned face." The whetstone resumed, slower. "We weren't traitors. I need you to write that down, Wren. Whatever the Choir says, whatever the histories say—we weren't traitors. Vael demanded obedience the way a fire demands fuel. There was a village called Hallowmere. Ask Maren about Hallowmere someday, when you're older, if there is a someday when you're older. We ended it to save what was left of the world, and the price was—" Astrid gestured vaguely at their own head, at the beautiful empty rooms. "The price was me. And I volunteered. I remember volunteering. I remember raising my hand."

Wren's charcoal had stopped. Their eyes were burning.

"Why are you telling me the sad ones?" they managed. "I wanted the good ones. I wanted the eel bet."

"Because the sad ones are the ones that make you *you*." Astrid set down the blade and looked at Wren across the fire, and their eyes were wet and steady at once, which Wren hadn't known eyes could do. "Anyone can keep the funny stories. It takes a keeper to hold the wounds. And you—" Astrid's voice caught, restarted. "You're going to walk out of here soon, little bird. Into whatever comes next. And I may not know why I love you. But I know that I do, the way I know I have forty-two fingers' worth of a family. So I need the person carrying my life to carry all of it. Not the curated version. All of it."

*I love you,* said plain, without the First Tongue, without any binding at all—and that was the thing, Wren thought, scribbling fast so the tears wouldn't blur the page, that was the whole thing, the entire secret underneath everything: words spoken imperfectly still held. Maybe they held best of all. Perfect words bound you. Human words *chose* you.

"I'll keep all of it," Wren said. "Every wound. Every eel. I'll be your memory, Astrid. That's my vow." And then, because vows in this valley had weight, because they had all learned what oaths cost, Wren added carefully: "Not in the First Tongue. Just mine. Just a girl's vow. Is that allowed?"

Astrid smiled—the second smile, wider than the first, cracking through exhaustion like sun through storm cloud.

"It's the only kind that counts."

* * *

Later, when Astrid slept—facing the door, always facing the door—Wren sat by the embers and read back through the ledger by the last light of the banked fire. Forty-two names. The eel bet. The scarecrow braid. Hallowmere, marked with a small black star to remind future-Wren to ask Maren, and to brace first. Page after page of a life rebuilt in a child's crooked hand.

At the bottom of the newest page, Wren wrote one more line, not a story but a promise, pressing hard enough to score the paper beneath:

*Astrid Blackwood. Mercenary. Forty-second of forty-two. Volunteer. The hands are older than the head. If you find this book and she doesn't know her own name, read it to her until she does. Start with the eel. End with the braid. Don't skip Hallowmere.*

Outside, the wind shifted, and brought with it a sound that stopped Wren's charcoal mid-word.

Singing. Far off, on the ridge, thin as wire in the dark—but unmistakable. Voices in unison, rising and falling, and underneath the melody a structure Wren knew in her bones, the corrupted notation made flesh, the stolen language bent back toward its master. The Choir was not waiting for the mist to die.

The Choir was singing it dead.

Wren closed the ledger, hugged it to her chest, and went to wake Astrid.
