## 21. Spire and Choir

The spire had been a watchtower once, before the valley forgot itself. Now it was a broken tooth of black stone rising from the burned terraces below the standing stone, its upper half sheared away by some violence older than either of us, its stairs spiraling up through open air like the ribcage of something enormous that had died on its feet.

I climbed it because Ilse was at the top.

Maren had told me—had shouted it across the smoking ruins of the choir-yard, her voice raw from singing hymns she no longer believed in: *She's gone up the old tower, Astrid. She's waiting for you.* And then Maren had knelt in the ash, weeping, her hands pressed flat to the scorched earth where Vael's new presence moved beneath the ground like water under ice, asking nothing, offering everything, patient as spring.

I left her there. I had no comfort for her yet. My hands were still shaking from the fight, from the names.

That was the worst of it—the names. Every Choir soldier I had cut down on the stairs and in the yard and among the burning beehives had been someone I remembered now, someone restored to me by Wren's imperfect tongue and the god's imperfect waking. Brother Callum, who used to sing off-key on purpose to make the younger recruits laugh. Sister Odile, who had taught me to bind a wound with one hand while holding a sword in the other. They had come up the mountain to seize a child for their god, and I had met them with steel, and they had died knowing my face, calling me by a name I had only learned again three days ago.

*Astrid Blackwood.* It sat strangely in my mouth, like a borrowed coat.

The wind at the spire's base smelled of ash and melted snow and, faintly, impossibly, of green things—the first green things, pushing up through charred soil because Vael was awake now and even His smallest attention made the world lean toward growing. I put my hand against the cold stone of the first stair and began to climb.

* * *

The stairs were treacherous. Half of them were missing; the others held. Frost glittered in the shadowed turns, and where the wall had fallen away entirely there was nothing between me and the valley floor but a hundred feet of thin mountain air. Below, the mistline lay in tatters—a great pale wound in the landscape, unraveling slowly, the valley's long invisibility bleeding out of it like heat from an opened oven. Through the gaps I could see the lowlands: brown fields waking, a river running silver, the distant smoke of Ashford where the Choir had mustered.

They would come up the mountain in the morning. Not all of them—many had already knelt, when the god woke and did not demand but *asked*, when He asked *May I?* and waited for the answer. But some would come. Faith does not dissolve gently. Faith is a bone that sets crooked or breaks.

Ilse would come. Ilse had already come.

I found her at the top.

The spire's crown was a broken platform open to the sky, ringed with the stumps of crenellations, and she stood at its center with her back to me, facing the standing stone on the far hill. Her white cantor's robe was filthy—ash to the knee, a dark stain along one side where shrapnel or a blade had caught her—and her gray braid had come half undone, streaming in the wind like a banner for an army that no longer existed. In her hands she held a hymnal. The old kind. The corrupted notation, the First Tongue bent into shapes that commanded instead of asked.

She didn't turn when my boots scraped the stone. She said, "You took your time."

"I killed eleven people on the way up." My voice came out flat. "It slows a person."

"Twelve," she said. "You missed Brother Anselm. He crawled behind the well. He'll live, which is more than he wanted." Now she turned, and I saw her face properly for the first time since the equinox night, since the valley fell, since everything. It was not the face of a fanatic. That was the cruelty of it. It was the face of a tired woman in her fifties with crow's-feet and chapped lips and eyes that had watched something terrible happen a long time ago and had never once looked away.

"Ilse," I said.

"Don't." She raised one finger, and the gesture was so ordinary, so human—a schoolteacher hushing a room—that it hurt worse than any blade. "Don't say my name like you know me. You don't know me. You *erased* yourself rather than carry what we carried. You got thirty years of peace. I got every single day."

* * *

The wind gusted, carrying sparks from the burning choir-yard below, and for a moment the two of us stood in drifting orange light like figures in a festival procession, if festivals were held for funerals.

"You want to know what I saw," she said. It wasn't a question. She wasn't asking permission. That was the whole difference between her faith and what slept in the ground beneath us now, and I think somewhere in her she knew it. "I was eight years old when He went quiet. Do you understand what that means? No—you wouldn't. You were a soldier. You had a company, a wage, a purpose. I had a village that ran on His blessing. The granaries never rotted. The wells never failed. The winters were short because He *asked* them to be—no. That's wrong. That's the new Him talking. Back then He didn't ask. Back then He decreed, and the world obeyed, and we called it love because we'd never been given another word for it."

She opened the hymnal. Pages fluttered. Even from ten feet away I could feel the notation pulling at something behind my ribs—the old binding-script, the language of command, still hungry after all these years.

"When He went silent, the granaries rotted within a season. The wells silted. The winter came early and stayed nine months. My mother boiled leather for soup. My little brother—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it the way soldiers master pain, with practice and contempt. "He died in February. He was four. He weighed nothing. And the faithful came through the village saying *the god has withdrawn His face because of sin, because of oathbreakers,* and we believed them, because the alternative was believing we had been abandoned by something that had never loved us at all."

"Ilse—"

"Thirty years." She said it like a sentence being passed. "Thirty years of slow starvation across half the world, because a company of mercenaries decided they knew better than a god. Because one of them"—her eyes fixed on mine, and they were wet, and furious, and utterly unbroken—"volunteered to be the lock."

"Yes," I said.

She blinked. I think she had prepared for denial, for argument, for the whole long dance of justification. Plain agreement took the ground out from under her.

"Yes," I said again. "I volunteered. I swore the silence-oath at the standing stone with the company dying around me, and the price was everything I remembered, everyone I loved, sealed inside the vow so the seal would hold. And it held for thirty years, and children starved, and I tended bees in a valley that forgot me, and I would do it again." I stepped closer. She didn't step back. "Because you're right about what He was, Ilse. You've been right all along. Vael demanded. Vael decreed. Vael loved us the way a hand loves a tool—and when the company refused to be tools anymore, He would have burned the world down to the last child rather than let one soul go free. We didn't end paradise. There was no paradise. There was a cage with warm air in it, and we broke it open, and yes, the winter that followed was ours, and I will carry that until I die, which may be soon."

Her knuckles whitened on the hymnal. "Then give Him back."

"No."

"The waking-words exist. The vessel spoke them—I felt the world *tear*, Astrid, I felt Him pour back in—but He came back *wrong*. He came back small. He comes to people in dreams and *asks* them things. He waits. Gods don't wait." Her voice cracked wide open. "A god who asks is just weather. A god who asks can be refused. What do we have then? What holds the winter back? What do I tell the villages?"

And there it was. Underneath the doctrine, underneath the army and the hymnals and the burned valley—all of it, all of it—there was a woman standing on a broken tower asking *what protects us now*, in the voice of an eight-year-old girl watching her brother starve.

I knew that voice. I had heard it in my own chest for thirty years, in the valley, in the silence I had built around myself brick by brick. *What protects us now.*

* * *

Below us, faintly, bells. The Choir's retreat-bell, rung from the low camp, calling the survivors down off the mountain. Or perhaps it was the valley's own bell, cracked for generations, ringing for the first time because someone had climbed its tower and decided the dead deserved a funeral. Sound carries strangely on mountains. Grief does too.

"I can't give you Him back," I said. "Even if I wanted to—and part of me does, God help me, part of me misses being certain the way you miss a limb—even if I wanted to, it isn't mine to give anymore. He woke through Wren. Through a child who learned His language wrong on purpose, because I taught it wrong on purpose, because perfect obedience is the thing that made Him a tyrant in the first place. The flaw is the mercy, Ilse. The imperfection is the door."

"He's *weak* now."

"He's *humble* now. There's a difference, and the difference cost me everyone I ever loved, and it cost your brother his life, and it cost you thirty years, and I am not going to stand here and pretend those prices weren't real." My throat tightened. I thought of names dissolving—Wren had promised to hold them for me, had filled three notebooks already with my stumbling stories, and I clung to that promise the way a drowning man clings to a rope thrown by a child. "But I can offer you something else. If you want it."

Her laugh was bitter and brief. "Absolution?"

"A place." I gestured—not at the ruined valley, not at the burned yard, but downward, outward, at the whole waking world, the fields going green along the mistline's ragged edge, the river running, the roads opening for the first time in a generation. "He asks now. Which means He can be answered. Which means faith is a conversation instead of a chain. Come down off this tower and be one of the voices that answers. Teach them the true Tongue—not the corrupted notation, not the binding-script, the real thing, the asking-words. You know it better than anyone alive besides me and a child. Don't waste that knowledge guarding a cage that's already open."

"And if I refuse?" Her hand drifted toward the knife at her belt—an old ritual knife, crescent-bladed, meant for sacrifice. "If I decide a weak god is worse than none? If I decide you should pay for the winter, here, now, with that blade?"

"Then I'll stop you," I said quietly. "And it will break me, because you're the last piece of the old world I have left, and tomorrow I won't remember why you mattered." I held out my empty hands. "But I'm asking you. Listen to me. I'm *asking* you. That's the whole new religion, right there. That's all of it. Someone asking, and someone free to say no."

The wind screamed through the broken crenellations. The hymnal's pages tore loose one by one and went spinning down into the dark like white birds, like letters never sent, like snow falling upward. Ilse watched them go, and her face did something complicated—grief and rage and exhaustion and, buried deepest, a terrible relief, the look of a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long she'd forgotten it could be set down.

"My brother's name was Tam," she said suddenly. "Nobody's spoken it in thirty years. Everyone who remembered him is dead or scattered." She looked at me, and her eyes were dry now, and very clear. "Will your god ask me? Truly ask? Will He wait for my answer, even if the answer is no?"

"He waited for a child to refuse Him," I said. "He waited while the valley burned. I think He's gotten very good at waiting."

She stood a long moment at the edge of the broken tower, the last cantor of the old Choir, the hymnal bare in her hands. Then she closed it. Then she dropped it. It fell end over end, a small dark shape swallowed by the dark below, and the sound it made when it landed was almost nothing at all.

"Tam liked honey," she said, in a voice like a confession. "He used to steal it from the stores. Mother beat him for it and he kept doing it." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, roughly, like a soldier. "Teach me the asking-words, mercenary. And then take me to see this god of yours. And if He demands anything—if He demands *one single thing*—"

"He won't," I said. "But if He does, we walk away together. Both of us. That's the deal."

"That's a terrible deal."

"It's the only one on offer."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her—rusty, disused, startled out of her like a bird from cover. She turned from the tower's edge and faced the stairs, and the wind pushed at both of us like a hand urging us onward, and far below, in the black terraces, the first green shoots of Vael's patient spring were already breaking the ash.

We started down together.

And at the bottom, small against the vast dark, a lamp was coming up the mountain path toward us—one light, bobbing, unhurried, familiar. Wren. Awake past midnight, notebook under one arm, walking straight toward the two most dangerous people on the mountain without a flicker of fear, because Wren had never once in their life understood what fear was for.

"Wren shouldn't be out here," Ilse murmured beside me, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before—wonder, maybe, or its first cousin.

"No," I said, and my chest ached in a way I couldn't name, because I couldn't remember why the sight of that lamp made me want to weep, only that it did, only that the feeling remained after the reason had gone. "But they always are. Come on. There's someone I need you to meet—and someone who needs to hear your story before I lose the parts of mine that matter."

The lamp drew closer. The bell rang on, far below, for the dead and the living alike.
