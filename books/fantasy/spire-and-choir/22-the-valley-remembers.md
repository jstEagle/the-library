## 22. The Valley Remembers

**Astrid**

The mist went out like a candle in the first week of spring.

Astrid stood at the door of the cottage with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands and watched it happen. For twenty years the Valley of Hush had worn its fog like a shroud—thick at the borders, thinning toward the center, always breathing, always hiding. Now the sun came up over the eastern ridge and found nothing to burn away. The air was clean as rinsed glass. Astrid could see all the way to the far wall of the valley, to the black scar of the fallen spire, to the green coming up along the river where last autumn there had been only ash.

They did not know why they were watching. That was the truth of them now: they did not know most things. They knew how to hold a cup. They knew the weight of a spade leaning by the door. They knew that when the child came out onto the step beside them, something in their chest turned over like a key in an old lock—not opening anything, just remembering that it had once been a lock.

"Good morning," Wren said.

"Morning," Astrid said, because the child had taught them that this was what one said. The words still felt borrowed. Everything felt borrowed now—their own hands, their own name, which Wren had given back to them like a coat left behind at someone's house. *Your name is Astrid. You were a mercenary. You saved us.* The facts fit well enough. It was the space between the facts that ached, the way a missing tooth aches: a tongue returning again and again to the absence.

"The garden," Wren said, tugging their sleeve. "You said. Before you forgot again."

Had they? Probably. There were gaps now, hours sometimes whole days, that closed over like water. But the spade was by the door, and the seed packets were on the shelf where they'd been set three days ago or thirty, and the ground beyond the fence had been turned once already, dark and waiting.

"All right," Astrid said. "The garden."

They went down together into the morning, and the valley, which had spent a generation forgetting everything that entered it, lay open under the sun with nothing left to hide behind.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren kept the book in a wooden box under the bed, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, though there was no damp anymore either. The valley had changed its nature when the god woke—or woke *partway*, which was how Maren put it, waking being a thing that happened in degrees for gods as for people. The Hush was gone. What replaced it was quieter and stranger: a kind of attention. When Wren walked the river path they felt watched, but not hunted. Watched the way a garden is watched by the gardener.

The book was Wren's own idea, and it was the most important work in the world, though it looked like nothing. A ledger bought from a trader in Ashford. Ink made from soot and river water. On the first page, in careful letters learned from Maren's old campaign maps:

*This is who Astrid Blackwood was.*

Every night, Astrid told stories. Not willingly, at first—they had to be coaxed, the way one coaxes a fire from wet wood—but the telling had become a ritual neither of them named. Astrid would sit by the hearth with their hands around a cup and say things like *I think I had a horse I loved. Bay mare. Bad temper,* and Wren would write it down, and in the morning Astrid would have lost the story but Wren would still have it, and could give it back.

That was the bargain no one had spoken aloud but both of them understood: Astrid's memories were leaving, one by one, paid out to the broken oath like coins from a purse. Wren could not stop the paying. But Wren could keep the ledger.

Today, after the planting, Wren climbed the hill behind the cottage to the standing stone. The stone still bore the old marks—the channels where blood had run, the First Tongue characters cut deep by hands two decades dead. Vael's presence gathered here more thickly than anywhere else, not as voice exactly, but as a pressure like the moment before rain.

"Hello," Wren said to it. They always said hello. The god liked that; Wren was almost sure the god liked that. Under the new speaking, under Wren's imperfect human mouth, Vael had learned to be a thing that waited to be greeted instead of a thing that demanded greeting.

*The tall one plants today,* the presence said, in that way it had of putting thoughts into your head like stones into a stream, smooth and cool. *What does she plant?*

"She's not a she, exactly, and not a he, exactly," Wren said, because correcting the god was allowed now and Wren enjoyed it enormously. "Barley. Beans. Something called heartsease, for the border. Maren says it's good luck."

*And will the tall one remember the planting tomorrow?*

Wren looked down at the ledger under their arm. "No," they said. "But I will."

The presence was quiet a long moment. When it spoke again, its attention had softened into something Wren had no better word for than *sorrow*, though it was a sorrow without self-pity, the sorrow of a large thing that understood the size of what had been asked of a small one.

*Then teach me the stories too,* Vael said. *I was gone a generation. I have forgotten faces as well.*

So Wren sat down against the standing stone, in the sun, and opened the book, and began to read aloud to a god about a bay mare with a bad temper, and a company of mercenaries who had once been the finest in the world, and a person named Astrid who had carried the silence of heaven on their back for twenty years so that children could grow up unafraid.

* * *

**Astrid**

The seeds went into the earth in rows, and Astrid's hands knew the work even when the rest of them didn't. That was the strange mercy of it. Memory lived in the body longer than in the head. Their hands remembered how deep to press a bean, how far apart, how to pat the soil down firm without crushing. Their hands remembered the sword too—Astrid caught them drifting through a parry once, empty-handed, some ghost drill—and that memory they did not mourn.

Maren came up the path at midday, walking with the stick now, her hip ruined since the fall of the valley, though she'd never once complained where Astrid could hear it. She carried a basket: bread, hard cheese, a jar of last year's honey traded up from Ashford.

"You've got the rows straighter than the Choir ever marched," she said, settling herself on the fence rail.

"I don't know what the Choir ever marched like," Astrid said.

"No." Maren's face did the thing it did sometimes—a flinch folded inside a smile. "No, you don't. That's the point of it all, isn't it."

They ate bread and honey in the sun. Below them the valley lay green and open, and people were moving in it—refugees mostly, the ones who'd drifted back after the equinox, after word went out that the Hush was lifted and the god within it asked rather than commanded. Some had come because they'd heard the stories of the godless years and wanted nothing to do with any god, even a polite one. Some had come because Ilse's paradise-promise had starved them too, and they'd rather try a god that knocked before entering. A village was growing down by the river, tent by tent, wall by wall. Someone had already raised a shrine, small and plain, and left offerings of bread and cut flowers, and no priest presided over it because no one had thought to want one.

"Do you remember her?" Astrid asked. "Ilse?"

Maren was quiet long enough that Astrid thought she wouldn't answer.

"Some days I remember her face," Maren said finally. "Some days I remember the sound the spire made when it came down. Some days I can't make myself remember either one, and I don't know if that's mercy or cowardice." She looked sideways at Astrid. "You're asking because you're afraid of becoming her."

"I'm asking because I'm becoming nobody," Astrid said. "There's a difference. Hers was a choice. Mine was a price. But I wake up some mornings and I don't know what I'd die for, and a person who doesn't know that—" They stopped. Patted soil around a bean seed that didn't need patting. "The child keeps a book. Writes down everything I say. Reads it back to me like scripture."

"And?"

"And yesterday I listened to a story about myself and cried without knowing why, and then the crying stopped and I didn't know I'd done it until Wren wiped my face." Astrid sat back on their heels. "Is that living, Maren?"

Maren took her time. Down in the valley, smoke rose from cookfires, thin and domestic.

"When the company broke at Karst Fen," she said, "and we were crawling out through the reeds with half our number dead, do you know what kept us moving? Not hope. Not Vael—he'd already gone quiet by then. It was that someone knew our names. Every man who fell, someone left alive carried his name out. That was the whole religion we had left, and it was enough." She tapped the fence rail with her stick. "You've got a keeper, Astrid. Better than most dead men ever got. Live in it."

Astrid looked at their hands, dirt to the wrist, and tried to feel what Maren said was true. And maybe they did, a little. Or maybe the feeling would be gone by morning and Wren would write down *today Astrid seemed happy in the garden* and read it back later, and later-Astrid would receive the happiness secondhand, like an inheritance.

It would have to be enough. It was, astonishingly, beginning to be.

* * *

**Wren**

In the evening Wren read to Astrid by the fire, as always.

Tonight's page was an old one, from the first weeks, when Wren's handwriting had been fat and uncertain:

*Astrid Blackwood. Age unknown. Carries a sword they have not drawn in twenty years. Once swore an oath in the First Tongue that silenced the god Vael, to save a dying company, and paid for it with every memory of everyone they ever loved. They took in a stranger's child during a snowstorm when every wise instinct said turn it away. They are teaching me their language badly and my language worse. They are braver than anyone I have ever met, including the god.*

Astrid listened with their head tipped, the way they always did, hearing their own life like a tale of someone else.

"That person sounds tired," Astrid said.

"They were," Wren said. "They earned rest."

"Hm." Astrid turned their cup in their hands. Firelight moved across a face that Wren had drawn from every angle in the margins of forty pages, so that even if Wren's own memory ever failed, the lines would hold. "Did they love anyone? This person?"

Wren's throat tightened. They had written that page in winter, sitting outside the sickroom while Astrid bled from the ears after the last lesson, writing fast before the knowledge finished leaving. *Loved a woman named Sefa, who died at Karst Fen. Loved a boy called Tam, who ran away and lived, which was its own kind of grief. Loved their whole company, every rotten brave one of them. Loves Wren. This one is not written in the past tense.*

"Yes," Wren said. "Read the last line."

Astrid read it. Read it twice. Then looked up, and for a moment—Wren saw it happen, saw the present reach back and touch the past through the paper—something behind their eyes aligned, brief as a struck match.

"Wren," Astrid said. "I don't remember loving you. But I believe it completely. Isn't that a strange thing?"

"It's the truest thing I know," Wren said.

Astrid reached over and ruffled Wren's hair, rough and awkward, the gesture of someone doing it right for the first time in years. Outside, spring rain had begun, soft on the new-turned garden, soft on the valley that no longer needed to forget anything, because it had finally been given something worth remembering.

"Tomorrow," Astrid said, settling back, eyes closing, "tell me the one about the bay mare."

"Tomorrow," Wren promised, and opened the book to a fresh page, and wrote it down.
