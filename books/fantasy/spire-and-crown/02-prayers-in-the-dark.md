## 2. Prayers in the Dark

The rain found Kettlehollow before Emrys did.

It came down the mountain in grey sheets, filling the cart ruts, drowning the torches, turning the switchback trail into a river of mud and loose shale. He walked at the head of the caravan with his hood back because there was no point to it—by the time they'd crossed the last ridge, he was soaked through to the skin, and the cold had settled into his bones like an old tenant who no longer bothered paying rent.

Behind him, six wagons groaned under their loads of contraband silk, Crown-stamped brandy, and a widow's worth of unlicensed prayer-candles that some fool in Lowmark still manufactured despite three centuries of edicts. Behind the wagons walked thirty-one souls: smugglers, debt-fleeers, a family of tanners with soot ground so deep into their knuckles it looked like tattoo, two brothers who hadn't spoken since the elder lost the younger's inheritance at dice. Refugees, if you asked the Crown. Cargo, if you asked the caravan masters. People, if you asked Emrys, though he'd learned long ago not to ask out loud.

"Whitlock." Doss Fellhand came splashing up beside him, a barrel-chested man whose beard held more rain than his hat. "That patrol on the south ridge. You see them?"

"I saw them."

"They saw us?"

"No." Emrys had called the fog himself, three hours back—a thick, blind white fog that rolled up out of the valley floor like something poured, swallowing the road whole while the caravan slipped through its belly. It had cost him. It always cost him now, a pulling sensation behind his sternum, as though the storm reached into his chest and borrowed what it liked without asking. Three hundred years ago, they said, the old gods gave the mountain folk their weather freely. These days the sky took payment up front.

"You're sure?" Doss pressed. "Because I counted lanterns. Four. Maybe five."

"Fog doesn't leave witnesses, Doss." Emrys pulled his collar higher. "That's rather the point of fog."

Doss grunted, unconvinced, and dropped back to harangue the rear guard. Emrys let him go. The truth was he *wasn't* sure, and hadn't been sure for months—not about the patrols, not about the fog, not about the strange, half-remembered way the storms had begun answering him lately, quick and eager, like dogs hearing their names after years of silence. He told himself it was only that he knew these mountains better than the Crown's surveyors ever would. He told himself a great many things.

Kettlehollow announced itself by smell first: woodsmoke and wet peat and the sour-sweet reek of the brewing sheds tucked into the cliffside. Then the light—a smear of amber down in the fold of the mountains where the haven crouched beneath an overhang of granite, half village and half cave, built by people who had learned over generations that the safest place in Thornfell was the one the sky couldn't see. Lanterns hung from iron hooks along the lower galleries. Laundry lines sagged between doorposts. Somewhere a fiddle was being tuned badly, which meant someone was hopeful enough to dance tonight.

The caravan funneled down through the rock gate, and the haven's gatekeeper—a woman named Brack with forearms like ship's cable—waved them through with the weary familiarity of long habit.

"Thirty-one," she said, counting heads with her eyes. "You're carrying mouths this time, Whitlock, not just goods."

"The goods are mostly mouths," Emrys said. "They eat."

Brack snorted. "Beds are short. Hearth-hall's open. And keep your people quiet—the Crown's men were through last week asking after unlicensed worship. Burned a stack of prayer-scraps right in the square." She spat, eloquently, into the mud. "As if paper ever hurt anyone."

Emrys felt something tighten behind his ribs. "Inquisitors?"

"Just soldiers. The robed ones come later, when the burning's done." She waved him on. "Go dry off. You look like something the river refused."

* * *

The hearth-hall of Kettlehollow was a cavern of a room dug deep into the mountain's flank, its ceiling black with three centuries of smoke, its walls hung with everything the haven's people couldn't bear to throw away: antlers, boat-hooks from a lake none of them had ever sailed, a child's wooden horse worn smooth by hands long dead. Down the center ran the great hearth itself, a trench of fire forty feet long, always burning, tended in shifts by families too poor to keep private flames.

Emrys came in late, after seeing the tanners settled and arguing the brothers into sharing a bunk. The hall was loud with the particular noise of people who have been frightened all day deciding, collectively, to stop: bowls clinking, the fiddle finding its tune, laughter breaking out in patches like sun through cloud.

He'd just taken a bowl of barley stew from the serving woman—he never remembered her name, and she never seemed to mind—when the screaming started.

It came from the far end of the hearth, near the wall, where a cluster of people had gathered around a pallet laid close to the fire. Emrys set the bowl down without thinking about it and moved, the old reflex of a man who has spent years walking toward trouble because trouble rarely walks toward anyone else.

A boy lay on the pallet. Seven or eight years old, maybe, hollow-cheeked, his lips gone a terrible grey-blue. The wet-lung sickness; Emrys had heard it coughing through the caravan for two days, that deep wet rattle that filled small chests like water filling a boot. A woman knelt over him—his mother, by the desperate architecture of her body, curved around the child like a shield that had already failed. She was rocking, and her voice was coming apart.

"He won't drink. He won't—he can't—" She looked up at the faces around her, and Emrys saw the exact moment hope left her, the moment the eyes go somewhere past the crowd, past the hall, past the world, looking for anything at all that might be bigger than the thing killing her son. "Someone do something. *Please.* Someone—"

The haven's herbwoman pushed through, a stooped creature with stained fingers, and did what could be done, which was nothing much: lifted the boy's head, tried to trickle willow-bark tea between cracked lips. Most of it ran down his chin. The fiddle had stopped. The whole hall had gone quiet except for the fire and the boy's rattling breath, each one sounding a little less certain than the last.

And then the mother did the thing that Emrys would remember for the rest of his life, in whatever life he had left.

She turned to the hearth. To the long trench of flame that every soul in Kettlehollow had warmed themselves at, cooked at, been born beside—and she prayed. Not to the Crown's sanctioned god-of-record, the distant and bureaucratic Light Above, whose worship required licensed clergy and stamped tithes. Not even really in words. Her hands opened over the fire, empty, and what came out of her was older than words:

"*Brannoc. Brannoc, hearthfather, first-fire, coal-in-the-cold. They burned Your name but the name is in me. Take my warmth. Take mine and give him his. Please. Please. Please.*"

Around the hall, people flinched. An old man whispered "*don't*" like a warning against touching a hot stove. Saying that name aloud was a crime now—had been a crime for three hundred years, since the Treaty of Ashfold erased the old gods from every ledger and lintel in Thornfell. Emrys had seen prayer-scraps burned in market squares. He had seen worse done to the people caught holding them.

But the mother kept praying, her voice cracking down to a whisper, her hands shaking over the flames, and the boy's breath rattled slower, slower—

The fire answered.

Not all at once. First the nearest section of the hearth guttered, drew back, gathered itself. Then the flame changed color—from hearth-orange to a blue so deep and clean it looked cut from glacier ice, a blue that threw no heat but *light*, a blue that made every face in the hall look drowned, look carved, look like figures on an old coin. The blue crept along the trench of the fire, foot by foot, forty feet of flame turning color like a word being spoken slowly, and the sound it made was not the sound of fire at all. It was low. It was steady. It was, Emrys thought with the hair rising on his arms, almost like breathing.

The boy gasped.

Everyone heard it—that sudden, shocking inhale of a chest remembering its work. Color came back into his face in a slow tide. His mother made a sound that wasn't a word and wasn't a scream but lived in the country between them, and she gathered the child up and the child *clung* to her, awake, alive, his eyes wide and fixed on the blue fire with an expression no seven-year-old should have had to wear: recognition.

The hall erupted.

It was not joy, not at first. It was terror wearing joy's clothing—people shouting, falling back, falling forward, someone laughing and someone weeping and someone reciting the Litany of the Light Above in a shaking voice as though volume could undo what everyone had just seen. The old man who'd whispered *don't* dropped to his knees. A young woman began, hesitantly, half-remembered, to sing something—a few notes of a melody that made several of the older folk turn sharply, their faces going strange, as though hearing a voice they'd buried calling from under the floorboards.

"*It's real,*" someone said. "*The old ones are real.*"

"*It's a trick,*" someone else said. "*It's witchcraft, it's—it's a test, the Crown's watching—*"

"*That's Brannoc,* said a third voice, an ancient woman by the wall, and her voice carried the way stone carries weight. "*That's the hearthfather answering, first time in three hundred years, and you'll all be telling your grandchildren you were here.*"

Emrys stood very still in the middle of it.

The blue fire burned on, calm, unhurried, casting its impossible light across faces he'd eaten beside for years. And Emrys felt the two halves of himself pull apart: the part that was awestruck, that wanted to kneel, that felt something in the deep cellar of his heart stir and knock—an answering thing, a kinship, because he too had spent his life listening to weather that shouldn't have listened back. And the other part, colder, older, the part exile had built brick by brick: the part that knew what happened next. The Crown did not tolerate miracles it hadn't licensed. Soldiers had burned prayer-scraps in this square a week ago. When the robed ones came—and they would come, drawn by rumor the way wolves are drawn by blood—there would be interrogations, and fires, and lists of names.

The gods waking was not salvation. It was a lit fuse.

The mother looked up from her son and found Emrys's eyes across the crowd—maybe because he was closest, maybe because something in his face must have shown. Her expression was fierce and ruined and grateful all at once.

"You saw," she said. Not a question. "You saw what he did."

Emrys could have lied. He was good at lying; exile teaches the trade. But the blue light was on his face, and lying in that light felt suddenly, strangely impossible.

"I saw," he said.

"Then you'll tell them." She wasn't asking either. "When they ask what happened here tonight. You'll tell them true."

He thought of a courtroom eleven years ago. Of forty names read aloud in a voice like slate. Of a storm he swore he never called, and no one in that room who believed him, and the long grey years since, learning that truth was a currency nobody in power wanted to spend.

"Yes," Emrys said quietly. "I'll tell them true."

The blue fire burned until dawn. By morning it had faded back to ordinary orange, and the boy was sitting up demanding breakfast, and Kettlehollow had changed forever in a single night—because you cannot unsee a god, and you cannot unknow that the world you were promised was the whole world is only a wall with something vast standing patiently behind it.

Emrys climbed to the gallery above the gate as the sun came up, and stood looking south, down the long valleys toward the lowlands, toward the archives and assizes and inquisitors' towers where the Crown kept its version of history locked in iron cabinets.

Somewhere down there, he knew, word was already traveling. A blue flame in Kettlehollow. A dead god breathing. And sooner or later, the robed ones would follow the rumor up into the mountains—and among all the names they'd come asking after, Emrys Whitlock's would be near the top of the list, because it always was.

He should run. Every instinct honed by eleven years of exile said *run now, run far, change your name again.*

Instead he stood in the cold morning light, watching the smoke rise from a haven full of terrified, hopeful people, and found—to his considerable alarm—that he had no intention of leaving.

Below him, the gatekeeper Brack was already hauling the bar across the rock gate. Not to keep people out.

To keep watch. Word traveled fast in the mountains, and faster on the roads below. And Emrys had spent enough years reading the weather to know what kind was coming: the kind that arrived in black robes, with sealed writs and a wagon for burning things.

Three days, he guessed. Perhaps four.

He went down to warn them.
