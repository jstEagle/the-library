## 4. The Debt at Hollow Bridge

The river came down out of the Thornfells the color of old pewter, and where it met the lowlands it spread wide and slow and patient, as if it had all the time in the world to remember everything that had ever been thrown into it. The locals called that stretch of water the Hollow, though no one living could say why. Emrys had his suspicions. Rivers kept their own ledgers, and this one had been collecting debts since before the Crown drew its first border on a map.

He stood on the eastern bank in the grey hour before dawn, watching mist crawl off the water like something breathing, and waited for the ferry.

Maren Coalwater did not keep schedules. She kept moods. The ferry came when she judged the light right for crossing, when the tolls were worth her trouble, or—more often than anyone admitted—when she simply felt like it. The boat appeared out of the fog now as a dark suggestion, then a shape, then a fact: flat-bottomed, tar-black, poled by a broad-shouldered figure with a lantern hooked to the prow that burned with a steady amber flame.

Emrys raised one hand.

The pole stopped. Even across forty yards of water he could feel the moment she saw him—the stillness of it, the way the lantern flame leaned sideways as if the air itself had flinched. Then the pole bit deep, and the ferry swung toward him with the unhurried certainty of a thing that had never once been hurried by anything.

"You're supposed to be dead," Maren said, when the hull ground against the shingle. "Or in Vaelmoor. Or both."

"Good morning to you too."

"Don't 'good morning' me." She planted the pole and looked him over with her single eye, grey-green and sharp as river ice, the other lid sewn shut over an empty socket by some surgeon's rough thread twenty years gone. She was sixty if she was a day, built like the keel of her own boat, hair the color of wet ash bound back with tarred cord. "Three years, Emrys Whitlock. Three years you've crossed my water maybe six times, and each time looking like a man who'd rather be anywhere else. And now you come at first light, alone, wearing your guilt like a Sunday coat."

"Is it that obvious?"

"You've been obvious since the day I fished you out of a snowdrift half-dead with Crown hounds on your scent." She jerked her chin toward the far bank. "Get in. Whatever it is, you can tell me on the water. I think better with current under me."

He stepped aboard. The ferry rocked, settled, and Maren pushed off with a motion so practiced it barely counted as movement. The Hollow took them. Mist closed around the boat until the banks were rumors and there was only the black water, the amber lantern, and the soft rhythmic knock of the pole against wood.

* * *

"I heard about Kettlehollow," Maren said, after a long silence. "Blue fire at the hearth. Old Brannoc answering prayers." Her voice was carefully flat, the way water is flat just before it goes over a fall. "Inquisitors came through two days behind you. Burned prayer-scraps in the square. Took names."

"Took anyone?"

"Took Tam Arless's boy for asking why." She said it without inflection, which was how Emrys knew it cost her. "They're calling it unlicensed divinity now. There's paperwork for it. A man from the Assize office read the charges off a scroll while the scraps were burning, like he was reading a market list." Her jaw worked. "Three hundred years we let those gods sleep quiet, and nobody bothered us. Now they wake up, and suddenly waking them is a crime."

"The Crown doesn't fear the gods," Emrys said quietly. "It fears what people do when they stop being afraid of the Crown."

Maren snorted. "You always did talk like a book someone left out in the rain." She poled them past a drowned willow, its branches trailing in the current like a drowning woman's hair. Ahead, through a tear in the mist, the towers of ruined Vaelmoor showed grey against the brightening sky—broken spires rising out of the flood plain, some standing to their second stories above the marshy water, others marked only by the tips of bell towers breaking the surface like shark fins. The drowned city. Even at this distance, Emrys thought he could hear it: a faint, arrhythmic tolling carried on the wind, bells underwater ringing for no congregation.

"They're digging," Maren said.

He turned. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at Vaelmoor, and her hand on the pole had gone white-knuckled.

"What?"

"The labor gangs. Conscription writs went out three weeks ago through every village on the flood plain. Healthy men between sixteen and fifty. They're tearing the old quarter apart stone by stone, hauling it out on barges. My Jory got taken in the second levy." Her voice stayed level. Her eye did not. It had gone glass-bright and terrible. "Sixteen years old, Emrys. Sixteen, and they put a pick in his hands and pointed him at a temple wall."

The bottom dropped out of Emrys's stomach. He gripped the gunwale. "Jory. Jory's conscripted."

"Third barge-load out Tuesday last. I stood on this bank and watched them take him." She finally looked at him, and the full weight of her gaze landed like a stone. "His father died in the fever year. You know that. He's all I have, and I stood on this bank and watched a Crown wagon carry him off toward that—" she flicked her chin at the drowned towers "—and I couldn't do a single thing except not cry in front of him, because if his mother cries, the boy goes to pieces, and a boy who goes to pieces in a labor camp doesn't come home."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere out over the water, the drowned bells tolled again, fainter now, or perhaps Emrys was only imagining it, the way you imagine a name called in sleep.

"Maren." His throat was dry. "I owe you—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice giving way. "Don't you dare stand on my boat and recite debts at me. I know what I did. I hid a condemned man in my cellar for four months while the whole flood plain pretended not to notice. I fed you, lied for you, took the lash scars across my back from the magistrate's questions and never once said your name." She leaned on the pole, bringing the boat around, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "I didn't do it so you'd owe me, Emrys. I did it because you were freezing and bleeding and somebody had to. That's not debt. That's just what people do."

"It's debt to me."

"Then pay it." She said it simply, terribly simply, and the mist seemed to pull back from the boat as if making room. "Not to me. To him. Get my son out of that pit before the digging kills him, because I've seen what comes out of Vaelmoor on the supply barges, Emrys. Men who dig too deep down there come back wrong. Quiet. Staring at walls. One of them, Cobb Fennick, walked into the Hollow last month with his pockets full of stones from the temple foundation and just—kept walking. We found him downstream three days later." She swallowed. "The water didn't want him either. It pushed him back against the reeds like it couldn't bear the weight."

Emrys looked at the drowned city. The morning sun had found the highest tower now, gilding its broken crown, and beneath the gold the stone was black with wet and older darkness. Three centuries under the water. Three centuries of bells ringing for no one.

And beneath it all, he was suddenly certain—not reasoned, not deduced, but certain the way you are certain of a storm coming in your bones—the gods were waking because someone was pulling their house down around their ears.

"When does the next levy go out?" he asked.

Maren's eye narrowed. "Why?"

"Because I'm going to need to get into Vaelmoor, and I'd rather walk in with the work gangs than swim."

* * *

She tied up at a rotting pier half a mile downstream from the excavation site, in the shadow of a customs house whose roof had caved in decades ago. From there they walked the levee road together, Maren limping slightly on an old knee injury, Emrys with his hood up and his face angled away from the road. The conscription camp sprawled along the shore where the flood plain began: rows of canvas tents gone grey with mud-spatter, a palisade of sharpened stakes, watchtowers manned by soldiers whose armor bore neither the sigil of any border lord nor the crest of the local garrison, but the black sunburst of the Crown Inquisition itself.

At the center of the camp, cranes of green timber reached out over the water on raked timber legs, lowering great iron hooks into the flooded streets. Barges sat low in the water, stacked with dressed stone. Masons' marks glinted on the blocks—even from the levee, Emrys could see the old glyphs chiseled into them, worn but unmistakable. Temple stone. And not just any temple. The glyphs matched the ones he'd seen as a child, in the one book his mother had owned: drawings of the Spire, the great temple-tower of Vaelmoor, heart of the old faith, sunk with the city three hundred years ago when the rivers were turned.

They were dismantling the Spire. Block by block, glyph by glyph, hauling the house of god apart and carting it away to—who knew? The Chancellor's new palace? Some private collection? Or somewhere worse?

"There," Maren said.

She pointed with her chin toward a line of men coming up the ramp from the water's edge, hauling a sledge loaded with cut blocks. Mud to the waist, faces blank, movements synchronized as beasts under the overseers' whips—but Emrys picked him out instantly, the way you pick out your own reflection in a crowd. Jory Coalwater had his mother's build already, broad through the shoulder even at sixteen, and his father's stubborn cowlick that no amount of mud could flatten. He stumbled on the ramp. An overseer's whip cracked near his ear. He straightened, adjusted his grip, kept hauling.

"He looks strong," Emrys said, and hated how useless the words were.

"He looks alive today." Maren's voice was gravel. "Ask me again next week."

They watched from the lee of the customs house as the gang reached the top of the ramp and turned toward the stone-yards. Jory passed within thirty yards of them. Close enough that Emrys could see the raw rope-burns on the boy's palms, the hollows under his eyes, the way he moved like someone trying to take up as little space in the world as possible. Sixteen years old. He'd been eleven when Emrys came half-drowned to Maren's door, and he used to sit on the cellar stairs listening to the fugitive upstairs tell stories about the mountains, eyes huge, asking questions about storms and stars and whether the old gods really had faces.

*I hid you,* Maren had said. But it hadn't been only her. It had been the boy too, sneaking bread down the stairs, keeping watch from the window, lying to the magistrate's men with a child's flawless conviction that grown-ups believed whatever children told them. *Is the storm-man still there?* he'd asked the soldiers, solemn as scripture. *Haven't seen nobody.*

And now the Crown had him in a pit, tearing down a god's house with blistered hands.

Emrys became aware that Maren was watching him watch the boy, and that her expression had shifted into something he couldn't read.

"You're planning something stupid," she said.

"Probably."

"How stupid?"

"Stupid enough that I need maps of the flooded quarter, and a way in that isn't the main gate, and someone who knows the tides of the Hollow better than the Crown's own engineers." He paused. "And I'll need help getting Jory out. A body can't just vanish from a labor camp without someone noticing. Someone has to make him disappear onto the water."

Something flickered across Maren's face—hope, quickly strangled, the expression of a woman who had learned over sixty hard years that hope was a luxury taxed at ruinous rates.

"And where would I find such a mapmaker?" she asked, too casually.

"Not a mapmaker. Not yet." Emrys turned from the camp, from the cranes, from the boy hauling stone out of a god's drowned house. "There's a girl in Greywater Ford. Wren Ashvale. Her father charted Vaelmoor before the flooding—drew the streets, the temples, the cisterns, all of it, back when the Crown still pretended the city mattered. She inherited the plates. If anyone knows how to walk into a drowned city, it's her."

"Ashvale." Maren chewed on the name. "Old Ashvale went mad, they say. Drowned himself in the Hollow after the survey. Left a daughter and a trunk full of paper nobody wanted."

"Nobody wanted the paper. That's exactly why I need it." He started back down the levee, and Maren fell into step beside him, pole over her shoulder like a spear. "I leave tomorrow. But Maren—"

"I know."

"The camp. The overseers. If they suspect—"

"I've been hiding fools from the Crown for twenty years, Emrys Whitlock." For the first time since he'd boarded her ferry, she smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing, like lightning choosing to be kind. "One more won't break me. But hear me—" she stopped, and gripped his arm with a hand like a mooring cleat, and her single eye held his with the full weight of everything she had ever done for him and never once mentioned, "—you bring my boy back. Not his body. Him. Whatever it costs, whatever door you have to open, whatever god you have to wake—you bring him back breathing, or don't bother coming across my water again."

Overhead, the clouds had begun to gather, though the morning had dawned clear. Emrys felt the pressure change against his ears, felt the old familiar itch in his fingertips, the storm-sense stirring like a dog hearing its name—and for the first time in three years, he wondered, really wondered, whether the storm had ever truly been his at all.

Far out across the flood plain, faint and arrhythmic, the drowned bells rang.

And this time, he was almost sure, they rang twice.
