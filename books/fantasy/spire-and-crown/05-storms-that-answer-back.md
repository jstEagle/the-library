## 5. Storms That Answer Back

The shrine stood where no map admitted a shrine should be—a shelf of black stone jutting from the cliff face above the Kettle Ravine, three days' hard walk from Kettlehollow and half a day beyond anywhere honest people went. Emrys had known about it for eleven years and avoided it for ten of them. It was the kind of place that remembered you.

He climbed to it in the grey hour before dawn, when the ravine fog lay thick as wool and the only sound was water dripping somewhere far below, patient as a debt collector. The path was goat-narrow, slick with lichen, and he took it without rope because his hands had learned these mountains the way other men's hands learned their wives' faces. The stone under his boots was cold even in high summer. It was always cold here. The folk of Thornfell said the old shrines drank warmth the way wells drank rain.

The shrine itself was simple, which was how he knew it was old. New faith built spires and gilt; old faith built a flat stone, a shallow basin worn smooth by centuries of rain, and nothing else. No idol. No inscription. Whatever god had been honored here had been honored so long ago that even His name had weathered off the world.

Emrys set down his pack, unslung his oilskin coat, and stood at the edge of the shelf with the whole drowned-grey valley spread out beneath him.

"Right," he said to no one. His breath smoked. "Let's find out what I am."

* * *

He had not called weather since the trial. Eleven years, and in all that time he had touched the sky only the way any man touches it—by standing under it.

But the fog on the pass, two nights ago. He kept circling back to it the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. The Crown patrol had been thirty yards below him, torchlight climbing through the pines, and Maren's silk caravan—her livelihood, her son's conscription money, four families' winter—had been laid out on the trail like an offering. And Emrys had done what he always did in those first panicked seconds: reached for the weather the way a man reaches for a knife that isn't there anymore.

Except it had been there. Fog had come up out of the ravine like something exhaled, thick enough to drown torchlight, silent enough to make the patrol's horses scream. It had wrapped the caravan and lifted it away like a mother's hand. And Emrys had stood in the middle of it shaking, because he knew—he *knew*—that the storm-calling had gone out of him at the assizes. They had made very sure of that. There were rites for it, ugly ones, performed by inquisitors with iron needles and Latin words, and he had screamed through every one of them while forty dead people watched from the gallery of his memory.

So either the rites had failed, or the fog had never been his.

Both possibilities frightened him equally, and one of them frightened him more than exile ever had.

He knelt at the basin. Rainwater from some earlier night still pooled in it, black and mirror-still. In the old days—the days before, when he'd been young and stupid and proud of the lightning in his blood—you left an offering at a shrine before you asked anything of the sky. Bread. Salt. Blood, if you were desperate.

He had brought bread. It felt absurd, holding a heel of dark rye over a basin older than the kingdom that had exiled him. But absurdity had become his native country, so he broke the bread and let the pieces fall into the water, and said the only prayer he remembered from his grandmother, who had been born before the treaties, who had hummed songs in a language the Crown had since burned out of every book:

*"Old ones under the hill, old ones under the wave, lend your ear to a beggar who has nothing left to give."*

The basin did not ripple.

The air did.

It was subtle at first—the pressure change before weather, that taste of ozone and wet slate at the back of the throat. Emrys had spent his childhood reading the sky the way other children read letters, and every instinct he owned stood up at once and pointed south, toward the ravine mouth, where the fog was beginning to move.

Not drift. *Move.* Against the wind.

He got to his feet slowly. Below him, the grey blanket of mist was folding itself upward, drawing long ropes of itself out of the ravine like a thing rising from sleep, and the morning light coming through it turned strange and greenish, underwater-colored, and Emrys Whitlock—who had faced inquisitors, border reavers, a rockslide in the Greymantle Pass, and once, memorably, a bear—felt his knees go soft as a child's.

Because this was not weather. Weather did not have attention. This had attention, and all of it was on him.

"I didn't call you," he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Whatever you are—I didn't call you."

The fog reached the edge of the shelf and stopped, curling against the cliff face like a tide against a seawall, and it waited. That was the worst part. It *waited*, with the courtesy of something vast being careful not to break what it had come to look at.

Eleven years ago, Emrys would have answered that waiting with power. He'd have thrown lightning at it just to prove he could, because he had been twenty-two and the strongest storm-caller the Whitlock line had produced in six generations, and strength had seemed like a personality. Now he simply stood there, hands open at his sides, heart going like a drum in a procession, and said, "If you've come for me, you should know I don't have much worth taking."

The fog laughed.

There was no other word for it. A low rolling sound, thunder that hadn't decided to be thunder yet, moving through the mist in slow pulses—and underneath it, or inside it, or perhaps having always been inside it and only now choosing to be heard, a voice.

It did not use his ears. He understood that later, trying to describe it to himself. It used the same place in him the storms had always lived, the deep hollow behind the sternum where the magic had sat like a second heart, and it spoke there directly, in a register below language:

***WHITLOCK.***

His name, but older than his name. Spoken the way his grandmother had pronounced it, with the vowels rounded and heavy, a border-name, a name from a country that no longer appeared on maps.

"You know my family," Emrys managed.

***WE LENT YOUR FAMILY ITS WEATHER.***

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, each ring spreading outward through him. He shook his head—not denial, exactly. Reordering. Everything he thought he knew about himself shifting its weight.

"The storms," he said. "The calling. It was never—it wasn't in our blood?"

***YOUR BLOOD IS A DOOR. THE WEATHER WAS NEVER YOURS. IT WAS BORROWED. AND BORROWED THINGS ARE OWED.***

Emrys sat down. Not gracefully—he simply discovered his legs were no longer in the business of holding him, and the black stone was cold through his trousers, and he let it hold him instead. Forty people. Forty people had died in the storm they'd hanged his name on, and his whole life he had believed—half believed, in the hours when honesty crept in—that somewhere in him was the capacity for that. That the monster the inquisitors described was real, sleeping in his marrow, and exile was merely mercy with teeth.

And now a voice out of a drowned age was telling him the monster had never lived in him at all. The relief was so enormous it terrified him, because if the guilt had been false, then it had also been *useless*, and he had built eleven years of penance on top of it, and penance was load-bearing.

"Who owed?" he asked quietly. "Who borrowed? My grandfather? His father? How far back does the debt go?"

***FURTHER THAN YOUR CROWN. FURTHER THAN YOUR TREATY. THORNFELL STOOD BEFORE EITHER, AND ITS STORM-WARDENS WALKED ITS PASSES, AND THEY CARRIED WHAT WE GAVE THEM THE WAY A CUP CARRIES WATER—GLADLY, AND NOT AS OWNERS.***

The fog pressed closer. Not threateningly—or not only threateningly. There was something in its nearness like a dog leaning its weight against a leg. Like loneliness.

***WE HAVE BEEN SILENT THREE HUNDRED YEARS, DOORWARDEN.***

The word went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"Don't," he said. "Don't call me that."

***IT IS WHAT YOU ARE.***

"It's what I *was*. Maybe. If any of this is true." He was talking to the fog with his hands now, gesturing, the way he argued with Maren about tolls. "They cut it out of me. At the assizes. Iron needles and prayers, I felt them take it, I *screamed*—"

***THEY CUT THE ROPE, NOT THE WELL.*** Something almost like amusement moved through the mist. ***AND NOW THE WELL IS RISING. AND NOW DOORS THAT HAVE BEEN SHUT SINCE YOUR GRANDMOTHER'S GRANDMOTHER ARE BEING ASKED TO OPEN. SOME BY FRIENDS. SOME BY MEN WITH IRON NEEDLES OF THEIR OWN.***

"What doors?" But even as he asked it, he saw it—saw it the way you see a shape in smoke and can't unsee it: Vaelmoor, the drowned city, the Crown labor gangs Maren's son had been marched into, digging beneath the floodline. Digging *down*. "What's under Vaelmoor?"

The fog went very still. Stillness, from weather, is more frightening than violence.

***THAT IS WHY YOU ARE HERE,*** the voice said. ***NOT FOR THE SILK. NOT FOR THE SMUGGLING. YOU HAVE BEEN WALKING THIS BORDER FOR ELEVEN YEARS LIKE A MAN PACING A GRAVE, AND EVERY NIGHT YOU CALL FOG YOU SHOULD NOT BE ABLE TO CALL, AND EVERY TIME WE ANSWER, AND EVERY TIME YOU DO NOT LISTEN.***

"We?"

***WE WHO ARE LEFT.*** And beneath the words, for the first time, Emrys heard it clearly: the plural was thin. Not the voice of a pantheon but of survivors. Three voices where there had been hundreds. Two. Perhaps fewer. ***WE ARE FEW, DOORWARDEN. OUR TEMPLE IS BEING UNSTONED. OUR NAMES ARE BEING BURNED OUT OF THE WORLD AGAIN, AND THIS TIME THERE WILL BE NO ONE ALIVE WHO REMEMBERS US AT ALL. WE HAVE BEEN TESTING THE OLD LINES, THE OLD BLOOD, LOOKING FOR A DOOR THAT WILL STILL OPEN. MOST ARE SEALED. MOST ARE AFRAID.***

"And me?"

***YOU CALLED FOG OVER STRANGERS.*** Simple as that. As if it explained everything. ***YOU HAD NOTHING, AND YOU SPENT IT ON PEOPLE WHO COULD NEVER REPAY YOU. THAT IS THE ONLY QUALIFICATION THE OLD ONES EVER REQUIRED.***

Emrys looked at his hands. Calloused from rope and pack-straps, scarred across the knuckles from a reaver's knife outside Hollow Bridge, ordinary hands, smuggler's hands. For eleven years he had thought of them as the hands of a dangerous man kept leashed. It had never once occurred to him that they might be the hands of a servant whose master had finally come home.

"If I say yes," he said slowly, "what does it cost?"

The silence stretched so long he thought the presence had withdrawn. Then:

***YOU ASK THE RIGHT QUESTION FIRST. THAT IS WHY THE DOOR CHOSE YOU AND NOT THE OTHERS.***

"That's not an answer."

***NO,*** the voice agreed, and there was something terribly gentle in it. ***THE ANSWER IS THAT ALL PASSAGE HAS A TOLL, AND THE TOLL IS ALWAYS PAID BY THE DOOR. WHEN THE HOUR COMES, YOU WILL UNDERSTAND, AND YOU WILL PAY IT ANYWAY, AND WE WILL GRIEVE IT. THAT IS ALL WE MAY TELL YOU.***

"That's a terrible bargain."

***YES.***

"And if I refuse?"

***THEN WE WILL DIE QUIETLY, AND THE MEN UNDER VAELMOOR WILL DIG UP WHAT SHOULD SLEEP, AND YOUR MOUNTAIN PEOPLE WILL LEARN WHAT THE CHANCELLOR'S ARMY IS FED ON. AND YOU WILL GO BACK TO HAULING SILK UNTIL THEY COME FOR KETTLEHOLLOW TOO, WHICH THEY WILL.*** A pause. ***WE DO NOT COMMAND YOU, EMRYS WHITLOCK. NO GOD OF THE OLD COUNTRY EVER COMMANDED ITS DOORS. WE ONLY ASK.***

The fog hung there, patient, immense, and Emrys sat on the cold stone of a shrine to gods whose names had been murdered, and felt the full weight of being asked.

He thought of Maren Coalwater's one eye fixed on him across the haven fire, saying *you eat at my table, you're mine to worry about.* He thought of her son Tam, sixteen and all elbows, marching in a Crown chain-gang toward a hole in the world. He thought of the dying child in Kettlehollow, and the hearth flame burning blue, and the mother's face lit by it—terror and hope in equal measure, the exact expression Emrys wore inside where no one could see.

He had spent eleven years believing his magic made him monstrous. It had been, in its awful way, a comfort. Monsters were simple. Monsters were owed nothing and owed nothing. But this—this voice calling him *doorwarden*, this ancient loneliness asking rather than commanding—this meant he had never been a monster at all. Just a man. A man with a choice, which was infinitely heavier.

"I want terms," he said at last. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "I want the boy out of the ground. I want Kettlehollow left alone. I want—" He stopped, because the third thing was too large and too small at once. "I want to know it wasn't wasted. The eleven years. Tell me that much."

***WALK SOUTH TOMORROW,*** the voice said, which was not an answer, or was the only kind the old ones gave. ***THERE IS A GIRL IN MILLBROOK WHO DRAWS MAPS OF A CITY SHE HAS NEVER SEEN, FROM HER DEAD FATHER'S CHARTS. SHE WILL TELL YOU SHE IS TRADING PASSAGE FOR PROTECTION. BELIEVE HER. BUT KNOW THAT WE SENT HER FATHER TO VAELMOOR LONG AGO, AND KNOW THAT HIS MAPS ARE THE ONLY ONES THAT SHOW THE DOOR.***

"You've been arranging us. Like pieces."

***WE HAVE BEEN PRAYING,*** said the voice, ***TO WHOEVER WAS LEFT TO LISTEN. APPARENTLY IT WAS EACH OTHER.***

Then the fog sighed out of existence all at once, the way snow slides off a roof, and the morning came in ordinary and pale, and Emrys was alone on the shelf of black stone with a basin of rainwater and a heel of bread gone soggy, and the whole sky empty and blue and utterly indifferent, the way skies had always been, the way he had believed they would always be.

He stayed until the sun cleared the eastern ridge. Then he packed up his things, climbed down the goat-path with legs that shook on every switchback, and stood at the bottom looking south toward Millbrook, where a mapmaker's daughter was waiting, though she didn't know it yet.

*Doorwarden.*

He said the word aloud once, testing it, and hated how it fit.

Then he turned south and started walking, because whatever else the voice had lied about, it hadn't lied about the men under Vaelmoor—and Maren's son was one of them.
