## 6. The Cartographer's Daughter

Wren Ashvale knew the man in her father's workshop was trouble the moment he stepped over the threshold, because trouble always came in wet from the rain, and this one came in dry.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was that he'd knocked. Nobody knocked at the Ashvale mapshop in Lowmark's tanner's row — they shoved, they bargained, they left coin on the counter and took what they wanted and called it commerce. But this man stood in the doorway with the rain streaming off his oilskin behind him like a curtain he refused to cross, and he rapped twice on the frame with one knuckle, patient as a doctor, and waited to be seen.

"I'm closed," Wren said, not looking up from the chart she was correcting. A river had moved two miles east since her father drew it, and rivers were liars, but ink was honest, and she intended to keep it so.

"The sign says otherwise."

"The sign is my father's. My father is dead. The sign doesn't know yet." She let that sit, then glanced up despite herself. He was younger than the stories made him — or older; it was hard to tell with a face like weathered rope, all creases and no give. Grey eyes. The kind of tired that didn't come from sleeplessness but from years of carrying something heavy up and down mountains. "You're him, aren't you."

"Depends which story you've heard."

"The one where Emrys Whitlock calls fog out of a clear morning and walks forty head of mules through a Crown checkpoint while the soldiers stand around scratching their arses."

Something flickered across his face — not pride. Closer to embarrassment, the way a man looks when praised for a trick he can't remember performing. "I've heard that one too," he said. "It grows in the telling."

"So does mold. Both are real enough." She set down her pen. "If you're here to buy maps, buy them. If you're here for anything else, the answer's no before you ask it."

He smiled, and it was a small, crooked thing, more apology than warmth. "I haven't even told you what I want."

"You're a smuggler. You want passage, or silence, or both. And I'm a mapmaker's daughter alone in a shop full of charts worth more than this whole street. Do the arithmetic yourself."

For a moment he just looked at her, and she felt it like a lamp being turned up — that particular attention some people had, the kind that made you feel counted. Then he said, quietly: "Your father was Aldous Ashvale."

Not a question. Her spine went cold anyway. "Everyone knows that. There's a plaque."

"There's a plaque in the Guildhall that says he was censured for falsifying coastal surveys. That's what everyone knows." He stepped inside at last, and the rain followed him only as far as the mat, as if even the weather respected his manners. "What I know is that Aldous Ashvale charted Vaelmoor. Street by street, cellar by cellar, eleven years before the Crown drowned it. Every arch, every cistern, every door. And when the water came up, those charts became the most dangerous documents in Thornfell — so dangerous the Crown bought them all. Except they didn't get all of them, did they. Because cartographers are like smugglers. We keep copies."

The shop was very quiet. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere down the row, a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

"My father didn't keep copies," Wren said. "That was the whole point. He gave them up. All of them. He signed the surrender himself, and after that he—" She stopped. After that he drank, mostly, and drew coastlines that weren't there, and died with his cheek on a drafting table while she slept in the next room pretending not to hear him weep about a city he'd loved better than his family.

"He kept one," Emrys said gently. "I know because I've spent three months asking questions in towns where asking questions gets you buried, and every road leads back to this shop. I'm not here to rob you, Wren Ashvale. I'm here to make you an offer."

"We're closed."

"You said that already." He unslung his pack and set it on the counter between them, and began, unhurried, to lay things out like a merchant displaying wares. A strip of dried venison. A lump of beeswax candle. A small tin of salt. "Safe passage south," he said. "Out of Thornfell entirely. Past the checkpoints, past the tolls, past the inquisitors who've started pulling people off the roads for having the wrong thoughts. I can put you in Callowmere by month's end, alive and unmolested, with enough coin to set up wherever you like. In exchange—"

"You want the Vaelmoor charts."

"I want *you*," he said, "with the charts. There's a difference, and it matters."

She stared at him. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the shingles like impatient fingers. "Why would a smuggler care about a drowned city? What's under Vaelmoor that's worth walking into a grave for?"

And there it was again — that flicker. This time she caught it properly, and it stopped her breath for half a second, because it wasn't greed and it wasn't fear. It was grief. Fresh grief, days old, worn raw.

"Someone I owe a debt to has a son in the labor gangs there," he said. "Digging. For the Crown. And whatever they're digging for, they're finding it, and people are dying of it, and nobody above the waterline seems to care." He met her eyes. "I'm going to get them out. To do that I need to move through streets that exist only in your father's memory. So. Passage south for your maps and your knowledge. That's the offer. Take it or leave it, and either way I'll leave you in peace tonight."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the back room with her father's strongbox open on her knees and the key — hidden for six years inside the hollow leg of his drafting stool, the one place she'd never looked until tonight because looking would have meant hoping — warm in her palm.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay three sheets of vellum and a leather folio thick as a psalter. The vellum sheets were Vaelmoor: the Lower City in section, showing the vaulted cellars beneath the Merchant Quarter; the temple district with its foundations drawn in her father's small, furious hand, annotations crawling up the margins like ivy — *older than the treaty, older than the Crown, ask Brannoc's priest about the deep stair, DO NOT trust the masons' marks*; and a third sheet, unfinished, edges burnt, showing a structure beneath the temple that her father had rendered in careful hatching and labeled with a single word: *GATE?*

The folio held everything else. Forty years of survey notes. Water tables. Tide calculations for a city that shouldn't have had tides. And letters — dozens of them — between her father and someone signed only *M.C.*, arguing in increasingly frantic terms about *the bells*, about *what the masons found under the third chapel*, about a final letter, dated nine days before the flooding, that read in its entirety:

*Aldous — They are not draining the basin to build the reservoir. Get your daughter and go south. Whatever they promised you, they are lying. The water is not the weapon. The water is the lid.*

Wren sat with that letter until the candle guttered, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that her father hadn't died of drink. He'd died of knowing. And she had spent six years being ashamed of a man who had been, all along, the bravest coward in Thornfell.

When the grey light came, she opened the shop.

Emrys Whitlock was sitting on her step, awake, as if he'd never moved. His cloak was damp with dew. He looked up at her and she saw — properly saw, now, in flat morning light without the drama of lamplight and rain — that he was not the storm-caller of the songs. The songs had a man carved out of thunder. This was a man with chapped hands and a healing cut on his jaw and shadows under his eyes deep enough to lose coins in. A tired man. A frightened one, maybe, though he wore the fear the way her father had worn his sobriety: carefully, constantly, one hour at a time.

"Yes," she said.

He blinked. "Yes?"

"To the offer. But I have conditions." She held up fingers. "One: the charts come back to me afterward. All of them. Two: if anyone dies down there, I draw where they fell, and their name goes on the sheet, and you carry that sheet out if I don't. Three—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway, "—three: you tell me why a smuggler with no stake in any of this is willing to drown for a ferrywoman's son. And don't lie to me. I can smell it. My father lied professionally for the last six years of his life and I got very good at the smell."

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, startled, rusty, like a hinge that hadn't turned in years — and rubbed a hand over his face.

"Because she hid me," he said. "Six years ago, when every road out of Thornfell had my name on it, Maren Coalwater pulled me out of the river half-drowned and put me in her cellar and lied to inquisitors with a straight face while they searched her house. She had a boy asleep upstairs. She risked him for me. She never asked me for anything until three weeks ago, and what she asked for was impossible, and I said yes before she finished the sentence." He shrugged, and the shrug carried weight, like a man setting down a load to show how heavy it was. "That's the whole ledger. One life owed. Everything else is interest."

Wren studied him. The legend said *storm-witch, exile, kinslayer of the flood-year*. The man on her step said *someone's debtor, someone's friend, afraid of doing the right thing and doing it anyway*. She thought of her father's last letter, hidden in a stool's hollow leg for six years while she polished the plaque that called him a fraud.

"All right," she said, and went inside, and came back with the folio and the three sheets wrapped in oilcloth, and pressed them into his hands. "But I carry them. You'll lose them. Smugglers lose things. It's practically liturgical."

* * *

They left Lowmark before noon, ahead of the market crowds, taking the drover's lane north along the river. Wren had expected — she admitted it to herself only later — the legend. Fog rising at a word, storms leashed like hounds, the whole theater of it. Instead she got a traveling companion who walked at mule-pace, knew which berries were safe by taste rather than book, and talked almost not at all except to point out where the path had washed and where the old border-stone stood half-swallowed by a hedgerow.

"That's a Thornfell stone," he said, stopping once, resting his hand on the mossy granite like greeting a horse. "Before the Treaty. There used to be one every league. The Crown pulled most of them."

"Why leave this one?"

"Too far off the road to matter." He brushed the moss away from the carved mark — a tower split by a lightning-bolt, worn nearly smooth. "Or too stubborn. Stones usually are."

By the third day she'd catalogued him the way she catalogued terrain: the long silences weren't brooding, they were listening — he listened to weather the way she read contour lines, constantly, automatically. He flinched, just slightly, whenever she hummed. When she asked why, he said, "My mother used to sing that one," and changed the subject with the grace of a man stepping over a corpse.

On the fourth night, camped in a shepherd's bothy with rain hammering the turf roof, she caught him studying her father's unfinished sheet by candlelight — the one marked *GATE?* — with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person reading their own death warrant and checking the handwriting for errors.

"It's real, isn't it," she said. "Whatever my father found. You know what it is."

He didn't startle. That was the thing about him; nothing startled him, as if surprise had been burned out of him years ago along with everything else. "I think it's a door," he said. "And I think the Crown has been trying to break it open for eleven years, and I think that's why Vaelmoor is underwater, and I think—" he paused, and the candleflame leaned in the draft, and for one uncanny moment the rain on the roof went silent, all of it, everywhere, like the world holding its breath — "I think the door has been waiting for someone specific, and I'm terrified it's me."

The rain resumed. Wren realized she'd been holding her pen — she always carried one, even to bed, even now — and that she'd been sketching him without noticing: the tired eyes, the chapped hands, the weight of the shoulders. Not the legend. Just the man.

"That's the first honest thing you've said," she told him.

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late. I'm a cartographer. Accuracy is a disease." She took the sheet from him gently and rolled it closed. "Get some sleep, Whitlock. Tomorrow we cross the fells, and if my father's tide-tables are right, the water in Vaelmoor moves whether there's a sea there or not. Which means somebody — or something — is still breathing down there."

Emrys looked at her, and for the first time since the shop, the tiredness in his face shifted into something else. Not hope, exactly. Something smaller and harder than hope. The look of a man who has decided, against all evidence, to trust the person holding the map.

"Then we'd better be quick," he said.

Two mornings later, cresting the pass above the drowned valley, Wren reined in and forgot how to breathe.

Vaelmoor lay below them in the grey dawn light — rooftops breaking the floodwater like ribs, spires leaning, and across the still surface of the drowned city, faint and impossible and unmistakable, came the sound of bells ringing beneath the water.

Her father's letters had mentioned the bells. She'd thought it poetry.

Beside her, Emrys Whitlock had gone white as the mist, and his hand, when it closed on her shoulder, was shaking.

"They're counting," he whispered. "Wren. They're counting down."
