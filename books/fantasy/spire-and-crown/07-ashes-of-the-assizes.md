## 7. Ashes of the Assizes

The dream always began the same way: with rain.

Not the gentle rain of Kettlehollow's eaves, not the mountain rain that smelled of pine and cold stone, but a rain like judgment—vertical, relentless, falling on the assize-hall at Corvenmoor as if the sky itself had come to watch. Emrys Whitlock stood in the dock of the great hall and listened to it hammer the slate roof above him, forty thousand drops a second, each one a small accusation.

Seven years ago now. Seven years, and still his body remembered every detail as though it had happened that morning: the smell of wet wool and tallow smoke, the creak of the dock timbers under his boots, the way the chains on his wrists had gone warm from his own feverish skin. The hall was built to hold eight hundred souls and held nearly twice that, packed onto benches built for prayer, and every one of them had come to see the Stormcaller of Thornfell unmasked.

He had wanted, desperately, to be unmasked. That was the cruelty of it. He had stood in that dock believing that truth was a door, and that all he had to do was walk through it and the whole terrible machine would have to let him go.

He had been twenty-three years old, and he had believed in doors.

* * *

The Lord Assizer, Magistrate Aldous Crane, was a small man made large by robes—a grey falcon embroidered across his chest, wings spread over a crown. His voice was dry paper. "Emrys Whitlock. You are called before this assize to answer for the deaths of forty souls at Millbrook Crossing, on the night of the Harvest Moon, in the year the Crown counts as 312."

"Three hundred and twelve years since the Treaty of Ashes," Crane said, and let the number hang there, because everyone in that hall knew what it meant: three hundred and twelve years since the old gods went silent, since Thornfell stopped being a country and became a borderland, since magic became something licensed, taxed, and feared.

"I did not call that storm," Emrys said.

His voice cracked on the second word. He remembered hating himself for it—the crack, the boyishness of it—and he hated himself now, seven years later, in the amber of the dream, all over again.

Crane did not look up from his ledger. "You are a registered weather-worker. Third circle. Licensed by the Crown Office of Works."

"I am."

"Your license permits the calling of rain for agricultural purposes. Irrigation. Hail-turning. Fog-lifting at the request of a magistrate." Crane turned a page. "It does not permit storm-calling. It does not permit the raising of winds sufficient to fell trees, flood a river valley, and drown forty men, women, and children in their beds."

"I know what my license permits," Emrys said. "I've read it more times than I've slept."

A ripple through the benches—not laughter, exactly. Something colder. He had learned since to name that sound: it was the sound people make when they want to believe the worst and are grateful to be given permission.

"The Crown's evidence," Crane continued, "will show that on the night in question, weather-signatures matching your registered pattern were recorded at the crossing by the Crown survey-station at Harrow Fell."

"That's impossible," Emrys said. "I was in Corvenmoor that night. I signed the works-ledger at the ninth bell. Ask the clerk. Ask anyone."

"We will hear the Crown's evidence," Crane said, in the tone of a man closing a window against weather, "in its own order."

* * *

They heard it in its own order, and its order was a masterpiece.

First came the survey-clerk from Harrow Fell, a nervous young man with ink-stained fingers who testified that the resonance-patterns scraped from the storm-glass that night matched, within tolerance, the pattern registered to Emrys Whitlock. Emrys asked to see the glass itself. It had been destroyed, he was told; the readings were preserved in transcription. He asked who had made the transcription. A junior clerk. Where was the junior clerk? Reassigned to the southern provinces. Emrys looked at the transcript they handed him instead—at the neat columns of figures, the confident little annotations in a hand he did not know—and felt the first true cold of that day slide down his spine, past fear into something older and quieter.

Someone had written those numbers. Someone had sat at a desk with a lamp and written them, deliberately, in an order designed to end in him.

Then came the witnesses from Millbrook Crossing. A widow whose husband had drowned driving cattle to high ground. A ferryman who swore he'd seen lightning strike the same oak three times, which no natural storm does, and which no third-circle irrigation worker could manage even if he tried. An old woman who wept so hard she could not finish her sentence, and whom Crane gently redirected to yes-or-no questions until her grief was filed away in tidy syllables.

Emrys listened to all of it with his hands chained in front of him, and he kept waiting for the moment when someone would ask the question that mattered. Not *did you call the storm*. Anyone could claim innocence; the world was full of liars. But: *who benefits? Who profits when a storm kills forty people in a river valley that the Crown has been trying to clear for a canal scheme these ten years?*

No one asked it. He was too young, too frightened, too certain that the truth would out on its own, to ask it himself.

That question would keep. It would keep for seven years, ripening in the dark of him like fruit in a cellar, until the night in a smugglers' haven when a hearth-flame burned blue at a dying child's bedside and Emrys understood, finally, that the world was not settled—that nothing was settled, that the silence of the gods and the verdict of Corvenmoor were both things done to the world by hands, and hands could be found.

But that was later. In the hall, in the rain, he only knew that his chest hurt and that the widow would not look at him and that he could not blame her.

* * *

His advocate was a Crown man named Pellworth, assigned by the court, who met him for eleven minutes the morning of the defense.

"You'll want to testify," Pellworth said, shuffling papers without reading them. "Don't."

"I didn't do it."

"They all say that." Pellworth glanced up. He had the eyes of a man who had watched many fires and expected none of them to surprise him. "Listen. The evidence is bad—I'll grant you that. Destroyed instruments, missing clerks, testimony gathered by the Inquisition rather than the magistracy. In a civil court I might worry it apart. But this is an assize touching unlicensed divinity and high weather-working, which means the Inquisition holds joint standing, which means—" he tapped the papers, "—the outcome is already written. Your task is to lose well. Plead for mercy. Mercy exists in this process. Truth does not."

"Then what is the point of any of it?"

Pellworth almost smiled. "The point, Mr. Whitlock, is that the country watches. The point is that they leave the hall afraid, and afraid people don't ask questions. Now. Will you take my advice?"

"No," Emrys said.

Pellworth nodded, as though Emrys had confirmed something he'd long suspected about the young, and left him alone with the sound of the rain.

* * *

So Emrys testified. He stood in the dock with his chains clanking and told them everything: where he had been, the ledger signature, the innkeeper who'd seen him at supper, the mile and a half of flooded road between Corvenmoor and Millbrook Crossing that no rider could have covered in the time allowed. He told them he was a third-circle worker and proud of it, that he had spent four years learning to coax rain onto barley fields and turn hail off orchards, that storm-calling was beyond his training and beyond his strength and he would no sooner attempt it than attempt to hold the moon in his cupped hands.

"And yet," said the Inquisitor, rising from the Crown's bench, "you are a Whitlock."

The word moved through the hall like wind through wheat.

Emrys knew what she meant. Everyone knew what the Whitlocks had been, once—before the Treaty, before the silencing. Weather-wardens of the old Thornfell kings, it was said; storm-speakers whose bloodline went back to the days when the mountains had their own crown and their own gods and their own name. His grandmother had sung him songs about it. His father had burned the songs' words from every book in the house and taught him instead to be small, to be licensed, to be useful and dull and safe.

"My family's history is history," Emrys said. "I can call fog. I can sweeten a sour sky. I cannot do what was done at Millbrook."

"The Inquisition," the Inquisitor said—she was tall, grey-eyed, her voice flat as ice on a pond—"has examined the resonance patterns. They bear the signature of old blood. Old craft. Craft that runs in lines, Mr. Whitlock, whether or not the bearer admits to it. Whether or not the bearer even knows what sleeps in him."

And there it was—the thing he would chew on for seven years without knowing he was chewing it. *Craft that runs in lines.* She had said it casually, filing it between objections, but it lodged in him like a splinter: if the storm bore the signature of old Whitlock craft, then either he had called it—or someone else had, someone with the same blood, or someone with stolen craft, someone who understood that the surest lie is one built around a truth.

At twenty-three, in the dock, in the rain, he did not follow the thought to its end. He was too busy drowning.

* * *

The verdict took the jury less than half a bell.

Guilty of high weather-working. Guilty of deaths by unlicensed divinity. The penalty for such crimes, in the years after the silencing, was death—but Crane, in his dry-paper voice, noted the Crown's mercy: the accused was young, the accused had cooperated, the accused's family lands at Whitlock Fell would be forfeit to the Crown in lieu of further penalty, and the accused himself would suffer exile from the borderlands of Thornfell, perpetual and total, upon pain of death should he cross the line again.

Forfeit the lands. Exile the man. Emrys heard the sentence and thought, distantly, how neatly the two halves fit together, like a lock and its key. Later—much later—he would stand in a cliff-shrine while a voice older than the Treaty named him *doorwarden*, and he would remember that the Whitlock lands at Fell sat directly above the old pilgrim road to Vaelmoor, and the thought would come back to him wearing teeth.

In the hall, in the rain, he only bowed his head. What else was there? He was guilty in the eyes of eight hundred strangers and innocent in fact, and he had just learned—in the marrow, where lessons live—that those two conditions could coexist indefinitely, comfortably, with the full approval of everyone watching.

The widow from Millbrook crossed the hall as the guards led him out. For one mad moment he thought she was coming to strike him, and he found he would have accepted it, would have knelt for it.

She stopped in front of him. Her eyes were red-rimmed and terribly steady.

"I don't think it was you," she said, very quietly, beneath the noise of the departing crowd. "My husband read weather his whole life. He used to say a storm that size has a *shape*, and shapes belong to someone. That storm had a shape I never saw on you, boy. I saw you at the market fair two summers back, turning hail off the cider orchards. You worked like a man petting a horse." Her jaw tightened. "I said all this to the Inquisition's investigators. They wrote it down. It wasn't read aloud."

"Why are you telling me?" Emrys whispered.

"Because forty people died," she said, "and whoever killed them is still walking around in the rain, and you're the only person in this building who will spend the rest of his life looking for them." She touched his chained wrist, briefly, the way one touches a gravestone. "Look for the shape."

Then she was gone, and the guards were pulling him away, and the last thing Emrys Whitlock saw of Corvenmoor assize-hall was the grey falcon on the magistrate's breast, wings spread wide over a crown, flying nowhere, forever.

* * *

He woke in the loft above Maren Coalwater's ferry-house with his heart going like a drum and the taste of rain in his mouth.

Dawn light came thin through the shutters. Below, he could hear Maren talking to her mule, and the river doing its endless work under the pilings, and somewhere down the valley a cart, and a child laughing—Tamsin's boy, probably, alive, loud, impossibly ordinary. The world of the living, going on.

Emrys lay still and let the dream drain out of him, and then, for the first time in seven years, he did not push the trial away. He lay in the grey light and turned it over like a stone found on a beach, examining the underside at last.

*Look for the shape.*

Who profited? The Crown had taken the Whitlock lands the day of the verdict—lands above the old pilgrim road, lands with cellars and standing stones and, if Wren Ashvale's father's charts were half as good as the girl claimed, records of what lay beneath Vaelmoor. The Inquisition had gathered the evidence, and the Inquisition's junior clerks vanished southward, and the survey-glass was broken, and the storm had a shape that belonged to old blood.

Old blood. His blood—or something wearing it.

And now the gods were waking, and the Crown was digging beneath a drowned city, and Seraphine Vale was burning prayer-scraps in Kettlehollow with a look on her face that Emrys had seen before, in a mirror, at twenty-three: the look of a person beginning to suspect that the story they've been given has a seam in it.

He sat up. The loft beams were low; he ducked out of habit, out of the old instinct to be small, and then—deliberately, for the first time—straightened his spine and let his head nearly brush the rafters.

"Maren," he called down through the floorboards, voice rough with sleep and something harder than sleep. "When Vale's wagons leave, I want to know which road they take. And send word to Wren Ashvale. Tell her the bargain's changed."

Below, the ferrywoman paused. "Changed how?"

Emrys looked at his hands—weather-worker's hands, third-circle hands, hands that had spent seven years calling fog to hide smugglers because it was all he trusted himself to touch. Somewhere under those hands, someone had hidden a storm with his name on it.

"Tell her," he said, "I'm done running from Corvenmoor. I'm going back for the ledger."
