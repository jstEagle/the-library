## 9. The Drowned Bells

Wren had drawn Vaelmoor a hundred times before she ever saw it.

Her father's maps hung in her memory like windows: the crescent of the harbor wall, the seven streets running spine-wise from the sea-gate to the temple hill, the aqueduct stitching across the valley on its stone legs. He had charted the city in the last summer before the flooding, when the engineers of the Crown came with their surveys and their ledgers and their polite, terrible arithmetic. *Population to be relocated: 11,400. Structures of historical significance: 212. Depth of intended waterline: forty feet.* She had copied those maps so many times as a girl that she knew the city's streets better than the lanes of her own village—knew them dry, sunlit, alive with the inked suggestion of market stalls and gulls.

Nothing had prepared her for knowing them wet.

They came down to Vaelmoor at dusk, through a fold in the hills that Maren called the Shepherd's Cut, and the city revealed itself the way drowned things reveal themselves: slowly, and all at once. The valley below was a lake now, gray-green and still as poured glass, but the water sat low in this season of drought, and the tops of things broke its surface like the backs of sleeping animals. A bell tower, truncated at the second story, its louvred openings dark. The ridge of a roof. The crown of the sea-gate arch, its carved faces worn to featureless suggestions by three hundred years of tide. And beyond them all, rising from the flooded heart of the city, the temple hill—a black mound crowned with scaffolding, torchlight, and the sound of hammers.

Even at this distance, even across a mile of dead water, Wren could hear it. Not the hammers. Under the hammers. A ringing, deep and slow, like a bell struck once and never allowed to finish speaking.

"The bells," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "The whole city's still got its bells."

"Aye." Maren stood at the edge of the cut with her one eye fixed on the water, her boat-hook resting on her shoulder like a soldier's pike. "Drowned bells ring strange. My gran used to say the water carries the strike down and the stone carries it back up, and what you hear is neither—it's the city remembering being struck." She spat, ceremonially, into the grass. "Gran said a lot of things. Half of them were lies she liked."

"And the other half?"

"Those were worse." Maren turned her head, and her single eye found Wren with the flat, weighing look she gave cargo she wasn't sure would float. "You can still turn back, mapgirl. There's no shame in it. What's under that water doesn't care about your father's pretty lines."

"I didn't come for my father's lines." Wren adjusted the oilskin satchel on her shoulder—the maps, wrapped in waxed cloth, riding against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "I came because someone has to draw what's actually there. Maps outlive empires. My father taught me that too."

Behind them both, Emrys said nothing. He had been quiet the whole descent from Kettlehollow, quiet in the way a sky goes quiet before weather, and when Wren glanced back at him she saw him standing with his face turned toward the lake, his eyes half-closed, as though listening for something beneath the bells.

"Emrys," she said. "What do you hear?"

His eyes opened. For a moment they seemed lit from within, pale as lightning seen through cloud, and then it was gone and he was only a tired man in a rain-cloak again.

"Wind," he said. "Underwater wind. That shouldn't be possible." He pulled the cloak tighter. "Let's get to the boats before I decide how much that frightens me."

* * *

Maren's boat was a smuggler's boat, which meant it was less a boat than a rumor of one—flat-bottomed, tar-black, masted with a spar that folded flat as a broken wing, oars muffled with leather sleeves. They put onto the water in full dark, no lanterns, navigating by Wren's memory and Maren's eye and the torch-glow bleeding down from the temple hill.

The city rose around them.

That was the only word Wren could hold onto as they slid between the rooftops. *Rose.* The water was clear near the surface, black beneath, and where the moon caught it she could see straight down into streets: cobbles furred with weed, doorways standing open, a cart frozen mid-passage with its wheels sunk to the hubs in silt. A child's hoop leaned against a wall. Laundry lines still stretched between upper windows, three centuries of current having combed them into banners of green silk-thin algae that waved when their wake passed, waving like hands, like sleeves, like people.

She made herself look. She made herself map it, silently, naming each submerged landmark as her father had named it, because naming was the only prayer she trusted. *Cooper's Row. The Weighhouse. Tanner's Stair.* Each name dropped into the dark water like a coin into a well.

Then the bells rang.

Not the deep remembered ringing—the actual bells. Somewhere below them, down in the drowned dark where no hand could reach, a carillon began to move. The sound came up through the hull first, through the soles of Wren's boots, through the bones of her seat, a shivering bronze pulse, and then it broke the surface all around them in silver rings that caught the moonlight and spread outward, wave over wave over wave.

Maren shipped her oar so fast it cracked against the gunwale. "That's not current," she breathed. "There's no current moves like—"

"It's not the water." Emrys's voice was very quiet. He was leaning forward, his hands flat on the thwart, and Wren saw that his knuckles were white. "It's answering us. It rang when we crossed the boundary of the old harbor line." He looked up at her, and in the dark his face was stripped of everything but the question underneath it. "Wren. Your father's charts. Where's the harbor line?"

She didn't have to open the satchel. She had redrawn those charts in her sleep since she was nine years old. "Twenty yards behind us. The sea-gate faces." Her mouth had gone dry. "We're inside the city proper."

The bells finished their phrase—a phrase, she realized, with cold spreading through her chest, not random striking but a sequence, seven notes descending—and fell silent. The silence afterward was worse. It had texture. It had attention.

"They know we're here," Maren said flatly. "Whatever *they* are."

"Keep rowing," Emrys said. "Toward the temple hill. Slowly. If they wanted us drowned, we'd already be learning how."

* * *

They grounded the boat in the flooded colonnade at the base of the temple hill, where the Crown's engineers had built a timber causeway up the slope—a crude wooden spine nailed over the older stone stair, lashed with rope and stained with pitch. Here the water ended; here the digging began.

Wren climbed the causeway with her father's smallest chart unrolled in her mind, matching what she saw against what had been recorded, and it was the mismatches that frightened her most. The temple of Vaelmoor had been a place of thresholds—her father had written that in his field notes, the notes he'd never published, the ones she'd found sewn into the lining of his map case after he died. *Every door in the complex stands open by design. The builders wanted no barrier between the god and the street. Remarkable. Unprecedented. The architecture of welcome.*

The Crown's masons had spent the last months building walls.

Fresh-cut blocks sealed the great western entrance, mortared over carvings that Wren could still see the edges of: spirals, interlaced knots, figures with raised arms whose faces had been chiseled away—not weathered, *chiseled*, recently, the granite scars still pale. Scaffolding climbed the façade like a disease. Chisels lay scattered on the steps alongside coils of rope, iron bars, and crates stenciled with the Crown's seal. And everywhere, ground into the mud and dust of the causeway, bootprints. Dozens of them. Fresh.

"No watch fires on the lower slope," Maren murmured, moving along the colonnade with her boat-hook held low. "Sloppy. Or confident. Or—" She stopped.

Wren followed her gaze and stopped too.

Blood.

A smear of it on the third step, dragged downward, as though something heavy had been pulled or had crawled. Another print of it on the column base beside it, a palm print, small—a woman's, or a youth's. And leading away from the steps, toward a side entrance half-hidden behind fallen masonry, a trail of drops spaced like a walking man's stride, getting further apart, getting weaker.

"That blood's not old," Emrys said. He crouched beside the smear and touched it, and Wren watched his expression change—watched the weariness fall away from him like a cloak dropped, replaced by something older and harder. "Hours. Maybe less. There's no crust on it yet."

"Masons don't bleed on their own stairs and walk off without a word," Maren said.

"No," Emrys agreed. "But conscripts might. If they were told to keep working. If they were told that stopping meant—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Maren's son Tam was somewhere in the labor gangs of Vaelmoor; they had all known that since Kettlehollow; they had all carried it up this hill in silence.

Wren found she had moved without deciding to, following the blood trail toward the side entrance. The doorway there was different from the others—not sealed, not yet, but *defaced*: the carvings around its frame gouged deep, the threshold stone split with wedges, and wedged into the split, driven in like nails into a coffin lid, iron spikes stamped with the Crown's seal. Someone had tried to close a door that had been built, three thousand years ago, to never be closed.

And above the door, where the chisels had done their worst work, one carving survived entire. Wren lifted her lantern—unhooded now, caution abandoned—and let the light climb the stone.

A figure. Tall, robed in waves rather than cloth, arms spread wide in the posture her father's notes had called *the welcome*. Its face had been chiseled away like all the others—but where the face had been, someone had carved new marks, fresh and shallow and frantic: a spiral, coiling inward, and at its center a single word in old Thornfell script that Wren recognized from her father's rubbings.

She read it aloud, because some things demanded air.

"*It wakes whether you dig or not.*"

The lantern flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

Behind her, Emrys drew a breath that sounded like the first breath after drowning. When Wren turned, she saw him staring at the defaced god, at the spiral, at the word, and his storm-grey eyes were wide and lit from within again, and this time the light did not fade.

"The digging isn't demolition," he said. Each word came slowly, as if he were translating from a language he'd only just begun to remember. "They're not tearing the temple down. They're digging *down*. Through the floors. Into whatever's underneath." His hand rose toward the fresh mortar sealing the western gate, toward the iron in the threshold, toward the blood on the steps. "Three hundred years the gods were silent, and the moment the Crown starts breaking this place open, they start answering prayers in Kettlehollow. That's not a coincidence. That's a cry for help—or a warning." He looked at Maren. At Wren. "Something under this hill is meant to stay sealed. And the Crown knows it. That's why the masons seal doors instead of opening them. That's why there's blood."

Down in the drowned city, every bell rang at once—one vast, brazen chord that rolled up through the bedrock, through the soles of Wren's boots, through the cage of her ribs—and stopped.

In the ringing silence that followed, from somewhere deep beneath the temple hill, faint and rhythmic and horribly patient, came another sound. Not bells. Hammers. But wrong—too slow, too deep, striking in time with nothing human.

Like a heartbeat. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the stone.

Maren tightened her grip on the boat-hook. "Tam's down there," she said, "with whatever that is."

Emrys stepped past the bloodied threshold into the dark.
