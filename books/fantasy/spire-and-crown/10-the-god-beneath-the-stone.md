## 10. The God Beneath the Stone

The water in Vaelmoor sang.

Emrys had heard bells drowned before—every smuggler who worked the Thornfell passes knew the story of the city the Crown had flooded rather than fortified—but knowing a thing and standing inside it were different countries. The streets rose around them like the ribs of some vast drowned beast, black stone furred with pale weed, and above, far above, where the floodwater met the cavern's vaulted dark, the bells of the old cathedral hung from their rotted yokes and rang. Not violently. Not even musically. Just a slow, patient tolling, as if the water itself were swinging them, or as if something beneath the stones were pulling the ropes.

"Current's wrong here," Maren said. She stood hip-deep at Emrys's shoulder, one eye catching the lantern light and the other a puckered seam of scar. Her voice was flat, ferrywoman's voice, the voice she used when the river was lying to her. "Water should pull toward the breach in the seawall. It's pulling *in*. Toward the temple."

Wren Ashvale floated her map-case on a scrap of oilcloth beside her, both hands white-knuckled on its straps. She was nineteen and had crossed half of Thornfell to be here, and she had not stopped shaking since they'd waded through the first submerged archway. But she hadn't turned back either, and Emrys had learned that this was the whole measure of a person: not whether they trembled, but what they did while trembling.

"The cathedral's there." Wren lifted her chin toward the far dark, where a shape too regular to be natural loomed against the cavern wall. "My father charted it before the flooding. Temple of the Ninefold Hearth. He said the masons' marks on it were older than any kingdom he'd ever mapped. Older than the Crown. Older than the treaty." She swallowed. "He said the city was built around it. Like a shell around a heart."

*Like a heart,* Emrys thought, and did not say aloud what had been sitting in his chest since the cliff shrine, since the voice that had named him *doorwarden*: that hearts could be caged, and doors could be guarded, and that he was walking toward both.

They went on. The water deepened to their waists, then their chests, then shallowed again as the street climbed toward the temple terrace—a long causeway of cracked flagstones rising out of the flood like the spine of a leviathan. And it was there, on the causeway, that Wren stopped so suddenly that Maren walked into her back.

"Emrys." Her voice came out very small. "The masons' marks."

He raised the lantern.

The temple facade should have been defaced—that much the Crown's work-gangs had seen to, chiseling away the faces of the gods wherever they'd been carved, hammering the nine hearth-stones into rubble. And they had been. But the destruction was wrong. Chisels do not leave gouges three inches deep in granite. Hammers do not crack a lintel stone clean through. The damage wasn't demolition. It was *excavation*—careful, methodical, centered on a single point low in the facade where the stones had been pried apart like ribs, opening a gap just wide enough for a man to pass sideways into the dark.

And the gap was slicked with blood. Fresh enough that it still shone.

"They're not tearing it down," Maren said quietly. "They're digging *in*."

Emrys set his jaw until it ached. "Then we go in after them."

* * *

Inside, the temple was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. It was the cold of a place that had been holding its breath for three hundred years. Their lanterns pushed back only a few feet of dark; beyond that, the nave opened upward into space they couldn't see, and the sound of the drowned bells came through the walls now, muffled and strange, like a heartbeat heard through a door.

The first body was a boy's.

He lay crumpled at the foot of the breached facade, a laborer's canvas coat soaked dark, a pick still hooked in his fingers. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Emrys knelt and pressed two fingers to his throat and found nothing, and closed the boy's eyes with his thumb, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the drowned bells weren't already saying better.

"He's been dead days," Maren said. "But look—" she turned the boy's hand over. The palms were blistered and split, but beneath the blisters, inked in cheap commercial dye, was a number. "They numbered them. Like cargo."

"Like contraband," Emrys said, and heard his own voice go flat and hard.

There were more. They found them down the breached gap and into the passages beyond—the temple's undercrofts, corridors of fitted stone older than any masonry Emrys had ever seen, walls carved with processions of figures whose faces had been hammered off by hands that had feared them. Some of the bodies were old. Some were not. And some—

"Some are breathing." Wren's voice cracked. She was kneeling by a man slumped against the wall, chest moving in shallow, rattling pulls. "Emrys, they're alive. Why would they leave them alive?"

Emrys didn't answer, because he was listening. Beneath the bells, beneath the drip of water, there was another sound now, drifting up from deeper in: a slow, rhythmic grinding of iron on stone, and beneath that, fainter, human voices. Singing. A work-song, the kind conscript gangs sang to keep time—and then the singing stopped, all at once, cut off like a snuffed candle.

Maren had gone rigid. "Tam," she whispered.

"Maren—" Emrys began.

"That's his gang's song. That's the one he hums when he's mending nets, I'd know it if I were deaf in my other ear too." She was already moving, splashing down the corridor, and there was nothing to do but follow.

* * *

The passage opened into a chamber that made Emrys's skin try to crawl off his bones.

It was round, and vast, and ancient beyond reckoning—the true heart of the temple, the thing the city had been built around. The ceiling arched up into darkness. The floor was a single sweep of stone worn smooth by centuries of kneeling pilgrims. And in the center, sunk into that floor, was a well—not a well of water but a shaft of open dark, ringed by nine pillars, each pillar wrapped in chains and iron bands stamped with the Crown's seal.

Suspended above the shaft, held in a cage of black iron no larger than a coffin, was a heart.

Not a metaphor. Not a carving. A heart the size of a wine barrel, veined in gold and ember-red, beating. Slowly. Enormously. Each beat sent a pulse of dim red light rolling across the chamber and a shudder through the floor, and each beat was answered, somewhere far below the shaft, by the tolling of the drowned bells. The cage was scorched and scarred; the god inside had been fighting it for three hundred years, and losing, and had not stopped fighting.

Around the well, in the torchlight of a dozen Crown lanterns, perhaps forty laborers knelt in rows, drilling and prying at the shaft's rim under the eyes of a squad of inquisition wardens in grey-and-silver. And Emrys understood, with a sickness that rose in his throat, why the living had been left alive. You cannot dig out a god with dead men. You work them until they drop, and you leave the bodies where they fall as a lesson, and you bring more.

A warden turned. Shouted. And the chamber erupted.

What followed was not a battle; Emrys would have been ashamed to call it that. It was chaos, and smoke, and Maren's ferry-hook whistling, and Wren screaming warnings, and Emrys reaching for the storm the way he had reached for it every night of his exile—and the storm *answered*, faster than it ever had, roaring up through him like a river through a broken dam, lightning crawling the chamber walls, wardens thrown sprawling, torches guttering blue.

It answered too fast. Too eagerly. Like something on the other side leaning into the connection.

In the middle of it, Maren fought her way to the row of kneeling laborers, seized a thin boy by the collar, and Emrys saw her face change—saw twenty years of iron crack straight down the middle.

"TAM."

Her son was barely recognizable. Gray-lipped, hollow-eyed, wrists chained to the laborer beside him, coughing blood-flecked spit with every breath. But alive. Barely. Blessedly, impossibly, barely alive.

"Get them out," Emrys shouted to Wren, pointing at the living. "All of them. Go!"

* * *

Getting forty people out of a collapsing temple through flooded streets should have been impossible. It took everything: Wren's maps, Maren's fury, the laborers' last scraps of strength, and Emrys standing alone in the heart-chamber holding the wardens at bay with lightning until the last of them had gone. By the end he was on his knees, drained to a thread, watching the wardens flee up the passage, and the chamber was quiet except for the heartbeat.

He should have run too. He knew that even as he stayed.

The heart beat. Red light rolled across the floor and broke against his knees like a tide.

"You're Brannoc," he said. His voice sounded absurdly small. "The hearth god. The one the mother prayed to in Kettlehollow. The one who answered."

The pulse of light stuttered. Changed rhythm. Three quick beats, two slow. Then again. Three quick, two slow.

*A pattern.* Emrys's mouth went dry. "That's—can you understand me? Beat once for yes."

One beat. Long, deliberate, shaking dust from the ceiling.

Three hundred years in an iron cage, and the first thing the god asked for was a conversation.

"Why did you answer the prayers?" Emrys asked. "After three hundred years of silence. Why now?"

Two beats. No. Then a pause, and a sequence Emrys couldn't read, and frustration rolled off the cage like heat off a forge. The heart flared, and the flare pressed against the inside of Emrys's skull, and suddenly he wasn't hearing beats at all—he was hearing a voice like coals settling, like a hearth in a house where everyone has died, like warmth remembering itself:

***Because someone is unmaking the door.***

Emrys staggered. "The Spire Gate."

***The gate is mine to hold. My brothers and sisters passed through it when your Crown burned us out of the world. I remained as its lock. And now they dig for me, little doorwarden, because a lock can be turned from either side—if you have the key.*** The voice guttered, dimmed, steadied. ***They mean to open the gate without a warden. To take the country behind it.***

"What country?"

***Mine.*** For the first time the voice carried something like grief. ***Thornfell. The true one. The one your treaty erased.***

Emrys knelt there in the red light, and thought of Kettlehollow, and Maren's ferry, and the widow's folded flag, and the lullaby the tavern keeper still hummed without knowing why, and felt the ground tilt under everything he understood about himself.

"I can free you," he said. "Tell me how."

***You cannot.*** Flat. Final. ***Iron forged with erasure-binds. Only the ones who forged it can loose it—or a power greater than theirs.*** A pause, and the heart's beat slowed, and when the voice came again it was quieter, almost gentle. ***But the gate does not need me free, doorwarden. It needs a warden willing to stand in it. That was always the price. Not strength. Willingness.***

"What does that mean? What does a warden *do*?"

***Opens. Closes. Stays.*** The heart flared once, hard, and the voice frayed to embers. ***They are coming back with more men and bigger irons. You have perhaps a week. Find what was erased. Remember it aloud. The gate answers memory, doorwarden—it opens for those who remember what was taken. Gather the country, or there will be nothing left to open the door onto.***

The light was failing. The heartbeat slowing, conserving itself, sinking back toward its long endurance.

"Wait," Emrys said. "Wait—who called the storm? The storm at my trial. Was it—"

***Ask the woman who hid you,*** the voice said, almost gone, almost tender, ***and ask yourself why she never told you what she saw.***

Then only the heartbeat, and the dark, and Emrys Whitlock kneeling alone in the oldest room in the world, holding a lantern against three hundred years of silence, with a name—*Maren*—burning in his mouth like a coal he could neither swallow nor spit out.

Above him, faint through the stone, the drowned bells began to toll faster.

Something, somewhere, had noticed the door had been touched.
