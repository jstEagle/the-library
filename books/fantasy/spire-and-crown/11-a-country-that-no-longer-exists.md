## 11. A Country That No Longer Exists

The dream began with the smell of bread.

Not Kettlehollow's bread—Maren's hearth-loaves, dense and dark with rye—but something older, sweeter, baked in ovens Emrys had never stood beside and yet knew the shape of by heart. He was walking a street paved in pale stone worn smooth by centuries of feet, and the houses leaned toward him like old men sharing a secret, their shutters painted blue, their doorposts carved with spirals that meant *welcome* in a language he did not speak and somehow understood anyway.

Above the rooftops rose the Spire.

It was whole here. Unbroken. A needle of white granite climbing into a sky the color of unpolished silver, its crown lost in cloud, its flanks wrapped in terraces and hanging gardens where bells hung silent among the vines. He had seen the Spire before—in Wren's maps, in the drowned ruins of Vaelmoor where only its topmost courses broke the water like a broken tooth. But this was the Spire as it had been, and seeing it made his chest ache in a way he had no name for, the way a man might ache looking at the face of a mother he never met.

"You are standing in Thornfell," said a voice like a landslide deciding to be gentle.

Emrys turned. The street behind him had filled—not with people, but with the idea of people: figures of grey smoke shaped like bakers and weavers and soldiers, going about the business of lives that had ended three hundred years ago. And at the center of them, taller than any man had a right to be, stood Brannoc.

He was not what Emrys expected. He was not fire, though embers drifted through him like snow falling upward. He was broad as a hearth and warm as one, built of stone and soot and banked coals, and his eyes were two doors opening onto rooms where fires had burned for ten thousand years without ever being allowed to go out.

"You're dead," Emrys said. "All of you. Three hundred years silent. The priests said—"

"The priests said what they were paid to say." Brannoc's voice rumbled through the cobbles, through the soles of Emrys's boots, through the marrow of him. "Come. Walk with me while I still have streets for you to walk on."

*While I still have.* The words landed cold despite the god's warmth. Emrys walked.

* * *

They walked through Thornfell as it had been, and it was like watching someone else's memory played out in smoke and bread-smell. Cities Emrys knew as ruins stood golden in a sun that no longer existed. The river Vael ran clear between quays crowded with barges flying a banner Emrys had never seen and recognized instantly: a white spire crowned with nine stars on a field of deep green. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat. He had never seen it. It was in his blood anyway.

"Thornfell," Brannoc said, and the word was not a name but an act of laying a foundation. "Before it was a borderland. Before it was a *problem* to be managed between two crowns. It was a country, Emrys Whitlock. It had laws written in three languages. It had a calendar that began its year at first frost, because frost was when we planted the winter wheat and paid our debts. It had—" the god paused, and the embers in him dimmed, "—it had me."

"What happened?"

"What always happens." Brannoc gestured, and the golden city folded up like a map being closed. Smoke figures froze mid-stride, mid-laugh, mid-prayer. "Two kings grew tired of fighting over us and decided instead to divide us. There was a treaty signed in a hall far from here, by men who had never tasted our bread. The Greenmantle Accord, they called it. It took everything we were and split it down the middle like a carcass—one half to the eastern crown, one half to the western, and the middle ground burned so neither side would have a claim."

"And the gods?"

"We were written out." For the first time, the voice cracked, and hearing a mountain crack was worse than hearing it fall. "Do you understand what I am telling you, doorwarden? We were not defeated. Defeat leaves ruins, and ruins leave memory, and memory leaves seeds. We were *unwritten*. The treaty included clauses about divinity—clauses no one has read in three centuries because no one remembers there is anything to read. Every temple rededicated. Every shrine torn down or renamed. Every song rewritten with new words. Every chronicle burned and replaced with chronicles that began history on the day of the signing, as if nothing had existed before except wilderness waiting to be claimed."

The frozen city dissolved around them. In its place: the Thornfell Emrys knew. The hard borderlands. The smugglers' passes. The villages that paid taxes to a distant throne and could not say why. The mountains themselves seemed smaller in this version, worn down, apologetic.

"Memory is our flesh," Brannoc said quietly. "Prayer is our breath. You have wondered why we went silent. This is why. A god whose country is forgotten is a fire with no hearth. We did not die, Emrys. We were *unmade*, slowly, clause by clause, verse by verse—and we have been lying in the dark beneath Vaelmoor ever since, holding the last ember of ourselves, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to remember us." The god turned, and those door-eyes fixed on Emrys with a weight that buckled his knees. "And now the Crown is digging us out—not to restore, but to destroy. To finish the work. When the last stone of my temple falls, the last ember goes out. Not just me. All of us. Every god your people's grandmothers' grandmothers whispered to. Gone past even forgetting."

* * *

The dream shifted. They stood now before the Spire Gate.

Emrys had heard of it the way children hear of dragons—as a story, a thing for frightening or thrilling, never for believing. A door in the roots of the Spire itself, older than the flooding of Vaelmoor, older than the treaty, older perhaps than the crowns on either side of the mountains. In the dream it was a simple arch of white stone, unadorned, its threshold worn into a shallow bowl by ten thousand years of feet passing through.

"It opens both ways," Brannoc said. "Out of your world and into ours—the country-under-the-country, the green fields where we kept our own hearths. When we were remembered, the gate stood open, and the folk of Thornfell could carry their dead to us and bring back spring in a jar. When the treaty unwrote us, the gate closed. It will open again under one condition and no other."

"Tell me."

"It opens for those who remember." Brannoc knelt—a god kneeling, the sky bending with him—and took Emrys's face in hands like warm stones. "Not for power. Not for blood. Not for the Chancellor's armies or the Crown's keys. The gate does not care who wants through it. It cares who *remembers*. And Emrys Whitlock—here is the cruelty of it, and you must hear all of it—your people do not remember. Three hundred years of erasure have done their work. The songs are half-forgotten. The flag is ash. The border-stones are walls on strangers' farms. What remains is scattered like seed after a storm, in taverns and attics and graveyards and the crooked heads of old women who hum tunes they cannot name."

"So I gather it." Emrys heard himself say it, and marveled at how steady his voice came out when everything inside him was tilting. "The way I'd gather contraband. Piece by piece, pass by pass."

"Yes. Every song sung true. Every relic held in living hands. Every name spoken aloud. Each fragment is a coal; together they are a hearth; and a hearth is all a god needs to stand up again. Gather enough of the country that no longer exists, and the gate will know its people. Open it, and we can leave this world with what dignity remains to us—or better, come back into it, if your people choose to take us home." The god's grip tightened. "But understand the cost of failure. If the Crown tears down the temple before the gate opens, we do not merely fade. Everything we touched fades with us. The old words. The old songs. The *names*. Your friend Maren Coalwater—her family name is a Thornfell name, older than either crown. Her son Tam. The haven of Kettlehollow, built on the bones of a town called Ketelholm, which was real once and will stop having been real if we go out."

Emrys's stomach dropped. "Stop having been real?"

"Erasure is not silence," Brannoc said. "Erasure is *retroactive*. When the last ember dies, the world will smooth over the places we occupied like water closing over a stone. People will not mourn us. They will not be able to. There will be nothing left to mourn *with*." He released Emrys's face and stood, and the dream-sky shuddered. "I show you this so you understand what you are carrying. Not a cause. Not a rebellion. A country. And a country is heavier than any cargo you have ever smuggled."

* * *

Then the dream turned against him, the way dreams do, and showed him the thing he least wanted to see.

He was standing in the great hall of the Assizes again—the trial, the storm-trial, the worst morning of his life—but the hall was wrong, stretched tall and hollow, and the gallery was full of smoke-figures who would not look at him. The judges' bench was empty except for a single document, glowing faintly, and he knew without reading it what it said: *Emrys Whitlock, called Stormbringer, exiled for the deaths of forty souls at Millbrook Ford.*

"That is what erasure looks like when it is done to a man instead of a nation," Brannoc's voice came from everywhere and nowhere. "A name attached to deeds that were not his. A story told so often that even the tellers believe it. Tell me, doorwarden—if a country can be unwritten by a treaty, what can be done to a man by a verdict?"

"I'm not a country," Emrys whispered.

"No," the god agreed, almost gently. "You are something more fragile. You are a story a handful of people believe. Maren believes you are the boy she pulled out of the river. Wren believes you are a tired man doing hard things well. The Crown believes you are a monster. Which of these is true depends entirely on who is doing the remembering—and for three hundred years, the ones doing the remembering about Thornfell were the ones who profited from the forgetting." The smoke-figures in the gallery finally turned their faces toward him, and every one of them wore his own face. "So. You know now what they did to us. You know what was done to you. The question the dream asks, the only question worth asking: when you walk out of this sleep and into the morning—which story will you spend your life making true?"

Emrys opened his mouth to answer—

—and woke with Maren's rough hand shaking his shoulder and the taste of woodsmoke in his mouth, though the hearth across the haven's common room had burned down to grey ash hours ago.

* * *

He sat up too fast. His head swam. Around him, Kettlehollow slept in the blue hour before dawn—Tam curled in his blankets, fever finally broken since the night the hearth-flame burned blue; Wren asleep sitting up over her maps, charcoal still smudged on her cheek; the smugglers and refugees breathing in the rhythm of people who had learned not to trust sleep enough to sink deep into it.

Maren studied him with her one eye. "You were talking in your sleep. Names. Whole lists of names, like a priest reading a death-roll." She crouched. "And then you said something over and over. *It opens for those who remember.* What does that mean, Emrys?"

He told her. All of it—the bread-smell street, the banner with nine stars, the treaty, the unwriting, the gate and its condition, the threat of retroactive oblivion. He watched her face as he spoke, expecting disbelief, and found instead something worse: recognition. Her jaw tightened at the mention of the Greenmantle Accord. Her hand went unconsciously to her collarbone, where Emrys knew she wore a small green stone on a cord, a stone she had never explained.

"My gran used to sing a song," Maren said at last, very quietly. "About a spire with stars on it. Nine stars. I always thought she'd made it up, or misremembered some southern ballad." Her eye came up to his, fierce and wet. "She sang it low, like she was hiding it. Like it was contraband." A short, bitter laugh. "Because it was. Because it always was."

"Maren—"

"Don't." She stood, wiping her eye with the heel of her hand, already moving toward the hearth to build the fire up, because that was what Maren did with unbearable things—she fed them into the flame until they became light. "Don't apologize. Don't philosophize. Tell me what you need."

Emrys looked around the sleeping haven—at Tam, at Wren, at the scarred tables where smugglers drank and refugees wept and nobody asked anybody's real name unless it mattered. A country that no longer exists. But it wasn't gone, was it? It was here, scattered, hidden in plain sight—in songs hummed over dishwater, in stones mortared into farm walls, in names like Coalwater and Ashvale that had crossed three hundred years on the backs of ordinary stubborn people who simply refused to stop being who they were.

"A ledger," he said slowly, and felt the weariness of exile lift by a single degree, replaced by something that felt dangerously like purpose. "The biggest ledger I've ever run. Songs, flags, border-stones, names, recipes, curses, prayers—every scrap of Thornfell that survived the treaty. I need a list of everywhere it might be hiding, and I need people willing to carry it." He met Maren's eye. "I need smugglers, Maren. But this time the cargo is a country."

Maren grinned, and it was the grin of a woman about to commit a magnificent crime. "Then you'd best talk to the mapmaker. Because if anyone knows where a dead country's buried treasure is marked down—it's the girl whose father charted every inch of it before the drowning."

Across the room, Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something. Emrys caught only one word of it, soft as an ember settling:

"*Ketelholm.*"
