## 12. Songs the Soldiers Still Hum

Wren Ashvale had drawn maps of countries that no longer existed before. She had inked the old borders of Thornfell from her father's charts, traced its vanished rivers, labeled its drowned towns in careful copperplate. But she had never understood, until now, that a country was not made of lines.

It was made of this: an old woman's hands unfolding cloth in firelight. A tune hummed off-key over dishwater. A stone pulled from a wall with both hands and held like a child.

They had left Vaelmoor four days ago with Brannoc's words ringing in all their skulls like a struck bell: *The gate opens for those who remember.* Emrys had said little on the road back into the high valleys. Maren had said less. It was Wren who had finally broken the silence at the second night's camp, unrolling her father's atlas across a flat rock and saying, "Memory's scattered. If we want it whole, we have to go collect it. Like surveying." And Emrys had looked at her with something that was almost hope, and said, "Then we survey."

So they surveyed.

* * *

The first stop was the Drowned Lantern, a tavern wedged into the shoulder of the mountain above Kettlehollow, so old that its foundation stones predated the Treaty of Ashes by two hundred years—though the tavern keeper would tell you that only if you asked, and only after your third cup.

His name was Aldous Crane, and he was broad as a door and gentle as snowfall, and when Emrys explained what they wanted—carefully, in the low voice he used for dangerous things—the big man went still with his rag halfway through wiping the table.

"You want the songs," he said.

"The old ones," Wren said. "Thornfell ones. Before the Crown."

Aldous set down his rag. He looked at the empty common room, at the smoke-stained beams, at the hearth where a pot of barley stew muttered to itself. Then he did something Wren did not expect. He laughed—a short, startled sound, like a man finding a coin in an old coat.

"Forty years I've been humming them," he said. "My da taught me. His ma taught him. We were told to stop singing them after the last garrison came through—Crown officer said the words were seditious. So we stopped singing them *out loud*." He tapped his temple. "But you can't garrison a man's skull, can you? Soldiers still hum them. Crown soldiers. Half of them don't even know what they're humming. Their grandmothers sang it to sleep and it just... stayed."

He cleared his throat, embarrassed suddenly, this enormous man made shy by a lullaby. "It's not much. It's about a shepherd waiting for spring on the fells. That's all. That's all it is."

He sang.

His voice was rough and untrained and it cracked twice, and Wren found her eyes burning anyway, because the melody was older than the words she could hear underneath it—older than the Crown, older than the treaty, a shape of sound that had been passed mouth to mouth down through generations of people who had been told their country never existed. The words named hills she had mapped. Named a river by its true name, the one her father had marked in faded red as *obsc.* The lullaby was a map. It had been a map all along, sung into children at bedtime so that no edict could ever burn it.

When he finished, the common room was very quiet. Maren had turned her face to the window. Emrys sat with his hands flat on the table, as if steadying himself against a swell.

"Aldous," Wren managed, "would you sing it again? Slower?"

The big man blinked. "Why slower?"

"Because I'm writing it down." She already had her book open, her pen moving. "Every note I can catch. Every word. And then—" she hesitated, then committed, "—then I'd like you to teach it to me properly. Until I can't get it wrong."

Aldous Crane looked at her for a long moment. Then at Emrys. Then he smiled, slow as sunrise over the fells, and pulled out a stool.

"Well," he said. "Sit down then, girl. This'll take all night. My da took a week teaching me the third verse, and he swore I was a worse student than the goats."

* * *

The widow lived alone at the top of a switchback trail above the village of Stonereach, in a house with a slate roof and a garden gone wild with late-season herbs. Her name was Edith Marlowe, and she was ninety-one years old, and when Emrys knocked on her door and told her who he was, she looked him up and down and said, "I know who you are. You're the boy they blamed. Come in before the wind does. It's rude, letting weather in first."

Her cottage smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and dust. She moved slowly, one hand on the furniture, but her eyes were quick and dark and missed nothing. When Wren explained what they were gathering—songs, stories, anything of the old country—Edith snorted.

"The old country." She lowered herself into a chair by the window. "You say that like it's a story. It wasn't a story. I was seven years old when the treaty was signed. I remember my father coming home from the assizes town with his hat in his hands, and he sat down at this very table—not this table, the old one, but the same spot—and he said, 'Well, Edie. As of today, we've always been part of the Empire.' Always. He said it like that. Like the word could reach backward and eat everything before it." She smiled thinly. "That's how they do it, girl. They don't burn the past. They make it never have happened."

Emrys crouched beside her chair. "Brannoc says the gate opens for those who remember. We think—if enough people remember, if the memory is gathered—"

"I know what Brannoc says." Edith's chin lifted. "You think a woman my age prays to a god nobody's heard of in three hundred years without reason? I've prayed to him every night since my husband died. Quietly. The way you pray when you know better than to be caught." She fixed Emrys with a look. "You want something of mine. I can see it in the map-girl's face. She's cataloguing my house already."

Wren flushed. Edith chuckled, dry as seed husks.

"It's in the chest," she said. "Under the bed. The blue one. Bring it here—it's heavier than sin, mind."

Maren fetched it. Inside, beneath layers of yellowed linen, lay folded cloth—and when Edith drew it out with both hands, Wren felt the room change.

It was a flag. Or what had been a flag once: deep green gone grey with age, embroidered at the center with a white spire rising from three wavy lines of silver thread. The edges were frayed. One corner had been mended with mismatched thread sometime long ago, clumsily, lovingly.

"My grandmother's brother carried it," Edith said. "At the last mustering, before the treaty. He didn't come home. The flag did. Folded up small, in a comrade's pack, with a note saying he fell at Hollow Bridge holding it up." Her thumb moved over the embroidery, tracing the spire. "The Crown's men went house to house after the treaty collecting these. Burning them in the square. My mother sewed it into a mattress ticking and we slept on our country for sixty years. Then I put it in the chest and told myself I'd give it to someone worth giving it to."

She held it out—to Emrys.

He didn't take it at once. Wren watched him look at it the way a starving man looks at bread he doesn't believe is real. "Edith," he said hoarsely, "this should stay with—"

"With whom?" she snapped. "With me? I'll be dead by spring, boy, and the worms aren't sentimental. Take it. Use it. And when you open that gate of yours—" her voice cracked, just slightly, the first fracture in all her flint, "—you tell them at Hollow Bridge that the Marlowes kept the colors. All this time. Tell them."

Emrys took the flag with both hands, the way you receive something holy. Wren wrote in her book, later, by lamplight: *One flag. Green field, white spire, three silver waves. Carried at the last mustering. Kept 84 years. Condition: worn, mended, unbroken.*

*Unbroken*, she decided, was the important word.

* * *

The border-stone nearly started a fight.

They found the farmer, Tam Breck, plowing a field below the ruins of an old watchtower, and when Emrys asked—politely, carefully—whether there might be any old stones on his land, stones with markings, Tam set down his plow and said, "Aye. There's one. And you can't have it."

"It wouldn't be taken," Wren said quickly. "Just copied. Drawn. Measured."

Tam Breck was a squat, sun-browned man with forearms like ham hocks, and he looked at each of them in turn—Emrys with his storm-grey eyes, Maren with her patch and her ferryman's shoulders, Wren with her ink-stained fingers—and something in his face shifted from suspicion to a kind of weary recognition.

"You're the ones from Kettlehollow," he said. "The ones the gods woke up for." He spat thoughtfully into the furrow. "Come on, then. But I'm telling you now, that stone stays in my wall."

The wall ran along the eastern edge of his property, dry-laid granite, generations old. About forty paces along, one stone sat differently from the rest—larger, squarer, its outer face worn smooth. Tam worked it loose with a crowbar and a grunt, and there it was: carved into the inner face, protected from weather for centuries, a mark Wren knew instantly from her father's notes. The Thornfell boundary sigil. A spire split by a vertical line, half above, half below—a symbol scholars had argued about for a hundred years.

She knelt in the dirt and began to draw it, and while she drew, Tam talked.

"Grandfather told me it was a border-stone. Marked where Thornfell ended and the Empire began, back before there was an Empire here at all. Said his grandfather helped move it when they redrawn the lines—said the family was paid to bury it facing inward, so the mark would be hidden." He shrugged. "We kept it in every wall since. Moved it each time the wall was rebuilt. Never lost it. Never showed it." He paused. "Felt like showing it would be finishing the job they started. Burying it proper."

Wren's pen stopped. She looked up at him. "You hid it *in plain sight*. In a wall anyone could see."

"Nobody sees a wall," Tam said. "Everybody sees a flag. That's why flags get burned." He nudged the stone with his boot. "You want to know the funny thing? My boy asked me last summer why we bother. Told him it was tradition. He said, 'Tradition of what?' And I didn't have an answer. Didn't have the words for it." He looked down at the exposed carving, at the spire split by the line, half in one country and half in another. "Maybe you've got the words. Maybe that's what you're doing—going round finding the words none of us had."

Emrys crouched and laid his palm flat against the carved face. Wren saw his lips move—she couldn't hear what he said—but the air around the stone seemed to deepen, the way light changes just before rain. When he stood, Tam was watching him with narrowed eyes.

"Stone stays," Tam repeated.

"Stone stays," Emrys agreed. "But Tam—when the gate opens, will you come? Will you stand at Vaelmoor and remember aloud with us?"

The farmer chewed on that, working his jaw like he was chewing tobacco. Then he bent, fitted the stone back into its place in the wall, and tamped it firm with the heel of his hand.

"I'll bring the stone," he said. "If it goes, it goes in a cart, standing upright, like a proper marker. Not smuggled in a mattress. Not buried in a wall." He straightened. "My grandfather hid it. I'll show it. That's the difference between us, and I've been waiting my whole life to make it."

* * *

They collected other things in the days that followed, smaller things, things that would have seemed like nothing a month ago. A wedding ring engraved with the spire sigil, from a jeweler's widow in Stonereach. A child's counting rhyme from a Kettlehollow grandmother—*one for the fell, two for the ford, three for the spire where the old lords warred*—that half the village's children still chanted without knowing why. A recipe, written on the flyleaf of a psalter, for a festival bread baked only on the eve of the old harvest, which the Crown calendar had renamed and repurposed two hundred years ago but whose shape—a spire, scored into the crust—no baker had ever quite stopped making.

Wren filled forty pages. Sixty. Her father had taught her that a map was an argument about reality—that every line drawn was a claim made—and she began to understand that she was drawing a different kind of map now, one made of melody and thread and carved granite and the taste of festival bread. Each piece was nothing. Each piece was everything. Together they were not a record of a dead country.

They were the country, waking up.

On the sixth night, camped in the ruins of the watchtower above Tam Breck's fields, she spread her pages out in the firelight and the others gathered around. Maren read the lullaby's words aloud, haltingly, learning them. Emrys held the flag across his knees like a sleeping animal. The border-stone sketch lay in the center of the circle, and when the firelight flickered, the carved spire seemed almost to move.

"We need more," Wren said. "Songs, names, places. Every fragment makes the gate easier to open—I can feel it, the way a key feels smoother when all its teeth are cut. But we need it fast, and we need it from everyone, and most of these people won't give it to strangers. They'll give it to each other." She looked up. "We shouldn't be collecting this. We should be teaching people to collect it themselves. Teach the lullaby to ten people instead of writing it down once. Show the sigil. Tell them what Edith Marlowe kept in a chest for eighty-four years."

Emrys was quiet for a long moment, firelight moving on his face. "A country made of couriers," he said softly. "Smuggled memory, house to house." He almost smiled. "It's the one cargo the Crown can't weigh."

It was Maren who spoke next, and her voice was strange—flat, careful, the voice she used on the ferry when the water was lying to her.

"There's smoke," she said, nodding east, toward the lowlands. "Been burning since sundown. Too steady for a wildfire."

Wren followed her gaze. Far off, past the black shoulder of the mountains, a dull orange glow stained the underside of the clouds—wide, diffuse, wrong. Not one fire. Many fires. Whole districts of them.

And beneath the glow, faint on the wind, came a sound that made the hair rise on Wren's arms: bells. Dozens of bells, ringing in patterns she didn't recognize—until she did, because Aldous Crane had hummed the pattern to her three nights ago, laughing, calling it the *tax-man's knock*, the old signal that had rung from Thornfell's steeples when the Crown's assessors rode into a valley to seize records, ledgers, and parish books.

The Crown wasn't just hunting them anymore.

Somewhere down in the dark, the unwriting had begun.
