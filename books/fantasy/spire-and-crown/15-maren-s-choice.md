## 15. Maren's Choice

The storm had passed three days ago and still the valley smelled of it—wet ash and ozone, the green bite of lightning-struck pine. Maren Coalwater stood at the edge of the refugee camp below Vaelmoor's drowned quarter, watching Emrys sleep.

He'd been sleeping for two days. The healers said he should be dead. The healers said a lot of things, in the careful voices people used around her, as if her one eye saw more than their two. Maybe it did. She had spent thirty years reading water, reading weather, reading the small lies people told with their hands—and she had read Emrys Whitlock wrong from the very first night.

No. Not wrong. She had read him exactly right, and that was the wound of it.

She crossed the camp, past cookfires where Kettlehollow's displaced families huddled against the mountain cold, past Wren Ashvale bent over her maps by lantern light, ink-stained fingers copying borders that no longer existed onto paper that might outlast them. The girl looked up as Maren passed, and there it was again—that question in her face, patient as a fisherman. *When are you going to tell him?*

Maren had been asking herself the same thing since the burning valley. Since she'd watched him call down something vast enough to crack the sky, and woken to the knowledge that she could not carry this one more mile.

His tent was canvas scavenged from a Crown supply wagon, still stenciled with the sunburst seal half-scrubbed away. She ducked inside without asking. That was the privilege of old women who owned ferry crossings; you went where you liked and dared anyone to comment.

He was awake. Sitting up against a rolled blanket, gray-faced, hands folded in his lap like a man at his own funeral. His eyes found her and something eased in them—the easing she had spent six years cultivating, the look of a man seeing family.

"Maren." His voice was gravel and river-bottom. "I was starting to think you were avoiding me."

"I don't avoid things. I'm too old and too ugly to bother."

"You're neither."

"Flatterer." She lowered herself onto the crate by the tent's entrance, joints announcing every year of her fifty-three. Outside, someone was singing—one of the old Thornfell lullabies, half-remembered, the words worn smooth like river stones. She listened to it for a moment. Let it steady her.

"I need to tell you something," she said. "And I need you to let me finish before you say anything. Can you do that?"

The ease left his face. He sat straighter, wincing. "You're frightening me."

"Good. Fear will slow your tongue." She pulled off her glove—the left one, the one she wore even in summer, even sleeping—and set it on her knee. Beneath it, the scars ran from wrist to knuckle: burn scars, old and silvered, the marks of iron held too long against flesh. "Do you remember the night you came to Hollow Bridge? Six years back. Autumn. You were half-drowned and full of fever and you told me your name was Tam."

"I remember."

"You told me your name was Tam because you thought I didn't know whose face was under all that mud. But I knew. Everyone on the border knew Whitlock's face by then. The storm-witch who killed forty souls at Millbrook Assize. They'd nailed your likeness to every post from here to the capital." She turned her hand over, let him see the scars plainly in the lantern light. "I knew what I was hiding when I hid you in my cellar. I want that understood first. Whatever else I say tonight, understand that I knew, and I chose, and I would choose it again."

Emrys had gone very still. It was the stillness of prey animals, she thought, or of men who have learned that motion invites the blow. "Then what is it?"

"The inquisitors came eleven days later." Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She had expected it to break. "Seraphine Vale's predecessor—a man called Doss, with soft hands and hard eyes. He came to the bridge with six soldiers and a writ, and he asked me if I'd seen a man matching your description, and I lied to his face. I've lied to Crownsmen my whole life; it's nothing. Smuggling silk is lying with cargo. But Doss didn't believe me, and Doss had ways."

She heard Emrys's breath catch. She kept her eye on the lantern flame rather than his face. Some things were easier said to fire.

"They took me to the tollhouse. Put my hand on the anvil there—you know the one, the old forge anvil from before the flooding, the one we use for mooring chains." A dry sound escaped her, almost a laugh. "Ironic, isn't it. The same iron. They heated a poker in the brazier and they asked me questions, and between each question they pressed it into my palm and closed my fingers over it. And I'll tell you what I learned about myself in that tollhouse, Emrys Whitlock. I learned that I am not brave. I held out through the first two. On the third, I gave you up."

* * *

The silence in the tent was total. Even the wind outside seemed to hold itself.

Maren made herself go on, because she had promised herself she would finish, and because a woman who had failed once should not fail twice in the same night.

"I told them a man named Tam had come through, headed south along the drover's road. I told them he'd paid with southern coin. I described the coat you wore. And they left, and I took my hand out of the brazier-water, and I rode after them—I rode all night, and I caught them at the ford above Gorse Fen, and I told them I'd lied, that I'd said anything to make the pain stop, that there was no Tam. And Doss looked at me with those soft eyes and he believed me. Or he decided I wasn't worth the trouble. Either way, they turned south, away from the bridge, and you lived."

"Why," Emrys said. It wasn't a question so much as a man testing whether his voice still worked. "Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because of how they always found you." She finally made herself look at him. His face was bloodless, but his eyes—his eyes were doing arithmetic. She could see the sums landing behind them, one after another, each one worse than the last. "Think, boy. Every safehouse you ever had in these mountains, every haven, every cellar and cave and croft. How many times did the inquisitors arrive within days? How many times did you slip out by an hour?"

"Kettlehollow," he whispered. "The winter raid. They came within a week of my arriving. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought someone had seen me on the road."

"Someone had. Me. Every time, Emrys. Every time they took someone from the havens—old Sorrel, the Tanners, the widow Brack—they asked about you, and somewhere in the hurting, someone talked. But it started with me. I'm the reason the pattern exists. I taught them that the Coalwater woman breaks. And after me, they knew who else to squeeze." Her jaw ached; she realized she was clenching it. "Six years. Six years of you running, and every run began at my door, with what I gave them at the anvil."

"That's not—" He stopped himself. She watched him wrestle with it, watched the loyal thing in him reach for absolution and the honest thing in him refuse to take it cheaply. That was Emrys. That was the man under the legend, the tired one Wren had seen and Maren had known all along. "You were tortured, Maren. There's no shame in—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her harder than she meant. The lantern flame shivered. "Don't you dare hand me forgiveness like alms at a church door. I've watched you give it away your whole exile—forgave the Crown that burned you, forgave the neighbors who spat, forgave a god who never answered. You forgive so easy it's almost a vice. So no. You don't get to absolve me in the first minute. Sit with it. Sit with what I did, the way I've sat with it for six years, every time the soldiers came and I knew—*knew*—that some part of it traced back to my hand on that poker."

Emrys sat with it. She gave him credit for that. He didn't fill the silence with comfort. He looked at his own hands, and outside the lullaby ended, and somewhere a child laughed, and the mountain wind moved through the camp with the smell of coming frost.

"What happened to the others," he said at last. "Sorrel. The Tanners."

"Sorrel died in the holding cells at Thornmark. The Tanners were released—fined everything they had. Widow Brack took the lash." Maren's voice stayed level. It had cost her years to make it level. "I've paid their families. Quietly, over the years. Ferry tolls forgiven, goods 'lost' in transit that arrived anyway. It doesn't balance anything. Nothing balances. But I owed it, so I paid it."

"And you never told me."

"How?" She spread her scarred hand. "Tell me when, boy. The night you arrived, bleeding and fevered, trusting me with your life? The morning after, when you looked at me like I was the first kind soul you'd met since the trial? The day you carried my nets when my shoulder went bad? The night Tam's son was born and you sat up with me till dawn because I was afraid?" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "There was never a good time to tell a man he owes his survival to my weakness. So I waited for a bad time, and the bad times never stopped coming, and now we're standing in the worst one yet, and I'll not walk into whatever's coming under Vaelmoor carrying this."

* * *

He was quiet a long while. Long enough that she began cataloguing exits—not for herself, but for him. The way a man leaves a room tells you everything about what the room meant to him.

But Emrys Whitlock had never once walked out of a hard room. That was his curse and his grace, both.

"When Doss believed you," he said slowly, "at the ford. You rode all night on a tortured hand to undo what you'd done."

"I did."

"And after. All these years. You fed me, sheltered me, bled for me at Kettlehollow when Vale's soldiers came through. You put yourself between me and a crossbow bolt at Hollow Bridge last spring." His voice was roughening. "One lie under torture, undone the same night, paid for across six years. And you've carried it alone because you loved me too much to make me carry any of it."

"It wasn't love that made me quiet. It was cowardice wearing love's coat."

"No." He said it simply, the way he said true things, without ornament. "It was love. Badly done, badly timed, but love. Maren—" He leaned forward, and winced, and leaned forward anyway, until he could take her scarred hand in both of his. His grip was weak from the valley, from whatever vast thing had poured through him and left him hollowed, but she felt it all the same. "You held a hot iron in your bare hand and then rode through a freezing night to take the lie back. Do you know what most people do? Most people never ride. Most people tell themselves the lie was necessary, that the fugitive was probably guilty anyway, that the Crown must have had its reasons. You rode."

Tears. Damn it all, tears, at her age, in front of the boy. She blinked them back and mostly failed.

"I'm not finished being angry," she managed. "At myself. You don't get to shortcut that either."

"I wouldn't dream of it." And there—there was the ghost of his smile, tired and crooked, the one that had made a whole mountainside of smugglers trust him with their lives. "We can be angry together. I've had practice."

She laughed despite herself, wet and ragged, and gripped his hands back, and for a moment the tent was just a tent and the world was just two stubborn people on a cold mountainside, forgiving each other in installments, the only way forgiveness had ever actually worked.

* * *

Later—much later, when the camp had gone dark except for Wren's lamp and the watchfires—Maren stepped out into the frost-breath night and found Wren Ashvale waiting by the embers of the nearest cookfire, maps weighted down with stones against the wind.

"Well?" the girl asked.

"He knows."

Wren studied her a long moment with those cartographer's eyes, measuring distances, plotting contours. Then she nodded once and rolled up her charts. "Good. He needed to know before the temple. Before the gate." She hesitated, and Maren watched the girl decide to say the next thing. "There's something else. I was checking my father's survey notes against the temple masonry today, the parts the Crown masons haven't torn out yet. There's a chamber beneath the heart-shrine. My father marked it *sealed by treaty*—and the treaty date is the same date Thornfell stopped existing. Same ink, same hand, all the way down the page."

Maren felt the cold settle into her bones, deeper than the night could account for. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the country and the cage were built in the same stroke." Wren looked east, toward the drowned city, where the underwater bells had begun ringing again at dusk—soft, arrhythmic, like a pulse quickening. "And meaning, I think, that whatever the Chancellor wants through that gate, the gods have known about it for three hundred years. They weren't silent, Maren. They were waiting to see if anyone remembered."

Out over Vaelmoor, beneath the black water, a bell tolled—once, deep, deliberate—and did not stop echoing when it should have.

Maren pulled her glove back over her scars and went to wake the others.
