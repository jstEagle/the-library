## 16. The Unwriting

Wren Ashvale learned of the Erasure Edict the way she learned most things: from paper.

It came pinned to the door of the Kettlehollow wayhouse, printed on Crown stock, the seal still sharp enough to cut a thumb. She stood in the grey light of dawn with her father's satchel slung over one shoulder and read it three times, because the first two readings felt like some fever-dream, like the words had rearranged themselves out of spite.

*By order of the Lord Chancellor, in the name of the King: all records, songs, inscriptions, boundary-stones, and other memorials pertaining to the former territory of Thornfell are hereby declared contraband. Possession constitutes sedition. Surrender or destruction to be completed within seven days.*

*Former territory.*

She had grown up on maps. Her father had taught her that a country was never really land — land was just stone and water doing what stone and water did. A country was ink. A country was names written down and agreed upon. And now she stood holding a piece of paper that un-agreed with everything she'd ever drawn, every border line she'd traced with her father's steady hand guiding hers, every village name she'd lettered in careful capitals: *Kettlehollow. Hollow Bridge. The Ninefold Pass. Vaelmoor.*

The paper didn't say *you were never real.* It didn't have to. It said *former*, which was worse, because *former* implied there had been a moment — a date, a signature, a quiet morning in some chancellery office — when Thornfell had stopped existing, and nobody had thought to tell the mountains.

Behind her, Tam Osgood the wayhouse keeper came out with a poker still in his hand from raking the hearth. He read over her shoulder. His breath smelled of woodsmoke and last night's ale.

"They can't do that," he said. "Can they?"

Wren looked at him. Tam had sung the same harvest song his grandmother had sung, the one about the reaping moon and the ninefold fields, off-key, every autumn of his life. He had a carved lintel over his door with the old Thornfell knotwork on it, worn smooth by a hundred years of hands touching it for luck.

"They've done it," she said. "This is them doing it."

* * *

By midmorning the haven was in an uproar.

Emrys stood on the low stone wall by the well, and Wren watched him do something she had never seen him do before: he failed to be calm. Oh, he wore the weariness well enough — he always did, that settled sadness like a coat he'd stopped noticing he wore — but underneath it his voice cracked once, twice, and his hands kept moving, sketching shapes in the air as though he could draw the situation into sense.

"Seven days," he said. "That's not enforcement time. That's not how long they'll give us to comply. That's how long we have."

Maren Coalwater leaned against the well's rim, her one eye narrowed at the edict's copy, which someone had passed hand to hand until it reached her. "To comply with what? There's nothing to surrender. You can't hand over a song."

"You can burn the person who remembers it," Emrys said, and the yard went quiet the way a forest goes quiet when something large moves through it.

Wren felt the truth of it settle into her bones. She had spent the last weeks copying fragments — Brannoc's lullabies, the ferry-chants Maren hummed at Hollow Bridge, the naming-songs for children that old Sefa still sang at births even though the Crown had forbidden them a generation ago. She'd thought of it as scholarship. Her father would have called it cartography of another kind: mapping what the maps wouldn't hold. But the Crown had just told her, in print, with a seal, exactly what her work was worth.

Sedition. Contraband. A hanging offense, if they caught you carrying it.

"We move tonight," Emrys said. "Everything we've gathered — the relics, the copies, the songs — goes to Vaelmoor. Down to the temple. If the gods are waking because their house is being torn down, then their house is the one vault in Thornfell the Chancellor can't burn without burning his own secret with it."

"And the people?" Maren asked quietly.

Emrys looked at her, and Wren saw something pass between them — some old ledger of debts and forgiveness that the rest of them could only guess at. "The people come too," he said. "Anyone who wants to go. We're smugglers. It's finally cargo worth smuggling."

* * *

They came in the night, and they kept coming for days.

Wren worked the eastern trail with Tam and a boy called Pip who couldn't have been more than fourteen and who carried a folded flag against his chest like a second heartbeat. The refugees came down out of the high valleys in ones and twos and straggling families, and each of them brought something, and the something was never what you'd expect.

A farmer named Aldous Crane arrived with a boundary-stone strapped to a handcart, a granite pillar older than any kingdom, its carved runes filled with moss. He'd pried it out of his own wall, he said, the wall his great-grandfather had built around it rather than move it. He'd walked three days with it rattling behind him while the Crown's criers read the Edict in every market square. When Wren asked him why — why risk the rope for a rock — he'd looked at her as if she'd asked why he breathed.

"It knows where my family's buried," he said. "It's been standing on that line since before there were lines. If I let them smash it, then nobody ever knew where we were. Not the dead, not the living. You understand? A man can lose his house. But if the stone goes, we were never *here*."

An old woman came down with nothing but a bundle of linen, and inside the linen were forty years of embroidery: the Thornfell ninefold star worked in thread on tablecloths, on christening gowns, on a funeral shroud she'd made for herself and refused to be buried in until the star could be stitched openly again. A cooper brought the mold for a festival bread no one had baked legally in twenty years. Two brothers carried, between them, a single violin whose body had the old knotwork burned into the belly, hidden under the chinrest where no inspector had ever thought to look.

And songs. Songs everywhere, leaking out of people like water from a cracked jar. In the firelight of the way-stations, Wren sat with her journal open and wrote until her fingers cramped: the reaping song from Tam; a boat-launching chant from a fisherman's widow; a counting rhyme children used to sing skipping stones across the Hollow, each verse naming one of the nine old thanes. Half the singers apologized as they sang. *I don't remember it right. My mother knew it better. There's a verse I've lost.* And Wren wrote down the broken versions too, because her father had taught her that a map with gaps was still a map, and sometimes the gaps told you where to dig.

On the third night, sitting by the fire with her ink-stained fingers aching, she looked up and found Emrys watching her work. He had a mug of something hot going cold in his hands and that expression he wore when he thought no one could see him — the one that wasn't sad exactly, but was adjacent to sad, the way dusk is adjacent to night.

"What?" she said.

"You're doing what I couldn't," he said. "For years I thought surviving meant keeping your head down and your name small. You're out here making yourself the biggest target in Thornfell, and for what? Paper."

"For what?" Wren set down her pen. She was tired, and cold, and the enormity of the week had gotten under her skin like grit. "Emrys. They're not destroying records. Records are copies. They're destroying *proof*. Proof that this place was ever anything but a line on their map. My father charted Vaelmoor street by street before the flooding. Do you know what happened to his originals? Burned in the Assizes purges, along with half the archives. Everything I carry, I copied from memory, from what he taught me before he died. I am the only copy left of some of these streets." She held up her journal. "This isn't paper. This is the country. This is what's left of it that fits in a bag."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere up the trail, a child was singing the thane-counting rhyme wrong, and her mother was correcting her gently, verse by verse, like handing down a lamp.

"When this is over," Emrys said at last, "whatever happens at Vaelmoor — you should know that what you're doing matters more than what I'm doing. Storms end. Gates close. But you—" he nodded at the journal, at the whole ragged caravan of memory camped around them, "—you're building the thing that outlasts all of us."

Wren didn't trust herself to answer. She bent back over the page and wrote faster, because he was wrong, of course — he was the doorwarden, he was the one the old god had chosen, he was the storm given legs — but it was kind of him to pretend otherwise, and kindness, she was learning, was also contraband now. Worth smuggling. Worth the rope.

* * *

On the fifth day, the Crown's riders came to the high passes, and the smuggling changed.

Before, the danger had been weather and patrols, the ordinary hazards of the trade. Now there were bonfires visible from the ridgelines — the Crown's crews burning confiscated prayer-scraps and songbooks in the valley towns, and the smoke rose straight in the windless air, black columns like accusations written on the sky. Wren counted six of them from the shoulder of Wolfjaw Rise and felt something in her chest turn from fear to fury, clean and cold as spring water.

"They're not even being subtle," Pip whispered beside her. He had the flag out, pressed to his chest, as if the smoke might take it. "Everyone can see."

"That's the point," Wren said. "A quiet ban is a law. This is theater. They want us to watch." She remembered something her father had said once, ink on his fingers, eyes far away: *When a powerful man burns books in public, he isn't destroying knowledge. He's advertising fear.* She'd been nine. She hadn't understood. She understood now.

That evening, Maren gathered the couriers at the last way-station before the Vaelmoor flats. She stood with her back to the fire so her shadow fell long across the assembled smugglers — thirty-odd of them, road-worn, cargo-laden, carrying linens and stones and songs — and delivered the briefing like the veteran of a hundred runs that she was.

"New rules," she said. "Nothing travels alone anymore. Pairs minimum, armed pairs preferred. The roads south of Hollow Bridge are crawling with inspectors, and the penalty's no longer confiscation — it's the rope, or the labor gangs if you're young enough to dig." Her eye found Pip, and softened by a degree only Wren noticed. "You don't have to carry what you carry, boy. No shame in passing it to someone else."

Pip's jaw set. "My gran sewed that flag. She hid it through two inspections and a marriage. I'll carry it into the ground before I hand it off."

Maren held his gaze a moment, then nodded once, the nod of one soldier acknowledging another. "Then hear the route. We go down the drowned causeway in three groups, spaced an hour apart. Wren leads — she has her father's charts, and the water's tricks change with the season. Emrys takes the rear with the relic-carts. We deliver everything to the temple mouth, and then—" she paused, and Wren saw her choose her words with care, "—and then we see what the doorwarden says next."

Afterward, packing her charts by lantern-light, Wren heard Emrys and Maren talking low by the horses. She didn't mean to listen. Their voices simply carried in the cold.

"The labor gangs," Emrys was saying. "How many do they have down there now?"

"Last count, four hundred. Conscripts from every haven between here and the coast. Digging day and night." A pause. "You're thinking about them."

"I'm thinking the Chancellor built himself a perfect trap," Emrys said. "Every person in Thornfell who remembers is walking toward Vaelmoor on their own feet. Every relic, every song, every witness, gathering in one place beneath his army's nose. He doesn't have to hunt us anymore, Maren. He just has to wait, and then shut the lid."

Wren's pen stopped moving.

But Maren's answer came slow and sure, and it was the answer Wren would carry down the drowned causeway with her, along with the flag and the songs and the stone that knew where the dead lay buried:

"Aye. Or maybe he's built himself something else. Maybe he's taken a scattered country and handed it a reason to stand in one place together. Chancellors aren't the only ones who can plan, Emrys Whitlock. Your god chose a doorwarden. But somebody had to choose the people too. Looks to me like that's happening whether he likes it or not."

* * *

They went down to the flats on the seventh morning, the last morning, the day the Edict took full force.

Wren led them onto the drowned causeway as the sun rose red behind them, and Vaelmoor opened ahead like a wound in the world — rooftops breaking the grey water, bell-towers leaning, the whole drowned city waiting with its bells ringing somewhere far below, a sound like grief practiced for three hundred years until it became music.

The refugees moved along the causeway in silence. Aldous Crane's boundary-stone rattled on its cart. Old Sefa was singing, very softly, the birth-naming song, and others picked it up verse by verse as they walked, until thirty voices carried it out over the water, and the water seemed to listen.

Wren walked at the front with her father's charts in her arms and her journal against her ribs, and she understood, finally, what she had become. Not a mapmaker. Not a scholar. A courier. The whole ragged column behind her — farmers and coopers and widows and one stubborn boy with a flag — they were all couriers now, running the last shipment of a country through enemy territory, delivering it to the only warehouse left that the Crown couldn't burn.

Because you could burn paper. You could smash stone. You could outlaw songs and hang singers.

But the Edict had made one fatal error, and Wren saw it now, clear as a contour line on a chart: it had given everyone a deadline. Seven days. And deadlines make people choose. All across Thornfell, in kitchens and barns and hidden cellars, people were choosing — pulling flags out of chests, prying stones out of walls, teaching children verses in whispers — and choosing, overwhelmingly, to remember.

Ahead, the temple entrance waited below the waterline, defaced by Crown masons, stained with blood the masons had tried and failed to wash away. Behind, on the ridgeline, Wren turned once and looked back north.

The bonfires were still burning in the valley towns. Six black columns of smoke against the morning sky.

And on the road behind them, as far as the eye could follow the winding trail down from the high passes, more refugees were coming. Dozens. Then scores. Carrying whatever memory they could hold.

"Hurry," Wren said quietly, to no one, to everyone, to the drowned city and the god beneath it. "We're coming. Bring everything."
