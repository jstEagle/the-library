## 22. The Mapmaker's Last Page

The ink was the color of deep water, and Wren Ashvale had mixed it herself—from oak galls gathered in the Thornfell passes and rain caught in a copper kettle on the roof of Maren Coalwater's ferry-house. She had learned, over two years of work, that everything worth making in Kettlehollow was made from what the mountains gave up grudgingly: ink from galls, paper from rag pulp pressed in the old mill, light from tallow and pine knots because candles were still taxed in some places and smuggled everywhere else.

She sat at the wide table by the window of the map-room—formerly a goat shed, formerly a smokehouse, formerly nothing at all until they had put a roof on it—and looked at the last page.

It was not the largest page. That honor belonged to the great survey of Vaelmoor, drawn from her father's drowned charts and Emrys's memory of streets he'd waded with water to his ribs, bells tolling somewhere beneath his boots. It was not the most beautiful; that was the star-chart of the Spire Gate, which she had rendered in silver leaf so that when you tilted it toward firelight, the gate itself seemed to open. It was not even the most important, if importance meant the pages that had mattered when it mattered—the route-maps that had carried three hundred refugees through the Erasure Edict's week of burning, each one annotated in her own hand: *safe at the third cairn*, *the ford lies here*, *remember the lullaby, sing it low*.

No. The last page was small. It was a single sheet, half the size of the others, and it showed a country.

Not the Thornfell Marches—that was what the Crown's new maps called it, in their new ink, with their new borders drawn in offices far away by men who had never been cold at altitude. Not the Protectorate of the Northern Reaches, which is what an earlier Crown had called it after the treaty, three centuries back, when a country was erased with a signature and everyone agreed to forget. And not the nameless blank space that had appeared on every official map for three hundred years, as though the mountains themselves were too shameful to name.

The last page showed **Thornfell**. The whole of it. Every valley, every pass, every shrine-stone and border-marker and buried bell-tower she and her father and a hundred other rememberers had charted or recalled or sung back into being. She had drawn the coastline of a nation that existed nowhere in law, and she had written its name across it in letters like standing stones:

*THORNFELL.*

Below it, smaller, in the careful script her father had taught her before the flood took him and his charts and half the city of Vaelmoor: *A country is not its king. A country is what its people refuse to forget.*

Her father had never said that. Her father had said things about meridians and scale bars and the proper way to render a river's braided mouth. But he had spent his life mapping a drowned city for a Crown that wanted it forgotten, and Wren thought that if he could have seen this atlas—*The Ashvale Atlas of Thornfell, Being a Complete Remembrance of the Nation, Its Borders, Roads, Songs, Shrines, and People*—he would have understood. He would have wept, probably, quietly, the way he did over beautiful things, and then he would have checked her scale bars and found one off by a finger's width and made her fix it before allowing himself any pride.

She checked the scale bars now. All correct. She had checked them eleven times.

Outside the window, the first snow of the season was coming down over Kettlehollow in fat, unhurried flakes, and the smell of woodsmoke and baking bread rose from the haven below, and someone—she could hear them, faintly—was singing.

Wren capped the ink, set down the pen, and let herself cry a little, the way cartographers do: efficiently, without smudging anything.

* * *

Emrys Whitlock taught the children the old songs on Thursday mornings, because Thursday had been market day in old Thornfell and market days began with the Peddler's Blessing, and because Thursdays were otherwise the day the bread ovens fired and nobody needed him underfoot.

He stood in the common house with its new roof beams—Seraphine had insisted on paying for those, out of the pension the shaken Crown still grudgingly sent her, and had refused all discussion of the matter—and twenty-three children sat cross-legged on the floorboards looking up at him with the particular ferocity of children who have decided a thing matters because an adult told them it does.

"From the top," Emrys said. "And mind the third verse. The third verse is where your grandmothers hid the words."

He did not sing well. Everyone knew this. His voice had gone rough after the storm in the burning valley, after whatever vast thing had poured through him and left him emptied—a man-shaped flask with nothing in it anymore. He could not call fog. He could not call wind. He had stood on the shore at Vaelmoor with his hand raised toward a closing gate and felt the last of it go out of him like heat leaving a stone, and he had said afterward, when Maren asked him how it felt, only: *"Lighter."*

But he knew all the verses. He had spent months collecting them, before the gate, walking tavern to tavern and farm to farm with a notebook, asking people to hum what their mothers had hummed. And now he gave them away freely, every Thursday, to children who would carry them into a century he would not see.

"*Oh, the peddler comes down from the high white fell,*" the children sang, wavering, off-key, glorious. "*With pins for the ladies and bells for the—*"

"Bells for the *kirk*," Emrys corrected gently. "Not the church. The kirk. It's an old word. It means the same thing, but it's *ours*."

"*Bells for the kirk and wool for the mill,*" they sang, and Wren, who had come down from the map-room with cold fingers and ink on her cheek, stopped in the doorway and listened.

In the corner, Maren Coalwater sat mending nets with her one eye and her good hands, pretending not to listen, which was how Maren listened to anything that moved her. Her son Tam—back from Vaelmoor, thin for a year and then less thin, and now broad-shouldered and loud and forever first in line at every meal—was supposed to be splitting wood but had drifted to the window to listen too, axe forgotten against the wall.

On the bench nearest the fire sat Seraphine Vale, former Inquisitor of the Crown, current resident, current taxpayer-in-absentia, current woman who had burned her name and career in a mountain pass two years ago and never once spoken of it unless asked directly, and then only briefly. She was teaching herself to knit. She was terrible at it. The scarf she was producing resembled something a spider might make while having a bad night. She wore it anyway.

"And the fourth verse," Emrys was saying, "is the one they tried hardest to burn. So we sing it loudest."

"*Though kings may draw lines upon paper pale,*" sang twenty-three children at full volume, startling a cat off the windowsill, "*no line ever held the wind in the vale—*"

Wren leaned against the doorframe and thought: *this is what I mapped.* Not the passes and the shrines. This. The sound of a country refusing, in small voices, in a room that smelled of bread, to be dead.

* * *

Afterward, when the children had scattered into the snow like thrown seed, Emrys found her in the doorway.

"You heard the third verse," he said. It wasn't a question. He had a way of knowing things now—not magic, just attention, the kind of attention a man develops when the loud power leaves and only the quiet senses remain. "You've got your listening face on."

"I always have my listening face on."

"You have two faces. One for listening and one for pretending you weren't." He nodded at her ink-stained hands. "Is it done?"

Wren looked out at the snow for a moment. "Come see."

They climbed together to the map-room, two figures leaving bootprints side by side, and inside she lit the lamps and turned back the cloth that covered the finished atlas, and watched his face as he took it in.

He was quiet a long time. Snow ticked softly against the window. Down in the haven, someone was laughing, and the ovens breathed their warmth into the afternoon.

"There it is," he said finally, and his voice was not entirely steady. "All of it."

"All of it. Eleven years of other people's memories and four years of my father's charts and two years of my life." She touched the title page lightly, afraid even now to smudge it. "There are going to be forty copies. I've found scribes in three towns who'll do it secretly—real ones, trained hands. And the master stays here, in Kettlehollow, where no one will think to look for treasure in a goat shed."

"Where did you learn to hide things this well?"

"From a smuggler." She glanced up at him. "Terrible influence. Exiled storm-caller. You may have heard of him—he's in my atlas, actually. Page thirty-one. The pass he opened during the Edict is marked there. I named it."

"What did you name it?"

"Whitlock's Gate." She said it before he could object, and saw the objection forming, and kept talking. "It's not about you. That's the thing I finally understand about maps, Emrys. You don't get to choose what things are named after you. The country chooses. It chose you the night you walked into a burning valley and didn't come out the same man. The least I can do is write it down straight."

Emrys looked at the page again—at the whole shape of Thornfell, whole and named and *his*, in the way a homeland belongs to everyone who remembers it—and Wren saw him swallow whatever large feeling had risen in him, the way men of his generation did, and then decide, visibly, not to.

"My mother used to sing the Peddler's Blessing," he said. "Before. Before the trial, before everything. I'd forgotten I remembered it until I started collecting the songs." He shook his head slowly. "Three hundred years the Crown worked at erasing this place, and it turns out all it took to bring it back was a mapmaker, a ferrywoman, a disgraced inquisitor, and a smuggler who can't even call fog anymore."

"And twenty-three children," Wren said, "who now know the fourth verse."

"And twenty-three children." He smiled—the real one, the rare one, the one that reached all the way up and made him look, for a moment, like the young man the trial records described rather than the tired one the exile had made. "Thank you, Wren. For all of it. For coming south with me when I offered you safe passage and gave you a war instead."

"You gave me a country," she said. "I consider the trade fair."

* * *

That evening the whole haven crowded into the common house, because word had traveled, the way it did in Kettlehollow—fast, sideways, through kitchen doors and net-lofts—and because Maren had declared that a finished atlas was a thing you feasted for, and Maren's declarations had the force of law in the haven and everyone knew it.

There was roast goat and barley stew and the blackberry wine Tam had brewed that tasted like punishment but was drunk with enthusiasm anyway. Seraphine wore her terrible scarf. Someone had produced a fiddle, and someone else claimed to know how to play it, and both claims proved partially true. The children performed the Peddler's Blessing for the assembled company and were applauded as though they had invented music.

Late, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and most of the haven had gone home to sleep, Wren sat with the atlas open on her knees and read aloud—not the routes and borders, but the marginalia, the small human notes she had collected alongside the geography. *Here stands the widow Aldith's flag, folded in a cedar chest since the treaty; she says it smells of her grandmother's lavender.* *Here the farmer Brack keeps a border-stone in his east wall and will tell you it is a foundation stone if you ask and a border-stone if you don't.* *Here the tavern keeper Haldo sings the Lullaby of the Long Watch and cries at the second line every time without exception.*

Maren listened with her eye closed. Emrys sat beside her, their shoulders touching, his hand resting near hers on the bench—not holding it, just near, the way they had been near each other ever since the light died at the gate and he had gripped her hand and chosen, without hesitation, to become an ordinary man.

"Read the last note," Maren said. "The very last one. On the last page."

Wren turned to it. Below the name THORNFELL, below her father's words about refusal and forgetting, she had written one more line, small, in the corner, where only someone reading closely would find it:

*This country survived in the keeping of smugglers, refugees, widows, liars, traitors, exiles, and children. Which is to say: it survived in the keeping of its people. May it never again require better ones.*

Silence, the good kind. Snow against the window. The fire settling.

"Well," Maren said at last, gruff, wiping her eye with the heel of her hand and blaming the smoke, which fooled no one. "I suppose that's that."

"That's that," Wren agreed.

But Emrys was looking at the window—at the snow, and past the snow, toward the dark line of the mountains where Vaelmoor lay drowned and quiet and sealed, where a gate stood closed behind departing gods, where bells perhaps still rang underwater for no one.

"No," he said softly. "That's the last page of the atlas." He turned back to them, and the firelight caught the scar the storm had left along his jaw, and he was smiling. "But countries aren't books, Wren. They don't end. They just keep getting written by whoever picks up the pen next." He nodded toward the common room door, beyond which the children's laughter still echoed faintly from the lanes, fading into the falling snow. "And I happen to know at least twenty-three of them already know the fourth verse."

Outside, the snow went on falling over Thornfell—over the passes and the shrines and the buried bells, over the haven and the ferry-house and the goat-shed map-room—settling soft and deep on a country that no empire had managed to bury, covering it the way a blanket covers a sleeping child: gently, completely, and only until morning.
