## 1. The Last Tollhouse

There are three hundred and eleven roads through the Thornfell mountains, if you count the ones the guild pretends don't exist, and I have catalogued every one of them twice.

The second time was unnecessary. Nothing had changed. That's rather the point of Thornfell — stone doesn't revise itself, and neither, apparently, does Guildmaster Corvin, who has told me the same four stories about our order's history so many times I could recite them in my sleep, and has never once answered a single question I've actually asked.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Mornings at the tollhouse begin with the ledger, and the ledger begins with me pretending anyone will ever read it again.

* * *

The Wayfarers' Guild tollhouse sits at the mouth of the Kestrel Pass like an old dog left behind by a caravan that isn't coming back. It was built to house twelve wayfarers, a quartermaster, a mapwright, and a small army of porters. It currently houses one apprentice — me — and one dying guildmaster, who occupies the east room and emits a coughing that sounds like the mountain clearing its throat before it decides whether to avalanche.

I lit the stove, put water on for tea, and opened the master ledger to a fresh page. The ink was old but serviceable; the paper was good rag stock, bought decades ago in quantities that suggested someone once believed the future would require a great deal of writing. Someone had been optimistic. I try not to hold it against them.

*Route 44, Shepherd's Cut,* I wrote. *Status: passable. Last traveled: fourteen months ago, goatherd, name unrecorded.* Then, because no one was watching and honesty costs nothing on paper: *Likely never traveled again.*

Three hundred and eleven entries like that. Some days I think of my work as preservation. Most days I know it for what it is: a man talking to himself with better penmanship.

Here is what you should understand about the Wayfarers' Guild, or what remains of it, which is to say what remains is me and a cough. Once, we were the arteries of these mountains. Every smuggler's path, every shepherd's scramble, every cliff-cut stair that official maps pretended was a landslide — we knew them, we kept them, we guided those who needed moving quietly from one side of Thornfell to the other. Kings paid us. Priests paid us. People with no names at all paid us best, because they paid in desperation, and desperation has always been the most reliable currency.

Now there is a tollhouse with a sagging roof, a ledger nobody reads, and a guildmaster who answers questions about the guild's past the way a locked door answers knocking.

I finished Route 44, blotted it, and went out to check the guideposts.

* * *

There are forty-one guideposts between the tollhouse and the tree line, and I maintain all of them, which means I walk the lower pass nearly every day chiseling fresh notches into wood that weather wants to erase. The notches are how wayfarers mark safe footing — two cuts for firm ground, three for ice, a spiral for *turn back, fool*. My hands knew the work so well I could do it while thinking about other things, which was fortunate, because my hands were often the only part of me interested in being useful.

It was cold enough that morning to make the chisel sing. I'd cleared nine posts when I heard the bell.

The toll-bell hangs over the pass road, and in the old days it rang for travelers paying their fee — a copper for foot traffic, silver for carts, and for certain clients, nothing at all, because the guild's real business was conducted off the books entirely. The bell hadn't rung in so long that the first time I heard it after taking over its care, I'd assumed the wind. This time I counted the swings. Wind doesn't ring a bell in clean threes.

Someone was coming down the pass.

I stood very still, which is the first thing they teach you — though "they teach you" is generous. Corvin taught me. He taught me the paths, the knots, the signs, the seven ways to cross a frozen river without drowning, and precisely nothing about why a guild that once numbered in the hundreds now fit inside one bad-tempered old man and his reluctant apprentice. When I asked, he looked at me with those gray river-stone eyes and said, *The past is a road with no footing, boy. Don't walk it.*

Which is a beautiful sentence, and also a non-answer, and I had begun to suspect it was both of those things deliberately.

The traveler came around the switchback below me: a tinker, by his pack, driving a mule with the resigned expression of an animal that has been lied to about the length of this journey. Ordinary. Unremarkable. I felt something in my chest loosen that I refused to call disappointment, because calling it disappointment would mean admitting I'd hoped, and hoping that a stranger might be a client — might be *anyone*, might want *anything* from the last wayfarer in Thornfell — was a humiliation I preferred to keep private even from myself.

"Toll's a copper," I called down, because the ritual matters. Rituals are what's left when everything else leaves.

The tinker fished the coin from his coat, flipped it up to me, and asked if the high road to Marrowdale was open. I told him the high road was open to fools and corpses, and the low road was open to everyone else. He laughed, took the low road, and was gone by midmorning, and the bell hung silent again, and I stood there holding a copper coin worth exactly one copper coin, feeling like a museum exhibit that had briefly mistaken itself for a working institution.

That was the whole of my human contact for the day. Some weeks it was the whole of it, period.

* * *

Corvin was worse in the afternoons. The cough had moved into something deeper, a wet rattle that he treated as a personal failing rather than a medical event. I brought him tea and the day's tally — one tinker, one copper — and found him propped against his pillows with the old guild seal in his hands, turning it over and over like a man trying to find the trick of it.

The seal was bronze, worn nearly smooth, stamped with the guild's sign: a road splitting around a mountain peak. There used to be dozens of them. There was one now, and he held it the way misers hold coins and fathers hold photographs, which is to say I couldn't tell which it was to him, and he wasn't saying.

"One tinker," I reported. "Low road."

"Mm." He didn't look up. "Shepherd's Cut still holds?"

"Ice bridge over the Kestrel gorge is thinning. I marked it triple-notch yesterday."

"Good." A pause. A rattle of breath. "You'll want to re-mark it after the next snow."

"Yes, Master." I set the tea down. And then — because I am apparently incapable of learning — I said, "Master, the ledger's nearly full. When I start the new volume, I thought perhaps I'd copy over the founding records too. The charter pages. The—"

"No."

He said it fast, faster than a dying man should be able to say anything, and his hands went still on the seal.

"The charter pages are lost," he said. "Flood. Years ago. Before your time."

"They're not lost. They're in the sealed cabinet in the map room. Third drawer. I dusted it last month." I kept my voice level, which cost effort. "I wasn't asking permission to read them, Master. I was asking whether you'd rather copy them yourself, while you still—"

"*Boy.*"

One word, and the temperature of the room dropped ten degrees. Corvin pushed himself upright, and for a moment the illness fell away and I saw the man he must have been on the roads: hard, quick, absolutely certain. "Some doors are locked because what's behind them is heavy. You lift it wrong, it falls on you. It falls on whoever's standing near you." His eyes were wet — from the coughing, I told myself. From the coughing. "Catalogue your roads. Mark your posts. That's the guild now. That's enough."

"That's a graveyard with good record-keeping," I said.

The silence that followed had texture. I stood in it and hated myself a little, the way you do after saying the true thing cruelly, and hated him more, the way you do when the true thing doesn't even land — when it just sinks into him without changing anything, the way rain sinks into stone.

"Tea's going cold," he said finally, and turned his face to the wall, and that was the end of the conversation, as it was the end of every conversation that strayed more than twenty years into the past. Twenty years. Everything before twenty years ago was flood-lost, burned, buried, irrelevant, *heavy*. Everything before twenty years ago was—

Twenty years ago was the year I was born.

I had noticed that arithmetic before. I noticed it again now, the way you notice a loose tooth: with your tongue, absently, telling yourself it's nothing.

* * *

Evenings, I worked the maps.

The map room was the tollhouse's heart, once — long tables, good lamplight, space for a dozen mapwrights bent over their drafting. Now it was one table, one lamp, and me, correcting charts of roads whose only remaining traffic was weather. I worked through the western passes first, then the southern scree, updating distances, noting rockfalls. Competent work. Quiet work. Work that filled the hours so thoroughly that I could almost believe filling hours was the same as filling a life.

Somewhere past midnight, I sat back and looked at what I'd done, and did the thing I always did, the thing I'd stopped admitting to myself was strange: I counted the roads.

Three hundred and eleven. And every chart, every ledger, every scrap of guild record I'd ever touched covered maybe two hundred of them. The rest existed only in the oldest survey notes, in fragments, in margins — roads the guild had known and then, somewhere in the last century, quietly stopped knowing. Stopped marking. Stopped teaching. As if the guild had once been a great book and someone had torn out chapters, and Corvin was the surviving page, and he had decided the missing chapters were none of my business.

One road in particular. There was a gap in the deep surveys, south and down, beneath the roots of the Spire itself, where the oldest notes simply stopped mid-sentence — a route description that broke off after the words *descend by the smelter stair to the* — and then nothing. Not torn away. Not burned. Just *stopped*, like a man walking who suddenly refuses to describe where he's walking to.

I'd asked Corvin about it once, years ago, young enough to ask anything. He'd gone white. Not annoyed — *white*, the color of a man seeing a ghost, and he'd said, "Where did you read that?" in a voice I'd never heard before or since, and I'd shown him the fragment, and he'd taken it, and I never saw it again.

I was twelve. I'd filed it under *old men are strange* and moved on.

I was twenty now, and alone in a lamp-lit room with three hundred and eleven roads and a master who was dying, and I found I could no longer file it anywhere. Because here was the thing about cataloguing dead roads: you spend enough years recording what no longer exists, and you start to develop a professional sensitivity to erasure. You learn what it looks like. It looks like a gap where a sentence should finish. It looks like a charter page in a locked drawer. It looks like a man holding a seal he won't explain, coughing toward a wall, choosing silence one afternoon at a time until the silence is all that's left to inherit.

The lamp guttered. Outside, wind came down the Kestrel Pass and made the toll-bell mutter in its sleep, half-ringing, a sound like a word almost spoken.

I blew out the light and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the mountain breathe around the tollhouse the way it had breathed around it for three hundred years, indifferent and immense. Somewhere in that dark were roads I knew and roads I'd mapped and roads somebody didn't want known, and I thought — not for the first time, but for the first time with any force — that a guild which survives by forgetting isn't a guild at all. It's just a man and a boy in a big empty house, keeping books for ghosts.

I went to bed. Sleep came slowly and left early.

And in the gray hour before dawn, when the frost was still on the windows and the stove had gone cold, I heard the bell ring again — three clean swings, a traveler's toll — except that no traveler could have reached the pass road by dawn from anywhere at all, and when I got to the window with the lamp, the high switchback above the tollhouse stood empty against the paling sky.

Except it didn't stay empty.

Something was coming down it. Something moving wrong — stumbling, lurching, catching itself on the rock and coming on anyway — and behind it, far up the switchback where the dawn hadn't reached yet, the shadows seemed to be detaching themselves from the mountainside, drifting down the path like ash off a dying fire.

The bell swung a fourth time. Nobody had touched it.
