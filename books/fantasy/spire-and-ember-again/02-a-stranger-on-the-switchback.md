## 2. A Stranger on the Switchback

There are three rules to the high paths of Thornfell, and I had followed all of them for eleven years without once breaking my stride.

One: never be seen unless being seen is the job. Two: never take what isn't yours, because everything on a smuggler's road belongs to someone with longer knives than you. Three: when trouble comes over the ridge, get small, get quiet, and let it pass.

I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that I was following rule three to the letter when the stranger found me.

It was late afternoon, that hour in the mountains when the light goes long and copper and every shadow looks like it's hiding something. I was coming down the switchbacks above Hollowmere with a pack of untaxed brandy and a head full of nothing much—counting my steps, mostly, which is what I do instead of thinking. Forty-one steps down, turn. Forty-two up the far side, turn. The path there is cut into the cliff face like a scar, narrow enough that a mule has to walk it sideways, with a drop on your left that goes down far enough to make even guild-bred legs thoughtful.

That's why I heard him before I saw him. Sound carries strangely on the switchbacks; it comes around the bends ahead of the thing making it.

What came around the bend was a man's breathing.

Not walking-breathing. Not climbing-breathing. This was the wet, torn breathing of a man whose lungs have been introduced to fire, and who has declined the introduction but can't leave the room. I did what any sensible Wayfarer would do: I stepped into the shadow of an overhang, made myself part of the rock, and waited for him to pass.

He didn't pass.

He came around the turn at a stagger, one hand out for the cliff wall and missing it, and I saw that he'd been burned. Not scorched—*burned*, the way wood is burned, deep and black along his left side, his coat fused to the meat beneath it. His face on that side had gone smooth as wax. His right eye found me anyway, found me standing in my shadow like a coward, and something in it lit up with a relief so naked it embarrassed us both.

"You're Guild," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "The badge. You're Guild."

I wasn't wearing a badge. Nobody wears badges anymore; badges are for guilds that still exist. But I had the tollhouse key on a cord at my neck, and the old iron stamp sewn into my collar where Master Corvin insisted it stay, and I suppose a dying man's eyes will find whatever they're looking for.

"I'm nobody," I said, which was rule one, spoken aloud.

"You're *Guild*." He took another step and his knee buckled and he caught himself on the rock with a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be. "Then listen. Listen, because I don't have—" He coughed, and the cough had ash in it, actual ash, gray flecks on his lips. "They're behind me. They don't stop. They don't eat, they don't sleep, they just *come*."

"Who comes?"

He laughed then, or made the shape of a laugh, and it was the worst sound of the whole bad afternoon. "You don't know. Gods. Eleven years I've run roads and you don't know." He fumbled at his shoulder, at the strap of a satchel riding under his ruined coat, and dragged it around front with fingers that were more scar than skin. "Take it."

"No," I said.

Rule two. Never take what isn't yours. And this—whatever this was—was so obviously somebody's that taking it felt like picking up a live coal. It was a plain courier's satchel, oilcloth over leather, the kind every runner on every mountain uses, except that the buckles were stamped with a sigil I didn't know: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a door standing open in a wall.

"Take it *off the mountain*," he said. "South. Past the low villages, past the river, keep going until someone tells you to stop and then go further. There's a name inside. A debt. It has to reach—" He stopped. His good eye went past my shoulder, up the switchback behind me, and every bit of relief drained out of his face as if someone had pulled a plug.

I didn't look. That's the part I keep returning to, nights when sleep won't come. Rule three said don't look, stay small, let trouble pass, and I obeyed it while a man died six feet from me asking for help.

"Please," he said, quieter now. He held the satchel out across the space between us, arm shaking. "I'm not asking you to fight them. I'm asking you to carry. That's all a courier ever does. Carry what matters farther than he can."

"I'm sorry," I said. And I meant it, which somehow makes it worse. "I can't help you."

He looked at me for a long moment. The wind came up the valley and moved his hair, which was half gone, and moved mine, and between us the satchel swayed on its strap like something hanged.

"They told me a Wayfarer would help," he said. "An old man in the lowlands. He said find the Guild, they remember the roads, they remember *everything*. He said it like it was a good thing." His voice cracked. "You don't even know what you are, do you?"

And then he turned—not away from me, exactly, but toward the drop, toward the valley—and sat himself down against the rock with the satchel in his lap, like a man settling in by a fire, and began unbuckling it.

"Don't," I said. Foolishness. What did I care what was in it?

"Somebody should know," he said. "If it dies with me, it worked. Do you understand? If it dies here, they *win*, and they've been winning for—" He got the first buckle open. Inside, I saw paper. Charts. And something else, something that gave off a faint warmth-light, like a banked coal wrapped in linen.

That's when I looked up the path.

I'll tell you what I saw, though words sit badly on it. Figures. Three of them, maybe four—the number kept sliding, the way a number slides when you look at falling snow. They stood on the switchback above us, where the light still reached, and they were made of ash. Not wearing ash, not dusted with it—*made* of it, gray drifting shapes in the rough suggestion of men, their edges constantly sloughing off and reforming, little clouds of themselves raining down the cliff face below their feet. Where their faces should have been there was only a smoother density, like a thumbprint pressed into smoke.

They weren't running. That was the terrible part. They had all the urgency of weather.

"Go," the courier whispered. "Go now, boy, they only want—"

The nearest one stepped off the edge of the path.

It didn't fall. It poured itself down the rock face like water finding a channel, ash streaming, and reassembled on our level ten feet from the dying man, and stood there. Waiting. The way a creditor waits.

The courier saw it too. He closed the buckle he'd opened. And then he did the last thing I expected, the thing I think about most: he smiled at me. At *me*, the coward in the shadow.

"Not your fault," he said. "Habit, right? You're taught not to touch. I had a partner like that once. Good woman. Terrible at goodbyes." He patted the satchel. "It'll wait. It's patient. It's the only thing about this business that is."

The ash-thing drifted forward. Where it passed over the stone, the stone went gray. Not burned—grayed, colorless, like the world forgetting that particular patch of rock. The courier watched it come with his one working eye and his breathing got worse and I understood suddenly that it wasn't the burns killing him, or not only. Something was being drawn out of him, slow as thread off a spool, and the nearer the thing came, the faster the spool unwound.

"Name," he said, and now his voice was thinning, going distant, a man calling from the far end of a tunnel. "My name is Aldous Vane. Remember that. Somebody has to remember—"

The ash reached him.

I won't dress it up. It flowed over him like fog over a grave, and where it touched him he grayed the way the rock had grayed, color leaving him in a slow tide, and the last thing to go was his right eye, which stayed fixed on me the whole time. Not accusing. That's the worst of it. He looked at me the way you look at weather, at something vast and indifferent that has simply happened to you, and then the eye went pale and then it went gray and then Aldous Vane was a shape of ash sitting against the rock, holding a satchel in a lap that no longer existed.

The figures turned. All of them, at once, the way birds turn.

They looked at me—or oriented toward me; I refuse to say looked—and for a long moment nothing in the world moved except the wind and the slow rain of ash off their shoulders.

Then the nearest one raised its hand. Not fast. Deliberate. And pointed—at me? at the satchel? at the space between us where a refusal had happened?—and held there, an accusation written in smoke.

Rule three said run. Rule three had kept me alive eleven years, and I broke it for the first time in my life, because guilt is faster than fear and it grabbed my legs before fear could vote.

I crossed the path. I knelt by what remained of Aldous Vane. The ash-things had stopped moving entirely, all of them, watching, and I remember thinking with absurd clarity: *they're waiting to see what I do. This is a test. Whose?*

The satchel was warm. That's the detail that undid me, later—the simple animal warmth of it, like a sleeping bird. Everything else about its owner had gone to gray dust, but the satchel sat there in the ruin of him, buckles bright, sigil sharp, warm as bread.

"Take it," I said aloud, to no one, to him. "All right. I'm taking it. Are you happy?"

Nothing answered, which I deserved.

I stood, satchel in both hands, and faced the ash-things down the length of the switchback, and I said the only true thing I had: "I don't know what this is. But it's mine now. Whatever that costs."

The nearest figure tilted its smooth face, considering. Then, without hurry, without malice, it turned and poured itself back up the cliff, and the others followed, streaming away up the switchbacks like smoke reversing, until the copper light took them and they were gone.

I stood alone on the high path with a dead man's bag and the beginning of a headache, and I told myself the obvious lie: *they're done with me. It wanted him. It got him.*

Below me, the valley was turning gold and blue with evening, Hollowmere's chimneys starting to smoke, ordinary as bread. Above me, the mountain went up into shadow, and somewhere up in that shadow was the tollhouse, and Master Corvin, dying by inches and silent as a locked drawer.

I looked down at the satchel. Through the gap where Aldous Vane had gotten one buckle open, a corner of paper showed, and on that corner, in ink that seemed to move—a flicker, a pulse, like a coal deciding whether to catch—there was the start of a line. A road. Drawn in burning ink, running up into the heart of Thornfell, along a route I knew better than my own hands.

Except the route on the paper didn't end where the real one ended.

It kept going. Down. Under.

I stood on the switchback until the cold came, holding a stranger's warmth in my hands, and I did not yet understand that I had already broken all three rules—that I had been seen, that I had taken, and that trouble, having found me, had decided to keep me.

But my feet, guild-trained and traitorous, had already turned uphill. Toward the tollhouse. Toward Corvin.

Toward answers, though I'd have given anything, in the end, not to learn them.
