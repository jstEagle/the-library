## 4. Ash at the Door

I had been a fugitive for eleven days when I learned that nobility is mostly a matter of footwear.

You don't think about shoes until you're walking out of them. Mine had been made by a cobbler in Vellamare who charged my father more for one pair than an innkeeper here earned in a season, and they had opinions about mountain paths that differed sharply from mine. By the fourth day I'd wrapped my feet in linen torn from a petticoat I no longer needed—there being no one left to petticoat at—and by the seventh day I'd stopped mourning them entirely, which I considered a sign of tremendous personal growth.

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which struck me as optimistic naming for a building three days' walk from any water deep enough to drown a cat. It sat at the crook of a valley where two smuggler's paths met, all low stone and turf roof and smoke that smelled of peat rather than the perfumed cedar of home. I'd chosen it because it was small enough that nobody would look twice at a woman traveling alone with a satchel full of stolen paper, and because the innkeeper, a broad woman named Marta with forearms like ship's cable, had taken one look at my blistered hands and said nothing at all.

That silence was worth more than gold. Gold I had. What I didn't have was anyone who could be trusted to keep their mouth shut about me, and so when Marta slid a bowl of barley stew across the table on my first night and said only, "Back room's free if you want it," I nearly wept into the broth.

Eleven days. That's how long my luck held.

* * *

Let me back up, because you'll want to know what a duke's daughter was doing eating barley stew in the armpit of the Thornfells, and the honest answer is: stealing.

Not coins. Not jewels. Anyone can steal jewels; my cousin Bertrand stole jewels recreationally and was merely tedious about it. No—I stole a ledger. A fat, ugly, leather-bound ledger from the private study of Lord Cassel Vane, who was to have been my father's business partner and my husband's uncle-by-marriage, in the arrangement that passes among great families for affection.

The wedding was set for midwinter. The dowry negotiations had gone smoothly, which should have been my first warning; smoothness in those rooms means everyone is hiding something large. I found the ledger while looking for the marriage contract, because I wanted to know precisely what I was worth, and there it was on Vane's desk beneath a decanter of brandy, unremarkable except that it was locked, and locked things in a man who deals in locked things are always worth opening.

I won't pretend I picked the lock with a hairpin and feminine cunning. I used a knife and twenty minutes and swore the whole time. Inside were names. Hundreds of names, written in a hand too neat to belong to Vane himself, each entry followed by figures—not sums of money, though money appeared, but quantities. *Units.* Years. And beside certain names, a notation I've thought about every night since: *collected.*

My brother Tam's name was forty pages in.

Tam, who had been dead six years by every account our family accepted, drowned in a hunting accident on Vane's own estate, mourned politely and buried quickly. There he was in the ledger, alive as ink: *Tammas Amadine — 19 units — collected, solstice, year of the Grey Frost.*

I did what any sensible woman would do. I put the ledger back, finished the brandy, and spent two sleepless nights assembling a plan that would have gotten me hanged in three jurisdictions. Then I stole it properly, along with a riding cloak and my mother's rings, and left a note for my father that said only: *Ask Vane what he collects.*

Then I ran north, because everything terrible in our family's history pointed north, toward Thornfell, toward the mountains where the old roads went and the older bargains were kept. The ledger mentioned the Ember Court the way merchants mention a bank—as something assumed, permanent, and best not examined. If Tam was in it, then the Court was real, and if the Court was real, then somewhere under that mountain my brother was either dead in a way worse than drowning or alive in a way I couldn't yet imagine.

Either way, I intended to find out. I'd like to tell you this was courage. Mostly it was spite, which burns longer.

* * *

The morning the ash came, I was in the stable loft pretending to mend a saddle, which is to say I was reading the ledger by a crack of light between the boards and trying to make the arithmetic mean anything other than what it meant.

The horses went first. All four of them pressed to the far wall at once, ears flat, making a sound I'd never heard a horse make—a low, continuous whine, like wind through a keyhole. Then the yard went quiet in a way yards don't. No birdsong. No cart wheels on the road below. Even the wind seemed to step aside.

I put my eye to the gap in the boards.

Three figures stood in the doorway of the inn. I say *stood,* but standing implies weight and intention, and these things moved like smoke deciding to be people. They were tall, grey, and wrong in a way my eyes kept trying to correct and failing—their edges drifted, little threads of them lifting off and dissolving, and where faces should have been there was only smoothness, the way a mannequin is smooth, suggestion without detail. Ash. They were made of ash, or dressed in it, or they were what remained when everything else about a person burned away.

Marta came out of the kitchen wiping her hands on her apron, and I watched her take them in, and I watched her do the bravest thing I saw in all my months in Thornfell: she didn't run. She planted herself in front of her own doorway like a fence post and waited.

"You've no custom here," she said. "We're quiet folk."

The middle figure spoke. Its voice was the worst part—not monstrous, not booming, but dry and reasonable, like paper turning over in a draft. "A traveler passed this way. We require a description."

"Travelers pass every way," Marta said. "It's a road."

"A young man." A pause, as though consulting something inside itself. "He carried a satchel. He is... overdue."

Something in the phrasing snagged me. Overdue. You say that of books and debts. Not of men.

"He came through," Marta said, after a moment too long. "Two days past. Bought bread and dried meat. Went east over the high path."

"East." The word came out flat, and the three of them turned their heads together, in unison, like weather vanes finding the same wind. "The high path leads to the tollhouse."

"It leads a lot of places."

"The tollhouse," the thing repeated, with the patience of a creditor. "There is a keeper there. An old man. And—" another pause, longer, and I felt rather than understood that something behind that blank face was straining, reaching for a memory it no longer owned— "an apprentice. Perhaps. There may be an apprentice."

Marta's jaw tightened. "If there's trouble coming to that boy—"

"There is no trouble." The ash-thing tilted its head, and drifting flakes of it settled on the doorstep like grey snow. "There is only collection. Trouble is what the delinquent calls it."

I stopped breathing. *Collection.* The word off the page, in the air, in a voice that had no lungs to make it. These were the Court's creatures. These were what the ledger's neat hand dispatched when a debt ran overdue, and they were hunting a courier with a satchel, eastward, toward a tollhouse with an old man and possibly an apprentice.

And I knew—knew with the cold certainty that arrives ahead of understanding—that whatever was in that satchel mattered to me. Because the Court wanted it, and everything the Court wanted was either a debt or a threat to a debt, and I had spent eleven days learning that the Court's debts were written in my family's blood.

The lead figure extended something I hadn't seen it holding: a scrap of parchment, grey-edged, scorched. "The bearer was marked. The mark persists. If he passed his burden to another—"

"He didn't give me anything," Marta said, too fast.

"We did not ask if he gave *you* anything." The voice was almost gentle. Almost. "We asked for a description. You have offered a direction instead. This is noted."

One of the flanking figures drifted forward, past Marta, into the common room, and I heard it moving through the space—no footsteps, just a soft hiss like sand poured on stone. It was searching. I pressed myself flat against the loft boards and did the only clever thing available to me: I pulled the ledger out of my satchel and shoved it into the hay-mow, deep, under three seasons of rotting straw, and covered the gap with a saddle blanket, and lay down next to it with my heart going like a drum corps.

The hiss came up the stairs. Crossed the loft. Stopped directly above me.

I looked up through the gap between the boards into a face that wasn't a face—grey, featureless, close enough that I could see it wasn't empty after all. There was depth in it, faint movements, like shapes suspended in dirty ice. For one absurd moment I thought I saw a mouth forming, as if the thing were trying to remember how asking worked.

Then, from below, the dry voice: "Enough. The trail is east. The tollhouse."

The presence above me withdrew. I heard the three of them recede down the stairs, across the yard, and then nothing—no gate creak, no hoofbeats, just the absence of them, and after a long minute the birds started up again as though embarrassed.

* * *

Marta found me ten minutes later, still flat in the hay, and hauled me up by the arm with those cable forearms.

"You heard," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I heard."

"They'll go to the tollhouse." She was already moving, pulling a canvas sack from a peg, filling it with a speed that told me she'd done this before, or prepared for it, or both. "Old Corvin keeps it. And there's a boy—Edmund. Quiet lad. Comes down for salt and lamp oil, doesn't talk much, knows every goat track on the mountain." She thrust the sack at me. "Those things don't come down from the high paths for weather. They come for debts. And if they think a debt's been handed to someone new—"

"Then whoever has the satchel is already dead," I said, "they just haven't been informed."

Marta looked at me then, really looked, the way you look at someone whose fine clothes have finally stopped fooling you. "You're not what you pretended to be when you walked in here."

"No," I agreed. "Neither are you, I suspect. Nobody keeps a back room free out of charity."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "Charity's for people with time. Go east. There's a shepherd's cut above the falls that joins the high path below the tollhouse—you'll beat them there if you leave now and don't stop to be noble about your blisters. Warn the boy. Get him off the mountain."

"Why?" I asked. "Why warn him? Why help me? You don't know me."

Marta tied off the sack and pushed it against my chest hard enough to rock me.

"Because my sister's name is in a book somewhere too," she said. "I don't know what book. But I know the shape of the hole she left." She turned away, back toward her kitchen, her fire, her ordinary and endangered life. "Go. And girl—if you find whoever's keeping that ledger of theirs, burn it or bring it down. Don't let them keep writing in it."

* * *

I took the shepherd's cut at a pace that would have scandalized my finishing governess, and my ruined shoes, and the entire concept of ducal daughters.

The path climbed through pine shadow and broke out onto bare granite above the falls, where the water roared up through the rock like the mountain clearing its throat. Twice I slipped and caught myself on stone that bit my palms, and both times I got up faster than I fell, because behind me, faint but persistent, I could feel it: that wrongness in the air, that hush that traveled with them. They weren't hurrying. Things made of ash don't hurry. They simply arrive, the way winter arrives, and arguing with winter is a hobby for the very rich or the very desperate.

I had been rich. I was working on the second thing.

By late afternoon the high path opened ahead of me, a ledge cut into the mountainside, and far along it—half a mile, perhaps less—I saw the tollhouse: a squat stone watchpost clinging to the cliff like a barnacle, smoke rising thin from its chimney. Smoke meant people. People meant a courier's satchel, an old man who knew too much, and a quiet boy named Edmund who was about to have the worst evening of his life unless I arrived first.

I stood there a moment, catching my breath, and I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the one inheritance my family actually left me.

I was terrified. Properly terrified, knees-and-stomach terrified, the kind that whispers *go south, go home, marry the awful man, at least the awful man is warm.* Every instinct bred into me across nine generations of careful, comfortable Amadines said that the correct response to ash walking was to be elsewhere.

But underneath the terror, steady as a coal, was the ledger. Forty pages in. *Tammas Amadine — collected.* My brother, filed away like grain, and a court of grey things calling human beings *delinquent,* and somewhere beneath this mountain a vault where nineteen units of my brother might still be flickering, waiting for someone with enough spite to come get him.

Terror, I discovered, is just resolve that hasn't picked a direction yet.

I picked east, toward the tollhouse, toward the smoke, toward a stranger with a satchel I already half-believed was meant to find its way to me. The light was failing. Behind me, patient as arithmetic, the ash was coming.

I ran.
