## 5. What Master Corvin Won't Say

The thing about dying men is that they get to be mysterious for free.

Master Corvin had been mysterious my entire life, but in the last two years he'd elevated it to an art form. He'd stopped teaching. He'd stopped walking the lower paths. He spent his days propped up in the tollhouse's back room like a coat someone had forgotten on a hook, coughing in a way that sounded less like illness and more like the mountain itself clearing its throat through him. And when I asked him questions—the questions I'd been asking since I was nine years old and knew better than to ask—he would look at me with those grey eyes that had once measured a hundred switchbacks a day, and he would say nothing at all.

Silence, from Master Corvin, was never empty. It was load-bearing. It held up whole decades of things he refused to let me know.

So naturally, when I found myself holding a map drawn in ink that moved like it was on fire, with a road on it that no chart in the guild's possession admitted existed, I did what any sensible apprentice of a nearly extinct order would do.

I took it to the one man who had made silence into a career.

* * *

I want it noted that I hesitated first. There was a full hour of hesitation, during which I sat at the tollhouse table with the map folded under a ledger of dead routes and told myself all the reasonable things. *It's probably nothing. It's probably a smuggler's joke, ink laced with ember-powder, some old trick to make buyers think a route is worth more than it is.* The Wayfarers' Guild had been con artists as often as guides; I'd read enough of the old ledgers to know that half our "secret paths" were secret mostly because nobody wanted them.

But the roads were right. That was what kept me at the table instead of feeding the thing to the stove. Every line of that map matched terrain I knew better than my own hands—the false pass above Kettering's Notch, the goat trail that wasn't a goat trail below the Widow's Tooth, the ford where the river ran shallow only if you knew which side to approach from. Whoever drew it had walked Thornfell the way I walked Thornfell. And then, in the northeast corner, past the last marked waypoint, the ink simply kept going—into a region every chart I'd ever catalogued called, in various hands across two centuries, *nothing*, *sheer drop*, or, in one particularly honest entry, *don't*.

And the ink moved. I don't mean shimmered. I mean it moved, slow as honey, like the lines were cooling from something hotter. When I held the map too long, my fingertips went warm, and not unpleasantly warm, either, which frightened me more than pain would have. Pain you can understand. Comfort from a dead courier's belongings is a different animal entirely.

The courier himself I hadn't been able to put out of my mind. The way he'd looked at me—not begging, exactly. Apologizing. Like handing me the satchel was the last discourtesy he could manage, and he was sorry for it. I'd refused him. I'd stood there with my arms crossed and my guild-trained caution all buttoned up, and I'd said *I can't take what I can't carry*, which is a proverb Master Corvin taught me and which I now understood had always meant something slightly different than I'd thought.

He died anyway. Of course he died anyway. My refusal hadn't saved me from anything; it had just made sure I watched.

Guilt arrives before understanding. Nobody warns you about that. You'd think guilt would at least have the courtesy to explain itself.

By late afternoon I'd run out of reasons not to, so I folded the map into my coat, took the lamp, and went down the hall to the room where the last Guildmaster of the Wayfarers' Guild was busy not telling me things.

* * *

He was awake, which was rare after midday. He'd arranged himself against the pillows with the particular dignity of a man who resents needing pillows, and he was looking out the narrow window at the high path—the same path where the courier had come apart in my arms three days ago.

"You've been sulking around this house for two days," he said without turning. "You burn the porridge when you're thinking too hard. This morning's was charcoal."

"It was rustic."

"It was a fire hazard." He turned then, and I saw how much the winter had taken. His face had gone from weathered to worn, like stone steps in old churches. But the eyes were still the eyes. They went straight to my coat pocket the way his eyes had always gone to the weak point in any argument, any cliff face, any lie.

"You found something," he said.

"I found something."

"Then before you show me—and you will show me, because you've already decided to and we both know it—I want you to consider whether some stones are better left unturned."

"That's not even your proverb. Yours is about carrying weight."

"The proverbs intermarry." He held out his hand. It shook. He pretended it didn't, and I pretended not to notice, because that was the arrangement between us: an entire architecture of mutual pretending, load-bearing, holding up everything we didn't say.

I gave him the map.

I wish I could tell you I handed it over casually, that I was testing him, that I had some plan. The truth is my hand shook worse than his, and the moment the paper left my fingers I felt naked, the way you feel when you've said something true in a room full of people who prefer lies.

Corvin unfolded it halfway. Then he stopped.

I have seen Master Corvin look at rockfalls with respect. I have seen him look at a whiteout with something close to affection, because he liked the mountain best when it was trying to kill him. In forty years of guiding men through Thornfell, through avalanches and ash-storms and one memorable incident involving a bear and a wedding party, I had never—not once—seen him afraid.

He was afraid now. It came off him like cold off ice. His face didn't just go pale; it went *absent*, like a house with everyone gone out of it. And his hands, the hands that had taught mine every knot worth knowing, began to fold the map back up with terrible care, the way you'd handle something wounded.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get this?"

"A courier. On the high switchback. He was burned—ash-burned, though I didn't know to call it that yet. Something was chasing him. Something that—" I stopped, because saying *men made of drifting ash* aloud made it real, and I still had a childish hope that if I kept things unsaid they might stay unreal. "He gave me his satchel. I told him I couldn't take it. He gave it anyway. He died on my path, Master."

Something crossed Corvin's face at *my path*. For a moment he looked almost proud, which was so wildly inappropriate that I nearly laughed.

"And the map?"

"Ember-ink. It moves. There's a road on it that isn't on any chart in the archive. I checked. I checked all of them, including the ones you keep locked and pretend are moth-eaten."

"Moths are thorough," he said faintly.

"Master."

"Don't 'Master' me in that tone, boy. It's insolent with extra steps." He set the map on the blanket between us, flat, and put both hands over it as if it might crawl away. Or as if he might. "Listen to me. I am going to say this once, plainly, without proverbs, because apparently plainness is the only coin you'll accept anymore. Take that map to the stove. Put it in the coals. Stand there until it's ash. Then sweep the ash into the river, and never speak of this day again, and I will die easier than I deserve to."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the wind worked at the tollhouse shutters, patient as ever. The mountain had been trying to get in for two hundred years. It had time.

"No," I said.

It came out smaller than I intended. I'll admit that. But it came out, and that mattered, because in twenty-six years of being Edmund Villalobos, apprentice, ward, burden, whatever I was to him—I had never once said no to Master Corvin. Not really. I'd grumbled and delayed and done the thing wrong on purpose to make a point, but the point had always been *convince me*, and he always had, or the mountain had on his behalf.

His head came up slowly. "Edmund."

"You heard me. I'm not burning it."

"You don't know what it is."

"Then tell me. That's rather my whole position, isn't it? You've spent my entire life refusing to tell me anything—where I came from, who my mother was, why the guild has no members under sixty, why there's a whole wing of the archive sealed with wax seals older than I am. I have catalogued routes nobody walks for a guild that won't say why it's dying. I have been *patient*, Master. I have been patient the way stones are patient. And now a man dies on my doorstep holding a piece of paper that makes you look like that—" I pointed at his face, at the fear sitting in it, "—and you ask me to burn the only answer anyone has ever handed me. So no. No. I won't."

For a long moment he just breathed. I could hear the rattle in it now, up close, the wet gravel sound that the winter was making of his lungs.

"You sound like her," he said finally.

The words went through me like a pick through shale. "Like who?"

He closed his eyes. Of course he did. He would sooner have recited the guild's secrets to the mountain goats.

"Who?" I pressed. "Master, you can't—you don't get to drop a stone like that and walk away from it. Who do I sound like?"

"A fool," he said. "You sound like a fool, asking questions that got better people than either of us buried under this mountain." He pushed himself upright, and the effort cost him visibly, and he did it anyway, because Corvin would have gotten out of a deathbed to win an argument. "Do you know what the Wayfarers' Guild actually sold, boy? All these years. All these tolls and ledgers and secret paths. Do you think it was guidance? Guidance is cheap. Any shepherd with good boots can guide."

"What did we sell, then?"

"Safe passage." He said it strangely, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ground. "Guaranteed safe passage. Through Thornfell, no matter the season, no matter what woke beneath it. Kings paid for that. Armies paid for that. Do you imagine we bought it with tolls?"

The map lay between us on the blanket, and even folded, even covered by his hands, I swear I could feel it warming. Or maybe that was me.

"With what, then?" My mouth had gone dry. "What did we pay?"

Corvin looked at me for a long time. And here is the thing I will remember until I die: he *almost* told me. I saw it happen. I saw the truth rise in him like water finding a crack, and I saw him reach for it, and I saw—God help me—I saw him choose the silence instead. Two hundred years of guild discipline, or cowardice, or love, or whatever it was, and it closed over his face like ice closing over a hole in a frozen lake.

"Burn the map, Edmund," he said hoarsely. "Please."

*Please.* In twenty-six years, I had heard Master Corvin say please perhaps four times, and never to me, and never like that—like a man pleading with the tide.

And that, more than anything, is what decided me. Because if the truth were small enough to survive daylight, he wouldn't have begged. Men beg only about the large things.

"No," I said again, and this time it came out steady, and I picked the map up off the blanket and folded it into my coat while he watched. "I'm going to find out what road this leads to, and what the courier died carrying, and why ash-men hunt a piece of paper. And when I come back, Master—if I come back—you're going to tell me everything. Every name. Every debt. Whatever it is you've been carrying alone all these years, you're going to put it down, because I have watched you die by inches for two winters and the only inheritance you've left me is silence, and I *refuse* it. Do you hear me? I refuse the silence. Give me anything else. Anything. But I'm done eating quiet and calling it bread."

He stared at me. The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind changed its mind about something and came around the peak from the north, and far off—so far I might have imagined it, and part of me still hopes I did—a sound rolled down out of the high passes that was not quite thunder and not quite a door.

Corvin sank back against the pillows. All the fight had gone out of him at once, the way it does, and what was left looked terribly old and terribly tired.

"There's a name," he whispered, "that you must never say aloud on the mountain. If you go—and you will go, you stubborn, mule-headed, *hers*—then hear me on this one thing. Some words are keys, boy. The Court beneath Thornfell does not listen for footsteps. It listens for names."

"The Court," I repeated. "What court?"

He turned his face to the wall. And that was the answer, or the whole of it I was getting: the last Guildmaster of the Wayfarers' Guild, silent as the grave he was rehearsing for, while somewhere north of us a road that shouldn't exist waited with its ink still glowing.

I stood there a moment longer, hating him and loving him in the same breath, which I suspect is the standard condition of sons.

Then I went to pack.
