## 7. Roads Out of Thornfell

I want to say I saw them coming. That some smuggler's instinct, honed over years of reading the mountain's moods, gave me warning—that I felt the change in the wind or heard the wrong kind of silence and had my boots on before the first ash-flake drifted past the window.

The truth is I was arguing with a goat.

"Master Corvin's medicine," I told her, holding the clay pot above her head while she stood on her hind legs against the fence post, hooves scrabbling at my elbow. "Which means it's not yours. Which means—ow. *Ow.* Fine. FINE." She got a finger's worth of the bitter paste off my knuckle and chewed it with an air of vindicated triumph while I sucked at the bite mark. "You're a menace. You know that? When he dies you're going to be insufferable."

She bleated. It sounded like agreement.

That was how it was, those last days at the tollhouse: me talking to animals because there was no one else to talk to, cataloguing dead roads in a ledger no one would ever read, watching Master Corvin fade by inches in the back room and hating him for every secret he took with him toward the grave. The map lay under the third floorboard in my sleeping loft, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a live coal even through the wood. Some nights I could feel it through the mattress, like a cat curled up against my spine.

I should have burned it. He'd told me to burn it, and I'd refused, and we'd parted angry—the last words between us possibly going to be *you ungrateful boy* and *you lying old man*, and neither of us meaning either one, and both of us too proud and too stubborn to fix it before sleep.

Remember that, when you judge what I did next. Remember that the last thing I'd said to the man who raised me was spoken in anger, and that I went to bed rehearsing better versions of the conversation, and that I woke to smoke where there shouldn't have been smoke.

* * *

It was the birds that told me, in the end. Not instinct. Birds.

The choughs that nested in the tollhouse eaves went up all at once—not scattering, not alarmed, just *leaving*, the way they do when something below them stops being worth the risk of staying. I'd seen it twice before. Once when a rockslide took the lower switchback. Once when men came looking for smuggled brandy and found instead a very calm young man selling them very honest turnips.

I set down the ledger. I crossed to the window without hurrying, because hurrying tells watchers things, and looked down the valley road.

They came up through the morning mist like a procession of bad thoughts. Four figures, walking in step, unhurried, their outlines smearing at the edges as if the air couldn't quite decide where they ended. Ash. Not ash-covered—ash-*made*. What I'd taken, from the courier's ravings, for pursuit was pursuit, and it had found the end of its road, and the end of its road was my door.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. Later I understood why: fear hadn't arrived yet. Fear takes a moment to find you, the way cold water takes a moment to soak through wool. In that gap, the body does what the body has been trained to do, and mine had been trained by twenty years of Master Corvin's voice saying *never run when you can walk, never walk when you can stand still, and never, ever let them see you count the exits.*

So I counted the exits. Back stairs to the goat yard. Goat yard to the scree chute. Scree chute to the old charcoal-burners' path, which didn't appear on any map made after the war, which was the whole point of it. Three exits, four ash-men, one dying guildmaster asleep—or not asleep—in the back room.

And here is the part I keep trying to make smaller than it was, and can't.

I went to his room first. I swear I did. I put my hand on the latch, and through the door I said, low, "Master. They're here. We have to go."

And he said—and this is the truth, whatever else gets said about that morning—he said, "No."

Not *I can't.* Not *leave me.* Just *no*, in that flat voice he used for closing arguments, the voice that meant the matter was decided and further discussion would be billed to my account.

"They'll kill you."

"They'll question me." A pause, wet coughing. "There's a difference, boy, and it's measured in how interesting your answers are. Mine stopped being interesting years ago. Yours haven't started yet. Go out the back. The charcoal path. Don't take the high road, don't take the river road—"

"The river road floods this time of—"

"*Don't take the river road,*" he snapped, and then, quieter, in a voice I'd never heard him use, almost gentle, which frightened me more than the ash-men did: "Edmund. Listen to me. Whatever happens, whatever they say, whatever you find at the end of that map—you were the best thing I ever stole. Now go."

*Stole.* I had half a second to wonder about that word, and then the front door opened without being knocked on, because doors are a courtesy between people, and these were not people, and I ran.

* * *

Here is what flight actually looks like, for anyone who's only met it in ballads. It does not look like leaping from balconies with your cloak streaming. It looks like crawling through a goat yard on your belly with straw in your mouth while your heart tries to beat its way out through your teeth. It looks like choosing the scree chute—a slide of loose stone that has broken ankles since before the guild existed—because the alternative is open ground. It looks like sitting at the bottom of that chute for a full minute, bleeding from both palms, listening to your own breathing, doing arithmetic with terror.

Four of them. One would stay with Corvin. Maybe two. Two might come after me. Could I outrun ash? Did ash tire? Did ash—

The map. Gods help me, in the middle of all of it, my hand went to the floorboard-shaped absence under my shirt where the map wasn't, because I'd grabbed it on the way past the loft, automatic as breathing, and now it rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat, warm through the oilcloth, and I understood with terrible clarity that it was the reason they were here, and that I was running *toward* keeping it rather than away from anything, and that this distinction mattered enormously and changed nothing at all.

I came up out of the gully behind the old kiln and looked back once.

I've looked back ten thousand times since. I'll look back ten thousand more. But that first look—I want to get it right, because memory is a liar and I refuse to let it lie to me about this.

Through the tollhouse window, lit grey by the morning, I saw Master Corvin sitting upright in his chair. Sitting *upright*, for the first time in weeks, spine straight, hands folded, facing the door like a man receiving visitors he'd been expecting for forty years. One of the ash-figures stood before him. The others were already turning away, heads swiveling toward the mountain, toward the scree, toward me.

Corvin saw me. Across that distance, through glass and mist and the smear of drifting ash, he saw me, and he did something I will carry until I die:

He shook his head. *Don't come back. Don't be stupid. Run.*

And then he smiled. Small. Crooked. The smile he used to give me when I solved a route problem he thought would stump me. *Well done, boy.*

I ran.

* * *

I ran for three hours. I know because the sun moved from the notch of Wolfjaw Peak to the shoulder of it, and I know that notch the way other men know the faces of their children. Up the charcoal-burners' path, along the shelf trail, across the flank of Cindermoss where the pines grow bent like old men apologizing for the wind. Twice I heard movement behind me—once a slide of stone with no stone to blame, once a silence so complete it had texture—and twice I did the things Master Corvin had beaten into me across twenty years: doubled back through my own tracks, crossed water, walked on stone where stone keeps no record.

By midday I was on the high shelf above the Hollow Bridge, and I allowed myself to stop, and that was when fear finally caught up with me, arriving all at once like a debt collector, and I sat down among the rocks and shook.

Not crying. I want to be clear about that. Shaking. There's a difference, and the difference is that crying is grief and shaking is the body demanding to know why you're still alive and whether you intend to keep being so.

"You left him."

I said it out loud, to the mountain, because the mountain doesn't answer and wouldn't pretend to.

"He told you to. You *left him* because he *told you to*, and you obeyed, because you always obey, because obedience is the only inheritance he ever gave you that you actually wanted—"

A stone clattered somewhere below. I shut my mouth so fast I bit my tongue.

For a long moment there was nothing but wind and the far-off complaint of a hawk. Then, closer, deliberate: another stone, and another, in a rhythm no landslide ever kept. Someone climbing. Someone who wanted me to hear them coming, which on Thornfell meant either a friend or someone so confident they didn't need stealth, and in my experience the confident ones were worse.

I moved. Low, quiet, along the shelf to the place where the rock folded back into a shallow overhang, and I put my back to stone and my hand on my knife and waited.

Boots on scree. Unhurried. Then a figure rounded the bend of the trail—smaller than the ash-men, moving like a person moves, swearing fluently and creatively at the loose rock in an accent I recognized from the lowland courts, all polished vowels ground rough by altitude—and stopped dead when she saw the knife.

She was maybe my age, dressed in clothes that had been expensive before the mountains finished ruining them, dark hair escaping a braid that had given up sometime yesterday, and she looked at my knife, and then at me, and her expression did something complicated that ended in exasperation.

"Oh, wonderful," she said. "Another one."

"Another one what?"

"A man with a knife standing in exactly the spot I was going to stand in." She pushed past me into the overhang like she owned it, dropped onto a flat rock, and began pulling thorns out of her boot with the air of someone settling in for a long grievance. "There's a village back the way I came. Or there was. There's ash walking through it asking questions, and everyone in it suddenly remembering urgent business elsewhere, so forgive me if I'm not in a sociable mood."

My blood went cold in a way the altitude couldn't explain. "Ash. Grey figures. Like—" I gestured vaguely, uselessly. "Like smoke that learned to walk?"

She looked up. For the first time she actually *saw* me, and I watched her recalculate everything, the way you watch weather change. "You've seen them."

"This morning. They came to my—" I stopped. My throat closed around the word *home*. "They came. I left."

Something shifted in her face. Not softness, exactly. Recognition. The look of one hunted animal noting the spoor of another.

"And you're carrying something they want," she said. It wasn't a question. "Because I am, and they don't stop, and I've been running since the ford at Marrow's Crossing, and I am *tired*, and you have a knife and a smuggler's walk and you clearly know these paths, and I have—" she hesitated, jaw working, some decision being made behind her eyes that cost her visibly, "—information. About what they are. About what they want. About what's under the mountain."

The map pressed warm against my ribs. Below us, far down the valley, thin as breath, a thread of grey was detaching itself from the tollhouse road and beginning, patiently, to climb.

Every lesson Master Corvin ever taught me said: trust no one, share nothing, walk alone. Every lesson he ever taught me had also just sat in a chair and told me to leave him to die alone, and smiled while doing it, and I was done taking lessons from dead men and dying ones alike.

"What kind of information?" I asked.

She stood, wincing, and stuck out a hand grimy with thorn-blood and scree-dust. "Sera," she said. "Sera Amadine. And if you shake it, we're allies, and if you don't, we're both dead within the week, so do think quickly."

Behind her, patient as winter, the grey thread climbed.

I shook it.
