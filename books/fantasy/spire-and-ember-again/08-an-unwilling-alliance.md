## 8. An Unwilling Alliance

*Edmund*

The trouble with stealing from a village you've robbed before is that the dogs remember you.

I'd been lifting provisions from Hollowmere's back gardens since I was eleven years old, back when Master Corvin still sent me down from the tollhouse for "supply runs," which was guild-speak for *take what we need and don't get caught*. The trick was never to take much. A man who loses a sack of onions grumbles. A man who loses his whole winter store organizes a search party. So I moved through the sleeping village like a debt collector—quiet, apologetic in principle, taking only what was owed, which as far as I could tell was nothing at all. Guilt is a poor traveling companion but an excellent lookout.

Three days out of the tollhouse and Corvin's white face, and my pack held half a loaf going stale, a heel of hard cheese, and a jar of pickled things whose provenance I chose not to investigate. Not enough to reach the lowlands. Certainly not enough to reach whatever waited under the mountain, if the ember-ink map meant what it seemed to mean.

So: Hollowmere. The Widow Brask's garden first—her fence had a gap behind the bee skep that even the bees had forgotten—and then the smokehouse behind the cooper's yard, where the latch was a joke and the ham inside was not.

I had the ham. I had one hand on the latch. And then someone put a knife against my ribs and said, in a voice like frost forming on a window, "Drop it."

I want it noted, for posterity, that I did not scream. What I did was freeze in the particular way smugglers freeze, which is to say completely, while every muscle simultaneously files a complaint. The knife pressed a little firmer. Behind me, breathing—controlled, deliberate, the breathing of someone who'd rehearsed this.

"Drop the ham," she said again.

"It's not even my ham," I said. "We're technically colleagues."

There was a pause. I felt her reconsider the geometry of the situation—the smokehouse, the hour, the boy holding stolen goods offering professional solidarity. The knife withdrew perhaps an inch.

"Turn around. Slowly."

I turned. She was younger than the voice suggested—not much older than me—with dark hair cut short in the manner of someone who'd done it herself with a belt knife and hadn't looked back. Fine clothes gone practical, a noblewoman's posture wearing a fugitive's coat like a disguise over a disguise. Her eyes were doing arithmetic on my face, and I got the distinct impression I wasn't coming out ahead.

"You're the tollhouse boy," she said.

"And you're the woman who watched ash men interrogate an innkeeper in Ferrow's Cross and didn't run fast enough." I'd heard about her from a drover two valleys over, the way you hear about weather coming. *A lady, alone, asking questions about the Court.* People who ask questions about the Court end up as cautionary tales. "Small world. Terrible neighborhood."

"You know what they are." It wasn't a question. Her grip shifted on the knife—not relaxing, recalibrating. "The ash-bound. You've seen them."

"I've seen one burn a courier alive on the switchback above my door." I let that sit. "He gave me something before he died. They want it back. Which means everyone standing within ten feet of me is standing in a fire they didn't light."

Something moved through her face—recognition, calculation, and underneath both, so brief I almost missed it, fear. Real fear, the kind that doesn't perform itself.

"Show me," she said.

"No."

"Show me or I shout, and this village learns what you are."

"They won't care about a ham thief."

"They'll care when the ash men come asking, and they will come asking, because I've spent four days learning how they hunt. They don't follow tracks. They follow *consequence*." Her voice dropped, went flat and certain. "Everything you touch, everything that touches you—they can read it. You've been carrying that satchel through three villages. How long do you think you have?"

And there it was, the thing I'd been refusing to say aloud since the tollhouse burned behind me: not long. Maybe not long enough. The enforcers had found the courier in the high wilds where no road officially existed. They were reading the world around him like a ledger.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Sera." Just that. No house name, no lineage, though the way she held her chin announced *Amadine* louder than any herald could have. "And you're Edmund Villalobos, which I only know because your master's guild kept records, and I have read them."

My skin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the night air. "You've read guild records."

"I've read a great many things I shouldn't have." For the first time, the knife-point certainty flickered into something almost like humor, dry and joyless. "It's a habit. It's also why I'm hiding in a smokehouse instead of a manor."

* * *

*Sera*

Let me be clear about what I knew when I put the knife away: almost nothing, and enough.

I knew he was guild. The Wayfarers' Guild, the quiet order of path-finders whose charters I'd paged through in my father's library while pretending to study betrothal contracts—boring, bureaucratic, obsessed with right-of-way and toll schedules. Except the deeper ledgers weren't boring at all. The deeper ledgers had columns that made no sense until you understood what was being paid, and to whom, and I had understood, alone in a lamplit study at seventeen, and had never slept properly again.

I knew the ash-bound hunted by consequence because I'd watched them work in Ferrow's Cross. The innkeeper—a soft man, frightened, honest by the standards of innkeepers—had sworn he'd seen no stranger. And Veyra—I learned her name later, though I knew her face first, gray and patient as a headstone—had simply stood there while her companion opened the man's strongbox and read his letters, his receipts, the lock of his dead wife's hair he kept wrapped in linen. Reading his life. Finding the shape of the stranger in the negative space of it.

They would do the same to this boy. To anyone near him. To me, if I stayed—but I'd already decided to stay, somewhere between the strongbox and the moment I followed him up the valley, so there was no point pretending otherwise.

"The map," he said now, in the smokehouse, arms crossed like a man guarding a door that was already open. "Why should I show you?"

"Because I know what's under the mountain better than you ever will."

"You don't know what's under the mountain. Nobody does. That's rather the point of *under*."

"The Ember Court," I said, and watched him flinch at the name, which told me the map had told him too. "The Magistrate's court. The parliament of the deep roads. Whatever your courier died carrying, it concerns them, and I have spent the last year learning their politics from the inside—which is more than your guild ever managed in four hundred years of paying them tribute and calling it friendship."

His eyes narrowed. "Tribute."

Damn. I'd said more than I meant to. But there was no walking it back, and honestly, no reason to—he needed to be frightened, and I needed him to understand that his fright had company.

"Your guild has treaties with the Court," I said. "Old ones. Sealed ones. Do you know what they seal?"

"I know my master went white as milk when he saw the map and ordered me to burn it. I know he wouldn't say why. I'm beginning to suspect you might."

Good. He was quick. Annoyingly quick—it would have been simpler if he'd been stupid, but the world rarely arranges itself for my convenience.

"I'll trade you," I said. "Piece for piece. Your roads for my knowledge. You get me to Thornfell's root alive; I get you past the Court's gate without being collected."

"*Collected.*" He tried the word out like a stone he suspected was rotten underneath. "You talk about souls being gathered like they're apples."

"Because that's what they are to the Court. Apples. Harvest." I kept my voice level, which cost me; my brother's name sat behind my teeth like a coin I couldn't spend yet. "You think the ash-bound are monsters? They were people. Debtors. The Court takes their memories as payment and leaves the obedience. I've seen the vaults' existence referenced in a merchant's letter. Shelves, the letter said. *Shelves of glass.*"

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the village slept its stupid, safe sleep, and I envied it with my whole heart.

"My master," he said finally, "is dying. I left him. The enforcers were coming and I ran, and I tell myself he ordered me to, and maybe he did, but I ran *gladly*, and I—" He stopped. Recalibrated. I recognized the maneuver; I performed it myself a dozen times a day. "Why are you telling me this? We met ninety seconds ago over a ham."

"Because you need to know I'm not lying to you, and nothing costs a liar more than a true thing offered free." I sheathed the knife fully, deliberately, letting him watch. "Here's my whole hand, Villalobos: I stole something from the Court's service. A ledger. Names. If those names are still under the mountain, some of them can be brought back. I intend to bring one of them back specifically, and I will burn every bridge in Thornfell to do it. I don't need you to like me. I need your paths."

"And I need—" He exhaled. "I need to know what my own guild did. Apparently you know more than I do, which is either very sad or very funny, and I haven't decided which."

"Both," I said. "It's usually both."

* * *

*Edmund*

We struck the bargain in the smokehouse at the hour before dawn, which is when all bad decisions are signed, sealed, and delivered.

Her terms: I guide, she navigates the politics. My terms: she tells me everything she knows, in order, no hoarding. Her counter-term: I stop flinching every time she says the word *Court*. My counter-counter-term: she stops pointing the knife at me during negotiations. We settled on the knife staying sheathed but visible, which Sera called "diplomacy" and I called "a hostage situation with extra steps."

Then we walked out of Hollowmere together before first light, two liars with a shared itinerary, and I hated how much lighter the road felt with someone on it.

Not that I said so. Saying so would have required admitting the last three days alone had been a gray corridor of ham crumbs and self-recrimination, every mile measured in how far I'd gotten from Corvin's coughing. With Sera beside me, the miles became just miles. She walked like a soldier and complained like a duchess, sometimes in the same breath, and she asked questions about the paths that were actually good questions, which annoyed me more than bad ones would have.

"Why does the trail bend here?" she demanded at the second stream crossing, where the water split around a stone shaped like a kneeling monk.

"Because the stone's load-bearing. Take the direct line and you'll find the ground beneath is a sinkhole full of meltwater and regret."

"That's absurd."

"That's Thornfell. The mountain doesn't forgive shortcuts. It forgives patience, occasionally, and never twice."

She wrote it down. Actually stopped, produced a little oilskin notebook, and wrote it down. I stared.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. It's just—you write things down."

"Memory is a currency," she said, snapping the notebook shut, and something in her tone closed like a door. "I prefer to keep mine in a format that can't be repossessed."

I didn't ask. There was a whole architecture of pain behind that sentence and I'd only been granted the ground floor, and besides, I had my own locked rooms. We walked on.

By midday we'd reached the old charcoal-burners' track above the Ferrow valley, and she laid it out for me in pieces, watching my face the whole time like a physician checking for symptoms. The Court's structure. The masked houses and their rivalries. The Magistrate, who was not a man, or not only a man, or possibly a committee of something worse wearing one shape. The tribute routes—the guild's own roads, the ones I'd catalogued my whole life in the tollhouse, marked in her notes with a symbol I recognized from the courier's map.

"These are payment roads," I said slowly. "Every route in the guild charter. Every single one."

"Now you see it."

"No. No, these are smuggling paths. Corvin had me maintaining their charts since I was nine. Toll schedules. Right-of-way registries. It's *bureaucracy*, it's the most boring—"

"The best cages are boring," Sera said. "Nobody audits a toll schedule. Nobody burns a ledger of right-of-way fees. You hide the harvest in the paperwork, and the farmers never look." She paused, and for once her voice lost its edge. "I'm sorry. I watched your face hoping I was wrong."

I sat down on the charcoal-burners' cold hearth-stone and looked at the mountains I'd lived inside my entire life, and they looked exactly the same, which was somehow the worst part. The same peaks. The same paths. All of it the same, and none of it.

"So the courier," I said. "The map. It's not a treasure map. It's—what? Evidence?"

"It's a key. Or a confession. Possibly both." She crouched across from me, elbows on knees, and I saw the exhaustion under her composure, four days of flight worn into her like weather. "The Court keeps its debts written in ember-ink because ember-ink can't be forged and can't be destroyed. If your courier carried a page of it, then someone inside the Court wanted that debt *seen*. Someone's fighting the Magistrate from within. Or someone wanted it delivered to the one family the debt names."

My mouth went dry. "The debt names a family?"

"I read the courier's effects while you slept last night. Don't look betrayed—you'd have done the same." She had me there; I'd already picked her pockets for rations twice on principle. "One name on the map's margin. Struck through in the same moving ink. I didn't recognize it."

"But you think I would."

"I think," Sera said carefully, "that the ash-bound stopped following the courier's trail three days ago and started following yours. And I think whatever name is written in that margin, the Court believes it belongs to you."

The wind came down off the spire above us, cold and smelling of snow and old fire, and somewhere far below, in the warm dark under the mountain, I imagined shelves of glass humming in the heat like wine glasses about to sing.

"Well," I said, standing, brushing off my trousers with hands that mostly didn't shake. "No time like the present to find out."

Sera rose too, and for the first time since the smokehouse, she smiled—an actual smile, crooked and unwilling, gone in a heartbeat.

"There it is," she said. "The guild stubbornness. I wondered when it would show up."

"You have no idea what you've allied yourself with."

"I have *some* idea. I read the records." She shouldered her pack and nodded up-trail, toward the high passes, toward the unmapped road waiting in the ember-ink like a vein of fire in stone. "Come on, Villalobos. The mountain's listening, and I'd rather it hear us coming than catch us creeping."

We climbed. And behind us, faint as breath on glass, the wind carried something that might have been ash settling—or might have been footsteps, patient and gray, keeping our pace mile for mile down the valley below.
