## 9. The Hollow Bridge

The bridge was on none of my maps.

That should have been the first warning. I have spent my whole life among maps—the good ones, the smuggler's ones, the ones inked on calfskin and hidden in false bottoms and memorized by men who died rather than repeat them aloud—and I can tell you that the Thornfells do not have blank spaces. They have *withheld* spaces. Places the old cartographers knew perfectly well existed and chose to leave white, the way you leave a chair empty at a table for someone who isn't coming back.

So when Sera and I crested the ridge at dawn and looked down into a gorge that no chart of mine admitted to, at a bridge that no chart of mine admitted to either, I did what any sensible smuggler would do.

I considered going around.

"There's no going around," Sera said, because she had apparently learned to read my face in the two days we'd been traveling together, which was two days longer than I'd have liked.

"There's always a way around."

"Not through this gorge there isn't. Look at it." She gestured with the hand not clutching her stolen ledger like a shield. The gorge ran east to west as far as the eye could follow, a raw gash in the mountain's flank, and the walls went down and down into a mist that didn't move right. Mist should drift. This mist sat. It pooled in the depths like something poured there on purpose, and I found myself thinking—not for the first time since taking up with a runaway noblewoman—that the mountains of my childhood had been keeping secrets from me the way Corvin had: patiently, and with intent.

"The bridge holds," she said.

"You've crossed it?"

"No." A pause. "But the bridge is here, and bridges that shouldn't exist are never accidents. Someone built it for a reason, and reasons are usually load-bearing."

I wanted to argue with that. I want to point out, even now, that it is a terrible principle of engineering. But she wasn't wrong about the shape of the thing. The bridge arched over the gorge in a single span of grey stone, no railings, no parapets, nothing to catch you if the world decided you'd had enough. It was old in a way that made the guild's tollhouse look like a child's cairn. And it was—this is the part I keep circling back to, the part I still don't have words for that don't sound like madness—it was *thin*. Not narrow. Thin. When the morning light hit it at an angle, I could see through parts of it. Not gaps. Through. Like looking at a memory of a bridge instead of the bridge itself.

"Right," I said. "Well. I've crossed worse."

"You haven't," Sera said mildly. "You told me last night you've never left Thornfell."

"I've crossed worse *in spirit*."

She gave me one of her looks—the kind that made me understand exactly how she'd survived whatever court she'd fled, and why whoever she'd fled from probably still had nightmares about her—and started down the switchback toward the near end of the span.

I followed, because following Sera Amadine into danger had already become, disturbingly, a habit.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about guilt, since the bridge seemed determined to make it the day's lesson.

I know it as a craftsman knows his tools. Guilt is the weight you carry so you don't have to set anything down. It's the reason I catalogued routes nobody traveled anymore, the reason I kept the tollhouse swept and the ledgers current for a guild with three living members, one of them dying. Competence is just loneliness wearing a work apron, and I wore mine well. I was proud of it. I am, in my worst moments, still proud of it.

And I knew—I knew before my boot touched the first stone—that the courier on the switchback was going to be standing on this bridge with me whether he was physically present or not.

He hadn't taken the satchel when I offered it back. That's the detail that gnaws. People remember that I refused him first, and they're right to, but the part I carry is different: after he collapsed, after the ash-things drifted away down the valley like smoke deciding it had better appointments, I knelt beside him and held out the satchel and said, *Here. Take it. You made it.* As if the bag were the point. As if he'd crawled burning up a mountain path because he wanted his papers delivered.

He'd looked at me with eyes going grey as slag and said, "It's not for me," and then he said something else, softer, and I have spent every night since trying to hear it again. I think it was a name. I think it might have been *Villalobos*, though that's impossible, because he was a stranger, and strangers don't know my name, and the alternative—that the name reached him some other way, that the map itself had been calling ahead of me like a bell through fog—is an alternative I'm not ready to sit down with yet.

He died holding my wrist. Not gripping. Holding. The way you hold someone you're trying to pass something to that isn't an object.

So yes. I stepped onto the Hollow Bridge carrying a dead man's satchel and a live man's failure, and I would like the record to show that I got four steps before the bridge noticed.

* * *

Sera went first, naturally. She didn't ask permission; she asked questions, briefly, established that I had no better plan, and then walked out onto the span like a woman crossing her own courtyard.

She didn't weigh anything.

I mean that literally. Watch closely and you'll see it: the stones under her feet stayed exactly as solid as they'd been when empty. No shimmer, no sag, no softening of those translucent patches. She moved across the gorge with her chin up and her ledger clutched to her chest, and the bridge bore her the way the ground bears anyone—as if it had never once considered doing otherwise.

Halfway across she turned, arms out slightly for balance, hair whipping in the wind that came up the gorge cold and mineral-smelling, and called back, "It's fine! Solid as—well. Solid as it ever gets. Come on."

And I stepped forward, and the bridge *saw* me.

There's no gentler way to put it. I felt its attention come up through my boots like water rising in a cellar. The stone under my foot went soft—not crumbling, not breaking, just *uncertain*, the way ice goes uncertain under a man who has heard too many stories about thin ice. My next step sank a finger's width. The step after that, the stone flickered, and I saw straight down through my own footing into the mist below, and the mist below was not mist. It was depth. It was the particular darkness of a place that has been falling for a very long time and hasn't finished.

"Edmund?" Sera's voice, sharp now.

"It's fine," I lied, in the great tradition of liars on bridges.

I stopped moving. That was instinct—stand still, distribute your weight, let the mountain forget you're there. Every smuggler learns it. But the bridge didn't forget. The bridge *remembered*, and I understood, with the cold clarity that comes when your body figures things out several seconds before your mind consents to, that remembering was precisely the problem.

Because the stones under Sera's feet had stayed solid, and Sera is a liar too. She lies about her name, her family, her reasons, half her history. I'd watched her do it easily, fluently, the way she does everything. But she doesn't lie to herself. Whatever she's running from, she runs from it with her eyes open. Her conscience may be crowded, but it's *honest*—every item on the shelf accounted for, labeled, owned.

Mine was not.

Mine had a courier on it who had died reaching for help I was too well-trained to give. Mine had a master dying alone in a tollhouse because I'd chosen the map over him, and I could dress that up as necessity all I liked, but the truth underneath was simpler and uglier: I ran because staying meant watching him die and hearing whatever he'd never say, and I am, at bottom, a man who keeps routes open in every direction including away. Mine had years of small refusals stacked like cordwood—every traveler turned away from the tollhouse, every question about the guild deflected, every silence I'd maintained because silence was the family trade and I was a dutiful son of it.

The bridge pressed down on all of it. Not cruelly. That's the thing I can't shake. It didn't punish. It *weighed*, the way a scale weighs, without malice, without interest, simply reporting the figure. And the figure was bad.

My knee went through the stone.

Not far—an inch, maybe two, enough that I felt the cold of the deep air kiss my shin through my trouser—but far enough. Far enough that the mist below stirred, just slightly, like something turning over in sleep. Far enough that I heard, rising up the gorge, a sound I will be hearing for the rest of my life: a faint, dry rustling, like pages, like ash, like a great many small things deciding to become interested in me.

"EDMUND." Sera was coming back toward me, fast, and I wanted to scream at her to stop because if the bridge judged her again while carrying worry for me—

"Don't," I gasped. "Stay—stay where you are."

"Like hell."

"Sera." I got the word out through my teeth. "If you come back for me, the bridge will weigh *that* too. Whatever you're carrying. It'll add mine to yours. Stay. *Stay.*"

She stopped. Four paces from me, close enough that I could see her knuckles white around the ledger, and for a moment neither of us breathed, and the bridge held us both in its patient arithmetic.

Then she did something I didn't expect. She sat down. Right there, mid-span, cross-legged on stone that held her without complaint, and she looked at me with those court-trained eyes and said:

"Say it."

"Say what?"

"Whatever it's weighing. Say it out loud. To me or to the gorge, I don't care, but say it." Her voice cracked, just once, on the next part. "I know what it's like to carry something you've never said to anyone. I carried mine for three years and it nearly—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The bridge isn't testing whether you're innocent. Nobody's innocent. It's testing whether you'll admit what you're carrying. So admit it."

The ash-rustle rose again from below, closer now, or maybe just more numerous.

And because I was kneeling in a ghost of a bridge over a pit full of listening dark, with a dead man's name caught in my teeth and nowhere left to store it, I said it.

"I let him die." My voice came out smaller than I intended. "He begged and I refused him, out of habit, out of *training*, and when he was dying I still thought about the rules first. And I left Corvin behind. He's dying, he's been dying for a year, and I left him alone in that tollhouse because I couldn't stand to watch, and I called it strategy, and it wasn't, it was cowardice with a map in its pocket, and I don't—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "I don't know how to carry people. I only know how to carry things. Things don't ask anything of you."

The stone firmed under my knee. Just slightly. Enough.

"That's not true," Sera said. "You carried the satchel. You carried me out of that inn cellar when the enforcers came. You've been carrying a dead guild on your back for years and calling it bookkeeping." She stood, slow, careful, offering her hand across the gap between us. "You're a terrible liar, Edmund Villalobos. It's your best quality. Now get up. We're leaving."

I took her hand.

I will not pretend the rest of the crossing was graceful. It took everything the bridge had apparently decided I'd earned—each step firming only as my weight committed, each translucent patch flickering between stone and absence, the whole span humming faintly under us like a struck bell that hadn't decided whether to finish ringing. Twice more it dipped. Twice more I said something true—one of them about my mother, whose face I cannot picture, whose absence has a shape I've lived inside so long I mistake it for my body—and twice the stone remembered itself.

Sera talked the whole way. Not comfort, exactly. Distraction with a blade in it. She told me about her brother—just a fragment, tossed off, the first coin from a purse she guarded like a fortress: *he used to sing when he thought no one was listening, badly, on purpose, to make our mother laugh.* Then she went silent, as if surprised to find she'd said it, and I understood the bridge had weighed her too, somewhere in there, and she'd paid a toll of her own.

We reached the far side as the sun cleared the eastern peaks.

The moment my last boot left the span, the bridge behind us faded. Not collapsed—*thinned*, the way breath thins in cold air, until it was a suggestion, an arch of almost-nothing over the gorge, and then not even that. Just the gorge, and the mist sitting in it, patient as debt.

"Well," Sera said shakily. "That was educational."

"We're not doing it again."

"On the return trip? Absolutely not. We'll go around. There's always a way around."

I laughed. It came out ragged, but it came out, and she smiled—quick, real, gone again—and for a moment we stood there on the far rim of the gorge, two liars with lighter loads than we'd arrived with, and I thought, absurdly, *maybe this arrangement will work after all.*

Then I saw what was on the road ahead of us.

A figure stood at the bend of the switchback below, perhaps two hundred paces down, where the path narrowed against the cliff face. At this distance it might have been a traveler—a hunched shape in a heavy cloak, leaning on the rocks.

Except the wind was blowing hard up the gorge, and the figure's cloak was hanging perfectly still.

And as I watched, it raised its head, and beneath the hood there was no face at all. Only drifting ash, and two points of pale light where eyes had once been, and—impossibly, horribly—its mouth opened, and what came out was not a challenge or a threat.

It was humming. Half a tune. A lullaby's worth of melody, worn smooth as river stone, and it stopped the instant it saw me recognize it, and the creature tilted its head with something terribly like curiosity.

Sera's hand found my arm. "Edmund," she whispered. "Tell me you know that song."

I didn't answer. Because I did know it. I'd known it my whole life without knowing where from—in the space before sleep, in the half-dreams of fever, a melody with no source and no name that I had always assumed was just something the mountains hummed.

The ash-thing turned and walked away down the switchback, unhurried, still humming, leading us—or being led—toward the deep roads.

And every hair on my body stood up, because I realized the song hadn't come from the mountains at all.

It had come from wherever my mother was.
