## 10. Corvin's Confession

They took me at the tollhouse door without breaking stride, which I confess stung more than the hands around my throat. Forty years I'd kept that tollhouse. Forty years of mending shutters and cataloguing dead roads and pretending the Guild of Wayfarers was anything more than a name carved over a door that no longer meant anything—and when the ash came for me at last, they didn't even bother to knock.

I suppose that's what comes of a life spent being unremarkable on purpose. You get so good at it that even your captors find you forgettable.

There were three of them. Not men—though they wore the shapes of men the way a scarecrow wears a coat. Ash moved beneath their skin like silt in slow water, and where their eyes should have been there was only a pale gray shine, the color of a road at dawn before anyone has walked it. They did not speak as they bound my wrists with cord that smelled of cold hearths. They simply looked at me, and I felt the looking go through me like a probe through a wound, searching for something soft to press.

I am an old man. My lungs are shot from forty winters of mountain air, my knees sound like green branches bending, and my heart has been failing by inches since the year Edmund Villalobos was born—which is a sentence I will come back to, because everything in this confession comes back to that year, whether I want it to or not.

So when the tallest of them finally spoke—a voice like wind moving through a burned forest—and asked me where the boy had gone, you might think I told them out of fear.

You would be wrong. I want that recorded clearly, wherever confessions are kept. I did not tell them out of fear.

I told them out of strategy.

* * *

Let me explain how strategy works when you're old and dying and tied to your own kitchen chair by things made of ash.

The boy had run west along the high path toward the Hollow Bridge, taking the supply satchel he absolutely should not have taken, wearing the boots I'd bought him two sizes too large so he'd grow into them, carrying a map that should have been ash in my hearth three days ago. He was sixteen. He was clever in the way smugglers are clever—quick hands, quicker eyes—but he had never once been down into the deep roads, and the map was pulling him toward the one place in Thornfell where cleverness is worth precisely nothing.

He was going to die. That was the arithmetic I did while the ash-things watched me with their road-colored eyes. Alone, unguided, hunted—he was going to die on some switchback or starve in some tunnel, and the Court would take the map off his cooling body, and his death would buy nothing, save nothing, mean nothing except one more grave the mountain wouldn't bother to mark.

But if I went with them—if I led them to him—then I would be there. And an old man who is present can bargain. Can stall. Can stand between a boy and a magistrate and spend whatever few coins of influence his long, quiet service had purchased. The Court remembered me. The Court *had* to remember me; I was the last signatory of record, the final living hand on the guild's founding pact. You do not spend sixty years keeping a debt's paperwork tidy and then get treated like any other debtor.

That was the plan. That is still the plan. I want that understood too.

The fact that my hands shook while I explained it—that the shaking had nothing to do with my heart—is a detail I decline to interpret.

* * *

"West," I told them. "Along the high path. He'll make for the Hollow Bridge, because it's the only crossing over the Gorge of Thorns inside two days' walk, and I taught him that myself." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high, the laugh of a man who has just heard his own voice say something unforgivable. "You see the irony. Everything he knows that could save him, I taught him. Everything he knows that will kill him—I taught him that too."

The tall one tilted its head. Ash sifted from its collar like dandruff from a corpse.

"You offer this freely," it said. It wasn't quite a question. Questions require curiosity, and I don't believe there was any left in what stood before me.

"I offer it in exchange," I said, "for the boy's life."

It considered this. I had the distinct impression that consideration, for them, was less thinking than weighing—some internal scale measuring my words against the Court's ledgers, finding their denomination, assessing their worth.

"The Court does not trade in lives," it said at last. "The Court trades in memory."

"Then here is my memory," I said, and this part I had prepared, God help me, I had prepared it in the hour after Edmund fled and before they arrived, turning the words over like stones looking for the smoothest one. "Sixty years of it. Every route, every cache, every smuggler's sign cut into the stone between here and the lowlands. Take the whole inventory. It's worth more than one boy's recollection of a mother he never met."

And there it was. The thing I'd sworn I'd never say aloud, said aloud, offered up like a merchant naming a price. Because that was the coin I actually intended to spend—not my memories, but his. His ignorance. The mercy of not knowing. I had spent sixteen years building that ignorance brick by brick, lying to him about his mother, about the guild, about the struck-through name in the chapel cavern that he'd find someday if he ever sheltered there, and now I proposed to sell that lie's continuation as if it were a kindness.

Which, I told myself, it was. Which, I told myself, every parent who has ever hidden a truth from a child has told themselves since the first fire was lit.

The ash-thing regarded me for a long moment. Then it did something I had not seen one of them do before. It smiled. Or rather, its face arranged itself into the shape a smile leaves behind, the way a bed holds the shape of the sleeper after he rises.

"The Magistrate will hear your terms," it said. "The Magistrate enjoys hearing terms."

* * *

They marched me east instead of west, which confused me until I understood: we weren't going to the boy. We were going to the Court. My bargain would be struck at the source, in the cathedral of cooled lava where the masked nobles trade in other people's yesterdays, and only afterward would the enforcers turn and follow my directions into the mountains.

I had time to think during that march. Three days, trussed like festival meat, hauled along paths I knew better than the enforcers did—they walked them competently, mechanically, the way a man reads a language he learned from a book rather than a lover. I could have misled them. A fallen cairn here, a false blaze there, and I could have sent them wandering into the Gorge of Thorns until the ash wore thin and the wind unmade them.

I thought about it. I want that honestly recorded as well. I lay awake on the first night, wrapped in my own coat because they didn't bother with blankets for things they considered already half-collected, and I composed the lies with real craftsmanship. Old habits. Sixty years of keeping roads secret will teach a man to build a falsehood the way a mason builds a wall—load-bearing, weatherproof, beautiful if you know where to look.

And then I thought about what would happen when the deception failed. Because it would fail. These were not bandits to be misdirected; they were instruments of a court that had been reading the mountain's memory since before my grandfather's grandfather cut the first toll-road. They would unravel my false trail, and they would return, and they would not ask twice, and whatever patience the Magistrate had extended toward a cooperative witness would curdle into something else entirely.

And Edmund would still be alone on the mountain. Still walking toward the bridge, toward the deep roads, toward the thing beneath the spire that I had helped feed for sixty years without once letting myself say its name.

So I slept. Or something near enough to sleep. And in the morning I kept my mouth shut and my feet moving east, and I called it strategy, and the word fit the deed the way a too-large boot fits a growing foot: loosely, painfully, with room to expand into worse.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about betrayal: it doesn't feel like falling. It feels like walking. One step, then another, each one reasonable, each one defensible, each one taken on ground that seems perfectly solid until you look back and see the bridge is gone behind you and has been gone for miles.

I betrayed the guild first, though I didn't call it that. I betrayed it by silence, by letting it dwindle, by cataloguing its dead routes instead of teaching them, by raising one apprentice instead of ten because one was easier to keep secrets from. I betrayed Edmund's mother second, though God knows I told myself that was protection—the debt was coming due regardless, and a soul surrendered quietly suffers less than a soul dragged screaming, or so I assured myself in the small hours of the year my hands did what they did. I betrayed her son third, by raising him on omissions the way you raise a plant in a dark room: carefully, tenderly, ensuring he would grow crooked and never know why.

And now this. Now the fourth betrayal, delivered in exchange for promises from creatures who do not promise, to a magistrate who does not forgive, on behalf of a boy whose forgiveness I had no right to expect and less right to need.

Except—here is the thing I keep circling, the thing that keeps me honest in this account even when honesty flays me—I do not actually intend to deliver him.

Not cleanly. Not wholly. Hear me out.

The bargain I struck in the ember-light of the Court's antechamber was this: my guidance in exchange for the boy's memory remaining his own. Not his freedom—the Magistrate laughed at that, a sound like a landslide deciding to be polite—but his *self*. He could be taken, questioned, even held. But what he knew of his mother, what he knew of himself, would remain sealed behind his own eyes. The Court agreed, because the Court believes all bargains are traps and enjoys letting the trapper discover which side of the trap he's standing on.

What I did not put into the bargain—what I carried out of that antechamber folded small against my heart like contraband—was the rest of the plan. The part where I lead them on the longest possible road. The part where old men fall ill in convenient ravines and burn bridges behind them. The part where Corvin Halloway, last guildmaster of the Wayfarers, spends whatever remains of his ruined life buying time with treachery, spending his credibility like a drunkard spends his last coin, so that somewhere ahead of him a boy with quick hands might reach the vault before the solstice and do the thing I never had the courage to attempt.

Is that strategy? Or is it just betrayal wearing strategy's coat, the way the enforcers wear men's coats?

I've decided it doesn't matter. I've decided that at my age, with my lungs, on this road, the distinction is a luxury. Call it what you like. The boy lives or dies by whether my treachery runs faster than my guilt, and I have never in sixty years let guilt set the pace.

* * *

On the third night, camped below the scree fields where the mountain's bones show through its skin, the tall enforcer sat across the fire from me—or stood in the shape of sitting; firelight behaved strangely around them, sinking into the ash of them rather than dancing off—and asked me a question I hadn't anticipated.

"Why does the boy matter to you?"

I gave it the prepared answer. Duty. The last apprentice of a dying guild; a master's obligation; the guild's honor, such as it remained.

It waited. Ash drifted off its shoulders and did not land anywhere, which I try not to think about.

"He has no one else," I said finally.

"That is not why," it said. Not cruelly. There was no cruelty left in it to be cruel with. It said it the way a ledger says a number. "You have lied four times tonight. The Court hears lies the way you hear bells. Why does the boy matter?"

And I looked into that gray shine where eyes should be, and I thought: this thing was a person once. Whatever the Court took to make it obedient, it left the shape of questions behind, the way a quarry leaves the shape of the stone. And I thought: if I cannot answer honestly to a hollowed man beside a fire, then every resolution I've made is ash too, and I may as well hand the Magistrate the whole rotten inventory of me.

"Because I sold his mother," I said.

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, snow slid off a ledge with a sound like a held breath released.

"The guild owed a debt. The debt was mine to pay, as guildmaster. The price was a soul, and I chose hers, because she was nobody, because she was lowland-born and unrecorded, because no one would come asking." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "Because she trusted me. She handed me her newborn son and asked me to keep him safe, and I said yes, and I kept him safe, and I paid for the keeping with her. And he grew up in my house calling me Master, and he has her eyes, and he asks about her every year on his birthday, and every year I lie to him, and every year the lie gets heavier, and I have carried it so long I no longer know whether I am a man or just the shape a lie leaves in a chair."

The enforcer was silent a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice that for one syllable sounded almost human:

"She sang. In the vaults. They all sing, at first."

And it turned away, and I lay back against the cold stone, and I did not sleep at all, and I told myself—strategy, old man, strategy—all the way down into the dark.

* * *

In the morning the scouts returned. Ash moves fast when it wants to, faster than any flesh, and theirs had gone out at first light in three directions like smoke seeking draft. The tall one read their report—I never learned whether that was gesture or speech or something older—and then its road-colored gaze came back to me, and there was something new in it. Something almost like courtesy. The courtesy of a headsman.

"They found him," it said. "On the high path. With company."

"Company," I repeated. My heart did its unreliable stutter, and for once I couldn't blame the years.

"A girl. Noble-bred, by the scent of her. Runaway." It paused, and the smile-shape returned to its face. "And farther back, following the same road at a slower pace—" it leaned close, and its breath smelled of every fire that had ever gone out— "a man's tracks. Old boots. A limping gait. Heading west, Master Corvin. Toward the boy."

I felt the blood leave my face, and the enforcer watched it go with what I swear was enjoyment, and I thought one word, one name, one curse, and it was not a holy one.

Because I knew those old boots. I knew that limp. There was only one man in Thornfell foolish enough, guilty enough, and desperate enough to be following Edmund west—and I had left him behind at the tollhouse specifically so that he could not interfere, could not confess, could not undo with his soft heart what I was attempting with my hard one.

Aldous. My predecessor's clerk. The one man alive besides me who knew what the debt was, and when it fell due, and what the boy would find in the vault if he reached it.

The enforcer extended its gray hand to help me up, courteous as a headsman, patient as ash.

"Come, Master Corvin," it said. "Your bargain stands. But I think you should see who else is coming to collect it."
