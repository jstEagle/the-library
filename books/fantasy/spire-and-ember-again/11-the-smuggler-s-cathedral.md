## 11. The Smuggler's Cathedral

The storm found us an hour after dark, the kind of mountain storm that doesn't fall so much as hunt—sideways, screaming, full of ice that wanted to flay us down to the bone and then apologize to the bone personally.

"We need shelter," Sera shouted, for the fourth time in as many minutes, as though I might have forgotten we were standing on a ledge above a drop that would make our deaths very brief and very thorough.

"I'm aware," I shouted back.

"You're *walking*."

"That's how I get places. It's a smuggler thing. You wouldn't understand."

She made a sound that the wind mostly ate but which I suspected was not complimentary. Two days of travel together had taught me her entire vocabulary of disapproval: there was the sniff, the sigh, the flat stare, and this—the noise she made when she'd decided complaining was beneath her but hadn't yet figured out what to do with all that leftover contempt. It was, I'll admit, growing on me. Like moss. Or a debt.

The truth was that I did know where shelter was. Every Wayfarer worth his salt carried a map in his head of every hole in Thornfell big enough to die in comfortably, and three miles ahead, tucked into the cliff face above the Greywater falls, was one of the oldest of them. The old-timers had called it the Cathedral. I'd been there exactly once, at fourteen, when Corvin still bothered to teach me things instead of just handing me ledgers and silence.

Getting there required crossing the falls' shoulder on a path about as wide as my conscience, which, given recent events, was narrower than usual.

"Is that—" Sera began, peering through the white chaos.

"Don't look down."

"I wasn't going to look down."

"Everyone says that. Then they look down." I kept my eyes on the rock, on the worn hollows where generations of boots had polished handholds into the stone. That's the thing about smuggler paths: they're not marked, but they're *worn*. A hundred years of people who couldn't afford to be seen, moving through the dark by touch and memory. You could read the mountain like a ledger if you knew how. Every groove was an entry. Every polished stone was a name nobody wrote down.

I thought about that more than I should have, later.

We came off the path into the cavern mouth as the storm redoubled behind us, furious at losing its quarry, and I stood there dripping and gasping while my eyes adjusted to a darkness that wasn't entirely dark.

Because the Cathedral glowed.

Not much. Not enough to see by, exactly—a faint warm shimmer from deep inside, like the last coals of a fire someone had walked away from years ago and never quite gone out. I'd forgotten that. Fourteen-year-old me had stood in this same spot and felt the hair rise on his arms, and Corvin had said, *Emberstone. The mountain's old blood. Don't touch it,* and then he'd changed the subject the way he always did, with the practiced ease of a man who'd spent forty years changing subjects.

Sera pushed past me into the dark, because Sera had never once waited for permission in her life, and then stopped so suddenly I nearly went into her back.

"Oh," she said quietly.

I understood why, a moment later, when my own eyes finished adjusting.

* *

The smugglers hadn't built a chapel. They'd *found* one—or made one, depending on how you think about these things—and they'd spent two centuries decorating it the only way people like us decorate: by carving.

The walls were covered in it. Prayers, mostly, though some were curses and a few were jokes, and at least one—I checked twice—was a recipe for stew, which tells you something about the spiritual priorities of the Thornfell trade. Names everywhere. *Maren of the Low Road, keep my feet.* *For Tam, who fell at Hollow Bridge, 3rd of Thaw.* *Blessed be the dark that hides us.* Thousands of them, cut shallow and deep, crude and careful, layered over each other like sediment. Some were older than the guild itself, if you judged by the letterforms. Some were fresh enough that the chisel-marks still held shadow.

And threading between them, running up the walls and across the ceiling in slow veins, was the emberstone—that same faint warmth, glowing like veins of memory in the rock.

"It's beautiful," Sera said, and for once there was no irony in it. She had her hood back and her face turned up, wet hair plastered to her cheek, and the emberlight moved on her skin like water. "It's like... a church, except honest."

"Smugglers don't do dishonesty well," I said. "Lying takes leisure. We never had any."

"You lie constantly."

"I exaggerate. It's different. Lying is a profession; exaggeration is a survival trait."

She snorted, but softly, and then went quiet again, walking slowly along the wall with her fingers hovering over the carvings without touching them, the way you hover over things in temples. I watched her mouth move as she read. She'd been reading everything since we left the tollhouse—signs, trail markers, the labels on my supply crates—with the hungry attention of someone who'd grown up surrounded by words that were chosen *for* her and was making up for lost time.

I busied myself with the practical work. Dry corner, out of the wind's reach. Fuel for a small fire—small, screened, the kind of fire you build when you assume you're being hunted, because we were. I got it going and put the kettle on, because tea is the one ritual no amount of catastrophe has ever managed to interrupt in the mountains. You can lose your home, your guild, your master, your entire understanding of your life. The kettle still goes on. It's not comfort, exactly. It's proof that tomorrow expects something of you.

Sera drifted over eventually and sat across the fire from me, knees drawn up, hands wrapped around the cup I handed her.

"They're all here," she said. "All the names. People the guild erased from its own records—they carved themselves somewhere the guild couldn't reach." Her jaw tightened. "My father keeps a library. Locked rooms, chained books, a man whose whole job is deciding who's allowed to know what. I used to think that was power." She looked around at the walls. "This is power. This is the opposite of a locked room."

"The guild has records too," I said. "Route ledgers. Toll tallies. Forty years of them, and I've catalogued every page." I turned my cup. "You know what's not in any of them? Where the guild came from. Who founded it. What it cost. Corvin's been dying for two years and I've asked him maybe a hundred times, and every time he looks at me like I've asked him to open a vein."

"And now?"

"Now I have a map drawn in ink that moves like it's alive, ash creatures hunting me across half a mountain, and a master who ordered me to burn the only clue I've ever gotten." I shrugged. "Now I ask louder."

Sera studied me over the rim of her cup. She has a way of looking at you that makes you feel audited. "You loved him," she said. Not a question. "Corvin. Despite everything."

"He raised me." The fire popped. "He found me somewhere—I don't even know where, he's never said—and he fed me and taught me the roads and never once told me anything true. And I loved him anyway, because when you're a child, the person who feeds you is the whole world, and it takes years to learn the world can be small and cruel and still be the only one you've got." I heard my own voice go flat and stopped talking, because I hadn't meant to say that much, and because the alternative was saying more.

Sera didn't push. That's one of the things I'd started to respect about her: she collected secrets the way other nobles collected porcelain, carefully, and she knew when not to handle one.

Instead she stood, brushed off her skirts, and said, "I'm going to read the walls. Wake me if the ash men find us."

"If the ash men find us, waking you will be redundant."

"*Wake me anyway.* I want to see their faces."

She took a torch stub from the fire and went wandering off into the carvings, and I sat alone with the kettle and the emberlight and the particular loneliness of a man sitting inside two hundred years of other people's prayers.

That's when I started reading them myself.

* *

I'd seen the walls before, at fourteen, but I'd seen them the way children see everything—impatiently, waiting for the lesson to end. Now I read them properly, working outward from the fire, and the Cathedral began to arrange itself into history the way ruins do when you give them time.

The oldest carvings were near the back, where the emberstone ran thickest. Simple petitions. *Keep us warm. Keep us hidden. Keep the road under our feet.* The handwriting of a hard century. Then, gradually, the prayers changed, and it was the change that made the back of my neck prickle.

They stopped asking for safety and started asking for *forgetting*.

*Let them forget us. Let the tolls pass unremembered. Blessed be the bargain, and blind be those who keep it.*

I stood in front of that one for a long time. *Blind be those who keep it.* Carved by somebody who knew exactly what the guild had paid for safe passage, and who had decided that ignorance was a mercy—or maybe that ignorance was the price, that forgetting was part of the payment, that the Court beneath the mountain ate memories the way I ate bread. I didn't know that yet. I want to be clear about that. What I knew was that the words frightened me in a way I couldn't name, the way a path frightens you when the fog comes in and you realize you can't remember the last landmark.

Further along, the prayers turned desperate. A cluster of carvings from what must have been the guild's final years, the decades when the Wayfarers stopped being a brotherhood and became a rumor. *Where did they take Maren?* *The collectors came at solstice.* *Do not sign. Do not sign. DO NOT SIGN.*

Someone had scratched over that last one, later, in a different hand: *too late.*

I crouched there with the torch, feeling cold that had nothing to do with the storm outside, and told myself I was looking for route information. Old chapels often marked water sources. Escape tunnels. Practical things. That was my story and I was sticking to it, right up until my eye caught on a section of wall set slightly apart from the rest—a panel of stone, smoother than the others, deliberately cleared, the way you clear ground for something important.

Family names. Dozens of them, carved in a careful column, each with a date. Not smugglers' aliases—real names, the kind you only carve when you're marking a debt or a grave. And I read down the list the way you read anything, idly, letting the names slide past—

Villalobos.

My own surname, cut deep into the stone, in letters that had been made with care and then—deliberately, violently, with a blade or a chisel dragged hard enough to bite—struck through. One clean line, corner to corner. An erasure. The kind of mark you put on a ledger entry when the account is closed.

The date beside it was the year I was born.

I don't remember standing up. I remember the torch shaking, though, and the light shivering across the walls until all the carved faces of all the dead smugglers seemed to move. I remember Sera's voice coming from somewhere behind me, distant, asking if I was all right, and I remember not answering, because I was busy doing arithmetic I didn't want to finish.

The year I was born. The year Corvin appeared with a child he refused to explain. The year the guild's records go strange and thin, the year the last entries trail off into nothing, the year my master started answering questions with silence. The same year someone stood in this cave with a chisel and struck my family's name through like a bad debt.

Like a *paid* debt.

"Edmund." Sera's hand on my arm. Warm. Real. "You've gone white. What is it?"

I pointed. My voice, when it finally worked, came out sanded down to almost nothing. "That's my name."

She read it. I watched her read it—the date, the strike-through, the careful cleared space around it as if the carvers had stepped back from that name the way you step back from a body. When she looked up at me, her expression had gone through surprise into something colder and more awake.

"The year you were born," she said.

"The year I was born."

"Edmund." She turned back to the wall, and her voice dropped, quick and low, the voice she used for facts she didn't like. "The ledger I stole—the Court's ledger—it lists souls by family line. Whole lines, sold together, generation after generation. I never looked for yours. I didn't know it mattered." Her fingers hovered over the struck-through name, not touching it, the same way she hadn't touched the prayers. "But debts like this don't skip. They *inherit*. If your family's name is on the guild's oldest wall, struck through the year you were born—"

"Then I'm not carrying the map," I said. The words came out of me like something being pulled with forceps. "I'm carrying the *bill*."

Outside, the storm screamed against the mountain, and somewhere beneath my feet—beneath all of us, beneath two hundred years of prayers scratched into stone by people begging to be forgotten—the emberstone pulsed once, slow and deep, like something far below turning over in its sleep.

And in the emberlight, I swear the next carving down the wall, half-hidden in shadow, wasn't a prayer at all.

It was directions. Down.
