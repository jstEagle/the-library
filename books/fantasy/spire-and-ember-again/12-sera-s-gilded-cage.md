## 12. Sera's Gilded Cage

The cage was gilded because my father believed that anything unpleasant could be made acceptable with enough gold leaf.

I learned this at seven, when he had the nursery bars painted. I remember thinking, even then, *the bars are still bars.* I remember saying it, too, which is why I spent that evening in the dark with no supper. My mother wept for me through the door and did not open it, and I understood that lesson as well: love, in House Amadine, was something you expressed on the far side of a locked thing.

By nineteen, I had catalogued every bar in my life. The corset laced by a maid who wouldn't meet my eyes. The tutors who taught me heraldry and harp and the precise angle at which to incline one's head toward a man whose family owned more of the river valley than mine. And now Lord Cassel Vane — no, *Lord* Cassel Vane, the title mattered to him more than his own name — who had visited our estate three times, eaten eleven meals at my father's table, and looked at me exactly the way one looks at a purchase awaiting delivery.

The betrothal contract sat in my father's study like a held breath.

"You'll be happy," my father told me, the night before the signing. He said it the way he announced harvest yields. "Cassel is a good match. His interests align with ours."

*His interests.* As though I were a road, or a toll, or a seam of ore. Perhaps I was. In the ledger-logic of the valley houses, daughters were assets that matured into alliances. I had been accruing value since birth, and now I was due.

"Will *I* be happy?" I asked.

My father's pen paused above the parchment. That pause told me everything. He hadn't considered the question. It wasn't cruelty; it was worse. It was category error. Happiness was not a line item.

"You will be *provided for*," he said, and signed nothing yet, but the ink was already mixed, and we both knew it.

That night I did not sleep. I lay in my bed beneath the canopy my mother had embroidered with doves — doves, the great fools, symbols of peace and domesticity stitched over the head of a girl being sold — and I made a list. Not of grievances. Of exits.

This is what no one understands about caged things: we are not dreaming of freedom in some vague, sunlit way. We are doing arithmetic. Window: two floors, gravel below, guards at the gate change at midnight. Servants' stair: locked after ten, key kept by Mrs. Halloway, who takes brandy after supper and sleeps like the dead she half-resembles. The east wall where the orchard meets the shepherd's track: unwatched, because no one believes a lady in silk would run toward mountains.

No one believes it because no one has ever been desperate enough to test the belief.

I tested it nine days later, on the eve of my wedding, wearing my mother's traveling cloak over a wedding dress I had cut the skirt from — because silk rustles, and rustle is death when you're moving through bracken in the dark — and carrying nothing but a knife, bread, and the one thing I had stolen that mattered.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The theft came first, and the theft is the part that matters, so let me go back to the beginning, where all cages begin: with an invitation.

* * *

Three weeks before my wedding, Lord Cassel hosted a gathering at Vane Hall, and among the guests — I never learned how he earned the invitation, and I have stopped asking questions whose answers only frighten me — was a magistrate of the Ember Court.

You must understand what the Court was to people like me. A story. A warning grandmothers used. *Mind your debts, child, or the mountain collects.* The Court beneath Thornfell was where desperate houses settled desperate accounts, and everyone spoke of it in whispers and jokes, the way you speak of things you've decided aren't real because deciding they're real would require deciding your whole world is built on them.

The magistrate wore a mask of pale porcelain, featureless except for two slits, and moved through Cassel's ballroom like cold air through a cracked window. People drifted away from it without seeming to notice they were drifting. I noticed. I have always been good at noticing — it's the one skill my gilded education actually sharpened, all those years of reading rooms instead of books.

And I saw the magistrate speak with my father.

I saw my father's face while they spoke. I had spent nineteen years learning that face, and I had never seen it like that. Not angry. Not afraid, even. *Small.* My father, who filled doorways and filled rooms and filled my childhood with the weight of his certainty, stood beside the masked figure looking like a man standing beside an open well.

They went into the study. The door closed.

Now. Here is where a sensible woman returns to the dancing and drinks enough wine to forget. Here is where a sensible woman tells herself that fathers have business, and business is dull, and dullness is safe.

I have never once been accused of being sensible.

The study window opened onto the rose terrace — my father's study, the room I'd been summoned to for lectures since I could walk, the room whose every creak I knew. I slipped out during a quadrille, crossed the terrace with my heart hammering a rhythm no orchestra could have matched, and put my ear to the stone.

I caught fragments. *...the arrangement holds... ...another season, perhaps two... ...you cannot renegotiate what was already paid...*

And then my father, voice cracking on something between fury and terror: "*It was supposed to be the guild's debt. The Wayfarers took that burden off us. You said—*"

"*The Wayfarers,*" the magistrate said, and its voice was the strangest thing I heard that night, smooth as poured glass, "*are nearly finished. Debts do not vanish, Lord Amadine. They wait. And when the holder fails, they return to the signatory.*"

A silence. Then my father, very quietly, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant: "What does the Court want?"

"What it has always wanted," said the magistrate. "What was promised."

I heard footsteps then — the magistrate moving toward the door — and I did the single most reckless thing of a life that has since become a competition in recklessness. I stepped back around the corner of the terrace, pressed myself against the cold stone beside the window, and waited.

The magistrate emerged. Paused. Turned its porcelain face toward the garden, toward me, toward the exact shadow I occupied.

"The roses bloom early this year," it said, to no one, and glided back inside.

I ran. I don't remember crossing the ballroom or making my excuses or the carriage home. I remember my hands shaking so badly I couldn't work the cloak clasp, and I remember one thought repeating like a heartbeat: *my father owes the Court something, and the guild that used to pay it is dying, and when it dies, whatever was promised comes due.*

I should have felt fear. What I felt instead was the strangest clarity of my life. Because if my father owed the Court — if every house in the valley perhaps owed the Court — then the Court kept accounts. And accounts meant records.

And records can be stolen.

* * *

Here is what else I knew, from a source I have never fully explained even to myself: my brother Tam had gone missing four years ago.

Missing is the word the family used. Tam was twenty-two, restless, brilliant at maps and terrible at propriety, forever running off to walk the mountain paths with the last of the Wayfarers — those gray, quiet guides whom polite society pretended not to see, the way you pretend not to see beggars, because seeing them would mean admitting the world has cracks. Tam loved the cracks. Tam *collected* them.

He left in autumn, saying he'd found a road on no map and meant to walk it. He didn't come home for winter. Or spring. My father hired searchers, then stopped hiring them, then forbade Tam's name at table. My mother took to her room for a season and came out of it older. And I — I was fifteen, and furious, and I decided my brother had chosen the mountains over us, and I hated him for it with the clean, stupid hatred of the young.

Four years is long enough for hatred to rot into something else. By nineteen, what I carried wasn't anger. It was a question I couldn't put down: *what kind of road makes a man forget to come home?*

The night I heard my father and the magistrate, that question grew teeth. Because I remembered — I had always remembered, filed away like everything else — that Tam's letters had stopped arriving not gradually but *all at once*, mid-correspondence, mid-sentence almost, his last note full of excitement about a tunnel and a light under the mountain.

*A light under the mountain.*

*What was promised.*

I sat up until dawn doing arithmetic again. Exits, I called them once. This time the sum was different. If the Court collected debts, and debts were souls — grandmother's warnings, whispered, laughed at, *believed* — then somewhere beneath Thornfell there was a record of what it held. And if Tam had walked a road under the mountain and never walked out—

Then maybe my brother wasn't missing.

Maybe my brother was *collateral.*

* * *

Getting into the Court's records was impossible. Getting into the Court's records *through the front* was impossible. So I did what any sensible woman would do, having failed at sensibility my entire life: I got engaged.

Lord Cassel Vane wanted a wife with connections, and the Court, it turned out, wanted something from Cassel — I pieced it together over the following weeks, watching servants, listening at doors, assembling the picture the way Tam used to assemble his maps. Cassel's house was old, and old houses in the valley had old arrangements, and the Court had recently begun calling in its paper. Cassel served as a tribute-bearer: twice a year, he led a party down into the foothills with wagons of goods — grain, cloth, wine, and other things the manifests never listed — and delivered them to a waystation where the ash-folk collected them. In exchange, the Court left Vane lands alone.

Twice a year. Tribute-bearers. Wagons going down.

And a bride-to-be who, by custom so ancient no one questioned it anymore, would accompany her lord on his first tribute journey after the wedding, to be presented, to be seen, to be *displayed* — because that is what wives were for, in the valley houses. Assets on parade.

I was going to be married into the Court's service either way. The only question was whether I arrived as property or as a thief.

So I let the engagement proceed. I smiled at Cassel across eleven more meals. I learned his household, his routes, his guards' rotations, and the location of his study — because tribute-bearers keep their own copies of their manifests, and Cassel kept his in a locked drawer, and men like Cassel believe locks are for thieves rather than for wives, which tells you everything about how they imagine wives.

The night before my wedding — my *real* escape night, the night of the cut dress and the east wall — I went to his study first.

The lock yielded to a hairpin and forty seconds. Inside the drawer: manifests, contracts, correspondence with the waystation. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a book.

Not a manifest. A ledger. Old, bound in leather gone soft as skin, its pages crowded with columns in an ink that seemed faintly warm to the touch. Names. Hundreds of names. Dates of entry. Amounts — not coin, the amounts were written in words like *years* and *songs* and *a face remembered at a window* — and against each name, a mark.

Some marks read *PAID.* Some read *HELD.*

I turned the pages with fingers that would not stop trembling, scanning, scanning, telling myself I didn't know what I was looking for, knowing perfectly well.

Amadine. There were three entries under Amadine — my grandfather, my father, each marked PAID in that warm, patient ink. Paid with what? I read the amounts and felt the floor tilt. *One summer of a daughter's laughter.* *The color of a wife's eyes.* My family had been paying the Court in pieces of itself for three generations, and no one had ever noticed the losses, because that is the genius of a debt collected in memory: you cannot miss what you cannot remember missing.

And then, fourth entry. Recent. Four years old.

*Tamasin Amadine. Entered: autumn. Held.*

The ink was still faintly warm under my finger. Four years, and the ledger remembered him better than I had been allowed to. *Held.* Not dead. Not lost. Held — the way you hold collateral, the way you hold something you intend to keep.

I heard a footstep in the corridor and did the arithmetic in half a second: window, two floors, gravel, guards changing at midnight. I folded the ledger flat against my stomach beneath the cloak I'd brought for exactly this purpose, closed the drawer, relocked the lock, and went out through the study door like I owned the place — because for one more day, by contract, I nearly did.

Then the wall, the orchard, the shepherd's track, the mountains rising black against the stars. I won't romanticize it. It was cold and terrifying and I fell six times and cried twice and laughed once, wildly, somewhere on the high path at dawn, at the absurdity of a runaway bride in a mutilated wedding dress climbing toward the one place in the world the Court's reach supposedly ended.

Thornfell. The smuggler's paths. The last roads of the dying Wayfarers' Guild.

If the guild was dying, its debts were coming due. If the debts were coming due, someone was about to come looking for the ledger's bearer. And if I was going to survive, I needed someone who knew these paths the way Tam had known them — someone gray and quiet and invisible, a guide, a Wayfarer, the last of a dying breed.

I'd been hiding in the high valleys for two weeks when I saw the ash-folk interrogate an innkeeper, and understood they weren't hunting me at all.

They were hunting a map.

And whoever carried it was walking straight into the same mountain that held my brother.

* * *

So now you know what I carry, Edmund Villalobos, when you catch me stealing your dried meat and grumble at me like a scandalized landlady. Now you know why I flinch at warm ink and count exits in every room and check your sleeping face for signs that you might sell me to save yourself.

I know what you think of me — the noblewoman slumming it in the wilds, all sharp tongue and soft hands. Let me tell you what my soft hands have done. They picked a lock on the worst night of my life. They held a ledger that weighs more than any book should. They tore a wedding dress into rags. They have been cold for two months, and they have not once opened a door back toward the life behind me.

My father sold my hand and paid the Court in laughter and eye-color and never noticed the withdrawals. My mother loved me through a locked door. My brother walked into a mountain chasing a light and became a line of warm ink in a stolen book.

*Tamasin Amadine. Held.*

Every step I take up this frozen misery of a mountain, I take toward that line. Not for freedom — freedom is just the word caged things use for the space between bars. For Tam. For the boy who loved cracks in the world, who is lying in some vault beneath the stone with his name marked HELD while our father pays the Court in pieces of the family he pretends is whole.

The guide I've found is young and stubborn and carries guilt like a second coat, and he doesn't trust me, and I haven't told him about the ledger, because a thief knows better than to show her whole hand.

But tomorrow, or the day after, when the moment comes — and it will come, moments like that always come — I am going to open that oilcloth wrapping and show him names. Guild names. Dead men's names, marked HELD in warm ink.

And I am going to watch the last Wayfarer understand that his guild didn't die out at all.

It was collected.
