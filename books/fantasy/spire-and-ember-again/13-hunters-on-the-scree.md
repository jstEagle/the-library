## 13. Hunters on the Scree

There is a particular sound a scree slope makes when someone is walking on it who does not want to be heard. It's not silence—nothing on scree is ever silent; the whole slope breathes and settles and mutters to itself like an old man in his sleep. It's a *careful* sound. Stones shifted with intent rather than accident. A rhythm too even for wind.

I had been listening to it for half an hour before I said anything.

"We're being followed," I told Sera, without turning around. "Three of them. Maybe four. They're good."

"They're *what*?" She'd been picking her way along the ledge above me, arms out like a tightrope walker, because Sera Amadine walked mountains the way she did everything else—as though they ought to be grateful for her attention. "Followed by whom? The ash men?"

"The ash men don't walk careful." I crouched by a boulder and pressed my palm flat against it, feeling for warmth. Nothing yet. That was something. "Ash men don't need to. They come straight at you like weather. This is somebody else. Somebody who knows these paths well enough to be polite about it."

"Polite," she repeated, in the tone of a woman cataloguing all the reasons she regretted her life choices. "You're telling me we're being stalked *politely*."

"I'm telling you we're being stalked *professionally*. Politeness would be them announcing themselves and giving us a head start." I stood, brushed grit off my knees, and finally looked back the way we'd come. The switchbacks below us stitched down through grey fields of broken stone, and beyond them the pines went dark and endless toward the valley. Somewhere under all that grey, three or four somebodies were climbing our trail with the patience of men paid by the hour.

Sera came up beside me and squinted down the slope. "I don't see anything."

"No," I agreed. "That's rather the point of them."

* * *

Let me explain what scree is, since you may have had the good fortune never to meet it. When a mountain gets old and tired, its face starts coming off in flakes—shale and shattered granite, acres of loose stone from the size of your fist to the size of a coffin, piled at the angle of repose, which is a surveyor's polite way of saying *the angle at which everything wants to kill you*. Walk wrong on scree and you start a landslide that carries you two hundred yards down and buries you under your own argument with gravity. Walk right on it, and it holds. Mostly. There's a craft to it. My master taught it to me when I was nine years old, and he taught it mean, which is the only way it sticks.

"There are three rules," Corvin had said, standing on a slope exactly like this one, hands behind his back like a schoolmaster at a hanging. "One: weight low, steps short. Two: never cross a slope below another traveler unless you've told them, because your stones will find their skull. Three—" and here he'd smiled, which he almost never did, "—scree remembers everyone who crosses it. So leave nothing for it to remember."

I was thinking about rule three as I studied the slope below us, because whoever was climbing it had left plenty. Not tracks—loose stone doesn't hold tracks—but *tells*. A stone here rolled a quarter-turn instead of a half. A patch of lichen scraped at knee height where someone had braced themselves. A smell, faint and mineral, of rock dust freshly turned. Little courtesies of movement that added up, if you knew how to read them, to *yes, there are people here, and yes, they are very good, and no, they do not know that you know.*

The trouble with being raised by smugglers' guildmasters is that you learn to read slopes the way priests read scripture, and then you spend the rest of your life unable to stop.

"We can't outrun them," I said. "Not uphill, not downhill. Scree punishes runners."

"So what do we do?" Sera had gone quiet in the way she did when fear arrived and she decided to give it a chair rather than a bed. I was learning her tells. The chin came up. The hands stopped fidgeting. It was, I'll admit, a little impressive.

"We let them catch us," I said.

Her chin came up higher. "*Pardon?*"

"Not here. Not on their terms." I pointed east, along the contour of the mountain, to where the scree field narrowed into a gully between two ribs of solid rock. "There's a place up ahead. Old smuggler ground. We call it the Throat. Narrow enough that four men become one man, steep enough that nobody can flank us, and the footing is mine, not theirs. If they want us, they come through single file, off-balance, on ground I've known since I was small enough to fit in a satchel."

"And if they simply wait for us to starve in your Throat?"

"Then we starve in comfort, with a view." I was already moving. "Come on. And Sera—"

"What."

"Step where I step. Exactly where I step. No questions until we're through."

She opened her mouth, thought better of it, and closed it again. For a noblewoman on the run, she was learning fast. Or she was terrified. With Sera those two things wore the same face more often than either of us liked to admit.

* * *

The Throat was exactly as I remembered it, which is to say ugly, narrow, and perfect. Two walls of living rock leaned together overhead like conspirators, leaving a slot barely wide enough for a man with a pack, floored with a jumble of stone that looked random and wasn't—not if you knew which slabs were seated and which were lying in wait. My master had brought me through here a dozen times, and once, memorably, had made me cross it blindfolded while he threw pebbles at my head.

"Character-building," he'd called it.

I'd called it several other things, none of which I'll repeat here.

We got through to the wide pocket at the top—a hollow maybe ten paces across, walled on three sides, open only to the slot below—and I set us up. Sera against the back wall, out of any thrown line. Me at the mouth of the slot, sitting down, deliberately visible, deliberately relaxed. I took out our water skin and drank from it, slowly, ostentatiously.

"You're going to just *sit* there?" Sera hissed.

"I'm going to sit here and look tired and stupid," I said. "Which, conveniently, requires no acting on my part right now."

"That's not—"

"Sera." I kept my voice level. "They've been following us since dawn. Men who follow for hours don't rush. They're reading us the way I read the slope. So we give them something to read. We give them a tired boy and a frightened lady cornered in a dead end, and we let them commit to the slot, because the moment they're in it, they belong to the mountain, and the mountain belongs to me."

She stared at me for a long moment. "You've done this before."

"No," I said honestly. "But my master did, and he made me watch, and he made me take notes afterward, and I hated every minute of it. Turns out hatred is an excellent study aid."

And then, because the mountain provides, the first stone clicked in the slot below us.

* * *

He came up the Throat the way water comes up a drain—inevitable, patient, wrong. First just a hand, gloved in grey leather, finding a hold I knew was there. Then a shoulder, a hooded head, a lean body moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had climbed a great many dangerous places and expected to climb a great many more. He stopped six feet below me, in the deepest part of the slot, where the walls pinched and his shoulders nearly touched both sides at once.

His hood tipped back. Ordinary face. Weathered, forty-ish, eyes the flat brown of a hawk's feathers. He didn't look like an ash-enforcer. He looked like a man. That was somehow worse.

"Afternoon," he said. His voice was pleasant. Genuinely pleasant, the voice of a man who talked to innkeepers for a living. "Nice spot you've found."

"It has its advantages," I said.

"Word is there's a courier's satchel on this mountain. Ember-ink map inside it. Belongs to certain parties down-valley who take a proprietary interest." He said it all easily, conversationally, a man discussing grain prices. "Those parties pay well for returns. Better than they pay for excuses."

"And what do they pay for witnesses?"

He smiled. It reached his eyes, which made it worse, not better. "Now that's the question, isn't it, lad."

Behind him, deeper in the slot, a second shape moved. Then, faintly, a third. Four after all. Good odds for arithmetic, terrible odds for everything else.

Here is the thing about the Throat that I hadn't told Sera, because telling her would have meant explaining, and explaining would have meant admitting how much I wanted this to work. The floor of the slot—the beautiful, innocent-looking floor—is a cheat. Halfway up, beneath a slab the size of a door, the old guild had cut a chamber out of the mountain and roofed it with that slab, propped on two wedges of wood that had been replaced every few years by every tollkeeper who kept the high routes. Pull the wedges, and the slab drops, and everything standing on it goes down into a hole with no ladder and no light and a floor of rubble thirty feet below.

Corvin had shown it to me when I was twelve. "Insurance," he'd said, and hadn't elaborated, and I hadn't asked, because by twelve I'd learned which questions got answered and which ones got you sent to bed hungry.

The wedges were behind a loose stone at my left hip. I'd checked them that morning, out of habit, the way other people check whether they've locked their door.

The man in the slot took one more step up. Onto the slab. His boot settled on it with a soft, trusting crunch.

"Last chance to walk away," he offered pleasantly.

"You know," I said, "that's funny. I was about to say the same thing."

I pulled the wedge.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because Sera saw all of it and later she'd describe it back to me in a tone I didn't know how to answer.

The slab dropped clean. The man dropped with it—one short shout, cut off by the dark—and the second climber behind him had time to grab the wall and hang there, half-in, half-out of the slot, cursing with real feeling. The third and fourth scrambled back down out of range. Dust boiled up out of the hole like the mountain exhaling.

And I laughed.

I laughed, kneeling at the edge of the gap with the wooden wedges still in my fist, watching the dust roll past me, and it wasn't a nervous laugh or a grim one. It was *joy*. Pure, uncomplicated, singing joy, the laugh of a boy flying a kite, and it went on for three full seconds before I caught it and strangled it.

Because in those three seconds, some part of me that had been asleep for years woke up and stretched. Every hour of blindfolded crossings and thrown pebbles and cold camps and Corvin's merciless drills—it had all been leading somewhere, and the somewhere was *this*: a trap sprung true, a threat neutralized, craft vindicated. I had spent my whole life being small and invisible and unremarkable, and now, for the first time, all that hidden knowledge had *bitten*, and the bite felt magnificent.

Below me, muffled by stone, the trapped man started swearing. Then, oddly, he started laughing too—a dry, rueful bark echoing up the shaft. "Well played, lad! Well played! You're guild-trained, no mistake!"

"Stay there," I called down, and my voice cracked slightly on the last word, joy curdling into something less comfortable. "Someone will fetch you out. Eventually. Probably."

I turned around, wedges in hand, ready to explain, ready to justify—

And found Sera looking at me.

Not at the hole. At *me*. Her face had gone carefully blank, the way faces go when they're working hard not to show what's behind them, and her eyes moved over my hands, my knees, my mouth, cataloguing. I watched her decide something. I didn't know what.

"You *enjoyed* that," she said.

"They were going to kill us."

"You enjoyed it," she said again, slower, tasting it. "The trap. The timing. The—" she gestured vaguely at the settling dust, at the whole elegant violence of it. "You lit up like a lamp."

I looked down at the wedges in my hand. Smooth pine, worn concave by decades of other hands doing exactly this. "It's what I'm for," I said finally. "Nobody ever told me what the Wayfarers were actually *for*, but apparently it's this. Holes in mountains and men in holes. It's the first thing I've ever been good at that worked."

Something shifted in her expression. The blankness cracked, and underneath it wasn't disgust, which I'd braced for. It was recognition. The specific, unwilling recognition of one hunted animal seeing the hunting-craft of another.

"My tutor taught me court dances," she said quietly. "Eight years of them. Steps and counters and how to lead without appearing to. Everyone said I was gifted." Her jaw tightened. "I always wondered what all that training was *for*. Now I know it was for running. And I hate that I'm—" she stopped, restarted. "I'm *good* at running, Edmund. I'm faster and cleverer and braver on the road than I ever was in a ballroom, and some traitorous part of me is proud of that, and I despise that part."

We looked at each other across the dusty pocket, two people holding the same shameful secret in different wrappers.

"The man in the hole," she said. "What happens to him?"

"He climbs out or he doesn't. There's a rubble chute at the bottom that connects to the lower gully—it's slow, but it's a way. My master pulled that trap twice in his life, and both times the men crawled out furious and alive." I hesitated. "Guild insurance isn't murder. It's delay. Murder costs more."

"*Costs more,*" she repeated. But she said it like she was filing the phrase away, learning the grammar of my world the way I'd been stumbling through hers.

Above us, on the rim of the pocket, gravel whispered.

We both froze. Then, from the lip of the hollow, a rope flicked down and over the edge, anchored somewhere out of sight, and a voice drifted down—pleasant, unhurried, the same voice as the man in the hole.

"Clever lad. Truly. But you've dropped your drawbridge behind you and we hold the high ground now, so let's talk terms before anyone else goes down a hole." A pause. "Or, failing that—before we drop something down *yours*."

Sera's hand found my sleeve. Her grip was steady. Her whisper was not.

"Edmund. Tell me you have another trick."

I looked at the rope, at the wedges in my hand, at the slot below with its angry occupant, and I did the fastest inventory of my life.

"As it happens," I said, "there's a second exit. It's horrible. You're going to hate it."

"How horrible?"

"Remember the blindfold story?"

Her face, I am pleased to report, went beautifully pale.
