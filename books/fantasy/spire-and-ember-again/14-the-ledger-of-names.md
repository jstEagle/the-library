## 14. The Ledger of Names

**Edmund**

Sera had been sitting on her pack for the better part of an hour, which in my experience meant one of two things: she was sulking, or she was deciding whether to tell me something that would ruin my week.

We'd made camp in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite, out of the wind that came screaming down off the scree like it had a personal grudge against travelers. I'd built the fire small — smuggler's habit, a flame you could smother with one boot if you needed to — and I was pretending to re-tune the tension on my bootlaces for the fourth time when she finally spoke.

"You're going to want to sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"Then you're going to want to lie down."

"That bad?"

She didn't answer. Instead she pulled the pack into her lap and worked at a seam along its bottom with a thumbnail until the stitching gave and a second bag slid out — flat, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a cord that had been knotted in the Court's fashion. I recognized the knot because Corvin had taught it to me when I was nine and told me never to use it where anyone might see.

"Sera," I said carefully, "what is that?"

"Insurance." She unwound the cord. "I told you I ran from an arranged marriage. That part's true. What I didn't tell you is what I took with me when I went."

The oilcloth fell open, and underneath it was a book.

Not a grand book. No gilded spine, no clasped covers. Just a ledger, the kind any counting-house in the lowlands would use to track grain shipments, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling. But even from across the fire I could feel it — the same wrongness I'd felt from the courier's map, a warmth coming off the pages like a hearth seen through a wall.

"The ledger," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "You said you stole a ledger."

"I said a lot of things. Most of them were true. This one I saved for when I trusted you." She looked up, and there was no mockery in her face at all, which frightened me more than anything she'd ever said. "And I decided this morning, watching you take apart that enforcer's patrol route like you were reading your own handwriting, that I trust you. Don't make me regret it."

I crossed over and sat beside her. The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, loose stone ticked down the slope, patient as a clock.

She put the ledger in my hands.

* * *

**Sera**

He opened it the way people open doors they've been told are locked their whole lives — quickly, guiltily, glancing back first.

I watched him read. I'd watched a dozen faces do it before mine, in the years since I ran: the servants who helped me escape, the fence in Marrowgate who sold me the oilcloth, the old woman in the pass village who fed us and asked no questions. Every one of them had the same progression. First confusion. Then recognition, because everyone has lost someone they couldn't account for. Then the horror, arriving slow as floodwater, of understanding exactly how much had been taken and how long it had been going on.

Edmund got through all three stages in the space of four names.

"This is—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "These are guild names."

"I know."

"No, you don't understand. These aren't just guild names. This is Halloway Fen. He trained under Corvin's master. There's a plaque for him in the tollhouse at Greywater — died in an avalanche, winter of — " His finger stopped moving down the column. "Winter of the year I was born."

"Keep reading."

He kept reading. I almost wished he wouldn't. It's a cruelty, showing someone this, but it's also the only mercy left, because the alternative is letting them go on believing the world is merely sad instead of hungry.

"Aldous Merrin," he whispered. "Brannic Ovel. The twins, Cass and Ilse Varrow — they were a legend, Sera, they mapped the whole eastern face of Thornfell, every guidebook still uses their — " His voice did something strange, folded in half. "They're all here. They're all *here*. In the vaults."

"Not all of them are in the vaults anymore. Some entries have dates of release. Look at the margins."

He turned pages. I watched his eyes track the little notations in ember-ink — *paid in full*, *term expired*, *renewed*. Renewed was the commonest word in the book by far. A soul held for sixty years, renewed. A soul held for ninety, renewed. The Court was not in the business of ending debts. Ending a debt ends an income.

"They didn't die out," Edmund said. It wasn't a question. It was a man standing in the ruins of a sentence. "The guild. Everyone always said the guild died out. Thinned out, lost its roads, faded away. Master Corvin said — " He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "Corvin said the mountains ate them. Avalanches, fevers, the usual. He said we were the last two because we were too stubborn to die."

"And now?"

"Now I think the mountain didn't eat anyone." He set the ledger down on his knee very carefully, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "I think the guild was collected. One name at a time. Over a hundred years. And the ones who were left — " He looked at me. "Sera, why would the Court leave two? Why leave *me*? If they were harvesting the guild, why stop?"

That was the question I'd been carrying since Marrowgate, and I gave him the answer I hated giving, because it was the answer the ledger itself gave, written in the hand of whatever clerk kept it down there in the warm dark.

"Because a farm doesn't slaughter everything," I said. "You keep breeding stock."

* * *

**Edmund**

I have been afraid before. I was afraid the night the courier died in front of me with ash in his lungs. I was afraid on the Hollow Bridge, when the planks went soft under my feet and the river below sounded like it knew my name. Fear and I are old acquaintances; we share a bed some nights and split the bill in the morning.

This was not fear. This was the thing after fear, where the fear burns off and leaves the structure underneath, and you see the shape of your own life for the first time from outside.

A farm. I was breeding stock. Corvin was the farmer, or the farmhand, or — no. No, that wasn't right either, and I knew it wasn't, because Corvin had gone white as bone when he saw the map and ordered me to burn it, and men don't burn evidence for masters they love. He'd been afraid too. He'd been afraid my whole life, and he'd raised me inside that fear like raising a lamb in a pen, keeping me small and quiet and invisible, teaching me every smuggler's path and none of the guild's history.

Keeping me ignorant. Keeping me *alive*.

"How many?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone older. "How many names are in there?"

"Four hundred and six," Sera said. "As of the last entry. Plus renewals — some souls have been collected twice, sold back and taken again. The Court calls those *second harvests*." Her jaw tightened. "There's a man in there who's been harvested three times. He keeps trying to buy his own soul back and the Court keeps lending him the coin to do it. At interest."

"That's — " I searched for a word and found only the small ones. "That's evil. Actual evil. Not the storybook kind."

"The Court wouldn't call it evil. They'd call it *stewardship*." She pulled the ledger back toward herself and turned to a page near the middle, marked with a ribbon. "There's something else. Something I should have shown you first, maybe. Or maybe there's no kind way to show anyone anything in this book."

"What?"

She hesitated. And Sera Amadine does not hesitate — she decides, she commits, she walks out of ballrooms and marriages and courts without looking back. Watching her hesitate was like watching a mountain consider moving.

"When I stole this," she said, "I stole it for one reason. One name. My brother Tomas went missing eight years ago. The family said drowned, crossing to the coast. There was no body. There was never a body. And then I married into a house that served the Court, and I learned what the Court collects, and I started asking questions with my ears instead of my mouth." She touched the ribbon. "His name is in here. Page two hundred and eleven. Collected the year he 'drowned.' Term: indefinite."

"Sera — "

"So understand that when I tell you the next thing, I'm not doing it to hurt you. I'm doing it because you deserve the truth and because I need you thinking clearly, and I can't have you finding this page yourself later, alone, with no one to catch you when your knees go."

"Show me," I said. And then, because some part of me already knew, already felt it coming up through the floor of the world like heat through stone: "Show me now."

She turned to the last section of the ledger. The recent entries. The ink there was fresher, the ember-glow brighter, and the handwriting had changed — a different clerk, or the same clerk grown careless, or the same clerk grown confident, because who was going to audit the Ember Court?

One entry. Near the end. The name was struck through with a single line, the way the chapel wall had struck through a name, and beside it, in the margin, a date.

My birthday.

Above the date, a notation in the Court's clipped shorthand, which Sera had taught me to read on the road without telling me why: *collateral. final debt of the Wayfarers' Guild. term: the child's majority.*

Below the name, a single word I will carry until I die:

*Maren.*

* * *

**Sera**

He didn't cry. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because it matters. Edmund Villalobos sat with his mother's soul-ledger open in his lap, on a frozen mountainside, with the fire dying between us, and he did not cry, and he did not rage, and he did not throw the book into the coals, which I confess I expected and would have forgiven.

What he did was worse to watch. He went *quiet*. All the wryness drained out of him, all the deflection, the little jokes he uses like shutters against a storm, and what was left underneath was a young man seeing the exact dimensions of the cage he'd been born in.

"Maren," he said. "Maren Villalobos."

"You knew the name?"

"No." His voice was flat and careful, a man walking on ice. "I never knew any name. Corvin said my mother died in childbirth. He said it the same way every time — same words, same tone, like a lesson memorized. Died in childbirth, winter of the year you were born, and never ask me about it again, boy. I thought the never-ask-me-again was grief." He looked up at me, and his eyes were terrible and clear. "It wasn't grief. It was inventory. He was checking that the collateral stayed forgotten."

"Edmund — "

"*Collateral.*" He tapped the entry once, lightly, like touching a bruise to confirm it's real. "Final debt of the guild. Term: the child's majority. That's me. That's the debt coming due at solstice. It was never about the courier's map. It was never about the guild at all. It's been about me since the day I was born. My whole life has been the interest accruing on my mother's soul."

The fire popped. Above us the stars wheeled, indifferent, beautiful, useless.

"I used to dream about her," he said, quieter now. "All children dream things. I dreamed a woman singing in a kitchen, and flour on her hands, and I woke up happy and never told Corvin because he'd get that look, that closed look, and change the subject to rope knots." He closed the ledger gently. "She was real. She's *down there*. She's been down there my whole life, humming lullabies into glass, and the man who raised me signed the paper."

"You don't know that he signed it."

"He was guildmaster. Who else?"

"Edmund." I leaned forward and took the ledger from his hands, because he was starting to grip it hard enough to tear, and I know what tearing feels like from the inside. "Listen to me. I have spent eight years hating the Court, and I will tell you the most dangerous thing I know about them, and it isn't their enforcers or their magistrate or their vaults. It's this: they want you alone. A debt is easiest to collect from one person. One debtor, one memory, one soul, sealed in a room with the arithmetic. Everything they do — the erasures, the secrets, the silence Corvin kept — everything serves that. Isolation is their favorite tool. It's practically their religion."

"So what do I do instead?"

"You do what nobody in that ledger ever got to do." I put my hand flat on the cover, over four hundred and six names. "You refuse to be one. You find the others. The families, the survivors, the people walking around with holes in their lives they can't explain — like my mother, who sets a third plate at dinner sometimes and weeps without knowing why. You gather them. Because a memory held by many hands can't be stolen from any one of them, and a debt owed by a *guild* — " I met his eyes. "A debt owed by a guild is owed by everyone who ever carried its name. Which means it can be contested by everyone who ever carried its name. Together."

He was silent a long moment. When he spoke again, the wryness was creeping back, but changed — tempered, like steel that's been through the fire and come out thinner and harder.

"You know what the worst part is?" he said. "I spent my whole life resenting the guild for dying. Resenting Corvin for being the last of it, resenting the empty tollhouses, the routes no one walked, the silence. I thought I'd inherited a ruin." He shook his head slowly. "I inherited a crime scene. And I'm the only heir."

"Then you're the only one who can settle the estate."

Something moved in his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go once the bleeding stopped. He looked down at the ledger, at the struck-through name, at *Maren*, and I saw him do the thing I'd seen him do on the scree slope that morning with the enforcers: turn fear into craft, grief into plan.

"Solstice is nineteen days away," he said. "The debt comes due at the Court, which means the collection happens at the Court, which means my mother gets moved — transferred, renewed, or worse. We can't let that ledger stay accurate." He stood, and the firelight caught him full, and for the first time since I'd met him on the mountain he didn't look like a smuggler hiding in plain sight. He looked like what he apparently was: the last name in a book of the dead, still breathing, still walking, still owed.

"Get your pack," Edmund Villalobos said. "We're going down the unmapped road. Tonight."

Far below us, deep in the bones of Thornfell, something answered — a faint red glow seeping up through a crack in the granite, pulsing slow and steady, like a heartbeat that had been waiting a hundred years for someone to come looking for it.

Or like something down there, sensing the ledger open at last, beginning to wake.
