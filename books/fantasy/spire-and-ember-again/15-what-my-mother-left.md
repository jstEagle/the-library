## 15. What My Mother Left

There is a particular kind of silence a man learns to keep around the subject of his own mother.

I don't recommend it. It's like carrying a stone in your boot for thirty years—you stop noticing it's there until someone asks you to take off your shoes, and then you can't remember how you ever walked at all.

Sera asked me that night in the smuggler's cathedral, sitting cross-legged on a slab of stone some dead road-thief had once used as an altar, her stolen ledger open across her knees. The candlelight did something kind to her face that I suspect she would have resented.

"You never said," she said. "About your family."

"There's nothing to say."

"Everyone says that. It's never true."

"It's true for me." I was re-wrapping my boots' laces for no reason whatsoever, because a man who is busy with his laces cannot be expected to make eye contact. "Corvin raised me. He didn't talk about before. There wasn't a before, as far as he was concerned. There was the guild, and there was me, and there were the roads."

"But your mother—"

"I don't know her name."

The words came out flatter than I intended, and once they were out I couldn't call them back, so I just kept talking, which is what I do when I'm frightened. Ask anyone. Ask Sera, specifically, since she'd had three weeks of it.

"She died when I was born. That's all Corvin ever said. Died when you were born, boy, and don't ask me more, because grief doesn't keep well in this house. He had a way of closing doors with his voice. You'd reach for a question and find the handle gone."

Sera didn't say anything. She had learned, somewhere in those three weeks, when to let me fill a silence and when to hold it open like a trap. This time she held it open. I hated her a little for it. I have never been more grateful to anyone.

"He wouldn't even tell me where she was buried," I said finally. "I asked him once—I must have been twelve. I'd found a grave up past the high switchback with fresh flowers on it, and I thought, maybe. Maybe that's her. Someone remembers her, anyway. And Corvin looked at me like I'd asked him to hand over his own heart on a plate, and he said, 'Don't go looking for graves, Edmund. The mountain has enough of them.' And that was that. Door closed. Handle gone."

"And you stopped asking."

"I stopped asking." I tied off a lace that didn't need tying. "You grow up in a house with one locked room, eventually you stop walking past it. Or you tell yourself you have. The walking-past part never really stops. You just get better at not looking like you're looking."

Sera closed the ledger slowly, as if she were afraid of waking it. "My mother used to say that families are just stories people agree to stop asking about."

"Your mother sounds wiser than mine is knowable."

"That's the most depressing thing you've ever said, and last week you told me the mountain probably ate a tax collector alive and deserved thanks for it."

"He was a tax collector, Sera. The mountain performed a public service."

She laughed—the short, surprised laugh she still seemed annoyed at herself for producing—and then the laughter went out of her face the way it always did when she remembered what she was carrying. Her brother's name, written in a ledger of souls. A gilded cage she'd climbed out of and couldn't stop looking back at.

I knew the feeling. I just hadn't known yet that I knew it.

* * *

I couldn't sleep after that. Nothing new; I hadn't slept properly since the courier died on the switchback with my refusal still warm in his ears. But that night the sleeplessness had a direction to it, and direction is dangerous in a man like me, because I am at my worst when I am curious and alone.

The cathedral cavern stretched away from our little fire into dark carved by generations of hands. Smugglers, road-wardens, guild men and women whose names the guild had supposedly forgotten—except they hadn't been forgotten, had they? They'd been collected. Sera's ledger sat by her sleeping roll like a black cat that had decided to adopt us, full of names of people the guild mourned in official records and sold beneath the mountain in unofficial ones.

And among the prayers cut into the walls—hundreds of them, thousands, petitions and curses and thank-yous scratched into stone by lamplight over two centuries—there had been a name struck through.

I'd noticed it days ago, when we first sheltered here. I'd noticed it and done what I always did with locked rooms: walked past without looking like I was looking.

Now I took the candle and went looking.

The wall near the old altar was dense with carvings, overlapping generations of handwriting, some careful, some frantic, some cut by someone who clearly had more fear than skill. Dates beside names, mostly. The smugglers' custom, Sera had guessed—leave your name and the date you passed through, so the road knows you, so the road owes you nothing. A superstition. A bookkeeping of the soul, scrawled in stone because paper burns and stone only weathers.

I found the struck-through name where I remembered it being, low on the wall, at the height of someone kneeling.

And I made myself read the date beside it.

I had known, of course. Some part of me had known since the first night, the way you know a storm is coming from the ache in an old break. But knowing a thing in the marrow and reading it in cold candlelight are different animals entirely.

The date was the third of Deepmonth.

The day I was born.

I stood there for a long time. Long enough for the candle to gutter and recover twice. Long enough for the cold of the stone floor to climb up through my boots and settle in my knees like it intended to stay.

The name above the date was scratched deep, deeper than any other carving nearby, as if whoever cut it had needed the stone to hold the letters whether the stone agreed or not. And across it, later—much later, by the weathering—a single line, drawn hard enough to chip the rock.

Struck through. Erased. The guild's oldest trick, applied to a chapel wall by somebody who knew exactly what erasure cost, because they'd done it professionally.

I didn't read the name. I want that understood. I stood in front of it with the candle shaking in my fist and I did not read the name, because some animal part of me understood that the moment I read it, the locked room would open, and I had spent thirty years learning how to walk past it, and I wasn't ready. I told myself I'd wait for morning. I told myself I'd wake Sera. I told myself a great many things, standing in that cold cathedral, and every one of them was a lie with good posture.

What I actually did was press my thumb against the strike-through line, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it still hurts.

It hurt.

* * *

Sera found me there at dawn. I heard her come down the passage—she moved quietly for a noblewoman, another thing she'd have been annoyed to be praised for—and she stopped when she saw me, and she didn't ask what I was doing, because the answer was written in my posture the way names were written in the wall.

"The date," she said.

"The date," I agreed.

"Edmund." She crouched down next to me, and her voice went gentle in a way that was worse than shouting. "Whose name is it?"

"I don't know." My voice came out sanded flat. "That's rather the point, isn't it? I don't know my mother's name. Corvin never said it. Not once, in thirty years. And now there's a name on this wall, struck through, dated the day I was born, and I'm standing here like a fool trying not to look at it, because—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Because if it's her, then Corvin didn't just refuse to tell me. He erased her. From the records, from the roads, from the one place in these mountains where people wrote things down so they'd be remembered. He struck her out of the smugglers' own cathedral. Who does that? What kind of man—"

"A frightened one," Sera said quietly. "Or a guilty one. They carve the same lines, sometimes."

I wanted to argue. I had thirty years of practice defending Corvin—he was hard, yes, but grief does that; he was secretive, yes, but the guild demanded it; he was distant, yes, but he fed me and taught me and never once raised his hand. I had built him a whole architecture of excuses over the decades, load-bearing and well-maintained, and Sera stood there with her ledger and her brother's stolen name and dismantled it with six words.

A guilty one. They carve the same lines.

"Read it to me," I said.

"Are you sure?"

"No. Absolutely not. Read it anyway."

She leaned close to the wall, tilting the candle, and I watched her lips move over the letters, and I watched her face change—not dramatically, nothing so merciful as drama, just a small settling, like snow sliding off a branch.

Then she straightened up and looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her hesitate.

"Sera."

"It's a woman's name," she said. "Maren."

The word went through me like a splinter finding its depth. Maren. I turned it over. I tried it against the empty space where a mother should have been, and it fit—not like recognition, I had no recognition to offer, but like a key fitting a lock that had rusted shut from disuse. Maren Villalobos. Maren. Had Corvin ever said it? In some half-dreamed hour, some fever, some drink? Had it been waiting in me all along the way the lullaby I sometimes hummed without noticing had been waiting, sourceless, orphaned?

"Maren," I said aloud, and the cathedral took the word and gave back nothing, and somehow that was worse than an echo.

"The strike-through," Sera said. Her hand hovered near my shoulder without landing. She'd gotten better at that, too—knowing when I needed contact and when contact would shatter me. "Look at the edges. It was cut years after the name. Decades, maybe. Someone came back."

"Someone came back," I repeated. "To unwrite her. Why? Why bother? If she was dead, if she was paid out, if she was—" I couldn't finish it. Sold. If she was sold, the way the ledger said the others were sold, the way the guild had been doing business beneath our feet for two hundred years while I catalogued empty roads and called myself the last apprentice of a proud tradition. "Why erase her from a wall nobody visits?"

"Because walls get visited," Sera said. "By people like us. Because someone was afraid that someday a boy would come looking for his mother's grave and find something else instead."

The candle flame leaned in a draft from the deep passages, and the shadows on the carved wall swung, and for one vertiginous moment the whole cathedral of names seemed to lean with them, hundreds of dead smugglers shifting in their stony sleep, all of them watching me arrive thirty years late to my own beginning.

"Corvin knew," I said. It wasn't a question anymore. It hadn't been a question since the tollhouse, since he'd gone white as tallow at the sight of the ember-map and ordered me to burn it. "He's known since the day I was born. He didn't raise me, Sera. He guarded me. Like a debt. Like collateral he was keeping safe for someone."

"Or keeping safe from someone."

"Is there a difference?" I got to my feet. My knees had gone stiff on the cold stone, and I welcomed the pain of it, because pain is honest in a way that hope isn't. "He told me she died in childbirth. Died, Sera. Simple, clean, the kind of death you put on a marker and water once a year. And instead—" I gestured at the wall, at the struck line, at the ledger lying open by the ashes of our fire with its terrible tidy columns of collected souls. "Instead there's a date and a scratch and a vault under a mountain, and a Magistrate who trades in memories, and a map drawn in burning ink that chose me, of all people, out of everyone on these roads. Chose me. I thought it was chance. I thought it was the courier's desperation, any hand would do. But maps don't choose hands, do they? Blood chooses blood."

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful steadiness of someone walking a ledge. "If your mother's soul is in that vault—"

"Then it's in the ledger." I crossed to it, knelt, and began turning pages with fingers that refused to be steady. "Every soul they hold, she said. Every name. If Maren Villalobos is down there, she's written here, and if she's written here, then she's real, and if she's real, then Corvin lied to me every day of my life, and I will drag the truth out of that dying old man if I have to carry him up the mountain on my back to do it."

"Edmund—"

"Here." My finger stopped on a line. The page swam. I blinked, and it didn't help, because the swimming was inside me, not on the paper. "Here. Maren Villalobos. Year of the Long Frost. Collected against the founding covenant, final payment outstanding." Final payment. Outstanding. As though she were a barrel of wine. As though she were still owed, still accruing interest, still sitting on a shelf of glass in the dark beneath Thornfell while her son grew up learning to walk past locked rooms.

The dread I'd been carrying since the switchback—that shapeless, patient dread—finally crystallized, and to my surprise it didn't crush me. It sharpened. All that fog condensed into one clean, cold point of purpose, the way breath freezes on a high pass.

"They didn't collect her because she owed," I heard myself say. "They collected her because the guild owed. The founding pact. Corvin's precious founding pact, the one he wouldn't speak of, the one the whole guild was built on. Safe passage through Thornfell, bought and paid for—with souls. And the last payment, the final one, the one that's still outstanding..." I looked up at Sera, and I felt my face do something I didn't recognize, something between a smile and a wound. "It was my mother. The guild spent my mother buying itself two hundred years of roads. And then it handed the bill's remainder to her infant son and called it raising him."

Sera put her hand on my shoulder then. I let it stay.

"What do you want to do?" she asked.

It was, I reflected later, the right question. Not what do you feel, not are you all right, not the thousand soft useless questions I'd have fumbled through for anyone else. What do you want to do. She was offering me my dread back as a weapon, hilt-first.

"I want to go down," I said. "Through the under-road, with your ledger and my map and whatever pieces of Corvin's confession we can pry loose. I want to stand in front of that vault. And I want to ask my mother's soul—whoever's listening in that court of ash and cooled blood—one question."

"Which is?"

"Whether she knew." My voice cracked on it, and I let it crack, because I was done with doors and handles and walking past. "Whether she knew what she was traded for. Whether she knew about me. Whether somewhere in that glass, all these years, she's been humming a lullaby to a son she was never allowed to meet."

Sera didn't answer. She didn't need to. Outside the cathedral's mouth, far above, the wind changed, and with it came a smell I knew too well from the switchback and the scree and every bad night since—woodsmoke and cold iron and something older underneath, something like a forge left banked but never out.

Ash. On the wind. Moving toward us.

Sera was on her feet before I finished breathing it in. "They've found the cathedral."

"No," I said, folding the map into my coat, tucking the ledger inside my shirt against my heartbeat, where it could feel what it was costing me. "They've found me. There's a difference, and I'm finally starting to understand what it is."

Above us, faint through miles of stone, something began to sing—an enforcer's wordless half-memory of a song, drifting down the deep passages like smoke.

And God help me, I knew the tune.
