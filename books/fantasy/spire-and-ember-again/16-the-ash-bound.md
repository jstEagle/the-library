## 16. The Ash-Bound

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives inside a person who has been emptied out, and I know it intimately, because it is the only home I have.

I do not remember my name. That is not quite true—I remember that there was a name, the way you remember there was once a window in a wall that has since been bricked over. You can see the outline of it. You can press your palm against the plaster and feel where the light used to come through. But the window itself is gone, and what remains is only the shape of its absence, which is not the same thing at all.

The Court calls me Veyra. They gave me the name when they gave me the ash, or perhaps the ash came with the name already in it, ground fine as pepper and just as flavorless. It is a serviceable name. It means nothing to me, which is, I suspect, the point. A person with no name cannot be summoned by love. A person with no past cannot be called home by anything.

I walk when they tell me to walk. I burn when they tell me to burn. In between, I stand very still in the high passes of Thornfell and watch the snow come down like ash coming down, and I think—*think* is perhaps too generous a word for what I do—I *drift*, the way smoke drifts along a ceiling looking for a draft.

The others are the same. There are eleven of us on this hunt, and we move through the mountains without leaving footprints, because ash does not press down on snow so much as settle on it. We do not speak unless spoken to. We do not hunger, exactly, though there is a hollow place behind my sternum that never fills, and I have learned not to look at it directly, the way you learn not to touch a bruise.

We are hunting a map. Or rather, we are hunting the boy who carries the map, though none of us were told why, and none of us asked. Asking questions is like scratching at the bricked-over window. It gets you nothing but dust under your fingernails and a strange, ringing ache that the Magistrate's stewards call *dissonance* and treat with more ash.

I have taken the treatment four times now. I know because there are four small scars on the inside of my left wrist, pale against skin that is otherwise the color of a hearth swept clean. Four treatments. Which means four times, something in me rose up and knocked against the inside of my skull hard enough that the stewards noticed. Whatever it was, they put it down each time. And each time, I woke with a little less of whatever the thing had been.

Here is what I know about myself, in total:

I am tall. My hands are a laborer's hands, or maybe a musician's—the calluses sit wrong for hauling, right for strings, but there are no strings anywhere in my life anymore, so the question sits unanswered like a coin on a dead man's eye. I flinch at the smell of woodsmoke, which is an inconvenient trait in an enforcer made of the stuff. And once, standing watch above the tollhouse road while my fellows interrogated an innkeeper who had done nothing worse than pour ale for the wrong traveler, I heard a child laughing somewhere down in the village, and something in my chest turned over so violently that I had to grip the fence post until the wood creaked.

I did not know why. I still do not know why. But I have begun to collect these moments the way a beggar collects buttons—individually worthless, but proof, somehow, that a coat once existed.

* * *

The Magistrate's stewards keep us in the under-galleries between assignments, in long dormitories carved from cooled lava, where the walls give off a warmth that never quite becomes comfort. We do not sleep, precisely. We enter something adjacent to sleep, a gray shallows where the emptied ones drift together like silt in still water.

It was there, in the shallows, that I first heard the song.

Not with my ears. The song came from underneath hearing, from the same bricked-over place as the window and the name. It was a lullaby—or it had the shape of one, slow and rocking, built for a voice pitched low over a cradle or a cookfire. I could not have told you a single word of it. I could not even have told you the tune, not honestly, not if you'd held a blade to my throat and demanded I hum it. What I had was the *feeling* of the tune, the way you can feel rain coming in an old scar.

I woke—came up out of the shallows, rather—and found myself humming.

Three notes. Only three notes, rising and falling, rising and falling, and my own voice sounded foreign to me, warm where everything else in me was cold. Around me the other enforcers lay in their rows, gray and still, and my humming moved among them like a spark moving across a dry floor.

One of them stirred. The one the stewards call Bren, broad-shouldered, patient, emptier than most. His eyes opened—not all the way, just a slit, the way a man opens his eyes when he hears music through a wall and wants to place it before he decides whether to get up.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out rusted, "did you get that?"

"I don't know."

"It's—" He stopped. His brow folded, and I watched him reach for something the way a man reaches for a rung that isn't there. "It's familiar."

"Yes."

Neither of us said anything else. There was nothing else to say, and also there was everything else to say, and neither of us had the words for any of it. He closed his eyes again. I stopped humming. But the three notes stayed in me after that, lodged like a splinter, and I found that when the dissonance came—and it came more often now—I could press my tongue against them the way you press your tongue against a sore tooth, and the ache would sharpen into something almost like *sense*.

This is what the stewards do not understand, I think. Or what they understand perfectly and do not care about, which amounts to the same cruelty. They believe that memory is a possession, that it can be removed cleanly, like a tooth, leaving the rest of the mouth intact. But a person is not a purse you can empty and refill. A person is a song, and if you take away the verses, the chorus keeps humming itself in the body whether you will it or not. They took my verses. All of them. Every year, every face, every grief and every ordinary Tuesday. And what is left of me walks their mountain and burns their enemies and hums three notes in the dark, because the chorus does not answer to the Magistrate.

The chorus answers to whoever taught it to me first.

And I would give—here is the terrible arithmetic of the emptied heart—I would give what little remains of me to know who that was.

* * *

We caught the old man on the fourth day of the hunt.

He came down the smuggler's switchbacks alone, moving badly, favoring his left side, and when our circle closed around him he did not run. He looked at us—at me, longest at me—with an expression I had no shelf to put it on. Grief, I think. Or guilt. Some weight carried so long it has worn grooves into the face that holds it.

"You're them," he said. "You're what's left of the debtors." And then, softer, as if the words cost him: "Gods. I knew some of you. Before."

Bren shifted beside me, ash stirring off his shoulders. The old man's eyes tracked the movement and something in his face collapsed inward, quietly, like a roof giving way over an empty room.

"Veyra," said our captain—a tall one, hollow-voiced, the stewards' favorite. "Take his satchel. Bind his hands. If he runs, burn the path behind him."

I stepped forward to obey. That is what I do; that is what I am *for*. I reached for the satchel strap, and the old man did not pull away. Instead he looked straight into my face, into whatever the ash leaves visible of what I was, and he said:

"Do you know the song? The one the guild mothers sang at the winter crossings? *Low the lamp and long the road—*"

The world cracked.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just a single hairline fracture running through the middle of me, and through the fracture came a smell—woodsmoke and bread—and a warmth on my cheek that might have been a hand, and three notes rising, falling, rising, and a voice, a woman's voice, saying words I could not hear but whose *shape* I knew the way I knew my own hands—

Then the dissonance hit, white and screaming, and I was on my knees in the snow without knowing how I'd gotten there, and the captain's hand was on the back of my neck, and the treatment was coming, I could feel it gathering in his palm like heat off a forge.

"*Dissonance,*" the captain said, bored as a clerk. "Steady her."

Through the roaring I heard the old man shouting—not fear, fury—"*She knew it! She knew the song, I saw it—you monsters, she KNEW it—*" and then something struck him and he went quiet, and I was dragged up out of the snow with the fracture still singing in me, three notes, rising and falling, and I understood, in the wordless way of the emptied, one thing absolutely:

They had lied to us. Not about the work, not about the debt. About the *cost*. They had told us we would feel nothing, that emptiness was peace, and it was not peace, it was a room with the furniture gone, and every day some part of me walked the walls of it touching the outlines of things that used to be there.

That night, bound to a tree at the edge of camp while the others stood their watch, I worked at the fracture instead of resting. I laid the three notes end to end and pressed. And pressing, I found a fourth note hiding behind them, and then a phrase, half a phrase, *low the lamp and*—and nothing, a cliff-edge where the rest should be.

But half a phrase is more than I had yesterday.

I began, very quietly, so quietly the ash itself barely stirred, to sing.

* * *

On the sixth day, the trail led us to a scree slope high on Thornfell's shoulder, and the hunt turned to blood. The boy was there—the map-bearer, young, quick, with a smuggler's easy cruelty in the way he moved stone—and the noblewoman, the runaway, sharp-eyed and furious. There was fighting. There was always fighting. I burned a path across the slope and drove them toward the kill-ground, and I did it competently, obediently, and all the while the half-song turned in me like a key turning in a lock that someone, somewhere, was slowly learning to pick.

And here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I will carry out of this chapter of my emptiness if I carry nothing else:

When the boy fell sliding past me on the scree, close enough that I could have ended it, close enough that I saw his eyes—wide, brown, afraid—he shouted something at me. Not a plea. A name. He shouted a name I did not know, at me, as if it were mine, as if it had always been mine, and the syllables went into me like water into a crack in stone, and somewhere far below the ash, something *moved*.

I let him go. I told the captain the footing failed me. The captain looked at me a moment too long, and I felt the treatment gathering again in the air between us like weather.

Let it come. Let them scrape me down to the last smooth nothing. Because I understand now what the Magistrate's whole grey kingdom is built on, what the debts and the ledgers and the polite masked nobles trading in other people's yesterdays are all built on: the belief that what is forgotten is gone.

But I am the proof otherwise. I am walking evidence, humming half a lullaby on a frozen mountainside, that what they take does not die—it waits. It waits in calluses and flinches and three stubborn notes, and it waits for a voice, any voice, to call it home.

Tonight the camp sleeps its gray shallows-sleep, and I lie awake in the snow with my scarred wrist pressed to my lips, and I sing the half-song over and over, adding nothing, losing nothing, keeping it lit the way you keep a lamp lit in a window for someone whose face you've lost but whose road you remember.

*Low the lamp and long the road.*

Whoever taught me that—whoever I was before I was Veyra—somewhere beneath this mountain or beyond it, there is a version of me that knows the rest.

And tomorrow, the trail turns downward, toward the deep roads, toward the Court itself.

Toward whatever is left of me.
