## 17. Corvin Returns

I want to say I saw him coming. That some smuggler's instinct prickled at the back of my neck, that I smelled woodsmoke on the wind and knew before my eyes confirmed it. That would be tidy. That would be the kind of story you tell afterward by a fire, when everyone's alive and the telling costs nothing.

The truth is I was asleep.

We'd made camp in a fold of the mountain where two rock shelves leaned together like conspirators, close enough to hold a small fire without advertising it to the sky. Sera had taken first watch, which she insisted on every night, and I had stopped arguing about it around the third night, mostly because losing arguments to her had become one of the few reliable pleasures left in my life.

So it was her boot in my ribs that woke me, and her hand clamped over my mouth before I could make the sound I was already making.

"Someone's coming up the trail," she whispered. "One man. He's limping."

"Could be anyone."

"It's nearly midnight, Edmund. Nobody honest climbs Thornfell at midnight."

She had me there. In eleven years of tending the tollhouse I could count on one hand the travelers who'd come through after dark, and every one of them had been either lost, desperate, or lying about being lost. Usually all three.

I rolled out of my bedroll and put out the fire with a handful of dirt, working blind until the last orange glow died. The cold rushed in immediately, the way it does up there, as if the mountain had only been pretending to tolerate our warmth and now wanted it back with interest.

The footsteps came slow. Dragging, actually — the left foot landing a half-beat late, the way feet do when they've been walking on something they shouldn't have been. I pressed myself against the rock shelf and watched the switchback below us, that pale ribbon of scree the moon had kindly illuminated for whoever was climbing it.

And then he came around the bend, and my whole body forgot what it was supposed to be doing.

He was thinner than I remembered. Grayer. His coat — the old guildmaster's coat, blue wool gone silver at the elbows, the one he'd worn every day of my childhood like armor against questions — hung off him now, torn along one sleeve and dark with something that might have been blood or might have been ash. He leaned on a broken branch for a staff. Every few steps he stopped and coughed into his fist, and the coughs sounded like they were coming up from somewhere below his lungs.

Master Corvin. Guildmaster of a guild of one, walking toward me out of the dark like an answer to a question I'd been too much of a coward to ask.

"He can't see us," Sera breathed. "Edmund. He cannot see this camp."

"I know."

"Do you? Because your face is doing something very stupid right now."

My face probably was. I stood up anyway.

* * *

"Edmund." My name came out of him like something he'd been carrying a long way and could finally set down. "Oh, thank every road. Thank every *road*."

He tried to hurry and his leg folded under him, and I caught him before the rocks did. He weighed almost nothing. When had that happened? He'd carried me on those shoulders once, over a flooded ford below the tollhouse, laughing the whole way across. Now I held him up the way you hold a bundle of kindling, and the smell of him was smoke and sickness and the particular sourness of someone who hasn't eaten properly in days.

"You're supposed to be dead," I said. It came out harsher than I meant it, which is to say it came out exactly as harsh as I meant it. "They took the tollhouse, Corvin. They burned it. I watched from the ridge. There was nothing left standing and no one left standing in it."

"They didn't burn it while I was in it." He laughed, and the laugh turned into another of those terrible coughs. "Ash-bound don't waste fire on a man who'll come quietly. They asked their questions. I answered wrong on purpose, slowly, and eventually they got bored enough to lock me in the root cellar instead of the ground. Old habit of theirs, locking things up. You'd know that if—" He stopped himself. His eyes went past my shoulder to Sera, who had not moved from the shadows and whose hand, I noticed, had not moved far from her knife. "Who's your friend?"

"Sera Amadine." She stepped forward just enough to be seen, which for Sera constituted a significant act of trust. "You're Guildmaster Corvin. Edmund talks about you when he thinks I'm asleep."

"I talk about you too," I said, because apparently I'd decided to be petty in front of company.

"Yes," she said. "You do."

Corvin looked between us, and something moved across his face — relief, maybe, or its cousin, the fear of having run out of time. "Then you know what I am," he said to her. "Good. Saves the speech. Edmund, boy, I need to tell you—"

"You need to sit down," I said. "You need to eat. And then you need to explain how a man walks out of an ash-bound prison cell, because I've met them, Master. I've felt what they do to a person. Nobody strolls away from that."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Guilt has a look, did you know that? It's very quick. Most people miss it. I'd had eleven years of practice reading this man's silences, so I caught it, filed it away, and hated myself for filing it.

"A lucky moment," he said. "A guard turned his back. Veyra — the tall one, the woman — she looked at me like she was trying to remember why she was holding the key. And then she opened the door and walked away. I don't pretend to understand it. Maybe the Court is cracking. Maybe the mountain itself is tired of them." He let me lower him onto the flat stone by the dead fire. "Does it matter? I'm here. I'm here, and I came to help."

Sera made a sound. It was very small. It was also, unmistakably, a laugh.

* * *

We fed him salt pork and the heel of the bread we'd bought in Hollowmere, and he ate like a man who'd forgotten food was a thing bodies required, mechanically, without pleasure. Sera sat across the fire from him with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, watching him the way she watched everything — as if it were a ledger and she was looking for the line that didn't add up.

"The tollhouse," I said. "How bad?"

"Gone. The ledgers are gone. Everything I spent thirty years pretending mattered." He wiped his mouth. "Good riddance, if I'm honest. Paper roads. Ink routes. A guild that was three men and a building, and then two men and a memory, and then—" He gestured at himself, at the ruined coat, at all of it. "You were always the last real thing it had, Edmund. I hope you know that. I hope you know I never meant for you to be."

"Then you should have told me things."

"Yes." No flinch, no deflection. Just the word, laid down plain. "Yes, I should have. I told myself I was protecting you. That's what cowards call it. Protecting. I told myself the guild's debts died with the men who signed them, and that if I never spoke your mother's name aloud, the mountain would forget to collect it." He closed his eyes. "Mountains don't forget, boy. Mountains are where forgetting goes to be kept."

My chest did something complicated. This was what I'd wanted — had wanted since I was seven years old and found the sealed drawer in his desk, since I was twelve and asked about my mother and watched him go white as the snowline, since I was twenty-five and realized I'd stopped asking because the silence had become the shape of our whole relationship. Here it was, finally: the opening of the drawer. And it tasted like ash, because everything did now, because he'd waited until the tollhouse was cinders and the ash-bound were hunting us across the scree to find his voice.

"You said her name," I said carefully. "Just now. You almost said it again."

"I know."

"Don't." I heard my own voice crack and despised it. "Don't ration it out to me like bread. Either tell me everything or tell me nothing, but stop deciding for me what I can carry. You've done that my whole life. I'm done being carried."

Sera shifted slightly. Out of the corner of my eye I saw her glance at me — quick, assessing, the look of someone recalibrating a map.

Corvin bowed his head. For a long moment the only sounds were the fire settling and the wind moving through the pines below us, that low ocean-sound the mountain makes when it wants you to think it's gentle.

"There's a road," he said at last. "Under the mapped ones. The ember-ink will show it to you — it showed it to the courier, that poor devil, and it'll show it to you because it chose you, though I pray nightly that I'm wrong about why. It runs down beneath the peak, into the deep galleries, all the way to the Ember Court's gate. It's the only way in that isn't the front door, and the front door is a coffin with hinges." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and steady at the same time, which is a trick I never learned. "I can take you to its mouth. I owe you that much. I owe you considerably more than that, and I know a debt acknowledged isn't a debt paid, but it's a start, and starts are all I've got left."

"And what do you want out of it?" Sera asked. Her voice was mild. Her eyes were not. "Forgiveness? Absolution? A place in whatever comes after?"

"Sera—"

"No. I want to hear him say it." She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she just aimed it. "Every person I've ever met who carries guilt like a lantern uses it to light the road they want you to walk. So. What do you want, Guildmaster?"

Corvin held her gaze for a long time. I watched him decide something — watched the decision move through his face like weather.

"To die on the right side of it," he said simply. "That's all. I sold a soul to keep a guild alive, and the guild died anyway, and the soul is still down there burning in a glass shelf, and I am going to walk back into that mountain and stand in front of it if it kills me. Which," he added, with a ghost of the dry humor I'd grown up on, "the odds currently favor."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a nightbird called twice and thought better of it.

And here is the thing I could not say in front of Sera, the thing I barely admitted to myself: some traitorous cellar of my heart had already forgiven him. Had forgiven him years ago, probably, the way you forgive weather. Because he was the only father-shaped thing my life had ever contained, and because wanting him to be good was easier than accepting the evidence. Sera looked at him and saw a liability with a limp. I looked at him and saw hands that had bandaged my knees, a voice that had taught me to read the mountain, a man who had chosen me — chosen me, a foundling nobody else wanted — when choosing cost him nothing and keeping me, later, cost him everything.

Longing is not judgment. I know that now. But sitting there in the firelight, watching the only family I'd ever had cough blood into his fist and offer to lead me home through the dark, I couldn't tell which one was driving.

"We leave at first light," I heard myself say. "You rest tonight. And Corvin—" I waited until he looked up. "If there's anything else you're not telling me, anything at all, you tell me before we reach that gate. Not after. Not when it's convenient. Before."

"Before," he agreed.

It sounded like a promise. Promises usually do.

* * *

Sera found me an hour later at the edge of the camp, where I'd taken the second watch and was pretending the wind was interesting.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You know what. Say it out loud so we both know you know."

I kept my eyes on the dark valley. "He's dying, Sera. Look at him. Whatever he did or didn't do down there, they worked him over, and he walked three days on a broken mountain to find us. That's not the posture of a man planning a betrayal."

"People have died for worse postures." She leaned against the rock beside me, arms crossed. "Veyra opened his cell, he says. Convenient. The one enforcer we've heard of showing a crack in the discipline, and she cracks for *him*."

"Or the Court really is fraying. You said yourself the ledger was hidden badly. Sloppy institutions rot."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. Then, in a different voice — lower, almost reluctant: "For what it's worth, I believe he means it. Most of it. That's what worries me. Men who mean most of it are the most dangerous kind, because they'll defend the lie with the same passion as the truth, and even they can't always tell which is which." She glanced at me. "And you believe all of it. Every word. Because you're starving and he's bread."

"That's unfair."

"It's completely fair, and it's also not an insult. Starving people eat fast. It's how we survive." She pushed off the rock. "Get some sleep. I'll wake you if our guest does anything more suspicious than breathe."

She was three steps away when she stopped. Turned back. Frowned at the bedroll where Corvin lay — already asleep, or performing it expertly — and then at the satchel slumped beside him. The courier's satchel. Mine, these weeks now, except I'd set it down by the fire when I'd gone to catch him falling, and I hadn't picked it up since.

"Edmund," she said slowly. "Where was the map when you went down to him?"

"In the satchel. By the fire. Why?"

She didn't answer. She crouched by the satchel, drew out the map, and unrolled it across her knees — and even from ten feet away I could see the ember-ink moving, brighter than I'd ever seen it, crawling and swirling like coals stirred by breath. All the mapped roads had faded to gray ghosts.

Except one. One line burned fresh and hot, running down and down and down, toward the heart of the mountain.

Toward the Under-Road.

The one Corvin had offered to lead us to.

"How," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "How does it already know where we're going?"

Sera looked up at me across the glowing map, and the firelight made her expression very hard to read.

"Maybe it doesn't," she said. "Maybe it's just following the plan. Or maybe—" she nodded, very slightly, toward the sleeping form of my master, "—it's following *him*. And we should decide, right now, before sunrise, whether we're walking behind a guide or a lure."

Above us, the peak of Thornfell stood black against the stars, and somewhere inside it, faint as a heartbeat, the ember-ink pulsed.
