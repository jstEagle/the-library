## 18. The Under-Road

The map had been waiting for us.

I want to say that more plainly, because it took me three days and one very cold night to understand it: the map was not a thing we carried. It was a thing that carried us. Every road I'd ever charted in my years with the guild had been indifferent to me — stone doesn't care who walks it, that was practically the first lesson Corvin ever taught me, back when his voice still worked without flinching. *The mountain owes you nothing, boy. That's why you learn its moods.* But this map — this impossible scrap of courier's leather with ink that moved like coals breathing — cared. It cared about me specifically, which was worse, because in my experience nothing good has ever cared about me specifically.

We found the road's mouth on the fourth morning out of the scree, in a fold of the mountain where no fold should have been. I'd walked the Thornfell high paths since I was nine years old. I knew them the way you know the back of your own hand, including the scars. And there was no scar here. There was a cleft in the rock maybe eight feet wide, curtained by dead ivy and a fall of grey scree, and when I pulled the ivy aside the air that came out was warm.

Not summer-warm. Forge-warm. The kind of warmth that has weight to it, that presses against your face like a hand checking whether you're ripe.

"That's not right," I said, because I am a master of understatement when circumstances demand it.

Sera pushed past me before I could stop her — she'd developed a habit of doing that, as if my caution were a personal insult — and stood in the mouth of the cleft with her face tipped up like a woman smelling rain. "It smells like her house," she said quietly. "The Court. Cooled stone and old fire." She turned back to me, and in the flat grey morning light her noblewoman's composure had a crack down the middle of it. "This is real. This is actually the way down."

"Of course it's the way down. It's on the map."

"The map," Sera said, "that appeared out of a dead man's satchel, drawn in ink that moves, leading to a court that officially does not exist, down a road that appears on no chart made by living hands. Forgive me if I don't share your confidence in its cartographic integrity."

"You've been reading my vocabulary again."

"I've been trapped with you for two weeks. Something had to fill the silence." But she said it without teeth, and that was new too. Somewhere between the ambush on the scree and the night she'd finally told me about her brother — about the ledger, and the name written in it in a clerk's neat hand — the prickliness between us had worn down to something almost like trust. Almost. Trust with edges still on it. Which is, I suspect, the only kind worth having.

I unrolled the map across a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles, and looked at the under-road again. It ran from our position — a small ember-bright mark that I had not put there and that had definitely not been there last week — down and down in a long descending spiral, through strata marked in symbols I didn't recognize, to a gate drawn at the bottom like a closed eye. The ink around the gate pulsed slowly. Patiently. Like something sleeping that knew exactly how long it had to sleep.

"Once we go in," I said, "we're inside the mountain. No sky. No high paths. If the enforcers find the entrance—"

"They won't. You said yourself no chart admits this road exists."

"No chart admits it exists because everyone who might have charted it stopped coming back." I rolled the map up before my hands could shake visibly. "Corvin used to say the guild's oldest routes were paid for. I thought he meant bribes."

"And now?"

"Now I think he meant exactly what he said, and I wish to every open sky above us that he hadn't."

Sera didn't answer that. She just picked up her pack, settled the stolen ledger inside it with the care other people reserve for infants or knives, and walked into the warm dark. I stood there a moment longer, being the last person in the world still standing under an honest sky, and then I followed her, because the alternative was standing alone on a mountain waiting for ash to find me, and I have never once in my life chosen the braver-looking cowardice over the uglier courage.

* * *

The first mile was smuggler's work, and I'll admit I enjoyed it despite everything.

The passage began narrow, forcing us sideways through squeezes where the rock pressed close enough to taste, and then opened without warning into galleries tall enough to swallow a church. Someone had traveled this road once, and often. There were iron rings bolted into the walls for ropes, their rust ground smooth in places by decades of hauling hands. There were niches cut at intervals, each holding the stub of a lamp bracket. And along the floor, worn into the stone itself, ran twin ruts — cart tracks, deep as finger-joints, polished to a dull shine.

"Carts," Sera said, running her thumb along one rut. "Heavy ones. Regularly."

"Tribute," I said. "Going down." I didn't add: *and souls coming up*, because neither of us needed the sentence finished. We'd both read the ledger. We knew what the Court collected and how it liked it packed.

The warmth deepened as we descended. Not dangerously — not yet — but it got into everything. My pack straps went soft and supple. The wax in my little store of candles slumped in their paper sleeves. Sweat stood on Sera's upper lip within the hour, and she stripped off her outer coat and tied it around her waist with the brisk irritation of someone whose wardrobe had not planned for an infernal commute.

"How far does the map say?" she asked.

"To the gate? Two days' walk, if the scale holds. Longer if the road winds the way mountain roads like to wind."

"And after the gate?"

"After the gate," I said, "the map stops being helpful and starts being decorative."

She snorted. It was, I noted, the first time I'd heard her do anything so undignified as snort, and I decided privately to treasure it, the way you treasure any evidence that another person is a person and not just a set of useful skills walking beside you in the dark.

We made camp that first night — if "night" means anything in a place where the only clock is your own exhaustion — in a gallery where the ceiling arched up into darkness and the walls gave back heat like bread ovens. I got a small fire going mostly out of habit; the tunnel air was warm enough that the flames seemed embarrassed to be there, flickering apologetically. Sera divided the food without being asked, giving me the larger share and pretending she hadn't, and I ate it and pretended I hadn't noticed, and between the two pretenses we built something that was almost companionship.

It was while I was re-rolling the map for the night that it happened.

My fingers were bare — I'd taken off one glove to feel the leather's grain, an old habit, checking for damp that would rot it — and when my skin touched the map directly, the ink moved.

Not the slow coal-breathing drift it always had. It *moved*. The roads rippled like water disturbed by a dropped stone, all of them shivering outward from the point where my fingertip rested, and the little bright mark that indicated our position flared white-hot, and for half a heartbeat I swear the whole map glowed from underneath, as though it weren't ink on leather at all but embers banked under a thin skin of skin.

I yelped. I will not pretend otherwise. I yelped and dropped the map and scooted backward on the stone, and Sera came up off her bedroll with a knife already in her hand, scanning the dark for whatever had attacked me.

"What?" she demanded. "What is it?"

"It touched me," I said, and immediately heard how that sounded. "The map. When I — my hand, without the glove, it—" I gestured helplessly at the thing lying on the stone between us, glowing gently now, its roads settling back into place like feathers smoothing after a ruffle.

Sera looked at the map. She looked at me. She put the knife away with the deliberate slowness of someone filing an incident report.

"Pick it up," she said.

"I'd rather not."

"I know you'd rather not. Pick it up anyway."

I picked it up. Because she asked, and because some traitorous part of me wanted to know, and because — this is the truest reason, and it took me until much later to admit it — because when my bare skin left the leather, the glow had dimmed, and dimming felt, absurdly, like watching a fire go out in a room where someone was cold.

The moment my palm lay flat against the map, the light rose again. Warmth spread up my wrist, not burning, not even uncomfortable — the warmth of a hand held in another hand. And the roads rearranged themselves. Subtly. A spur that had branched east now bent west. A symbol I hadn't been able to parse sharpened into legibility, and I read it, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

It wasn't a distance marker. It was a name.

*Villalobos.*

Written in the ember-ink, in a flowing antique hand, right there on the route my family's memory had been sold to buy. And beneath it, smaller, in the same hand, a line I had to hold the map close to the firelight to read:

*For the bearer, when he comes. It will know him.*

* * *

I didn't sleep after that. Sera didn't make me try to explain myself, which I appreciated, because I'm not sure I could have. Instead she sat across the dead fire from me, knees drawn up, and watched me watch the map, and eventually she said the thing I'd been circling all night without landing on.

"It chose you."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's a good thing. Things that choose people in stories are never the things you want choosing you. Cursed swords choose people. Doomed quests choose people. Nobody ever gets chosen by a comfortable chair and a plate of sausages."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Edmund. Think about it properly, instead of hiding behind jokes. Who drew this map?"

"A courier. A dying courier who bled on it and shoved it at me because I was the nearest pair of hands."

"Who sent the courier?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because I had asked myself that question on the first night, turning the courier's effects over by candlelight — the burned cloak, the seal-less satchel, the map itself — and I had arrived at an answer and then buried it under six layers of practicality, the way I bury most things.

"Someone who wanted it to reach a Wayfarer," I said finally. "Someone who knew the guild's paths well enough to send him down the switchback where I'd be. Someone who knew—" I swallowed. "Someone who knew there was exactly one Wayfarer left."

"And who would know that?"

"Corvin." The name came out flat and strange. "Or whoever Corvin answers to. Or—" I stopped, because the next name in that list was one I'd only learned existed a week ago, scratched on a chapel wall with a line struck through it. "Or someone who used to be one of us."

Sera nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd already worked out. Knowing her, I had. "And the map leads to the Court. To the vaults. To the debt. And it warms to your hand, and it carries your name, and it waited — Edmund, it *waited* — for the one person alive with blood-right to walk this road." She leaned forward, firelight catching the sharp planes of her face. "Your mother sold her soul to keep this guild alive. Maybe the map is the only inheritance anyone thought to leave you."

"That's a horrible inheritance."

"It's a *map*," she said. "In my family, inheritances come with obligations to people who hate you. At least yours comes with directions."

I laughed. It surprised us both — a short, cracked sound in the warm dark — and after a moment she laughed too, and for a little while the gallery didn't feel like the throat of a mountain at all. It felt like a room. A small, hot, borrowed room with two people in it who had each lost everything except each other's company, making jokes over a fire because the alternative was screaming.

Then the map flared in my hands — sudden, hard, white — and the laughter died in both our throats at once.

Down the tunnel, far below us, past the winding dark, something answered. A pulse of red light, deep in the mountain's guts, visible even around the bends of the road, like a heartbeat seen through a chest. Once. Twice. Then steady.

The gate at the bottom of the map had opened its eye.

"It knows we're coming," Sera whispered.

"No," I said, and my voice sounded calmer than I felt, which is the only magic I've ever reliably performed. "It knew we were coming before either of us was born. It's just being polite about it now."

We broke camp in silence and walked down into the red.

* * *

The second day underground changed the road's character entirely, the way a face changes when you learn what's behind it.

The cart ruts grew deeper. The iron rings gave way to chains, great rusted links thick as my arm, anchored into the rock and running down-slope in parallel lines like the veins of some enormous buried animal. And the walls began to carry marks. Not the smugglers' prayers of the cathedral cavern — those had been desperate and human, carved by hands that shook. These were official. Chiseled borders. Formal script in a language I didn't know but recognized anyway, because I'd seen its cousin on the Court's tribute-seals in Sera's ledger: the Ember Court's own hand, claiming the road, naming it.

Sera stopped at one of the inscriptions and traced the letters with a fingertip, her lips moving. "It says this is the Under-Road of the Founding Debt," she translated quietly. "'Let none descend but those who owe, and none ascend but those who are forgiven.'" She looked back at me. "Poetic, for a tax code."

"Everything about the Court is poetic. That's how they get away with it. Nobody audits a poem."

We walked on. The heat climbed past uncomfortable into punishing, and we rationed our water and spoke less, and the red glow below us grew from a rumor to a fact to a presence, staining the tunnel walls the color of a blacksmith's heart. Somewhere in the last hours I realized I'd stopped consulting the map for direction. It wasn't necessary. The road knew where it was going, and so — impossibly, horribly — did I. At every branching, my feet chose before my mind did, and every time, the map's bright mark confirmed the choice after the fact, like a tutor nodding approval.

"Sera," I said, at the seventh such branch. "Stop a moment."

She stopped. I could see her cataloguing my face, deciding how worried to be.

"When I touch the map, it answers. When I stand at a fork, I know the way. I've been telling myself it's smuggler's instinct, but it isn't. Instinct doesn't hum." I held up my free hand. It was trembling faintly, and not from fear. From resonance. From being, somehow, in tune. "This road was bought with my mother's soul. What if the payment didn't just purchase passage? What if it — keyed the lock? What if every Villalobos who ever walked this mountain left a thread, and the threads are all still here, and the map is just the spool?"

Sera was quiet for a long moment. The red light breathed against her face.

"Then the Court made a mistake," she said at last. "They took your mother's memory and her name and her song, and they filed them in a vault, and they thought that was the end of it. But you can't erase a person, Edmund. You can only move them somewhere else. And everything they moved—" she gestured at the humming dark around us, at the map glowing in my grip, at me, "—everything they moved is still pointing home."

I wanted to kiss her. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now. I wanted to kiss her and I didn't, partly because the timing was apocalyptic and partly because I'm a coward in precisely one direction and it's that one. Instead I said, "When this is over, remind me to argue with you about something trivial. I miss arguing about trivia."

"Noted," she said. "Now walk, Wayfarer. Your road's waiting."

* * *

We reached the gate at dusk on the second day, if dusk can be said to exist forty fathoms under a mountain. The tunnel simply ended — widened, actually, into a vaulted antechamber the size of a market square, its ceiling lost in red-lit haze — and there, filling the far wall, stood the gate from the map's final drawing.

It was a door built for giants and finished by jewellers. Black stone shot through with veins of cooling gold, thirty feet high, its surface carved with the same formal script as the road's inscriptions, spiraling inward toward a central seal shaped like a closed eye. The eye was open now. Through the seam around its lid seeped a light the color of the map's ink, and the heat rolling off it came in slow waves, like breath.

"Well," Sera said. "Any ideas?"

"One." I walked forward alone — she let me, which I think cost her something — and stood before the seal, and unrolled the map, and laid my bare hand flat upon it one last time.

The map blazed. Light poured up my arm and out through my fingertips, and the ember-ink lifted off the leather entirely — every road, every name, every patient pulsing mark rising like sparks off a bonfire — and streamed across the chamber and into the gate's open eye. The black stone drank it. The gold veins brightened. And the great seal shuddered, groaned, and began to turn, grinding through some mechanism older than any kingdom I could name, and the heat of it washed over me like a tide, and in the last moment before the doors parted I heard — faint, unmistakable, impossible —

a lullaby.

Four notes. Rising, falling, rising again. A tune with no words attached, hummed in a voice I did not know and knew completely, coming from somewhere beyond the gate, from the vaults, from the shelves of glass where the Court kept what it had collected.

My mother's voice. Singing to a child who couldn't possibly remember.

The gates swung wide onto a bridge of black glass spanning a lake of slow fire, and on the far shore, torchlit and vast, the Ember Court opened its arms.

And standing at the near end of the bridge, silhouetted against the blaze, leaning on a walking staff and looking older than the mountain itself—

was Corvin.

"Hello, boy," my master called across the fire, in a voice gone soft and strange. "You took the long way down."
