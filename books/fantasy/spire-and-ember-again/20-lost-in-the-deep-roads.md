## 20. Lost in the Deep Roads

I have been lost before. I want that on the record, because it matters to what happened next.

I was lost at nine, when Corvin sent me down the Weeping Stair with a lantern and a list of waymarks and no instructions beyond *don't stop walking*. I was lost at fourteen, in the whiteout above Kettle Pass, when I dug a snow cave and sang smuggler's songs to keep from sleeping. I was lost for most of my twenties in the subtler sense, the kind where you know exactly where your body is and haven't the faintest idea where you're going.

None of it prepared me for the under-road.

The tunnel we'd fallen into—or fled into, or been swallowed by; the distinction felt academic by the second hour—was warm. Not summer-warm. Forge-warm. The stone sweated. Somewhere below us, or ahead of us, or possibly all around us, something vast breathed heat through the rock like a banked fire deciding whether to wake up. The ember-ink map had gone quiet in my satchel, which I chose to interpret as *resting* rather than *abandoning us*, because I am an optimist in the way that drowning men are swimmers.

"Sera," I said. "Be honest with me."

"I'm always honest with you. It's one of my least popular qualities."

"Which way is down?"

She stopped. She had been walking three paces ahead of me for the better part of an hour, chin up, hands clasped behind her back like a duchess inspecting a garden, which was either genuine composure or the most elaborate act of spite ever performed against a tunnel. In the red glow of my lantern her face was all shadow and cheekbone.

"That's not a direction," she said.

"In here it might be."

We stood in a junction of four passages, each of them identical, each of them breathing that same slow furnace breath. The walls were veined with something faintly luminous—not the living gold of the map's ink but a duller rust color, like veins in old marble, like the mountain's own tired blood. My feet hurt. My hands shook. And somewhere behind us, muffled by miles of stone, the Ember Court's gate had sealed with a sound I was going to hear every time I closed my eyes for the rest of my life: a boom, then a long grinding sigh, like the mountain swallowing.

Corvin had signaled them. Corvin, who taught me to read stars through cloud cover. Corvin, who held my hair back when I had fever at eleven and told me stories about roads with names instead of numbers. Corvin, who had raised me in a tollhouse full of silence and called it love.

He'd led us straight to the door and knocked on it for them.

I sat down. Not gracefully. I sat down the way a puppet sits when someone cuts the strings, and the lantern clinked against the stone, and the rust-veins pulsed once as if annoyed at me personally.

"Edmund." Sera's voice came from very far away, which was strange, because she was standing right there. "Edmund, get up. We can't stop here."

"Why not?" I heard myself laugh. It came out wrong, thin and high, a stranger's laugh. "It's warm. It's the first warm thing that hasn't betrayed me today."

"You're in shock."

"I'm in a tunnel under a mountain that eats memories, being hunted by people made of ash, having been sold out by the only father I've ever—"

The word snagged. *Father.* I couldn't finish the sentence. I discovered that if I tried to finish it, something in my chest would tear along a seam I hadn't known was there, so I didn't try. I just sat there with the unfinished sentence sitting in me like a swallowed stone.

Sera crouched in front of me. Up close, I could see what her duchess posture was for: her hands weren't clasped anymore, they were knotted together hard enough that the knuckles had gone white, and there was a fine tremor running through her shoulders that she was holding still through sheer aristocratic stubbornness. She was terrified too. She'd been terrified since the inn at Hollowmere, probably since long before that, and she had walked every step of this anyway.

That should have comforted me. Instead it made everything worse, because if *she* could be this afraid and still standing, then my sitting down wasn't fear. It was just truth.

"He's dead to me," I said. "That's the phrase, isn't it? People say it all the time. He's dead to me. Like it's a decision you make over tea."

"Edmund—"

"Except he isn't, is he? Because I don't get to decide that. He decided it years ago, when he left the guild, when he left *me*, and I spent six years telling myself he had reasons, good ones, secret ones, that someday he'd come back up the high path and explain everything, and now he has come back, and the explanation is—" My voice did something humiliating. I let it. There was no one left to perform competence for. "The explanation is that he sold us. Sold *me*. Walked me to their front gate like a goat to market."

Sera said nothing. That was the thing about her—I learned it properly that night, in the dark and the heat. Everyone else would have rushed to fill the silence. Sera let it sit there between us, enormous, until I had finished being awful in it.

Then she said: "My brother's name is Aldric."

I looked up.

"You've never asked why I ran," she went on. "You're polite in a feral sort of way—you ask about routes and rations and whether I can hold a knife correctly, and you never once asked what a nobleman's daughter was doing alone in Thornfell with stolen linens and no escort. I assumed you didn't care."

"It seemed rude."

"It seemed *safe*," she corrected, gently, which was worse. "Fine. Here is the answer you never asked for. Two years ago I was betrothed to Lord Cassian Vael, who is charming, wealthy, and holds a seat in the Ember Court's outer circle. Do you understand what that means? Not metaphorically. There are families in the lowlands who serve the Court. They host its collectors. They arrange tribute. My marriage was meant to be a trade route—one more road for memory to travel south, dressed up in silk and a wedding feast."

The rust-veins pulsed. Her voice stayed level, the way water stays level right up until it goes over the edge.

"Aldric found the ledger," she said. "Our family's copy of the tribute rolls. He was seventeen and curious and he read past the page he was supposed to read, and he found his own name in it. Written in ember-ink. Dated for collection." She paused. "He told our parents. Our parents told him he'd misread it. So he told *me*, because I was the only person in that house who had ever believed him about anything, and two weeks later the Court's collectors came for him, and everyone pretended it was a fever. A fever that leaves no body. A fever that strikes the whole household blind to the empty bed."

"Sera—"

"I stole the ledger the night before my wedding," she said. "Not the wedding vows. Those I could have survived. But the contract—the part where my dowry included access, introductions, a lifetime of opening doors for the Court's people—I couldn't sign my name to that. So I took the ledger and I ran north into mountains full of smugglers' paths, because I reasoned that anyone who knew those paths hated the Court enough to help me." She smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile. "And the first one I met refused to take me in out of habit, and then watched a man die on a switchback, and then couldn't sleep for guilt, and followed me anyway."

"I didn't follow you," I said. "We were going the same direction."

"We were going the same direction," she agreed, "and you have complained about it every league of the way, and you have also, without being asked, taught me twelve waymarks, given me the dry bedroll twice, and stood between me and an ash-enforcer with nothing in your hand but a frying pan."

"It was a good pan."

"It was a *terrible* pan, Edmund. It bent."

I laughed again, and this time it came out human, cracked and wet around the edges, and I put my face in my hands because there was nothing else to do with it.

* * *

Here is what despair actually feels like, in case you've been spared it: it doesn't feel like sadness. Sadness moves. Despair is stillness. It's the moment on the Weeping Stair when I was nine and the lantern guttered and I thought, with perfect calm, *I will simply sit down here and become part of the mountain*, and the only reason I kept walking was that Corvin had said *don't stop*, and obedience was the only shape my courage had back then.

Corvin wasn't here to tell me anything now. That was the whole problem. Every compass I owned pointed at a man who had sold me, and without him I was just a boy with a lantern in a hole in the world, and the world was warm the way a mouth is warm.

Sera sat down beside me. Not across from me—beside, shoulder almost touching shoulder, in the manner of someone settling in for a long siege rather than delivering a speech.

"When Aldric was taken," she said, "my mother burned his drawings. All of them. He used to sketch birds—he was obsessed with kestrels—and she burned them because looking at them hurt, and I stood in the courtyard and watched the ash go up, and I thought: *that's how they win. Not by taking him. By making the taking tidy.*" She turned her head, and in the red light her eyes caught the glow like the map's ink did. "The Court doesn't feed on souls, Edmund. Or—it does, but that's not the meal. The meal is the forgetting afterward. The tidy house. The burned drawings. Every person who shrugs and says *fever* and goes back to breakfast. That's what sustains them. Grief that's been filed away."

"So what do we do," I said, "grieve loudly?"

"We remember *on purpose*." She said it like a vow. Like something she'd rehearsed in the dark of a hundred campsites while I snored. "Aldric liked kestrels. He whistled off-key. He once pushed me into the ornamental pond at midsummer court and blamed a duck, and everyone believed him about the duck because Aldric was that kind of believable, and I have carried that duck story through two winters and one broken engagement and I will carry it into that Court if I have to crawl. Because as long as I remember him wrong and loud and completely, they haven't finished eating."

The tunnel breathed. The rust-veins dimmed and brightened, dimmed and brightened, patient as tides.

"And you?" she asked. "What do you carry?"

And that was the moment. I want to be precise about it, because I spent a long time afterward trying to locate exactly where the turning happened, and it was there, in that question, asked without pity. Not *what do you have left*. Not *why go on*. Just: *what do you carry.*

Because the answer arrived before I could stop it, and the answer was a lullaby.

I don't know where it came from. That's the truth, and the truth is stranger than any version I could invent. There is a song I have known my whole life—four notes rising, two falling, then a hummed line I could never find words for—and I always assumed Corvin had sung it to me as a baby, except Corvin didn't sing, Corvin claimed music attracted things best left unfed, and yet there it was, lodged in me like a splinter of light, older than my earliest memory. I used to hum it on the high paths without noticing. I'd caught myself doing it the night the courier died, and stopped, ashamed, as if humming at a deathbed were some failure of gravity.

Four notes rising. Two falling. Then the hum.

"My mother," I said. My voice came out rough. "I don't remember her. Corvin wouldn't speak of her—wouldn't speak of anything, that was the whole architecture of my childhood, a house built entirely out of things unsaid. But there's this song. I've had it as long as I've had anything. And in the vault—" My hands started shaking again, but differently now. "In the vault, before the alarms, before everything went to ash and screaming, there was a soul on the shelves burning gold, and it was *humming*. Four notes rising. Two falling. And I knew it, Sera. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, which is to say I'd never once thought about it and couldn't live without it."

"That's her," Sera said quietly. "That's how she's been keeping herself. A song is the smallest room a soul can live in, and she built one and waited in it for twenty-some years."

"For me," I said. "She's been waiting in a song for me."

The despair didn't vanish. I want to be honest about that too, because the books make it sound like a door closing, clean and final, and it wasn't. The despair stayed. It sat beside me in the warm dark like a third traveler. But it stopped being the largest thing in the tunnel, because grief with a name and a melody turns out to weigh less than grief that's just a hole, and I found that I could stand up inside it.

I got to my feet. My knees objected. I ignored them, which is most of what competence is.

"All right," I said. "The ledger. You have it memorized?"

"Every name. Every date of collection. Why?"

"Because the Court keeps records," I said, and the smuggler in me—the part that had taken joy in the scree ambush, the part Sera had watched with that grudging half-smile—woke up and stretched. "Records mean order. Order means rules. And rules, Sera, are just roads with better manners. If they catalogued Aldric, they shelved him somewhere. Shelves can be found. Found things can be taken. I've spent my whole life learning how to take things through country that doesn't want me there, and I refuse—do you hear me, I *refuse*—to have walked this far to stop at a locked door."

"The door sealed."

"Doors seal. Roads don't." I pulled the map from my satchel and unrolled it, and the ember-ink stirred at my touch, sluggish but alive, gold threads waking in the dark like something smelling smoke. "This thing chose me. It dragged us to the unmapped road, it opened the gate's side-passage, it's been leading since Hollowmere. It wants to go home as badly as we do. So we stop wandering and start asking it nicely."

"How do you ask a map nicely?"

"You put your blood on it and hope," I said. "It's worked before."

"It's worked *once*."

"Once is a road," I said. "Everything starts as once."

She looked at me for a long moment—assessing, the way she assessed everything, a noblewoman pricing goods—and whatever she saw must have cleared inspection, because she rose, brushed off her ruined skirts with two efficient strokes, and said, "Then bleed on it, smuggler. And while you do, I'll tell you about the duck."

* * *

The map took my blood the way dry ground takes rain—greedily, silently, spreading it along lines I hadn't drawn. And the ink answered. Gold crept out from beneath my palm and crawled down the parchment and pooled at the lower edge, and there, where no cartographer had ever admitted to drawing anything, a passage appeared: thin, branching, descending, marked with a symbol I recognized from the tollhouse records—a smelter's mark, the old guild sign for *fire that serves*.

And beside it, in handwriting that moved like embers and looked, impossibly, like my own:

*The deep road remembers its walkers.*

"Well," said Sera, reading over my shoulder. "That's either an invitation or a threat."

"With this family," I said, rolling the map closed, "it's usually both."

We turned south, into the warm dark, toward the smelter road and the Court that fed on forgotten things. Behind us lay the sealed gate and the man who had betrayed us; ahead lay everything else. I hummed four notes rising, two falling, and Sera, without comment, hummed them back.

Somewhere below, in a glass shelf in a vault of quiet, I liked to think something gold heard us coming.

And began, very softly, to sing along.
