## 21. The Ember Court

There is a particular kind of terror that comes from being dressed well in a place where you should not be. I had felt it before—at my presentation at the Amadine estate, at three separate betrothal dinners, at the funeral of an aunt whose death I may have accelerated through sheer force of wishing. But none of those compared to walking toward the Ember Court wearing the robes of a tribute-bearer, with a stolen ledger sewn into my bodice and Edmund Villalobos shuffling beside me like a man who had never once been inside a building with a ceiling.

"Stop fidgeting," I murmured.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're adjusting your collar for the eleventh time. Tribute-bearers don't have collars they're allowed to adjust. They have collars that were decided for them." I kept my eyes forward, on the line of robed figures ahead of us winding down the under-road. "You look like a servant who has stolen his master's clothes and is hoping no one looks too closely."

"That's essentially what's happening."

"Yes, but you're meant to *commit* to it. Confidence is ninety percent of disguise. The other ten percent is not sweating visibly, which you are currently failing at spectacularly."

He wiped his palms on his robe. "You've done this before. Infiltration."

"I've attended court functions," I said. "Which is the same thing with better wine and slower murder."

* * *

The road down was warm. Not summer-warm—*forge*-warm, the kind of heat that came up through the soles of your boots and made you aware, constantly, that you were walking on the skin of something vast and sleeping. The ember-ink map had led us here, through tunnels that glowed faintly along their seams like veins of cooling iron, and Edmund had gone quiet somewhere around the third hour of descent and hadn't properly recovered.

I understood why. I'd grown up in the lowlands, in marble halls and manicured gardens, and even I felt the weight of this place pressing down. For Edmund—a man who had spent his whole life in thin mountain air and crooked smuggler paths—descending into the belly of Thornfell must have been like drowning slowly while fully conscious.

The tribute procession ahead of us numbered perhaps thirty: bearers from villages I recognized, settlements scattered across the foothills, each delegation carrying their tithe. I saw a family hauling a chest bound in black iron. Two sisters carrying between them a birdcage draped in cloth, though whatever was inside didn't sing or shift. A lone old man cradling a small clay jar against his chest the way one might carry an infant, or ashes.

"What do you think they're paying?" Edmund whispered.

"Tribute," I said. "That's all we need to know. Don't ask questions you don't want answers to. That's the first rule of court survival."

"You said the first rule was 'never be interesting.'"

"The second rule, then. There are many rules. Courts generate them the way rot generates flies."

Ahead, the tunnel opened.

I had prepared myself for a great deal. I had imagined a cavern, a fortress, something crude and terrible—the lair of ash-things, all jagged stone and menace. What I had not prepared for was beauty.

The Ember Court was a cathedral built inside a volcano's throat—or rather, a cathedral that *was* the volcano's throat, its walls the cooled lava itself, frozen mid-flow into columns and arches and buttresses of black glass shot through with veins of dull red, as though the mountain had tried to scream once, millennia ago, and the scream had hardened into architecture. The vaulted ceiling rose higher than any cathedral in the lowlands, lost in a haze of drifting embers that hung suspended in the air like a sky full of slow, patient stars. And the light—not torchlight, nothing so honest—came from the walls themselves, pulsing faintly, breathing almost, so that the whole space seemed to expand and contract with a rhythm just slightly slower than a heartbeat.

"It's beautiful," Edmund said, and then immediately looked ashamed of having said it.

"Don't be ashamed," I said quietly. "It's meant to be. That's how it catches you."

* * *

We joined the queue at the Court's threshold, where a figure in grey robes checked each delegation against a list written on what appeared to be strips of vellum—but the writing moved, rearranging itself, and I realized with a lurch that the vellum was skin, and the writing was remembering itself.

"Name your tribute," the grey figure said when our turn came. Its voice was dry as paper. Its hood tilted toward Edmund, then me.

Edmund opened his mouth. I stepped smoothly forward. "Memory-tithe, carried personally, by right of blood-debt," I said, using the phrase I'd pried out of a trembling innkeeper two valleys back who had once paid the Court himself. "Our house sends its regards to the Magistrate."

The hood regarded me for a long moment. Underneath it there was a face, technically. Features arranged in the approximate configuration of a face, the way a scarecrow approximates a man. Then it gestured us through, and my heart resumed beating only because I ordered it to.

"How did you know that?" Edmund breathed as we passed beneath an arch of fused obsidian.

"I told you. I served the Court for two years before I ran." I kept my smile fixed and pleasant, the smile I'd worn through every dinner where I was being appraised like livestock. "You learn the liturgy. Courts are temples, Edmund, whatever else they pretend to be. Every transaction is a sacrament. Every humiliation is dressed up as an honor. You survive by learning the words and saying them at the right moments."

"And if we say them at the wrong moments?"

"Then we find out what happens to tribute-bearers who are interesting." I glanced sideways at him. "So don't be interesting."

* * *

Inside, the Court proper unfolded in tiers descending toward a central floor, and this was where awe curdled into revulsion, because now I could see what the nobles were doing.

They sat in alcoves carved into the lava walls behind screens of smoked glass, dozens of them, masked in porcelain faces—serene, identical, beautiful in the way corpses at well-funded funerals are beautiful. Between the alcoves moved servants bearing trays, and upon the trays were—

I stopped walking. Edmund bumped into me.

Upon the trays were memories. I don't know how else to describe it. Small things, mostly: a brass button; a child's wooden horse; a lock of hair tied with ribbon; a seashell; a wedding ring worn thin. And around each object hung a faint shimmer, a heat-haze distortion, as though the air itself remembered something and couldn't let go. When a masked noble extended a languid hand and drew one of these objects close, the shimmer sank into the porcelain mask, and sometimes the mask would tilt, would *savor*, the way a man tilts his head over a good vintage.

"They trade them," Edmund whispered. His voice had gone strange. "Memories. Like—"

"Like coin. Yes." My mouth tasted of copper. "First memories sell highest. A mother's face. The smell of your childhood kitchen. The moment you fell in love. The nobles collect them the way my father collected hunting trophies—except a trophy stag doesn't wake up one morning unable to remember why it ever ran."

A tray passed near us. On it lay a small tin whistle, dented, and around it the haze shimmered gold. A noble's hand drifted toward it, hesitated, chose instead a faded letter beside it, and the whistle went on to the next alcove, unwanted, still humming with someone's whole childhood.

I thought of my brother. Tomas, who had been clever and loud and eight years old the last time I saw him, before the Court's service took him, before I found his name in a stolen ledger among thousands of others filed under *assets*. Somewhere in this cathedral of cooled fire, some porcelain-faced creature might be wearing my brother's memory of me like a brooch.

My hands wanted to shake. I did not permit them to. This, too, was court training: you do not tremble where the predators can see it. You tremble later, alone, in a locked room, and then you wash your face and come back and smile.

"This way," I said, and steered us toward the eastern colonnade, away from the trading floor, toward the vault levels below.

* * *

The lower galleries were quieter. Tribute-bearers were permitted—expected—to wait in certain antechambers while their tithes were processed, and processing, I knew from my years of service, could take hours. It was designed to take hours. Waiting was part of the ritual. Waiting wore you down until you'd agree to anything just to be allowed to leave.

We settled onto a stone bench in an antechamber lit by a single vein of glowing rock, surrounded by other waiting delegations: the old man with his clay jar, the sisters with their silent birdcage, a merchant couple who stared at nothing with the particular blankness of people who had already paid more than objects.

Edmund leaned close. "The map. Do you feel it?"

I did. Through the layers of my clothing, the ember-ink map pressed against my ribs, and it was *warm*, warmer than the air, and it pulsed—that same slow rhythm as the breathing walls. As though it were glad to be home.

"It knows where the vaults are," he murmured. "I can feel it pulling. Down and east."

"Then we wait for the changing of the watch. Midnight by the ember-clocks, when the nobles retire to their alcoves and the floor clears." I recited it like scripture, because I had learned it as scripture: two years of service, two years of memorizing the rhythms of this place while smiling at people I despised, all so that someday I could come back and steal my brother out from under them.

"Sera." Edmund's voice dropped further. "Those trays. Those objects. If someone sold a memory here—sold it properly, gave it up—would they know? Would they feel the hole?"

I looked at him. In the vein-light his face was half ember, half shadow, and he looked very young and very frightened and very determined, all at once, which I was learning was simply his face.

"Yes," I said. "And no. That's the cruelty of it. You feel that something is missing. You reach for it and your hand closes on nothing, and everyone tells you there was never anything there at all. Eventually—" I gestured at the merchant couple on the far bench, staring at nothing. "Eventually you stop reaching."

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "My master raised me. Corvin. He never spoke about my mother. Never. I used to think she died, or left, or that I'd done something—" He stopped. Started again. "What if I can't remember her because there's nothing left to remember? What if the reason the questions hurt isn't grief? What if it's just—an empty shelf, and I've spent my whole life grieving furniture?"

The question landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I thought of Tomas again. I thought of how, in the two years since I fled my marriage and stole the ledger, I had caught myself forgetting things about him—the exact pitch of his laugh, whether he'd been missing teeth when he grinned, small treasons of memory—and each loss had felt like a door closing in a house I couldn't find anymore.

"Then we get it back," I said. "Whatever's on the shelves. We get it back, and we put it where it belongs, and anyone who stands in the way learns what an Amadine does when her family is stolen." I paused. "Well. What a runaway Amadine does. Which is mostly theft and poor decisions, but with excellent posture."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first in days. "Excellent posture," he repeated.

"It matters more than you'd think. Half of nobility is posture. The other half is knowing where the exits are." I stood, smoothing my borrowed robes, and nodded toward the corridor beyond the antechamber, where the ember-veins in the walls ran brighter, running down and east, deeper into the mountain's heart. Toward the vaults. Toward the shelves of glass I had read about in the ledger's final pages and had seen in my dreams every night since.

"The watch changes soon," I said. "Stay close, don't be interesting, and Edmund—if we see the Magistrate, you let me do the talking."

"Why you?"

"Because I'm better at lying to powerful people than you are, and because unlike you, I've had practice wanting something in this building without letting it show on my face." I started toward the corridor, then stopped, because down the eastern passage, past the colonnade, a figure had appeared at the far end of the gallery—one of the ash-bound enforcers, grey and drifting, its hood turning slowly, methodically, from delegation to delegation, the way a searcher turns the pages of a book looking for a particular word.

And then it turned toward us, and stopped.

"*Sera,*" Edmund breathed.

"Don't run," I said through my teeth, arranging my face into serene, stupid, tribute-bearer calm. "Whatever happens—don't. Be. Interesting."

The enforcer began to walk toward us, and behind its shoulder, at the end of the gallery, the great doors of the Magistrate's hall swung open, spilling light the color of a dying sun across the polished floor—and a voice like cooled honey called out, unhurried and absolutely certain:

"Bring them to me. I believe one of them has something of mine."
