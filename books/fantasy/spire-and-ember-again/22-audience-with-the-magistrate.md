## 22. Audience with the Magistrate

The trouble with disguises is that they only work on people who aren't looking for you.

I'd known that as a smuggler's maxim since I was nine years old. Corvin used to say it while teaching me to walk like a porter, slouch like a pilgrim, and bleed like a man who'd already lost the fight. *Nobody sees a face they've already decided isn't worth seeing.* For twenty years I had been the least interesting person in every room in Thornfell, and I'd built a whole life on it.

So of course the Ember Court saw through me before I'd crossed half its floor.

But let me back up, because the getting-in part deserves telling. Sera was magnificent, and I want that on the record, because later—well. Later.

* * *

The tribute-bearers arrived at the Court's outer gate twice a season, Sera told us, from villages too small or too frightened to refuse. They came carrying goods the mountain wanted: letters, heirlooms, wedding rings, locks of hair from children who'd grown up and moved away. Memory-adjacent things. Things soaked in someone's past. The Court's stewards met them at a gate of black iron shaped like interlocking hands, counted the offerings against a ledger, and waved them through without once looking at their faces.

"Because looking costs them something," Sera said, adjusting the hood over my hair. "Every face is a memory they might accidentally keep. The nobles down here are terrified of accumulation. It's the one vice they can't afford."

"And we're going to walk in wearing borrowed faces."

"We're going to walk in wearing *boring* faces." She handed me a covered tray—the kind with a cloth draped over whatever sat beneath—and took up her own. "You're a courier's son from Hollowmere bringing your father's letters. I'm his second wife. Try to look resentful about it."

"That won't require acting."

"There's my boy."

The gate opened onto a cathedral of cooled lava, and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it properly. The walls were basalt folded like frozen cloth, veined with threads of ember-light that pulsed slow as sleeping breath. The ceiling rose so high that ash drifted down through it like snow falling upward in reverse, settling on the shoulders of masked figures who glided between pillars of glassy stone. Everything was warm. Not fire-warm—hearth-warm, bathwater-warm, the warm of a hand on the back of your neck. It got under my skin immediately, and I hated how much my body liked it.

The masks were what did it, though. Every noble wore one: porcelain faces, smooth and featureless except for a single painted expression each—a smile, a frown, a look of mild surprise—frozen forever. They traded beneath those masks. I watched a woman in a gown of grey silk press a small tin to another's palm, and the other woman shuddered with pleasure, and somewhere behind her frozen smile she whispered a price, and the first woman nodded, and that was that. A memory changed hands as casually as a coin.

"They can't feel their own pasts," Sera murmured, eyes forward, voice barely moving her lips. "That's what the Court does to its own, eventually. They sell and sell until there's nothing left inside to feel with. So they buy. Always buying. It never fills the hole."

"How do you know all this?"

"I read a ledger," she said, "that no one was supposed to read. Walk."

* * *

We were three columns deep in the procession of tribute-bearers when the steward stopped us.

He was tall in the way that suggested he'd been tall for a very long time, robed in ash-grey, his mask painted with an expression of courteous attention. He lifted the cloth from my tray, examined my father's supposed letters—actually bundles of blank paper Sera had aged with tea and smoke—and set the cloth back down.

Then he looked at my face.

I did everything right. I gave him the dull, put-upon stare of a man hauling his dead wife's correspondence across a mountain. I slouched. I breathed through my mouth a little. And none of it mattered, because behind the porcelain, something leaned close, the way you lean toward a smell you almost recognize.

"Villalobos," the steward said.

It wasn't a question. My blood went so cold that the Court's warmth felt like an insult.

Sera's hand found my elbow—steadying, warning, *don't run*. Running would have been correct, probably. Running was certainly what every instinct I owned recommended. But there were enforcers along the walls, drifts of grey in human shapes, and one of them had already turned its head toward us with the slow inevitability of weather.

"The name is familiar to the Court," the steward continued, and now the courteous attention in his painted expression seemed almost tender. "The Magistrate will receive you. Both of you. Come."

There's a particular flavor of doom that arrives politely. I've tasted it exactly once, and this was it.

* * *

They took our trays at a door of hammered copper and led us up—or what felt like up; the stairs spiraled in ways that made my inner ear file a formal complaint—into a chamber that was smaller than I expected and worse.

It was round, walled in the same veined basalt, lit by a single brazier whose flames burned white instead of orange. There was a table. There were two chairs. There was a third chair behind the table, occupied.

The Magistrate wore no mask.

I want to be clear about why that was horrifying. Downstairs, the masks were mercy—porcelain where a face should be, a fixed lie standing in for a truth too empty to bear. The Magistrate had no such arrangement. What sat behind the table had a face, technically. Features moved across it the way light moves across water: a jaw that was old, then young, then neither; eyes that were brown, then grey, then mine, then Sera's, then someone else's entirely. It wasn't shapeshifting. It was borrowing. Every few seconds the face tried on a different person, wore them briefly, and discarded them, and I understood—with the animal certainty you get in the worst moments of your life—that each of those faces had belonged to someone who'd come down here and never come out.

"Edmund Villalobos." The voice matched the face problem—it was a woman's voice, then a man's, then something with harmonics no throat should produce. "Last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild. Do you know how long I have waited for a Villalobos to walk through my door?"

"I'm flattered," I said, because terror makes me glib, and glibness has kept me alive more times than skill has. "Though I'll note I didn't come voluntarily, so the waiting seems like it worked out poorly for everyone."

The face settled—for just a moment—into something almost human. An older man, tired, kind around the eyes. It lasted two seconds and was somehow more frightening than the churn.

"Honesty. Good. Your guildmaster never managed it. Corvin sweated through three audiences and lied in every one." The Magistrate gestured, and the ember-map's weight against my ribs suddenly felt loud enough to hear. "You carry something of mine."

"It's mine. A dying man gave it to me."

"A dying man who stole it from a dead one, who stole it from a coward, who stole it from the guild that drew it in the first place. Yes. Provenance is complicated below the mountain. Possession, however—" the face flickered to a young woman, grief-stricken, gone again "—possession is simple. You have it. I want it. And I am prepared to pay."

"Everyone keeps offering me things today," I said. "It's making me nervous."

"What do you know of your mother?"

The room went very quiet. Even the brazier's white flames seemed to lean in.

"Nothing," I said. "Corvin said she died birthing me. He wouldn't say her name. Wouldn't say anything. Twenty-six years of nothing."

"Mm." The Magistrate laced long fingers together—fingers that were scarred, then ringed, then smooth, then scarred. "And has it never struck you as strange? A guildmaster who records every road, every toll, every debt in meticulous books—leaving one page blank? Not lost. Blank. Scrubbed. As if the memory itself were dangerous?"

I thought of Corvin's study. Of the gap in the ledger I'd found at fourteen, the pages cut cleanly away, the spine re-sewn by hands that knew thread. I'd assumed shame. Debts, maybe. Some scandal.

"She didn't die birthing you, Edmund Villalobos." The Magistrate's voice dropped into gentleness, which was the cruelest register it had used yet. "She was sold. The year you were born. To cover the final debt of the founding pact. Her soul sits in my vault as we speak—burning gold, brighter than any I hold. She has been down here twenty-six years, singing a lullaby to shelves of glass, waiting for someone to remember her name."

Beside me, Sera made a small sound. I couldn't look at her. I couldn't look anywhere. The warmth of the Court pressed against my skin and I wished, violently, to be cold.

"You're lying," I said. It came out flat and unconvincing even to me.

"I have no reason to lie. I am not a creature of falsehood—I am a creature of *exchange*. I deal in true things. That is what makes me honest, if nothing else." The face settled again into the tired old man, and held. "Here is my offer. Give me the map. Give me your memories of her—all of them, every story Corvin let slip, every question you asked him, every night you wondered. I will take them, and in exchange I will release her soul to rise free. She goes home to the sky. You stay whole, alive, unharmed, rich in everything except her."

"That's not a trade," Sera said sharply. "That's—"

"It is the only trade on the table." The Magistrate didn't raise its voice. The room raised it for them. "Consider what the boy gains. He never knew her. He mourns an absence, a shape with no features. Sell me the shape, and the real woman walks free. His grief buys her freedom. Is that not the finest use grief has ever been put to?"

And here is the thing I am not proud of, the thing I will carry longer than the scars from this mountain:

For a moment—one long, warm, terrible moment—I wanted to say yes.

Not because I doubted Sera, or the plan, or the ledger full of names. Because the offer fit a hole in me perfectly. Twenty-six years of wondering. Twenty-six years of watching other men talk about their mothers and feeling that specific quiet shame, like a stutter you learn to hide. And the Magistrate stood there offering to make the hole *useful*. To convert the ache into rescue. To let my ignorance be the price of her freedom, so that even my emptiness would finally have meant something.

I could know her by giving up knowing her. I could save her by selling the last of her. There was a symmetry to it that felt, in that warm room, like grace.

My hand had moved. I don't remember deciding. It had simply risen to my collarbone, where the map hung hidden, fingers curling around the strap—

"Edmund." Sera's voice. Low. Not pleading. She knew better than to plead. "Tell me what you're trading. Say it out loud. All of it."

I looked at her.

"My memories of her," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "All of them."

"And after?" she said. "After she rises free, and you walk out of here, and some spring day you find yourself humming a tune you don't know, or loving the smell of rain on hot stone and having no idea why—what happens when you meet her freed soul someday, on some road, and feel that pull, that recognition, and have nothing inside you to answer it with? She'll know you, Edmund. And you'll be a stranger holding a stranger's hand, wondering why you're crying."

The Magistrate's face flickered—annoyance, quickly smoothed—and I realized with a cold drop in my stomach that it hadn't mentioned any of that. Hadn't mentioned that freed souls sometimes lingered. Hadn't mentioned what remembering feels like from the other side.

"You would erase her twice," Sera said quietly. "Once when they sold her. Once when you sold the last of her. I've read the ledger. I know what erasure looks like. I'm not letting it happen to you, and I am *certainly* not letting it happen to her."

The warmth drained out of the room—or out of me; I honestly couldn't tell the difference anymore. My hand fell from the map's strap. The temptation didn't vanish—you don't get to unwant a thing like that—but it stepped back, and I stepped with it, and I turned to the thing behind the table wearing a dead man's tired kindness.

"No," I said.

The Magistrate regarded me for a long moment. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat through stone, I heard—or imagined I heard—a sound like singing.

"No?" The face began to churn faster now, features cycling like cards being shuffled by an angry hand. "No, Edmund Villalobos? You understand what refusal means. The debt comes due at solstice. If you will not trade, I will simply take—at solstice, with the pact's full weight behind me, I will collect everything owed, and the Villalobos account has *compounded*."

"Then I suppose," I said, and found, to my genuine surprise, that I was smiling—that wild, weightless smuggler's smile that shows up when the bridge starts collapsing behind you, "we'd both better hope I'm faster than your paperwork."

The Magistrate rose. The chair didn't scrape; the room simply decided the chair had never existed.

"Guards," it said, in a voice made of every voice it had ever worn, "escort our guests to the vaults. Let them see what remains of the guild. Let them see what *refusal* keeps company with."

The doors opened onto drifting ash, and the last thing I heard before the enforcers closed around us was the Magistrate, softly, in a voice that was suddenly, horribly, my mother's:

"Bring me the boy's name. I'll start collecting from there."

* * *

They marched us down. Deeper than the trading floor, deeper than the copper doors, along corridors where the ember-veins in the rock pulsed quicker, as if the mountain itself had begun to hurry. Sera walked beside me, wrists bound in grey cord that drank the warmth from skin, and she didn't say *I told you so*, which is how I knew she was frightened too.

At a junction ahead, two enforcers peeled off to lead, and the one directly behind me stumbled—just slightly, just for a step—and I heard it make a sound. Not words. A hum. Three notes, rising, the opening of something that might have been a song.

I knew the song.

I had no idea how I knew the song.

And the vaults, glowing gold through the grates ahead, waited to tell me why.
