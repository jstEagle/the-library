## 23. The Price of Remembering

The Magistrate's offer hung in the air of the Ember Court the way smoke hangs in a closed room—everywhere, inescapable, and slowly filling up all the space where my good sense used to live.

*Your mother's freedom,* he had said, in that voice like cooling stone. *In exchange for the map, and for your memory of her.*

The trouble—the whole rotten, beautiful trouble of it—was that I didn't have a memory of her. Not one. Corvin had made sure of that, whether by silence or by something worse, and so the Magistrate was offering me the only version of my mother I would ever get to know: hers, given back, at the price of never knowing what I'd paid.

I stood there in the cathedral of cooled lava with the masked nobles drifting around us like moths who'd gotten tired of lamps and decided to worship candles instead, and I thought: *that's not even a real trade.* And then I thought: *but it's her.* And then I thought nothing at all for a long moment, because grief does that. It empties you out and leaves the shelves standing, waiting to be stocked.

"Take your time," said the Magistrate, and there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened me more than any threat could have. Cruelty you can brace against. Kindness from a thing that eats memories is a fishhook wrapped in bread.

Sera's hand found my sleeve. She didn't pull. She just held on, the way you hold onto a railing in an earthquake—not to stop the shaking, but to know which way is down.

"I need a moment," I heard myself say.

"Moments are our principal commodity," the Magistrate replied. "We have rooms for them."

* * *

They gave us a alcove off the great hall—a niche carved into the lava wall, curtained in some fabric that wasn't silk and wasn't wool and moved like it was remembering being water. A servant brought wine that tasted faintly of iron and, underneath that, of something I couldn't name, which worried me until Sera sniffed hers and said, "It's just wine. They don't drug tribute-bearers. It's bad for the merchandise."

"The merchandise," I repeated.

"We're pretending to be tribute-bearers. Try to keep up." But her voice had no edge in it. She set her cup down untouched and looked at me with those grey eyes that missed nothing, and she said, "You were going to say yes."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. I looked at my hands instead. They were steady, which seemed like a betrayal of their owner.

"Edmund."

"I don't remember her," I said. The words came out flat as coins on a counter. "That's the joke of it. That's the whole—Corvin raised me and he never once told me her name. Not her name, Sera. I've been carrying a struck-through line on a chapel wall around in my head for weeks, and I don't even know if Villalobos is her name or his or mine by accident. So what exactly am I selling? What memory? He asked for my memory of her and I have—" My voice did something embarrassing, and I stopped talking until it behaved. "I have a shape. A space where she should be. Maybe that's what they buy. Maybe empty things weigh the same here."

Sera was quiet for a while. In the Court, quiet is a currency too; everyone spends it carefully. Then she said, "Can I tell you about my brother?"

"You've mentioned him. Tam. In the ledger."

"I've mentioned his name." She turned her cup in a slow circle without drinking. "Names aren't the same as stories. You of all people should know that."

Outside the curtain, the Court murmured. I caught fragments of trade—a viscount selling the smell of his grandmother's kitchen, someone bidding on the last summer of somebody's childhood—and each fragment landed on me like ash. I nodded at Sera to go on.

* * *

"My brother was four years older than me," she began, "and he was the kind of boy who took clocks apart. Not to break them—he always got them back together, mostly—but because he needed to see how a thing worked before he could trust it. He took apart our father's study once. All of it. Every drawer, every lock, the false bottom in the strongbox. Father beat him for it. Tam just said, 'Now I know where everything is,' and smiled through the blood, and Father couldn't stand him after that. Couldn't stand that Tam knew him."

She spoke the way she did everything now—precisely, without wasted motion, a noblewoman's diction worn smooth by two years of running. But her hands weren't precise. Her hands were shaking.

"When the marriage was arranged—I've told you about Lord Vessery, the one with the teeth—I started looking for ways out, and Tam started looking for leverage. That's what he called it. Leverage. He found the Court's ledgers. Not the public ones, the real ones, the ones that record what's actually held in the vaults beneath this mountain. And he found our family name in them."

"In the ledger," I said. "The one you stole."

"The one I stole *because of*. Listen. Our grandfather—my father's father—was a guildman. Wayfarers' Guild, third rank, road-warden on the eastern passes. Everyone said he died in an avalanche. There was a plaque. Mother wept at it every year." Sera's laugh had no humor anywhere near it. "He didn't die in an avalanche. His name is on shelf forty-one, row six, in a glass jar under this mountain, and the debt that put him there passed down my family's line like an heirloom nobody would talk about. When Tam read that—when he understood that Grandfather wasn't dead but *kept*, that the Court holds souls the way a miser holds coin, waiting for the exchange rate to improve—he went a little mad. I understand it now. I didn't then. I thought he was obsessed. I told him to let it go. I said, 'Tam, he's gone, grieving longer doesn't bring anyone back.'"

She stopped. The curtain swayed though there was no wind.

"What happened to him?" I asked, though I already knew, the way you know the shape of a room before you open the door.

"He went to negotiate. That was Tam—he believed everything could be taken apart and understood, even a court of memory-eaters. He walked up to the Magistrate's own door with proof of the debt and demanded Grandfather's release." Her voice went very level, the way water goes level right before it goes over the edge. "And the Magistrate listened so politely. And then he explained that debts pass down lines, and that a soul already held can be held against collateral, and that Tam, by coming here, by *knowing*, had assumed the obligation. They didn't take him by force, Edmund. He signed. He thought he was trading himself for Grandfather—one for one, clean arithmetic, very Tam. And the Court accepted the signature and kept them both, because the original debt hadn't come due yet, and a debtor in hand is worth two in the grave."

The wine sat untouched between us. Somewhere beyond the curtain, a masked figure laughed at something, and the sound was bright and hollow as a dropped bell.

"So when I tell you," Sera said, "that a memory traded is a person erased twice—you should understand that I'm not speaking philosophically. I watched it happen. Because here's the part I haven't told anyone. When they took Tam, they didn't just file his soul away. They took the *memory of him* as handling fee. From everyone who loved him. My parents woke the next morning in a house with a son's bedroom and no son, and no grief, because you can't grieve what you can't remember, and no questions, because the questions were taken too. There's a room in my father's house that's kept locked, and when I asked why, Mother said, 'What room?'"

Her hands had stopped shaking. That was somehow worse.

"I only remember Tam because I was already running when it happened. I'd stolen the ledger three days before and gone over the wall, and distance apparently blunts whatever instrument they use—it reached me as a kind of... static. A hole shaped like a person. I sat in a ditch outside Amadine lands and cried for a brother I could describe perfectly and could not, for the life of me, remember loving. I knew he took clocks apart. I could not remember his voice. I still can't." She looked up at me. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

I understood. That was the horror of it—I understood completely, and understanding changed nothing about the ache in my chest, because the ache didn't care about arithmetic.

"If you give them your memory," Sera said quietly, "you won't lose a fact. You'll lose *her*. Whatever small true thing lives in you—the shape you were talking about, the space where she should be—that space is her last foothold in the world. Your mother has been in a jar under a mountain for twenty-some years, Edmund, and the only place left on earth where she still exists uncollected is inside you. Inside the hole. If you sell the hole, there isn't a second copy in some archive. There's nothing. She gets erased twice—once by the guild that sold her, once by the son who traded the last of her away to buy her back. And the Court wins either way, because either way they end up holding everything, and you end up standing in a corridor feeling sad about a stranger."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because every objection I raised in my head died before it reached my mouth. *But it's her. But I'd finally know her. But the map, the debt, the solstice—* and against each one, Sera's voice: *a hole shaped like a person.*

"And besides," she added, with the terrible gentleness people use when they're about to hurt you for your own good, "think about what you'd actually be buying. Freedom for her soul—fine. And then what? She rises out of this mountain with twenty years of captivity behind her and comes looking for her son, and her son looks at her with pleasant, polite blankness. 'Madam, I think you're mistaken.' You'd free her into a world where nobody remembers her, including the one person whose whole body was built out of her remembering. Is that rescue? Or is it just moving the jar?"

* * *

I want to be honest here, since honesty turns out to be the entire point of everything that followed, and it seems fitting to start practicing early.

For a long moment after Sera finished speaking, I wanted to say yes anyway.

Not because I doubted her. Because wanting doesn't consult evidence. Because I had spent my whole life being a man-shaped absence—unremarkable Edmund, invisible Edmund, keeper of roads nobody traveled and tollhouses nobody needed—and here, at last, was a door out of the absence. Even a door with teeth in it. Even a door that cost everything. Grief makes bargains like a drunkard makes promises: sincerely, repeatedly, and with no intention whatsoever of thinking it through.

I could feel the Magistrate's patience on the other side of the curtain, vast and mineral, a patience that had outlasted guilds and dynasties and would happily outlast me. I could feel the ember-map against my ribs, warm as a sleeping animal. I could feel the shape of her—the space where my mother should be—pressing up against the inside of my chest like a hand against a window.

And I thought about Tam, taking apart clocks. *Now I know where everything is.*

The Court knew where everything was. That was its whole nature. It had catalogued my grandfather and my mother and a hundred guildmen whose names Corvin erased, and it knew exactly where everything was, and knowing had made it a thing that ate children's faces out of their mothers' memories for a handling fee. Knowledge wasn't the virtue I'd been taught to think it. Some things you're supposed to carry without opening.

"Say it out loud," Sera said. "Whatever you decide. Say it out loud. Words are the only weapon they've left us that works."

So I said it out loud.

"No."

The word came out smaller than I meant it to. I said it again, bigger.

"No. I won't sell her twice. I won't sell the map, and I won't sell the hole, and I won't stand in front of my mother someday and watch her realize her own son is a locked room." I laughed, and it came out cracked and half-mad, and I didn't care. "Twenty-six years of Corvin's silences, and I finally understand the lesson. Silence was the payment. Forgetting was the fee. The whole guild ran on it—safe roads bought with erased names, and every tollhouse on every pass just a receipt. Well. I'm done paying."

Sera exhaled, slow, and something in her shoulders came down an inch that might have been relief or might have been the beginning of hope, and I honestly couldn't tell which frightened her more.

"They'll take it badly," she said. "The refusal. The Magistrate doesn't hear 'no' often, and when he does, he collects the conversation itself."

"Then we'd best not be here for the collection." I touched the map through my shirt, and it pulsed against my palm like a second heartbeat, warm and insistent, and I swear on every erased name in that ledger that it felt less like a tool in that moment and more like a companion. Like something that had chosen its bearer a long time ago and was waiting for me to catch up. "You said the vaults are below the tribute galleries. Shelf forty-one, row six."

"Row six," she confirmed. "Why?"

"Because there's a song stuck in my head." I frowned, trying to look at it directly, the way you try to look at a star. "A lullaby. I don't know where I learned it. I don't remember learning anything—I don't remember *her*—but it's there, Sera, it's been there my whole life, hummed into some corner of me that Corvin's silences never reached. And if the Court deals in memory, then maybe..." I trailed off, because saying it aloud made it either true or ridiculous, and I wasn't ready to find out which.

"Maybe what?"

"Maybe the song knows the way to her better than I do."

I stood, and because there was no one in the alcove but Sera and the listening dark, and because words spoken aloud were the only weapon left, I did the maddest thing I had done yet in a life newly full of them.

I hummed it. Softly, barely more than breath—the little rising phrase, the falling answer, the phrase again, patient as footsteps on a stair. A lullaby with no name, from nowhere, belonging to no one.

Except that somewhere far below us, down past the tribute galleries and the smelter tunnels and the shelves of glass, something answered.

Sera heard it too. Her eyes went wide, and her hand closed hard around my wrist.

"Edmund," she whispered. "Something just moved in the walls."
