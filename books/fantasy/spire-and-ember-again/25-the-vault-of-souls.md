## 25. The Vault of Souls

*Edmund*

The map had been trying to tell me something for three days, and I had been doing my best not to listen.

That's the trouble with smuggler's craft: it teaches you to notice everything and trust nothing, which is a fine way to stay alive and a terrible way to stay sane. The ember-ink roads had been crawling across the vellum since we'd entered the under-road, rearranging themselves like ants disturbed in their nest, and every rearrangement ended the same way—with a single line thickening, darkening, pointing down and to the left, toward a door that wasn't marked as a door at all.

"It's the wall," Sera said.

"I can see it's the wall."

"You're staring at it like you expect it to apologize."

"I'm staring at it because maps don't point at blank stone unless they're broken, and if it's broken then we've walked three days through a mountain's warm gullet for nothing, and I would very much prefer the map be right and the wall be lying."

Sera leaned against the tunnel wall with her arms crossed, which was how she stood when she thought I was being an idiot but wanted to give me room to arrive at it myself. She'd been doing that a lot lately. It was infuriating. It was also, I was beginning to suspect, effective.

"Touch it," she said.

"The wall?"

"The map, Edmund. Touch the map to the wall."

I looked at the ember-ink. The ink looked back, or gave the impression of looking, the way fire does—patient, indifferent to whether you got the joke. Three days ago, back where the under-road forked, the map had flared warm under my fingers when no one else could feel it. Corvin had never mentioned that. Then again, Corvin had never mentioned much of anything, and he was currently somewhere behind us being dead or captured or worse, so his omissions were going to stay omissions.

I pressed the vellum flat against the stone.

The ink *drank*. That's the only word for it. The roads poured off the page and into the rock like water into cracked earth, and where they went, lines of ember-light bloomed in answer—a door, tall and narrow, drawing itself out of what had been solid mountain. The seam glowed, then cooled, then swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, which frightened me more than any grinding shriek would have. Doors that announce themselves want something from you. Doors that open silently have already decided you're coming in.

Cold air rolled out. Not cold the way the tunnels had been warm—cold like a cellar, like still water, like the space between heartbeats.

"Well," Sera said quietly. "It likes you."

"Don't say that."

"Why not?"

"Because things that like me in this mountain have a habit of wanting pieces of me in return."

She didn't argue. That was the other thing about Sera Amadine: she knew when a joke was load-bearing and let it stand without testing it.

We went in.

* * *

*Sera*

I had seen the Ember Court's ledger. I had stolen it out of my husband-to-be's study with shaking hands and a forged seal, and I had read it by candlelight in a hayloft until dawn, and I had thought—I had genuinely believed—that reading names on paper was the worst it could do to me.

I was wrong.

The vault opened before us like the inside of a cathedral built by something that had heard about cathedrals secondhand. Shelves. Rows upon rows of them, rising into darkness, carved from black glass or something pretending to be black glass, and on every shelf—

Lanterns. No. Not lanterns.

Jars. Vessels of clear glass, each one cradled in a niche, each one holding light that moved. Not flickered—*moved*, the way a sleeper moves, turning over, reaching, settling. Some burned white and small. Some guttered like candles in a draft. And the whole chamber hummed with a sound just below hearing, a chord made of a thousand almost-voices, and standing in it felt like standing inside someone else's held breath.

"They're all here," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Every name in the ledger."

"Is your brother—" Edmund started.

"There." I pointed before I could stop myself, before dignity could intervene. Third row, fourth shelf, a vessel burning a steady, stubborn blue—the exact blue of Tam's eyes when he was being contrary, which was always. "That's him. That's *him*, Edmund."

He didn't say anything stupid. He didn't say *are you sure* or *it might just be a light*. He put his hand briefly on my shoulder, hard, the way you'd grip someone on a cliff edge, and then he let go and we went down the aisle together.

Up close, the vessels were worse. Each one had a label etched into the glass—not names. Numbers. Dates. Ledger entries. A soul reduced to an accounting notation, filed between two others, dusted occasionally by hands I refused to imagine. Tam's read *AMADINE, T. — COLLATERAL, THIRD INSTANCE.*

Third instance. They'd taken from my family three times, and I hadn't known until the ledger told me, and even the ledger hadn't told me what the taking *felt* like.

I pressed my palm against the glass. The light inside surged toward my hand like a fish rising to feed, and the hum around us shifted—one thread of the great chord bending toward something that was almost, almost a voice.

"He knows me," I whispered. "Edmund. He knows me."

"Then we get him out." Edmund was already scanning the shelves, the walls, the ceiling, doing the thing he did—that quick smuggler's inventory, exits and weights and weaknesses. "There'll be a mechanism. There's always a mechanism. Vaults are just locks that got ambitious."

"And the key?"

He held up the map. It had gone quiet in his hands, the ember-ink settled into its roads like a dog finally home, and one road—the last road, the one that had drunk itself into the door—now pulsed gently, pointing deeper into the vault, toward a dais I could just see past the far shelving. On the dais sat something small and dark.

"That," Edmund said, "or nothing."

* * *

*Edmund*

The dais held a lock, and the lock was shaped like a hand.

Not metaphorically. It was a depression in black stone the size and shape of a spread palm, ringed with ember-script that I couldn't read and didn't need to; the map was already pulling toward it, tugging at my grip like a hound at the end of a lead.

"Every instinct I have," I said, "is telling me not to do this."

"Your instincts also told you not to take the courier's satchel," Sera said. "How did that work out?"

"Guiltily."

"And your instincts said don't trust me, and here we are, alive, having robbed exactly nobody yet. Your instincts are a coward with a good memory." She nodded at the lock. "Do it."

I laid the map into the hand-shaped hollow.

The stone closed over it like water closing over a dropped coin—and then the whole vault *woke*. Every vessel on every shelf brightened at once, a thousand souls turning in their sleep toward the sound of a key, and the hum rose from below-hearing into actual sound, into something with melody in it, and I staggered back a step because it was—

It was beautiful. That's the horror of this place nobody warns you about. It was beautiful the way a choir is beautiful, and every voice in it was imprisoned.

The nearest shelf shuddered. Glass ground against glass. One vessel, then another, slid an inch toward the aisle's edge, straining.

"They know the door's open," Sera breathed. "They all know."

"Then we work fast." I turned to the shelves, and my smuggler's brain did what it always did under pressure: went cold, went practical, went to work. "Glass comes out of these niches forward, not up—see the lip? Lift and pull. Don't tip them. Whatever's inside, don't tip it."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've moved enough contraband to know that anything carried carefully is carried alive, and anything tipped is spilled." I reached for the nearest vessel—a small white flame, trembling—and lifted it free.

It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. Holding it was like holding a sleeping bird and a hot coal at once, warmth and weightlessness together, and through the glass the light pressed toward my palms as if my hands were familiar. As if it remembered hands.

"Oh," I said, very quietly. "Oh, this is people. This is actually—"

"Yes." Sera's voice was flat and hard, which was how she sounded when she was keeping herself from breaking. "Now you understand why I stole a ledger instead of sleeping. Come on. We take them down row by row. We start with Tam."

We worked. I won't dress it up as anything more heroic than that—we worked, shelf by shelf, lifting vessels and laying them into the padded satchels we'd brought for exactly this, and the hum changed as we went, threading itself higher, brighter, a chord building toward something. Sera lifted her brother's vessel with both hands and held it against her chest for a long moment before she could make herself pack it, and I looked away to give her that privacy, and that was when I saw it.

Far aisle. Top shelf. Set apart from the others, in a niche of its own, the way you'd display something precious rather than store it.

It burned gold.

Not white, not blue, not the guttering grey of the oldest flames. Gold—deep, warm, molten gold, the color of the ember-ink on the map, the color of forge-light, and it was larger than the others, and it was *moving* more than the others, turning restlessly, pressing at its glass in slow, patient cycles, like something that had been knocking on the same door for twenty years and had not yet given up.

And it was humming.

The vault hummed with a thousand voices, but this one I could pick out, the way you can pick out one instrument in an orchestra, one voice in a crowd, and the reason I could pick it out was that it was singing something. A melody. Simple, four notes falling and rising, four notes cycling over and over with the patience of tide.

My hands stopped working.

I knew that song.

I don't mean I recognized it. Recognition is a thing you do with your head. This was lower than that, older, in the place where you keep how to swallow and how to breathe. I knew the melody the way I knew my own heartbeat, and I knew—arriving all at once, terrible and total, like a floor giving way—that I had known it before I knew anything else. Before words. Before Corvin. Before the tollhouse and the routes and the guild's long silence.

Someone had sung that song into me so early and so often that it had become part of the architecture.

"Sera," I said. Or tried to. What came out was barely air.

She looked up. Followed my stare. Saw the gold flame, and saw my face, and whatever she saw there made her set down the vessel she was holding very carefully and come to me.

"Edmund. What is it? What do you—"

"I know it." My voice didn't sound like mine. "That song. I know it. I've never heard it in my life and I know every note of it, and I don't—" My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to a boy standing in a vault full of stolen souls, which was accurate. "Nobody sings to an apprentice, Sera. Nobody sang to me. Corvin didn't—there was no one—"

The gold flame pressed against its glass.

Toward me.

The way Tam's soul had pressed toward Sera's hand. The way a sleeper turns toward a familiar footstep. The way—

"No," I said.

"Edmund—"

"*No.*" Because if I named it, it became real, and if it became real then twenty years of Corvin's silences rearranged themselves into something I couldn't survive knowing, and the struck-through name in the smugglers' cathedral flared up in my memory—*VILLALOBOS, M.*, scored through with a single deliberate line—and the date beneath it, the date I had told myself meant nothing, the date that was—

The date that was three days after my birth.

The gold flame hummed its four falling notes, patient, hopeful, twenty years patient, and the sound went through the glass and through my ribs and found the place where the song had always lived, and it *fit*. It fit like a key. It fit like a hand into a hand-shaped hollow.

Sera took my arm. Her grip was iron and her voice was gentle, and she was the only reason I stayed upright.

"Edmund," she said softly. "Whose song is that?"

And from the top shelf, in a voice made of light and glass and twenty years of waiting, the gold flame hummed the next verse—the part of the lullaby that had words, the part I had never consciously learned, the words rising in my throat unbidden, in a language I shouldn't have known, in a voice that was not entirely mine:

*Sleep, my sparrow, the road is long,*
*but I will walk it beside you—*

The vessel flared so bright I had to shield my eyes.

And somewhere far above us, through miles of warm stone, the vault's opened door began to ring with footsteps.

Many. Descending fast.

* * *

*Edmund*

I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that I did not faint. My knees had opinions, and my vision went grey at the edges, and Sera's hand on my collar was the only argument that prevailed—but I did not faint. Villalobos men, apparently, fall apart standing up.

"My mother," I said. The words came out of me like splinters. "Sera. That's—"

"I know." She was already moving, snatching up satchels, her face pale and set. "I know, Edmund. And I also know that sound."

The footsteps. Dozens of them, ringing down the stair beyond the door, accompanied by the dry whisper of ash settling and resettling—the sound the enforcers made when they moved, the sound I'd hear in my sleep for the rest of my life if I was granted one.

"We can't carry them all," I said. The vault stretched away into darkness, hundreds of vessels, thousands, every shelf a debt, every flame a name. "Sera, we can't—"

"No. But we can carry some, and we can leave the door open, and the map is still in the lock." She shoved a satchel against my chest. My arms closed around it on pure animal reflex. "Take her. Take your mother and go. I have Tam. We run now and we grieve later, in that order, and if you freeze on me, Edmund Villalobos, I will drag you out of here by the ear, because I did not cross half of Thornfell and marry-adjacent myself to a magistrate's son for nothing—"

The gold vessel came off its shelf into my hands, and the world narrowed to warmth and weight and a hum that harmonized with my own pulse, and every plan I had, every clever smuggler's route out of this place, emptied clean out of my head.

Because the footsteps stopped.

All of them. At once. At the threshold.

And a voice that was not Veyra's, not any enforcer's, a voice like cooled lava grinding against cooled lava, spoke into the vault with the calm of a landlord greeting trespassers:

"Little Villalobos. At last. Your mother and I have *so* much to discuss."
