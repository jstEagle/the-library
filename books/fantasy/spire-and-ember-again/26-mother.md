## 26. Mother

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside glass.

I had spent my whole life around quiet — the tollhouse quiet of snow on a roof, the trail-quiet of a man alone who has learned not to talk to himself because the echo answers back too honestly. I thought I knew every flavor of it. I was wrong. The vault's silence was the silence of a held breath, and it had been holding for twenty-six years.

The shelf in front of me held perhaps forty vessels: squat jars of smoked glass, each one stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each one holding something that moved. Not light exactly. Light doesn't *want* anything. These flickered the way a candle flickers when someone walks past it — leaning, reaching, wanting out.

And one of them was burning gold.

Not amber, not honey, not any of the polite colors a jeweler would charge you extra for. Gold like the last hour of a summer evening. Gold like the crust of good bread. It sat third from the left, modest as a widow at a wedding, and it was humming.

A lullaby.

I knew the tune before I knew that I knew it, which is the only way I have ever known anything about my mother. The melody came up through my chest rather than my ears, the way a name comes up when someone calls it across a valley. Four notes down, three notes up, a little hitch at the end like a stumble caught and turned into a dance step. I had hummed it my entire life without knowing where I'd gotten it, the way you inherit a scar or a temper or a way of standing with your weight on your heels. I'd assumed it was mine. Some private invention of a lonely boy in an empty tollhouse.

It wasn't mine. It was hers. And she was singing it to me through four inches of smoked glass while I stood there holding a stolen map and a stolen future and absolutely nothing resembling a plan.

"Edmund." Sera's voice, low, from the aisle behind me. "We have minutes. Maybe less. Tell me you've found something."

I didn't answer. There are moments when answering would require a functioning throat, and this was one of them.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about my mother before tonight, so you can weigh it properly against what I know now.

I knew her name was not spoken in the tollhouse. That's it. That's the complete inventory. Corvin raised me from infancy — fed me, taught me the paths, taught me the ledger-marks, taught me how to read weather in the way pines lean and how to lie to a toll-inspector with a straight face and a friendly cough. He never once, in twenty-six years, said the word *mother* in my hearing. When I asked — and I asked, believe me, I asked with the tireless cruelty only children possess — he would go still in a way that frightened me more than shouting ever did, and say, "You have the guild, boy. The guild is enough."

The guild is enough. What a thing to tell a child. What a thing to *believe*, which he plainly did, right up until the night he ordered me to burn a map and I watched his hands shake.

So I built her out of scraps, the way smugglers build a bridge out of rope and hope. A woman who sang. A woman whose absence had a shape, because Corvin's silences always bent around her like water bends around a stone. A woman who must have died, or left, or been taken — I tried all three stories on over the years, wore each one until it fit badly, and settled finally on *died in childbirth*, because it required nothing of anyone. No villain. No choice. Just the mountain being the mountain, indifferent and old.

I want you to hold that story in mind — the gentle one, the convenient one — because I held it right up until the moment the gold jar hummed, and then I set it down, and it broke, and I have never been able to pick it up again.

* * *

"Edmund." Sera's hand on my shoulder now. She'd crossed the aisle without my hearing her, which should have terrified me — Sera Amadine moves like a debt collector, all purpose and no apology — but terror was busy elsewhere. "What is it? What are you looking at?"

"That one," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "Third from the left."

She leaned past me. I watched her face do the arithmetic — the gold color, the way my hand had risen without permission and was hovering an inch from the glass, the lullaby, which she could hear too, faintly, the way you can hear a neighbor's fire through a wall.

"Edmund," she said carefully, "whose song is that?"

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and then, because lying to Sera had stopped working somewhere around the scree slope and neither of us had remarked on it since: "I know it. I've always known it. I thought I made it up."

Her breath went out slow. In the ember-light of the vault her eyes looked like coins at the bottom of a well. "The struck-through name," she said. "In the cathedral. The chapel with the carved prayers."

"Maren Villalobos." I'd found it two nights ago, or a lifetime ago, depending on how you measure such things — my own family name cut into smuggler's stone among two hundred years of prayers, and a single line struck through it, clean and deliberate, the way a merchant strikes a debt paid. I'd told myself it meant death. Death was the merciful reading. "The date under it. Sera, the date under it was the year I was born."

"Yes."

"And the ledger." My voice was doing something strange, climbing registers like a boy's. I hated it and couldn't stop it. "Show me the page again. The V page. You showed me the guild names, the ones they collected, but show me—"

She already had the ledger out. Of course she did; Sera travels with that book the way other people travel with a knife, closer than a knife, actually, because a knife only threatens your body. She turned to the page without looking for it. She'd known. I understood, in some distant, cold corner of myself, that she had suspected since the chapel, maybe since the bridge, maybe since the first night when I'd lied to her about my name and she'd let me, and she had said nothing, because what do you say? *Excuse me, stranger, I believe the soul in that jar might be your mother*? There is no graceful way to open that conversation.

There it was, in the Court's precise, terrible script:

*Maren Villalobos. Collateral. Year of the Long Frost. Tendered by G. Corvin, Guildmaster, against final covenant balance. Term: perpetual.*

Perpetual. Not twenty-six years. Not until the debt came due at solstice. Perpetual, the way a stone is perpetually at the bottom of a lake.

"Tendered by G. Corvin," I read aloud, because reading it aloud made it worse and I needed it worse, needed the full weight of it, needed to feel every pound of what he had done so that I would never again be able to make it small. "He signed it. He signed it the year I was born. He didn't lose her, Sera. He didn't fail to save her. He *sold* her."

"Edmund—"

"He raised me." The words were coming out of me like splinters coming out of a wound, each one taking a little more flesh with it. "He raised me on her money. On her *soul*. Every meal, every lesson, every winter he kept the tollhouse warm — he paid for it with her, and then he watched me ask about her, year after year, and he said *the guild is enough*, and I believed him, I *thanked* him, I—" 

My hand had closed into a fist without consulting me, and it was aimed at the shelf, at the glass, at everything, and Sera caught it. Both hands, firm, ungentle, the grip of a woman who had learned to stop people from hurting themselves in the Court's service and had gotten very good at it.

"Don't," she said. "Not here. Nothing in this room responds well to rage. Look around you, Edmund. Look at what they do with rage."

I looked. The jars flickered. All of them, down both aisles, dozens upon dozens, leaning toward the sound of my voice like flowers toward a window. Souls don't sleep. Nobody had ever told me that, and now nobody would ever need to. They had been listening the whole time — to our footsteps, our whispers, our careful thief's breathing. Listening for years. For decades. For *perpetual*.

And one of them was singing my lullaby.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody warns you about grief: it doesn't arrive alone. It arrives the way ash-enforcers arrive, in company, and its companions are worse than it is. Rage came first, hot and stupid, wanting to smash things — the jars, the shelves, the whole cathedral of cooled lava, Corvin's face if I ever saw it again, my own hands for every year I'd spent not asking harder questions. Then shame, sliding in under the rage like water under a door: shame that I had eaten and slept and grown tall on the interest of her imprisonment; shame that I had stopped asking; shame that some small, practical part of me was even now noting the exit routes and the guard rotations because the smuggler in me does not stop working, not even kneeling at his mother's grave, and I despised that part and needed it desperately.

And underneath all of it, patient as bedrock, love. That was the cruelest companion of the three. Because I loved her. Twenty-six years of nothing — no face, no voice, no grave to visit, not even a lie to comfort myself with — and I loved her anyway, the way a root loves water it has never seen, blindly, by necessity, growing toward it in the dark. All those years I'd told myself the ache in the tollhouse was loneliness, ordinary loneliness, the kind any last apprentice of a dying guild might carry. It wasn't. It was her. It had always been her, humming through the walls of my life, four notes down, three notes up, the hitch at the end.

"Talk to her," Sera said quietly.

"What?"

"She can hear you. They can all hear you." She glanced down the aisle, at the leaning lights, and something moved across her face that I'd learn later was her brother — every jar in this room was somebody's somebody, and Sera Amadine carried that arithmetic like a stone in her boot. "Twenty-six years, Edmund. Don't spend another minute of them on Corvin. He's had enough of your time."

She was right, which was infuriating, which was typical. So I knelt — knees on cold stone, in a stolen coat, in the deepest room beneath a mountain that had been eating my family for generations — and I put my palm flat against the glass, and I said the only thing I had.

"Hello, Mum."

The gold flared. Not much. A candle catching a draft. But the lullaby changed — the same four notes down, three notes up, but slower now, softer, the way you sing when the child has finally stopped crying and you're trying to keep it that way.

And I came apart.

I'm not going to dress it up. I am, by trade and temperament, a man who keeps his face closed — Corvin trained that into me before I could write, the tollkeeper's mask, pleasant and blank and revealing nothing. The mask held for perhaps four seconds. Then I was kneeling on the floor of the Ember Court's soul-vault with my forehead against the glass that held my mother, sobbing like something newly born, making sounds I had never made in my life and did not recognize as coming from me, and the only reason I didn't care who heard was that everyone who might have heard was either dead, traitorous, or made of ash.

Sera knelt beside me. She didn't touch me again. She just stayed, which was its own kind of touch, and after a while I noticed she was crying too, silently, in the efficient manner of a woman who has had practice, and I understood that she was looking down the aisle at the other jars, at all the mothers and brothers and daughters in their smoked-glass cells, and doing sums she'd been doing since she stole that ledger out of a gilded cage.

"We're getting them out," she said. Not a comfort. A report. "All of them. Starting with her."

"How?" It came out wrecked. I didn't care anymore. "The map opens doors, Sera, it doesn't unmake contracts. The Magistrate said—" 

"The Magistrate offered you a trade." Her voice went hard and bright, blade-bright. "Your memory of her for her freedom. And you refused, and you were right to refuse, because that's how they work — every door they open is a door they own. So we don't use their doors." She tapped the ledger. "This is a record. Records are claims. And somewhere in this vault there's a mechanism older than the Magistrate's paperwork, because nobody builds a prison without also building the thing they're most afraid of: a way out. We find it. Tonight. Before the debt comes due and they move her deeper, or spend her, or—"

She stopped herself. I watched her decide not to say whatever came next, and I loved her a little for it, and I hated the Court a great deal more for putting that restraint in her.

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there. Time in the vault ran sideways, measured in flickers instead of minutes. At some point I stopped crying, mostly because I ran out of the immediate supply, and I began to talk — really talk — the one-sided conversation of a lifetime arriving all at once.

"You'd hate the tollhouse," I told her. "It leaks. Corvin says it's character. He says that about everything that's broken, which is how he avoids fixing it." A wet laugh escaped me, absurd in that place. "I keep the ledgers now. Routes, tolls, the old marks. Nobody comes through anymore. I catalogued a road last month that hasn't seen traffic since before I was born, and I wrote it up like it mattered, because it was the only thing I had that felt like mine."

The gold pulsed gently. Listening. The way she must have listened to Corvin's lies, I thought, and the rage stirred again, and I set it down deliberately, the way you set down a hot iron. Later. There would be time for rage later, or there wouldn't, but either way it would keep, and this — this window, this impossible half-hour of a mother hearing her son's voice for the first time in twenty-six years — this would not.

"Sera thinks there's a way to break the vaults. She's usually right, which is annoying, so plan on it." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "I'm going to get you out of here. I don't know how yet. I've been improvising since a dying man fell over my doorstep, and honestly the improvising hasn't gone badly, all things considered, so — stay gold. Stay loud. Sing as loud as the glass lets you, and I'll find you. Even if they move you. Even if they bury you at the bottom of this mountain. I grew up on these paths, Mum. Nobody knows Thornfell like I do. Not the Court. Not Corvin. Nobody."

The lullaby rose — four notes down, three notes up, the hitch — and then it did something new. It changed key. Lifted. And though I had no words for the tune, no memory of lyrics, my mouth shaped sounds around it anyway, half-remembered syllables surfacing from somewhere below thought, and for one vertiginous moment I was an infant again, held against a heartbeat, learning a language made entirely of warmth—

Every jar in the vault flared at once.

Down both aisles, dozens of imprisoned lights surging toward the sound of a lullaby sung in a mother tongue none of them had heard in years, decades, forever — and the vault's silence broke, replaced by a sound like wind through a forest of tiny chimes, and it was beautiful, and it was the worst sound I have ever heard, because beauty carries.

Footsteps. Above us, beyond the vault door, through the galleries of cooled lava — boots on basalt, many boots, moving with the unhurried precision of a household that has heard something break.

Sera was on her feet, ledger away, knife out, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. "They heard it," she breathed. "Of course they heard it. The whole mountain probably heard it."

The gold jar flared hard, urgent now, the lullaby fracturing into something faster — a warning, a push, *go, go* — and I realized with a jolt that she was telling me to run. Twenty-six years in a bottle and her first act of motherhood available to her was *run, child*.

"Not without you," I whispered, and wrapped both hands around the jar.

It was warm. Warm as a loaf, warm as a hand, and through the smoked glass I felt — or imagined I felt, and decided I didn't care which — a pulse answer my grip.

Boots, closer. A voice, dry as paper, giving orders in a language that predated the lowlands. Somewhere above, an alarm began — a deep, tectonic note, the sound of the Ember Court clearing its throat.

Sera looked at me, at the jar, at the door, and did the fastest arithmetic of her life. "Tell me you have a way out of here."

I grinned at her through the ruin of my face, and it surprised us both. "Sera. I'm a Wayfarer. I *always* have a way out."

Which was true, in the sense that I had a map drawn in living fire in my coat, a road no chart admitted existed, and absolutely no idea whether it ran toward daylight or straight into the Magistrate's open hand.

The vault door began to open.
