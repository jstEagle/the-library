## 27. Alarm in the Court

I have been in exactly two kinds of trouble in my life: the kind you can talk your way out of, and the kind that involves bells.

The Ember Court, it turned out, had bells. Of course it did. A cathedral of cooled lava and masked aristocrats trading in other people's memories was never going to settle for a polite cough when something went wrong. When Edmund's hand came away from the vault's glass shelf with his mother's soul cradled against his chest like a stolen egg, every bell in the place began to ring at once—a sound like a thousand knives being sharpened underwater—and I understood, with the particular clarity of someone who has made this mistake before, that we were now firmly in bell territory.

"Edmund." I grabbed his sleeve. "Run first. Feel guilty later. That's the order of operations."

He wasn't listening. He was looking at the soul in his hands—the gold one, the one that had hummed a lullaby at him from behind glass—and his face was doing something I recognized from mirrors, back when I still had reasons to look in them. It was the face of a person meeting a ghost they'd been promised didn't exist.

"Edmund." Harder this time. "The lullaby will keep. The enforcers won't."

That got through. Whatever else he was—naive, stubborn, raised by a man who thought silence was a form of love—he was a smuggler's apprentice, and smugglers' apprentices know what a closing door sounds like.

We ran.

* * *

Let me tell you what running through the Ember Court is like, since apparently I'm the one keeping notes on our disasters.

The Court is built inside the throat of an old volcano, all galleries of black stone veined with ember-light, and the architecture has opinions about visitors. Corridors that had stretched politely wide for the tribute-bearers an hour ago now narrowed like a fist closing. The masks in the galleries turned to follow us—not the people wearing them, mind you, just the masks, which is somehow worse. Somewhere above us, a voice like cooled honey announced, in tones of mild administrative disappointment:

*"A debt has been taken without payment. The Court reminds all guests that memory is currency, and theft of currency is theft of self."*

"They rehearse that," I panted, taking a corner hard enough to bruise my shoulder on basalt. "You can hear the rehearsal. Nobody improvises 'theft of self.'"

"How are you joking right now?" Edmund gasped.

"Because if I stop joking I'll start screaming, and screaming echoes in here."

It did echo. Everything echoed. And worse than the echo was the ash.

They came out of the walls—or rather, they came out of the seams between the walls, because the Ash-Bound don't use doors, doors being a courtesy extended to people who exist properly. They poured into the gallery ahead of us like smoke deciding to have shoulders. Six of them. Then eight. Their faces were smooth where faces should be busy, and their eyes were the color of a fire that has been told to wait.

Edmund skidded to a halt so fast I nearly went through him.

"The map," he said. "Sera, the map—it got us in, maybe it gets us out—"

"It got us in by lying to a gate," I said. "Gates hold grudges."

But he already had it out, the ember-ink crawling across the vellum like veins of light under skin, and I'll give him this: he read roads the way I read ledgers, fast and greedy. His finger stopped on a passage branching off the gallery we'd come through.

"There. The old smelter road. It's not marked as closed."

"Nothing in that court is marked as closed. Things are marked as *forgotten*, which is the same thing with better manners."

"Then we go somewhere forgotten."

Which, in hindsight, was either the smartest thing anyone said all night or the reason we're both still alive. With the Ember Court, those are usually the same sentence.

We went left, through a service archway that smelled of cold iron and older grief, and the ash poured after us.

* * *

Here's the thing about being hunted by the Ash-Bound that nobody tells you, mostly because everyone who could tell you is either dead or an Ash-Bound: they're not fast. They're inevitable. There's a difference, and the difference is that inevitability doesn't tire, doesn't hesitate, doesn't make mistakes, and never, ever loses interest. You can outrun a man. You cannot outrun Tuesday.

We ran anyway. Edmund clutched the soul against his chest and it burned faintly gold through his fingers, and every time an enforcer got close, the light flared and they flinched—half a heartbeat, no more, but half a heartbeat is a fortune when you're spending them the way we were.

"Why do they do that?" he shouted over his shoulder.

"I don't know! Maybe souls are hot!"

"That's not—" He ducked a grasping hand of drifting cinders. "Veyra flinched too. In the vault. Before we even touched anything. She looked at the shelf and she *flinched*, Sera."

I hadn't seen it. I'd been watching the exits, because that's my job in this partnership—he finds the hope, I find the doors. But I filed it away, the way you file everything in the Court, because in a place that eats memory, noticing things is the only wealth that can't be taxed.

The smelter road opened ahead of us: a great sloping tunnel cut into the mountain's root, its walls furred with dead slag, its floor scarred by cart-rails gone soft with rust. Cold air moved through it from somewhere below, smelling of snow and pine, and I have never in my life been so grateful for the smell of pine, which previously I would have described as "aggressive."

"Down," Edmund said.

"Down is where the mountain keeps its teeth."

"Down is *out*. Can you feel that air? That's outside air. That's air that hasn't been owned by anyone."

So we went down, and the ash came down behind us, and the bells kept ringing their underwater knife-sharpening song, and I remember thinking, with the strange bookkeeping clarity that arrives in the worst moments: *we are going to die in a tunnel, and the last thing I will have said to anyone was a joke about rehearsal.*

Then Veyra stepped out of the dark, and everything changed.

* * *

I should explain who Veyra is, or was, to me, before this moment.

She was the one who interrogated the innkeeper at Thornfell village, back when I was nothing but a runaway in a borrowed coat watching terror through a window. She'd stood in that common room like a column of smoke given posture, and she'd asked her questions in a voice with no weather in it at all, and when the innkeeper couldn't answer—because he genuinely couldn't, the Court had already collected whatever he might have known—she'd waited, patient as frost, and then moved on. I'd hated her with the clean, simple hatred you're allowed to feel for monsters.

Monsters, it turns out, are just people with the interesting parts removed. I knew that by then. Sera Amadine, expert on having your interesting parts removed by institutions.

She stood in the smelter road with three of her kind behind her, and the tunnel was narrow enough that there was no around her, only through, and Edmund put himself between her and the soul in his arms like a boy defending a campfire with his body.

"Give it back," Veyra said. Her voice was still weatherless. Still patient. But her head tilted, just slightly, toward the gold light leaking through Edmund's fingers, and I saw it again: the flinch. The half-heartbeat of something that was not obedience.

"We can't," Edmund said. "It's my mother."

And here is what I will believe until I die, whatever else the Court took from the world: that sentence did something to her.

Not visibly. Not dramatically. If you'd blinked you'd have missed it entirely. But her smooth face moved—microscopically, the way ice moves on a pond before it breaks—and behind her eyes, in whatever hollow room the Court had left of her, a draft came through.

"A mother," she repeated. The word sat in her mouth like a coin she'd found in an old coat pocket. Familiar shape. Unknown value.

"The debt is owed," said one of the enforcers behind her. Its voice was wind through a burnt field. "The Court requires—"

"I heard the requirement," Veyra said, and did not turn around.

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. I counted, because counting is what I do instead of praying. In those four seconds I watched a creature designed to be an instrument stand very still and try to remember whether instruments can choose, and I thought about my brother, whose name sits in a ledger beneath this mountain, filed like a debt, and I thought: *this is what waking up looks like. It looks like standing in a tunnel, failing to move.*

"You were in the vault," Veyra said to Edmund. "You sang."

"I—what? No. I didn't sing."

"The soul sang. You knew it. Your mouth moved." Her head tilted further. "I knew the song too. I don't know how I knew the song. I don't know how I know anything. That is—" and here her voice did something extraordinary, something I suspect hadn't happened in that tunnel in a hundred years— "that is *wrong*. Isn't it? Knowing without knowing. It's wrong."

"Yes," Edmund said softly, the way you speak to a spooked horse. "It's wrong. They took it from you. All of it. And they left you with the shape of it, and the shape hurts, doesn't it?"

"Don't," Veyra said.

But she said it the way people say *don't* when they mean *yes, keep going, but slowly.*

Behind her, the other enforcers had gone still in a different way—the stillness of machinery awaiting instruction. One of them drifted forward. "The debt. The Court requires—"

"*I heard it.*" Sharper now. Weather coming into her voice at last, and the weather was a storm. "I have heard it for sixty years. I have heard nothing *else* for sixty years." She raised one ash-grey hand, and the enforcers behind her stopped as if she'd struck them. Whether that was authority or kinship, I couldn't say. Possibly in the Ash-Bound there's no difference.

Then she looked at me. At me specifically, and I want it recorded that being looked at by an ash-enforcer with something almost like recognition is an experience I would not recommend at any price.

"You," she said. "The noblewoman. You watch doors. You count."

"I... do, yes."

"You counted my hesitation. Just now. Four seconds." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. On her ruined face it was nearly a smile, and it was the most terrible and wonderful thing I saw that whole night. "No one counts us. We are not countable. We are *spent*." She stepped aside. Out of the road. Out of the way. "The old smelter tunnel runs down past the cooling sumps. The Court forgot it when it forgot the forges. Forgotten things are blind spots. Go."

Edmund stared at her. "Why?"

And Veyra—Veyra, who had stood in an innkeeper's common room asking questions with no weather in her voice—looked down at her own grey hands as though consulting them for the answer.

"Because I half-remember a song," she said, "and I am tired of only having half of anything." Her eyes came up, and they were burning now, actually burning, low orange coals in that smooth face. "Go quickly. When the others arrive, I will report that the thieves fled upward, toward the galleries. It will be a lie. I don't—" and here the wonder cracked her voice open like river ice— "I don't think I have ever lied before. It feels *enormous.*"

"It feels that way the first time," I said. "It gets smaller. That's not necessarily good."

For one instant—one impossible, unrepeatable instant—Veyra laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusted and unused, like a gate opening in a house where everyone had died. But it was a laugh.

"Go," she said. "Before I remember how to obey."

* * *

We went.

The smelter tunnel swallowed us, and the bells faded above, and the cold clean air rose up to meet us like a promise finally kept. For a long time neither of us spoke. We climbed down through the dark past the bones of forges, past cooling sumps glazed with ancient slag, and the gold light of the soul lit Edmund from below like a man carrying dawn in his arms.

Finally, when the rails under our feet began to slope toward genuine daylight, he said, quietly: "She lied for us. She's never lied. What happens to her when they find out?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty even if the truth was ugly.

"Probably the same thing that happens to everyone in that court who becomes inconvenient," I said. "They'll collect what's left of her. File it. Ring the bells." I watched his face fall and decided—against all my training, against every survival instinct my family ever beat into me—to add the rest. "Unless we come back. Unless we break the whole rotten ledger open and get her the rest of her song back. That's the other option. It's a worse plan. It's also the only one I like."

Edmund looked at me then, and the soul-light caught his eyes, and he nodded once, and I understood that I had just volunteered myself for a war I'd spent my whole life running from. My brother would have laughed at me. My brother, whose name I carried folded in my coat next to a stolen ledger, filed under debts in a court of ash.

"Almost there," Edmund said, pointing to a grey thread of daylight far below.

And that was when the mountain shuddered—once, deep, like something enormous turning over in its sleep—and from far above us, muffled by miles of stone, the bells changed their tune. No longer alarm. Something slower. Something deliberate.

Something that sounded, God help me, like a countdown.

"They know," Edmund whispered.

"No," I said, starting down toward the light at a run. "They *suspect*. There's a difference, and we're about to spend the rest of the night making sure everyone in that court learns it."

Below us, the daylight widened. Above us, the counting continued.
