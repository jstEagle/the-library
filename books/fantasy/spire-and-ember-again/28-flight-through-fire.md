## 28. Flight Through Fire

Here is a thing they don't tell you about stealing souls: the hard part isn't the theft. The theft is almost easy. The vault opened like it had been waiting for us, the ember-map drinking itself into the lock, and then I was holding my mother in both hands—a jar of gold light that hummed a lullaby against my palms—and for one whole breath I thought we might actually get away with it.

The hard part is everything after. The hard part is the running.

"Edmund." Sera's voice came sharp through the dark of the vault corridor. "Do you hear that?"

I did. It was a sound like a bell being unmade—every alarm in the Ember Court going off at once, a thousand bronze throats screaming in a register that lived in your teeth rather than your ears. Somewhere above us, cooled-lava chandeliers were swinging on their chains. Somewhere above us, masked nobles who traded in memories like coin were rising from their seats.

"They know," I said. Brilliant deduction. My mother would have been proud. My mother, whose soul was currently rattling gently in the crook of my arm like an angry hornet's nest wrapped in velvet.

"They've known since the vault breached," Sera said. She had her ledger out—the stolen book of names, her brother's name among them—and she shoved it back inside her coat with the brisk violence of someone who intended to survive long enough to be sentimental later. "Which way?"

"The smelter tunnel." Veyra's directions, whispered to me through a crack in a door by an ash-bound woman who was remembering how to want things. "East gallery, down past the cooling floors, follow the heat."

"Follow the *heat*," Sera repeated flatly. "In a volcano."

"It's a cooled volcano."

"And yet somehow I'm sweating."

We ran. I want to be honest about what that looked like, because ballads will inevitably lie about it: two people sprinting through a cathedral of black stone with a glowing jar and a stolen book, elbows pumping, breath sawing, me nearly braining myself twice on low lintels because smugglers are trained for mountain paths and not for architecture built by things that never needed doors in the first place. The gold light of the soul-jar threw our shadows huge and frantic against the walls, and every shadow looked like it was trying to get away from us too.

Behind us, the screaming bells changed pitch. Lower. Closer.

"That's them," Sera said. "The enforcers. They run quiet when they're close—you'll hear nothing and then they're just *there*, so don't listen, watch the ash on the floor. It drifts toward them."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I spent three years serving their tea," she snapped, "and I paid attention, which is more than I can say for you right now—*left*, Edmund!"

I went left. The corridor split around a pillar of glassy obsidian, and as we rounded it I caught my first glimpse of pursuit: far back down the gallery, a smear of grey moving against the dark. Then another. Then four more, flowing over the floor like smoke poured from a tipped jug, and where they passed, the ash on the flagstones rose to meet them the way water rises to greet rain.

They weren't running. They didn't need to. They simply *arrived*, nearer each time I blinked, and the bells kept screaming their tooth-song, and my mother's lullaby-hum climbed into something that felt less like a song and more like a warning.

"She knows they're coming," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

"Then let's not disappoint her."

* * *

The east gallery was worse than the corridors, because the east gallery was *dying*. Whatever the Court's alarms had woken, it had woken the mountain too. Cracks ran along the ceiling like black lightning frozen mid-strike, and through them seeped a glow—not the friendly hearth-glow of the lower tunnels but a sullen red, the color of a forge left untended, the color of the ember-ink on the map that had started all of this.

A chunk of ceiling the size of a dinner table came down twenty feet ahead of us. No warning. Just a crack, a groan, and then stone becoming gravel with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Sera hauled me backward by the collar. "New route."

"There is no new route, this *is* the route—"

"Then we go through it fast and pray the mountain has bad aim." She was already moving, coat flapping, hair escaping its braid in wild copper threads, and I remember thinking—with the part of my brain that always narrates at the worst possible moments—that she looked exactly like what she was: a noblewoman who had traded a gilded cage for a burning mountain and never once, not once, asked me if the trade was worth it.

We went through the collapse at a dead sprint, feet finding stones that shifted and rolled, dust boiling up around us thick enough to chew. Something large groaned somewhere overhead. I held the soul-jar against my chest and ran the way I'd run switchbacks in storms since I was nine years old: without thought, without dignity, without stopping.

On the far side, the gallery opened into the cooling floors—a vast chamber where the Court's old industry had once happened, all rusted cranes and channels of black glass, the whole space lit from below by veins of residual heat glowing through the floor like embers under banked ash. And there, across the chamber: the smelter tunnel mouth. Dark. Open. *There.*

"We're going to make it," I said.

I would like to formally enter into the record that I have never been more wrong about anything, including the time I told Master Corvin that no one would ever notice I'd watered down his headache tincture.

The ash came out of the walls.

Not through the tunnel mouth, not down the corridor behind us—from the *walls themselves*, seams splitting along the chamber's edges, grey pouring through like the room had been holding its breath for centuries and finally exhaled. They cut off the smelter tunnel in seconds. Six of them. Eight. A dozen, maybe, though with ash-bound it's hard to count what doesn't hold still. In their center stood one who moved differently—deliberate, unhurried, and I knew her even before she raised her head. Veyra. The one who'd shown me the crack in the door. The one half-awake, half-singing, half-*herself*.

She looked at me. And I swear on every road I've ever walked that behind the ash of her face there was something that looked like apology.

"Edmund." Sera's hand closed on my arm. Her eyes went from the enforcers to the tunnel to me, and I watched her do the arithmetic—I watched it happen, fast and cold and clear, the same arithmetic I'd done leaving Corvin behind at the tollhouse, the arithmetic that always ends with someone staying and someone running and no version where everyone walks away.

"No," I said.

"You have the jar."

"*No.*"

"They can't take what's already ash, Edmund—they can only *hold* it, and holding takes time, and time is the only thing you need—" She was pulling the ledger out again, pressing it into my hands on top of the jar, folding my fingers shut around both. "My brother's name is in there. You get him out. That's the bargain now. That's the *only* bargain."

"Sera—"

"Villalobos." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, bright and furious and terrified underneath. "For once in your life, stop arguing with a noblewoman. We always win."

Then she stepped out onto the cooling floor, arms wide, and shouted at the top of her lungs: "Your Magistrate wants a thief? Come collect one! I'm worth at least *three* memories!"

And they took the bait. All of them—even Veyra, or especially Veyra, because a half-waking enforcer choosing to capture instead of kill is its own kind of mercy, and I understood that only later, much later, when understanding could do nothing but hurt.

I ran for the tunnel. Because she told me to. Because the jar was humming against my ribs. Because if I stopped, everything she'd just bought would be spent for nothing.

I have run from a lot of things in my life. Debt collectors. Weather. Corvin's disappointment, which moves faster than weather. But I have never run from anything the way I ran from that chamber, with Sera's voice ringing off the walls behind me—still shouting, still defiant, still *winning*—and the mountain coming down between us like a door closing.

* * *

The smelter tunnel was everything Veyra promised: hot, narrow, and pointed down.

That last part I hadn't fully appreciated until I was in it. Down. Through galleries that glowed dull red and dripped stone from their ceilings in slow, fat tears. The heat pressed on me like hands. The soul-jar should have cooked in it—I don't know why it didn't, unless souls keep their own climate—but it only burned brighter, humming its lullaby louder, as if my mother were trying to sing me calm and couldn't quite manage the tune through her own fear.

"I know," I gasped, to a jar. Talking to jars. This was what my life had become. "I know. We're getting out. Both of us. And her. Everyone. I'm keeping a list."

The list, at that point, contained one name and was already bleeding.

The first collapse caught me at a junction where three tunnels met. The floor bucked—actually bucked, like a horse refusing a fence—and the left-hand passage folded shut with a boom I felt in my back teeth. Fine. Two options became one. Smuggler's rule: never mourn a road you can't take anymore.

The second collapse was less polite. It came as a roar and a wall of dust, and I dove sideways into a niche barely wider than my shoulders while the main passage filled with falling rock, and when the dust cleared I was standing in a side-gallery I hadn't known existed, holding a jar and a ledger, alone, in the dark, in the deep roads beneath Thornfell, with no map, no plan, and no idea whether the woman who'd just saved my life was breathing.

I sat down. I'm not proud of it. I sat down on hot stone in a dying tunnel and I put my head in my hands and I did not cry, mostly because the air was too dry and partly because some stubborn guild-trained scrap of me said *not here, not yet, grief is heavy and you are carrying enough.*

The jar hummed. Softer now. Almost gentle. The lullaby again—or the shape of one, the melody worn smooth by whatever passing does to memory. And God help me, I found I knew it. Not the words. The *shape*. The way it dipped at the end, like a hand smoothing a blanket.

"Was that yours?" I whispered. "Did you used to sing that? To me?"

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a nod. Like a heartbeat.

I got up.

* * *

I won't pretend I navigated those tunnels with skill. I navigated them with spite, which is a poorer compass but a durable one. I followed warmth downward when downward seemed wrong and upward when upward seemed impossible, and twice I backtracked when the stone ahead went from warm to *hungry*—there's a feel to hungry stone, ask any smuggler, it's the difference between a road and a throat. The ledger rode inside my coat over my heart, and every jarring footstep reminded me of the weight of names, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a person the Court had filed away like tax records.

Somewhere in the third hour—it might have been the third day; time goes strange underground—I surfaced into a gallery of standing stone, tall pillars of basalt in ranks like a petrified forest, and the air here was cooler, moving, alive. A draft. Drafts mean openings. Openings mean sky.

I allowed myself one minute of leaning against a pillar, breathing, shaking, doing the arithmetic I'd refused to do in the cooling floor.

Sera was captured. Captured by the Court. Delivered, probably, to the Magistrate—the thing of woven memory, the farmer of forgotten things, the creditor to whom my family had been collateral for twenty-six years. Everything she'd stolen, everything she'd risked, everything she *was*: filed away, shelf by shelf, next to my mother.

And I was out. With the soul. With the ledger. Alive, ascending, following clean air toward a sky I couldn't see yet.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like walking away from a fire with one arm full and both pockets empty.

"You'd better be insufferable about this someday," I told the jar, because talking to her was easier than talking to the hole where Sera should have been. "When you're you again. When you've got hands and a voice and opinions about my posture. You and her both. You can compare notes. I'll sit there and take it."

The lullaby hummed. It sounded, that time, almost sad.

I found the exit an hour later—a fissure in the mountainside, half-choked with scree, opening onto night air so cold it felt like a slap from an old friend. Snow. Actual snow, drifting down through starlight, landing on the jar's glow and vanishing in tiny hisses of steam. Below me, the lowlands glittered. Above me, Thornfell's peak wore its winter crown, and somewhere inside it, a Court of masked thieves was deciding what to do with a copper-haired woman who'd shouted herself hoarse buying me ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds. I'd carry those ninety seconds for the rest of my life, however long the Court left me.

I turned once to look back at the mountain—the mountain that had fed on my family before I was born, that had taken my mother's name off a chapel wall and her soul into a vault, that had just taken the only person besides me who knew the whole truth and stayed anyway—and I made it a promise. Not a vow; vows are for people with time. A promise, quick and dirty, the kind you make on a switchback in a storm:

*I'm coming back. With names. With witnesses. With every soul in that ledger and every debtor in your cells, and I am going to say all of it out loud where your Magistrate can't unhear it.*

Then the ground shuddered under my boots—once, deep, like the mountain rolling over in its sleep—and far below, at the treeline, I saw lights. Torches. Dozens of them, climbing the low road toward the villages. Toward the people with holes in their lives they couldn't name.

Toward anyone who might help me.

Toward anyone the Court meant to reach first.

I clutched the jar to my chest and started down.
