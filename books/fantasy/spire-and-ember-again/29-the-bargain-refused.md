## 29. The Bargain Refused

There is a particular pleasure I take in hospitality, and it is this: a guest who believes she has been captured, when in truth she has been *received*.

Sera Amadine sat in my audience chamber with her wrists bound in cooled glass—painless, I assure you; pain is such a crude seasoning—and she looked at me with the expression I have come to treasure above all others over these long centuries. Not fear. Fear is common. Fear is what the debtors wear when they first understand what they've signed. No, what Sera wore was arithmetic. She was counting exits. She was weighing my words against her brother's name in a ledger she had stolen from me, and she was deciding, even now, how much of what I said to believe.

I do so admire a guest who does her own sums.

"You're wondering," I said, "why I haven't simply taken your memories and been done with it."

"The thought occurred," she said. Her voice was steady. Good breeding will do that for you; it holds the spine straight while everything behind it crumbles. "You seem to be enjoying the conversation."

"I am." I let the chamber breathe around us—the great vaulted dark of the Ember Court, walls of lava that had cooled into black glass a thousand years before anyone thought to build a road through Thornfell, lit from below by veins of ember-light that pulse like something sleeping. "Conversation is one of the few pleasures left to me. You'll forgive an old creditor his indulgence."

"You're not old," she said. "You're not anything. Veyra told me what you are. What you were."

"Veyra." I tasted the name. One of mine, or she had been, once—a debtor emptied down to obedience, and yet some stubborn grain of song had survived in her like a seed in winter soil. It happens. Memory is a weed, madam. You may burn the field and still find it growing through the cracks of your own foundations. "Then perhaps we can dispense with the theater. You know I am not a man. Shall I tell you what I am instead?"

She said nothing, which from a noblewoman is a full sentence.

I rose—or rather, I arranged myself into the shape of rising, because the shape comforts guests and shapes are all I have anymore—and I walked the long gallery of my court. The masked nobles had withdrawn at my gesture; even they prefer not to watch me work. Their masks are porcelain and their memories are rented, every one of them, little lords and ladies who sold their worst years to me and now cannot remember why they weep at certain songs. They think themselves collectors. They are inventory with better tailoring.

"Do you know what a forgotten thing weighs?" I asked her.

"No."

"Neither does anyone else. That is rather the point. A forgotten thing has no weight at all—that is its tragedy and its exquisite utility. When a village forgets a flood, the flood costs them nothing. When a mother forgets her grief, she smiles again by spring. Everyone believes forgetting is loss." I turned back to face her. "Everyone is wrong. Forgetting is *harvest*. Nothing vanishes, Lady Amadine. It only changes hands."

Something moved behind her eyes—not belief, not yet, but the terrible willingness of a clever person to be convinced if the arithmetic holds. I pressed on, gently. One does not rush a vintage.

"Your guildsmen believed they bought safe passage," I said. "A charming story, and mostly true, as the best lies are. The Wayfarers' Guild came to me four hundred years ago, hungry and hunted, with roads no one would grant them and enemies on every side. And I offered them terms. Passage through Thornfell, unmolested, forever—in exchange for payment rendered annually at the pact-stone. They assumed I wanted gold. Gold!" I confess I laughed, and the ember-veins in the walls flickered with my amusement, which unsettled her nicely. "Gold is dead weight. Gold remembers nothing. What I asked for was their surplus. The griefs they wished to set down. The shames they could not carry. The names too painful to speak. Every year, at the solstice, the guild brought me what its members most wished to forget—and I took it, and I kept it, and in return the mountain opened its roads to them like a well-fed dog opening its mouth."

"That's monstrous," she said.

"It's *commerce*," I corrected. "Do not mistake me for a monster, Lady Amadine. Monsters take. I trade. Every soul in my vaults came here through a door someone opened willingly. Your guild did not die out—it was collected, installment by installment, until there was nothing left of it but debts and a dying man who couldn't remember why he was ashamed. Ask yourself: what kind of order forgets its own founding? What kind of guild lets itself dwindle to one apprentice and one liar?" I spread my hands. "They forgot because they paid me to help them forget. I have simply been very patient about calling in the balance."

She was quiet for a long moment. The ember-light moved across her face, and I watched her do what her class does best: reframe catastrophe as inheritance.

"My brother," she said finally. "Tomas. He's in your ledger."

"He is."

"He never made any bargain with you. He was nine."

"And who signed for him?" I asked softly. "Who stood at a contract and gave his name as surety? Debts run through families like water through limestone, my lady. Your father owed me—I believe it was a horse race, or a mistress, or both; the details were surrendered long ago, which is fitting—and when he could not pay, he offered collateral. Tomas's memory of your father. Then Tomas's memory of himself, piece by piece, year by year, until what remained was a boy-shaped absence that everyone agreed was best sent away." I tilted my head. "Did you ever wonder why no one spoke of him? Why his portrait came down? Families are my favorite farms, Lady Amadine. They fertilize themselves."

I will grant her this: she did not cry. Her jaw tightened and her eyes went bright and wet, but she held, and I found myself almost sorry for what I intended next. Almost. Sentiment is a luxury I traded away centuries ago, along with my face, my name, and whatever else I was before the mountain taught me what hunger was. I remember none of it. That is the joke at the center of me, the one no one else has ever been permitted to laugh at: I am made entirely of other people's discarded pasts, and I have no past of my own. I am a cathedral built from lost things, and I am so terribly, endlessly hungry, because nothing I hold is truly mine and therefore nothing I hold fills anything.

"You're going to offer me a bargain," Sera said. "That's why I'm alive. That's why any of us are kept alive. You don't destroy assets."

"Your education proceeds admirably."

"So make your offer. I won't take it, but I'd like to hear how you dress it up."

I smiled with a face I had borrowed from a drowned merchant two hundred years ago, because it had been a good smile once and deserved further service. "No offer. Not for you. You misunderstand your position, my lady—you are not the counterparty. You are the *message*." I let that settle. "The boy will come for you. He came for the soul in the gold jar; he will certainly come for the woman who bled for him in my galleries. And when he comes, I will make him the offer. His mother's freedom—for the map, and for one memory of her. Any one he chooses. Her voice. Her face. The lullaby he somehow knows without knowing how he knows it. He may keep a single piece of her and walk away whole, and she goes free, and everyone lives, and the debt rolls forward another generation as it always has. Elegant, yes?"

"And if he refuses?"

"Then I take everything, as I took it from the enforcers, as I took it from your brother, as I have taken it from ten thousand souls who also stood where you stand and said *never* with tremendous conviction." I drifted closer, and I confess I let my true nature show through the merchant-shape for just a moment—let her see the seams, the way the borrowed face hung on something vast and patient and hollow, a thing woven wholly from stolen memory, thread upon thread upon thread. Guests should see the loom at least once. "But here is what your boy does not yet understand, and what you may carry to him as my courtesy: refusing me changes nothing. The debt is not a sum. There is no figure he can pay, no vault he can empty. The debt is *memory itself*. I do not want his gold or his blood or his map. I want the forgetting. I have always wanted only the forgetting. And human beings—" I touched her chin, lightly, almost tenderly, with fingers that had belonged to a harpist whose hands I particularly admired, "—human beings *line up* to give it to me. Every grief set down. Every shame surrendered. Every name too painful to say aloud. Your guild was not my victim, Lady Amadine. Your guild was my farm, and it tilled itself, and it thanked me for the harvest, and the only crime your Edmund can commit against me is to remember—to stand in the open and say the names, all of them, aloud, in company, where forgetting cannot reach."

I released her then, because I saw it land. I saw her understand, in one cold flash, exactly what Edmund might do and exactly what it would cost me—and I saw, beneath that, the flicker of hope she was already trying to hide, the way one hides a lamp in a window during a raid.

Which is why, of course, I said the last part.

"One more thing, since you've been such an attentive guest." I walked to the gallery's edge, where the ember-veins ran deepest, and looked down into the warm dark. "The boy's mother. The gold jar. Do you know why I kept her separate from the others? Four hundred years of collections, and hers is the only soul I have ever shelved apart." I let the silence stretch until she leaned toward it. "Because she is the last payment on the founding covenant. The original signature. When the guild's first masters signed, they pledged not their surplus but their *foundation*—one soul, held in perpetuity, the keystone of the whole arrangement. While she burns in my vault, every freed memory returns to me. Every name spoken aloud comes back to my keeping by morning. Break the keystone, and the arch falls—but the keystone cannot be broken from outside. Only the covenant's heir may release it. And the covenant's heir is the son of the collateral." I turned back to her, and I let my voice go gentle, which is the cruellest register I own. "So you see, my lady, the boy cannot save her without unmaking the only shield his people have ever had. And he cannot keep the shield without leaving his mother in my shelves forever. Every road from this moment runs through my hands. I built the mountain's roads, after all. I have simply been waiting four hundred years for someone to walk the last one."

Sera looked at me for a long time. Bound, beaten, alone in the belly of the thing that had eaten her brother's childhood—and she smiled. Small, sharp, noble to the marrow.

"He won't take your bargain," she said.

"They never do," I agreed pleasantly. "At first."

And then, far above us—through a thousand feet of stone and glass and sleeping fire—I felt it. A tremor in the ember-veins. A wrongness in the deep roads. Something moving where nothing should move, carrying light where light had been sold, and beneath it, faint as a seed cracking, a sound I had not heard in four centuries:

Someone, in my mountain, was saying a name aloud.

I turned my face toward it, and every mask in the Ember Court turned with me.

* * *

"Well," I said to the emptying dark, to the trembling walls, to the small defiant spark walking its impossible road toward me through the deep places of the world. "Let us see what you remember, Edmund Villalobos."

"Let us see what you're willing to forget."
