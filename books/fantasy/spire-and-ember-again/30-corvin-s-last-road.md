## 30. Corvin's Last Road

I have been many things in my life: a smuggler, a liar, a guildmaster of a guild that was mostly a rumor, and a man who was very good at pretending the past was a locked room he'd simply misplaced the key to. I have never been a fast walker. This is unfortunate, because for three days now I have been walking as fast as a man can walk while his insides are being slowly unmade by ash.

The Court's punishment is elegant, if you admire craftsmanship. They don't beat you. They don't burn you. They put their hands on your temples and take a little of you out — a memory here, a warmth there — and they leave the holes open. The holes ache. The holes fill with cold. By the second day I had forgotten the name of my favorite horse, which sounds like nothing until you realize you can remember the horse perfectly, its crooked foreleg, its opinion of bridges, and the name is just gone, a tooth knocked from a smile. By the third day I had forgotten my mother's face, and I confess I sat down on a rock and laughed until it turned into something else entirely.

They took more when I refused to tell them where Edmund had gone. Which is a strange sentence to write about myself, because I did refuse. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one keeps them anymore. Corvin Villalobos — no. Not Villalobos. That name belongs to better people than me. Corvin Ashe, guildmaster of nothing, looked the Magistrate's grey clerks in their eyeless faces and said *I don't know where the boy is*, and it was even true, because Edmund had gotten good at disappearing, and God knows who taught him.

Then they let me go. That's the part I still don't understand, walking. They let me go like a fisherman throws back a fish too small to bother gutting. Or — no. Like a man opens a cage door to see if the bird will fly home to him. They wanted me to lead them to Edmund after all. They wanted me to do it without knowing I was doing it. Perhaps there's a tracker in what's left of me, some scent they planted behind my eyes. If so, I've spent these three days doubling back through streams and sleeping in thorn thickets to spoil it, and I'd like to think old habits die harder than old men.

* * *

Let me tell you how I found them, since apparently the universe has decided I get one last act and it intends to make me work for it.

I followed the ember-ink. You can't see it unless you know to look — a faint warmth in the air, like standing near an oven door — but I helped draw that map forty years ago, back when I still believed the guild was a brotherhood instead of a ledger. The map bleeds heat along whichever road its bearer walks. It's how the Court always found our couriers. It's how they found the one who died on Edmund's switchback, poor devil, though I didn't know that yet either. There were a great many things I didn't know yet, standing at the edge of a hollow below Raven's Tooth with the cold eating through my coat, watching a fire that was too well-hidden to be anyone's but my apprentice's.

He'd built the fire small and screened it with stone, the way I taught him. He'd posted no watch, which I also taught him, or rather failed to teach him not to do, because I taught him everything as though he'd only ever need to survive alone. That was the whole curriculum of the Wayfarers' Guild in its final century: *be invisible, be unremarkable, be no one*. I told myself I was keeping him safe. What I was actually doing was making sure that when the debt came due, there would be as little of him left to collect as possible. A man can dress cowardice in a master's robes and wear it for thirty years without ever once looking down.

The girl saw me first. Of course she did. Sera Amadine came out of the dark with a knife already open and her weight forward, and I thought, *there's a noblewoman who's learned something worth learning.* She didn't lower the blade when she saw what I was. "You're the guildmaster," she said. "The one who sold him."

"I never sold Edmund."

"You sold everyone else." She said it flatly, without cruelty, which is worse. Cruelty you can argue with.

Edmund woke then, and I watched his face do something I will carry into whatever comes after dying, if anything does. It wasn't anger. Anger I could have borne; anger is a kind of respect. It was hope. For one naked instant, before he strangled it, my boy looked at me across that dead little fire and hoped I'd come to help him. Thirty years of silence, one betrayal already behind us, and he hoped. I have robbed houses, lied to magistrates, and signed away souls with a steady hand, but I have never in my life felt as richly ashamed as I felt under that hope.

"Corvin." Just my name. Then: "You're hurt."

"Ash-taken," I said. "It doesn't matter."

"It matters," Sera said, and didn't lower the knife.

* * *

Here is what I gave them, there at the fire, while my hands shook so badly I had to press them against my knees to keep the parchment still. And here is why I'm writing this account at all, in my head, walking, addressing no one, because a confession you speak aloud can be taken from you, and I have learned what the Court does with things it takes.

The covenant. The original one, inked before Thornfell had its current name, before the lowland villages were villages, back when the first Wayfarers were eleven desperate families fleeing a war nobody remembers because — and here is the joke, the beautiful rotten joke — the Court made sure nobody remembers it. Eleven families wanted passage through the mountains. The Ember Court offered it. Safe roads, hidden ways, freedom from tolls and soldiers and hunger. And the price was not gold. Gold is nothing to a thing that eats memories. The price was *remembrance itself*: each family pledged one soul per generation to the vaults beneath the mountain, and in exchange, the mountain forgot them. Their names fell off deeds and gravestones. Their neighbors' memories softened around them like wax. They became the perfect smugglers — unremarkable, untraceable, *ungrieved* — and every generation, the Court collected its tithe, and every generation, the guild told itself the same lie: that the ones collected had simply gone away. Died quietly. Moved to the coast.

I knew. I have known since I was Edmund's age, since old Guildmaster Ferren drank himself honest one night and showed me the covenant and wept and swore me to secrecy and died two winters later with the secret intact, the coward, passing it to me like a hot coal passed hand to hand down a line of men all pretending not to be burned. I knew when I took over the guild. I knew when the debts mounted and the Court grew hungry and the tithe came due one soul short, and I stood in that cold vault with the Magistrate's clerks and gave them a name to balance the books.

I gave them Maren Villalobos. Edmund's mother. My — it doesn't matter what she was to me. She was everything, and I wrote her name on a line, and I went home to her infant son and raised him and never told him, and called it protection, and called it love, and slept at night, most nights, because I had made myself very good at not looking down.

At the fire, I laid the covenant out where they both could see it. Edmund read it twice. His face went through weather — confusion, horror, and then that terrible stillness he gets, the one I flattered myself he inherited from me, when really he inherited it from her.

"The pact-stone," he said finally. His voice was very quiet. "Below the peak. The one the old routes all bend toward, like water around a rock. I always thought it was a marker."

"It's the anchor," I said. "Every road in Thornfell is a clause in that contract. Break the stone and you break nothing — the stone's just where the words live. But the words—" My voice failed, and I hated it, and I pushed through. "The words can be *ended*, boy. Not broken. Ended. A covenant dies when its terms are fulfilled publicly. Spoken aloud. Every name the guild erased, every soul it sold, remembered openly, witnessed freely, by people with no debt of their own. Remembrance given, not taken. The Court feeds on forgotten things. It cannot digest what the whole world is looking at."

Sera was already ahead of me, the way she always is. "The ledger," she said. "The names in the ledger. Read aloud, at the stone, on solstice eve, when the debt comes due—"

"When the claim is strongest," I said. "Yes. When the Court comes to collect, it must come up out of the mountain to do it. And it must stand in the light and hear itself refused."

"And it'll fight," Edmund said.

"It will fight like a starving thing fights," I said. "And it will offer you anything. Anything, Edmund. It offered me your mother's life once, years ago, in exchange for the guild's silence for another century. I want you to understand that I refused that offer too, and I want you to understand that refusing it changed nothing, because the debt was still there, growing interest. The Court doesn't need your consent. It only needs your forgetting."

* * *

We walked together for half a day. I want that written somewhere too, even if the somewhere is only the inside of my own dissolving skull. Half a day, the three of us, on the high road toward the pact-stone, and for perhaps an hour of it — crossing a shoulder of scree with the wind at our backs and the valley opening below like a held breath finally released — for perhaps an hour I allowed myself to imagine that this was what the guild should have been all along. Not a brotherhood of secrets. A company of witnesses. The girl walked ahead reading the ledger, mouthing names to fix them in her memory. Edmund walked beside me and asked questions — real ones, the kind he'd stopped asking at fifteen when he learned each one cost him a slammed door — and I answered every single one, and the answering felt like lancing a wound I'd kept bandaged so long I'd mistaken it for part of my body.

"Why her?" he asked, at the worst moment, on the worst stretch, where the path narrowed above a drop. "Of all the names. Why her?"

"Because the debt needed a Villalobos," I said. "The founding families' lines were the collateral. And because—" I made myself say it. I owed him the whole ugly architecture of it. "Because I tried to give mine instead. The clerks wouldn't take it. A guildmaster's soul is already half the Court's, they said. Tainted goods. They wanted something clean. Something that loved the mountain and trusted the guild." I watched the drop beside my boots. "She volunteered, Edmund. That's the part I can't — she *volunteered*, because the clerks told her the alternative was the guild's dissolution and the roads closing, and there were families in the lowlands who'd starve without the smuggling runs that winter. She did the arithmetic and she paid it. And I let her, because letting her was easier than finding another way, and I have been letting easier win for my entire miserable life."

He didn't speak for a long time. The wind filled the silence. Then he said, "You should have told me," and it wasn't forgiveness and it wasn't condemnation, it was just true, the way the ground is true, and I said, "Yes," and we kept walking.

* * *

They came at dusk. I heard them before anyone — you learn to hear ash, a dry whisper like paper turning pages — and I knew what they were before I turned: four enforcers, grey and drifting, and behind them something larger wearing the shape of a clerk, sent not to kill me but to finish the collection. They'd decided I was worth more empty than free. Or perhaps the bird had flown too far from the cage, and it was time to clip what remained.

I didn't run. I'd like to report some final flourish of courage, but the truth is simpler: I couldn't have outrun a slow door, and besides, I had done my sums. Four enforcers against a wounded old man, a runaway, and a boy whose gift is invisibility — the odds only work if someone stands in the narrow place and makes the enemy spend time going through him. Time was the whole currency now. Solstice was tomorrow. Every hour they spent on me was an hour not spent hunting the stone.

"Take the ledger and go," I told Sera, and to Edmund I said — I had rehearsed something clever on the walk, some guildmaster's farewell, and what came out instead was: "Your mother used to sing when she cooked. Badly. Off-key with tremendous confidence. Don't forget that. Whatever else they take from you, don't forget that."

Then I stepped into the narrow place, and I drew the old guild blade that hadn't tasted anything but whetstone in twenty years, and I made them pay in minutes.

It went about as well as you'd expect. I got one of them — Veyra, the tall one, I caught her across the shoulder and felt something underneath the ash that flinched like a person, which I choose to believe matters — and then the clerk-shape put its hands on me and began taking, and I will not describe it except to say that they started with the warm things. They took the smell of bread baking in the tollhouse. They took the sound of rain on the high passes. They took, I discovered with a grief that arrived pre-sharpened, every memory I had of Maren's face, leaving me the fact of her and none of her, a name with a hole where the woman lived.

But they were slow, and careful, and greedy, and greed buys time, and Sera and Edmund bought their lives with mine, which was the entire transaction I intended. The last thing I saw clearly was Edmund at the ridge-line, turned back, screaming my name, the girl hauling him away, and I thought: *good, good, keep moving, don't you dare come back for me, I have buried enough people I loved by staying.*

* * *

That was yesterday. Since then I have been doing the only thing left to do, which is follow.

They didn't kill me. I understand that now, limping along the smelter road with my coat stiff and my shadow feeling wrong, like a coat worn inside out. They took what they wanted and left the rest as bait, because the Court has read me correctly for thirty years: I am a man who comes back. I abandoned Edmund once and spent fifteen years telling myself it was strategy. I betrayed him once and told myself the same. The Court knows that whatever else I am, I will crawl to that pact-stone if crawling is what remains, and where I go, perhaps they hoped, the map goes, the boy goes, the whole rebellion arrives gift-wrapped.

So here is my last act of tradecraft, the sum total of everything the Wayfarers' Guild ever taught me, deployed one final time: I am going to arrive anyway. Let them watch. Let them count me among their assets. Because I hold the one thing they cannot afford for me to hold, the thing they were too arrogant to search a dying man for, since what threat could a hollowed husk possibly pose?

I tore the covenant in half before they took me. The half with the terms, the ending-clause, the words that end the words — I sewed it into the lining of my coat thirty years ago, the night Ferren died, because some part of me even then was keeping receipts against the day I'd have to answer for myself. The Court took my mother's face today. It cannot take what it doesn't know exists, and it has never once imagined that Corvin Ashe might be carrying the instrument of its destruction against his own traitor heart.

I can see the smoke of the gathering below the peak now. Dozens of fires where dozens of fires should not be — the remnant families, the ones with holes in their lives, come up out of the valleys because a boy with his mother's stubborn chin asked them to climb. And above them, ringing the slopes, the grey drift of enforcers, patient as frost.

I am not going to make it to the front of that crowd. I know the arithmetic. I taught Edmund arithmetic. I will make it to the edge, and I will put the covenant's ending into his hands, and I will say the thing I have been composing for three days and thirty years, and then I will sit down, and I suspect sitting down will be permanent.

There he is. At the stone. Turning, hearing my footsteps on the frozen grass, his face doing that thing again — the hope, the terrible hope.

Well then, boy. Last lesson. Pay attention, because I'll only say it once, and this time I mean every word.
