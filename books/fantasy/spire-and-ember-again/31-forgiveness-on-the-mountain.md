## 31. Forgiveness on the Mountain

The snow had stopped falling by the time I understood what Corvin was trying to tell me.

That's the thing about dying men in the mountains—they don't announce themselves. They just get quieter, and you mistake quiet for rest, because you want to. Because admitting otherwise means admitting there's something you can't fix with a rope, a fire, or a fast pair of boots.

I'd found him at the treeline below the old switchbacks, half-buried in a drift, and for one merciful moment I thought he was dead already, because that would have been simpler. Then his eyes opened, and they found me the way they always did—first, before anything else in the world, as if I were the fixed point he navigated by.

"Edmund." My name came out cracked, like ice under a boot.

"Don't talk." I dug him out with my hands, which was stupid, because my hands were already bleeding from the scree and the cold turns blood to glue. "Don't—just lie still. I've got a shelter kit. There's an overhang two hundred yards up. We'll get you warm."

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and thin, and it scared me more than screaming would have.

"Two hundred yards," he said. "You always did overestimate my legs and underestimate your own stubbornness."

"Shut up and let me work."

I got him moving anyway, because that's what apprentices do—we obey the last instruction we were ever given, even when the man giving it is past caring whether we follow. He weighed nothing. That's what I remember most, later. A man who had once carried me up a rockfall on his back, one-armed, swearing cheerfully the whole way, now light enough that I kept checking I hadn't lost part of him in the snow.

* * *

The overhang was shallow and mean, but it broke the wind, and I got the little spirit-stove going and wedged him against my pack with his cloak bunched behind him. His lips were gray. The wound the Court's punishment had left in him wasn't bleeding anymore—it had gone past bleeding into something worse, a blackened spread across his side like frost made of shadow, and when I peeled back his shirt he flinched not from pain but from shame.

"You shouldn't look at that," he said.

"I'm a Wayfarer. Looking at ugly things is the whole job."

"Not anymore," he said. "Not after tomorrow."

Tomorrow. Solstice eve. The pact-stone. Every name aloud. I'd spent the climb down from the high passes rehearsing it—the ledger names, the order, the cadence—and I'd told myself Corvin was somewhere behind me, dying or not, and that grief could wait its turn in line.

Grief, it turns out, doesn't queue.

"Here." I pressed the tin cup into his hands. Broth, more or less. Mostly salt and memory. "Drink."

He drank a little. Then he set the cup down with the exaggerated care of a man conserving his last strength for words, and I knew—I knew the way you know weather coming over a ridge—that he was about to spend them.

"The covenant," he said. "In your pack?"

"Wrapped in oilcloth. Along with everything else you should have told me twenty years ago."

"Good boy." He closed his eyes. "I taught you that distrust. I want you to know I know that. Everything sharp in you, I put there, and none of it was kindness."

"Corvin—"

"Let me finish. I've been practicing this speech since the Ash Road, and if I stop I'll lose my nerve, and I have very little else left to lose, so let me finish." His hand found my wrist. His grip was nothing. A bird's grip. "I sold her. You understand that now. Not the Court. Not the guild. Me. I signed the paper. I held the pen. Your mother came to me the winter you were born and asked for passage out of Thornfell—for herself and for you—and I had a debt come due and no soul to pay it with, and she was right there, and she trusted me, and I—" His voice went to pieces. He gathered them again the way he'd taught me to gather a frayed rope: quickly, without ceremony, because ceremony wastes time. "I told her the road south was open. I gave her to them and told the village she'd abandoned you. And then I took you home and raised you and told myself every night for twenty years that keeping you was penance, when really it was just another way of keeping the secret."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

I had known. That's the obscene part. Since the vault, since the lullaby, since the struck-through name on the chapel wall—I had *known*, in the way you know a bone is broken before you set it. But knowing a thing and hearing it said are different animals. Knowing is a map. Hearing is the road itself, under your feet, going down.

"You let me grieve her," I said. "My whole life. You watched me ask about her and you changed the subject. You watched me invent her—a voice, a face, a reason she left—and you never once corrected me. Do you know what I used to imagine? That she was a spy. That she was running from bandits. That she'd died heroically somewhere and you were protecting me from the details." I heard my own voice go strange. "She was in a *vault*. She was humming to me through glass while you taught me knots."

"Yes."

"One word. That's all it would have taken. One word, any year, any night, any of ten thousand nights around a fire, and I'd have hated you and climbed down there myself at fifteen."

"And I'd have lost you to the Court at fifteen," he said simply. "Or lost you to the truth, which amounts to the same thing, the way I counted it. So I did the arithmetic cowards do. I added up the years and called the sum love." He looked at his hands, at the black frost creeping past his wrist now. "It wasn't love. Love would have been one word."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my body just decided for me, walked to the mouth of the overhang, and stared out at the white valley where tomorrow several dozen people with holes in their lives were climbing toward a stone that might kill us all.

Behind me, Corvin said nothing. That was his gift, in the end—he never followed, never pressed, never filled silence with excuses. He'd taught me that too. *A man who talks in the gaps is a man hiding something in them.*

"Edmund." Quiet. "You don't have to say anything. I didn't crawl here for absolution. I crawled here because the covenant has to be broken by someone who carries the debt, and I am the last signatory who can still speak. Tomorrow, when you read the names, read mine last. Read it loudest. And then forget me the way the Court forgets—completely, and on purpose. That's the only payment I have left, and it isn't much, but it's mine to give."

I turned around.

He was watching me with those eyes that had measured a thousand ridgelines and found a way over every one of them, and they weren't asking for anything. That was the unbearable part. Rage needs a hook, and he wouldn't give me one. He'd hung himself out in the cold air of that overhang like a coat someone had left behind, and waited to see if I'd take him or leave him, and either way he'd have called it fair.

"You're an idiot," I said.

A flicker of the old dry humor. "Runs in the trade."

"You think forgetting is a payment? You spent twenty years teaching me that memory is the whole craft—that a Wayfarer's real cargo is what happened on the road, carried forward, passed on, kept alive. And now, at the end, you want to be the one thing I *don't* carry?" I crouched down in front of him. My eyes were doing something embarrassing and I let them, because there was no one left to perform competence for. "No. Absolutely not. You don't get to opt out of being remembered. That's the Court's trick, Corvin. That's the *Magistrate's* trick. Forgetting isn't mercy—it's inventory. It's how they farm people. And I will not do their work for them, not even for you. *Especially* not for you."

His breath caught. "Then what—"

"So I'm going to forgive you instead. And you're going to hate it."

"Edmund—"

"Because carrying hate is just another kind of forgetting. That's the thing nobody tells you. Hate feels like holding on, but it isn't—it's holding a grudge so tight you crush everything else out of your hands. Twenty years of hating you would be twenty more years of not remembering her lullaby, not remembering the good roads, the bad jokes, the time you fell in the river at Grey Ford and came up still holding the tea kettle. I don't have room for it. Tomorrow I need every name, every memory, every scrap of light I've got, and hate is heavy, and I am done carrying things that only weigh."

He was crying. Old men cry quietly, mostly, and he did—no sound, just the tears cutting clean lines down a face gray with ash and cold, and his hand still on my wrist, gripping now with whatever the black frost had left him.

"I don't deserve it," he whispered.

"No," I agreed. "You really don't. That's what makes it forgiveness and not a transaction. You taught me that too, actually—you just taught it by counterexample. Every debt you ever taught me to count, you were counting wrong. Debts are the Court's language. I'm done speaking it."

"Your mother sang that song," he said suddenly, desperately, a man paying out his last coin. "The lullaby. *Low the lantern, low the load.* She sang it on the switchbacks when you were a baby, strapped to her chest, and I heard it once, from the tollhouse, and I—" his breath rattled— "I stole it. Kept it. Sang it to you when you couldn't sleep and told you I'd made it up. It was hers. Everything good I ever gave you was hers first, and I let you believe it was mine."

*I knew it,* some small vicious part of me thought. *I always knew you didn't make it up.*

But the larger part, the part that had walked out of the Ember Court with a glass soul under its arm and a crowd of strangers climbing behind it—that part just felt the last locked door inside me swing open, and out of it poured not rage but something almost like relief.

"Sing it with me tomorrow," I said. "At the stone. When I say her name, you sing. Loud. Off-key. Let the whole mountain hear what you stole and give it back."

"If I'm alive."

"If you're alive," I conceded. "And if you're not, I'll sing it twice. Once for you. Once for the lying old thief who raised me."

He laughed again, and this time it was almost the old laugh, and then it turned into coughing, and the coughing went on too long, and when it stopped I saw that we had crossed some line together in that shallow overhang, though neither of us said so. The black frost had reached his shoulder. His breathing had developed a rattle like wind through a loose shutter.

"Tell me about the roads," he said, after a while. "Not the guild roads. Yours. The ones you'll make."

So I told him. About the new guild—not a guild, Sera kept correcting me, a *school*, or maybe just a very organized group of friends with maps—where every route was written down and posted where anyone could read it, where no toll was taken in souls or secrets, where the records were public and the doors were open and the only oath was *remember aloud*. I told him about Veyra learning to hum again. About Sera's brother eating breakfast like it was a religious observance. About the villagers with holes in their lives who were, right now, climbing toward us through the snow, carrying each other's missing pieces.

"That sounds," Corvin said, when I finished, "like a guild that would have gotten us all killed forty years ago."

"It probably will tomorrow."

"Good," he said. "Good. Better than good. It's the only kind worth dying near." His eyes were closing. "Edmund. The cup."

I put it in his hands. He drank, all of it this time, and handed it back with the fussy precision of a man putting his affairs in order, and lay back against my pack.

"I'm going to sleep now," he announced, in the tone he used for announcing a change of route. "Wake me if the mountain does anything interesting."

"Corvin—"

"Nothing," he said, before I could speak. "I'm asking for nothing. That's the whole point. Whatever you give me now is a gift, and gifts are the one currency the Court can't touch." His eyes opened one last time, and they were clear, and they were looking at me the way they always had—first, before anything else in the world. "You were the best thing I ever stole."

And then he slept, and I sat with him through the small hours while the stove hissed and the wind talked to itself in the pines, and somewhere past midnight, between one rattle of breath and the next, the rattle stopped, and the silence afterward was so complete I could hear the snow beginning again, softly, like the mountain settling its accounts.

* * *

I buried him at dawn, above the treeline, where he could see the roads.

It took three hours to cut through the frozen ground, and I talked the whole time, because that's what he'd have done—filled the gaps, kept the pace, narrated the work. I told him about the routes I'd charted that he'd never walked. I told him the tea kettle story wrong on purpose, the way he always told it, inflating the river and shrinking the kettle. I told him I was angry, still, and would be for a while, and that anger and forgiveness apparently could share a tent without strangling each other, which was news to me.

I stacked the stones carefully. Wayfarers' cairns are supposed to be anonymous—one traveler's mark for another, no names, because names were dangerous, names were debts. I put his name on it anyway. Full name, deep-cut, the way the chapel wall should have borne his mother's and everyone's mothers'. *Guildmaster Aldous Corvin, who lied, and carried it, and came back up the mountain anyway.* Let the weather argue with it.

Then I took out the covenant, wrapped in its oilcloth, and the ledger, and I read the names once through alone, quietly, as a rehearsal—and stopped at the last page, where his signature sat at the bottom of the founding pact like a stain.

I didn't strike it through. That was the chapel wall's method, the old method, the Court's method—strike the name, take the person, call it accounting. Instead I wrote beneath it, in the margin, in my own hand:

*Paid in full. Remembered anyway.*

* * *

They came up out of the valley through the morning—Sera first, of course, because Sera climbed like the mountain owed her money, then Veyra steady and slow, then the remnant, dozens of them, strung out along the switchback like beads on a thread. People with holes in their lives. People who had lost names they couldn't mourn and mourned names they couldn't find. They stopped when they saw the fresh cairn. Word travels fast among the grieving.

Sera reached me last, which meant she'd hung back on purpose, which meant she'd already heard.

"Edmund." She looked at the cairn, then at me—at my hands, ruined from the digging, at whatever my face was doing. "Are you—"

"I forgave him," I said. It sounded strange in the open air. True, but strange, like a word from a language I'd only read. "Last night. Before the end. He asked for nothing and I gave him the one thing he didn't want."

"Did it help?" she asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely. Sera always asked the real question, the one polite people leave in the ditch.

I thought about it honestly, because he'd earned honesty if he'd earned anything.

"No," I said. "It didn't help *him*. He was past helping. It helped *me*. It emptied my hands." I flexed my fingers, stiff and split and finally, finally empty. "Tomorrow I need them empty. Hate is heavy, Sera. I've been carrying it up this mountain alongside everything else, and I just now realized it weighs the same as the ledger, and I can't carry both."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she did something I didn't expect: she took off her glove and pressed her bare hand flat against the cairn, briefly, formally, the way you greet a stranger you expect to become an ally.

"Then he walks with us," she said. "As memory. Not as weight."

Veyra came and stood beside me, looking down at the stone. Her face, usually so smooth with borrowed emptiness, was working through something. "I knew him," she said slowly, like a woman reading letters through frosted glass. "Before. In the Court. He came down once, to bargain. He bargained *badly*—he gave too much away, kept asking for one extra year, one extra soul deferred. The Magistrate called him the worst merchant it had ever met." A pause. "I think that was a compliment."

"It was," I said. "From him, it was the highest one."

Below us, the remnant was gathering itself, checking packs, stamping feeling back into feet. Above us, the peak of Thornfell stood against a hard blue sky, and somewhere on its flank, half-buried in centuries of snow, waited the pact-stone—and beneath the stone, awake now and hungry and *listening*, the thing that had farmed my family for four generations.

The solstice was tomorrow. The debt came due at dark.

I shouldered my pack. I checked the covenant, the ledger, the ember-map warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Then I looked at the cairn one last time.

"Keep watch on the roads, old man," I said. "We're coming through."

And we started to climb.
