## 32. Rallying the Remnant

I had spent my whole life learning how to be invisible in the mountains. It turned out that walking into a village and asking strangers to follow me up a frozen peak was a completely different skill, and I was catastrophically bad at it.

The first lowland town was called Merrow's Ford, and it smelled like tallow and wet wool and the particular despair of a place where nothing has happened in forty years. I came down off the ridge at dusk with Corvin's covenant wrapped in oilcloth against my chest and my boots leaking, and I stood in the square like an idiot for a good ten minutes before I worked out that people don't gather around you just because you have something important to say. They gather because you're on fire, or because you've set something else on fire.

So I did what any last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild would do.

I knocked on doors.

* * *

The first door belonged to a cooper named Hesk, a broad man with sawdust in his beard and the look of someone who had been interrupted mid-argument with himself. He opened the door, looked me over—the mud, the frostbite, the general air of a man who had recently been chased through collapsing galleries by creatures made of ash—and said, "We don't want any."

"I'm not selling anything."

"Then you're begging, preaching, or recruiting." He crossed his arms. "Which is it?"

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it makes you honest at the worst possible moments. I could have lied. I'd spent years lying by omission, keeping to smuggler's paths, saying nothing, being no one. But I was so tired, and my mother's soul was somewhere above me under all that stone, and the solstice was two days away, and I heard myself say:

"Recruiting. Sort of. Do you ever feel like there's a hole in your life? A shape of something missing that you can't name?"

Hesk's face did something complicated. The argument he'd been having with himself stopped, and something older surfaced instead.

"My wife says I talk about a sister I never had," he said slowly. "Talk about her like she's real. Marta. I know her birthday. I know she bit her nails. I don't—" His jaw worked. "There's no record of her. My mother's letters mention seven children. There were six of us."

My chest went tight. "That's why I'm here."

He didn't invite me in. Not yet. But he didn't close the door either, and in the lowlands in winter, not closing your door is practically an embrace.

* * *

It got easier after Hesk, which is a terrible thing to say, because what it really means is that once you've seen one person carrying that particular hollow, you start seeing them everywhere. They were everywhere. That was the horror of it, and also—God forgive me—the hope.

The miller's widow who set three places at table out of habit and couldn't explain the third. The old schoolteacher who kept a spare slate chalked with a name she didn't recognize in handwriting she did. The young man at the tavern who woke every night at the same hour weeping and had learned to tell his friends it was a dream, though he could never remember dreaming anything.

They were holes shaped like people. The Court had fed on forgotten things, and the guild had been its farm, and generations of collected souls had left these little absences scattered through the valley like tooth marks. Nobody remembered who was taken. But the world remembers, in its way. Tables remember. Chalk remembers. Hands remember the shape of a sister's hair.

I stood in the taproom of the Drowned Otter that night, on a chair, with ale sloshing near my boots, and I told them everything. The guild. The pact. The Ember Court beneath Thornfell. The ledger Sera had stolen, with its columns of names. I held the covenant up and read from it until my voice cracked, and when I got to the part about how it could only be broken by public remembrance—every name spoken aloud at the pact-stone—a woman near the fire started crying before I even finished.

"You're saying they can come back," she said. "The ones who were taken."

"I'm saying their souls are still burning down there. I'm saying the debt comes due at solstice, and if we do nothing, the Court collects again—more this time, maybe everyone. And I'm saying there's a stone below the peak, and names written in a ledger, and that remembering out loud is the one weapon they can't take from us." I swallowed. "Because they feed on forgetting. That's the whole of it. That's what they are. So we're going to stand in the snow and refuse to forget."

Silence. The bad kind, the kind where you can hear yourself dying in front of an audience.

Then Hesk stood up. Big man, sawdust beard, the one with the sister-shaped hole in his life. He looked around the room and said, "Marta bit her nails. I'm the only person alive who knows that now, and I don't even know how I know it. If climbing a mountain gets her back—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—then I'll carry anyone who can't walk."

And that was it. That was the whole speech that mattered, and it wasn't mine.

* * *

We left Merrow's Ford at dawn, eleven of them and me, and I want to be clear that it was chaos. Nobody trains you for this. Corvin taught me seventeen ways to cross a scree slope and zero ways to organize a column of civilians who have never seen snow above their boot-tops. The miller's widow brought a ham. A full ham, wrapped in cloth, as if we were going on a picnic rather than to a confrontation with a creature woven entirely out of stolen memory. The schoolteacher argued with me about the route for a full mile before conceding that perhaps the man raised on smuggler's paths knew the mountain better than her memory of a map from thirty years ago.

But they climbed. That's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing that still catches in my throat: they climbed. People with soft hands and unbroken lives except for the hole, hauling themselves up switchbacks in wind that cut like a knife, because a stranger told them the missing pieces of their hearts were sitting in glass shelves under a mountain, and they believed him.

Or maybe they didn't believe me. Maybe belief wasn't required. Maybe when you've spent twenty years setting a third place at the table, you'll climb anything on the chance of filling it.

* * *

The second village was Bracken Hollow, and there I found Tam.

Tam was fourteen, maybe fifteen, all elbows and suspicion, mucking stables for a farmer who paid him in soup and contempt. I found him because he was the only person in the yard who watched me arrive without pretending not to. Everyone else developed a sudden fascination with fences.

"You're the one from Merrow's Ford," he said. "Word came down yesterday. Madman from the mountains recruiting folk to fight ash demons."

"Not fighting them, exactly. Remembering at them."

"That's worse." But he came closer. "You knew my da?"

I hadn't. I said so.

"He went up Thornfell hunting when I was small. Never came back. Everyone said avalanche." Tam kicked at straw. "But there weren't no avalanche that winter. Dry year. I asked. Nobody liked me asking." He looked up, and his eyes were hard in a way no child's eyes should be. "Your ledger. Would his name be in it?"

"It might," I said, because lying to this boy felt like kicking a dog. "If the Court took him. I won't pretend the odds are good. What's down there has been down there a long time, and souls burn a long time too, but—" I stopped. Made myself say it plainly. "Some might be past saving. I can't promise you your father walks out of that mountain."

Tam nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed something he'd known since he was small enough to be lied to successfully.

"Good," he said. "Everyone else promises things. You're the first one who didn't." He shouldered a rake like a spear. "When do we leave?"

"Tam—"

"I can climb before I can walk, I know every goat track on the south face, and I'm small enough that ash-things will overlook me till it's too late." A ghost of a grin. "Also I'm coming whether you say yes or not, so you may as well get the credit for saying yes."

I thought of Sera, who had barged into my story the same way, all prickles and refusal to be left behind. I thought of how the best things in my life had arrived by refusing to take no for an answer while I stood there holding a door I didn't know I was opening.

"Yes," I said. "Go tell your farmer you quit. Take bread. Leave the rake."

He took the rake anyway. I let him. Pick your battles, Corvin used to say, usually right before losing one.

* * *

By the time we reached the third settlement—a strung-out line of cottages along a river called the Liss, where the water ran so cold it seemed offended by warmth—I had stopped knocking on doors and started letting the story do the work. Word traveled faster than I did. That's the thing about valleys: they gossip like rivers run, downhill and everywhere.

At the Liss I met the Vess family, and I need to write this down properly because I never want to forget it, and writing it down is the whole point of me now, isn't it?

Old Maren Vess was ninety-one and blind, and she sat by her fire while her granddaughter read her my message—the whole account, the guild, the Court, the ledger—and when the girl finished, Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Read me the names."

The girl read them. All of them, the long columns, name after name after name, and Maren sat with her ruined eyes turned toward the ceiling, and tears ran into the creases of her face, and she whispered along with some of them. Not many. Four, maybe five, out of hundreds. But she whispered them like she was greeting guests she'd waited decades to receive.

"I taught half those children their letters," she said when it was done. "One day they were there and the next day the village couldn't recall them, and I thought my mind was going, because everyone agreed there had never been such children at all." Her hand found her granddaughter's. "Everyone agreed. That's the devil of it. When everyone agrees, you start agreeing too, because the alternative is standing alone in a world that says you're mad."

"You're not mad," I said. "You never were."

"No," she agreed placidly. "But I was alone, and alone is nearly as bad." She turned her face toward me, those white eyes missing me by a fraction and finding me anyway. "Young man, I cannot climb your mountain. My legs finished that argument forty years ago. But I can sit at the foot of it, and I can pray, and I can remember out loud right here, and if remembering is the weapon, then distance doesn't matter, does it?"

I knelt by her chair. "No," I said. "It doesn't."

"Then here's what you take from me." She gripped my wrist with a strength that startled us both. "You tell them—every soul down there, every name in that ledger—you tell them Maren Vess remembers. Tell them somebody kept the count. Somebody refused."

I promised her I would. I have never meant a promise more.

* * *

Sera found me on the road north of the Liss, coming down off the ridge with snow in her dark hair and that expression she wore like armor—half sneer, half dare, all of it hiding more than she'd ever admit. She fell into step beside me without greeting, which was our way.

"Well?" she said. "How many?"

"Twenty-three from Merrow's Ford and Bracken Hollow. More meeting us at the trailhead. Plus Maren Vess praying at the river, which I'm counting, because I think God counts it."

"And the boy?"

"Tam?" I glanced at her. "How did you—"

"He marched past our camp an hour ago carrying a rake and radiating the specific fury of a teenager who has decided the world owes him an answer." Something softened at the corner of her mouth. "He reminds me of someone. Insufferable. Refuses instructions. Probably going to save all our lives at the worst moment and be unbearable about it forever."

"Sounds familiar."

"Don't flatter yourself." But she bumped my shoulder with hers, lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking, and we walked a while in silence.

Then, quieter: "Edmund. Tomorrow we climb, and the day after is solstice, and whatever happens at that stone—" She stopped. Started again, which she almost never did. "My brother's name is in that ledger. Aldric. I've carried that ledger across half the country telling myself I was doing it for justice, for principle, for all sorts of noble rot. But tomorrow I'm going to stand in front of a mountain full of monsters and shout my brother's name into the wind, and I need—" Her jaw tightened. "I need to not be the only one shouting."

"You won't be," I said. "You were never going to be. That's rather the entire plan."

"I know. I just needed to hear it."

"For the record, I need to hear things too. Constantly. Ask anyone."

"Nobody's here."

"Then ask the ham."

She laughed—short, surprised out of her, gone quickly, but real. We walked on toward the trailhead where fires were already being lit, where twenty-some strangers with holes in their hearts were warming their hands and watching the peak of Thornfell rise black against the stars, waiting for a madman from the mountains to lead them up it.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I sat by the biggest fire and took out the ledger and read names until my voice gave out, practicing. Because that was the job now. Not swords, not smuggler's craft, not any of the seventeen ways to cross a scree slope. Just names. Said aloud. Said together. Said stubbornly, in the cold, in the teeth of a thing that had farmed our families for centuries and called it commerce.

Hesk sat down across the fire from me, big hands open on his knees. "Say Marta next," he said. "When you get there. Say it loud."

"Marta," I said. "Loud."

"And the widow's third place at table—that'll be her son, Joss. She'll want him early too."

"Joss. Early."

Around the fire, others drifted closer, feeding me names like kindling, each one a small act of defiance against everything that had ever told them to forget. The schoolteacher corrected my pronunciation twice. Tam, who should have been asleep, sat cross-legged at the edge of the light with his rake across his knees, listening to every syllable like a man memorizing ammunition.

And high above us, Thornfell stood against the stars with its crown of snow, and beneath it, deep in the warm dark, the Ember Court was counting down to solstice, certain—as it had always been certain—that the valley would stay agreeable, that everyone would agree, that forgetting would win again.

Let it be certain one more night.

Tomorrow we climb, and the day after, we speak.
