## 34. Every Name Aloud

The ledger weighed less than a sparrow's egg and more than a mountain. That's the thing about names — nobody tells you how heavy they are until you have to carry them.

I stood on the pact-stone with the book open in my hands and the whole valley gathered below me in the snow, and I confess my first thought was not noble. My first thought was: *I am going to be sick on this ancient sacred rock in front of two hundred people.*

"Edmund." Sera stood at my shoulder, hood back, letting the solstice-eve moon find her face. She'd stopped hiding it somewhere on the climb up from the lowlands, and I understood why. Tonight was no night for hiding anything. "Whenever you're ready."

"I'm never ready," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

"Then be unready loudly." She squeezed my arm once, hard enough to bruise, which is Sera's way of saying something tender. "We're all here because we chose to be. Give them something to do with their fear. Fear with a job is just courage that hasn't been introduced to itself yet."

I looked out at them. Two hundred souls, give or take, standing in the blue snowlight below Thornfell's peak. Farmers with holes in their memories where a daughter used to be. A blacksmith who'd spent thirty years setting an extra place at his table without knowing why. Old Marta from the ferry crossing, who had come up the mountain on legs that hadn't climbed anything steeper than her own stairs since the war, because she said there was a name in her chest rattling around like a coin in an empty purse and she wanted it back.

And around us, at the treeline, at the scree slopes, drifting down through the pines like ash off a dying fire — the enforcers. Dozens of them. Veyra stood nearest, at the edge of the crowd, her mask pushed up onto her forehead for the first time since I'd known her. Underneath it her face was ordinary. That was somehow worse than the mask. Ordinary faces shouldn't have to carry what hers carried.

They hadn't attacked. Veyra had told us they wouldn't — not yet. The Court wanted to see what we were about. The Court, I suspected, was afraid, though I don't know if a thing like that can feel afraid or only calculate the shape of it.

Corvin's covenant lay folded against my heart, wrapped in oilcloth, the parchment so old it had gone the color of tea left too long. *Broken only by public remembrance,* he'd wheezed, bleeding out on the high road with snow in his eyelashes. *Every name. Spoken aloud. At the stone. The pact has no power over what is remembered together.* And then, because he was Corvin even at the end: *Don't mumble, boy. You always mumble.*

So I didn't mumble.

I opened the ledger.

* * *

The first name was Aldric Fenwick. I know that because I read it aloud, and my voice cracked on it like ice under a boot, and then I read it again, steadier, and the second time something happened.

Aldric Fenwick turned out to be the blacksmith. The one with the extra place at his table.

He made a sound I will hear until I die — not a cry, nothing so simple. It was the sound of a man finding a room in his own house he'd never known existed, and opening the door, and finding his whole life waiting inside it. He said, "My wife," and then he said, "Her name was Bess," and then he sat down in the snow and wept like a child while the people around him held him up.

One name. One soul's worth of remembering. And somewhere beneath our feet, I felt it — faint as a heartbeat through a floorboard — the mountain flinch.

"Aldric Fenwick," I said again, because Corvin had taught me that a road walked twice becomes a road forever. "Guildmaster of the Third Crossing. Collected in the year of the Long Frost."

Then the next name. Then the next.

I won't pretend I remember them all in order now. Grief does strange things to memory, and so does joy, and I felt both that night in quantities no one should have to hold alone. But some of them stay sharp as broken glass.

There was Tamsin Hale, collected the year I was four, whose granddaughter stood in the third row and screamed the name back at me before I'd finished saying it — screamed it like a curse, like a prayer, like both at once — and then began reciting everything that came flooding back: *Tamsin Hale, who taught me letters, who smelled of rosemary, who hummed when she baked.* The mountain shuddered again, harder.

There was Oren Villalobos.

My hand stopped on the page.

Villalobos. My name. A name I'd been told belonged to nobody, a dead-end line, a family of one boy raised by a guildmaster who answered questions about the past with silence or with lies, whichever came to hand faster.

Oren Villalobos. Wayfarer of the Inner Roads. Collected seventeen years ago.

The year before I was born.

Sera's hand found mine on the page. "Say it," she whispered. "Edmund. Say it."

"Oren Villalobos," I said, and my voice did not crack this time; it went very quiet instead, the way a river goes quiet just before it goes over the falls. "Wayfarer of the Inner Roads. My—" and here the word stuck, because I had never once in my life said it aloud about anyone, "—my uncle. My mother's brother."

The crowd took it from me. All of them. Two hundred voices saying *Oren Villalobos* into the cold, and the pact-stone beneath my feet began to sing — a low, grinding note, like a bow drawn across a frozen lake — and hairline cracks spidered out from where I stood, glowing ember-orange, the color of the ink on the map, the color of the Court.

* * *

Here is what I understood then, standing in the middle of that singing stone, and I want to set it down plainly because it is the truest thing I know:

The Ember Court fed on forgetting. That was its whole economy. Every soul sold, every memory taken, every name struck from a chapel wall or a guild roll — none of it vanished. It was *harvested*. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in those glass shelves of the vault, all of it sat stored like grain in a granary, and the Magistrate grew fat and eternal on the interest.

But debt runs both directions. That's the joke of it. That's the beautiful, terrible joke. The covenant bound *us* to remember nothing — but it bound the Court to keep everything. Perfectly. Inviolably. Because stolen goods must be kept intact or the theft fails.

And a thing kept can be claimed.

Every name spoken tonight wasn't just grief. It was a receipt. It was two hundred people walking up to the greatest thief in the world and saying, *we know what you have, and we are asking for it back, and asking is the oldest magic there is.*

The enforcers felt it first. Of course they did — they were made of the debt itself, ash-bound, hollowed, their memories taken as payment down generations of the guild's bad bargains. As the names rose, they began to sway like trees in wind. Some pressed their hands to their masks. One dropped to its knees in the snow, and where its fingers touched, the snow didn't melt — it *remembered*, frost blooming outward in patterns like ferns, like feathers, like handwriting.

Veyra was singing.

I hadn't noticed when she started. A thread of melody under the names, half-remembered, the song she'd hummed in the vaults without knowing why — and other enforcers caught it, the way fire catches, ragged voices joining in on a tune none of them could name and all of them knew.

"The lullaby," Sera breathed beside me. "Edmund. They're singing your mother's lullaby."

No. Not my mother's. That was the thing I finally saw, standing there with the ledger shaking in my hands. It had never belonged to her. She had learned it the way everyone learns lullabies — from someone who loved them, who learned it from someone who loved *them*, back and back and back, a road of mothers stretching further than any map. The Court hadn't just stolen individuals. It had stolen the roads between them. And tonight, in the snow, the roads were reopening.

I kept reading.

* * *

By midnight — if it was midnight; the sky above the peak had gone strange, stars swimming like fish under ice — we had passed three hundred names, and the mountain was shaking in earnest now. Not trembling. Shaking. Snow slithered off the pines in white avalanches small enough to laugh at and large enough to kill, and nobody laughed, and nobody left. Old Marta from the ferry crossing planted herself at the front with her cane driven into the snow like a flagpole and shouted names with the rest of them, names I hadn't reached yet, names she was somehow reading off the inside of her own returning memory, and each one landed on the stone like a hammer-blow.

The cracks in the pact-stone glowed brighter. Through them, deep down, I could see movement — light, gold and restless, rising through the rock like sap through a tree in spring. Like sparks climbing a chimney.

Like souls looking for the door.

"They're coming up," Sera said. Her face was lit orange from below, and she was smiling, and she was crying, and she managed to do both without compromising either, which is the most Sera thing I have ever witnessed. "Edmund, they're following the names. Every name is a rope and they're climbing them—"

And then the cold changed.

I don't know how else to say it. Cold is cold, but this was different — a cold with *attention* in it, a cold that looked at you. The fires in the stone dimmed. The singing faltered. Down the whole length of the valley, every bird, every wolf, every living thing went silent at once, the way a tavern goes silent when the wrong man walks through the door.

At the treeline, the remaining enforcers — the ones still masked, still bound, still obedient — turned as one and knelt.

"Oh," said old Marta, very calmly, gripping her cane. "It's come up to complain, has it?"

The snow at the head of the valley began to move uphill. Against the wind. Gathering. Drawing itself up out of the drifts and the shadows and the long dark throat of the smelter tunnel behind the peak, pulling itself together the way a debt pulls together after years of interest, until it stood taller than the pines and wore a shape that was almost a man, woven entirely out of things that had been forgotten — I could see them, all of us could, fragments surfacing and sinking in its body like faces in dark water. A child's shoe. A wedding ring. Half a sentence someone never finished saying.

The Magistrate had come to collect.

Its voice, when it spoke, was every voice we'd lost, layered and cold:

"*You are spending what is not yours.*"

Sera's hand found mine. Veyra stepped forward, mask in her hands, and planted herself between the thing and the crowd. And the pact-stone sang louder, and the golden light beneath it burned brighter, and somewhere in my chest the last name on the ledger waited — the one I'd saved, the one I'd been saving all night without admitting it, the one written in the oldest ink of all.

But that comes next. Everything comes next.

The Magistrate looked down at me across the snow, at the little book in my hands, and smiled with a mouth made of other people's smiles, and said my name — my full name, the one I'd only just learned to say myself — and the mountain held its breath.
