## 35. The Magistrate Descends

I had not worn my true shape in nine hundred years, and I will confess—it fit like an old coat that had been let out too many times.

From the surface of things, from behind the magistrate's mask of cooled basalt and patient courtesy, I watched the boy speak names into the snow. That was the indignity of it. Not the crowd—crowds were weather, crowds came and went like weather—but the *names*. Each one spoken aloud struck the pact-stone like a hammer on a bell, and each strike rang through me in a place I had not permitted myself to have in centuries.

Do you know what it is to be made entirely of other people's forgotten things?

No. Of course you don't. You keep your memories the way misers keep coin, hoarded and unexamined, certain they are yours because no one has ever come to collect. I was once a miser too. Then I learned the great secret of Thornfell: that memory is the only currency that appreciates when spent by someone else. A soul given up willingly, or taken carelessly—the distinction mattered less with every century—is worth more than gold, more than blood, more than love. Gold sits. Blood dries. But a memory, fed to something hungry, becomes *appetite*.

I am what nine hundred years of appetite built.

And the boy was unraveling me one name at a time.

* * *

Let me be precise about what was happening on that mountainside, because precision is the last dignity available to a creature mid-unraveling.

Edmund Villalobos stood at the pact-stone with the ledger open against his chest, reading. His voice was not remarkable. That is what galled me most, if I am honest—I have dined on emperors' recollections and drunk the childhoods of poets, and this smuggler's apprentice read names in the flat, careful voice of a man checking a supply list, and it *hurt worse than any of them*. Because he did not perform grief. He did not perform rage. He simply refused to stop.

"Marisol Vane," he said. "Collected winter of the Long Frost."

The stone shuddered. Somewhere beneath us, in the vaults I had carved from the mountain's cooling heart, a shelf of glass flickered.

"Aldric Fen. Collected spring of—" He paused, squinting at Sera's stolen ledger. "Spring of whatever year the bridge fell. The ledger doesn't say which year the bridge fell."

"It was the year the bridge fell," Sera Amadine said beside him, dry as slate. She had her brother's hand in hers, and she was not letting go, and I noted this the way a general notes a supply line: her grip was doing more work than any sword on that mountainside.

"The year the bridge fell," Edmund repeated, and moved on.

Around them, the remnant stood in the snow—forty, fifty souls now, the ones with holes in their lives they could never name until a stranger knocked on their doors and told them what was missing. Farmers. A midwife. Two children who had been told their grandmother died before they were born and were now learning, name by spoken name, that she had been collected instead, which is a different thing entirely, and children understand the difference faster than adults do. Children always know when something has been *taken*.

And around all of them, my enforcers stood in a ring, ash-grey and patient, waiting for the order that would not come cleanly.

That was the second indignity. Veyra.

I had made Veyra myself—or rather, I had *unmade* her, which is the same craft performed in reverse. I had taken a debtor woman's memories as payment, sanded her smooth, and left obedience where a self had been. For three hundred years she had been my finest instrument. And now she stood at the ring's edge with snow gathering on her shoulders, and she was *humming*. Under her breath. A scrap of song I did not recognize, which meant it was hers, which meant it had survived, which meant my sanding had missed a grain after all.

Instruments do not hum.

People hum.

* * *

"You should stop him," said the thing wearing Guildmaster Corvin's face, standing at my elbow in the snow. Corvin had crawled back down the mountain to die among his own kind of failure, and I had let him, because a dying man makes such useful bait. "You still can. One word and—"

"One word," I said, "and your enforcers will find that obedience frays exactly where the names are loudest. You've heard them. Listen."

He listened. Beneath the boy's flat recitation, my ash-bound were shifting. Not moving—shifting. Weight passing from foot to foot. Hands flexing. The small animal fidgets of bodies remembering they are bodies.

"They're holding," Corvin said.

"They're *waiting*," I corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference has a name, and the boy is going to say it eventually." I turned from the pact-stone and looked down through the mountain's skin, into the Court, where my vaults were flickering shelf by shelf like a city losing its lamps. "Your guild sold me nine hundred years of safe passage, Master Corvin. Do you know what I bought with it? Not roads. Roads are nothing. I bought *forgetting*. Every traveler who crossed Thornfell without fear, every merchant who never wondered why the passes were always open, every guild member who signed the book and never asked what the ink was made of—they paid me in the memory of the bargain itself. A debt no one remembers is a debt that never comes due. Your guild was not my customer, old man. It was my farm."

Corvin's face did something complicated. Grief does that, in the last hour of a life; it stops being an expression and becomes a landscape.

"I know," he said. "I've known since I was younger than the boy."

"And you farmed for me anyway."

"I farmed for you anyway." He said it steadily, and I will grant him this: there was no excuse in it. Just the fact, set down like a stone. "So did my master. So did his. We told ourselves the toll was small. A name here. A face there. Never ours." His mouth twisted. "Until it was mine to pay, and I paid it with a woman who trusted me, and I told myself *strategy*, and I have been telling myself strategy for twenty-five years."

"Yes," I said. "You were excellent at it. Self-deception is the richest soil there is. Nothing grows a debt like a good conscience."

Below us, Edmund spoke another name. The pact-stone cracked—a sound like ice giving way on a lake, thin and bright and utterly irreversible.

It was time to stop being a mask.

* * *

Understand what I did next, and why.

A creature made of stolen memory cannot afford to lose the memories that hold it together. Most of what I am is sediment—centuries of small forgettings, the color of a dead woman's kitchen, the way rain sounded on a roof in a village that no longer exists, ten thousand first kisses whose owners forgot them on their wedding days. Sediment holds. But at my core, pressed down under all of it, are the *load-bearing* memories: the founding pact, the covenant's terms, the faces of the first guildmasters kneeling in the snow where that boy now stands. If those go, the sediment has nothing to rest on.

The boy was reading toward the core. The ledger was ordered alphabetically, but memory is not alphabetical, and neither was the mountain shaking itself apart beneath us. He would reach the founding names within the hour. Perhaps within minutes.

So I did what any sensible monster does when the house begins to burn: I came out of it.

* * *

The mountain opened.

Not violently—that was the old way, the wasteful way, and waste is for creatures with reserves. I rose through the pact-stone itself, unhurried, letting the stone remember what it had been before it was a stone: a seam of magma, a vein of the mountain's own hot blood. Snow flashed to steam in a ring thirty feet wide. The crowd staggered back. My enforcers did not move, which I noted, and did not like.

And I let myself be seen.

I will not describe myself fully, because description is a kind of containment and I refuse to be contained even in prose. But I will give you the truth of it: I was tall as the memory of a cathedral, and I was made of everything the valley had ever lost. Faces moved across me the way light moves across water—a woman laughing, a soldier's last look at his door, a child's drawing of a horse that didn't look like a horse. I wore nine hundred years of other people's yesterdays, and where the boy's names had already struck home, I was *thin*. Translucent. You could see the snow through me in places, falling and landing on nothing.

The crowd saw. The crowd understood, in the way crowds understand, instantly and without words: *that is where our missing things went.*

Good. Fear is efficient. I had work to do and no patience left for subtlety.

"Edmund Villalobos." My voice came out of every face I wore at once, which produces a curious choral effect I have always found pleasing. "You have cost me a great deal tonight. More, if we are honest, than your entire species has produced in nine centuries of diligent forgetting. So I am going to make you an offer, and I want you to listen carefully, because I am only ever generous once."

He looked up at me. He did not step back. I catalogued that—fear present, legs steady—and filed it under problems requiring bespoke solutions.

"Everyone goes free," I said.

The words moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. I felt hope ignite in forty chests at once, and I fed on the edge of it—not much, just the trimmings; even starving, one keeps table manners.

"Every soul in my vaults. Your mother. The Amadine boy. The midwife's grandmother, the soldiers, the farmers, the whole sad inventory—released. The ash-bound wake with their memories returning. The Court closes. The mountain keeps its roads open, freely, forever, no toll, no pact, no guild required." I let the promise settle, let them taste it. "Everyone walks away whole. Everyone but you."

Sera Amadine moved first—of course she did; she had been moving first since she stole my ledger out of a gilded cage. She stepped in front of Edmund with her brother's hand still locked in hers, dragging the boy along like luggage. "Whatever it costs—"

"It costs his mother," I said pleasantly. "Specifically. Permanently. Irrecoverably."

Silence. Even the wind went quiet, and the wind and I have an arrangement, so that took some arranging.

"He speaks the founding names," I continued, addressing the crowd now, because the crowd was the true audience and Edmund merely the instrument, "and the covenant breaks, and everything I hold comes loose—including the oldest item in my collection. Marisol Villalobos. Sold to me by that man—" I gestured, and every face on my surface turned briefly to regard Corvin, who did not flinch, which surprised me, and impressed me, and annoyed me, all at once. "—in the year of the boy's birth, to cover the guild's final debt. Her soul burns in my deepest vault. It has burned there for twenty-five years. It is the warmest thing I own."

I bent—if bending can describe what a creature of that scale does to bring itself nearer to a single mortal—and looked at Edmund directly, face to face, my borrowed eyes finding his ordinary brown ones.

"But here is what your friends will not tell you, boy, because they love you, and love is a propaganda department. If the covenant breaks, she does not pass on to some gentle elsewhere. Souls kept as long as hers *dissolve* when the vessel breaks. Twenty-five years in glass, burning. There is nothing left to free. There is only light, and then less light, and then none. Unless—" and here I smiled, and every stolen mouth on my surface smiled with me, which I mention only because it was magnificent, "—unless the debt is paid rather than broken. Paid in full, by the heir, voluntarily. Your memory of her, Edmund. The lullaby you somehow know. The warmth you've chased your whole life without knowing its name. Give them to me, and I release her intact—whole, unburned, free to rise. She goes on to whatever waits beyond keeping. And you remain, alive and well and entire in every way except one: you will never think of her again. Not with grief. Not with longing. Not with the particular ache of a man who never got to say goodbye. She will simply be a shaped absence, a door in your heart that opens onto wall. You will not even miss her. Missing requires remembering."

I straightened. Let the offer hang. Offers are like knives; they do their best work suspended.

"That is my generosity," I said. "One soul saved truly, at the price of one memory falsely. Or one soul lost entirely, along with everyone else's freedom, because you were too proud to pay a fair price. Choose."

* * *

Now. Here is what I expected, and I record my expectations faithfully because a creature made of memory ought to keep honest books.

I expected the crowd to waver. They did—hope is cowardly; it flinches at any price. I expected Sera Amadine to argue, fiercely, on both sides, because she is intelligent enough to see the arithmetic and decent enough to hate it. She began to. "Edmund, if there's any chance she—"

I expected Corvin to say nothing, because Corvin had spent his whole life saying nothing at precisely the moments speech mattered. He opened his mouth. Closed it. For once, the silence was not cowardice; it was a man finally understanding that this choice was not his to touch.

What I did not expect—what I had not priced in, and I despise myself, insofar as I have a self to despise, for the oversight—was the children. The two who had learned their grandmother's name an hour ago. The smaller one tugged Edmund's sleeve and said, in the carrying voice of the very young:

"If you forget her, who tells us about her?"

And Edmund Villalobos looked down at the child, and then at Sera, and then at the crowd—his strange, ragged, assembled family of the hole-ridden and the half-remembering—and something happened in his face that I have seen perhaps five times in nine hundred years, and never once been able to purchase at any price.

He was not afraid anymore.

That is the flaw in my entire economy, if you want the truth of it, the accounting error at the bottom of everything I am. I trade in private memory. What is held alone can be taken alone. But what that boy was building on that mountainside—in the snow, in the dark, out of farmers and midwives and runaway nobles and reformed monsters and dying old men—was something I had no ledger column for. He was converting private grief into common property. He was making his mother *everybody's*.

You cannot buy what has already been given away.

But I did not know that yet. I knew only that he was about to answer me, and that the mountain was trembling, and that somewhere below us my vaults were going dark shelf by shelf, and that Veyra had stopped humming and started, very quietly, to sing.

"Choose," I said again, and my chorus of stolen voices shook, just slightly, on the second syllable.

Edmund opened his mouth—

* * *

—and what he said then, and what the crowd did, and what I became when the pact-stone finally broke: those belong to the next telling. Suffice it to say that I, who have collected every forgotten thing this valley ever lost, was about to learn what it feels like to be the thing forgotten.

The snow kept falling through the places where I was thin.

And the boy began to speak.
