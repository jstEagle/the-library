## 36. What Edmund Chooses

The Magistrate's offer hung in the air like smoke that had decided to stay.

*Everyone goes free except you.*

I want to be honest with you, because I've spent this whole account being honest about my cowardices and I don't see why the last one should get special treatment: for three full heartbeats, I considered it.

Three heartbeats. That's how long it took me to imagine it—Sera walking down off this mountain with her brother's hand in hers, Veyra blinking in the sunlight with her memories stitching themselves back together behind her eyes, the villagers of Thornfell going home to lives they'd only half-remembered losing. All of them free. And me, alone on the pact-stone, emptied out, a man-shaped hole where a smuggler used to be. No grief for my mother, because no memory of her to grieve with. No guilt about Corvin, because no Corvin to feel guilty about. A clean ledger.

The Magistrate saw those three heartbeats. Of course it did. It was woven out of stolen things; it knew exactly what temptation looked like from the inside.

"Reasonable," it said, and its voice was every voice that had ever signed away something precious and called it practical. It wore faces now—or suggestions of faces, pressed into its surface like coins into wax. I recognized one of them. Guildmaster Aldous Renner, first of his name, whose signature sat at the top of the covenant. He looked tired. He'd been tired for four hundred years.

"It is reasonable," the Magistrate went on, drifting closer across the snow, and where it passed, the snow didn't melt so much as *forget* how to be cold. "One soul against hundreds. One memory against a mountain's worth. You have spent your life learning to weigh costs, Wayfarer. Weigh this one."

And here's the thing—the thing I've never told anyone, not even Sera, not even in the quiet after everything—I almost did. Not because I wanted to be a hero. The opposite. Because I wanted so badly to stop *being* anything. There is a kind of exhaustion that doesn't live in the body. It lives in the carrying. Every road I'd ever kept secret, every name Corvin wouldn't say, every year I'd spent cataloguing routes nobody traveled for a guild that had already eaten itself—that weight had a shape, and the shape was me, and the Magistrate was offering to set it down.

Then Sera said my name.

Just that. "Edmund." Not loudly. She was standing at the front of the crowd with Tomas's arm looped through hers, snow in her hair, and she said it the way you'd remind someone they'd left a lamp burning.

And I remembered what she'd told me in the deep roads, when I was at my lowest and she'd dragged me up by the collar of my own despair: *a memory traded is a person erased twice.* Her brother hadn't just been taken by the Court. He'd been taken again every time someone chose to forget him rather than fight for him. The Court didn't feed on forgetting. It fed on the *choosing.*

"No," I said.

The word came out smaller than I meant it to. Words do that sometimes. But the Magistrate heard it, and the crowd heard it, and most importantly *I* heard it, which is the only audience that matters when you're deciding who you're going to be.

"No?" The Magistrate's borrowed face of Aldous Renner smiled, and it was not a kind smile, because it wasn't really a smile at all—it was a memory of a smile, worn like a coat. "You would trade your mother's freedom for your own grief? How very sentimental. How very *human.*"

"You're doing it wrong," I said, and now my voice found its footing, because there is nothing like an enemy underestimating you to steady the knees. "You think I'm choosing between her freedom and mine. But that's the trick, isn't it? That's always been the trick. The guild fell for it. Four hundred years of masters falling for it, one at a time, each of them thinking their sacrifice was noble, each of them handing you another piece of us. You never take whole people, Magistrate. You take them *apart*, one bargain at a time, and you make each bargain look like mercy."

The wind came down off the peak then, hard and clean, and it moved through the crowd the way wind moves through wheat, and I understood suddenly that I wasn't standing in front of these people. I was standing *among* them. Somewhere in the last hour—somewhere between the pact-stone and the speaking of names—they had closed around me without my noticing, the way family does.

"So here's my counteroffer," I said.

* * *

I want to explain what I did next, because it will sound mad, and I'd rather it sound mad on purpose than mad by accident.

I turned around. I faced the crowd—all of them, the villagers and the remnant families and the freed enforcers still learning how to stand like people, old Marta from the lowlands who'd lost a sister she couldn't name, the cooper's boy with the hole in his childhood shaped exactly like a friend—and I raised my voice the way I'd raised it over the wind on the high paths for twenty years, the way you learn to when your livelihood depends on being heard once and clearly.

"I need you to do something for me," I said. "I need you to remember me."

A ripple went through them. Confusion. Old Marta frowned. The cooper's boy shifted his weight.

"Not later. Not when this is over. *Now.* While I'm talking to that thing. I want you to watch me and hold onto me. Everything you know of me—the tollhouse, the map, the lullaby I hum when I think no one's listening, the way I take my tea, which Sera says is criminally weak—" a shaky laugh moved through the crowd, and I grabbed onto it like a handhold on scree— "every argument we've had, every kindness, every name I've spoken tonight. Hold all of it. Share it between you. Don't keep it private and don't keep it safe. Keep it *common.*"

"Why?" That was Veyra. She stood near the front, ash still sifting faintly from her shoulders even now, even waking, and her voice was rough with the effort of asking questions instead of obeying orders. "What good does—"

"Because it can't steal what isn't held alone," I said. "That's the whole architecture of the thing. Look at it. Look at what it's made of."

They looked. And I watched them see it—really see it—for the first time. The Magistrate hung above the snow, vast and shifting, and it was beautiful the way a landslide is beautiful if you're far enough away, and it was made entirely of pieces. A face here. A song there. Fragments of weddings and funerals and first days of school, all of them torn loose from their owners, all of them held by one thing and one thing only, and that thing was *isolation*. Every stolen memory in that towering mass had been taken in a private room, from a single person, in a bargain no one else witnessed. That was the Court's genius and its prison both. It could only eat what was kept alone.

"The guild kept secrets," I said. "That's how it died. Corvin kept secrets from me, and his master kept secrets from him, all the way back to Renner at the top, each of us holding our little piece of the debt alone until the debt owned us outright. So we're done with that. Done forever. From this moment, everything I am belongs to everyone standing on this stone, and if that creature wants a piece of me, it has to negotiate with two hundred people at once, and I have met smugglers, tax collectors, and Sera Amadine, and I promise you there is no negotiator alive worse than a crowd that's decided it loves you."

Sera's mouth did the thing it does when she's trying not to smile at one of my jokes and mostly failing. Tomas laughed out loud—a real laugh, rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut—and the sound of it hit the Magistrate like a thrown stone. It flinched. Actually flinched, this mountain of stolen centuries, because somewhere inside it, a fragment of some forgotten father's laughter recognized itself and *ached.*

"Do you understand what you're proposing?" the Magistrate said, and for the first time its voice cracked along its seams. "Memory shared is memory *diluted.* You will be less. Less sharp, less singular, less—"

"Less *yours,*" I finished. "Yes. That's rather the point."

* * *

It came at us anyway. Of course it did. You don't build a court out of other people's souls and then respect a well-argued refusal.

But here is what I learned in that moment, and I give it to you freely, whoever finds this account, because it may be the only true magic I ever learned: a memory held in common cannot be pulled out whole. The Magistrate reached for me—I felt it, a cold like the moment before a sneeze, a pressure behind my eyes, hands made of absence closing around the shape of my childhood—and it got *fragments.* Slivers. It tore at me and I came apart into two hundred pieces and every piece was already somewhere else, already held, already loved by someone who gripped it and refused.

Old Marta had my grandmother's bread recipe—I don't know how she had it, I barely knew I had it, but grief keeps strange attics—and the Magistrate couldn't pull it because Marta was reciting it aloud, defiantly, into the wind. The cooper's boy had the story of my first solo crossing, the one where I got lost for three days and cried behind a boulder, and he shouted it to the crowd with relish while the thing shrieked. Sera had—well. Sera had more than I'm comfortable writing down. Let's say she had the night in the cathedral cavern when I told her I was afraid I'd turn out to be exactly like Corvin, and she had it, and she held it up like a lantern, and the Magistrate recoiled from it as though burned.

"You cannot have him," Sera said, and her voice carried the way voices do when they've finally stopped being afraid. "He's not yours. He's ours. And *ours* is not a thing you know how to carry."

The Magistrate dimmed. I swear before any god you care to name, it dimmed—not from weakness, but from confusion, because in four hundred years of harvesting solitary hearts, no one had ever done this. No one had ever stood in front of it *pre-shared.* It reached again and again and each time came away with less, because the crowd was getting better at the holding, faster, laughing now in places, calling fragments back and forth like children trading treasures—*he takes his tea weak! He hums off-key! He once bribed a border guard with a poem!*—and every shouted detail was a brick in a wall it couldn't climb.

And in the middle of it all, I stood on the pact-stone feeling the strangest thing I have ever felt in a life spent feeling strange things in lonely places.

I felt *lighter.*

Not emptier. Lighter. The way a pack feels lighter when you finally admit your companions can help carry it. I had spent thirty-six years believing that my memories were mine alone to haul, that love was a private weight, that grief was a solo crossing. I had built my entire self the way the guild built itself: sealed, silent, secret. And it turned out the seal was the wound. The silence was the debt. Every locked door in me had been an invitation to theft, and every open one was a wall.

"Veyra!" I called. "The song!"

She knew instantly. She threw back her head and sang the half-remembered melody that had lived in her hollowed-out heart for decades, and the crowd caught it, and I caught it, and somewhere beneath the mountain my mother's soul caught it too, because the ground beneath the pact-stone began to *hum.*

The Magistrate screamed. It was not a sound so much as an unmaking—a chord of every stolen voice crying out at once—and it gathered itself for one final plunge, one desperate lunge at the stone, at me, at the fragile web of shared light we'd spun around ourselves—

And that is when I saw it hesitate.

Not fear. *Arithmetic.* I could nearly hear the calculation turning over in its ancient, patched-together mind: two hundred holders, one target, diminishing returns, the vault below already shaking, the enforcers standing down, the covenant fraying thread by thread with every name the crowd had spoken tonight. It had fed on loneliness for four centuries, and loneliness was leaving the valley like fog before sun.

"There is still the covenant," it said quietly. Almost gently. A merchant's parting shot. "The pact stands until it is broken *properly.* Spoken words are not enough. The stone remembers the old terms. Break them wrongly and everything held beneath comes apart with it—every soul in the vault, scattered past finding. Including hers."

It let that land. It was good at letting things land; it had practice.

"Come to the stone alone, Edmund Villalobos. Speak the ending as the covenant demands: one voice, one keeper, one sacrifice. Or watch them all fade. Those are the old rules. The old rules are the *only* rules."

And the crowd went quiet, because everyone could feel it—the trap inside the trap, the final lock, the last demand that the hero stand alone.

I looked at Sera. She looked at me. And I started to laugh.

"What," she said.

"It's still doing it," I said. "Even now. Even dying. It still thinks the answer is *alone.*" I turned to the crowd—to my impossible, stubborn, tea-critiquing, memory-hoarding family—and I held out my hand. "It says one voice must speak the ending. Fine. Then we'll need two hundred throats, because I have never once in my life managed to say anything important by myself, and I don't intend to start at the end."

I stepped to the center of the pact-stone. The old terms glowed up through the frost, waiting, smug, certain of their grammar.

"Everyone," I said, "take my hand. Take each other's hands. And when I begin—when I speak the first word of the ending—*say it with me.* Every word. Every name. Let the stone try to find the one voice it demands. Let it search."

Below our feet, the mountain shuddered. Above us, the Magistrate drew itself tall for the last fight of its long, hungry life.

I opened my mouth to speak the covenant's end.

Two hundred voices waited to catch the words.
