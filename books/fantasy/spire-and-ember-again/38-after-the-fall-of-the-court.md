## 38. After the Fall of the Court

The first thing I did after the mountain stopped screaming was sit down in the snow and laugh.

Not the good kind of laugh. The kind that comes out of you sideways, like a cork from a bottle, when your body has been holding terror in reserve for so long that it doesn't know what to do with the sudden absence of it. I sat in a drift up to my waist, my brother's hand in mine, and laughed until it turned into something else, and Tam held on to me the whole time because he always was the steadier of the two of us, even at nine years old, even now.

Above us, Thornfell's peak was smoking. Not burning—smoking, the way a forge smokes when the fire has finally been raked out. Great grey plumes rose off the summit and unraveled into a sky so blue it looked almost embarrassed about itself. And through that smoke, rising like sparks off a bonfire, came the souls.

I had seen them in the vault: flickering things in glass, dimmed down to embers by centuries of feeding. But free, in open air, under an honest sky—they were *luminous*. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They poured out of the shattered crown of the mountain in slow spirals, gold and white and one astonishing shade of green, drifting upward past the snowline, past the clouds, past anything I had words for. The crowd below us on the mountainside had gone silent again, every face tilted up, every mouth open. Somewhere near the front, an old woman was calling out names—not to anyone, just calling them, over and over, like she was afraid the souls would get lost without someone shouting directions.

Maybe they would have. Maybe that was the point.

Tam squeezed my hand. "Is one of them—" he started, and couldn't finish.

"I don't know," I said. It was the truth. Our parents' souls hadn't been in the ledger; the Amadine debts had been paid in coin and land and my hand in marriage, which apparently the Court considered roughly equivalent. But I watched those rising lights anyway, all of them, every single one, and let myself imagine that somewhere in that bright ascending river were two people who had once taught me to whistle through my fingers and cheat at cards.

"They'd be proud of you," Tam said, because he was nine years old for exactly as long as it took to say something like that, and then he was fifteen again and mortified by his own sincerity, and shoved me, and we walked down the mountain together arguing about who owed whom a rescue.

For the record: I owed him. He'd been in the vaults eleven years. I'd been free the whole time, wearing nice dresses and hating myself in private. There is no arithmetic that makes that come out fair.

* * *

The valley had changed overnight, or perhaps it had changed years ago and we had only just been permitted to notice.

You have to understand what Thornfell's shadow did to the lowlands. For generations, the villages around the mountain had lived with holes in their lives—gaps where a person should have been, a name no one could put a face to, griefs that arrived without reasons attached. Edmund called them the collected families. The Court had fed on forgotten things, and it had been thorough, and its leavings were everywhere: mothers whose children had been taken before memory could fix them in place, guild members erased mid-sentence, whole households reduced to a feeling of wrongness at the dinner table.

Now the holes were filling in.

It happened unevenly, messily, the way all true things happen. A woman in Lowmark woke on the second morning after the solstice and remembered her son—not dead, not gone, but *collected*, thirty years ago, for a debt her own father had signed. She stood in her kitchen and said his name aloud to nobody and then sat down on the floor and stayed there until her neighbors found her. By noon half the village was crying and the other half was cooking, because that is what people do when grief arrives late: they feed each other through it.

In Brasten Hollow, a man discovered he had a sister. She was sixty-one years old and lived four streets away and had spent her whole life wondering why her family felt incomplete. They met in the market square and didn't recognize each other at first—how could they?—and then she said their mother's lullaby under her breath, some old thing neither of them knew they still carried, and he went white as flour and said, "Say that again."

I heard that story thirdhand and it undid me anyway. That's the thing about memory: it isn't private property, whatever the Court believed. It leaks. It travels. A song survives in a body that doesn't know why it knows it, waiting for someone to say *say that again.*

And the enforcers—

Gods, the enforcers.

They had scattered across the valley like ash on wind, and at first the villages wanted nothing to do with them, and I understood that too. These were the creatures who had burned couriers and hunted children and interrogated innkeepers half to death. People saw the grey cloaks and reached for pitchforks on reflex.

But Veyra walked among them, and Veyra vouched.

She was changing by the day. It was extraordinary and unsettling to watch—the memories coming back to her not in floods but in fragments, small and specific and devastating. She'd appear at our fire in the evening, stand there awkwardly in a cloak she'd stopped wearing the hood of, and announce things like: "I had a dog. His name was Bristle. I don't remember his face yet, but I remember he was afraid of thunder and I used to sing to him." Or: "I was a toll-keeper's daughter. From the eastern passes. I think—I think I collected the tolls myself, once. Before." Her hands would shake when she said *before*, and then she'd steady them, deliberately, the way you'd smooth a wrinkled page.

Edmund sat with her through most of those evenings. The two of them made a strange pair—the last apprentice and the ash-bound hunter, passing a memory back and forth like a cup of water. Sometimes she'd remember something and he'd write it down for her, in a little book he'd started keeping, because writing it down meant it couldn't be taken twice. "That's the whole point of the new guild," he told me once, tapping the book. "Records anyone can read. Nothing precious enough to steal."

"Nothing precious enough to steal," I repeated. "You've clearly never met my family."

He laughed. It was a good sound on him. He'd earned it the hard way.

* * *

On the fifth day, Veyra remembered her name.

Not Veyra—that was the Court's name for her, a handler's label, functional as a brand. Her name was Maren Ostreva, and she had been a Wayfarer, one of the guild's road-guides, and she had died—or been collected—in the year 812, and she had a daughter. The daughter would be forty now. Maren stood in the middle of our camp holding this information like a live coal and said, very quietly, "She'll think I abandoned her."

"She'll think nothing of the kind," I said. "She'll think the same thing everyone here thinks: that the mountain took you. Because it did."

"The mountain took me because I owed it," Maren said. "I signed. We all signed. Safe passage, safe roads, and if the debt came due—" She stopped. Looked at her hands. "Do you know what the worst part is? I remember signing. I remember thinking it was fine, because it would never come due, because it never comes due, because the guild always pays. And then the guild started paying *us*."

Nobody had an answer for that. Edmund wrote it down anyway. Later I understood why: he wasn't recording her shame. He was recording the mechanism. Every confession like hers was another piece of how the Court had worked, how it had farmed an entire mountain range for centuries by making people complicit in their own harvest. You couldn't fight a thing like that with swords. You fought it with ledgers—with honest ones, kept in daylight, read aloud.

Maren left on the sixth day, heading east toward the passes, toward a daughter who didn't know she was looking for her mother. She took the hood down for good before she went. When she hugged Edmund goodbye—and she did hug him, this woman who three weeks ago had been hunting him across the scree—he held on a beat too long, and I pretended not to see, because some things deserve privacy even in a camp with no walls.

She wasn't the only one who left. By the end of the week, the remnant of the enforcers had scattered to the four winds, each of them carrying their handful of returning memories like embers cupped against the cold. Some went searching for families. Some went searching for graves. One—a big quiet man who had half-remembered a trade before he'd half-remembered anything else—took up his old tools in Brasten Hollow and started mending roofs, and the villagers let him, warily at first and then entirely, because a man who fixes your roof in winter earns forgiveness faster than any speech could buy it.

A few chose not to stay at all. One of Maren's former sisters-in-ash walked into the deep roads rather than face what she might remember, and no one stopped her, because you cannot force a person to want themselves back. Edmund wanted to go after her. Sera, I told myself, let her go. Some roads a person has to walk unburdened by company, even the company of the forgiven.

* * *

Tam and I stayed longer than we needed to.

There was no reason to. The Court was gone, the vaults were empty, my brother was free, and the sensible thing—the Amadine thing, the thing drilled into me since birth—was to go home, reclaim whatever remained of my name and estate, and resume the life that had been interrupted. My betrothed's family had presumably written me off as dead, which suited me fine. My father's creditors had presumably done the same, which suited me even better.

But I kept finding reasons. The roads needed surveying, now that they belonged to no one. The survivors needed organizing. Edmund needed—well, Edmund needed a great deal, though he'd have bitten me for saying so, and mostly what he needed was simply not to be alone in the wreckage of everything he'd inherited, and I decided that counting as a reason.

One evening I caught him at the tollhouse—the old one, the crumbling one where this whole catastrophe had begun—sweeping snow out of a room that had no roof worth mentioning. He'd propped the bloodstained map up against the hearthstone, the ember-ink map, and it had gone dark and ordinary now, just ink on calfskin, its work done.

"You're going to rebuild this ruin?" I asked.

"I'm going to build something *in* it," he said. "Different thing." He leaned on the broom. "Corvin kept everything locked. Routes, records, names—all of it secret, because secrets were the currency. What if a guild kept nothing secret? What if every road was mapped and every map was public and every name was written down where anyone could read it?"

"That's not a guild," I said. "That's a library with delusions of grandeur."

"It's a *start*," he said, with the particular stubbornness I was beginning to find less infuriating than I should have.

I looked at him—this smuggler's apprentice turned soul-thief turned covenant-breaker, standing in his ruined inheritance with a broom—and I thought about my brother upstairs in the loft, sleeping a full night's sleep for the first time in eleven years. I thought about Maren walking east. I thought about a man in Brasten Hollow fixing roofs.

"All right," I said. "But I'm doing the accounts. Your handwriting is a crime."

"It's *distinctive*."

"It's a threat to literacy."

He grinned, and outside, the mountain smoked gently into the sunset like an old man dozing by a fire, and for one whole moment the future seemed like a place we might actually survive arriving at.

Then Tam came down the ladder two rungs at a time, white-faced, holding a piece of paper.

"It's a courier," he said. "From the coast. He says—" Tam swallowed. "He says there are others like the Court. Other courts. And one of them has heard what happened at Thornfell. And it's sent someone to see."

Edmund and I looked at each other over the folded map, and the sunset suddenly felt a long way off.

"Well," I said. "Good thing we're keeping records."
