## 39. A New Guild of Small Roads

The first rule of the new guild was written on the door, and it was not a rule at all. It was a question: *Where do you need to go?*

Sera painted it herself, in letters tall enough to read from the road, and when I pointed out that a guild with no rules was barely a guild at all, she handed me the brush and said, "Then add one."

I thought about it for a long time. Long enough that my arm went numb holding the brush over the empty space beneath her question. Every rule I could think of—the ones Corvin had taught me, the ones carved into the old tollhouse ledgers in his cramped, secretive hand—came down to the same thing: *know something others don't, and make them pay for it.* That had been the Wayfarers' whole architecture. Knowledge as toll. Memory as coin. A mountain full of roads, and every one of them a locked door.

I put the brush down and left the space blank.

"Good," Sera said. "It'll drive them mad."

*Them*, in this case, being everyone. Because here is what I learned in the spring after the mountain shook: people do not trust a gift. We had opened every road through Thornfell—every smuggler's path, every switchback, every hollow bridge and under-road—and posted the maps at the crossroads where anyone could copy them, and half the valley looked at those free maps the way you'd look at a stranger offering you meat. *What's wrong with it? What's he want?*

What we wanted was nothing. That was the part they couldn't swallow.

* * *

We made our seat in the old tollhouse, because it seemed right to hang the new thing from the bones of the old one, the way a climbing rose will use a dead fence. I swept out forty years of dust and found, behind a loose stone in the hearth, Corvin's original ledger—the real one, not the sanitized copies he'd shown the few apprentices who came before me and after me, which is to say me. There were three of us total, across thirty years. The guild hadn't died out; it had been collected. But it had also, apparently, been failing long before the Court came to collect. Even secrets need someone to keep them.

I sat up most of that first night reading Corvin's true ledger by lamplight, and I'll tell you honestly what I felt, because I promised myself this account would be honest even if no one ever reads it: I felt sorry for him. Not forgiveness—that had already happened, on the mountain, in the snow, and forgiveness isn't a feeling anyway, it's a decision you make once and then have to keep making, daily, like water in a dry season. But sorrow. He had spent his whole life guarding a vault whose contents were shame, and shame compounds interest faster than any debt the Ember Court ever ran.

In the margin of the last page, in handwriting gone shaky near the end, he'd written: *Tell the boy everything, or tell him nothing, but do not do what I did, which was both.*

He'd done both. And then he'd died before he could decide which.

I closed the ledger and put it on the shelf behind the counter where anyone could reach it. That was the second thing about the new guild, after the question on the door: our records were open. All of them. The routes, the debts, the names, the confession. If a farmer wanted to come in off the road and read how the Wayfarers sold souls beneath Thornfell for three hundred years of safe passage, he was welcome to. Several did. Most of them read for an hour and left looking like men who'd eaten something rich and regretted it. One old woman stayed all afternoon, running her finger down the list of collected names, and when she got to the end she said, quietly, "My sister's name should be there. She went missing in '61. Nobody would say where."

I turned the ledger around so she could write it in herself.

She stared at me like I'd offered her my teeth. Then she took the pen, and her hand shook, and she wrote *Marta Ellensdottir, taken '61, remembered* — and I swear the room got warmer. Just a degree. Just enough to notice. Some habits die hard in a building that used to sit on top of a court of embers.

* * *

Veyra came back to us in the third week of spring, walking up the high road with a pack on her shoulders and an expression I'd never seen on her before, which was uncertainty. Ash-bound enforcers don't do uncertainty. They do obedience and emptiness, and occasionally, if you're lucky, a half-remembered song. But the Court's fall had cracked her open along with everything else, and what had poured back in was life, and life is uncertain by its nature.

"I don't know what I'm for," she said, standing in the doorway like a soldier reporting for orders she knew wouldn't come. "They made us for guarding. For collecting." Her hands flexed at her sides. "There's no one left to collect."

"There's plenty left to guide," I said. "You know every path on that mountain better than I do. You walked them for a hundred years hunting people. Walk them again helping them instead. It's the same feet. Different direction."

She considered this with the gravity she brought to everything, which I was learning was not emptiness after all but thoroughness—a mind that had been denied choices for a century and now treated each one like a stone she might step wrong on.

"Will people run from me?" she asked.

"Some will. At first." I didn't lie to her. Lying to Veyra seemed like a category error, like lying to weather. "But you're the reason some of them are alive. The ones who were in the Court know which way you pointed them. Word travels."

"It does," she said. "That's how they caught us, in the end. Word traveling." And then, after a pause that lasted about a season: "I remember a song. I still only have pieces of it. I've been asking people. An innkeeper in Lowmark knew the second verse."

"What is it?" I asked.

She hummed it. Four notes, rising, then falling away like something letting go. My chest went tight without my permission, because I knew it—I'd known it my whole life without knowing I knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat. It was the lullaby the gold soul in the vault had hummed. It was the song my mother had sung to me before Corvin carried me out of whatever room she'd sung it in.

"Where did you learn it?" I asked, careful.

"I don't know," Veyra said. "That's the whole problem with me. I don't know anything about myself except what I gather now, piece by piece, like a magpie." She looked at me with those grey eyes that had once been full of ash. "You recognized it too. Your face did something."

"It's a lullaby," I said. "From the lowlands. Old one."

It wasn't a lie. It just wasn't the whole truth, and I heard Corvin in my own mouth as I said it, and I hated it, and I understood him a little more, and hated that too. Some inheritances you break. Some you have to carry a while longer before your hands are strong enough to set down.

Veyra nodded slowly. "I'll find the rest of it," she said. "Piece by piece." And she picked up her pack and walked into the tollhouse like she owned a share of it, which, by then, she did.

* * *

Here is how the new guild actually worked, in practice, in that first spring—which is to say, chaotically, gloriously, and nothing like a guild at all.

A shepherd lost two lambs above the scree slopes and came in asking for the old paths, and I drew him a map while he watched, explaining every fork aloud so he could hold it in his head and didn't need my paper at all. A merchant wanted to move cloth over Thornfell ahead of the toll-riders in the valley towns, and Sera negotiated him down from secrecy to openness: we'd show him the fast road, and he'd tell every other merchant about it, because a road known to all is worth more than a road known to one—it brings traffic, and traffic brings trade, and trade brings the little roadside economies that had starved to death in the years when the only people moving through Thornfell were smugglers and ghosts.

"You're giving away the guild's entire value," a former toll-collector told me, one of the remnant, a man named Osric who'd spent twenty years shaking down carters on the south pass and now couldn't meet their eyes in the market. "Knowledge was the only thing we had."

"That was the problem," I said. "Knowledge hoarded curdles. You saw what it curdled into." I tapped the open ledger. "Knowledge shared ferments. Turns into something else entirely. Something you can build with."

Osric didn't look convinced, but he started coming by anyway, and eventually he started teaching—road-craft, weather-sense, how to read a scree slope for the sound it makes before it moves. He was good at it. Better than I'd been at anything in my first year, because teaching forces you to understand a thing all the way down to its roots, and Osric, to his credit, wanted roots. He wanted to know *why* the slope sang before it slid, not just that it did. The old guild had punished questions like that. Questions were how apprentices learned too much. The new guild ran on them.

By midsummer we had eleven members. By autumn, nineteen. We called ourselves nothing grand—Sera refused to let me name it anything with "order" or "way" in it, on the grounds that both words had been ruined by men like Corvin—so we were just the guides of Thornfell, lowercase, uncapitalized, a profession rather than a mystery. Our sign was an open hand. Our dues were a story: every member, once a season, had to bring back one piece of knowledge the roads had taught them and give it to the common book, where anyone could read it. Weather patterns. Safe fords. Which village mothers made the best travel bread. Sera insisted on that last category personally.

And underneath it all, quiet as groundwater, ran the other work. The remnant work. Families with holes in them.

Because the Court was dead, but its appetite had left scars shaped exactly like itself. All across the lowlands there were people who had lived beside an absence so old they'd built their lives around it without knowing—an empty chair nobody could explain, a name that made their mother go silent, a photograph-less gap in a family story that everyone stepped around like a hole in the floor. We'd found dozens of them in the weeks before the solstice, when I went down asking people to climb with me. Now, in the peace after, more kept coming. They'd heard. Word travels.

They came to the tollhouse and stood in the doorway, embarrassed, apologetic, sure they were wasting our time. *I don't know why I'm here,* they'd say. *I just—there's something missing. There's always been something missing.*

And we'd sit them down, and open the ledger, and the recovered lists from the Court's collapse—Sera had saved the great book of souls from the vault before the galleries sealed, hauling it up a collapsing tunnel on her back while I carried my mother's light, and I have never loved anyone more than I loved her in that moment, filthy and bleeding and hugging a ledger like a child—and we'd read names until one of them landed. Until the person in the chair went white and said, *that's my grandmother's name, but we were told she drowned,* or *that's my uncle, but there's no grave,* or simply, wordlessly, began to cry, because grief needs a shape before it can start to leave.

Then we'd climb with them to the pact-stone—or what remained of it, a field of shattered granite below the peak, each fragment warm to the touch even in winter—and they'd say the name aloud themselves. Their own voice. Their own remembering. That was the whole rite. No incense, no robes, no fee. Just a name, spoken where the debt had been made, taking the memory back.

The mountain grew lighter every time. I don't mean that mystically. Well. I mean it mystically too. But mostly I mean that the passes got easier, the weather gentler, the old dread that hung over Thornfell like smoke lifting year by year. Debts of memory don't vanish when the creditor dies. They have to be paid out deliberately, name by name, and we were paying, and the mountain knew it.

* * *

One evening in late spring, Sera found me on the roof of the tollhouse, which was where I went to think, because the shingles were warm and the view ran all the way down the valley to the lowlands, where the evening lamps were coming on one by one in villages that now knew every road through the mountains and feared none of them.

"You're brooding," she said, dropping down beside me. "On a roof. Very dramatic. Very last-apprentice-of-a-doomed-order of you."

"I'm planning," I said.

"You're brooding *with a purpose.* I can tell the difference. Brooding has no ledger."

I laughed despite myself. Below us, lamplight spilled from the tollhouse windows where Veyra was teaching map-reading to three shepherds' daughters who'd decided, with the terrifying certainty of the young, that they would be the first women of the new guides, thank you very much, and no, they didn't need permission, they needed the eastern passes charted. Osric was arguing amiably with a carter about whether the north scree was passable in fog. The common book lay open on the counter inside, filling day by day with other people's handwriting.

"It's strange," I said. "I spent my whole life learning to be invisible. Small roads, quiet steps, no names given. And now—" I gestured at all of it, the light, the noise, the girls arguing about contour lines. "Now the whole point is to be seen. To be *known*. Loudly. In writing."

"Terrifying?"

"Every morning." I leaned back on my palms. "Corvin trained me to survive by being forgettable. Turns out forgetting is the enemy. I keep waiting for someone to hand me a bill for all this openness."

"No one's coming," Sera said. She said it gently, but she said it plainly, because she was Sera. "The Court is rubble. The Magistrate is a stain on a shattered rock. Whatever's left of the ember-ink, it's drying up. You won. Let yourself have it."

"I'm working on it. It goes against years of training."

"I know." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "I married into a court of masked memory-traders. Trust me, I know what unlearning costs." She was quiet a moment, watching the valley lights. "Tomas wrote again. From Lowmark. He wants to come stay a month. Learn the roads himself."

"Your brother's welcome. He knows that."

"He says he wants to walk the under-road. Where you found—" She stopped. She always stopped there, and I always appreciated the stopping. "He says he wants to see where his name was kept."

"Then he should come. Names do better in daylight."

We sat there while the last light went off the peaks and the first stars came on, and I thought about how a guild is just a promise wearing an organizational structure, and how the old promise had been *we will keep the roads safe, and the price is hidden,* and how ours was simpler and harder: *we will keep the roads open, and the price is honesty.* Paid daily. In public. No exceptions.

Below us, one of the shepherd girls laughed at something Veyra said, and Veyra laughed too—a rusty, astonished sound, like a door opening in a house long shut—and I thought: *piece by piece, like a magpie.* All of us, gathering ourselves back. All of us, singing what verses we had.

* * *

It was nearly dark when I climbed down, and Sera had gone in to arbitrate the fog dispute, and the tollhouse was warm and loud and full, and I stood in the yard a moment just breathing it in, this ridiculous abundance of company.

That was when I noticed the lamp.

Someone had lit the ember-lamp on the gatepost—the one I kept for signaling, the one whose flame burned ember-orange no matter what oil fed it, a habit of the old magic that lingered in the glass. Except I hadn't lit it. And it wasn't burning orange.

It was burning gold.

The same gold as the soul in the vault. The same gold as the sparks that rose through the snow the night the Court fell, the hundreds upon hundreds of freed souls streaming up past the peak toward whatever waits above the sky. Gold, steady, patient, hanging in the glass like a held breath.

And beneath it, on the flagstone at the foot of the gatepost, in a fine drift of ash that had fallen from a cloudless sky, someone had drawn a single line with a fingertip. A line through nothing—a struck-through mark, the kind you'd draw through a name on a wall to show a debt paid, a sentence finished.

Except this line wasn't through a name. It was pointing. Down the valley, toward the lowlands, toward the old churchyard in the village where Corvin had raised me, where I'd buried an urn of mountain snow the winter after the fall because my mother had no grave and I could not stand for it.

Toward the marker that bore no name, because I had never known her full name to carve.

I stood in the yard a long time, heart going like a drum, while the gold lamp burned and the ash-line gleamed faintly in its light, and behind me the tollhouse roared with laughter and argument and the scratch of pens in the common book, all the small noisy machinery of a world being rebuilt.

Then I went inside, took down the folded ember-map from its place of honor on the shelf, and put on my boots.

Some roads, it turns out, are still waiting to be walked.
