## 40. Spire and Ember

The stonecutter in Lowmark wanted to charge me double for the extra letters.

"Villalobos is a long name," he said, squinting at the paper I'd given him like it had personally offended his chisel. "And this middle part — *Maren Ilsa* — that's more stone. More stone is more coin."

"It's her whole name," I said. "Every letter of it. That's rather the point."

He grumbled, but he did it properly, and when he was finished he stood back with his arms crossed and looked at the marker the way a man looks at a thing he's decided to be proud of despite himself. *MAREN ILSA VILLALOBOS*, cut deep into grey granite from the foothills of Thornfell itself, set into the earth on the south slope where the sun reaches first in the morning and lingers latest in the evening. Below the name, because I'd insisted, one more line:

*She was remembered.*

Not *is*. The stonecutter had suggested it. I'd told him no. She *was* remembered — by Corvin, who sold her; by a guild that struck her name through; by a court that shelved her soul like a jar of preserves. And now she *is* remembered, which is a different tense entirely, and one you don't carve in stone. You have to keep saying it out loud for that one to stay true.

It's been eight months since the pact-stone shattered. Eight months since the mountain shook off its debt like a dog shedding rain, since souls rose through the snowfall in their thousands, sparks going up while the snow came down, and for one impossible moment the whole of Thornfell looked like a hearth seen from inside the chimney. Eight months since my mother's soul touched my brow — warm, brief, unmistakable — and I knew her the way you know a song you've never heard sung: completely, instantly, and with no time at all to hold onto it.

I've spent those eight months learning the difference between holding on and carrying forward. It turns out they look similar from a distance. They are not similar at all up close.

* * *

The ember-lamp took three tries to get right.

Veyra helped with the first two. She's been reclaiming herself in pieces, the way ice comes off a pond — not all at once, not smoothly, and with alarming cracking noises in between. Some days she remembers things she'd rather not. Most days she remembers the song, which she taught me properly at last, all six verses, including the one about the miller's daughter that apparently scandalized half of Thornfell a hundred years ago and scandalizes nobody now because nobody remembers why it was rude.

"The wick," she said, on attempt number two, as smoke curled up from a flame that guttered and died. "You're packing it too tight. An ember needs air, Edmund. It's not a candle. It doesn't want to be contained. It wants to be *fed*."

"You sound like Sera lecturing me about ledgers."

"I sound like someone who spent forty years as an ash-thing guarding a vault of stolen souls and has developed opinions about containment." She handed me the lamp back. Her hands still don't always do what she asks them to — the memory of them was taken along with everything else, and sometimes they move like they're following old orders — but they were steady enough that morning. "Looser. Trust it to burn."

Attempt number three burned clean and gold, the way the lamp would burn every evening after, once I set it at the head of her grave. Ember-lamps are guild-craft, or they were. Old smugglers' lights, designed to burn without being noticed, to mark a safe path only for those who knew to look. There's a poetry in repurposing them that I refuse to examine too closely because it might make me cry, and I've done my allotted crying for several lifetimes.

Today, the first day of spring proper — the day the high passes open and the first caravans come over Thornfell instead of around it — I brought the map.

* * *

It lay folded in my coat pocket the whole walk up, and I could feel it the entire way, which is absurd, because it weighs nothing. Less than nothing. Maps drawn in ember-ink don't so much have weight as have *opinions*, and this one had been opinionated at me for months. When I carried it toward the lowlands it went quiet and satisfied. When I carried it anywhere near the deep roads it warmed against my ribs like a cat demanding attention. For a while after the Court fell I half expected it to crumble to ash, its purpose fulfilled, its roads unmapped again.

It didn't. It just waited.

That's the thing about the old guild ways I'm keeping and the old guild ways I'm burying: some tools are made to be used once and some are made to be passed on, and the trick — the whole trick, the thing Corvin died without understanding — is knowing which is which.

The covenant was the first kind. I spoke its ending aloud with three hundred people on solstice eve, every name in the ledger before it, and when the last syllable left the crowd the stone didn't crack so much as *exhale*, and what was left of it is now a scatter of pebbles that the village children fight over, believing — correctly, as far as I can tell — that they're lucky.

The map, I think, is the second kind.

* * *

The grave sits on a shelf of hillside above the old tollhouse, which is still standing, mostly, because I can't bring myself to tear it down and can't quite afford to fix it. Sera calls it "the ruin with sentimental value." She says it fondly. Mostly.

I knelt in the wet grass and unfolded the map across the flat top of the marker, weighting the corners with four small stones, and for a long moment I just looked at it.

Ember-ink moves. You never get used to it. The roads breathe. The mountain range shifts like something sleeping and dreaming of running. And there, cutting down through the heart of Thornfell where no honest chart admits a road exists, the under-road still glowed faintly — dimmer now, its fire banked, the way a forge looks an hour after the work is done. Every road on that map was a road the guild kept secret. Every secret was a debt. Every debt was a person. I traced the main line with one finger, the smuggler's path I'd walked my whole life, invisible and unremarkable, and thought about how many feet had passed over it carrying burdens they weren't allowed to name.

"Well," I said to my mother, because talking to graves is either madness or grief and I've stopped distinguishing, "this is yours, really. It led me to you. Took the long way about it, but then, so did I."

The ink stirred under my finger. Not dramatically. Just a ripple, like acknowledgment.

I folded it along its old creases — carefully, the way Corvin taught me to fold charts, though I suspect he'd have objected strenuously to the purpose — and opened the little iron door at the base of the marker. The stonecutter had hollowed it at my request, a niche just big enough, lined with lead against the weather. I set the map inside, and beside it I placed the ember-lamp, lit, burning steady and gold in the noon light where nobody needed it to be hidden anymore.

Then I closed the door and sat back on my heels.

"That's it done," I said. "Its work and mine. Well — mine's not done, obviously, I've got a meeting Thursday about the ferry rates at Millbrook, which I'm dreading, the Millbrook ferry people are the worst negotiators on either side of the mountain, and that includes the ones who literally traded memories for coin." I paused. "Sorry. You probably wanted something more solemn. Sera says I do this. Fill silence with logistics. She says it's how I pray."

The wind came up the valley and moved the grass, and the lamp-flame leaned and righted itself, and if there was an answer in that, it was the kind you're allowed to believe in without anyone checking your work.

I stayed until the light went long and amber. Then I stood, brushed off my knees, and started down the hill toward home.

They were waiting at the fork in the path, because of course they were. They always are. It remains the strangest and best feature of my life.

* * *

Sera was arguing with Tam about the ledger.

This is their love language. I've stopped trying to translate it. My sister-by-choice — because that's what she is now, whatever the word for a bond forged in collapsing lava-tunnels and shared terror and one truly catastrophic attempt at cooking river trout — stands with her hands on her hips and her brother's stolen ledger open under her arm, and Tam, who spent years as a name in a glass shelf beneath a mountain and came out of it with his memories returned and his patience permanently spent, gestures at the sky as though appealing to a higher court.

"—all I'm saying is if we're writing down every road, we should write down every *condition*. The ford at Alder Crossing is fine in autumn and a liar in spring. A road without its seasons isn't a road, it's a rumor."

"And I'm saying," Sera said, "that the whole point of the new guild is that anyone can read it, which means it has to be *readable*, which means we organize it, which means—" she caught sight of me and her expression shifted into the particular blend of exasperation and relief she reserves for me specifically, "—Edmund. Tell him."

"Tell him what?"

"That the records need structure."

"I was going to say hello," I said. "I climbed a mountain today. I buried a map. I'd hoped for at least a *hello* before the organizational disputes began."

Tam grinned — he has his sister's grin exactly, which is to say it arrives like weather — and clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to stagger. "Hello. Your mother's grave looks good. Veyra swept the path up this morning, and Marta left cuttings from the rosemary, the stubborn kind, the kind that'll survive anything." He glanced up the hill behind me, toward the small gold point of lamplight just visible above the grassline. "She'd have liked it up there. The view."

"You remember her?" I asked, and hated how careful my voice came out.

"A little." He said it gently, which cost him something; gentleness always costs Tam something. "The remnant pooled what we each had. Bits of songs, mostly. Someone remembered her laughing at a tollhouse door. Someone else remembered her arguing with a guildmaster about ferry rates, actually, which — Edmund, I'm sorry, but I think you come by the dreading honestly."

I laughed, which surprised me, and meant it, which surprised me more.

Sera tucked the ledger away and threaded her arm through mine, and we started down the road together, the four of us — Tam ahead, already complaining about the state of the bridge planks; Veyra falling into step beside me with the unhurried gait of a woman relearning what to do with freedom, which is mostly, she's discovered, walking places for no reason at all.

Below us the valley opened out: Lowmark's chimneys smoking supper-fires, the new waystation going up at the crossroads with its big public board where anyone may chalk a warning or a welcome, the first spring caravan crawling up the switchbacks with lanterns already lit against the dusk. On the board, in a dozen different hands, someone had written *ROAD OPEN — THORNFELL HIGH PASS — SAFE, VERIFIED TUESDAY.* No secrets. No tolls. No debts.

"Corvin would hate it," I said.

"Corvin," Veyra said mildly, "would be terrified of it, and then, given a year, secretly proud, and then insufferable about having predicted it." She smiled at whatever my face did. "I guarded that man's memories for thirty years, Edmund. I know the architecture."

We walked on. The lamp burned gold behind us on the hill, small and steady, marking a safe path for anyone who knew to look — and now everyone knows to look. That was the whole lesson, the one written in ember-ink and paid for in souls: a light you hide helps no one, and a memory you clutch alone can be taken from you, but a memory held by many hands, spoken aloud, carved in granite, chalked on a public board for strangers —

That one stays lit.

"Thursday," Sera said, as the first stars came out over the spire of Thornfell, "you're taking me to the Millbrook negotiation. You negotiate like a man apologizing for existing."

"I negotiate like a *guildmaster*."

"You negotiate like a man apologizing for existing *with excellent maps*." She squeezed my arm. "Fine. We'll both go. Tam can mind the records. Veyra can terrify them by standing near the window looking enigmatic."

"She does that anyway," Tam called back.

"I know," Sera said. "It's why we keep her."

And the high road ran on ahead of us, open and unhidden, all the way home.
