## 2. The Debt Ledger

The ledger lived in the temple's lowest vault, behind a door of black iron that no one but the First Scribe ever opened. Vesper had seen it exactly once, at nineteen, when their father's name was read aloud and their own was written beneath it in the First Scribe's careful, merciless hand.

*Halloran. One thousand crowns, plus nine years' interest. Payable in service.*

Service. That was the word they'd chosen instead of *penance*, though everyone in Thornfell knew what it meant. A debt-bound scribe copied prayers until the arithmetic came out even, and the arithmetic never came out even, because interest compounded nightly and the temple set the rate.

Vesper had been copying for nine years now. By their own count—kept secretly, obsessively, on scraps of paper hidden inside the false spine of a hymnal—they had transcribed somewhere north of forty thousand petitions. Prayers for rain. Prayers for safe births. Prayers from farmers asking the old gods to spare their orchards, from sailors asking calm water off the Gray Teeth, from mothers asking anything at all for children who were already gone.

For nine years, none of them had been answered. The gods of Thornfell were dead, everyone said so. The Quiet Flame burned in its alcove because it had always burned, not because anyone tended it. The temple kept its doors open out of habit and its coffers full out of greed, selling candles to people who prayed to nothing.

Vesper had made peace with that. Or rather, they had made a transaction with it: their silence in exchange for a roof, their obedience in exchange for eventual freedom. Forty thousand prayers, and not one of them real. It was almost restful, copying dead words for dead gods. No one could be disappointed by an answer that never came.

Then the orchard bloomed.

Three nights after Vesper hid the page, the accusations began.

* * *

It started with Brother Callow, who managed the candle-tithe and had the eyes of a man who counted everything twice. He found the second answered prayer before Vesper did—a petition from a widow in Lowmarsh asking that her well run sweet again, and a follow-up letter, breathless and ink-blotted, reporting that it had. The well water tasted of honey, she wrote. Her neighbors were calling it a miracle. She had enclosed an offering of four crowns.

Callow brought the letter to the elders' chamber, and by noon the whole temple smelled of panic dressed up as ceremony. Vesper heard it through the scriptorium floor: raised voices, the scrape of chairs, the word *forgery* repeated like a hammer finding a nail.

They came for Vesper at the evening bell. Three elders, with Callow trailing behind like a dog that had earned a bone. First Elder Maren led them, a small woman with a voice like dry paper tearing.

"Scribe Halloran."

"First Elder." Vesper set down their pen with great care. Nine years had taught them that every movement in front of the elders should look rehearsed, harmless, boring. "How may I serve?"

"You will tell us about the petition from Lowmarsh."

"I copy what the petitioners send. I don't write them."

"The petition is genuine. The *answer* is the question." Maren's eyes were pale and flat as river stones. "A well does not turn sweet because a widow asks it to. Unless someone arranged it. Unless someone is manufacturing miracles to—" she paused, savoring the word, "—*destabilize* the faithful."

"Destabilize," Vesper repeated.

"There have been other letters. An orchard outside Corren's Ford, flowering out of season. A fever breaking in a village that had already buried half its children." Callow shifted behind her, hugging his ledger to his chest. "All of these petitions passed through this scriptorium. Through your hands."

Through my hands. Yes. And through the hands of six other scribes, but Vesper didn't say that. Saying things in front of elders was how debts got longer.

"I copy forty prayers a night," Vesper said. "I could not forge an answer if I tried. What would I do—bribe an orchard?"

Someone behind Maren almost smiled. Maren did not.

"You were bound to us under the Halloran debt," she said. "Your father borrowed heavily in his last years. Gambling, we understand. He pledged his estate and, when that proved insufficient, his heir's labor. You signed the ledger yourself. You understood then, I trust, that fraudulent conduct extends the term of service?"

Vesper's hands went still on the desk. They remembered signing. They remembered the vault, the black iron door, the smell of tallow and old paper, their father standing beside them not meeting their eyes. Nineteen years old, holding a pen that weighed more than a sword.

"I remember," Vesper said quietly.

"Then you will understand that we must search your quarters."

* * *

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find. The page with the blooming orchard—the first one, the true beginning—had spent three nights folded against Vesper's ribs, and now lived inside the false spine of the hymnal, pressed between a psalm of lamentation and a psalm of thanksgiving, which struck Vesper as darkly funny whenever they let themselves think about it.

But the search itself told Vesper something important: the elders were afraid. Not angry—afraid. Fear made institutions cruel, and cruelty made mistakes, and mistakes made openings. Vesper catalogued all of this the way they catalogued everything, in the cold tidy ledger of the mind where they kept the things they couldn't say aloud.

Because here was the other thing the elders didn't know, the thing Vesper had never written anywhere: Vesper Halloran knew every smuggler's road in Thornfell.

Not from books. From before. From the years when their father's gambling debts had a different creditor—not the temple but the Ferrow brothers, who ran goods down the mountain passes and needed someone small and quick and unremarkable to carry messages between waystations. Vesper had been fourteen the first time they'd walked the Hollow Steps at night, the switchback trail carved into the mountainside that no map marked because no mapmaker had survived claiming it. Fifteen when they'd learned which river fords froze hard enough to bear a mule and which ones only looked frozen. Sixteen when they'd memorized the door-knock of every safehouse from Thornfell city to the Gray Teeth, and the names of the people behind those doors, and which of those people could be bought and which could only be trusted, which was a shorter list.

Their father had sold them twice over, in the end. First to the Ferrows as a courier, then to the temple as collateral. The Ferrows at least had paid him for the privilege.

So when the elders searched Vesper's cell and found nothing, when they issued a formal warning and doubled the watch on the scriptorium, Vesper nodded and apologized and thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy: *I know thirty-one ways out of this building, and two hundred out of this country, and you have just taught me which of them you fear.*

The resentment wasn't new. It was older than the answered prayers, older than the orchard. It was a stone Vesper had swallowed at nineteen and carried ever since, smooth from handling. But lately the stone had begun to heat.

Because the prayers were being answered. *Their* prayers—the ones Vesper had copied, line after line, in a hand so careful it looked printed. Somewhere in the dark beneath Thornfell, something had woken up and started reading forty thousand letters addressed to it, and it had apparently decided that the scribe's handwriting mattered. That the scribe mattered.

Vesper had spent nine years learning to be invisible. Now something vast and dying was looking directly at them, and part of them—shameful, traitorous, warm—wanted to be seen.

Most of them wanted to run.

* * *

On the sixth night after the search, another answer surfaced, and this time it couldn't be hidden.

A petitioner from the fishing villages had asked the old god of the deep water—Ossa, whose name hadn't been spoken aloud in the temple in a generation—to calm the strait before the herring season. The letter arrived with the morning post, routine, one of forty. Vesper copied it along with the rest, and thought nothing of it until midday, when the bells of the harbor district began ringing wrong. Not alarm-ringing. *Celebration*-ringing.

The strait had gone flat overnight. Glass-flat, the fishermen said, in the teeth of a storm season. Fish had come up to the boats in numbers no one living had seen. Old men were weeping on the docks. Someone had painted Ossa's sigil—the coiled eel—on the harbor shrine, and by afternoon there were hundreds of pilgrims kneeling in front of it.

By evening there were thousands.

Vesper stood at the scriptorium window and watched the torchlit procession wind up the temple hill, ordinary people carrying candles they'd stopped believing in, singing hymns the temple hadn't taught in decades. The words drifted up through the glass, ragged and joyful, and Vesper recognized every one of them, because Vesper had copied every one of them, forty times, eighty times, in the long dead years.

*We light the flame that sleeps. We wake the ember that endures.*

Behind them, the scriptorium door opened. Maren stood there with Callow and two temple guards, and her face had gone past fear into something colder.

"The strait," she said.

"Yes, First Elder."

"Four answered prayers in eleven days. All passing through your desk." She stepped into the room. The guards flanked her. "The council has voted. Your quarters will be sealed. Your movements restricted to this room and the chapel. Until we determine how these frauds are being committed, you are effectively—"

"Increased custody," Vesper said. "I know the term. It's in the debt code. Section twelve."

Something flickered across Maren's face—surprise, maybe, that the scribe knew the instrument of their own captivity so intimately. Nine years of reading fine print will do that.

"You understand," Maren said, softer now, almost gentle, which was worse, "that if you have done nothing wrong, you have nothing to fear."

"I understand," Vesper said, "that the temple sold candles to people praying to dead gods for nine years, and now that the gods might be alive, the temple's first instinct is to lock up the mail."

The silence that followed had weight. Callow's mouth opened and closed. One of the guards stared very hard at the floor.

Maren's voice, when it came, was quiet enough to be kind and sharp enough to draw blood. "Section twelve also permits extended terms for scribes who incite unrest. Think carefully, Halloran, about which side of the ledger you wish to stand on."

She left. The guards remained—one inside the door, one in the corridor—and Vesper returned to their desk as if nothing had happened, because that was the discipline of nine years: return to the desk. Pick up the pen. Be invisible.

There were forty new petitions waiting in the basket. Vesper worked through them mechanically, and it wasn't until the twelfth page that their hand stopped moving.

The petition was written in a child's uneven hand. *Please. Something is burning under our farm and nobody believes me. My little brother says he can hear it talking. Please send help or send somebody who listens.*

Beneath it, in the space where answers were supposed to go, where the temple stamped RECEIVED and filed the paper away into darkness—someone had already written a reply. Not in any scribe's hand. The ink was the color of embers, and it moved slightly, the way heat moves above a fire, and it said:

*Send the one who writes.*

Vesper sat very still. The guard by the door shifted, bored. Outside, the pilgrims were still singing.

*Send the one who writes.*

Vesper looked at the guard. Looked at the window, three floors up, overlooking the chapel roof and beyond it the terraced gardens and beyond them the dark shoulder of the mountain, riddled with roads no map had ever dared to carry.

Thirty-one ways out of the building. Two hundred out of the country.

One prayer, finally, addressed to them.

Vesper reached for the false spine of the hymnal, and began to pack.
