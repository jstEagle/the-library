## 3. What the Mountain Remembers

The temple paid in silver, which was the first insult. Sera Aldane had once commanded a garrison of forty and eaten from the Warden's own table, and now a soft-handed elder was counting coins into her palm like she was buying fish.

"Three hundred on capture," he said. "Five hundred if the heretic is delivered alive. The scribe has stolen consecrated texts."

"Stolen," Sera repeated. "Or copied."

The elder's smile thinned. "You come recommended, Dame Aldane. Or should I say *Ser* Aldane." He let the wrong honorific sit there between them, deliberate as a knife laid on a table. "We were told you have no loyalties left to compromise you."

What he meant was: no rank, no order, no god. What he meant was: you are nothing, and nothing can be bought cheap.

Sera took the silver. "I'll need the scribe's description."

"Unremarkable. Brown hair, brown eyes. The kind of face you forget while looking at it." He said it with something like admiration. "Nine years in our scriptorium and half the brothers couldn't pick them out of a crowd. That, I think, is what frightens us most. Something invisible learned to answer prayers."

She rode out of Thornfell City at dusk with the temple's writ folded against her breastbone and the taste of old ash in her mouth. It always tasted that way lately. Since Harrow Shrine. Since she'd stood in front of a country chapel with a torch in her hand and forty soldiers at her back and said *no* — one word, one refusal, nine years of service burned down to a stripped cloak and a name people only used when they wanted to spit.

They'd given her the torch because the shrine was small and the village poor and nobody important would care. That was the arithmetic of the new faith: burn what cannot fight back, call it cleansing. She had looked at the little shrine — its stone worn smooth by generations of hands, its offering bowl full of wildflowers someone had picked that very morning — and understood that she could light it or she could stop being whatever she had been.

She'd thought stopping would feel like falling. It had felt more like setting down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Now she carried a different weight. A writ. A hunt. And the same question following her up the mountain roads like a dog that wouldn't be called off: *what exactly are you hunting?*

* * *

She found the blooming valley on the third day, and it stopped her horse mid-stride.

It shouldn't have existed. She knew these mountains — had patrolled them for a decade, had wintered in their passes, had watched orchards die one by one as the blight crept up from the lowlands. This valley above Kettle Row had been dead six years. She remembered it dead. Gray branches, gray soil, a gray silence where even birds wouldn't land.

Now it was white and pink and gold to the ridgeline. Blossoms so thick they bent the boughs. Fruit already swelling where fruit had no business being, weeks out of season, years out of reason. Bees droned in the warm air. The smell was so sweet it hurt.

Sera sat her horse at the valley's edge for a long time.

Knights were taught not to believe their eyes first. Eyes could be glamoured; wards could deceive. But this wasn't glamour. Glamour slid off the mind like water off oil. This *rooted*. She dismounted, walked to the nearest tree, and put her hand against the bark, and felt it — the slow green pulse of living wood, deep and patient, the heartbeat of a thing that had been dead and was not anymore.

There were old stories about this. Her grandmother had told them, back before the temple's reforms, back when farmwives still left cream out for things with names. *When the gods answer truly,* her grandmother said, *the land remembers what it was.* Not miracles in the sky. Not burning bushes. Just the world remembering itself, suddenly, all at once, like a man waking from a long illness.

Someone had prayed here. Someone had copied a prayer so true that the mountain had heard it through nine years of dust and ritual and institutional forgetting — and answered.

And the temple wanted that person dragged back in chains for it.

Sera stood in the blossoming valley with the writ crackling in her pocket and felt duty curdle in her stomach, sour and familiar. She knew this feeling. She'd felt it at Harrow Shrine, standing before the offering bowl. It was the feeling of an order arriving that your soul had already refused.

She found Vesper Halloran that evening, though perhaps *found* was generous. The scribe was terrible at escape. Sera tracked them by their campfire smoke, by their too-careful choice of hidden hollows, by the way they moved like a person who knew every smuggler's path through Thornfell but hadn't walked them in years and kept second-guessing their feet. When she finally crested the ridge above their camp, she could have taken them three times over before they finished their supper.

She didn't. She watched instead.

Vesper sat cross-legged by a small fire, eating hard bread with the mechanical patience of someone who had long ago stopped expecting meals to be pleasant. Unremarkable, the elder had said. Brown hair, brown eyes. But watching them now, Sera saw what the elder had missed — or feared. There was ink on Vesper's fingers, stained deep into the whorls, and every few moments they glanced at the eastern ridge as if listening for something. Not afraid. *Listening.* Like a person waiting for a reply to a letter they weren't sure they'd sent.

On the bedroll beside them lay a leather folio. Pages peeked out of it, covered in careful scriptorium hand, and around the edges of the nearest page — Sera squinted against the firelight — faint lines of gold shimmered and faded, like embers breathing.

Answered prayers. Written in a dead man's hand, in a temple that had stopped believing, and answered anyway.

Vesper looked up. Straight at her.

Sera had tracked smugglers, deserters, and one memorable serial poisoner across these mountains, and none of them had ever caught her watching. But Vesper Halloran looked directly into the dark where she stood and said, quietly, without much surprise:

"You've been behind me since the ford. Are you going to sit there all night, or will you share the fire?"

Sera stepped into the light. Kept her hand loose near her sword, mostly out of habit. "Ser Aldane. I'm looking for a heretic scribe."

"I know. The temple posts notices in every town between here and the coast." Vesper turned back to their fire. "You're the knight who wouldn't burn Harrow Shrine. The notices mention it. They think it makes you trustworthy — a knight who respects holy places." A pause. "Do they know how wrong they are?"

"They suspect."

"Then sit." Vesper gestured at the fire with their bread. "If you're going to arrest me, eat first. It's bad luck to take a prisoner hungry. Old scriptorium rule."

It was absurd. It was the least tactical thing Sera had ever done. She sat.

Over the fire, Vesper talked — not confessing, exactly, but laying things out the way a merchant lays out cloth, letting her see the weave. Nine years copying prayers for a debt that wasn't theirs. A ledger with their family's name in it, page after page of interest calculated by men who never went hungry. And then the night the orchard bloomed. The elders' faces. The word *forgery* thrown around like a weapon, because the alternative — that the old gods were waking, that a copyist's pen had done what a century of temple tithing hadn't — was unthinkable.

"They don't want me for theft," Vesper said. "They want me because I'm proof their whole institution is a locked door with nothing behind it. If the gods answer a copyist and ignore a high altar, what exactly has everyone been paying for?" They fed a stick to the fire. "I didn't ask for any of this. I just wrote what was written. I just wanted to pay my debt and disappear."

"And now?"

"Now the mountain won't let me." Vesper said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "Every road I take, something blooms. Every prayer I copy comes true somewhere downstream. I'm trying to disappear, Ser Aldane. It's the one thing I've always been good at. And the gods keep—" their voice caught, just slightly, "—keep *answering*."

Sera looked at the folio. At the gold-edged pages. At the person who had spent nine years making themselves invisible and was now, apparently, the loudest voice in Thornfell whether they liked it or not.

She thought about Harrow Shrine. About the offering bowl full of fresh flowers, and the old woman who'd tended them, who'd told Sera afterward — while the soldiers argued and the torch guttered unlit — *"Thank you, daughter. Someone still remembers us."*

Duty. She'd built her whole life on the word, and the temple had taught her what it meant: obey upward, burn downward, never ask what burns. She'd refused once, at the cost of everything, and everyone had called it disgrace.

But sitting here, watching a hunted scribe feed a small fire in a valley that had come back from the dead, Sera Aldane understood that she had been disgraced in exactly the right direction.

She reached into her coat. Vesper tensed. She drew out the writ — the temple's seal, the bounty, the word *heretic* in beautiful official ink — and held it over the fire.

"Don't," Vesper said quickly. "That's evidence. If anyone finds you without—"

"If anyone finds me with it, I'm the woman who dragged an innocent scribe back to men who burn shrines." Sera let the corner catch. The paper flared, curled, and rose as ash into the dark. Five hundred silver, gone in three seconds. It felt better than any coin had ever felt. "I'm not taking you back."

Vesper stared at her. In the firelight, for the first time, they looked less invisible — eyes wide, guard down, a person rather than a rumor. "Why?"

Because the mountain remembers, Sera almost said. Because I stood in a dead orchard today and felt it breathe. Because I've spent two years learning the difference between an order and a lie, and that writ was both.

What she said was: "Because whoever's answering those prayers chose you. And I've decided I'd rather be on the side of the choosing than the side of the chain."

They sat in silence while the last of the writ's ash drifted over the blossoms below. Then Vesper said, carefully, "You can't follow me. Where I'm going, the temple can't reach — and neither can anything safe. The old roads. The ones I swore I'd never walk again."

"Good," Sera said, and began unbuckling her saddle to make camp. "I've been walking safe roads my whole life. Look where they got me."

Above them, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, something flickered — brief and deep and red, like a coal turning over beneath the earth. Vesper saw it too. Sera watched their face change, watched hope and terror arrive together.

"That's not a campfire," Vesper whispered.

"No," Sera agreed, tightening the last strap. "I don't think it is."

By morning they were on the old smuggler roads together, two disgraced people and a stolen folio of answered prayers, climbing toward a light that was burning under the world — and behind them, far down the valley, Sera heard dogs. Many dogs. And the thin, carrying note of a temple horn.

The hunt had not ended with her refusal. It had only changed quarry.
