## 4. The Child Who Hears Fire

The smuggler roads of Thornfell were not roads at all. That was the point.

Vesper had learned them at nineteen, ledger-bound and furious, walking behind men who measured distance in bribes and silence. There was the Hollow Way beneath the old drovers' bridge, where the river ran shallow enough to wade and the toll-wardens never looked down. The Cinder Track through the burned orchards, where the ash still held heat in winter and no one asked why. And the Stair—forty-one steps cut into the cliffside below Marrowgate, hidden by a curtain of ivy that only parted for people who already knew it was there.

Vesper knew all of them. Had sworn, three years ago, to forget every one.

Now they walked the Cinder Track with a stolen page folded against their heart and Sera Aldane's silhouette moving ahead of them like a blade being drawn slowly from its sheath.

"You're limping," Vesper said.

"I'm favoring the leg that has an excuse," Sera said. "There's a difference."

The valley behind them had not stopped blooming. Vesper could feel it the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue—the orchard's blossoms pressing outward somewhere south of them, petals opening in defiance of season, of sense, of everything the temple had taught Vesper to copy without believing. Every hour they put between themselves and Thornfell City, the elders would be composing accusations. *Forgery. Heresy. The scribe Halloran has defiled the canon.*

They hadn't defiled anything. They'd copied a prayer. That was the whole horror of it: Vesper had done their job perfectly, and the world had answered.

"Stop," Sera said.

Vesper stopped. Ahead, where the Cinder Track bent around a collapsed kiln, smoke rose—not hearth-smoke, not cookfire. It moved wrong, too straight, too deliberate. A signal fire.

"Smugglers?" Vesper whispered.

"Smugglers don't signal." Sera's hand went to the sword she wasn't supposed to carry anymore. "Bounty men do."

Vesper's stomach dropped through nine years of careful work. Of course. The temple wouldn't send inquisitors first; inquisitors were expensive and slow. They'd post a bounty, and the bounty would travel down the same hidden veins Vester had once helped map, because who else knew the country's secret arteries but the people who'd bled them?

"We go around," Vesper said. "There's a cut through the kiln field—"

The humming started then.

It came from inside the ruined kiln itself, thin and off-key, a child's tune with no words. Sera looked at Vesper. Vesper looked at the kiln. The smoke above the bounty camp bent sideways, toward the sound, as if listening.

Then the cold iron ring of the kiln's broken door began to glow.

Not red-hot. Not burning. Just *lit*, the way coals hold light after the fire's gone—a dull amber waking in metal that should have been stone-cold for years. The humming swelled, and frost that had crusted the kiln's mouth since autumn slid off in a hiss of steam.

Sera drew her sword. Vesper grabbed her wrist.

"It's a child," they said.

"It's a signal fire with a voice."

"It's a child." Vesper heard their own voice go flat and certain, a tone they didn't recognize. Nine years of invisibility, and here was the first thing they'd ever been sure of out loud. "Whatever she is, she's eight years old and alone in a kiln, and there are six armed men forty yards away deciding whether to follow the smoke or the singing. We're not leaving her."

Sera held their eyes for a long moment. Then she sheathed the sword. "You've changed," she said, almost accusingly.

"I've been changed *at*," Vesper said. "Come on."

* * *

Her name was Pip, though she wouldn't say if it was short for anything, and she was wedged into the kiln's ash chamber with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around something wrapped in rag—a loaf, Vesper realized later, half-burned, that she'd been trying to warm.

She didn't cry when they pulled her out. She looked at Vesper's temple-gray sleeves and flinched like she'd been struck.

"You're one of them," she said. "The ink-men. They took Doss."

"Who took Doss?"

"The ink-men. He hummed too. Not like me. Worse." She hugged the rag-wrapped loaf tighter. "They put him in a wagon with bars and took him north. Then they came back for me because I lit the inn's hearth without touching it. I didn't mean to. It just *listens* to me."

Vesper crouched, putting their face level with hers. Up close, they could see it—or thought they could—a faint warmth under her skin, like light behind paper. Like a page held too close to a candle.

"What are you?" Vesper asked softly.

Pip considered this with the gravity of someone negotiating terms. "Warm," she said finally.

"She's more than warm," Sera murmured. She had gone very still, the way she did before saying something true. "I served at the Shrine of the First Flame before they stripped me. I saw the relic there—the scale, the ember they keep in glass. It flickered like that. Like something alive pretending to be dead." She looked at Pip with something close to grief. "Child, do you know what a dragon is?"

"Doss said the last one lives under the mountain and it's dying, and when it dies the gods die too, and everyone says that's a story." Pip shrugged. "But the fire in me doesn't think it's a story. The fire in me is *sad* all the time."

Vesper felt the folded page against their chest grow suddenly heavy. The prayer they'd copied. The orchard that had bloomed. The gods, answering—and now this small sad spark walking the same road, hunted by the same men.

The pieces weren't fitting together yet. But they were starting to lean toward each other, like iron filings sensing a magnet.

"Pip," Vesper said. "Those men at the signal fire. Are they looking for you specifically?"

"They're looking for 'the ember-child.' There's a drawing." She produced it from her shirt with the pride of a professional: a charcoal sketch of a small figure wreathed in flames, rendered by someone who had clearly never seen either. Beneath it: *REWARD — ALIVE. THE QUIET FLAME PAYS IN GOLD.*

The Quiet Flame. Vesper's own temple. Their own elders, spending tithe-money on bounty sketches.

Something in Vesper's chest, some last patient clerkish thing that had spent nine years saying *yes, elder; of course, elder; the copies will be ready by morning, elder*—that thing stood up, brushed off its robes, and resigned.

"Right," Vesper said. "We're taking the Stair."

* * *

The Stair was worse than Vesper remembered. Forty-one steps cut for smugglers' boots, not for a child's legs or a knight's bad knee, and night came down the cliffside like poured ink. Vesper carried Pip on their back, her chin hooked over their shoulder, her humming—soft, unconscious, comforting herself—making the ivy curtain glow faintly green where they passed through it.

"That song," Vesper said quietly, halfway up. "Where did you learn it?"

"Nowhere. It was already in my head when they found me in the ash-field. Doss said maybe I was left there because I'm a dragon egg." She snorted. "I told him dragon eggs aren't this small. Also I'd have hatched by now."

"And what does the fire say? When it's sad?"

Pip was quiet for three steps. Four. Five.

"It says *soon*," she whispered. "Like it's counting down. Like someone's holding their breath under the mountain and they can't hold it much longer." Her arms tightened around Vesper's neck. "Mister scribe. Am I going to be the reason everyone dies?"

The question hit Vesper like a fall. Nine years old, maybe ten, carrying a dying god's countdown in her chest and asking a debt-bound copyist whether she was the end of the world.

"No," Vesper said, and meant it with everything they had. "You're going to be the reason we *hurry*."

Below them, torchlight bloomed at the base of the cliff. Voices. Six men, maybe seven, and one of them shouting up at the dark with the confidence of someone paid by the hour:

"HALLORAN! The temple knows the Stair, scribe! You showed it to us yourself, remember? Winter before last, for a cut of the toll!"

Vesper remembered. That was the worst part. They remembered every road they'd ever sold, every path they'd sworn never to walk again and then walked anyway, because the debt ledger didn't care about oaths.

"They know the Stair," Sera said grimly, drawing steel at last. "How many ways off this cliff?"

"One," Vesper said. "And it's the one I swore I'd never use twice."

"Which is?"

"The Kiln Road. Through the mountain's throat." Vesper shifted Pip higher on their back and looked up, into the black seam in the rockface that even the smugglers called cursed. "Hold my shoulders. Both of you. And whatever breathes on us in there—"

"Don't apologize to it," Sera said.

"*Don't name it,*" Vesper corrected. "Names are how it collects."

They stepped into the mountain's throat together, torchlight dying behind them, a child's ember glowing faintly ahead like a lantern that had opinions about where they were going—and far below, muffled by stone, the bounty hunters began to climb.
