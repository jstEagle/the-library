## 5. A Country That No Longer Exists

**Vesper**

The map said nothing was there.

Vesper had copied that map. Three years ago, a junior cartographer's commission, forty identical sheets of the northern reaches of Thornfell, and where the mountains folded into the high pass the ink simply stopped—a clean white space with the cartographer's neat notation: *uninhabited. rockfall. no water.*

Vesper had written those words themselves. Had believed them, or at least never thought to question them, because questioning was not part of the work. The work was copying. The work was silence.

Now they stood on a ridge above that white space and looked down at a city.

It filled the valley like a cupped hand holding light—towers of pale stone, terraced gardens gone wild, streets laid out in spirals that followed some logic older than any Vesper knew. No smoke rose from its chimneys, but windows glowed. Not lamplight. Something warmer, more uncertain, like coals breathing under ash.

"That's not possible," Sera said beside them. She'd said it twice already, once at the tree line and once at the river crossing, and each time the city had failed to stop existing.

"The wards," Pip said. She sat between them on the cold grass, her small hands folded in her lap the way she did when she was listening to something far away. "They're like a blanket pulled over it. But it's got holes now." She pointed with her chin. "See? There. And there. The holes are getting bigger."

Vesper saw nothing but evening haze. They were learning to trust the girl anyway.

"Oldfall," they said quietly. The name came up out of their childhood like a stone from a well—some grandmother's story, some forbidden rhyme. *Oldfall the forgotten, Oldfall the unforgiven.* A place mothers threatened children with. A place that wasn't real.

"It's real," Sera said. Her hand rested on the sword she hadn't worn since Thornhollow; she carried it wrapped in sacking now, slung across her back like something shameful. "My order's oldest records mention it. One line. 'The first city, struck from every ledger.' We were told it was allegory."

"And you believed that?"

"I believed everything," Sera said, "right up until an orchard bloomed in winter because a tired scribe wrote a prayer too honestly." She looked at Vesper then—not accusing, not quite. Measuring. "You've known about this place longer than either of us. You just didn't know you knew."

The words landed somewhere tender. Nine years of copying prayers for gods the temple had declared decorative. Nine years of ink and silence, and all along the silence had been full.

Pip stood, tugging Vesper's sleeve. "They're waiting," she said. "The broken ones. They don't like waiting anymore. It hurts them."

*The broken ones.* Vesper's mouth went dry. "We should go down before dark."

They went down through streets that remembered being grand. Moss climbed statues whose faces had been deliberately chiseled smooth—every one of them, hundreds, erased by patient hands. Fountains ran dry except one, at the center of a great spiral plaza, where water still moved though Vesper could not see the source. The air smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

At the plaza's heart stood a temple. Smaller than the Temple of the Quiet Flame. Humble. Its doors were open.

Inside, there were no pews. No altar. Just a circle of stones on the floor, and standing among them—

Vesper's knees nearly buckled.

There were eleven figures in the circle, and none of them were whole. One was only a voice, a shimmer in the air that spoke without a mouth. One was a pair of hands, floating, weathered as old bark. One was a woman to the waist who faded below like smoke. Another was a child made of river-water, flickering. They stood in various stages of dissolution, fragments wearing the shapes of what they used to be, and when they turned toward the doorway all at once, the pressure of their attention was like standing under a waterfall.

"You came," said the voice. It sounded relieved. It sounded exhausted. "Nine years we whispered through your pen, little scribe. Nine years, and finally you walked instead of wrote."

"I didn't—" Vesper's voice cracked. They steadied themselves against the doorframe. "I copied what I was given. I didn't know anyone was listening. I didn't know anyone was *there*."

"We were always there." The hands drifted closer, palm-up, an old gesture of offering. "Starving. But there."

Sera stepped forward, putting herself slightly ahead of Vesper—an instinct, Vesper realized, that she probably didn't even notice anymore. "You're the old gods. The ones the temple says are dead."

"We say it too, sometimes," said the river-child sadly. "It's easier than saying *fading*."

"Why am I here?" Pip asked, blunt as only children can be. "Why do I hear fire?"

The circle went still. Even the shimmer stilled.

"Show her," said the voice, to the others. "She should hear it from us, not from the mountain."

And then they showed her—all three of them at once, the vision pouring into the room like floodwater—and Vesper understood, in one staggering instant, why the temple feared answered prayers, why the wards were failing, why the last dragon mattered.

Because beneath Thornfell, in a vault of stone older than the country itself, something enormous was dying. And everything above it was dying with it.

* * *

**Sera**

Sera had knelt before altars since she was six years old. She knew the grammar of worship—the bow, the recitation, the proper posture of awe. None of it prepared her for the way the fragments of gods arranged themselves around a dying dragon's memory like mourners around a bed.

The vision broke, and she found herself gripping Vesper's arm harder than she meant to. She let go. Vesper didn't step away. Small mercies.

"So explain it plainly," Sera said, because someone had to, and grief made Vesper quiet and made Pip go strange and glassy-eyed. "Plainly. Like a report."

The hands—the god who called himself Marrow-of-the-Hill, though his hill had been quarried away centuries ago—drifted to the center of the circle. "Plainly, then, knight-who-wouldn't-burn."

He knew. Of course he knew. Sera's jaw tightened and she let it go.

"There is a dragon beneath your country. The last. She has burned beneath Thornfell since before Thornfell had a name, and her fire is the root of every ward, every blessing, every wall that keeps the hungry dark of the deep places out. Your temple prays to a Quiet Flame. She is the flame. She is going out."

"How long?" Vesper asked.

"Seasons. Perhaps fewer. She has begun to dream her last dreams, and her dreams leak." The voice-god rippled. "You have seen what leaks. Blooming orchards. Children who hum fire."

Pip hugged her own arms. Sera wanted to pick her up and couldn't figure out how to do it without making it worse.

"When she dies," Marrow continued, "we die. All of us, whatever is left. And the wards die hours later. And what lives in the deep places—" he paused, and the pause itself was terrible, "—has waited a very long time for the walls to come down."

"The Ember Door," Vesper said. "You brought us here for the Ember Door."

"Not an exit," said the river-child, and her voice was young and infinitely weary. "Everyone always hopes it's an exit."

"It is the last gate," Marrow said. "Beneath this city, beneath the mountain's root. It opens onto the dragon's vault. Through it, a mortal could reach her. Speak to her. Perhaps—" the hands spread wide, helpless, an ancient gesture of surrender, "—perhaps rekindle her. We cannot. We are too thin. Prayer needs flesh to carry it, little scribe. Prayer needs lungs and a tongue and a heart that can break."

The silence stretched. Sera heard her own pulse.

"You want Vesper to walk through," she said flatly. "Into a dying dragon's vault. Alone."

"We want Thornfell to survive," said the voice-god, gently. "What we want for ourselves stopped mattering long ago."

"No." Sera stepped forward. "No. Find another way. You're *gods*. You've had nine years of answered prayers—"

"Nine years of one scribe's honest ink," Marrow corrected, "and it nearly unmade us with effort. Knight. Look at us. We are not gods anymore. We are the memory of gods, arguing over scraps of belief." His voice roughened, and for a moment Sera heard the hill in him, vast and buried. "But we are enough to open a door. And the child is enough to carry a spark. And the scribe is enough to speak. That is the whole of our army."

Sera laughed, and it came out ugly. "An army of a disgraced knight, a debt-bound scribe, and a seven-year-old."

"Eight," Pip said.

Everyone looked at her.

"I'm eight," she said, with dignity. "And I'm not scared of the fire. The fire asked me to come."

Sera closed her eyes. When she opened them, Vesper was watching her—not the gods, not the door, her. Waiting, she realized, for her to say it was madness. For permission to refuse.

Instead what came out of her mouth was: "Nobody decides tonight. Nobody walks anywhere tonight. We eat, we sleep, and tomorrow we look at this Door with clear eyes." She turned to the fragments of divinity in the ruined temple and lifted her chin the way she had once lifted it before generals. "That's not a refusal. It's tactics."

Marrow's floating hands made a slow gesture that might have been amusement. "Very well, knight. Tactics." He drifted toward the plaza, gesturing them after him. "We will feed you what food remains in a city that no longer exists. And while you sleep, consider this: the temple has noticed where its heretic has gone. Inquisitors move slowly, but they move. And they are not the only ones hunting you."

Outside, the false stars behind the failing wards flickered—once, twice—like a lamp running low on oil.

Above the valley, far off in the true dark, something answered the flicker with a sound that was almost a bell and almost a scream.

Pip grabbed Vesper's hand. "They heard us," she whispered. "The things in the deep. They heard the door open."

Vesper's fingers tightened around hers, and Sera saw it plainly: the scribe who had spent nine years learning to be invisible had stopped being invisible the moment they chose to hold on.

"They'll have to get through us first," Vesper said.

It wasn't much of an army. But it would have to do.
