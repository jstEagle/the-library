## 6. Terms of Surrender

Sera Aldane had learned, in the years since her disgrace, to read a room by its silences. The hall beneath Oldfall was the quietest she had ever stood in—and it was not empty. It was full.

The gods of Thornfell did not appear as she'd imagined them from childhood catechisms. No towering figures wreathed in glory. Instead they were absences shaped like presence: a hollow where warmth should be, a doorway that opened onto weather that wasn't there, a column of dust motes turning slowly against their own fall. Nine of them. Maybe fewer. It was hard to count things that kept forgetting themselves.

They had gathered around Vesper like moths around a candle that hadn't been lit yet.

"The terms are simple," said the one that spoke through the doorway. Its voice came out as wind through a keyhole. "The last dragon dies within the season. When its flame goes out, we go with it. When we go, the wards go. When the wards go—" it paused, and Sera felt the pause like a hand pressing down on her chest, "—Thornfell becomes a country that no longer exists. Not conquered. Not burned. Simply *unmade*. Every border forgotten. Every name unwritten."

"But there is another way," said the dust-mote god, and its voice was almost gentle. "A herald. A living voice. Prayer made flesh instead of ink. Through such a vessel, the flame could be carried past its own death. Rekindled."

Sera looked at Vesper.

Vesper stood very still in the center of the gathering, hands at their sides, the way they stood when copying prayers—spine straight, expression smoothed into nothing. But Sera had spent three weeks on smuggler roads with them now. She knew what lived under that stillness. She knew the way Vesper's thumb pressed white against their forefinger when they were afraid and refusing to show it.

"And the cost," Vesper said. Their voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse.

"You would be consumed," the doorway-god said. "Slowly. A voice used is a voice worn. Years, perhaps a decade. You would speak for us until there was nothing left of you but the speaking."

The silence after that had weight. Pip, sitting on a fallen lintel with her knees drawn up, had stopped humming. That frightened Sera more than anything—the child always hummed.

"No," Sera said.

Every fragment of divinity in the room turned toward her. It felt like being looked at by weather.

"This isn't your bargain to refuse," said the dust-mote god, not unkindly.

"Then I'm refusing it anyway." She stepped forward, boots loud on stone that should have echoed and didn't. "I've heard this speech before. Different words, same shape. *Give yourself to the duty. The cause is greater than the one who serves it.* I wore a knight's oath for eleven years. I know exactly how that story ends."

"Ser Aldane." The doorway-god's tone cooled. "You were stripped of your rank for refusing a lawful order."

"I was stripped of my rank for refusing to burn a shrine full of people who'd done nothing but believe wrong." Her voice came out harder than she intended. Good. Let it be hard. "And you want to know what happened after? I'll tell you what happened after, since your kind seems so fond of terms."

She hadn't told this story on the road. Vesper had never asked; that was one of the thousand small reasons Sera had started—

Well. One of the reasons.

"I had a command once. Forty riders, a garrison at Caelwatch, a reputation. And an order came down from the Temple: clear the valley shrine at Marrow Ford. The worshippers there had turned to the old gods when the Quiet Flame stopped answering. Heretics, the decree said. I rode out to do it, because that's what duty is—you don't get to choose which orders are clean." She swallowed. "I stood at the edge of that village with torches behind me and forty men waiting, and I looked at the shrine—a little thing, cedar and river-stone, older than the temple that ordered it burned—and I understood that if I gave that order, I would keep everything. Rank, name, pension, honor guard at my funeral. And I would lose the only part of myself worth keeping."

"So you refused," Vesper said quietly.

"So I ran." The word tasted the way it always tasted. "That's the part the ballads leave out, if anyone ever sings this one. I didn't make a stand. I took my horse and I left in the night, and my lieutenant—Brannoc, twenty-six years old, believed every word I'd ever taught him—he gave the order in my place. Because someone had to, and his commander had abandoned him. The shrine burned. Eleven people died in it. Brannoc hanged himself the following spring." She made herself look at each absence in turn. "That is what abandoning duty costs. Not freedom. Just the transfer of the bill to someone poorer."

The gods were silent. Even the dust motes seemed to hang.

"And now you're asking them"—she pointed at Vesper, and her hand was not quite steady—"to pay a bill that isn't theirs. Again. They've already spent nine years paying a debt their family signed. You want to be the next creditor in line? You want to stand there, ancient and holy, and tell me this is different?"

"It is different," said a god Sera hadn't identified yet—a shape like frost forming on the inside of a window. "Because no one is forcing them."

"*No one is forcing them*," Sera repeated. "Do you hear yourselves? Do you remember what it was like, having bodies, having names? Nobody forces anyone. They just arrange the world so that refusal looks like murder."

"Sera." Vesper's voice. Low. Not angry—that was the terrible part. Gentle. "It's all right."

"It is not all right."

"It's all right that you're afraid." Vesper crossed the space between them, and up close Sera could see the exhaustion carved under their eyes, the ink stain on the side of their right hand that never quite washed out. "But don't make my choice for me. I spent nine years letting other people make my choices. The temple. The ledger. My family's name. I'm not going to start my freedom by handing it to someone else's fear—even yours. Especially yours."

Sera's jaw tightened. "You'd die for them. For gods who let a whole country forget them."

"I'd live *for* something. There's a difference, and I only just learned it." Vesper's mouth curved—not quite a smile, something rawer. "You think I don't know what consumption looks like? I was consumed for nine years, Sera. Slowly. Politely. On schedule. At least this time I'd be choosing what eats me."

"That's not—" Sera stopped. Started again. "That's not the argument I want to have."

"No?"

"No." And here it was, the thing she'd been carrying since the blooming orchard, since the night she'd let a fugitive scribe walk past her sword and then followed like a dog too honest to pretend otherwise. She said it plainly, because she had lost enough by lying to herself. "I don't want to argue you out of dying because the strategy is bad. I want to argue you out of it because I—" Her throat closed. She forced it open. "Because I love you. There. Said in front of gods, so it counts as sworn testimony."

Pip made a small sound on her lintel, half gasp, half delight.

Vesper stared at her. "Sera—"

"Don't." Sera held up a hand. "Don't say anything kind yet, because I'm not finished, and if you're gentle with me right now I will lose the thread entirely. Here is my actual argument, and it's the only one I have: I abandoned my duty once, and it cost a village and a boy who trusted me. So I understand the arithmetic of sacrifice better than most people alive. And I am telling you, as a woman who has done the math with blood—the answer is not to give yourself away. The answer is to find a third door. There is *always* a third door. We haven't found it because we've been running, and running narrows the field of view. So give me time. Give us time. Let me be the one who searches, because searching is the only duty I have left and I would rather die than fail it twice."

The frost-shaped god drifted closer. "There is no third door, knight."

"There was no third door at Marrow Ford either," Sera said. "And then I found one, and it was made of cowardice, and people died anyway. But I have gotten better at doors since."

Something that might have been laughter moved through the gathered absences—brief, rusted, like a bell rung after centuries of disuse.

"She argues like a herald already," murmured the dust-mote god.

"I argue like a soldier," Sera corrected. "Heralds announce. Soldiers hold ground."

Vesper was looking at her with an expression Sera had never seen on them before—unguarded, undone, the careful invisibility cracked wide open. "You love me," they said, as if testing whether the words held weight.

"Since approximately the second week," Sera said. "It's been extremely inconvenient."

A sound escaped Vesper—half laugh, half sob—and then they crossed the remaining distance and took Sera's face in their ink-stained hands, and kissed her, there in the hall of fading gods, brief and fierce and trembling. When they broke apart, Vesper rested their forehead against hers.

"A third door," Vesper whispered. "All right. We look for a third door. But Sera—if there isn't one—"

"If there isn't one," Sera said, "then I'll stand beside you at whatever door there is, and I will hate every second of it, and I will not let you go alone. That's my term of surrender."

Above them, deep in the bones of Oldfall, a sound began.

At first Sera thought it was the mountain settling. Then Pip was on her feet, humming gone rigid, eyes enormous and fixed on the ceiling.

"That's not the mountain," the child said. "That's bells. Temple bells. They found us."

The frost-shaped god's voice dropped to something ancient and cold: "The wards have been breached. Inquisitors. Many."

Sera's hand went to her sword out of pure habit, though steel had never once mattered against the Temple's long arm.

"How many," Vesper asked, "is many?"
