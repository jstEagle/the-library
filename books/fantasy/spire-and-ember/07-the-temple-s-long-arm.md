## 7. The Temple's Long Arm

The bells of Oldfall rang wrong.

Vesper had noticed it on the third morning, standing in the ruined colonnade where the gods kept their court. The city's bells were bronze gone green, cracked down the middle, and yet they rang—sound without source, a vibration in the teeth rather than the ear. Nine years in a temple taught you what bells were supposed to mean: hour, office, alarm.

These meant *hunger*.

"They know we're here," Sera said, coming up beside them with her sword wrapped in sacking, as if that made it less of a confession. "The Quiet Flame's people. They've been asking after a scribe and a knight in every village this side of the pass."

"Then they're wasting their questions." Vesper kept copying—old habit, a prayer to Ash-and-Seed for the greening of things, ink going down onto paper the gods had given them, paper that felt warm as skin. "Nobody remembers Oldfall. It's not on any map."

"Maps aren't how they found you before." Sera nodded toward Pip, who was sitting cross-legged on a fallen god's knee, teaching herself to whistle through cupped hands. Every note came out faintly orange. "It'll be her. A child who makes fire follow her around like a stray dog? Inquisitors can smell that from three provinces off."

Vesper's pen stopped.

They had thought about this, in the way you think about a stone in your boot—you acknowledge it, you keep walking. But hearing it said aloud, in Sera's flat soldier's voice, made it real in a way nine years of careful invisibility had trained them to dread. They had spent nearly a decade learning how not to be seen. And now everything they loved glowed.

"We move tonight," Vesper said. "The Ember Door is two days' climb. If we go up through the ash tunnels—"

"Vesper." Sera's voice did something gentle and terrible at once. "Look at her hands."

Pip's fingers were streaked with soot, and beneath the soot the skin had gone faintly luminous, like coals under ash. She'd been getting brighter for days. The dragon beneath Thornfell was dying, and its last spark—the one lodged in a seven-year-old's chest like a swallowed star—was burning faster to make up the difference.

"She doesn't have two days," Sera said. "Maybe not one."

* * *

They didn't make it out of the colonnade.

Vesper heard them first—not footsteps but the absence of them, the way Oldfall's ever-present whisper of wind went suddenly, deliberately quiet, as if the city itself were holding its breath. Then light. Lantern-light, white and merciless, pouring into the ruin from three directions at once.

"Inquisitors," Sera breathed, and moved—too late.

There were six of them, robed in the Flame's white and grey, and they moved like men who had done this many times and enjoyed it moderately. At their center walked a tall figure Vesper recognized with a sick lurch of the stomach: Inquisitor Corvane, who had signed the accusation of forgery against them, who had once held their copied prayers up to the light and called them *counterfeit devotions*. He looked exactly as he had across the scriptorium desk—patient, tidy, a man whose cruelty was administrative.

"Scribe Halloran." He said it like reading an inventory line. "And Ser Aldane. The temple thanks you for sparing us a search. We've been walking these ruins for two days, and I confess the dust disagrees with me."

Sera stepped in front of Pip. Vesper watched her do it—watched the disgraced knight put her body between a child and six armed servants of the institution that had stripped her rank—and felt something in their chest crack along an old fault line.

"Walk away, Corvane," Sera said. "You're standing in a dead god's house. Ask permission."

"Gods." Corvane smiled thinly. "Yes. About that. The temple has reviewed the evidence, Ser Aldane. The blooming valley. The answered petitions. We've concluded there is indeed something in Thornfell answering prayers." He turned his gaze to Vesper, and it was almost warm. Almost reasonable. "Which means the temple requires it. Faith is a resource, Scribe. Resources require management. You understand management. You copied our ledgers for nine years."

"You don't believe in them," Vesper said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Your whole temple. You stopped believing decades ago. That's why nothing answered until—"

"Until you." Corvane spread his hands. "Which makes you either a miracle or a key, and the temple has no doctrine for miracles. Keys, however, we have procedures for." His eyes slid past Vesper, past Sera, and settled on the small bright figure behind them. "And there it is. The spark. Do you know what the College would pay for—"

Pip bit him.

It happened fast—a flash of small hands and sharper teeth, and then Corvane staggering back with blood on his knuckles and fury cracking his administrative calm for the first time. One of the inquisitors raised a hand and spoke a word of binding, and the world went white and silent, and when Vesper could see again—

Pip was gone. Lifted, bound in light, slung over a robed shoulder like a sack of grain. Her humming still came through the binding, muffled, stubborn, and where her breath touched the inquisitor's robe, the fabric smoldered.

"No—" Vesper lunged and was caught, arms pinned, Sera shouting somewhere close, and Corvane leaned in so only they could hear.

"The Door, Scribe. Its location. You have until moonrise tomorrow. Deliver it to the shrine at the mountain's foot, alone, or we return the child to the College in Merrowgate. They have such interesting instruments there. Instruments designed for exactly what she is." He straightened his cuffs. "You of all people should appreciate a debt honestly stated. Terms. Deadline. Consideration. This is simply a ledger."

He left them kneeling in the dust of a dead god's colonnade, and the bells of Oldfall rang hunger, hunger, hunger.

* * *

The gods gathered around them like weather.

Not in bodies—they rarely managed bodies anymore—but as pressure and presence, warmth at Vesper's back, a smell of rain on old stone, a voice that arrived inside rather than through the ears. Ash-and-Seed. The Mother-of-Crossings. The nameless one who was only a feeling of doors.

*They will torture her,* the gods said, and grief made even their voices frayed. *Or worse. They will study her. They will take the spark out of her and she will be empty, and the dragon will die alone, and everything ends.*

"I know." Vesper sat on the fallen god's knee where Pip had sat, and their hands wouldn't stop shaking. "I know."

*Give them nothing. The Door must not—*

"Don't." Vesper's voice cracked like green wood. "Don't tell me what must not happen. Don't you dare. She is *seven years old.*"

Silence. Even gods, it seemed, knew when to stop talking.

Sera found them there an hour later, sitting in the dark with ink-stained hands and no plan. She sat down without asking, shoulder against theirs, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"If you give them the Door," she said finally, "the temple seals it. Or claims it. Or burns whatever's behind it out of spite. Everything dies anyway—slower, maybe, but just as sure."

"I know."

"And if you don't, they take her apart in Merrowgate looking for the flame."

"I *know*, Sera."

Sera was quiet a moment. "Then stop knowing and tell me what you're actually thinking."

Vesper stared at their hands. Nine years of copying other people's prayers. Nine years of being the instrument, the invisible one, the one who paid a family's debt in silence. And now the choice in front of them wasn't between duty and freedom—it was between betraying gods and betraying a child, and both options were written in someone else's ledger, in someone else's hand, and Vesper Halloran was so tired of living in other people's ledgers.

"I'm thinking," they said slowly, "that Corvane trusts his own cleverness. He thinks he's given me an impossible choice because he thinks I'm like him—that everything is negotiable if the price is right. He thinks I'll trade the Door because I'm a coward with a good excuse." They looked up. "So I'll trade it. I'll give him exactly what he asked for."

Sera went very still. "Vesper—"

"A location. The shrine at the mountain's foot, at moonrise. He'll get his trade." A thin, terrible smile. "But nobody said which door I have to describe. There are forty smuggler roads through that mountain, Sera. I guided men through them for six years. I know passages that don't exist on any temple map—passages that flood, that collapse, that open onto sheer drops. I'll give him the Ember Door's *name* and a route that delivers his inquisitors into the mountain's teeth." They exhaled. "I buy Pip back with a lie, and the gods keep their gate."

For a moment Sera said nothing. Then, quietly: "And if he checks the route against your old guides? Your old partners? Half the smugglers in Thornfell want your head already. Someone will sell him the truth for coin."

The smile died on Vesper's face.

Because that was the flaw, wasn't it. The beautiful, terrible flaw. The bounty on their head wasn't just temple coin—it was smuggler coin too, and every road Vesper knew, every secret passage, every trick of the mountain, was known by someone else who had once called them friend. Corvane didn't need Vesper's honesty. He needed only enough rope, and Vesper's own history would provide the rest.

"Then I go myself," Vesper said. "I lead them in. I walk them into those passages personally, and I get Pip back at the exchange, and then—" They faltered. "And then I deal with what comes after."

"That's suicide."

"It's a debt," Vesper said, and heard how bitter their own voice sounded. "I've been paying debts my whole life, Sera. What's one more?"

Sera caught their wrist—hard, sudden, her grip like the last honest thing in the world. "Listen to me. You are not paying anyone anymore. Not the temple. Not the gods. Not some ledger your father signed before you could write." Her voice dropped. "You walk into that mountain to save her, fine. I'm coming. And when it's done, you and I are going to have a conversation about what you owe, and the answer is going to be *nothing*, and you are going to learn to live with that."

Vesper couldn't speak. The gods pressed close, wordless, and somewhere above them the broken bells rang and rang.

* * *

Moonrise came silver and cold over the shrine at the mountain's foot.

Corvane stood among his inquisitors with Pip bound and silent at his side—her glow dimmed under layers of suppression-warding, her eyes finding Vesper's across the shrine stones with a trust that hurt worse than any blade. Vesper walked forward alone, as agreed, and unrolled a map across the altar with steady hands.

"There," they said, marking the passage. "The Ember Door. Three days in, through the deep ways. I'll guide you myself."

Corvane studied the map. Studied Vesper. And smiled the smile of a man who believed he had already won.

"Of course you will, Scribe," he said, and cut Pip's bindings, and pushed her stumbling into Vesper's arms. "Of course you will."

And as Vesper held her—small, shaking, smelling of smoke and cinders—Pip whispered against their ear, in a voice that carried something older than either of them:

"He knows about the false roads. Vesper. *He already talked to Marlow.*"

The name landed like ice water. Marlow—who had taught Vesper the mountain's secrets. Marlow, who owed them nothing anymore except the price of a bounty.

Corvane was already turning toward the mountain, lanterns rising, six white robes kindling against the dark.

"Come along, Scribe," he called back pleasantly. "Lead the way."
