## 8. Guttering

The vault had no windows, so Pip counted her days by the sound of the locks.

Three bolts in the morning. Two at night. The heavy one—old iron, older magic—only turned when someone brought food, and the food was always cold and always bread and always carried by a man who wouldn't look at her. She'd tried talking to him on the first day. And the second. By the fifth she'd stopped, because his silence had a shape to it, and the shape was fear.

They were afraid of her humming.

Good, she thought, curled on the narrow cot with her knees to her chest. Be afraid.

But the truth was that Pip was afraid too, and the vault knew it. The walls were stone cut from the mountain's own bones, and they drank warmth the way sand drank rain. Her spark sat low in her chest, banked small, barely an ember. She kept it that way on purpose. If she let it rise—if she hummed the way she hummed for Vesper, the way hearths leapt and candles stood up straight and proud—the inquisitors would come down the stairs with their iron censers and their prayers written backwards, and those prayers hurt. She'd learned that on day two.

So she was quiet. She was small. She did what Vesper had taught her without teaching her: how to be invisible in plain sight.

*You have to be nobody,* Vesper had said once, walking the smuggler roads with her hand light on Pip's shoulder. *Nobody gets followed. Nobody gets asked questions. It's not cowardice, Pip. It's arithmetic.*

*Is it lonely?* Pip had asked.

Vesper hadn't answered, which was its own answer.

Pip lay in the dark of the vault and thought about loneliness like it was a country she might have to live in someday, and then she stopped thinking about anything, because the dragon spoke.

*

It didn't speak in words. Not at first.

It came up through the floor, through the mountain, through nine hundred years of stone, and it arrived in Pip's chest as a feeling—the feeling of a fire going out in a room where everyone is asleep. A slow cooling. A dimming so gradual that no one notices until they wake shivering and don't know why.

Pip sat up.

The spark in her chest answered before her mind caught up, flaring bright against her ribs, and for one terrible moment she thought the inquisitors would feel it too, would come running with their backwards prayers. But no footsteps came. No locks turned. Whatever the dragon was doing beneath Thornfell, it was happening below the range of any ear but hers.

*Oh,* she said, inside herself, the way you talk when you're the only person in a room. *Oh, you're real.*

She had always known, in the way children know things—completely and without evidence. Vesper believed her. Sera believed her. But belief was different from this: the vast slow attention of something enormous turning toward her through the dark of the world, the way a sleeper turns toward a candle.

The last dragon. Dying under the mountain. And she could feel every year of it, every century of its long decline, the way you can feel a fever coming on before the sweat starts.

It wasn't angry. That was the thing that undid her. Something that old, that diminished, should have been furious—and instead what poured up through the stone was patience, worn smooth as river glass, and underneath the patience a grief so large it made the vault feel like a thimble.

*I'm here,* Pip said. *I'm listening.*

And the dragon showed her.

Not words. Pictures made of heat. A time when there were hundreds of dragons and the sky over Thornfell was dark with wings, and the old gods were young and loud and walked openly in orchards and rivers and the hearts of people who left offerings of bread and honey. A time when the wards were not failing because the wards were not needed—when the land itself was held, the way a mother holds a sleeping child, by things that loved it.

Then the pictures narrowed. Wings falling. Fires guttering. Gods growing quieter and quieter, retreating into shrines, then into stones, then into the space between one prayer and the next. Until only one dragon remained, coiled beneath the whole country like a coal buried in ash, keeping the wards alive by burning itself down.

Keeping them alive right now. Tonight. While Pip sat in a vault and the inquisitors upstairs wrote decrees about heresy and the temple elders wrung their hands over a scribe who'd run away with a knight and a child.

*You're the flame,* Pip understood. *Under everything. You're why the wards hold.*

The dragon's answer was a slow dimming, like a nod.

*And you're dying.*

Another dimming. This one longer. And threaded through it—not fear, not pleading, nothing so simple—a request. It took the dragon a long time to shape it, and Pip felt every second of the shaping, felt the great mind gathering itself the way an old woman gathers strength to cross a room.

When it finally came, it was smaller than she expected. Smaller than rescue. Smaller than save me, or save them, or even remember me.

*Witness,* the dragon said. Or rather, the meaning of witness, passed in heat and pressure and the ache behind her sternum. *Someone must see. Someone must carry the seeing out of the dark. That is all I ask. That is enough.*

Pip pressed both hands flat against the cold stone floor.

*I'll do it,* she said. *I promise. But first—*

She thought of Vesper. Of Sera's rough hand ruffling her hair. Of hearths blazing and the three of them eating stolen apples on a mountainside while Sera told a story badly and Vesper corrected the details and Pip laughed until her ribs hurt.

*First I have to get out of here.*

The dragon went still. Then, slowly, deliberately, it did something it had not done in centuries.

It gave her a little more fire.

*

It came up through the floor like spring water, and Pip drank.

Not with her mouth—with the spark, which opened like a flower and took the gift in, and grew. Warmth spread through her limbs, through her fingers, into the soles of her feet where the cold had lived since they locked her in. For the first time in six days she wasn't shivering.

She stood up on the cot and put her palm against the ceiling, because the dragon's heat had shown her something else, something the inquisitors hadn't figured out yet: the vault's wards were written to contain a frightened child. They were not written to contain a hearth-fire with a dragon's blessing behind it.

The iron lock on the door was the oldest part. Old iron, old magic, backwards prayers scratched into its face. Pip looked at it for a long time.

Then she started to hum.

It was the song Vesper hummed when they copied prayers at night, the one they probably didn't know they hummed, the one that sounded like ink drying and lamplight steadying. Pip had heard it a hundred times on the road. She gave it back now, gave it to the spark, and the spark rose in her chest like bread rising, like dawn rising, like everything in the world that comes back if you wait long enough.

The lock began to glow.

At first it was just a dull red, the color of a poker left too near the coals. Then brighter. Orange. Gold. The backwards prayers blistered and flaked off the iron like dead skin, and Pip watched them go with fierce satisfaction, because those prayers had hurt her, had scraped against her spark like wire, and now they were ash.

The iron groaned. Wept a thin line of molten metal down the door. And then, with a sound like a sigh, it let go.

Pip climbed down from the cot. Her legs shook. Six days of cold bread and colder stone, and courage turns out to weigh something after all. But the door swung open at her touch, and the corridor beyond was empty, torchlit, and pointed up.

She took one step, then stopped. Turned back. Put her hand on the vault wall, on the stone that had drunk her warmth for six days.

"Thank you," she whispered—to the wall, and past it, down through nine hundred years of rock, to the coal in the ash. "I haven't forgotten. Witness. I'm carrying it."

The mountain breathed. Somewhere far below, something vast shifted its weight, and the shift felt—for one impossible moment—like gratitude.

Then Pip ran.

*

The stairs were steep and the torches were spaced too far apart, and twice she heard boots above her and pressed herself into doorways, small and silent, nobody, arithmetic. But the temple was loud tonight—she could hear shouting two floors up, running feet, the clang of the alarm bell that meant the inquisitors had finally noticed their prisoner's door hanging open like a wound.

Let them shout. Let them chase. They'd find a melted lock and a cold cot and no child, and by the time they sorted out which way she'd gone, she'd be gone.

Except.

Except that at the top of the stairs, in the antechamber where the censers hung and the backwards prayers were stacked in bundles like kindling, Pip found a map.

It was spread across a table, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, and it was covered in ink marks and annotations in a sharp, precise hand. She recognized the handwriting before she recognized the geography—she'd seen Vesper write, had watched that same careful slant form letters on scraps of paper by firelight.

But this wasn't Vesper's map. This was a copy of Vesper's map. Every hidden road, every smuggler's path, every secret way through the mountains of Thornfell—and at the center, circled twice, underlined, annotated in red:

*THE EMBER DOOR — CONFIRMED.*

Beneath it, another line, in different ink, fresher:

*Inquisitors depart at dawn. Full company. Take the scribe alive; take the child if recoverable.*

Pip stood very still.

They knew. They knew where the Door was, or they knew how to make Vesper tell them, and either way a full company of inquisitors was going to march into the mountain at dawn with iron and backwards prayers and whatever else temples kept in their arsenals, and Vesper and Sera were up there right now, probably sleeping, probably trusting the dark to keep them—

No. Not probably. Definitely. Because Vesper trusted maps, and Sera trusted roads, and neither of them knew that trust had a price tag and the temple had already paid it.

Pip's spark flared so hot it singed the edge of the map, and she snatched her hand back, heart hammering. Careful. Careful. Fire was a tool, not a temper. Vesper had said that too—or maybe Sera had—but somebody wise had said it, and she held the words around the heat like cupped hands around a match.

She memorized the map instead. Every line, every mark, the circle drawn twice around the Door. She was good at remembering—she'd had to be, living on the streets, knowing which doors opened and which faces lied. She folded the memory away tight and safe.

Then she took the candlestick from the corner of the table, tipped it, and let the flame kiss the map's edge.

Paper catches fast when it wants to. The map went up in a bloom of orange, and Pip watched the Ember Door burn, watched the red annotations curl and blacken, watched the temple's careful plans become smoke, and she felt the spark in her chest purr like a cat.

Somewhere down the corridor, a bell began to ring. Shouting. Boots. The whole temple waking up to fire where no fire should be.

Pip ran the other way.

She knew these halls now—six days of listening through stone taught you a building's bones. There was a laundry chute that emptied into the delivery yard. There was a gap in the yard wall where the frost had cracked the mortar, wide enough for a thin girl who'd spent years learning exactly how small she could be. Beyond the wall: the terraced gardens, the orchard—the orchard that had bloomed out of season, she realized, the night Vesper's copied prayer was first answered, the miracle that had started everything—and beyond the orchard, the hills, the dark, the road.

Her road. Back to Vesper. Back to Sera. Back to the two people who had looked at a street orphan who heard fire and decided, without being asked, that she was worth keeping.

Pip squeezed through the gap in the wall, thorns catching her sleeve, and stood for a moment in the garden dark with the temple burning its alarms behind her and the whole country spread out ahead.

Down through the soles of her feet, faint and fading, she felt the dragon's attention begin to slip away—the great mind folding itself back down into the deep, spending its last strength on the wards, on the slow work of dying. But before it went, it pressed one final thing up through the earth, gentle as a hand on her head:

*Hurry, little flame.*

"I'm hurrying," Pip whispered.

And she ran—through the orchard, past the trees still impossibly in blossom, petals scattering in her wake like snow that had forgotten what season it was—out onto the hills road, with the alarm bells fading behind her and the mountains rising black against the stars.

Dawn was maybe four hours off. The inquisitors would ride at first light, following a map that no longer existed, hunting a Door whose location lived now only in the memory of a nine-year-old girl with a dragon's ember in her chest.

Pip ran faster.

She just had to get there first.
