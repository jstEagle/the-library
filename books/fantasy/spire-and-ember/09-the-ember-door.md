## 9. The Ember Door

The mountain had a heartbeat, and it was failing.

Vesper felt it through the soles of their boots as they descended the last stair—a slow, arrhythmic thud, like a heart remembering, belatedly, to keep beating. The smuggler roads of Thornfell had taught them every kind of dark: cellar-dark, grave-dark, the dark behind a temple elder's eyes. This was none of those. This was the dark inside a living thing, warm and vast and dying.

Pip rode on Sera's shoulders, her small hands knotted in the knight's cropped hair, humming. The tune was nothing—three notes, four—but where she hummed, the stone walls glowed faintly amber, veins of light waking in the rock like embers stirred by breath.

"She's louder down here," Sera said quietly. "Listen."

Vesper listened. Beneath Pip's humming, beneath the mountain's failing pulse, there was something else: a voice too large to be a voice, more felt than heard, saying the same word over and over in a language older than Thornfell's oldest wall.

*Remember me. Remember me. Remember me.*

"The dragon," Vesper said.

"What's left of it," said a god.

It stood on the landing below them—or rather, it occupied the space where standing might have happened. It looked like a woman made of harvest-light and then like a river given shoulders and then like nothing Vesper could hold with their eyes for longer than a blink. Nine years copying prayers had taught Vesper the gods' names and their epithets and their feast days. It had not prepared them for the way a god's attention felt: like being read.

"You came," the god said. Its voice was the sound of a hearth relighting. "We did not dare hope. Hope is expensive, for things like us."

"Where's the Door?" Vesper asked. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Behind them, somewhere far above, the wards of Oldfall were failing; they could feel that too now, a thinning at the edges of the world, like frost retreating from a window.

"Below." The god turned—it did not turn, but its attention moved—and the stair went on.

They descended another hour. Pip stopped humming once, pressed her face into Sera's hair, and whispered, "It's sad. It's so sad. It just wants somebody to say its name right."

Sera's jaw tightened. She had been silent since Oldfall, since the argument in the god-haunted square where Vesper had told her *I have to do this* and she had answered with nine different reasons why they shouldn't and finally, terribly, with none. She walked like a soldier again now, hand near her sword, eyes moving. Guarding them. Even now. Especially now.

At the bottom of the stair, the passage opened.

Vesper had imagined the Ember Door as a door—hinges, a lintel, something with an other side. What they found instead was a cavern the size of a cathedral, and in its center, a slab of black stone worn smooth as a page, and above the slab, suspended in nothing, a flame the size of a fist.

That was all. A flame no bigger than a candle-heart, burning without fuel, without flicker, guttering.

Around it, the air shimmered with shapes—the gathered gods of Thornfell, dozens of them, fragments and splinters, some no larger than moths, some vast as weather. They had come to watch. Or to mourn. Or both.

"The Door is not a way out," the first god said gently. "It never was. It is where the first prayer was spoken over the first fire, before your temples, before your ledgers, before anyone thought to write such things down. It is an altar. Altars only ask one thing."

"A sacrifice," Vesper said.

"Not blood." The god's light dimmed, apologetic. "A voice. We are made of being-said, little scribe. Every prayer you ever copied fed us a crumb. When the temples stopped believing, we starved. When the dragon dies, the last ember of the first fire goes out—and we go with it, and the wards go with us, and everything your country was becomes exactly what your maps already say it is."

Vesper stepped toward the altar. The flame leaned toward them, the way a plant leans toward light.

"And if I speak?" they asked. "If I give you my voice?"

"Then you become our herald in truth. Not a copyist. The original. Every prayer Thornfell needs will pass through your mouth, and it will cost you, slowly, year by year, until there is less of Vesper Halloran and more of us. That is the bargain. We will not pretend it is kind."

"That's a terrible bargain," Sera said flatly.

"Sera—"

"No." She crossed the cavern in five strides and took Vesper's face in both hands, gauntleted fingers careful as a squire's. Up close her eyes were wet and furious. "I stood at an altar once too. Did I tell you? The day they stripped my rank. They offered me a choice—burn the shrine or burn my name—and I told myself duty was duty either way. I chose wrong, Vesper. I chose the version of duty that eats the person doing it. I know what that road looks like from the middle, and I am *not* watching you walk it."

"You'd rather watch the whole country die?"

"I'd rather find a third thing! There's always a third thing, someone just has to be stubborn enough to look—" Her voice cracked. "You spent nine years making yourself invisible. You think I didn't notice? You apologize for taking up space. You flinch when people say your name. And now the gods want to make that permanent, dress it up in glory, call it sacrifice, and you're going to say yes because some part of you still believes disappearing is what you're *for*."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Vesper opened their mouth to argue and found, to their horror, that they couldn't—not cleanly, not honestly. Because she was right. Because some part of them had already been composing the prayer, the way they composed everything: carefully, invisibly, expecting nothing back.

"It doesn't have to be disappearing," Vesper said slowly. The thought arrived as they spoke it, which was how their best thoughts always arrived. "That's what the temple taught me sacrifice meant. Vanishing. Being used up. But—" they looked at the flame, at the moth-small gods circling it, at Pip watching from the shadows with her enormous serious eyes— "you said prayers feed you. You said I'd be the original. Then I wouldn't be erased, would I? I'd be *read*. Out loud. Forever."

The god's light flickered—surprise, perhaps. Gods, apparently, could be surprised.

"That is... one interpretation," it admitted.

"Then here are my terms." Vesper's heart was hammering, but their hands were steady, and it occurred to them, distantly, that this was the first time in nine years their hands had been steady for the right reason. "I don't vanish. I don't dissolve. I stay *me*—stubborn, resentful, bad at authority, fond of exactly three people. I speak for you, but I speak as myself. And when this is done, I get to walk out of this mountain and eat breakfast with my family. Those are the terms. Take them or find another scribe."

For a long moment the cavern held only the sound of the guttering flame.

Then every god in the room laughed—a sound like rain on a hot roof, like a congregation exhaling—and the harvest-light woman bowed, which was somehow also a sunrise.

"Accepted," she said. "Oh, *accepted*, little scribe. Nine hundred years of priests asking what they must give, and you're the first to negotiate."

Sera made a sound that was half sob, half laugh, and kissed them—quick, fierce, tasting of salt and iron—then stepped back and did the thing she did instead of crying: squared her shoulders and posted herself between Vesper and the world.

Which was when the world arrived.

* * *

They came down the stair in white and ash-grey, six of them, lanterns high, and Inquisitor Maren Corvane walked at their head with the calm of a person who has never once doubted a ledger.

"Halloran." Her voice carried through the cavern without echo, as though the mountain itself declined to repeat it. "By writ of the Temple of the Quiet Flame, you are remanded for forgery, heresy, and the theft of sacred property." Her gaze touched the flame, the gods, the altar, and dismissed each in turn. "There is nothing here. There has never been anything here. Kneel, and the tribunal may yet be merciful."

"They're real," Pip said, stepping out of the shadow, chin up, spark-light dancing in her eyes. "You just stopped listening."

"Child, go stand with the knight."

"No."

Maren sighed and gestured, and two acolytes moved toward the girl, and Sera drew steel with a ring that woke every ember-vein in the walls, and Vesper understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the moment. Not the negotiation. Not the kiss. *This.* The moment where the old life and the new life stood in the same room and only one of them could leave.

"Maren." Vesper stepped forward, past Sera's outflung arm, onto the smooth black stone. "You copied prayers beside me for six years. Do you remember any of them?"

The inquisitor frowned. "That is not relevant."

"It's the only relevant thing in the world." Vesper turned to the flame. It guttered low—so low now, barely a coal, the dragon beneath them breathing its last long breaths. Around the cavern the gods had gone still, waiting, and Vesper felt their attention the way a page feels a pen.

Nine years of copying. Thousands of prayers in a dead hand, in a dead language, for believers who believed in nothing but ink. Every one of them lived in Vesper's memory still, filed and perfect, because Vesper Halloran forgot nothing they wrote.

They had thought that was a curse.

They opened their mouth, and gave the words back—not copied this time. *Original.*

"*Hearth-mother, who lit the first fire so the dark would have an exception—remember us, who forget you. Orchard-father, who taught the seed to gamble—remember us, who count only losses. River-that-was, wolf-of-the-thorn, grandmother-of-storms—*" the names rose in them like water finding its level, and with each name a god brightened, flared, *arrived*— "*we do not bring you offerings. We bring you witnesses. Say our names back, and we will say yours, and between us we will make a country worth the trouble—*"

The flame roared.

Not guttered—*roared*, a pillar of gold fire slamming upward into the cavern roof, and beneath their feet the mountain's heartbeat slammed with it, strong, sure, alive, and the light poured down through miles of stone and out through the failing wards of Oldfall and along every ley and ward-line in Thornfell, and Vesper heard—everywhere at once, impossibly—the sound of cold hearths catching, of dead orchards budding, of a thousand small fires remembering themselves.

Maren Corvane fell to her knees. Not from command—from awe, the old kind, the kind the temple had built walls against for centuries. Her lantern shook. "What *are* you?" she whispered.

Vesper looked down at their own hands. Light ran under the skin there now, faint as dawn under a curtain, and their voice, when they spoke, carried harmonics that had never been human.

"I'm the scribe," they said. "And these are my terms."

Sera reached them first, sword still drawn, laughing and swearing in the same breath. Pip hit Vesper's legs like a small comet, humming, and where she hummed the new fire danced.

Above them, far away, the mountain rumbled—and somewhere in the deep, something vast and ancient and not-dead-after-all exhaled, and spoke its first word in three hundred years.

It was Vesper's name.
