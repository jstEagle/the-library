## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The lamp above Lior Voss's desk had a habit of guttering at the exact moment she found something interesting, as though the light itself were reluctant to illuminate what she was reading. Tonight it did so again — a single stutter of flame, a breath of shadow across the page — and she reached up without looking to adjust the wick, because she had learned years ago that the lamp could not be trusted and her own hands could.

The entry before her read, in the neat copperplate of a guild scribe dead forty years:

*Received of Maren Ostwick, one (1) shadow, worn thin at the edges, in exchange for safe passage through the Door of Winter. Shadow to be returned upon presentation of this receipt or its bearer.*

Below that, in a different hand — smaller, sharper, slanting leftward the way handwriting does when its owner is thinking faster than they write — someone had added: *Shadow never reclaimed. Interest accruing.*

Lior sat back in her chair and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her palms until colors bloomed against the dark of the archive. Around her, the Ledger Room of the Guild of Impossible Things stretched away into aisles of shelving that creaked like an old ship at anchor. Ten thousand volumes, give or take. Trades of borrowed shadows. Sold years. Doors mapped into existence by the simple act of drawing them. The guild called it all *inventory*, said the word with the flat satisfaction of people who had never once wondered what inventory dreamed about at night.

She knew better. She had known better since she was nine years old, since the night her mother walked out of their rooms above the map-sellers' row carrying nothing but a satchel and the expression of a woman going to settle an account, and had not come back.

*Interest accruing.* Her mother's hand. Of course it was. It was always her mother's hand, somewhere in these books, annotating, correcting, occasionally striking through an entire transaction with a violence that tore the vellum. Eleven years gone, and Isolde Voss still filled every corner of the ledgers like ink fills the veins of a feather.

"You're late with the equinox audit," said a voice from the doorway, and Lior did not startle, because Guildmaster Corvane announced himself the same way every time — as though lateness were a moral failing rather than a fact of arithmetic. "The other auditors finished theirs a week ago."

"The other auditors," Lior said, dipping her pen, "read what the ledgers say."

"And you read what they don't." He came down the aisle with his lantern held high, a broad man gone soft at the middle, wearing the guild's gray-and-gold sash with the ease of long ownership. He stopped at the end of her desk and looked at the open volume without appearing to look at it. That was Corvane's particular talent: seeing everything while admitting to nothing. "Which is why I don't ask you what you find there, Voss. A courtesy I'd thank you to extend in both directions."

"Of course, Guildmaster." She turned the page with two fingers, smooth as dealing cards. "I'll have the audit on your desk by equinox eve."

"See that you do." He lingered a moment longer than the conversation required. His eyes moved over the shelves behind her — aisle nine, where the oldest covenant-era volumes were kept under lock — and then he grunted, some private conclusion reached, and withdrew the way he had come, his lantern-light sliding along the spines until it turned the corner and left her alone with the dust and the dark and the ten thousand impossible things.

Lior waited until his footsteps faded entirely. Then she pulled the volume back toward herself and looked again at the marginal note in her mother's hand.

She had taught herself the cipher at fourteen, which was to say she had spent three years pretending she wasn't doing it. The ledgers' public entries were written in plain script for any guildsman to read; but threaded beneath them, in strokes so fine they might have been flaws in the paper, ran a second text. Her mother had called it — had called everything, in the letters that came the first year and then stopped coming — *the true accounting*. Not the trades themselves, but what the trades were *for*. Payments, Isolde's hidden notes said, over and over, in a dozen different formulations across a dozen different decades of entries. *Payment rendered. Payment due. Payment deferred — the border grows restless.* As though the entire guild, with its pompous seals and its auction bells and its self-important little economy of shadows and years, were merely a hand passing coins to something vast and patient on the other side of the world's edge.

Lior had copied every ciphered note into her own private book, in her own hand, and kept the book wrapped in oilcloth beneath a floorboard in her rooms. She told herself this was diligence. Thoroughness. The kind of initiative that would earn her a senior auditor's chain someday, if senior auditors were ever chosen for anything besides their fathers' names.

She told herself this every night, and every night the telling wore a little thinner, like a coin passed too many times.

* * *

The trouble with being the only person who could read a thing, Lior thought as she worked her way down aisle twelve near midnight, was that it made solitude feel less like a condition and more like a sentence. The other junior members of the guild avoided her — not unkindly, exactly, but with the careful distance people keep from someone who is known to be strange in a useful way. They saw her hunched over volumes no one else could parse and drew the obvious conclusion: the daughter reads like the mother, and we all remember what happened to the mother.

What happened to the mother was that she vanished. That was the official finding, delivered by a magistrate with a cold and filed without ceremony eleven years ago. *Vanished*, as though Isolde Voss had been a wisp of smoke, a rumor, a chalk line rubbed out. No body. No note. Just an empty room, an empty satchel-hook, and a guild full of men who suddenly became very interested in locking certain shelves.

Lior had been nine. She had waited up for three nights with the lamp lit, because her mother had promised — had actually promised, hand on Lior's head, smelling of ink and cold air — that she would teach her the cipher when she turned ten. Then the waiting had curdled into anger, and the anger had hardened into competence, because competence was the only shape grief was permitted to take in a guildhall. You could not weep at your desk. You could work.

So she worked. She learned the cipher alone, by lamplight, by comparing hundreds of entries until the pattern surfaced the way coastlines surface when a tide goes out. She learned that the cipher changed every seven years — that her mother had re-keyed it, generation after generation, and that the current key had been set the very year Isolde disappeared, as though she had known she was leaving and wanted to leave the door locked behind her. Or unlocked. Lior had never decided which possibility frightened her more.

Now she stood before shelf 12-C and took down the volume marked only with a date: the year of her own birth. The binding was cracked leather, cool to the touch, and heavier than a book that size had any right to be. Everything in this room was heavier than it should be. She had long ago stopped remarking on it.

She carried it back to her desk, set it beside the lamp, and opened it to the entries for the month she was born.

There it was. Third entry, ninth day of the month, in the copperplate of old Scribe Aldery:

*Received of Isolde Voss, cartographer, one (1) name, firstborn daughter, to be held in trust. In exchange: continuance of the eastern wards, term of twenty-one years.*

Lior read it four times. Her pen hovered over her transcription book and did not move.

She knew this entry. She had copied it at sixteen, had stared at it for a whole night, had concluded — sensibly, she had thought — that it was metaphor. Guild bookkeeping was full of poetry dressed as precision. *One name, firstborn daughter* meant a christening fee, surely. A registry tax. Something dull and administrative wearing a dramatic coat.

But tonight, with eleven years of cipher-work behind her and the fine hidden strokes swimming up beneath the copperplate like fish rising to feed, she read the second text for the first time. Beneath the entry, in her mother's sharp leftward slant, written small enough that it must have taken an hour to inscribe:

*Not metaphor. Not a fee. A lien. They hold her name the way a lender holds a deed. When the term ends, they will come to collect — unless the debt is discharged, or the debtor succeeds. Twenty-one years. Count them, Lior. I am sorry. I am so sorry. I am trying to buy you time.*

The date of Lior's birth had been twenty-one years ago last spring.

The lamp guttered. This time she did not reach to fix it. She sat very still in the shrinking circle of light and listened to the archive breathe around her — the tick of cooling stone, the whisper of vellum settling, the far-off bell of the night wardens marking the third hour. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that believed itself solid, girdled by wards that had held for six hundred years, under stars that had burned unchanged since before the guild kept records.

*They will come to collect.*

Her hand, when she finally made it move, was steadier than she had any right to expect. She wrote the date of her birth in her transcription book. She wrote today's date beside it. Between the two she wrote the arithmetic no one else in Vellum's Reach could do, because no one else had spent eleven years learning to read a dead woman's — a *missing* woman's — handwriting in the dark.

Ninety-one days. Perhaps ninety-two, depending on how the covenant counted a year, and she suspected it counted precisely.

She closed the volume. She closed her transcription book and wrapped it in the spare oilcloth she had begun, months ago without knowing why, to keep in her desk drawer. Then she snuffed the lamp and stood a moment in the total dark of the Ledger Room, among ten thousand impossible things, feeling the strangest sensation of her carefully ordered life: that the mystery she had spent a decade pretending was just work had finally, politely, finished pretending back.

Above the archive's high windows, through the leaded glass, the equinox sky was beginning to pale toward dawn. Lior gathered her books, climbed the stairs, and stepped out into the gray morning air of Vellum's Reach — where, though she did not yet know to look for it, the first star of the evening sky had begun, very faintly, to flicker.
