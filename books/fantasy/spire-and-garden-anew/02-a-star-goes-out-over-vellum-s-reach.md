## 2. A Star Goes Out Over Vellum's Reach

The equinox audit began, as all guild business did, with the lighting of lamps that were not needed.

Lior Voss had never understood this ritual — forty candles set in iron sconces along the Long Table of the Guildhall of Vellum's Reach, lit one by one by the youngest apprentice while the guildmaster recited the Charter of Accounts. The hall had windows. The windows faced east, and the equinox dawn came through them like a blade laid flat against the world, silver and thin and honest. But the Charter said candles, and so there were candles, and Lior had learned long ago that the Guild of Impossible Things loved its rituals precisely because they made no sense. Sense could be audited. Ritual simply endured.

She sat at her station near the end of the Long Table, third from the wall, where the light was worst and the supervision was worst still. This was deliberate. She had spent two years arranging to be overlooked.

Before her lay Ledger Nine — *Trades of the Autumn Quarter, Year Unrecorded* — open to a page she had copied out three times now in her own hand, hidden beneath the floorboard of her room, because she did not trust what she was seeing and wanted witnesses that were not paper.

The entry read:

*Borrowed: one shadow, whole and unfrayed, from a woman of the Tanner's Row, payment rendered in the form of a door that opens only when wept upon.*

*Returned: not yet.*

*Interest accruing.*

The cipher was her mother's. That much Lior knew with the certainty of someone who has spent ten years learning a handwriting the way other people learn a face. Not the guild cipher — the guild cipher was taught, formal, a lattice of knots and tally-marks any senior cartographer could puzzle through given a week and good light. No. Beneath the guild cipher, woven into it like a second thread in cloth, ran another system entirely: smaller, older, meaner with its secrets. Her mother's cipher. And Lior had taught herself to read it alone, at night, by candlelight, telling herself the whole time that this was diligence. Professional development. A cartographer ought to be able to read every map in the archive.

It was not diligence. She knew that too. It was the closest thing to conversation she had left.

Her mother had been gone eleven years. No body, no note, no grave. Just an empty chair in the mapping room, a cold cup of tea gone to dust, and forty-one ledgers full of entries no one else in the guild could fully read — trades of borrowed shadows, sold years, doors mapped into existence and then mapped back out again. The guild called them anomalies. The guildmaster called them *legacy irregularities* and said the word the way a man spits out a fruit stone. Lior called them what they were, privately, in the dark, in the journal she kept under her pillow: *her mother's voice, going on without her.*

"Voss."

Lior looked up. Guildmaster Corvane stood at the head of the table, a narrow man assembled mostly of angles and disapproval, his chain of office polished to a shine that suggested he did it himself each morning with great care. Behind him, the apprentices had finished the candles. Forty flames stood in their sconces like a jury.

"The Autumn Quarter," Corvane said. "Report your anomalies."

"There are none," said Master Ellery from the far end, before Lior could speak. He always spoke first; he considered speed a form of seniority. "The ledgers have been quiet since midsummer."

"They have not been quiet," Lior said. "They've been *still*. There is a difference."

A few of the younger cartographers glanced up. Stillness versus quiet was a distinction only she seemed to care about, and she had stopped apologizing for it. A quiet ledger was a ledger with nothing happening. A still ledger was a ledger holding its breath.

"Explain," Corvane said.

"A quiet ledger sits inert. Ink doesn't move. But these—" she turned Ledger Nine toward him, careful not to let anyone see which page, "—these feel like a room where someone has just left. The entries haven't changed in weeks, but the margins have. The spacing. It's as if the text is being... maintained. Tended."

"Tended," Ellery repeated, with the gentle contempt of a man handling a junior's overwrought metaphor. "Ledgers are tended by clerks, Voss."

"Yes," Lior said. "Who is tending these?"

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Corvane looked at her for a long moment — not with anger, she noticed, but with something wearier, something that might once have been fear and had been folded small and put away.

"The audit proceeds," he said. "Line by line, quarter by quarter. Voss, you'll take the eastern columns. Ellery, the western. We conclude by dusk, as the Charter requires." He paused at the end of the table, and added, almost as an afterthought, almost kindly: "And Voss — the legacy irregularities are not your inheritance. Whatever you imagine you've found in them. They belong to the guild."

"Of course, guildmaster," Lior said, which was the lie she told best, because she'd had practice.

* * *

The equinox audit was, in truth, hours of exquisite boredom punctuated by moments of private vertigo.

The eastern columns concerned themselves with the mundane miracles: rain bought for the vineyards south of the city, a bridge unmapped after a toll dispute, six inches of river traded upstream for reasons the ledger declined to specify. Lior worked through them with the mechanical devotion of long habit, her pen scratching, the autumn light crawling westward across the table, and all the while the other thread of the cipher murmured underneath the official text like water under ice.

*Payment rendered. Payment rendered. Payment rendered.*

Every entry, she noticed — not for the first time, but today with a new and unpleasant clarity — every single entry followed the same grammar. Something borrowed. Something paid. Interest accruing. As though the ledger were not a record of trades at all but a schedule of installments. As though the guild were not a guild but a debtor, and had been for centuries, and the impossible things were merely how it kept up its payments.

She had never dared follow that thought to its end. Today, with the equinox light lying flat and strange across the page, the thought followed her.

At midday the bells rang for the turning of the season, and the guild observed the traditional pause — everyone standing, everyone silent, everyone facing east toward the windows. It was the one moment of the year when the entire guildhall looked outward at once. Lior had always found it moving, in a way she was embarrassed by: forty cartographers, the people who drew the world so it would hold still, pausing to look at the sky doing exactly that.

The sky was clear. The first stars of evening would not come for hours yet, but the sun hung low and swollen over the eastern hills, and above it, faint as breath on glass, the day's first star had risen — the one the old maps called the Equinox Eye, visible only on this day, only at this hour, a small white point that generations of Vellum's Reach children were taught to find and make a wish upon.

Lior found it. She did not wish. She counted it instead, the way she counted everything — a habit her mother had given her along with her name. *One star. Steady. Present.*

The star went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. It went out the way a candle goes out when pinched between wet fingers — abruptly, completely, with a small violence that leaves the wick smoking. One moment the Equinox Eye hung above the eastern hills; the next there was a hole in the sky where it had been, and the hole was blacker than the sky around it, blacker than night, black in a way that made Lior's eyes slide off it like rain off oilcloth.

No one else moved.

She understood why a heartbeat later. Around the rim of the sky, faint and patient, the other early stars were emerging — and they began to go out too. One. Then another. Then three in quick succession, west to east, a sequence, an order, as though someone were walking along a shelf of candles and snuffing them with a careful hand.

"Guildmaster," Lior said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The stars."

Corvane turned. He was not a tall man, but he filled the window for a moment, silhouetted, and beyond his shoulder Lior watched a fourth star die, and a fifth.

"I see them," he said quietly.

"Should we—"

"Silence," Corvane said.

The word fell into the hall and closed over it like water. Forty cartographers stood in the equinox light and watched the sky being put out, and no one spoke, because the guildmaster had ordered silence, and the guild obeyed its rituals even when the rituals failed them. Lior thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this is what dread looks like when it arrives politely. It dresses itself in procedure. It waits for the proper forms.*

The seventh star went out. The eighth. Between extinguishings there was a rhythm — she caught herself counting, because counting was the only prayer she knew. Two seconds. Three. Two. Three. Not random. Not decay. *Deliberate.* Someone, somewhere, was doing this, and doing it carefully, and taking their time about it, the way you take your time when the task matters. When it must not be interrupted.

When it must not be *noticed*.

The ninth star went out, and Lior stopped watching the sky, because a worse thing had happened.

Ledger Nine, open on the table before her, had changed.

She had memorized the page — of course she had, she memorized everything, it was the other thing her mother had given her along with her name. The entry concerning the Tanner's Row shadow had occupied the upper third of the leaf, followed by four lines of margin notes in her mother's cipher. She could have redrawn it blindfolded.

The margin notes were gone. In their place, in ink that was still — she touched it with one fingertip, and it smudged — *still wet*, new text had written itself in the same cipher, the same hand, the handwriting she had spent ten years learning the shape of in the dark:

*Count them, little cartographer. When you reach the number I could not, stop reading and run.*

Below it, smaller, pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear:

*Or don't run. You were always the stubborn one. Either way — finish the count.*

Lior looked up. Across the hall, Corvane was watching her. Not the sky. Her. And his face, stripped of its angles and its disapproval, was the face of a man watching something he had hoped for eleven years and prayed would never come.

Outside, the tenth star went out, and the counting went on.
