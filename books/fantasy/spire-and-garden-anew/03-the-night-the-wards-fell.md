## 3. The Night the Wards Fell

The moon rose over Vellum's Reach like a coin held up to a moneylender's eye, and Lior Voss was in the archive again, because of course she was.

She had told herself she would sleep. She had even made it as far as her narrow room above the chandler's shop, had unlaced her boots, had blown out the lamp. But the ledgers were rewriting themselves — she had watched it happen that morning, ink crawling like insects across a page she'd memorized only three days before — and a cartographer who could not sleep did not lie staring at a ceiling. A cartographer who could not sleep went back to work.

So here she stood at midnight, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with whatever string had been nearest, alone among the guildhall's deep shelves where the air smelled of tallow and old vellum and something else now, something faintly wrong, like the smell of a storm that has not yet decided to arrive.

The Ledger of Impossible Things lay open on the reading table before her. Entry 4471. It had read, when she first decoded it at nineteen: *One borrowed shadow, returned slightly used, against two years of good harvest.* Tonight it read: *One borrowed shadow, RETURNED OR FORFEIT, against two years of good harvest, INTEREST ACCRUING.*

The capital letters had not been there this afternoon.

Lior pressed her palm flat against the page, as if she could hold the words still by force. The vellum was warm. Not lamp-warm, not hand-warm — warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, from somewhere inside.

"You're changing," she murmured to it. "What are you changing *into*?"

The ledger did not answer, which was normal. What was not normal was the sound it made instead: a soft, papery exhalation, like a book settling, except books did not settle when no one had touched them.

Outside the high archive windows, the city of Vellum's Reach lay silvered under the equinox moon. She could see the spire of the guildhall itself, the crooked rooftops of Inkrow Street, the canal stitched through it all like a line someone had drawn and then regretted. And beyond the northern wall, past the last lamps, the border wards — invisible by day, a faint blue shimmer by night, the oldest working in the city, older than the guild, older than the maps.

Her mother had walked out past those wards eleven years ago and never come back. Lior had stopped expecting her home around year four. She had never stopped mapping the routes she might have taken.

She looked back down at the ledger, and her blood went cold in a slow, deliberate way, like water filling a boot.

Entry 4472 had appeared. She had not seen it written. The ink was still wet enough to shine.

*The account comes due at moonrise.*

"It *is* moonrise," Lior said. Her voice sounded very small in all that stone.

And then every ward-lamp in the archive guttered at once — not blown out, but *inhaled*, the flames leaning toward the north windows as though pulled by a breath drawn somewhere far away — and the shimmer of the border wards, visible even from here on clear nights, simply

stopped.

Not faded. Not flickered. Stopped, the way a heart stops, the way a sentence stops mid-word when the speaker dies. One moment the world had an edge; the next it did not.

Lior stood very still. Somewhere below her, in the city, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then a hundred, a rising tide of them, and under the barking a sound she had no name for yet — a low, vast rustling, like wind through a forest of dry paper, coming from the north, from the unmapped dark, from the place her ledgers had been quietly describing her whole life.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're *real*."

She had meant it as a curse. It came out as wonder, and she hated herself a little for that, and then she stopped hating herself because the first window shattered.

* * *

Sef had been a warden for nine years, and in nine years the most exciting thing that had happened on his watch was a smuggler who turned out to be smuggling cheese.

He was on the wall above the North Gate when the wards died. He felt it before he saw it — a sensation like a floorboard giving way beneath the sole of your foot, that lurch of the world being less solid than advertised. He gripped the parapet and looked north and saw the shimmer gone, and beyond it, where there had always been nothing, there was now very much something.

They came walking out of the dark in shapes that were almost right.

That was the horror of it, Sef thought later, and would think for the rest of his life: they were almost right. A woman in a grey dress, walking with a slight limp, except her limp switched legs every fourth step. A man leading a horse, except the horse's shadow fell the wrong direction from its body. A child carrying a lantern, except the lantern held darkness instead of light, and moths orbited it anyway, faithfully, stupidly. They came in borrowed shapes — he understood that much instinctively, the way you understand a lie in your mother's voice — things from the unmapped country wearing the appearances of people who had once traded with them, or been traded, or been forgotten by both.

"Sound the bell," Sef said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Sound the—"

The bell tower was already ringing. Someone below had seen. Good. Fine. He turned to shout orders to the two younger wardens beside him and found them both staring north with their mouths open, and one of them — Pell, nineteen years old, fast with a crossbow — whispered, "That's my grandmother."

It was not his grandmother. His grandmother was buried in the Fenward cemetery, Sef had attended the funeral. But the shape wore her face the way a coat is worn, and it smiled at the wall with too many teeth arranged too kindly, and Pell raised his crossbow and Sef knocked the bolt aside before he could loose it.

"Don't," Sef said. "You don't shoot what wears a face. You don't know whose."

"But it's *walking at us*—"

"Then we hold the gate and we don't open it and we ring the bell until the guild tells us what these things are." He heard himself say *the guild* and felt the absurdity of it like a physical weight. The Guild of Impossible Things. Nine years he'd joked about them, their fussy archivists, their ledgers full of nonsense trades. Borrowed shadows. Sold years. Mapped doors. He'd assumed it was ritual, theater, the rich amusing themselves with mystery.

The grey-dress woman reached the gate. Up close she smelled of cold ash and, underneath it, faintly, of violets. She looked up at Sef with eyes like unlit lamps and said, in a voice like pages turning:

"We have come to collect. Where is the keeper of accounts?"

"I don't—" Sef began.

"The accounts are overdue," said the thing wearing Pell's grandmother. "Eleven years overdue. We will enter the city and find the keeper ourselves."

"The guildhall," Sef said, before he could stop himself, and then wanted to bite his own tongue out, because the shapes all turned at once, smoothly, like weathervanes finding the wind, and began walking east toward the spire, unhurried, certain, leaving the gate unassailed as if walls had never been the point.

He ran. He took the wall stairs three at a time and dropped into the street and ran east through streets filling with screaming, past doors being barred, past a lamplighter standing frozen with his pole raised while a shape that might once have been somebody's sold youth drifted through him like smoke through a doorway. The guildhall spire rose ahead of him against the moonless-feeling sky, and as he closed the distance he saw why the night felt wrong without the wards:

There were no stars over the eastern quarter either. Just a clean black absence, star-shaped, spreading.

And the guildhall's west wing — the archive wing — was on fire.

* * *

Lior heard the shouting before she understood it, and understood it before she believed it.

She had done the sensible things with remarkable calm, she would think afterward. She had taken the current ledger off the table. She had taken her mother's cipher key — a strip of vellum covered in her mother's cramped left-slanting hand, which Lior had copied and hidden in seven places and kept the original sewn into her coat lining since she was sixteen. She had taken the map case. She had gotten as far as the archive door when the smoke came down the stairwell like a grey flood, and the heat behind it, and the sound of timber going.

Fire in an archive was not an accident. Fire in *this* archive, on *this* night, was a sentence being executed.

She turned back — of course she turned back, the older ledgers were there, her mother's originals, forty years of covenant in a hand she knew better than her own reflection — and got three steps before the stairwell collapsed in a bloom of orange and the shockwave threw her against the shelving. Books rained down around her. Through the burning doorway she glimpsed shapes moving in the smoke — the grey woman, the lantern-child, others, walking calmly through flame that should have killed them, unhurried, searching the shelves, *reading the spines.*

Looking for something. Looking for ledgers.

Lior snatched the nearest armful of her mother's originals off the floor, hugged them to her chest, and ran for the far window.

The drop into the herb garden was twelve feet. She had mapped this building; she knew the ground below the window was soft loam. This was, she realized with a wild, giddy clarity as she kicked the casement open, the single most useful thing cartography had ever done for her, and she was about to bet her life on soil composition.

She jumped.

The landing drove the air out of her and one of the ledgers into her ribs, and she lay in the loam for one gasping second looking up at the burning west wing, at the smoke boiling black against a sky with a growing hole in it where stars used to be. Terror, yes — terror like ice water in her chest. But underneath the terror, appalling and undeniable, something else was singing.

*The map finally matters.* All those years of decoding entries no one believed were real, all those nights alone with a cipher and a lamp, and the impossible things had walked out of the unmapped dark exactly as the ledgers described, and they were real, and the pages in her arms were not accounting. They were a treaty. They were the only treaty. And she was possibly the only person alive who could read them.

Someone grabbed her collar and hauled her upright so hard her feet left the ground.

"Are you insane?" said a warden — wall-uniform, soot already on his jaw, eyes wide with a fear he was holding onto by main strength. "Everyone's running *out* of the guildhall and you jumped *toward* it—"

"I work here," Lior said.

"You work here." He looked at the burning archive, at the ledgers clutched to her chest, at her singed eyebrows, and something in his face shifted from contempt to recognition. "You're the reader. The guildmaster sent word — the things at the gate asked for the keeper of accounts, and the old man went white as salt and said *get me the Voss girl, she can read the pages.*"

"My mother isn't—"

"They mean you," the warden said. "They've waited eleven years. They're not going to wait longer. Can you read what's burning in there?"

"Some of it. Most of it. Why?"

"Because half the city council wants to burn the rest 'for safety' and the other half wants to surrender them to those things at the gate, and I think both halves are about to kill us all." He hauled her toward the east courtyard, away from the heat, moving fast. "Sef," he added, as if remembering names were a thing people did. "Warden Sef. And you're Lior Voss, and tonight you're the most important person in this city, so try not to die."

"That's a lot of pressure to put on a stranger," Lior said, and was distantly amazed to hear her own voice come out dry.

Behind them the west wing groaned, settled, and sent up a column of sparks. Above the smoke, the star-shaped absence in the sky widened another degree. In her arms, pressed between the burning building and her heartbeat, the old ledgers felt warm again — animal-warm, awake — and as Sef pulled her through the courtyard gate toward the guildmaster's tower, Lior felt the pages shift against her chest, and looked down.

Fresh ink was blooming through the topmost cover, writing itself in her mother's left-slanting hand, the hand Lior had spent eleven years learning to imitate without ever once admitting why:

*If you are reading this, the account has come due, and I am sorry, and you must go east. Take the road that remembers being drawn. Do not let them take the ledgers. Do not trust the Reckoners. And Lior — the wards were never meant to keep them out. They were meant to keep the debt contained. I'm afraid I've run out of time to explain what we owe.*

The message ended there, mid-sentence, ink still wet, as though the writer had been interrupted — eleven years ago, or five minutes ago, or right now, somewhere east in the dark.

Lior stopped walking. Sef turned back, exasperated, and saw her face, and stopped too.

"What?" he said. "What is it?"

Lior looked east, past the walls, past the dead wards, into the unmapped dark where the stars had gone out — where her mother had written to her from, apparently, impossibly, *now* — and felt the terror and the exhilaration twist together into something new. Something with a direction.

"She left me instructions," Lior said. "We're going east."

"*We?*" said Sef.

But he followed her anyway, toward the tower, toward the guildmaster, toward the smoking ruin of everything either of them had understood about the world — and above them, one by one, the last stars over Vellum's Reach went out like candles pinched by a careful hand.
