## 5. The Cartographer's Refusal

The word *succession* followed Lior out of the council chamber like a debt collector who knew her address.

She did not run. Running was for people who had somewhere to go, and besides — she was a cartographer of the Guild of Impossible Things, and if there was one thing the last two days had taught her, it was that every road out of Vellum's Reach now existed only on paper. So she walked, at the pace of a woman with nowhere in particular to be, down the guildhall's long stair and through the Cloister of Instruments, past the great orrery that had stopped turning the night the stars went out, its brass spheres hanging dumb as dropped coins.

*Debtor-in-succession.* They had said it the way one says *rain expected by evening*. As though it were weather. As though she had not spent eleven years teaching herself a dead woman's cipher in stolen hours, telling herself it was diligence, telling herself it was *work*, when all along she had been reading her mother's handwriting because she missed her mother's handwriting.

The ledgers were a covenant. Her mother had written them. And now four surviving guildmasters, their faces lit by lamps that guttered whenever the wards shuddered, had looked across a table at her and informed her that the debt was hers.

She reached her own chamber on the third floor of the map-wing and locked the door behind her, which was a ritual gesture and nothing more. Locks kept out burglars. Nothing in this city needed to burgle anymore; the things walking Vellum's Reach wore borrowed shapes and came in through doors that had stopped believing they were doors.

Her room was small and precise: drafting table, ink stones, forty-one rolled maps filed by region, a cot she slept in perhaps five hours a night. On the wall above the table hung the only thing she owned that was not an instrument — a child's drawing, faded almost to nothing, of a woman holding a little girl's hand on a hill above the city. She had drawn it at six. Her mother had vanished three years later, and Lior had never taken the drawing down, because taking it down would have been admitting it was a relic instead of a promise.

She began packing anyway. Not because she had decided anything. Because her hands needed work, and work was the only prayer she knew.

Compass, calipers, the good pen with the split nib. Ink — all of it, even the inferior black. Chalk. Her field ledger, the small one, the one with her own cipher in it, the one thing in the world written entirely in her hand. She rolled maps without looking at them and then, halfway through the fourth, made herself look.

It was the survey of the Northreach Road, drawn the year before last, in summer, when the wards still hummed and the border between realms was a line you could stand beside and feel like standing beside a warm wall. She remembered the day she'd drawn it. She remembered the smell of cut hay from the farms east of the road, the warden checkpoint at the milestone where Sef —

She put the map down as if it had burned her.

Sef. Warden Sef Corren, who had dragged her out of the burning archive by the collar of her coat, who had stood in front of the guildmasters yesterday and said, in that flat, stubborn voice of his, *I trust her reading of the pages*, and who had been stripped of rank for it by sundown. Disgraced for trusting her. For trusting the daughter of the woman whose signature sat at the head of every covenant page like a brand.

He had found her afterward in the corridor and said nothing, which was somehow worse than anything he could have said. Just stood there, warden's grey gone from his shoulders, wearing the plain coat of a man who belonged to no company at all, and waited until she met his eyes, and then nodded once, as if confirming a delivery.

She had hated him a little for that nod. She hated him a little now, remembering it. It was easier than hating her mother, and she was saving what hatred she had left for the guildmasters, and underneath all of it, in the cellar where she kept the things she refused to look at, was the truth she could not yet afford: that fury at being conscripted into a debt was a much more comfortable emotion than grief at being abandoned by the only person who had ever signed anything on her behalf.

Lior finished packing. Then she unrolled the Northreach Road again, smoothed it flat, and did the thing she had been avoiding since the council chamber broke apart into argument.

She checked whether the road still existed.

Cartography has rules, and the first rule is that a map is a lie agreed upon. A map is not the country; it is a set of claims about the country, and the country is free to disagree. Every cartographer learns this in her first year, usually by leading a survey party down a road that a flood took out the winter before. But there are degrees of disagreement. There is *the bridge is out*, and there is *the river moved*, and then there is what Lior saw now, which was the ink itself going strange under her lamp — the road-line still black, still confident, but *unattached*, floating over terrain that no longer matched it, the way a name floats over a face you can no longer connect it to.

She pressed her palm flat against the parchment and closed her eyes, and did the other thing, the secret thing, the thing she had taught herself from her mother's ledgers: she read the road the way the covenant read it.

And the road answered.

Not in words. In absence. The Northreach Road had been mapped by a surveyor named Aldous Ferrin ninety-one years ago, and its existence in the world had been renewed each season by the guild's impossible trades — a shadow here, a year there, payments flowing north along the covenant lines like blood through a limb. The trades had stopped when her mother vanished. Eleven years of arrears. And so the road, which had existed because someone once drew it and many someones had paid for it, had begun — gently, politely, the way everything in this catastrophe happened — to stop.

The farms were gone first. Then the milestone. The wardens' checkpoint at the north gate still stood, but the land beyond it was softening, losing its edges, becoming the unmapped dark that pressed against the city like deep water against a hull.

Every road out of Vellum's Reach was dying the same death. Except —

Except the ones written into the covenant itself. The ledger-routes. Her mother had mapped them in cipher, threaded through the trade-entries like veins through a leaf: paths that existed not because anyone had surveyed them but because the covenant *needed* corridors between the realms, and had written its own roads into its own terms. Roads that remembered being drawn. Roads that would exist as long as the covenant did — and the covenant, whatever else had failed, was not dead. It was starving. Starving things cling hardest to their bones.

Only she could read them. That was the trap inside the trap. The guildmasters had not merely named her debtor-in-succession; they had named her the only person alive who could find the door out of a burning house, and then expressed surprise, hurt even, when she did not thank them.

Lior sat back. Her lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft, and for a moment the light went the color of old honey, and she felt — distinctly, unmistakably — the sensation of being *read*. Something on the other side of the wall of the world, consulting its books.

"Find your own cartographer," she said aloud, to the room, to the dark, to the ledger on her table. Her voice cracked on the last word, and she despised herself for it.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. There was no other cartographer. There was no one else. There was only her, and the cipher her mother had left in her hands like a loaded weapon, and the choice the guildmasters had tried to make for her: succeed, or let both realms go dark together.

Well. She had refused worse offers from better liars.

* * *

She left the guildhall at the hour the bells had stopped ringing — the bells had gone quiet two nights ago, all of them, at once, as if the city had been asked to hold its breath — and went down through the Cloister and out the postern door into the Saltmarket, where the stalls stood empty and the lamplighters had given up pretending. Above her, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover nothing, which has texture and depth and the promise of clearing. This was a nothing with intention behind it. A nothing that had been *called home*, the guildmaster had said, though he would not say called home by whom, or for what payment.

The north gate was a crush of refugees and rumor. Wardens who still wore their grey held the line at the archway, and beyond it she could see the problem everyone was shouting about: the Northreach Road simply ended. Not blocked. *Ended*, twenty paces past the gate, dissolving into a darkness that was not night but absence-of-map, a darkness in which the eye slid off everything and found nothing to rest on. People stood at the edge and threw stones into it and the stones made no sound of landing.

Lior did not go to the gate. She went along the wall, west, counting arches, until she reached the old customs yard where the guild kept its survey stores, and there she unrolled the smallest of her mother's ledger-pages against a barrel, by shielded lamplight, and read.

There. A route. Not the Northreach — something older, something that predated the walls, a covenant-road that ran from a place her mother had marked only with the cipher-glyph for *threshold* out toward the eastern hills. It existed because it had been written into the terms of trade in the covenant's second revision, one hundred and forty years ago. It had never been walked by ordinary feet. It did not appear on any map but hers.

It also, according to the marginal note in her mother's hand — that hand, that slanted, impatient, beautiful hand — passed through territory the covenant had *traded away*, which meant territory now populated by exactly the things the trades had carried off. Shadows. Years. Names nobody used anymore.

Lior folded the page with hands that were not quite steady.

"You're leaving."

She did not jump. She was proud of that. She turned, and there he was, of course, leaning against the customs-yard wall in his plain disgraced coat, arms crossed, watching her with the expression of a man who had been waiting a long time and intended to wait longer if necessary.

"Warden Corren." She put weight on the title she knew stung.

"Not anymore. You heard."

"I heard you vouched for me in front of four guildmasters and got broken for it. I didn't ask you to."

"No," Sef said. "You didn't ask anyone for anything. That's rather the whole problem with you, Lior Voss." He pushed off the wall and came closer, and up close she could see he hadn't slept either, and that he had packed, actually packed, a warden's kit reduced to a bedroll and a knife and a lantern with a cracked shutter. "You're going east. Through the traded lands. Alone."

"I'm going wherever I'm going, and how I go about it is none of—"

"The roads are gone," he said, flatly, cutting through her. "I've walked the wall since moonrise. North, south, west — all of it's coming apart past the first mile. Whatever you're reading in those pages of yours, it's the only way out of this city that still exists, and you're the only person who can read it, and you're going to walk into the dark alone because asking for help would mean admitting you need something, and gods forbid the great cartographer need anything from anyone—"

"Stop." Her voice came out low and dangerous, and she was distantly surprised to hear it, because she hadn't known she was this angry until it was already out. "Stop talking as if you know me. You don't know me. You dragged me out of a fire because the guildmasters told you to, and you vouched for me because—" she faltered, because she genuinely did not know why, and the not-knowing made it worse, "—because it was the decent thing, and decency isn't a claim on me. You want absolution for your ruined career? Go to the guildmasters. I'm not the one who took your rank."

"No," Sef agreed quietly. "Your mother did. When she stopped paying. When she vanished and left the whole covenant to rot and left you holding the cipher and left the rest of us holding the dark." He said it without cruelty, which was the unforgivable part. "I'm not here for absolution. I'm here because someone has to witness what happens out there. Someone from the bright side of the border, with a name and a rank I no longer have and a memory that works. You're going to do something in the dark, Lior — I don't know what, and neither do you yet — and things done in the dark with no witness have a way of never having happened at all."

The wind came off the unmapped dark and touched them both, and it smelled of nothing at all, which was the most frightening smell Lior had ever encountered. Behind them the city huddled. Ahead, past the customs yard, the covenant-route waited in her mother's cipher like a sentence half-finished.

"And if I refuse?" she said. "If I refuse the succession, refuse the debt, refuse all of it — if I just give the pages to the guild and let them find another reader?"

"They'll find none. And you know it, because you've already checked." He was right, and the knowing of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. He softened, barely. "Refusing the debt doesn't dissolve it. It just means it falls on someone with weaker hands. You told the Reckoners' question to the guildmasters yourself — I heard you through the door. *Who signed on my behalf?* Fine. Don't inherit it. But somebody forged this covenant into a chain, and the only person positioned to reforge it into something else is currently rolling up her maps and pretending she's running away."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. The lamp between them guttered and steadied.

"I don't forgive you," she said finally. "For the speech. Or the nod in the corridor. Or for being right, which is the worst thing you've done."

"Understood," said Sef, and shouldered his pack.

They went out through the customs yard together, not quite side by side, a deliberate arm's length of resentment between them, to the place where the city's last true cobbles gave way to ground that was only mostly sure of itself. There, by shielded lamplight, Lior read the threshold-glyph aloud in her mother's cipher, and the air ahead of them remembered being a road.

It rose out of the dark the way a thought rises: hesitant, then certain. Old paving, older than the walls, older than the guild, running east into country that had been traded away and had learned to live without being looked at. Somewhere out there were the shadows, and the years, and the names, and — pointed toward by a silent figure with her mother's hands, if the tollhouse rumors were anything — perhaps an answer to what her mother had actually done.

Lior stepped onto the road first. It held.

Behind her, Sef closed the shutter of his lantern to a thread of light, and the dark closed over them both like water, and the last thing Lior saw of Vellum's Reach was the guildhall spire, cracked and crownless, black against a sky with no stars — and the last thing she felt, beneath her boots, faint and rhythmic and patient, was the road remembering her mother's footsteps, and waiting, with terrible gentleness, to learn hers.
