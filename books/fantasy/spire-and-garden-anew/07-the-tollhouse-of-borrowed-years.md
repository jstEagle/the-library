## 7. The Tollhouse of Borrowed Years

Sef had stood watch on the border for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once been afraid of the dark itself. He had feared what came out of it — the ward-breakers, the smugglers of impossible goods, the things that wore borrowed shapes — but the dark had always been a wall to him, a thing with an outside and an inside, and his whole life had been spent on the lit side of that wall.

The road had cured him of that.

It was not a road, exactly. That was the trouble. It was the memory of a road, held together by ink and intention, and walking it felt like walking on someone's certainty. Beneath his boots the surface was firm enough — packed earth the color of old paper — but at the edges, where the light of Lior's lantern failed, the world simply stopped being decided. He had watched a stone at the roadside forget its own shape twice in one hour: once as stone, once as something like a sleeping animal, and once as nothing at all, just a hole in the dark with opinions.

"Stop looking at the edges," Lior said, without turning around. She walked ahead of him the way she did everything — precisely, quietly, as if the unmapped dark were merely a poorly organized archive she intended to correct. "They look back."

"I know," Sef said. "That's why I'm looking."

She glanced over her shoulder then, and in the lantern light her face was thinner than it had been three days ago, when they'd left the smoking ruin of Vellum's Reach behind them. Three days since the wards fell. Three days since he'd dragged her out of the burning archive by the collar of her coat while the guild's four centuries of careful records went up like a festival pyre. Three days since the guildmasters had stripped him of his rank in front of everyone — *for trusting the cartographer*, they'd said, as though faith were a crime worse than arson — and he had walked out of the guildhall with nothing but his warden's coat and a stubbornness he hadn't known was a form of loyalty until it was too late to be anything else.

"The tollhouse should be close," Lior said. "The ledger shows it here." She tapped the page she carried strapped to a board on her forearm, the way other travelers carried a shield. The covenant-ledger routes glowed faintly under her fingertip, lines of her mother's cipher rearranging themselves as the road remembered or forgot itself. "A day past the second milestone. If the milestones still exist."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we're lost in a country made of everything my guild ever threw away, and we'll have to ask directions from a sold shadow." She said it flatly, but Sef had learned to hear the grief underneath her flatness. She carried it the way he carried his disgrace — close against the ribs, where it could warm or cut depending on the hour.

* * *

They smelled the tollhouse before they saw it.

Not smoke, exactly. Something older than smoke — the scent of candle-wax burned down to the nub, of paper gone soft with handling, of years. That was the only word for it. The air took on the smell of years passing, of birthdays kept and forgotten, of long afternoons that had belonged to someone once and belonged to no one now.

The structure rose out of the dark like a cairn built by a desperate giant. It was made of doors — hundreds of them, thousands, stacked and mortared together into walls, each door a different wood, a different age, some carved and some plain, some no wider than a child's shoulders and some tall as barn gates. And every door, Sef realized as they drew closer, bore marks. Not hinges. Marks. Tallies scratched into the grain, names worn half away, dates in scripts he didn't recognize — and beneath all of it, faint as breath on glass, the same cipher that covered Lior's ledger pages.

"It's built from lifetimes," Lior whispered. She had stopped walking. Her hand lay flat against the ledger board as if steadying herself against a ship's roll. "Stacked. Someone traded their years and the years were — made into this. Into a place. A door is a threshold; a life is a threshold; I suppose the covenant doesn't distinguish."

"That's obscene," Sef said.

"Yes," she said simply, and started forward again, and he understood that she was cataloguing the obscenity somewhere behind her eyes, adding it to the growing ledger of what her guild — her mother's guild, her inheritance — had actually been doing all these centuries. He almost pitied her. Almost. Pity was a luxury he'd surrendered along with his rank.

The tollkeeper sat in a doorway that served as a window, and Sef's stomach turned over when he saw it, because the tollkeeper was not one person. It was a shifting collage — a young man's hands, an old woman's posture, a middle-aged face that kept trying several different expressions and settling on none. Its eyes were the worst part. They were patient the way ledgers are patient.

"Travelers," it said, in a voice assembled from several voices, none of them quite agreeing on pitch. "The road continues east. Passage is owed."

"We know how tolls work," Sef said, stepping forward before Lior could. This, at least, was familiar ground. Checkpoints, tariffs, the grammar of authority — he'd enforced it for eleven years. "What's the rate?"

The tollkeeper's face rippled with something that might have been amusement. "You are a warden of the bright realm. You know the rate. You collected it often enough on your side of the wall."

"Years," Lior said quietly. "It takes years."

"A portion," the tollkeeper agreed. "Freely given. The covenant requires willingness. Coerced payment sours the mortar." It gestured with a borrowed hand at the door-walls around it. "Why do you think the structure holds? Every plank consented."

Sef felt something cold settle into his chest, and recognized it, because he'd felt it once before — the night the wards fell and he'd watched a thing wearing his dead brother's shape walk calmly through the gap. It was the cold of understanding arriving faster than courage could organize a defense.

He stepped between Lior and the tollhouse window.

"How much?" he said. "For two passages east."

"Sef." Lior's voice cracked across the space between them like a whip. "Don't."

"It's years, not blood. I'm thirty-four. I can spare—"

"You don't even know what you'd be sparing *from*." She pushed past him, and there was fury in her face, but underneath the fury — he saw it clearly, and it undid him more than the tollhouse ever could — there was fear. For him. "Look at the keeper. Look at what it's made of. Those aren't portions. Those are people who gave until there wasn't enough left to walk away with. You'd hand over your years to a magistrate of the realm that ate your career, and you'd do it without blinking, because that's what you do — you pay what's asked, you stand where you're posted, you—"

"I swore an oath," Sef said.

"To a guild that erased the roads and blamed you for noticing!" Her voice rang off the door-walls, and somewhere in the stacked lifetimes, something stirred — a door creaked on hinges that were also knuckles, also spines. Lior lowered her voice, but it lost none of its edge. "Your oath was to protect people. So protect yourself for five minutes and let me think."

* * *

She thought. Sef watched her do it, and it was like watching a cartographer survey a battlefield.

She paced the length of the tollhouse front, reading the door-marks, running her fingers over the cipher-tallies, murmuring fragments of her mother's notation under her breath. The lantern-light moved across her face in slow tides. Twice she stopped entirely, went very still, and Sef knew she was cross-referencing something against the ledger on her arm — the living text answering the dead structure, or failing to.

Finally she turned back to the tollkeeper.

"The covenant requires freely given payment," she said. "Of value commensurate with passage. Yes?"

"The covenant requires it," the tollkeeper agreed.

"Then define value." She lifted her chin. "Because I've read the original clauses, keeper. All of them. The ones your Reckoners don't quote anymore. Value under the covenant is not measured in quantity. It's measured in cost. In what the giving costs the giver." Her voice didn't shake. Sef marveled at that. His own voice would have shaken. "So I'm offering a trade. Not years. A memory. One memory, whole and mine, of the person whose debt brought me onto this road. Take it or refuse it, but measure it honestly."

The tollkeeper went still — truly still, for the first time, all its borrowed parts aligning into something like attention. When it spoke again, its voices had found a single pitch, and the unity of it was somehow more frightening than the discord.

"A memory of the cartographer Maren Voss," it said. "Mother of the debtor-in-succession. The guild has never once offered such a coin. The guild trades shadows, hours, the outer rooms of the self. Never the load-bearing walls." Its many-voiced mouth curved. "We will weigh it."

"How?" Sef demanded. "How do you weigh a memory?"

"The way everything is weighed here," the tollkeeper said. "By what remains when it's taken."

Lior closed her eyes. Opened them. "Then take it," she said, "and see."

* * *

Later — much later, sitting in the lee of the tollhouse while the road east unspooled faintly before them like thread pulled from a spool — Sef would try to describe what happened, and fail, and try again, and fail better.

There was no light show. No tearing. The tollkeeper simply reached out with those young-old hands and touched Lior's temple, and she gasped — a small sound, quickly strangled — and then she swayed, and Sef caught her elbow, and she straightened, and she was still Lior, still herself, still carrying the ledger and the lantern and the furious competence he'd come to rely on against his will.

But when she looked at the tollhouse, her face changed.

"What did it take?" he asked. "What did you give it?"

"I don't know," she said. And then, horribly, gently: "That's the point, isn't it. I knew that. I agreed knowing I wouldn't get to keep the knowledge of what I'd lost. That was part of the price." She pressed her palm flat against the ledger board. "I can feel the shape of the hole. Like a tooth gone from a row. My tongue keeps going to it. There was a memory of my mother, and it mattered, and now it's mortar."

The tollkeeper had already begun to work. Sef watched, sickened and transfixed, as the memory became architecture — as a new door formed in the wall of stacked lives, grown rather than built, its wood bearing a grain he somehow recognized without being able to name. A woman's handwriting surfaced in the cipher-tallies across its face. The door fit perfectly, the way a word fits a sentence, and the whole tollhouse seemed to settle around it, deeper, sturdier, as if it had been missing a load-bearing wall and only now understood its own strength.

"She's in the walls now," Lior said. Her voice was steady. Her hand, Sef noticed, was not. "Part of the road east. Everyone who passes will lean on her a little and never know."

"Lior—"

"Don't." She held up the hand that wasn't shaking. "I did the arithmetic. Years would have bought us passage and cost you decades. A memory buys passage and costs me one evening I can no longer grieve properly. That's a fair trade, Sef. That's a *cheap* trade. Stop looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm brave." She turned away, shouldering her pack. "I'm not brave. I'm solvent. There's a difference, and my mother's ghost knows I've had eleven days to learn it."

* * *

They walked east through the rest of that night, because the tollkeeper told them the road was safest before dawn, when even the dark realm's hungriest things were busy digesting whatever they'd caught at dusk. Sef carried both lanterns. Lior hadn't asked him to. He'd simply taken the second one, and she'd simply let him, and neither of them had remarked on it, which was its own kind of conversation.

Somewhere past the third milestone — which existed, a leaning post of gray stone with her mother's cipher crawling down its length like ivy — Lior finally spoke.

"Why did you offer first? Your years. You didn't hesitate. You didn't even negotiate." She wasn't accusing him. She sounded genuinely puzzled, and the puzzlement hurt more than anger would have. "You've seen what the covenant does with payments. You stood in that burning archive. You know what these ledgers cost people. And you put your hand out anyway."

Sef considered lying. He was, he reflected, remarkably bad at it — it was probably why he'd made such a poor warden and such an easy mark for disgrace.

"Because it was the first order anyone's given me in eleven years that made sense," he said. "Protect the person who can read the map. Pay the toll. Get her east. It was simple. I've been drowning in complicated since the wards fell — guildmasters lying, roads vanishing, my whole career revealed as a fence operation for stolen years. And then there was one clear thing. One order I could follow with a clean heart." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than he expected. "Turns out I'm not a warden anymore. Turns out I'm just a man who guards things. Old habit."

"You're guarding me."

"I'm guarding the map," he said. "The map happens to have legs."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly suppressed, but real. It was the first time he'd heard her laugh, and he filed it away with the strange tenderness of a man discovering a room in his own house he'd never entered.

They walked on. The dark pressed close at the edges of the lantern-light, full of discarded things — he could feel them now, the way you feel weather coming. Sold shadows huddled in the verges like beggars at a fire. Forgotten names drifted past on currents of air too faint to stir the dust. Once, something small and quick crossed the road ahead of them, and Lior froze, and it paused, and in the lantern-glow Sef saw that it was a childhood — someone's entire childhood, traded away, running wild and feral through the unmapped country with the bright, heedless speed of the very young.

Lior watched it go until the dark took it.

"They're everywhere," she said softly. "Everything we ever paid. My whole guild, four hundred years of 'impossible trades,' and it was all — *this*. A country of the discarded." Her jaw tightened. "And my mother walked into it. On purpose. And I just paid a tollhouse with a piece of her so I could follow."

"You could turn back," Sef said. It was not a challenge. It was, he realized as he said it, an offer — the last clean exit he could hold open for her, before the road narrowed and the debts deepened and turning back stopped being a thing either of them could afford.

"No," Lior said. And then, quieter, with the weariness of someone setting down a weight she'd been told was hers to carry: "No. But thank you for making it a question instead of an order. Nobody's done that yet. The guild conscripted me. The Reckoners sentenced me. Even my mother—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again. "Even my mother signed my name into things before I was born, I think. Maybe especially before I was born."

Ahead of them, the road bent eastward around a hill that had no business existing — a hill shaped, Sef thought uneasily, like a clenched fist — and beyond it, far off, there was a smudge on the horizon that might have been cloud and might have been smoke and might, if you squinted, if you believed, have been the faintest suggestion of a glow. Red-gold. Hearth-colored.

Lior saw it at the same moment he did. He heard her breath catch.

"That's east," she whispered. "That's the Last Hearth. Or the direction of it." She fumbled the ledger board up, scanning, and her finger stopped on a line of cipher that had not been there yesterday — he was nearly certain it had not been there yesterday — fresh ink gleaming wetly in the lantern-light, in her mother's unmistakable hand:

*The fire remembers who fed it. Come hungry, little cartographer. Come honest.*

Below the line, as they watched, a second line began to write itself, letter by letter, the pen-strokes appearing out of nothing like frost forming on a window:

*But hurry. The stars are not the only things being called home.*

Sef looked up from the page to the distant glow, and the glow looked, suddenly, less like a beacon and more like a wound — the one bright place in a dark that was closing around it like a fist around a coal.

"Hurry," he repeated, and broke into a run, and heard her boots strike the remembered road behind him, keeping pace.
