## 9. The Court of Unpaid Debts

They did not bind her hands. That was the first thing Lior noticed, and the thing that frightened her most.

Chains, she could have understood. Ropes, manacles, the crude arithmetic of force — all of that belonged to a world she knew, a world where the Guild of Impossible Things kept its own cells beneath the archive for debtors who tried to flee their entries. But the Reckoners simply walked on either side of her through the streets of the unmapped dark, tall figures in robes the color of ledger-covers, their faces smooth as unmarked vellum, and they trusted her to keep walking because there was nowhere else to walk. The road itself had been entered into evidence, she realized. She was not being escorted. She was being *filed*.

Sef stumbled behind them, his warden's coat torn at the shoulder where something with too many joints had gripped him during the capture. He had fought. Of course he had fought; he was Sef, who had once held a bridge against a flood with nothing but his rank and his stubbornness, and lost both. Two Reckoners had turned toward him with the unhurried patience of clerks approaching a smudged entry, and Lior had shouted — *he's mine, he's my witness, you can't take what's attached to a case* — words she invented on the spot, praying the grammar of this place would hold them. It had. The Reckoners had paused, inclined their smooth heads as though consulting some inner docket, and permitted Sef to continue as *evidence in motion*.

Now the road rose toward a structure that hurt to look at directly.

The Court of Unpaid Debts was built like a scale, or perhaps like an open ledger laid spine-up: two vast wings of black stone balanced upon a central hall, and from the hall's peak hung chains that ended in nothing, swaying slightly, as if waiting for weights that were always about to arrive. Light came from within — not firelight, not starlight, but a pale luminance like the glow off fresh ink, rising through seams in the floor. As they crossed the threshold, Lior felt it pass over her skin the way a magnifying glass passes over a page: reading her.

*Name,* said the light, though no mouth moved. *Lior Voss. Cartographer, Third Chair, Guild of Impossible Things. Daughter of Maerin Voss, Cartographer, First Chair, deceased — *

*Missing,* Lior said aloud, before she could stop herself. Her voice cracked strangely in the ink-lit air. "Not deceased. Missing. There's a difference, and your own records should know it."

The nearest Reckoner stopped. Its face remained blank, but something behind the blankness shifted, the way a page shifts when a hand turns it. When it spoke, its voice was dry and even, the voice of someone reading aloud a figure they have checked three times.

"The distinction is noted. The distinction is also, at present, unsupported by payment. This court recognizes only what has been settled."

"Then we're going to have a very short trial," Lior said, "because nothing in my life has ever been settled."

She hadn't meant it as a joke. But somewhere in the high dark of the court, something that might have been laughter moved — a rustle, a turning of many pages at once — and the Reckoners resumed walking, and Lior understood that she had just been entered into the record as *disposition: contentious*, and that here, disposition was destiny.

* * *

The courtroom was a bowl of benches descending toward a floor of polished obsidian, and on that floor, drawn in lines of faint silver, was a map.

Lior's breath caught. She knew maps the way other people knew their mother's voice — and this one was wrong in every way that mattered. It showed both realms, the bright country of Vellum's Reach and the dark expanse around her now, but not side by side, not divided by any border. They were drawn as one continuous surface, folded over itself like a letter, the mountains of one realm becoming the valleys of the other, rivers running through both without pausing at any line. In the center, where a cartographer would put a compass rose, stood a mark she recognized with a lurch: the sigil of the Last Hearth, a flame inside a circle, drawn small and precise and *old*.

Someone had mapped this before her. Someone had believed both realms were one country.

Before she could study it further, the Reckoners guided her to a low stand at the map's edge, and Sef to a smaller one beside it, and the benches filled — not with people, at first, but with presences. Shadows sat beside shadows. A woman made entirely of stacked years, her skin flickering through ages like a shuffled deck, took a seat near the front. Something vast and patient coiled in the highest gallery, too large for the space and fitting it anyway. And scattered among them, wearing borrowed shapes that never quite fit — a jaw slightly wrong, eyes set a half-inch too far apart — sat the traded things, the ones the covenant had bought and sold, come to watch another account be read.

Lior looked at them and thought of the tollhouse, of the shadow with her mother's hands pointing east, and had to press her nails into her palm to keep her face still.

A gavel struck. No one held it. It simply sounded, three times, from everywhere.

"This court sits," said a voice from the bench — and there was a bench now, a great slab of black stone that had not been there a moment before, and behind it stood three Reckoners taller than the rest, their blank faces marked each with a single line of silver script. "In the matter of Voss versus Arrears. The debtor-in-succession stands accused of the debts of Maerin Voss, First Chair, outstanding at the time of her departure: four hundred and eleven trades unredeemed, nine years of interest accrued, and one covenant clause invoked without remittance. How does the succession answer?"

Lior's heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Every instinct she had — the guild-trained instinct, the instinct drilled into every cartographer since childhood — said *accept the terms, negotiate the reduction, survive*. That was how the guild survived. That was how her mother had survived, presumably, signing and signing and signing until the day she signed herself away.

But her mother had also taught her cipher in the margins of ledgers, in games disguised as lessons, and one of the first rules of cipher was this: *the message is never the words on the page.*

"I don't answer to that name," Lior said.

The court went still. Even the ink-light seemed to lean closer.

"The succession answers," the central Reckoner said, "because the debt descends."

"Show me the signature."

A pause. Then, from the floor of the court, the silver map began to move, lines crawling and reassembling, and up out of the obsidian rose a page — vast, translucent, hanging in the air like a captured moth. Upon it, in a hand Lior knew better than her own reflection, was written a trade agreement. Her mother's handwriting. Her mother's careful, looping, unmistakable script, committing the Guild of Impossible Things — and naming, in the final line, *her heirs and assigns* — to the maintenance of the covenant between realms.

"There," the Reckoner said. "The signature."

"That is my mother's signature," Lior said. "It is not mine. I was not consulted. I was not present. I was a child learning letters while she bound me to four hundred and eleven debts." She stepped forward, off her mark, and no Reckoner stopped her, and she understood dimly that this too was procedure: the accused was permitted to approach the evidence. "You are magistrates. You enforce contracts. So tell me the first principle of contract, the principle underneath all your precision." She pointed at the hanging page, at the final line, at the words *her heirs and assigns*. "What is a signature? It is a self, given willingly, to bind that self. One self. My mother could sign away her own name, her own years, her own shadow — that was hers to spend, however terribly she spent it. But she signed *mine*. She wrote a claim on a person who did not exist yet, who could not consent, who had never seen these pages." Her voice was shaking now, and she let it shake. "And a claim on what was never yours — what is that called, in your law?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down like deep water.

The left-hand Reckoner spoke first, its voice slower, almost reluctant. "A claim on what was never yours... is theft."

"Then this entire covenant of succession," Lior said, turning to face the benches, facing the shadows and the stacked-years woman and the vast coiled thing in the gallery, "is built on theft. Not just mine. All of it. Every heir conscripted, every child bound, every successor named in a line they never read — the whole architecture of inherited debt is theft, laundered through generations until it looks like law." She drew a breath that burned. "I am not here to argue that my mother owed nothing. Maybe she did. Maybe the guild did. I'm a cartographer; I follow lines to their source. But you cannot collect from me what was stolen *into* my name. You cannot make the stolen goods pay for the thief. If your law means anything — if all this precision, all this merciless bookkeeping, means anything at all — then the debt dies with the one who signed it, and everything after is a new question."

"A new question," the central Reckoner repeated. Its blank face gave back nothing, but the silver script upon it seemed to writhe, recalculating. "And who answers new questions? The old covenant provided answers. Order. Payment. Without enforcement, there is only—" it paused, searching its own records, and found, apparently, nothing— "*unmapped territory.*"

"Yes," Lior said quietly. "There is. I'm a cartographer of impossible things. Unmapped territory is where I live."

The rustle went through the court again, that page-turning murmur, but different now — sharper, agitated. In the benches, the woman of stacked years had risen to her feet, her faces cycling rapidly, and Lior saw with a start that several of the traded things had risen too, silently, their borrowed shapes leaning forward. They were not looking at the judges. They were looking at *her*.

The right-hand Reckoner leaned toward its colleagues. Their conference was conducted in total silence, three blank faces inclined together, and Lior had the mad impression of watching three copies of the same ledger disagree with themselves.

At last the central Reckoner straightened.

"The argument is noted as sound," it said, and something in the court's pale light dimmed, as if the room itself flinched. "The precedent is noted as dangerous. The debt, however, remains. Four hundred and eleven trades do not vanish because their inheritance is illegitimate. What was traded was given by the bright realm and received by ours, and it cannot be returned by argument." It raised one long hand, and the hanging page descended, folding itself smaller and smaller until it lay on the obsidian before Lior's feet, no larger than a card. "The court therefore rules paradoxically: the succession is void, and the debt stands. These are incompatible findings, and the court resolves them thus — the debtor shall satisfy the arrears not as heir, which she is not, but as author, which she claims to be. You argued yourself outside our law, cartographer. Very well. Finish the covenant *your* way. Reforge it at the Last Hearth, willingly, in terms both realms accept. Succeed, and the accounts close clean — yours, your mother's, the guild's, all of them. Fail..." The blank face tilted, almost gently. "...and you will be unmade, and entered as *paid in full*."

The gavel struck once. The court began, everywhere at once, to rise.

"And if she refuses?" Sef's voice, raw and loud, cracking across the benches. He was on his feet, disgraced coat and all, pointing at the judges with a hand that shook. "Your own rule — she proved no one can be bound without consent. So she doesn't consent. She walks away. What then?"

Every eye — every blankness, every shadow — turned back to the floor of the court.

The central Reckoner regarded Sef for a long moment. Then it regarded Lior.

"She does not walk away," it said, "because she has already told this court who she is. A cartographer goes where the map is unfinished." The script on its face settled, final as drying ink. "The court is adjourned. Witness — you are attached to the debtor. Guard her well. Your testimony will be required."

Lior picked up the folded page from the obsidian. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. Somewhere east, past roads that remembered being drawn, the last dragon-fire was guttering, and the stars above two realms were dark, and she had just talked her way out of a prison and into a sentence with the whole sky as collateral.

Beside her, Sef exhaled shakily. "That was either the bravest or the stupidest thing I've ever watched."

"It's usually both," Lior said. She tucked the page into her coat, over her heart, where the ledgers used to sit. "Come on. We have a covenant to break."

Above the court, in the highest gallery, the vast coiled presence uncurled and began, silently, to follow.
