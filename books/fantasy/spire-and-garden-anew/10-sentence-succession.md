## 10. Sentence: Succession

The Court of Unpaid Debts had no ceiling. Lior noticed this first, in the strange way the mind seizes on irrelevancies when it is trying not to think about its own ending: above the three magistrates, where a roof should have been, there was only darkness — but not empty darkness. It was the unmapped dark itself, pressing down like water over glass, and through it moved shapes that might have been stars that had never been lit, or debts that had never been paid, drifting in slow orbits around the bench.

The chamber was built of stacked paper. That was the second thing she understood, once her eyes stopped watering from the cold. Walls of compressed ledgers, thousands upon thousands of them, their spines worn to velvet by handling, their pages breathing faintly when the air shifted. The whole court was an archive standing upright, and Lior felt a terrible kinship with it. She knew what it was to be made of records.

She stood in the center of a circle drawn in ash — real ash, she realized; it crumbled and reformed at the edges as though the circle were digesting itself — and the three Reckoners regarded her from their bench of black wood.

They were not shaped like people. They were shaped like verdicts. The one on the left was tall and narrow as a quill, its face a smooth plane except for a single vertical seam where a mouth should be. The center magistrate was broad and patient-seeming, its surface rippling like ink settling in water, and it was this one that had spoken so far, in a voice like a page turning very slowly. The third was small, almost childlike, and had not moved since the trial began, and Lior suspected it was the most dangerous of the three for exactly that reason.

Behind her, Sef stood where they had placed him — outside the ash circle, among the gallery of things that had come to watch. And what a gallery it was. Shadows with the posture of tradesmen. A woman whose outline flickered between middle-aged and ancient, a decade of her life visibly cycling through her. A man who was mostly hands. They did not jeer or murmur. They watched with the terrible attentiveness of creditors, because that was what they were, every one of them: entries in a ledger, owed or owing, gathered to see whether the daughter of Meren Voss would honor the account.

"Your argument," said the center Reckoner, "was theft."

Lior's throat was dry as old vellum. "My argument was *that* your covenant is theft."

"Yes." The seam-faced one's mouth opened a fraction, and something like laughter came out — dry, papery, awful. "We heard you."

She had argued for what felt like hours, though time in this place moved like syrup poured uphill. She had laid it out the way she'd lay out a map: here are the borders, here are the roads, here is the territory no one wants to name. Her mother had signed the covenant's renewals on behalf of two realms. On behalf of the bright realm — Vellum's Reach, the guilds, the cities that never knew their shadows were being sold — and on behalf of the dark realm too, whose inhabitants had never been asked whether they wished their years and names traded away by a woman with a ledger and good intentions. A contract signed on behalf of another, Lior had told them, is not a contract. It is a theft with better handwriting.

"You have made our task difficult," said the center Reckoner, "which we note is often the mark of a correct argument."

"Then release me."

"No."

The word fell into the ash circle and the ash drank it. Around the chamber, the gallery stirred — a rustle like wind through the paper walls, hundreds of pages turning at once.

"The debt stands," the Reckoner continued, unhurried. "You mistake the nature of what you argued. You proved the covenant was made without consent. This is true. We have known it was true for four hundred years."

Lior blinked. "You— *what?*"

"We are magistrates of unpaid debts," said the child-shaped one, and its voice was the voice of someone reading aloud in an empty room. "Do you imagine we do not know which debts are just? Every day we collect on bargains that should never have been struck. That is what makes us merciless. Mercy requires the belief that the law can be wrong in particular cases. We know it is wrong in all of them. It changes nothing. A debt unpaid does not vanish because it was unjustly incurred. It only becomes heavier, and finds new shoulders."

"So you admit it's unjust, and you'll enforce it anyway." The fury came up in her like floodwater, and she let it, because fury was warmer than fear. "That's not justice. That's just arithmetic with teeth."

"Yes," said all three Reckoners, together, without shame.

* * *

Sef moved forward then — one step, boots scraping on the paper floor — and a dozen shadows detached themselves from the walls to bar his way. He stopped, jaw tight, hands open at his sides. Lior had seen him face burning buildings and things wearing borrowed faces with the same set to his shoulders, and she understood suddenly that he wasn't bracing for danger. He was bracing against silence. Warden's instinct: stand between the harm and the person, even when standing is all you can do.

"The accused will speak when spoken to," said the seam-mouthed Reckoner, though whether to Sef or to her, Lior couldn't tell.

"I'm not finished speaking," Lior said.

The center magistrate's ink-ripple stilled. "No," it agreed. "You are not. That is precisely the finding of this court."

It rose. When it moved, the whole chamber seemed to move with it, ledgers shifting in their walls like feathers ruffling.

"Hear the sentence. Your argument is sound: the covenant binds those who never signed it, and binding the unsigned is theft. Therefore you cannot be held to your mother's contract. Therefore you cannot inherit her debt." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "Therefore the debt has no holder at all. And a debt with no holder does not dissolve. It *falls*. It falls upon both realms equally, and it will crush them equally, and there is no one left to catch it — unless someone chooses to hold it."

"That's not a sentence," Lior said slowly. "That's a trap dressed as a door."

"It is both," said the Reckoner, with what she could almost have sworn was approval. "All true doors are traps. All true traps are doors. You of all people should appreciate this — you are a cartographer. You know that every road is also a commitment."

The child-shaped one spoke next, reciting, and its recitation had the weight of law settling into place like stones into a wall:

"Lior Voss, found not bound, yet bound by the falling of the unbound debt. Sentenced to succession — not inherited, but *chosen*, which is the only succession this court recognizes. You shall take up the covenant and finish it your own way. Reforge it. Rewrite it. Burn it and write a better one, if you can. But it must be reforged willingly, by both realms, at the Last Hearth, before the final fire gutters out. Fail, and the debt falls entire. Fall, and—" the small magistrate tilted its head, "—you will be unmade. Not killed. Unmade. Your name returned to the ledger of names, your years returned to the tollhouse, your shadow returned to whoever holds it. As though you had never been traded at all."

The gallery rustled. Somewhere behind her, Lior heard Sef breathe out a word he probably thought was under his breath.

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "Refuse the sentence, I mean. Not the debt. Can I refuse?"

"You already refused once," said the seam-mouthed one, and its papery laugh came again. "In the guildhall, before the masters. We counted it. It is why you stand in the circle rather than simply standing in chains. Refusal is permitted. Refusal is always permitted. Refuse now, walk out of this court, go back to Vellum's Reach, and live whatever remains of a world with no covenant and no stars. Some choose that. We do not stop them. We only record them." Its mouth-seam widened by a hair. "The recording is the punishment."

Lior looked at the ash circle around her feet. It had stopped digesting itself. It lay still and fine as snowfall, and she understood that it was waiting — that everything in this room was waiting — for her answer.

Her mother had chosen this. That was the thought that kept cutting through everything else, sharp and unwelcome. Not fled. Not been taken. Chosen — walked into the dark with a ledger under her arm and signed herself into the space between realms, and never told her daughter, and never came home. Lior had spent two years telling herself a hundred stories about Meren Voss's disappearance, and every one of them had made her mother either a victim or a coward, and both stories had been easier than this one: that her mother had seen the same trap-door, the same falling debt, and had stepped onto it deliberately, and had trusted — had *assumed* — that Lior would follow.

Assumed. Or hoped. Or perhaps, in the end, had simply left the map where her daughter would find it, the way cartographers do, and let the road do the asking.

"I don't accept," Lior said.

The chamber went very still.

"I don't *accept*," she repeated, "because accepting means the sentence is yours and I'm only living inside it. I'm not accepting succession. I'm taking it. There's a difference, and if your court can't tell the difference, then your arithmetic has worse problems than teeth."

For a long moment none of the Reckoners moved. Then the center magistrate sat back down, and the sound of it was the sound of a book closing gently, and Lior felt the ash circle around her feet soften and blur at its edges, no longer a cage but a threshold.

"Noted," said the Reckoner. "The distinction is recorded. It may matter later. It usually does."

* * *

They were given until moonrise-equivalent — some hour the dark realm kept in its own way, marked by a slow brightening along the paper walls — to prepare for departure toward the Last Hearth. A Reckoner escort would accompany them to the edge of the court's jurisdiction, no further. Enforcement, the magistrates explained, ended where choice began. Beyond that line, whatever Lior did would be hers alone, and so would whatever happened to her.

She found Sef in the antechamber, sitting on a bench made of a single enormous ledger, turning his warden's badge over and over in his fingers. It was dented, half-melted from the night the archive burned, and he was looking at it the way a man looks at a key to a house that no longer exists.

"They took your rank," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard the escort mention it — *the warden who aided the debtor forfeits standing in the bright realm's service* — delivered with the same polite dread that had followed her since the stars went out.

"They didn't take anything," Sef said. "I put it on the table myself. Figured if I was going to be dragged into an unmapped country on the strength of your reading, I ought to be dragged as a free man." He turned the badge once more, then closed his fist around it. "Guildmaster Corvane wanted me to recant. Say I was deceived. Say you misled me about the ledgers." He glanced up, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "I told him the truth instead. That I read enough of your margins to know you were right before I knew what you were right about."

"Sef—"

"Don't." He stood, and tucked the ruined badge away inside his coat, over his heart, which was such a warden thing to do that Lior nearly laughed and nearly cried in the same breath. "Don't thank me and don't apologize. Both would just delay what I actually came to say."

He knelt.

Right there, on the paper floor of a Reckoner antechamber, with shadows watching from the seams in the walls, Sef of the Vellum's Reach wardens went down on one knee like a man swearing into a knightly order, and said, in the formal cadence of a testimony — the cadence every warden learned first, before swordwork, before wards:

"I, Sef, having witnessed, do swear witness. What I have seen, I will say. Where you go, my word goes. If you are unmade, I will carry the record of you back to the light and speak it until someone listens. If you succeed—" he paused, and something crooked and warm crossed his face, "—I will say I was there when it happened, and no one will believe me, and I will enjoy that immensely."

Lior stared at him. "You know what witness means here. In this realm. It's not ceremonial. The Reckoners said it themselves — testimony is currency. If it comes to it, they'll *spend* you."

"Then they'd better get a good price." He rose, brushing paper-dust from his knee. "You refused to inherit a debt you never signed. I'm choosing mine. Seems only fair you should have to live with at least one thing that's your fault."

And there it was — the trap and the door, both at once, the whole shape of her sentence rendered suddenly personal. She had walked into that court arguing that no one could bind another, and she was walking out of it bound twice over: once by a debt she had chosen, and once by a man who had chosen her, freely, stupidly, bravely, and neither bond had been forced on her, which meant neither could be dismissed. Chosen debt. The phrase arrived in her mind complete, like a line from a ledger written in a hand she was only beginning to recognize as her own.

"All right," she said quietly. "Witness."

The word settled between them, and somewhere in the paper walls, faint but unmistakable, a page turned — as if the dark realm itself had entered it into the record.

* * *

They left the Court of Unpaid Debts through a door made of the oldest ledger in the walls, its cover soft as skin, its title worn away. The escort of Reckoners walked before them without footsteps, and the gallery of traded things pressed close along the route — not threatening, Lior realized, but *watching*, the way people watch a ship leave harbor when some of them had hoped to be aboard.

At the edge of the jurisdiction, where the paper country gave way to a landscape of ash and long grass that remembered being fields, the lead Reckoner stopped.

"A last observation," it said, "free of charge, as courts sometimes are." Its ink-dark face turned toward the east, where — impossibly far away, at the rim of the world — a smudge of orange light stained the underside of the dark, like a coal breathing under a banked fire. The Last Hearth. Even from here, Lior could feel it, a warmth against her sternum, faint and failing. "Your mother stood where you stand. She was given the same sentence. She chose the same word — *taking*, not accepting. We have wondered, these two years, what she saw that made her leave so quickly afterward."

"She didn't tell you?"

"She told us nothing. She only asked us one question." The Reckoner began to turn away, then paused. "She asked: *if a covenant must be reforged by both realms willingly — how willing can anyone be, while the Reckoners still enforce the old one?*"

The magistrate's seam of a mouth opened slightly, closed.

"We did not answer her," it said. "We have not yet answered ourselves. Go east, Lior Voss. The fire has days. And be advised—" the papery dryness in its voice deepened into something almost human, almost regretful, "—we are not the only ones who heard her question. Others heard it too. Some of them intend to make sure it is never answered."

It dissolved into the dark like ink into water, and Lior stood looking east at that guttering stain of light, Sef's shoulder solid beside her, and understood that the hardest part of her sentence had not been pronounced in any court at all.

Somewhere between here and the Last Hearth, someone was waiting to make sure she failed — and they had a two-year head start.
