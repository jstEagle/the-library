## 11. The Guttering Hearth

Sef had spent his whole life believing that light was a thing you made — a lantern lit, a ward kindled, a torch thrust into the dark on your own authority. Standing at the rim of the crater and looking down at the Last Hearth, he understood for the first time that light could also be a thing you *kept*, the way you keep a promise, the way you keep vigil over the dying.

The fire was small.

That was the thing no one had told him, the thing the old warden's songs had gotten wrong in every verse. He had imagined a blaze to shame the sun, a pillar of dragon-flame visible from either border of the world. Instead, what burned at the bottom of that bowl of ash was barely larger than a cookfire — a low orange tongue of flame curled around something dark and vast and still, like a bird's nest built around a sleeping mountain. It guttered as they watched. It leaned east, then west, then steadied itself with an effort Sef could almost feel in his teeth, the way a tired man steadies himself against a wall.

"It's dying," Lior said beside him. Not a question. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when the ledgers said something she didn't want them to say.

"The fire has days," said the guide they'd followed out of the unmapped country — a woman made mostly of borrowed shadow who had never given them a name and whom Sef had privately begun to think of as the Ferryman, though she carried no boat. "Perhaps four. Perhaps five if the wind stays kind. The wind will not stay kind."

They stood at the edge of the hearth-light, where the ash began, and Sef looked down into the crater and saw what the songs had also gotten wrong: the Last Hearth was not alone, and it was not empty.

* * *

The refugees came into view gradually, the way things do when your eyes are adjusting between two different kinds of darkness. First the fires — dozens of them, small cookfires and hand-fires and palm-fires, scattered across the crater floor in rough rings around the central flame. Then the shapes hunched over them: hundreds of figures, maybe more, wrapped in whatever they'd carried out of whichever world they'd fled. Some of them cast shadows that moved wrong, lagging half a heartbeat behind their owners or pointing in directions the light didn't justify. Others cast none at all. A few of the shadows, Sef noticed, sat apart from their people entirely, hunched at their own small fires, keeping their own counsel.

"Both realms," Lior murmured. She had her ledger out — of course she did; Sef had stopped being surprised by how often she reached for it, the way another person might reach for a hand — and she was scanning pages by the distant glow. "Look at the clothing. That group there, by the eastern rim — those are Vellum's Reach colors. Guild grey. And across from them—" She stopped. Sef followed her gaze to a cluster of tall figures whose outlines seemed drawn in charcoal that hadn't finished drying, whose faces were suggestions rather than features. "Those are from the other side. From the dark realm proper."

"They've been coming for weeks," the Ferryman said. "Since the stars went out. When the stars went out, everyone understood at once, in their bones, what it meant. You can lie to people about wards falling. You cannot lie to them about the sky going dark." She gestured with a hand that was more gesture than hand. "They come because the fire is the last warm thing. And they trade."

"Trade what?" Sef asked, though some part of him already knew, some part that had stood in the Tollhouse of Borrowed Years and watched Lior pay a memory of her mother for two passages east.

"Warmth," the Ferryman said simply. "An hour of it. A night of it. A place close to the flame versus a place far from it. Everything is bargained here. The fire does not give freely — it cannot afford to. Nothing this far gone can afford generosity."

They descended the crater's inner slope single file, boots sinking into ash fine as flour, and the smell of the place resolved itself slowly into components Sef would carry for the rest of his life: cold stone, old smoke, and underneath it all something faintly sweet and terrible, like the air after lightning, like the last breath of something enormous.

* * *

The first refugee who spoke to them was a man from the bright realm — Sef could tell by his accent, the flat vowels of the river towns south of Vellum's Reach — sitting at the outermost ring of fires, where the warmth was thin enough that his breath still fogged. He was mending a boot with strips of bark and thread pulled from his own cuff.

"You'll want to register," he said without looking up. "With the Hearthwardens. They keep the lists. Closest rings go to the oldest debts — longest service, biggest trades paid in. Newcomers start at the ninth ring." He bit off the thread. "You look strong. Someone will want your hours."

"Your hours," Sef repeated.

The man finally looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and very calm, the calm of someone who had done all his panicking earlier and found it hadn't helped. "Time near the fire is time. You sit in the third ring for one hour, you come away one hour warmer than the ninth ring gives you. So the ninth-ring folk sell their hours to whoever can buy. An hour of my warmth for a strip of dried meat. Two hours for a blanket. I sold six hours yesterday for a knife." He held up the boot, inspected his work, frowned at it. "I'm saving up for a night in the second ring. My daughter sleeps better when I'm not shivering next to her. Shivering's catching, you know. Cold is catching too. Everything here is catching; that's the whole economy."

"And the fire itself?" Lior asked. "Can anyone approach it?"

The man laughed, short and without humor. "The fire isn't for sale, cartographer. The fire is the bank. Everything's measured against it and nothing touches it. There's a dragon in there." He said it the way you'd say *there's weather coming*. "Old Ashvane. Coiled around the coals. She doesn't move much anymore. Sometimes at night she opens one eye and looks at us all, and everyone stops trading for a minute. Just stops. Like a courtroom when the judge walks in." He went back to his boot. "Register with the Hearthwardens. Ninth ring. Don't sell more hours than you can spare — the cold takes the ones who overspend. We bury them shallow, out past the eastern rim. The ash keeps."

* * *

The Hearthwardens turned out to be neither wardens nor guildsmen but something older and stranger: three figures in layered robes stitched from what looked like every kind of cloth ever woven, wool and silk and leather and one panel that Sef was fairly sure was shed skin, translucent and scaled. They kept their lists on tablets of slate, writing with burnt sticks, and when Lior gave her name the middle warden paused, stick hovering.

"Voss," the warden repeated. "Any relation to the covenant-keeper?"

"I'm her daughter."

The three wardens exchanged a look over her head, a whole conversation conducted in eyebrows and the slow blink of eyes accustomed to smoke. Then the middle one wrote the name down, and something in the way the burnt stick scratched against the slate made Sef's neck prickle — as if the list were not recording her arrival so much as expecting it.

"Ninth ring tonight," the warden said. "Rations at the western cistern, such as they are. And cartographer—" the burnt stick pointed, not unkindly, "—the dragon asks for names sometimes. When she wakes. If yours comes up, answer true. She can tell."

* * *

The ninth ring was a circle of stones maybe two hundred paces from the central flame, close enough to feel the fire as a presence against the face and too far to feel it anywhere else. Sef sat down on the ash-hard ground beside Lior and discovered that his body, which had walked for eleven days through a country that did not officially exist, had opinions about the difference between almost-warm and warm, and that almost-warm was its own particular cruelty — enough heat to make you aware of exactly how cold you were.

Around them, the ninth ring's population settled into the night's negotiations. A woman with a shadow that wept — actually wept, dark droplets sizzling where they struck the stones — traded three hours of her warmth to a gaunt man in exchange for a heel of black bread, and the transaction was conducted with the weary courtesy of people who had done this many times and expected to do it many more. Nearby, two children played a game with pebbles and a circle scratched in ash, and Sef realized with a lurch that the circle was a map of the fire rings and the pebbles were people, and that the children were playing at trading warmth, and that they knew all the rules.

"They're running an economy on the deathbed of the world," Lior said quietly. She wasn't writing in the ledger. She was just holding it, both hands, like ballast. "Look at them, Sef. This is what happens when everything else fails. People don't stop bargaining. They just get smaller about it. Empires trade provinces; refugees trade hours."

"That's not the worst thing I've seen people do under pressure."

"No." She was quiet a moment. "It might be the best thing. Look — that woman sold her hours and kept her bread to share. Half the ring is doing that. Trading what they must and giving what they can, and nobody's written a law about it. Nobody's enforcing anything." Her thumb moved along the ledger's spine. "The Reckoners would call this chaos. I'm starting to think it's the only honest market I've ever seen."

Sef watched the shadow-that-wept finish its transaction and return to its owner, folding itself around her shoulders like a damp cloak. "Do you ever stop reading everything?" he asked. It came out more gently than he'd intended, which surprised him, because eleven days ago he had still half-believed she was a fraud and a liability, and nine days ago he had learned she was neither, and somewhere since then the disbelief had quietly become something he didn't have a word for yet.

"Reading is how I pray," Lior said. "Don't tell the guildmasters. They think prayer is auditing."

Despite everything — the dying fire, the ninth ring, the ash in his boots that would apparently never come out — Sef laughed. It startled a nearby trader into nearly dropping her scales.

* * *

He woke once in the deep of the night, and the waking was not his own; the whole crater woke with him, a ripple of stillness passing outward from the center like the silence after a bell.

The dragon had opened her eye.

From two hundred paces Sef could see almost nothing of her — a curve of scale like a hillside at night, a suggestion of wing folded tight, and one eye, amber and ancient and banked like a coal, open and moving slowly across the rings of refugees. Wherever the gaze passed, the trading stopped. The woman with the weeping shadow froze mid-bargain. The children set down their pebbles. Even the fires seemed to burn more attentively.

The eye found Lior. Sef was sure of it — sure the way you are sure of being looked at through a window at night. The gaze rested on her for three heartbeats, four, five, and Lior sat perfectly still under it, awake now too, her chin lifted, meeting it without flinching, and Sef thought: *she can tell.* Whatever test the dragon asked silently of sleepers and wakers alike, Lior was answering it true.

Then the eye closed, and the crater exhaled, and the pebbles started moving again, and the weeping shadow resumed its quiet grief, and the great fire at the center of everything guttered once, twice, and steadied.

But Sef did not go back to sleep. He lay watching the central flame and counted its stumbles. Eleven stumbles before dawn. He counted again the next night, when they had bartered their way inward to the eighth ring — Lior trading annotated maps of safe road-paths to a Hearthwarden for warmth, the warden's hands shaking slightly as she took them, as if maps were relics — and the count was fourteen, and the Ferryman, materializing out of the dark the way she did, said only: "The wind has turned. Fewer days than I told you."

On the third night, Sef climbed to the crater's rim alone, partly to stand watch, partly because he needed to see it whole one more time: the little fire, the vast still shape around it, the rings of desperate gentle commerce, the absolute black above where stars should have been. He thought about the guildhall archive burning behind him, about his rank forfeit, about the oath he'd sworn to a woman who hadn't asked him to swear it. He thought about his mother, dead eight years, who had taught him that a warden's job was not to fight the dark but to know it well enough to greet it by name.

And that was when he saw the lights coming down the far slope.

Not cookfire-orange. Not ember-red. A cold white, orderly, spaced at even intervals — lanterns in ranks, descending the northern slope of the crater in a column too disciplined to be refugees. He counted the intervals between them and felt his stomach drop through the ash beneath his feet, because he had seen that spacing before, in another life, on parade grounds, and because the Ferryman had described them to him once with something that in a creature of shadow amounted to fear.

Reckoners. A full patrol of them, magistrates of the dark realm, come down into the last warm place in creation — and Sef did not need to be a reader of impossible ledgers to know they had not come to trade hours.

He was already running for the eighth ring, ash flying, shouting Lior's name into the dark.
