## 13. What the Stars Were Bought With

The crater of ash held its breath around the Last Hearth, and in that held breath Lior sat across from a dragon who had once been a woman.

Ashvane's fire burned low at the crater's center — not a bonfire but a presence, a slow golden respiration that rose and fell like something sleeping and dreaming of waking. Its light reached only so far: a ring of warmth perhaps fifty paces wide, beyond which the unmapped dark pressed close as water against glass. The refugees huddled inside that ring like coins left on a windowsill, trading warmth in whispers, in shared blankets, in the small desperate economies of people who had learned that everything could be taken.

Lior had not slept. She doubted she would again until this was finished or she was unmade.

"You said my mother went to negotiate with the stars," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended, scraped raw by the road, by the tollhouse, by the shadow with her mother's hands pointing east into nothing. "I've been turning that over all night. You don't negotiate with stars. Stars are — they're *light*. They're fixed points. They're what you navigate *by*."

Ashvane's head was enormous — a hill of charcoal scales and old scars, crested with horns worn smooth as river stones. When she turned to look at Lior, the movement took a long time, the way a season takes time, and yet there was nothing ponderous in it. There was patience. The terrible, bottomless patience of someone who has watched centuries go by and learned not to flinch.

"When you were a girl," Ashvane said, and her voice was a hearth-voice, low and banked, with embers moving underneath, "did anyone ever tell you where starlight comes from?"

"The sun," Lior said. "Reflections. Distances."

"Your guild taught you distances." Something moved at the corner of the dragon's mouth that might have been a smile on a smaller face. "The guild is very good at distances. It was never good at debts."

* * *

They walked to the crater's rim while the fire breathed behind them. Ashvane moved slowly now; Lior could see the cost of every step, the way the dragon's scales had gone grey at the joints, the way her wings folded against her back like letters no one would ever send. At the rim, the ash fell away into darkness so complete that Lior's eyes ached trying to find a floor in it.

"Look up," Ashvane said.

Lior looked up. The sky over the dark realm was not black — black would have been a mercy, an absence, a simple nothing. It was *occupied*. Shapes moved up there, vast and slow, and here and there among them glimmered points of light that were almost stars but not quite — dimmed, veiled, like lamps seen through fogged glass, like eyes half-closed.

"They didn't go out," Ashvane said. "That is the first thing your mother understood, and the last thing she managed to tell anyone. A star cannot go out. A star can only be *called home*."

"Called home to where?"

"To themselves. To their origin. To the place from which they were first lent out." The dragon settled onto the ash with a sound like a mountain deciding to sit. "You think of light as given, cartographer. Free. Falling on you whether you deserve it or not. But in the oldest ledgers — older than your guild, older than your realm — light was always a loan. The stars are creditors. They lent their fire to both realms at the beginning, and the covenant was the repayment schedule."

Lior felt the ground tilt under her, though the ground did not move. "The covenant. My ledgers."

"Your ledgers. Your mother's ledgers. Her mother's before her, if the line runs true, though I suspect it does not run quite the way the guildmasters told you." Ashvane's eye found her — one eye, vast as a doorway, amber shot through with veins of dying gold. "Every trade your guild ever recorded — every borrowed shadow, every sold year, every mapped door — those were payments. Interest, if you like, rendered in the only currency the stars accept, which is *selfhood*. Pieces of selves, freely given, rising like smoke to keep the great accounts balanced. And in exchange, the stars kept burning. Kept lending their light. Kept the border between realms soft enough to pass through and firm enough to survive."

"And when my mother disappeared—"

"The payments stopped. Not because she stopped paying. Because she *changed the terms*." Ashvane exhaled, and the breath stirred the ash at their feet into little ghost-whorls. "She invoked a clause. One of the original clauses, written into the covenant by its first authors and forgotten by everyone since. A clause of suspension. She called the stars' collateral due — not to keep it, but to hold it. To take the light off the table while the two realms decided, finally, whether they meant to keep their bargain or let it die."

Lior's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. She thought of the equinox audit, the stars snuffing out in sequence over Vellum's Reach, pinched one by one like candles by a careful hand. Careful. Deliberate. *Ordered.*

"She did that," Lior said. "She put out the stars."

"She *withdrew* them. There is a difference, and the difference is everything." Ashvane's voice deepened, and for a moment the hearth-fire behind them flared in sympathy, throwing their shadows long and doubled across the ash. "If the stars had simply gone out, that would be death, and death is honest. This is worse than death. This is a *question*, asked at the scale of sky. Your mother bought time — years of it, maybe a decade — by mortgaging the light itself. And the price of that clause, the part she may not have fully understood—"

"Was that when the time ran out, there'd be no more payments coming. No more trades. Nothing to renew the loan with." Lior closed her eyes. She saw the ledger pages rewriting themselves overnight, entries dissolving and reforming like ice on a pond. She saw the wards falling at moonrise. She saw Sef dragging her out of the burning archive with the covenant pages clutched against his chest like a rescued child. "So the stars started coming home. And everything they'd been holding up started to fall."

"Now you begin to see the shape of it." Ashvane lowered her great head until her eye was level with Lior's, close enough that Lior could see herself reflected in it — a small, smudged figure, ash-grey, absurdly upright. "And do you know the cruelest part? Do you know why your mother's gambit was brilliant and doomed in the same stroke?"

"Tell me."

"Because the clause requires an answer. That was its design. Suspension of the covenant forces a reckoning — the realms must either reforge the covenant willingly, both of them, together, or let it lapse forever. No enforcement. No Reckoners. No debtors-in-succession dragged into contracts they never signed." The dragon's voice roughened, gravel and grief. "Your mother knew exactly what she was doing, child. She built the trap you raged about in the Court of Unpaid Debts into the sky itself. She made it impossible for anyone to sign on another's behalf ever again. Including her daughter."

* * *

Lior walked away from the rim without deciding to. Her feet carried her along the crater's edge, past the huddled refugees, past a woman from the bright realm teaching a shadow-child to cup its hands around a coal, past a warden's discarded helmet half-buried in ash. The hearth-light followed her like a dog that wasn't sure it was wanted.

*Reforged willingly,* she thought. *Both realms. Together.*

It sounded so simple when Ashvane said it. It sounded like the kind of thing written in the front of storybooks, in gold ink, above a picture of two hands clasped. But Lior had spent her life reading the fine print beneath the gold ink. She knew what willingness cost. She had watched a tollhouse built from stacked lifetimes and paid passage with a memory of her mother's face. She had stood in a court where justice was arithmetic and watched magistrates who could not be argued with, only survived.

And now she was supposed to gather two entire realms — the guildmasters in their ruined hall, the wardens with their broken pride, the Reckoners with their merciless precision, the traded things with their borrowed shapes and grieving hands — and ask them all to *choose* each other. Freely. Without compulsion. Without law.

Her knees gave out and she sat down hard in the ash, and for a long moment she simply breathed, and the breathing hurt.

"It's too big." She said it aloud, to no one, to the fire, to the veiled stars. "She left me a task the size of the sky and called it an inheritance. She might as well have left me the ocean and a teaspoon."

Behind her, wingbeats — slow, laborious, deliberate. Ashvane lowered herself to the ash nearby, folding her ruined wings, arranging her vast body with the care of someone conserving what little remained.

"May I tell you what I have learned about size?" the dragon said. "I have been large. I was a woman once, and then I chose to be enormous, and I will tell you honestly that largeness taught me nothing except how much of the world fits inside a single wound. Size is a liar, cartographer. It makes you believe a task must be done all at once, at full scale, or not at all. But nothing true was ever done that way. Covenants are not signed by realms. Realms do not have hands." Her eye glinted. "Covenants are signed by people. One at a time. Each signature small enough to fit in a palm."

Lior looked down at her own hands. Ink-stained. Calloused from the pen and the road. Hands that had learned a dead woman's cipher by lamplight, alone, telling herself it was just work.

"She knew I'd refuse," Lior said quietly. "The succession. The debtorship. She wrote the clause knowing I'd stand in that court and call it theft — knowing I'd refuse exactly the way I refused. That was the point, wasn't it? The Reckoners' sentence. *Finish it your own way.* She designed even my rebellion into it."

"I suspect," Ashvane said, "that your mother understood something most authors never learn: that you cannot write a person. You can only write the conditions in which a person might choose to become an author. Everything else — every other path — she closed. Not to trap you. To *free* you. There is a difference, and I know it is a bitter one to taste."

"It tastes like ash."

"Yes." The dragon laughed — a low, cracked sound, like a door opening in an empty house. "Everything here does, lately."

* * *

They sat together as the hearth-fire breathed. Somewhere in the refugee camp, someone had begun to sing — a thin, wavering melody, half a lullaby from the bright realm and half a work-song from the dark, stitched together in the middle where neither language had the words. Other voices joined it, badly, off-key, and the song became a strange new thing that belonged to neither realm and both.

Lior listened until the knot in her chest loosened by a single degree.

"Ashvane. If we reforge it — the covenant, the new one — the stars come back?"

"In waves. As signatures accumulate, the collateral returns. Light is patient about returning home to its work." The dragon paused. "But understand what you would be asking of them. The stars were burned by the old covenant too, in their way. Lending light for centuries and receiving pieces of selves as interest — that is not a life any more than it was for the shadows in the tollhouse. If you reforge the covenant, you must offer them something better than repayment. You must offer them *partnership*. And they will drive a hard bargain. They have been creditors a very long time, and creditors forget how to be anything else."

"And if we fail? If the realms won't choose each other?"

Ashvane was quiet for a moment. The fire at the crater's heart guttered, and in the sudden dimness Lior saw how truly vast the dark was, and how small the ring of light, and how many faces crowded inside it.

"Then the light goes home for good," the dragon said at last. "And both realms learn to live in the dark, or don't. That is what willingness means, child. It means the outcome is genuinely unwritten. Your mother understood that too. She bet the sky on us — on all of us — because she believed that free things choose better than bound ones." A pause, and then, softer: "I confess I am no longer certain she was right. But I am certain it was worth betting on."

Lior stood. Her legs shook, and she let them. She looked up at the veiled stars — the creditors, the collateral, the light waiting to be invited home — and felt despair rise in her like floodwater, and then, underneath the despair, something harder and hotter, something that had been growing in her since the night the wards fell, fed by every refusal and every cost and every hand offered along the road.

Defiance. Not the hot defiance of the court, arguing law with magistrates. A colder kind. A cartographer's defiance.

"You said covenants are signed by people, one at a time," she said. "Fine. Then I'll start with the hardest signature first." She turned to face the dragon fully, and the hearth-light caught the ledger strapped to her back, and she felt its weight change — no longer a chain, no longer an inheritance, but a draft. An unfinished document awaiting its final clause. "The Reckoners. They enforce the old covenant. They exist because of it. If anyone in either realm has a reason to refuse a new one, it's them — and if I can bring *them* to the table willingly, the rest will follow."

Ashvane's eye widened slightly, amber catching firelight. "Child, the Reckoners do not come to tables. They come to collect."

"Then I'll need to give them something to collect." Lior pulled the ledger around and opened it against her forearm, thumb finding the final page — her mother's cipher, the lesson disguised as a lock, still half-decoded, still whispering. "Because a patrol is already coming. Sef saw their signal-fires an hour ago. They're coming to collect on my sentence early."

The fire guttered again. Out past the ring of light, at the crater's far edge, torches had begun to descend the slope — precise, evenly spaced, moving with the merciless rhythm of arithmetic walking.

"And this time," Lior said, closing the ledger, "they're going to find out what I'm willing to pay."
