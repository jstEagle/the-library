## 14. Sef's Testimony

Sef had been a warden for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other: trouble announces itself. It came with horns, with runners, with the particular quality of silence that fell over a camp when the dogs went quiet at once. Trouble was polite, in its way. It gave you time to be afraid.

The Reckoners did not announce themselves. They simply arrived, the way frost arrives on a window — already there when you looked, as if they had always been there and you had only just noticed.

He saw them first from the ridge above the Last Hearth, where he'd gone to count the refugee fires out of an old warden's habit. Below him the crater glowed with its guttering ring of dragon-fire, and around it huddled perhaps three hundred souls from both realms, trading warmth in small desperate bargains: a coat for an hour nearest the flame, a song for a place beside someone's sleeping child. The light of the hearth turned everything the color of old honey.

And walking up the ash-slope from the east, unhurried, came nine figures in robes the color of a closed ledger.

Sef's stomach dropped through the mountain.

*They've come early.* Lior's sentence had given her until the covenant was finished or failed — that was the shape of it, the paradox the Reckoners had pronounced in their courtroom of unpaid debts. But Reckoners collected like rivers ran downhill, and Sef had spent enough years among magistrates of the bright realm to know that *early* was simply another word for *before you're ready.*

He took the slope at a run, boots skidding on ash, and by the time he reached the crater's rim his lungs were full of cinders and his mind was already drafting what he would say. That was the warden's curse and gift both: even terrified, some part of him was always composing testimony.

Lior was below, at the fire's edge, bent over the final ledger page with Ashvane's great head curved above her like a question mark of smoke and scale. She didn't see them yet. Nobody did. The refugees were too busy surviving to watch the horizon.

Sef made a choice then, standing on the rim with his heart hammering, and it was not the choice the old Sef would have made. The old Sef — the warden who had dragged a heretic cartographer out of a burning archive because orders said so, the warden who had been stripped of his rank for trusting her and had spent the whole road south nursing that grievance like a coal in his fist — the old Sef would have gone to Lior quietly. Warned her. Let her run.

Instead he walked down into the crater, straight across the open ash, and planted himself between the descending patrol and the hearth.

* * *

The lead Reckoner stopped ten paces from him. Up close they were worse than at a distance: faces like unfinished sentences, features present but somehow uncommitted, as though they had been described rather than born. Its voice, when it spoke, was the sound of a quill scratching.

"Warden Sef of Vellum's Reach. Rank: revoked. Standing: none."

"That's right," Sef said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "No rank. No standing. Which means you have no authority over me, and I have nothing left to lose, which makes me the most dangerous man on this mountain."

"We do not deal in danger. We deal in arrears." The Reckoner's gaze slid past him, toward the fire, toward Lior. "The debtor-in-succession has been granted latitude. Latitude is not immunity. The covenant she is remaking does not yet exist. Until ink dries, the old terms hold, and the old terms say we collect."

"Collect what?" Sef spread his arms. "Look around you. Look at what's here to collect. Shadows. Years people paid away. A fire that's got days left in it. You'll take the last warm thing in two realms and call it justice?"

"Yes," said the Reckoner, without hesitation, without cruelty. That was the horror of them, Sef thought. There was no cruelty in it at all. "Justice is not required to be kind. Only exact."

Behind him, he heard Lior's voice, sharp with alarm: "Sef, get away from them—"

He didn't move. Something had settled in him, cold and clear as well-water. Nineteen years of being the instrument of other people's law, and here at the end of everything, with no badge and no guild and no rank to hide behind, he finally understood what he actually believed.

"No," he said.

The Reckoners went very still. Even the ash seemed to stop falling.

"You are obstructing collection," the lead one said. "This carries penalty."

"I know. I'm about to pay it." He turned, deliberately, so that he faced the whole crater — the refugees, the traded things huddled in their borrowed shapes, Lior with the ledger clutched to her chest, the dying dragon whose breath stirred his hair like a hot wind off a forge. Then he turned back. "But I'm a witness. That's the one thing I still am. I swore it on the road, in front of the Court of Unpaid Debts, and your own law says a sworn witness must be heard before sentence executes. Am I wrong? Check your books. You love your books."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind the lead Reckoner, one of the others opened something that was not quite a hand, and pages rustled though Sef could see no book.

"The witness will be heard," the Reckoner said at last. "Briefly."

* * *

Sef had testified before. Depositions, tribunals, the endless gray machinery of guild discipline after the archive burned. He knew how to stand, how to speak, how to keep his eyes level. But every testimony he'd ever given had been built to protect himself, or to protect the guild, or to protect the law. This one was different. This one he built out of the only material he had: what he had seen.

"I'm going to tell you what I witnessed," he said, loud enough to carry to the crater's rim. "Not what I believe. What I saw. Because I know you don't trade in belief."

He pointed to the eastern slope, where the traded things had made their camp apart from the others — shadows wearing shapes, years wearing faces, the discarded inventory of two centuries of impossible trades.

"Three nights ago, I watched a shadow grieve." His voice caught, and he let it. "You heard me. Grieve. It sat at the edge of the fire-light where it couldn't be warmed — because what does a shadow know of warmth? — and it held its hands the way a woman holds her hands when she's lost a child. Folded. Pressed. Rocking. And beside it sat another, older, and it was humming. A lullaby. In a language neither realm speaks anymore. These are the things your covenant calls *payments*. These are what we bought our borders with. Not coin. Not labor. People. Pieces of people. And they remember what they were, and they mourn it, and your ledger calls that *settled accounts*."

The Reckoners did not move. One of them, near the rear, tilted its unfinished face very slightly, the way a person tilts their head when hearing a familiar song from an unexpected room.

"I watched a man pay a year of his life for passage at the tollhouse," Sef went on. "A year he'd earned. He set it down on the counter like a coin and it *was* a coin, a little disc of light, and I watched the tollkeeper stack it with ten thousand others. Ten thousand years, stacked like cordwood. And do you know what the tollkeeper said? Said it like a prayer, like an apology: *we keep them safe until someone comes back to claim them.* No one ever comes back. But they keep them anyway. Because somewhere under all that precision, even the tollhouse knows it's a warehouse of stolen lives."

"Sef," Lior whispered behind him. He couldn't tell if she was warning him or holding him up. Maybe both.

"And the fire." He turned fully toward the hearth now, toward Ashvane, whose vast eye had opened and fixed on him with an attention that felt like sunlight through a magnifying lens. "I've been a warden nineteen years. I've guarded granaries in famine years. I know what scarcity makes of people. Scarcity makes them animals. It should have, here, with days of fire left and hundreds of mouths. But I have watched these people — from both realms, people who were taught to fear each other's shadows — share that fire freely. Freely! A woman from the bright realm gave her cloak to a thing made of traded darkness because it was shivering, and when I asked her why, she said, *it looked cold*, as if that were answer enough. As if it were the only answer there ever was." His voice cracked clean through and he let it break, because breaking was true and true was all he had. "That fire is going out. Everyone here knows it. And they give it away anyway. So you tell me — you with your exact justice — which covenant is holding this mountain together tonight? Yours? Or theirs?"

* * *

The silence afterward was total. Even the wind held its breath. Sef stood in it, emptied, wrung out, feeling the strange lightness of a man who has handed over the last of his gold and found he can still stand upright.

The lead Reckoner regarded him for a long moment. When it spoke, its quill-scratch voice had changed — not softened, exactly. Reckoners did not soften. But something in it had shifted register, the way a judge's voice changes when ruling against precedent.

"Your testimony is irregular."

"It's true."

"Truth is frequently irregular." The Reckoner's gaze moved past him, taking in the crater, the refugees, the shadow-camps, the fire. "We have reviewed the record of this journey. The tollhouse ledgers confirm your account of the years. The grief-behavior of the traded is... documented. Anomalous. Payments are not designed to grieve." A pause, long as a turning page. "There is a clause. Old. Rarely invoked. Article Nine of the original compact: *where enforcement produces evidence contrary to the covenant's purpose, collection shall be suspended pending review.*"

Sef's breath left him slowly. "Then you'll wait. You'll let her finish—"

"We will suspend collection," the Reckoner said. "Temporarily. Under protest, which we hereby enter into the record." Its unfinished eyes found Sef's. "Understand what this costs, witness. Your testimony has purchased a truce. Truces are debts too. Someone will owe for this night. And you—" the voice dropped, became almost gentle, which was infinitely worse, "—you have spoken against the covenant you once served. Whatever remains of your standing in either realm is forfeit. Not revoked. Forfeited. There is no rank you can be restored to, in the bright country or the dark. You are a man outside all ledgers now."

Sef laughed. It startled him, coming out of him, rough and real. "Outside all ledgers." He shook his head. "Lady, I've been trying to get thrown out of ledgers my whole life. You've just done me the biggest favor anyone's ever done."

The Reckoner studied him a moment longer, and then — Sef would swear this to his grave — something almost like acknowledgment passed across its unfinished face. It turned. The patrol turned with it, nine closed-ledger silhouettes against the ember-glow, and they withdrew up the ash-slope and were gone between one step and the next, the way frost leaves when the sun finds the window.

* * *

Afterward, Lior found him sitting on a warm stone at the fire's edge, hands shaking now that shaking was permitted.

"You absolute fool," she said, but she sat down beside him, close, shoulder to shoulder, and her voice was thick. "They could have unmade you. On the spot. No trial, no—"

"They'd have had to listen first. Witness's privilege." He managed a grin. "Turns out nineteen years of guild bureaucracy taught me something useful after all. Know the loopholes."

She laughed — wet, cracked, real — and then pressed her forehead briefly against his shoulder, the way soldiers do, the way family does. "Truce," she said. "We have a truce. How long?"

"'Pending review.'" He looked up at the black sky, at the single star that had flickered back to life two nights past and burned there alone, small and stubborn, like a candle carried down a long stair. "Which means however long it takes them to convince themselves we're wrong. Days. Maybe less. They'll come back with more than nine next time, Lior. They'll come back with the whole court."

"Then we finish it." She lifted the ledger, and in the starlight and the firelight he saw that her mother's cipher had begun to unravel under her pencil — lines of meaning emerging from the page like a coastline rising from fog. Her eyes were wide, and wet, and burning. "Sef. This page. It's not a lock. It never was. It's a *lesson*. She wrote it to teach whoever came next how to rewrite the covenant. She was teaching *me*." Her voice broke on the word, and firmed again. "I know what I have to draft now. I know the terms. But I need the night, and I need the fire, and I need—" she glanced at him sideways, suddenly young, suddenly uncertain, "—I need a witness who's good at testifying."

Sef looked out over the crater — at the shared fire, the grieving shadows, the small stubborn star — and flexed his ruined, rankless, free hands.

"Lucky for you," he said, "I'm available."

Above them, the lone star pulsed — once, twice — and far to the east, faint as a struck match, a second light kindled in the dark.
