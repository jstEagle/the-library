## 17. The Reckoners' Objection

Sef had learned, in his years as a warden of Vellum's Reach, that the worst betrayals never announced themselves. They arrived wearing the face of procedure. They came with seals and signatures and the soft rustle of documents that had been in motion long before anyone thought to ask where they were going.

So when First Magistrate Orenvale rose from her seat at the negotiation table and said, in a voice like a ledger closing, "The Reckoners find these terms unacceptable," he was not surprised. He was only, terribly, confirmed.

The hearth-light had burned through most of the night. Around the great crater's rim, the negotiators sat in a rough crescent — guildmasters from the bright realm on one side, delegates of the traded things on the other, Lior at the center with the new covenant spread before her on a board of ash-wood, its pages still warm from her hands. The fire itself guttered below them all, a coin of gold light in a bowl of grey ash, and every few minutes it seemed to shrink, and everyone pretended not to notice.

Orenvale stood. She had not spoken much during the drafting — had listened, actually, which should have been the first warning. Reckoners did not listen. Reckoners tallied.

"Unacceptable," she said again, letting the word sit in the cold air like a stone dropped into a well. "Clause by clause." She lifted one long finger. "Debts payable in memory and labor. Memory is not fungible. One woman's grief cannot be weighed against another's, and so no debt can ever be settled, and so no debt can ever be *closed*." A second finger. "Borders open to the mapped and unmapped alike. You propose to abolish the border entirely. A covenant without a boundary is not a covenant; it is a surrender." A third finger, and now her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than anything sharp she might have said. "And no enforcement. No penalty. No surety. Magistrate Voss—" she inclined her head toward Lior with terrible courtesy "—you have drafted a promise. We do not deal in promises. We deal in *payment*."

The crater went quiet. Even the wind coming down off the ash-slopes seemed to hold itself.

Lior did not flinch. Sef watched her from his place near the eastern edge of the crescent, where he'd stationed himself out of habit — wardens watched edges; that was the whole of the trade — and he saw her fold her hands over the covenant pages as if she could warm them by will alone.

"A contract enforced by fear," she said, "is just a threat with good handwriting."

A few of the traded things made sounds at that — the shadow-delegates shifting, the nameless ones who had once been years or seasons murmuring in voices like paper turning. But Orenvale only smiled, and Sef felt his stomach drop, because he had seen that smile before. He had seen it on the faces of magistrates about to read a sentence they had decided days ago.

"Perhaps," Orenvale said. "But consider what happens to your beautiful handwriting, Magistrate, when there is nothing left to write upon."

She turned her head. Not toward Lior. Toward the fire.

*

Sef understood then — all at once, the way a man understands a collapsing roof not by reasoning but by the sound the beams make — and everything that had happened since the truce reassembled itself in his mind into its true shape.

The Reckoner patrol had come three nights past, and he had stood witness, and his testimony had bought a truce. He had paid for that truce with his rank, his name struck from the warden rolls, his thirty years of service reduced to a line in someone else's ledger: *disgraced for trusting a cartographer.* He had thought the price was the point. He had thought the truce was the prize.

It had been a feint. It had all been a feint.

Because look at what the truce had purchased them: three days of open negotiation. Three days in which every delegate of both realms had gathered in one place — in one *crater* — around the last fire in either world. Three days in which the Reckoners had done nothing but listen, and count, and wait.

They didn't need to reject the covenant. They needed everyone who might sign it standing in the same bowl of ash when they moved.

"The hearth-fire," Orenvale was saying, and her voice carried now with the flat clarity of a verdict already written, "is the last unencumbered asset of the old covenant. As surety against default, the Court of Unpaid Debts hereby seizes—"

"No." Lior was on her feet.

"—the hearth-fire," Orenvale finished, "effective immediately."

What happened next happened fast, and Sef's mind would keep it forever in fragments, the way disasters are kept.

The Reckoners moved first — of course they did; they were creatures of sequence, of *order*, and their order was this: six figures in grey robes peeling away from the crescent and descending the inner slope of the crater in perfect formation, unhurried, their feet leaving prints in the ash that closed behind them like water. Not running. Running would imply urgency, and urgency implied doubt, and Reckoners did not doubt. They simply descended, six lines drawn down the throat of the world, toward the fire that kept every refugee in the crater alive.

The refugees understood before anyone else. They always did; they had the least left to lose and therefore the sharpest eyes. Sef heard the cry go up from the huddle nearest the slope — an old man's voice, cracked, screaming something about the warmth, *they're taking the warmth* — and then the whole ragged settlement was in motion, hundreds of bodies surging between the descending greys and the guttering flame, arms linked, a human wall thrown up out of desperation and almost nothing else.

"Stop them!" one of the bright-realm guildmasters shouted — Master Corvane, Sef thought, the one with the ruined hands — though to whom, Sef could not imagine. There was no one to stop them. That was the whole horror of Reckoners. You could not fight a verdict. You could only be found in contempt of it.

And the traded things — the shadows, the borrowed years given shape, the forgotten names walking around in bodies of smoke and dusk — the traded things turned toward the Reckoners too, and Sef saw something in their postures he had seen only once before, at the tollhouse, in the stacked lifetimes: grief organizing itself into fury.

"They'll kill them," he said aloud, to no one. "They'll unmake them. Unpaid debts don't get trials — they get *collections*."

He was already moving.

*

Here is what Sef knew about himself, and had known since the night the wards fell and he dragged a cartographer out of a burning archive instead of saving the archive: he was not a brave man. He had spent thirty years being a careful one. Wardens survived by knowing which orders to follow slowly, which doors to check twice, which fires to let burn themselves out. His disgrace had not changed that. If anything, losing his rank had stripped away even the performance of courage, and left him with the plain truth underneath: he was a middle-aged man with bad knees and a warden's instincts and no institution left to hide inside.

And none of it mattered, because his legs were carrying him down the crater slope anyway, skidding through ash, toward the place where the grey robes met the human wall.

"Lior!" he shouted, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "LIOR!"

She was still at the table. Of course she was. She was gathering the covenant pages, pressing them flat against the ash-wood board, shouting something back at Orenvale — he couldn't hear the words over the rising roar of the crowd, but her posture told him everything: she was arguing. In the middle of a battlefield assembling itself around her, she was *arguing*, because words were the only weapon she had ever trusted, and God help him, some stubborn part of him believed she might be right.

But she couldn't see what he could see. She was facing the magistrate. Her back was to the slope.

And on the slope, above the descending Reckoners, Sef saw the thing that turned his blood to ice water: more of them. Dozens. Grey figures cresting the crater's rim in silent file, from the north and the south and the east, ranks unfolding out of the dark like a verdict being read aloud in a language with no word for mercy. The patrol that had granted the truce had never been the whole court. The whole court had been waiting beyond the light, patient as interest accruing, and the truce had simply been the time it took them to arrive.

The truce hadn't been broken. It had *expired*. It had been designed to expire, here, now, with every signatory gathered and the fire low and the choice reduced to its simplest terms: kneel, or burn.

"*Lior!*" He reached the bottom of the slope, shoved through the press of refugees — elbows, ribs, a child's crying face pressed against his hip — and broke through to the table just as the first of the six grey robes reached the human wall at the fire's edge.

What happened at that wall, Sef did not fully see, and would spend the rest of his life grateful for the mercy of the angle. There was a sound like a page tearing. There was a light — not the fire's warm gold but something colder, whiter, the color of a debt being settled — and one of the linked arms came apart into drifting ash, and the man who had owned it screamed, and the wall held anyway, because people are astonishing, because they will link arms around the last warm thing in the world even while their neighbors come apart beside them.

Sef grabbed Lior's shoulder and spun her around.

She fought him for half a heartbeat — pure reflex, a woman who had learned in the dark that hands from behind meant trouble — and then she saw his face, and stopped.

"The rim," he said. His voice came out flat and fast, the warden's voice, the report-of-a-fire voice, the voice you used when panic would kill faster than the flames. "North, south, east. Full court. The truce was the trap — they wanted us gathered. They're taking the fire as surety and they'll take the signatories as collateral, and they'll call it *procedure*."

Her eyes went where his pointed. He watched her see it: the grey ranks along the crater's rim, dozens deep, motionless, patient, the ash drifting up past them like snow falling in reverse. Watched her understand, in the space of two breaths, that every hour she had spent drafting, every clause she had argued for, every fragile thread of agreement she had woven through the night — all of it had been permitted. All of it had been *scheduled*.

"They let us finish," she whispered.

"They let us *gather*." Sef heard his own voice shake and did not care. "There's a difference, and it's the difference between a trial and an execution."

Below them, the white light flared again. Another section of the human wall dissolved. The refugees were falling back now, not breaking — falling back in order, which was somehow worse, an organized retreat by people who had nowhere to retreat *to* — and the six grey robes walked through the gap they left without hurrying, closing on the hearth-fire like a hand closing on a candleflame.

And the fire — the last fire, Ashvane's fire, the dragon-fire that had burned since before either realm had names — guttered. Hard. Down to a coal. Down to a memory of a coal.

Somewhere in the dark above the crater, Sef knew, the stars that remained were watching. And somewhere in the ash below, the dragon who had been a woman lay too weak to rise, spending what was left of herself on shields that were failing.

Lior looked at the fire. Then at the covenant in her arms. Then — last, longest — at the rim, at the full weight of the Court of Unpaid Debts assembled to collect on a world.

Sef had served under commanders who froze at moments like this, and he had served under one, exactly one, who didn't. He recognized the set of her jaw now, the same way he'd recognized it in a burning archive when she'd looked back at the shelves and he'd had to drag her. She wasn't freezing. She was *choosing*. He could nearly hear the ledger turning behind her eyes, page after page after page, until it found the entry she wanted.

"Sef," she said quietly. "How many can you hold off?"

He laughed — one short, disbelieving bark, the laugh of a disgraced warden with bad knees and no rank and a sword he'd sold two towns ago to buy passage. "Hold off? Lior, I'm a *warden*. I arrest drunk cartographers and fine smugglers. I've never held off anything in my life."

"I know." She was already moving toward the fire, covenant board under her arm, ash swirling around her boots. "I'm asking anyway."

And Sef Yarrow — careful man, cautious man, a man whose entire career had been built on knowing which fires to let burn — looked at the grey ranks descending, and at the human wall thinning, and at the coal-bright heart of the last hearth in creation, and felt something in himself settle into a stillness colder and clearer than anything he had ever known.

There was no rank left to lose. There was no order left to follow slowly. There was only the side he had chosen, in a burning archive, in a tollhouse, in a courtroom of merciless magistrates, chosen again each morning like a debt he paid gladly — and he found, standing at the edge of the last battlefield either world would ever need, that the choosing felt less like ruin and more like relief.

"Then I'll hold off all of them," he said, "for as long as it takes you to do whatever mad thing you're about to do."

Lior smiled at him — quick, fierce, terrified — and turned and ran down into the ash toward the dying fire, toward the closing grey robes, toward the six figures who had come to seize a world's last warmth as payment on a debt she had already proven was never hers.

Behind them, at the crater's rim, the full Court of Unpaid Debts began, at last, to descend.

* * *

The first Reckoner reached the hearth-ring before Sef did.

It did not walk so much as arrive — one moment at the edge of the firelight, the next inside it, its grey robe stirring in the heat that should have consumed it and did not. The refugees scattered from it like sparks. And it extended one long, grey hand toward the coal of the hearth-fire, fingers spreading, in the gesture Sef had seen clerks make over a debtor's final assets: the gesture of a man about to close a book.

Sef hit it at a dead run.

He had no weapon. He had, as it turned out, never needed one. He had thirty years of warden's authority in his spine and nothing left to spend it on but this, and he slammed his shoulder into the grey robe with everything his bad knees had left, and the Reckoner — weightless, appalled, a creature of procedure encountering a body that refused to be procedural — staggered sideways into the ash.

For one heartbeat, the thing simply looked at him. Its hood tilted. Beneath it there was no face, only the pale suggestion of one, the way a form suggests itself through vellum — and Sef understood that it was consulting something. Some internal ledger. Some entry that would tell it precisely what he was, what he was worth, what column he belonged in.

Let it look, he thought wildly. Let it search every page.

*Disgraced warden. No rank. No property. No debts outstanding.*

*One loyalty. Fully paid. Non-transferable.*

"You'll have to enter me by hand," Sef said, and planted himself between the Reckoner and the fire, arms wide, the way the refugees had linked theirs — and behind him he heard Lior drop to her knees at the hearth-stone, heard her tear the old covenant from her board, heard her begin, in a voice that cracked and steadied and cracked again, to read.

The Reckoner raised its hand.

All around the crater, the grey ranks came down out of the dark like winter finally arriving, and the last fire in either world shuddered down to a single coal, and Sef Yarrow, who had never held off anything in his life, held.
