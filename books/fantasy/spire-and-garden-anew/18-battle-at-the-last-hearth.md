## 18. Battle at the Last Hearth

Lior heard the Reckoners before she saw them — a sound like ledgers closing, hundreds of them, in perfect unison.

She was standing at the edge of the hearth-crater with the old covenant open across her forearms, ink still tacky on her draft of the new one, when the sound rolled down from the crater's rim. The refugees nearest her went still. Even the traded things — the shadows and borrowed years huddled at the fire's margin like beggars at a feast — lifted whatever passed for their faces.

"They're coming," Sef said. He had been pacing the rim since dusk, warden's instincts refusing to let him sit. Now he came down the ash-slope two steps at a time. "Patrol of twelve. Full magistracy. They crossed the truce-line without herald."

"The truce was yours," Lior said. "It cost you your rank. Apparently it cost them nothing."

"I know." His jaw worked. "I'm sorry. I thought testimony would—"

"It did what testimony does. It bought us a night." She closed the covenant carefully, as if the book could bruise. "They were never going to let us finish. A covenant rewritten willingly is a covenant they can't enforce. And enforcement is all they are."

Below them, in the shallow bowl of the crater, the Last Hearth burned on — a dragon-fire no taller than a cookfire now, blue-white at its heart, guttering in a way that made Lior's chest ache to look at. Around it sat the negotiators: three guildmasters who had followed her from Vellum's Reach, two Reckoner defectors who had come over during the drafting, a delegation of the traded things that communicated in gestures and grief, and Ashvane.

The dragon lay coiled at the fire's edge like a mountain pretending to be a hill. Her scales had gone the grey of old ash. When she breathed, the flame leaned toward her mouth as if listening for instruction.

Ashvane opened one eye. "I hear them too," she said. "The sound of books closing. I wrote the first page of that sound, long ago." Her voice was wind through a canyon. "How much longer do you need?"

"Until dawn," Lior said. "The final clause needs both realms' marks. The bright realm's representatives are arguing over the word *labor*."

"Arguing is good," Ashvane said. "Arguing means they've stopped counting."

* * *

Sef saw them first because he had spent twenty years learning to see things the guild preferred unmapped.

They came down into the crater from the north breach — twelve figures in robes the color of settled accounts, moving with the terrible economy of things that had never once wasted motion. Behind them, at the rim, stood the wardens he had once commanded, holding staves that hummed with the last charge of the fallen border-wards. And behind *them*, drawn up like an audience, the dark realm's own conscripts: shapes that had been people once, before the trades took them, now wearing the blankness of things owned.

His stomach turned. Not at the Reckoners. At his own kind, standing in formation to protect a fire they'd sworn to guard, about to be ordered to put it out.

He ran for Lior.

"They'll take the hearth first," he said, dropping beside her at the drafting-table — a slab of crater-stone where the new covenant lay under a cloth weighted with four stones. "Surety. That's how they think. Seize the asset, negotiate from strength. If they get the fire, everything we drafted tonight is paper."

"Ashvane," Lior called.

The dragon rose. It took a long time — joints like grinding continents — but when she was upright she filled half the crater, and the refugees pressed back against the walls not from fear but from awe. The guttering flame sank low as she moved, drawing itself into her lungs.

"I have perhaps one fire left," Ashvane said. "After this, I am only a woman again, and an old one. Choose the moment."

Sef looked at Lior. Lior looked at the covenant under its cloth.

"Not yet," she said. "Not until I've tried words."

"Words," Sef repeated. He had heard her try words at the Court of Unpaid Debts; he had watched her argue a paradox into law. But that had been a courtroom. This was a battlefield, and battlefields had never once listened.

"Lior." He said her name the way you say a thing you might not get to say twice. "There are forty armed beings coming down that slope. Words are what you fall back on when the fighting fails. Let the fighting fail *first*."

"No." She was already walking toward the north breach, covenant in her arms. "That's exactly backwards. Fighting is what you fall back on when words fail. We've just never had the courage to find out if they did."

He swore — softly, in the old warden's cadence, the curse his mother had taught him — and went after her. Because someone had to stand next to the madwoman, and because he had testified already that he believed in her, and because a witness who abandons the testimony is just another liar.

* * *

The Reckoners halted ten paces from the hearth. Their leader stepped forward — a tall figure whose face was a ledger-page, lines of fine script shifting where features should be, and Lior recognized with a lurch the handwriting. It was her mother's cipher. The Reckoners' faces were written in the same hand that had taught her to read.

"You are in arrears," the leader said. Its voice was the closing of the ledger, made speech. "Sentence of succession, entered at the Court of Unpaid Debts. You were commanded to finish the covenant. Instead you unmake it. This is default. Default forfeits surety." It gestured at the hearth. "The fire will be held against future settlement."

"The debt isn't mine," Lior said. "You ruled so yourselves. You ruled my argument sound."

"We ruled it *sound*. Sound arguments do not extinguish obligations. The obligation predates you. It will postdate you. Step aside."

"No."

The word came out smaller than she wanted. She planted her feet anyway.

"I'm going to read something," she said. "All of you. Wardens. Conscripts. Traded things. Everyone. I'm going to read the covenant you enforce, from the beginning, aloud, and I'm not going to stop until someone makes me."

The lead Reckoner tilted its written face. "To what purpose?"

"To find out," Lior said, "who you are."

And she opened the book, and began to read.

* * *

Sef had expected the fighting to start immediately. It did not, and the not-starting was worse.

Lior's voice carried strangely in the crater — the ash walls threw it back, so that the covenant seemed to speak from everywhere at once. She read the opening clauses: the naming of the two realms, the acknowledgment of debts between them, the founding of the Guild of Impossible Things as keeper of accounts. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. He watched the knuckles whiten around the pages and admired her for it anyway.

Then she reached the fourth clause, and slowed.

"'Let there be established,'" she read, "'an order of magistrates, empowered to assess, collect, and enforce all obligations herein described, such order to exist *for so long as the covenant stands, and no longer*.'"

She looked up.

"Do you hear it?" she asked the crater. "Do all of you hear it? The Reckoners exist because the covenant says so. Clause four. They are not the covenant's masters. They are its *children*. Every debt they ever collected, every life they ever seized, every sentence they ever passed — none of it was theirs. It was lent to them by a document. And documents can be rewritten."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

The lead Reckoner's scripted face rippled. For the first time, the fine writing seemed to be reading *itself*.

"That clause," it said slowly, "is not commonly cited."

"Because you burn the copies," Lior said. "Because you are the ones who keep the archives. Because a magistrate who knows he exists only at his master's pleasure becomes very motivated never to look for the master." She turned a page. "Shall I go on? There's more. There's the clause about the stars. Shall I read what the stars were collateral *for*?"

"Enough." The Reckoner's voice cracked like a spine breaking. "Wardens! Take the book."

And the crater broke.

* * *

What Sef remembered afterward, in the years when he allowed himself to remember it, was not the violence. It was the hesitation.

The wardens came down the slope at a run — his former comrades, men and women he had eaten with, trained with, buried friends with. And halfway down, they began to falter. Because Lior kept reading. Her voice rose over the clash of staves, over the shriek of the traded things scattering, over Ashvane's rising growl, and she read clause after clause, and each clause was a mirror.

A young warden named Pell — Sef had held her when she was born, her father had been his second — stopped dead ten feet from him, stave raised, and stared at nothing.

"Sef," she said. "Is it true? What she's reading? Are we—"

"Clause nine," Lior's voice rang out. "'The wardens of the border are constituted as guarantors of passage, that the realms may know each other, *not as jailers of either*.' Do you understand? Your order was founded to open doors. Someone changed the wording while you slept."

Pell lowered her stave. Just that. One woman, one choice. But Sef saw it catch, the way fire catches — a warden beside her hesitated, then another, and the charge down the slope lost its shape, became a confusion of individuals each deciding something alone.

The Reckoners did not hesitate. They were past deciding. Twelve figures converged on the hearth-fire itself, and Ashvane met them.

* * *

Lior would carry the sight of it forever: the old dragon spreading wings that had not opened in centuries, wings gone translucent as parchment, and pouring out her penultimate fire.

It was not a weapon. That was the terrible beauty of it. Ashvane breathed her fire *around* the negotiators, around the drafting-stone, around the huddled refugees — a ring of blue-white light, a wall that was also a shelter. The lead Reckoner walked into it and its robe began, very slowly, to smolder, and it did not stop walking, and Lior understood that these creatures could not stop, that stopping was the one thing the covenant had never taught them.

"ASHVANE!" she screamed. "Your last fire! Save it!"

"For what?" The dragon's voice was fraying, burning through. "For a dawn I won't see? Child, fire is only borrowed—" She coughed smoke shaped like words. "Read. Keep reading. The book is the only blade here that cuts everyone equally."

So Lior read.

She read while the ring of fire blazed and dimmed. She read while Sef fought — actually fought, stave against stave, holding the breach against the few wardens still obeying orders, buying her seconds with bruises. She read while the traded things crept out of hiding and stood in a silent crescent behind her, facing the Reckoners, facing the things that had catalogued them, and said nothing, and their saying nothing was the loudest testimony in the crater.

She read the clause about the tollhouses. The clause about the shadows. The clause — her voice breaking here, and she read through the break — about the woman who had first given her shape to the fire, whose name had been struck from every record except this one, and she read the name aloud:

"*Ashvane.*"

The dragon's head turned. In the heart of her dying fire-ring, the great eye found Lior, and something passed between them that had no clause and needed none.

"You remembered me," Ashvane said.

"You wrote yourself out," Lior said. "I'm writing you back."

* * *

Sef went down under two wardens at the north breach and got up again, which surprised all three of them. His stave was splintered. Blood ran into his eye. Through the red haze he saw the lead Reckoner break through Ashvane's fire-ring — through it, though its flesh smoked and its written face was curling at the edges like paper near a flame — and reach the drafting-stone, and tear the cloth away, and stand over the new covenant with its hand raised to destroy it.

"No," Sef said. He didn't remember crossing the distance. He hit the Reckoner from the side, a tackle with nothing behind it but a disgraced warden's stubborn weight, and they went down together onto the stone, and the Reckoner's hand closed on his throat, and its face — inches from his, script unraveling, whole clauses of itself burning away — looked at him with something that in any other creature he would have called fear.

"What are you," it rasped.

"A witness," Sef choked.

And then the fire-ring collapsed.

* * *

It went out all at once, the way a life goes out. Ashvane folded down over her own flames like a house settling, and the blue-white ring died, and the Last Hearth itself — the final fire, the original flame — stood alone and small in the ash, barely taller than a candle, and every creature in the crater turned to look at it, and in that moment of turning, everything hung.

The lead Reckoner rose off Sef, smoking, half its face-script burned away, and staggered toward the hearth.

Lior stepped between them.

She had no stave. No fire. Only the old covenant in one hand and her mother's cipher running through her mind like a second pulse. The Reckoner loomed over her, and its remaining written eye contracted.

"Move," it said.

"Clause one," Lior said. Her voice shook. She let it shake. Courage, she was learning, was not the absence of shaking; it was reading on through it. "Clause one, the oldest line, the line even you must obey: '*This covenant binds only what consents to be bound.*' Read your own foundation. Read it! The covenant was consent, once — two realms agreeing, freely, to owe each other something. Everything enforced after that was a corruption. You are enforcing a theft and calling it justice. And I—" her voice cracked wide open— "I am the author's daughter, and I am telling you: the account is closed. The debt is refused. The covenant dies tonight unless it is reborn as something chosen."

The Reckoner stood over her. Smoke curled from its shoulders. Behind it, the wardens had stopped entirely — Pell and the others forming a ragged, uncertain line, weapons down, watching. The traded things had crept closer, a tide of forgotten faces. Sef limped up to stand at Lior's shoulder, bleeding, swaying, present.

Above them all, the hearth-flame guttered down to almost nothing. An inch of blue. A breath of heat.

"Everything enforced was corruption," the Reckoner repeated. Its ruined face-script writhed, seeking, Lior realized, some clause to answer her with — and finding none, because there was none, because she had found the flaw at the root of its whole existence. "If the covenant dies... we die with it."

"Yes," Lior said gently. "I know."

"And what replaces us?"

Lior looked at the tiny flame. At the negotiators rising to their feet inside the dead fire-ring. At the shadow-things and the wardens and the smoking, trembling magistrate that had been built to be merciless and was discovering mercy was possible and therefore mandatory.

"Something signed," she said. "By everyone. Willingly."

The Reckoner's hand — the one that had been reaching for the hearth — slowly, joint by joint, turned palm-upward. Opened. The gesture of a debtor. The gesture, Lior thought, of a child.

"Then write quickly," it said. "The fire has minutes."

* * *

Sef heard the words and felt the world tilt. Minutes. All of it — the drafting, the arguing, the clauses fought over line by line through the night — and now minutes, with the flame down to a blue seed and the crater full of exhausted, smoking, weeping combatants who had suddenly run out of enemies.

He grabbed Lior's arm. "The final clause. It needs both realms' marks. There's no time for the bright realm's delegation to—"

"There doesn't need to be." Lior was already running for the drafting-stone, snatching up the quill, tearing back the cloth. "Consent doesn't need permission. It needs witnesses." She looked up, wild-eyed, ink-stained, alive in a way he had never seen her. "Sef. You swore yourself my witness. Both realms are in this crater right now — wardens of the bright, Reckoners of the dark, and everything traded between. If they mark it, it's signed."

Around the stone, they gathered — haltingly, unbelieving: Pell first, then the other wavering wardens; the lead Reckoner pressing a smoking handprint into the margin; the defectors; the eldest of the traded things, pressing close a shadow that left a smear of cold dark where warmth should be. Ashvane dragged herself forward, dying, immense, and breathed one thread of flame across the page to seal it — the last of her last fire.

Lior bent to write the final clause.

And stopped.

Because the quill hovered over the space where the covenant required a name — the author's name, the heir's name, the name entered at her mother's hand twenty years ago and passed down like a chain — and Lior Voss understood, in the guttering ember-light, that the last act of the old covenant was waiting for her signature, and that whatever she wrote in that space would decide what she was for the rest of her life.

The flame shrank to a spark.

She dipped the quill.
