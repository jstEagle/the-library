## 2. The Night Without Stars

Wren did not sleep.

She told herself it was the debt arithmetic, the way numbers could gnaw at a person like rats in a grain sack, but that was a lie she kept in a small drawer of her mind alongside other lies: *the entry means nothing; the Guildmaster doesn't know your name; you are an ordinary scribe and ordinary scribes die quietly.* She sat at her desk in the scribblers' dormitory with one candle burning down to its collar, copying nothing, writing nothing, listening to the Spire breathe around her.

The Spire did breathe. Everyone who worked in it long enough learned this. The great tower rose from the city's heart like a needle through cloth, forty stories of grey stone and older stone beneath that, and at night its bones settled and sighed, and somewhere far below — deeper than the vaults, deeper than the flooded cisterns the porters whispered about — something turned over in its sleep.

Or so the junior clerks joked. Wren had never thought much about what lay beneath. Above was what mattered. Above was where the ledgers lived, and the ledgers were the world.

She had copied acquisitions for six years. Bottled laughter from a wedding in Lowmarsh, three drams, provenance verified. A widow's grief, one full year, decanted into a jar of smoked glass that wept condensation no matter the season. The last word a drowned man ever said, recovered by divers off the Salt Quay and sold to a collector whose name was itself redacted. Wren recorded these things in her small, exact hand, weighed them, priced them, filed them, and went home to bread and candle-ends and the slow erosion of her debt. Impossible things were simply inventory. That was the first lesson of the Ledger Guild, drilled into every apprentice before they were allowed to touch ink: *a thing catalogued is a thing contained.*

The line in her own handwriting would not stay contained. It sat behind her eyes all night, patient as a spider: **One childhood, complete, unbroken. Seller's mark appended.**

*Unbroken.* No acquisition was unbroken. Grief came in years, laughter in drams, memory in shreds and tatters, because wholeness was expensive and nobody paid for expensive when fragments would do. A whole childhood was like a whole ocean in a bottle. It should not have been possible. It should not have been possible in her handwriting, either, because Wren Iversen had never written it, and yet the hand on that page was hers down to the little hook she made on the descender of *g*, a flaw three different guildmasters had tried and failed to correct.

The candle guttered. Outside the dormitory window, the city lay under a sky the color of unwashed wool, and the moon hung above the rooftops, waning, a day or two past full — no. Not full. Wren frowned at it, counting backward the way she counted everything. The almanac in the common room said the turn fell on the twelfth night hence. Thirteen turns, the old priests said, and the moon began again. Everyone knew that. Everyone also knew, in the way everyone knows things without knowing they know them, that the thirteenth turn was never spoken of in the Spire. The almanac printed twelve moons and a blank space, and generations of clerks had copied the blank space faithfully, because a thing catalogued is a thing contained, and some things the Guild preferred uncontained and unmentioned.

The moon looked wrong tonight. She could not say how. Paler, perhaps. Or closer.

Wren blew out the candle and lay down on her narrow cot and stared at the ceiling beams until, near what the bells called the Hour of Ash, she finally drifted into a sleep thin as onion skin.

---

She woke to silence.

Not quiet — the Spire was never quiet; there was always the groan of settling stone, the distant clank of the night-porters, the wind worrying the banners on the high terraces. Silence. The kind of silence that has eaten something.

Wren sat up. Around her the dormitory slept on, thirty cots, thirty breathers, Sister Maren in the corner snoring like a landslide. Nothing was wrong here. Everything was wrong outside, and it took her three full heartbeats to understand what.

No stars.

The window showed the usual slice of sky between the Spire's shoulder and the tenement roofs, and that slice of sky was empty. Not clouded — she knew clouded; clouded skies had texture, weight, a bruised silver along the edges where the city's lamps bled upward. This was flat black, absolute, a hole cut in the world. And the border wards — she could feel their absence the way you feel a missing tooth, a bright spot where pressure used to be. Every child in Vhal grew up under the wards' hum, a faint singing at the edge of hearing, the sound of the kingdom being held shut against whatever waited outside it. Wren had heard that hum every night of her life the way you hear your own heartbeat.

It was gone.

In its place, from very far away, from beyond the borders, from beyond everything, came a sound like a held breath being slowly released.

Then the screaming started.

It rolled up from the lower city first, a wave of it, street by street, district by district, thousands of voices discovering the same absence at once. Bells began to clash — not the ordered peals of the alarm code but every bell in Vhal rung at once by hands too frightened to count. Dogs howled. Somewhere a building's shutters banged open and a man's voice cracked across the rooftops: *"The stars! The stars are gone, look up, LOOK UP—"*

As if anyone could do anything else.

Wren was at the window with the cold stone biting her palms before she remembered standing. Below, the streets were filling — nightclothes and bare feet and lanterns lit with shaking hands, people pouring into the squares like blood from a wound. She looked up, all the way up, following the Spire's grey flank to its crown, and saw the watch-towers' beacons flaring one after another, and beyond them, where the sky should have been salted with ten thousand familiar fires —

Nothing. One breath, and the whole heaven snuffed like a candle between wet fingers.

Only the moon remained. It hung alone in that vast black nothing, and now she could see what had been nagging at her all evening: it was *full*. Round and white and impossibly detailed, every crater sharp as a coin struck fresh, hanging in a sky that should have shown it waning. As if the moon had been waiting for the stars to leave so it could show its true face.

And as Wren watched, the moon began, very slowly, to move. Not across the sky. *Down.* Descending by increments so slight only a scribe trained to notice drift in a ledger column would ever catch it — but catching it, unmistakably, the way you catch a ledger drifting toward ruin.

"Thirteen turns," someone said behind her, and Wren spun.

Sister Maren stood among the sleeping cots, awake, dressed, her old face grey as porridge. She wasn't looking at Wren. She was looking at the moon, and her lips were moving around words Wren couldn't hear, an old prayer or an old warning.

"Sister?"

Maren's eyes found her, and for a moment the old woman looked straight through her, at something behind her, or inside her. "Thirteen turns," she said again, softer. "It was always going to come back around. We just hoped—" She stopped herself, pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Dress warmly, child. Tonight is going to be long."

*We,* Wren thought, watching the old woman hurry out. *Hoped.* Since when did laundry sisters hope anything about moons?

But there was no time to chase it. The corridor outside had erupted; the dormitory was waking in stages, confusion spreading cot to cot like fire through dry grass. And over it all, cutting through the din with the terrible clarity of authority, the Spire's great horn sounded from the summit — three notes, then silence, then three again.

Assembly. All hands. Now.

* * *

The Scribblers' Hall had never held so many people, and it had certainly never held them in this state. Six hundred clerks and copyists packed shoulder to shoulder beneath the vaulted ceiling, and the air was a stew of fear-sweat and lamp oil and the ghost of a thousand years of ink. Nobody spoke above a whisper. In the Guild, whispers were louder than screams.

Wren wedged herself near the back, beside Tam Vey — the courier, the one with the quick eyes and the quick fingers, the one everyone liked and nobody trusted, which in the Guild amounted to a character reference. He had materialized at her elbow the moment the crowd compressed, which was either accident or design, and with Tam it was never accident.

"You felt it too," he murmured. Not a question. "The wards going out. Like a tooth."

"Like a tooth," Wren agreed, and hated how grateful she was that he'd named it.

Above them, on the iron balcony that overlooked the hall, the doors opened, and Guildmaster Corvane came out.

He was not a large man. He wore the plain grey of a senior clerk rather than the Guildmaster's ceremonial black, and his face was the face of a man who had spent forty years subtracting everything unnecessary from himself, including, apparently, mercy. But when Corvane walked into a room, the room reorganized itself around him, the way water reorganizes around a dropped stone.

Tonight he did not walk. He stood at the rail and gripped it, and Wren — who had spent six years learning to read the truth in documents, and had lately begun learning to read it in faces — saw that his knuckles were white.

"Clerks of the Ledger." His voice carried without effort, the way a blade carries. "At the Hour of Ash, the border wards failed. Not frayed. Not weakened. *Failed*, simultaneously, across nine hundred miles of frontier. At the same hour, every star visible from Vhal was extinguished." A pause. "You will not speculate. You will not gossip. You will not write a single word about tonight that is not entered in a sealed ledger under seal-number and witnessed signature. Am I understood?"

Six hundred voices murmured assent.

"The Guild has contained impossible things for four hundred years," Corvane said. "Laughter. Grief. Last words. Whole seasons of a life, when the price was right. We contain them because containment is the difference between a kingdom and a bonfire. Tonight the sky has reminded us what waits outside our ledgers." His gaze moved across the hall, slow, deliberate, a lighthouse beam. "Effective immediately, all archives are sealed. All acquisitions suspended. All active entries are to be transferred to the Deep Vault by morning bell. No exceptions."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, through whatever slept beneath them.

*All active entries.* Including an anomalous acquisition in a hand identical to hers. Including a page that named a seller. Including, if the Guildmaster's watchers had done their work, a thread leading directly to a debt-bound scribe named Wren Iversen.

Beside her, Tam Vey had gone very still. Stillness, on Tam, was louder than shouting.

"No exceptions," Corvane repeated, and now his voice dropped half a tone, and the hall leaned in to catch it. "I know what you're thinking. Some of you are thinking: *surely my little project, my pet acquisition, my careful marginalia — surely those can wait.* They cannot. There are things in our stacks older than the Guild's charter. There are things in our stacks that were never meant to be *in* stacks at all. If the wards are down, then the walls matter more than they have ever mattered, and I will not have one loose page, one loose clerk, one loose *anything* left where the night can reach it." He straightened. "Sealing begins at dawn bell. Return to your desks. Copy nothing. Touch nothing. Sleep if you can."

He turned to go, then stopped, and delivered the last of it almost as an afterthought, which was how Wren knew it was the point of the entire speech:

"And clerks — the Deep Vault does not merely store. You all know what the Erasure is for. Pray you never learn personally."

He was gone. The hall exhaled into chaos.

* * *

They were not permitted to return to their desks, actually — the corridors were already jammed with porters hauling sealed crates toward the vault stairs, and the flow of traffic ran one direction only, down. Wren got swept along in it, Tam's hand light on her elbow, steering her through the crush with a courier's instinct for gaps.

"Third alcove," he breathed against her ear. "Archive C. Your anomalous entry — it'll be in the transfer queue by now. Everything gets logged twice before it goes down. Once in the master index, once in the—"

"I know how my own guild works, Tam."

"Do you?" He smiled, quick and crooked, and for once there was no humor in it. "Because I've been running pages through these corridors for eight years, Iversen, and I'm telling you: tonight, for the first time, there are things moving through this Spire that aren't on any manifest. Porters going down to the Deep Vault and not coming back up. Doors locked that haven't been locked since the founding. And the Guildmaster—" he glanced over his shoulder, "—the Guildmaster looked scared. Corvane doesn't get scared. Whatever's under us, whatever those wards were holding *out* — he knows more than he said. Which means somebody in this building knows everything. And everything, in this place, is written down."

Wren's heart was doing its hammering again, the same rhythm as last night, the same rhythm as the moment she'd found the entry. "If the entry goes into the Deep Vault before I see it—"

"Then it goes where even Guildmasters need seven signatures to follow." Tam's grip tightened on her arm, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "So don't let it go down. The transfer queue runs through Archive C. Third alcove. The night-clerk on duty owes me a favor and sleeps like the dead. You'd have maybe an hour between the second logging and the crate-seal."

"An hour to do what? Read it? I've read it. I wrote it, apparently."

"An hour," Tam said, "to find out who sold a whole childhood to the Ledger Guild — and why the Guildmaster thinks that sale matters more than the stars going out."

Around them the Spire groaned and settled, its ancient bones shifting under the weight of panic, and far below — deeper than the vaults, deeper than the cisterns — Wren could have sworn she felt something turn over in its sleep.

Above, through the high windows of the stairwell, the moon climbed alone in the starless dark, round and white and patient, descending by degrees no one else seemed to see.

Nine days, the Guildmaster had said. Wren looked at the moon and corrected him silently, the way she corrected any misfiled figure.

Eight.
