## 3. Sealed Stacks

The Spire did not sleep so much as it held its breath.

Wren had lived beneath its shadow for eleven years and had learned the difference. Sleeping buildings snored — pipes knocking, shutters clapping in wind, the small honest noises of a structure settling into itself. The Spire did none of that. At night it simply went quiet the way a man goes quiet when he is listening for footsteps on the stair, and every candle guttering in a thousand windows seemed less like light than like held breath let out slowly through the fingers.

She moved through that held breath now, past the second bell, with a stolen ring of keys cold against her sternum where she'd sewn them into her coat lining.

The plan had been forming for three days, ever since she'd found the entry in her own handwriting that she had never written. *One childhood, complete, unspoiled. Provenance: private sale.* She had turned the page over looking for watermarks, for the faint ghost of erasure, for anything that would let her dismiss it as a misfiled joke or a test set by a suspicious supervisor. Guild scribes did test one another that way sometimes — planted errors, false entries, little traps to see who was paying attention. But no one had claimed it. No one had laughed when she'd asked, too casually, whether anyone else had been working the acquisitions folio last month. Master Ellery had only looked at her a moment too long and said, "Folio's yours this quarter, Iversen. If there's an error in it, it's your error."

So it was hers. And if it was hers, then somewhere in the deep stacks was the acquisition record itself — the intake document, the provenance chain, whatever paper trail the Guild kept for a thing as impossible as a bottled childhood. Ledgers referenced; archives confirmed. That was the first law of ink. You could lie in a ledger, but the archive remembered everything, because the archive was where lies went to be filed.

Getting in was the problem. Since the night the stars went out — six days ago now, though the city still spoke of it as though it had happened that morning — the Guildmaster had ordered the deep stacks sealed. Not locked. Sealed. Wax poured over the hasps, guild-seal pressed into each cooling pool, ward-threads strung across every door like spider silk made of scripture. All acquisitions suspended. All retrievals suspended. *Until the sky is explained,* Corvane had said, in that voice like a door closing, *the past is under quarantine.*

Which meant, Wren thought grimly, that the past was exactly where she needed to go.

* * *

The archive occupied the Spire's third root — below the counting floors, below the vaults, below even the cisterns, down where the stone stopped being dressed and started being merely old. To reach it she had to pass the night clerk, and the night clerk was the problem, because the night clerk was Brother Hask, and Hask did not sleep, did not blink enough, and had once reported a junior scribe for taking an extra lump of sugar.

She solved him with tea.

Not drugged tea — she wasn't ready to be hanged for anything worse than trespass — but tea brewed from the bitterleaf the Garden traders sold cheap at the river market, the kind that tasted like boiled bark and sat in the stomach like a warm stone. Two cups of it, delivered to his desk with her most apologetic smile and a story about owing him from the winter festival. Hask drank to be polite, because Hask was nothing if not scrupulous about reciprocating debts, and within the hour his meticulous eyes had gone heavy-lidded and his meticulous pen had begun to wander into the margins of his log, drawing small, precise, pointless spirals.

"Long night," Wren murmured, pausing at his desk on her way out of the scriptorium, playing the part of a colleague heading home.

"Mm," said Hask, and drew another spiral.

The log would show her leaving. It would not show her coming back. She had checked the sightlines three times, and the stair to the third root descended from a corridor the clerk's desk could not see, behind a rack of drying maps that smelled forever of size and iron-gall.

The keys were the other problem, and they were the reason her hands shook as she walked.

They belonged to Under-Ledgerer Sef, who kept the archive keys on a ring clipped inside his coat, because Sef trusted everyone and checked nothing, and whose coat hung in the scribes' cloakroom every evening from sixth bell until dawn while he drank himself agreeable at the Drowned Bell. Wren had taken the ring an hour ago, pressing wax into the hole his absence left in her conscience. She would return them before he woke. She told herself this the way you tell yourself the rope will hold.

At the bottom of the stair, the air changed.

It always did. Everyone said so, even people who'd never been down there — the third root breathed differently, cooler and older, air that had been standing still since before the city had a name. It smelled of dust and vellum and, underneath, something mineral and strange, like the inside of a geode, like rain that had fallen on stone a very long time ago and never dried. The lamps here burned low and blue-tinted, fed on rendered fat mixed with ground lapis that the Guild imported at obscene cost, because ordinary flame, the archivists claimed, was hungry, and hunger had no place among the records.

Wren stood before the sealed door and took out the keys.

The wax seals came away in brittle flakes under her thumbnail — she'd practiced on candle stubs all week, learning how the Guild's sealing wax fractured, how to lift a seal whole and press it back onto softened wax so that it looked untouched. Her forgery would not survive daylight. It didn't have to. It had to survive until dawn, and then it had to survive being blamed on someone else, and she hated herself a little for how easily that thought arrived, how naturally the Guild's arithmetic of blame had settled into her like sediment.

The lock turned. The ward-threads across the frame sang a single low note as they parted around her — attuned, she realized, not to keys but to intent, humming a warning she could feel in her back teeth — and then she was through, and the door swung shut behind her with a sound like a book closing, and the blue dark of the sealed stacks closed over her head.

* * *

Inside, the archive went on farther than any building had a right to go.

She had heard the numbers, of course. Every scribe had. Nine miles of shelving, the senior archivists boasted, though no one had measured them in living memory, and the shelves were said to rearrange themselves by principles known only to the Head Keeper, who had held the post for ninety years and might, some whispered, be less a man than a habit the archive had gotten into. Ordinary scribes were permitted in the shallow galleries only — the recent decades, the mundane acquisitions, the tax rolls and land grants and the sad little ledgers of defaulted debts that were Wren's daily bread.

The deep stacks held the impossible things.

Bottled laughter, catalogued by pitch. A widow's grief, decanted into grey glass and shelf-marked under sorrows, chronic. The last song of a village that no longer existed, pressed into a disc of black wax that wept if you warmed it. Wren had spent six years transcribing references to such things without once seeing them, and now she walked between their shelves in the blue lamplight and felt like a thief in a cathedral, which, she supposed, was precisely what she was.

The catalogue was the key. Every acquisition, however strange, began as paper: an intake docket, a provenance affidavit, a valuation. And dockets were filed by date, and dates were orderly, and order was the one god the Guild had never enslaved.

Her entry — the entry in her own hand — was dated the ninth of the Harvest Month. Which meant the intake papers would live in the autumn gallery, third range, in the section where the shelving smelled of cedar because the oldest documents were kept against rot.

She found the gallery by counting ranges, her lamp held high, shadows swinging across spines of leather and vellum and stranger materials — a binding that looked like bark, one that looked like skin, one that looked, when she refused to look directly at it, like the edge of a thought. The autumn gallery's docket boxes ran along the wall in ranked oak drawers, each labeled in the Head Keeper's immaculate antique hand.

*Ninth day, Harvest Month.*

The drawer stuck. She pulled harder than she meant to, and it gave all at once, and she stumbled back with a fistful of paper fanning across the floor like startled birds.

She knelt to gather them, heart hammering at the noise, and that was how she saw it — face-down on top of the pile, its docket number glowing pale in the lamplight.

*Acquisition 9-H-1147.*

Her hands were steady. That was the strange thing. She had expected them to shake, and instead they moved with the flat competence of someone else's hands, someone who had done this before, someone who handled terrible paper every day — which, she supposed, she had, and did. She turned the docket over.

*Item:* One childhood, complete. Duration: seven years, four months, eleven days. Condition: unspoiled. Emotional residue: high. Notes: subject retained physical form and subsequent memory continuity; extraction clean; no visible scarring.

*Provenance:* Private sale. Seller's signature affixed below.

And below, in ink gone brown with age — though the docket was dated barely two months past, and ink should not brown in two months unless the paper was drinking it, unless something in the archive was older than its own filing — a signature.

Wren knew that signature the way you know your own reflection in a window you're not looking into. The forward-leaning *M*. The crossbar on the *a* that hooked backward like a small accusing finger. The way the final letter trailed off unfinished, as though the writer had always been called away mid-name, all her life, by something more urgent than finishing.

*Maren Iversen.*

Her mother had been dead for nine years.

Wren knelt on the cold archive floor with the docket in her hands and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for six days. The entry was dated the ninth of Harvest Month. Her mother had died in the spring, nine years ago, when Wren was twelve — died of the lung-rot the tanneries gave away free with their wages, died in a charity cot with a priest reading words she'd stopped believing in years before. Wren had signed the death register herself. She remembered the weight of the pen. She remembered the smell of the cot-room, camphor and wet wool. She remembered being angry, even then, that grief felt so much like boredom.

Dead women did not sign dockets.

But the signature was not forged. That was the thing that cracked her open, kneeling there in the blue dark. Wren had spent six years detecting forgeries for the Guild; she knew a copied hand the way a dog knows a stranger's smell. Forgeries hesitated. They were drawn, not written — every stroke a decision, every curve a rehearsal. This signature flowed. The *M* leaned forward with impatience. The crossbar hooked back with that small accusation. Whoever had signed this docket had signed it the way her mother had signed everything: quickly, half-apologizing, already turning toward the next demand.

Either someone alive knew her mother's hand better than Wren did —

— or her mother's hand had been taken, along with everything else. Bottled. Filed. Shelf-marked under *hands, familial*, and lent out, or sold, or used, to sign away the rest of her daughter's life.

Wren sat back on her heels. The lamp flame bent and steadied. Somewhere far off in the stacks, a shelf groaned — the archive settling, or rearranging, or turning over in a sleep of its own — and the sound rolled down the nine miles of shelving and came back changed, and she could not have said what it changed into.

*Subject retained physical form and subsequent memory continuity.*

She read it again. Then a third time, mouthing the words, letting them become real the slow way, the way cold becomes real when it reaches the bone. *Extraction clean; no visible scarring.* Seven years, four months, eleven days. She counted backward without wanting to. From her twelfth year, back through the lung-rot spring, back through the tannery stink and the festival lanterns and the name of the street she'd grown up on — which she could not remember, she realized, could not remember the street, could not remember the door, could not remember whether the stairs had gone up or down — seven years, four months, eleven days landed her precisely at her fifth birthday.

The birthday she had no memory of. The party she had been told about a hundred times, the honey-cake, the paper crown, the cousin who cried — all of it secondhand, all of it stories, all of it, possibly, a cover story, a prosthetic past fitted over the stump of a real one.

"No," she said aloud, and the archive took the word and gave back nothing, which was somehow worse than an echo.

Because here was the shape of it, assembling itself in her chest with the terrible click of a lock turning: the entry in her own handwriting. The handwriting she had never written but which was, she admitted it now, in the place where honesty lives when the lamps are low — which *was* hers, stroke for stroke, as unmistakable as her mother's *M*. Someone had used her hand the way someone had used her mother's. The Guild did not record the future. The Guild recorded what was brought to it. Which meant the entry was not a prophecy and not a prank.

It was a receipt. Written in advance. In her hand, because it was her childhood being entered — entered twice, sold twice, the same seven years passing through the Guild's doors like a coin passed from palm to palm, worn a little smoother each time.

Someone had sold her childhood once already. And someone was selling it again.

Wren gathered the scattered dockets with hands that had finally, finally begun to shake, refilled the drawer, and stood a long moment in the aisle with the docket pressed flat against her breastbone, over the keys, over her heart, as though paper could be kept warm enough to stay true.

She should take it. Run. Burn it. But the archive would know — the archive always knew — and a missing docket was a shout, while a returned docket was a whisper. She slid it back into the drawer with the others, after copying every word of it onto the inside of her cuff in pencil-faint strokes, and pushed the drawer home, and stood listening to the nine miles of silence.

That was when she noticed the sound.

Footsteps. Soft ones, unhurried, somewhere deep in the stacks — deeper than any clerk was permitted, deeper than the lamplight reached. Not approaching. Circling. The way a person circles when they have been watching for some time and are deciding.

Wren killed her lamp and stood in the absolute dark, the docket's words still burning on the inside of her eyelids, and listened to the footsteps come closer, patient as tide, until a voice spoke out of the blackness — young, male, amused, and quiet as a secret:

"You're the one who wrote the entry."
