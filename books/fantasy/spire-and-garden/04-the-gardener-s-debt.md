## 4. The Gardener's Debt

Tam Vey had been in worse rooms than this one. He had been in a wine cellar in Vashkerry that flooded with something that was not water; he had been inside the mouth of a tax collector's vault, breathing through a reed while the man counted coins three feet above his face. He had once spent an entire night folded into a confessional booth with a priest who talked to him for six hours about his sins, believing Tam to be the god of small mercies.

But he had never been in a room like this one, and he did not like it.

The Guild's counting-house annex sat below the waterline of the Spire district, and everything down here smelled of ink and cold stone and something older underneath — a sweetness gone to rot, like fruit left in a sealed jar. The walls were shelved floor to ceiling with ledgers bound in grey leather, thousands of them, their spines stamped with dates going back further than Tam cared to think about. A single lamp burned on the clerk's desk, its flame so steady it looked painted on.

And the ledgers *hummed*.

That was the thing no one warned you about, before you came to steal from the Ledger Guild. Everyone knew the Spire bought impossible things — bottled laughter, a widow's last year of grief, the sound a mountain made before it decided to be a valley. Everyone knew the prices were ruinous and the contracts were iron. What nobody said, because nobody who learned it ever came back to say it, was that the acquisitions were still *alive* in there. That if you stood close enough to the shelves on a quiet night, you could hear them. Not words. Not quite. More like the murmur behind a door where someone is talking in their sleep.

Tam pressed his palm flat against ledger 4471-C and felt it tremble against his skin like a bird's heartbeat.

*Hold on,* he thought, not knowing who he was reassuring. *I'm getting you out.*

He was a courier of the Verdant Order, which meant — in the Guild's phrasing — a thief of the Garden cults, and in his own phrasing, a man who carried things home. Seeds out of burning houses. Names off drowned stones. Once, a jar of somebody's grandmother, or at least the smell of her kitchen, rescued from a condemned tenement before the Memory Tax assessors could bottle it for the open market. The Gardens paid him in food and shelter and the particular warmth of being needed, and in eleven years he had never once been caught.

He was caught now.

He heard the footsteps on the stair — not the heavy boots of Guild wardens but something lighter, quicker, hesitant — and he did what any sensible thief would do, which was nothing at all, because the only exit was a window too narrow for a cat and the lamp would betray any movement. He slid behind the shelf, drew the stolen ledger against his chest, and held his breath the way you hold a sleeping child: carefully, and with a prayer.

The door opened. Lamplight stretched long across the floor.

Then a voice, low and uncertain, speaking to the empty room as though rehearsing:

"I know someone's here. The dust is disturbed along the third shelf, and the humidity seal on 4471-C was broken, and whoever did it didn't log the breach, which means they weren't authorized, which means—" A breath. "Which means please don't make me call the wardens."

Tam closed his eyes. Of all the luck. Of all the rotten, star-scattered luck.

Because he knew that voice. He'd been listening to it for two days through the reading-room wall, tracking the scribe with the ink-stained fingers and the debt-chain glinting at her wrist — Wren Iversen, the one whose name kept surfacing in the Garden's oldest correspondence like a fish rising in dark water. *Iversen.* Even the surname was a key turned halfway. And here she was, alone, at midnight, giving him every reason in the world not to call for help.

He stepped out of the shadow.

She did not scream, which he noted with professional respect. She went very still instead, the way small animals do, and her eyes went from his face to the ledger under his arm to the window and back, calculating exits, weighing odds. Her wrist chain caught the lamplight. Debt-bound, then, and deep enough that they chained her to the work itself.

"You're the night reader," Tam said. "Third desk by the archive grate."

"You're a thief."

"Courier," he said. "But I've been called worse by better-dressed people."

For a moment neither of them moved. The ledgers hummed around them, patient as insects. Then Wren Iversen did something Tam did not expect: she walked past him, deliberately, and set her hand flat on the empty space where 4471-C had lived for sixty years, as though checking whether the shelf itself missed it.

"Do you know what's in there?" she asked.

"No," Tam admitted. "That's rather the point. We don't know what half these things are. We just know they shouldn't be owned."

"It's a grief," she said quietly. "A whole grief. Someone's mother died and the Guild bought the mourning. All of it. Thirty years of it, decanted." Her fingers curled away from the shelf. "It's been humming since I opened the case. I thought at first it was the building settling."

*Ah,* Tam thought. *There it is.* The Garden's letters had said she was sensitive — that she noticed things in the records that trained archivists missed, that she'd flagged seven anomalous entries in a single season, that she had once refused to catalog an acquisition because it "seemed to be asking for something." The Order had sent him for the ledger pages, yes. But the letters had also said, in the old keeper's careful hand: *if the girl can be turned, turn her.*

He hadn't planned to try. Plans were for after escape.

"Put it back," she said. "Please. If the wardens find it missing, the fine gets assigned to whoever last touched the record, and the last person to touch the record was me."

"And if I do put it back," Tam said, "you'll call the wardens anyway, and I'll spend the rest of my short life in a Guild cell being asked very creative questions about my associates."

"I should," she said. But she didn't move toward the alarm cord, and they both heard the sentence end where it ended.

The lamp flickered — the first unsteady thing in the room — and in that flicker Tam made his decision, the way you step off a ledge you've already been falling from.

"I'll trade you," he said.

Her chin came up. "I don't have anything you want. I'm in debt to my eyebrows."

"You have something the Gardens have wanted for longer than you've been alive." He shifted the ledger under his arm, watching her face. "You have your own history, Wren Iversen. Or the lack of it."

* * *

She laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Everyone in this city has holes in their childhood. The Guild sells memory by the pound. Half the nobility can't remember their own weddings because they sold the boredom out of them."

"Not holes," Tam said. "A hole. One continuous absence, ages four to nine, five years wide, clean-edged, professionally cut. You've never wondered why you can't remember your mother's face? Why you dream about a garden gate you've never seen? Why certain words make your hands shake?" He took a slow step closer, and she did not retreat, which he filed away as either courage or a trap. "I've read your file. Well — the parts of it the Order could buy. Your debts were purchased four times before you were twelve. Someone kept selling pieces of you to pay interest on a loan you never signed."

"That's a lie the cults tell to recruit people," she said, but her voice had thinned, and her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the hollow of her throat. "Grief-sellers. Memory-poachers. You find a lonely person and you tell them they're special, that something was taken from them—"

"Someone *was* taken from you."

"—and then you own them instead of the Guild." She lifted her chin higher. "At least the Guild is honest about the chains."

Tam almost smiled at that. Almost. It deserved a smile; it was exactly what he'd have said at nineteen, standing in the ash-gardens outside Vashkerry, refusing the Order's bread because accepting it felt like a leash. He remembered the woman who'd fed him anyway, day after day, until hunger made philosophers of them both.

"The Guild isn't honest about anything," he said. "It's just honest about the price. There's a difference, and it matters less than you'd hope." He patted the ledger against his chest. "Here's the trade, and I'll only say it once, because I can hear the wardens' shift changing two floors up and I intend to be a story by then. This book goes back on the shelf, untouched, and in exchange I leave you one page. Torn fair, logged nowhere, deniable. A page from the acquisition index of the year you lost. Seller's name, buyer's signature, item description, price. Everything the Guild knows about who sold your childhood, and what they got for it."

The silence that followed had texture. Tam had stood in enough silences to know them: the silence before a bridge breaks, the silence in a sickroom at dawn, the silence of a person deciding whether to believe you. This one was all three.

"Why?" she said finally. "Why would you give me that? What does the Garden get?"

"The truth," Tam said, "is that we want you to come find us when you've read it. The truer truth is that the keeper said you might be important, and keepers are wrong about important people roughly nine times in ten, and I'm tired of being the errand boy for nine-tenths probabilities." He shrugged. "And the truest truth, if you want it, is that I've carried other people's memories for eleven years and I have never once seen what it does to a person to learn their own were sold. I'd like to watch it happen to someone who isn't already broken."

"That's a terrible reason."

"It's the only kind I have."

She looked at the ledger. At the shelves. At the window too narrow for a cat. Tam watched her do the arithmetic — the fine assigned to her name, the wardens, the debt-chain at her wrist, the word *seller* sitting in the air between them like a coal — and he watched her arrive somewhere she didn't want to be.

"If I take your page," she said slowly, "and the name on it is no one. A stranger. A dead broker. Then I've committed a crime for nothing, and you've cost me everything I have left, which is my ignorance."

"Yes," Tam said. "That's the shape of it."

"And if the name is someone I know?"

Tam held her gaze and did not flinch, though something in him wanted to. Because he had read the page himself, three nights ago, by candlelight in a safehouse that smelled of rosemary and rain. He knew the name. He knew the signature. He knew that the seller's mark on the Iversen acquisition matched, stroke for stroke, the family hand recorded in the Guild's own genealogical rolls — and he knew what that knowledge had done to the old keeper, who had put the page in Tam's hands with fingers that shook and said only: *be gentle with her. Whoever she becomes, be gentle.*

So he said, "Then you'll know," and let her hear everything he wasn't saying.

Wren Iversen stood in the humming dark of the counting-house for a long moment, lamplight pooling at her feet, and Tam Vey — thief, courier, carrier of other people's ghosts — understood that he was watching a woman stand at the edge of her own life and decide whether to jump.

"Give me the page first," she said.

"Ledger first."

They moved at the same time, wary as duelists, and for one absurd moment the exchange happened in midair — grey leather for folded paper, her fingers brushing his, both of them gripping their half of the bargain like it might bolt. Then it was done. The ledger went home to its shelf, its hum smoothing back into the chorus of the wall. The page went into her hands.

She didn't read it there. He'd expected her to tear it open immediately, greedily, the way he would have. Instead she folded it smaller, and smaller again, until it disappeared into her sleeve, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't name — gratitude and accusation sharing a face.

"Two floors up," she said. "The warden shift changes at the bell. After the bell, the stairwell's clear for maybe ninety seconds. I'd hurry."

Tam blinked. "You're letting me go."

"I'm letting myself find out," she said. "Those aren't the same thing, whatever you want to believe." She turned back toward the desk, toward the lamp, toward the small obedient life she was visibly deciding to burn down, and paused with her hand on the chair. "Courier. The entry I flagged last week — the one in my own handwriting that I never wrote. Do your Garden files say anything about entries that appear in ledgers on their own?"

The question landed in Tam's chest like a dropped stone. Because yes. Yes, the files said things. The files said the oldest acquisitions sometimes *wrote themselves forward*, that the Guild called it bleed-through and denied it existed, that the Garden's founding texts spoke of a sleeper beneath the city whose dreams leaked into paper the way water leaks into stone.

"No," he lied, gently.

She nodded, having clearly known it was a lie, and sat down, and picked up her pen, and became again a scribe in a lamplit room, and Tam Vey went up the stairwell on the bell with the taste of ash in his mouth and one thought repeating all the way to the window, over the rooftops, across the starless dark toward the last green places:

*Be gentle with her. Whoever she becomes.*

Behind him, in the counting-house, a girl unfolded a page by lamplight and began, syllable by syllable, to stop being who she had been.

Above the Spire, where stars should have been, the moon hung alone in the black — and it seemed to Tam, swinging down onto the wet slate of a neighbor's roof, that it had grown, just slightly, since sundown.

One turn nearer thirteen.
