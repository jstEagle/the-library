## 5. Thirteen Turns

The priest's chapel smelled of tallow and cold stone and something older beneath both — a sweetness like fruit going soft in a dark room. Wren had followed the smell through three streets of shuttered houses, past the Guild criers still shouting contradictory orders about the sealed ledgers, down a stair that hadn't been swept since before her grandmother was born.

The door had been unlocked. That was the first strange thing. Every other door in the Spire district had been barred since the night the stars went out; she could see the fresh nails glinting in the lintels as she walked. But this door — narrow, iron-banded, marked with a faded sigil like a sprouting seed inside a circle — stood open to the dark, as if it had been waiting for her specifically.

Inside, a single candle burned on an altar that was not an altar. Wren knew altars. The Ledger Guild kept one in its entry hall, all polished basalt and gilded accounting: *In Ink, Truth.* This was a plank of driftwood laid across two stones, and on it sat a bowl of river water, a handful of dried petals gone gray with age, and a moon calendar carved from bone — but wrong. She counted the notches twice, because the first count made no sense.

Thirteen moons. The circle was divided into thirteen turns, and the thirteenth notch, at the top, had been worn smooth by centuries of thumbs rubbing over it. Worried at, the way she worried at the edge of a page when the numbers wouldn't balance.

"You counted," said a voice from the shadows behind the altar. "Good. Most people count it twice and then decide they miscounted."

The priest was small and folded up like a letter someone had given up trying to read. He sat on a stool against the wall, wrapped in a robe that might once have been white and was now the color of old teeth, and his eyes were milky at the edges — cataracts, or something else. He held a cup of tea he did not appear to be drinking.

"I'm looking for Brother Aldous," Wren said. It was the name Tam Vey had given her, grudgingly, in the flooded undercity, along with a warning: *He'll talk in circles. Don't let him. You don't have time for circles.*

"Aldous is dead." The old man sipped his tea anyway. "I am Brother Emeric. I was Aldous once, before the Spire decided priests should have names that sound like furniture. Sit down, scribe. Your feet are bleeding through your boots. I can smell the ink and the blood, and both of you have come a long way tonight."

Wren looked down. Her boots were, in fact, ruined — she'd waded half a mile of standing water to shake the Guild hunters Tam swore were following them, and the leather had split along the seams. She hadn't noticed. She hadn't noticed a great many things lately, and each unnoticed thing felt like another page torn out of her.

She sat on the floor across from him because there was nowhere else, and the stone was cold through her wet clothes, and she found she didn't care.

"Tam said you'd know about the moon," she said. "About why it's—"

"Wrong?" Emeric smiled without much humor. "Yes. Everyone's noticed it's wrong. They just haven't noticed *how* wrong, because noticing would require them to remember what right looks like, and the Spire has spent four hundred years making sure nobody remembers anything it doesn't sell back to them at interest." He set down the cup. "You work in the Ledgers. Tell me what you catalogued last month. Before the stars went out."

Wren hesitated. Then, because the routine of it steadied her, she recited: "A bottled laugh, provenance unknown, third harvest. Six hours of a ferryman's grief, decanted. A lullaby removed from a woman in the Lowbridge district — she sold it to pay a debt, and now she hums nothing at all. A memory of snow, from a child who'd never—" She stopped.

"Go on," Emeric said softly.

"From a child who'd never seen snow." Wren's mouth had gone dry. "I wrote that entry. I remember writing it. And then I thought — how can a child remember snow she never saw? Where does a memory come from when it isn't yours?"

"Now you're asking the question," the priest said. "Four hundred years late, but welcome all the same."

He rose, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and lifted the bone calendar from the altar. His hands shook, but the shaking stopped when he touched the thing, as if the calendar held him steady rather than the reverse.

"Before the Spire," he said, "there was the Garden. Not these sad little plots the cults tend in cellars — the true Garden, which was not a place but a practice. We were keepers. Not of a god, exactly. The word 'god' makes people think of thunder and demands. Think instead of... a well so deep it dreams. A presence beneath the city older than the city, older than the kingdom, older than the idea of kingdoms. It slept, and its dreaming leaked upward, and everything that grew here grew rich on it. Memory. Meaning. The sense that things *belong* to their names."

"The impossible acquisitions," Wren whispered. "Bottled laughter. Grief by the hour."

"Harvested from its dream," Emeric said. "When the Spire came, they didn't kill it. Killing it would have ended the supply. Instead they bound it deeper and built taps. Pipes, we called them in the old tongue, though your ledgers call them acquisition rites. Every impossible thing in every ledger is a cup dipped into a sleeping mind. And a mind that is drunk from, scribe, wakes slowly. In pieces. In *anger*, eventually."

He turned the bone calendar in the candlelight. The thirteen notches caught the flame one after another as it rotated, little crescents of light.

"The moon keeps the count," he said. "It always has. Twelve turns, and the year closes, and the god sleeps on, because twelve is a sleeping number — the Spire's number, order and closure, the double-entry of heaven. But the moon does not belong to the Spire. Once in an age — once in a very long age — the moon completes a thirteenth turn before the year releases it. A turn the calendars refuse. A night that belongs to no ledger." His clouded eyes found hers. "On that night, the binding thins. On that night, the god beneath us can be *named*. Speak its true name, and it wakes whole — and remembers everything that was taken, and everyone who took it."

"And the war," Wren said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. Tam had mentioned war only once, obliquely, the way you mention a fire in a neighboring county. "There was a war."

"There was a war," Emeric agreed. "The last time the thirteenth turn came, eight hundred years ago. The god woke halfway — named by half, spoken by half — and what rose from the deep was grief wearing a body. Cities burned that are ash fields now. The Ashmarch. You've heard the name; the Spire teaches it as a cautionary fable about foreign invaders. It was not foreigners." He set the calendar down with terrible gentleness. "It was us. It was our debt coming due."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and earth, a bell began ringing the hour — the Spire's great bronze voice, counting out time as though time were still a thing anyone could trust.

"So the Spire will silence it," Wren said. "On the thirteenth turn. That's the other option. You said named awake — or silenced forever."

"They'll try." Emeric lowered himself back onto his stool with a sigh. "Silencing requires knowing the name too. You cannot unmake a word you cannot speak. For four hundred years the Guildmasters have collected fragments of the name — bought them, stolen them, harvested them from the god's own dream like everything else — believing that if they owned enough pieces, they could own the whole. Own the name, own the choice. Wake it as a weapon or bury it as a vault. Either way, *they* hold the key." He paused. "But names are not coins, scribe. Names do not sit quietly in vaults. A true name wants a throat. And a true name, denied every throat for four hundred years, will find another way to be carried."

"What way?"

Emeric smiled again, and this time there was no humor in it at all, only a vast and ancient pity that made Wren's skin prickle.

"You told me about the child who remembered snow," he said. "Tell me about yourself, Wren Iversen. Tell me about the years you lost."

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She hadn't told him her full name. She was certain of it. She had introduced herself as Wren, nothing more, the way you do when you're hunted and frightened and every syllable feels like evidence.

"How do you know my—"

"The same way I know your boots need resoling and your mother signed something she regretted until the day she died." He waved a hand, dismissive of his own impossible knowledge. "Old keepers hear the well, even shallow ones like me. And you—" he leaned forward, and the candlelight moved in his eyes like something swimming, "you ring, girl. You've rung since the moment you climbed my stairs. Like a struck bell that hasn't finished sounding."

"I don't understand."

"No. But you *feel* it. You've felt it since the stars went out, haven't you? Since the wards fell. Tell me I'm wrong."

She couldn't tell him he was wrong. Because it was true — since that breathless moment when the sky had emptied itself, Wren had felt a pressure behind her sternum, a fullness, a sensation like standing in a doorway between two rooms where the air moved differently. She had blamed fear. Fear was easy to blame. But fear didn't hum. Whatever lived under her breastbone hummed, low and constant, and it had grown louder every night as the wounded moon crawled toward its impossible completion.

"When I was seven," she heard herself say, "there were two years I can't account for. My mother told me I'd been ill. Fever. The Guild records say the same — I checked, years ago, because the gap itched at me the way an unbalanced column itches. Two years, filed and closed. But fevers don't leave *shapes*, Brother Emeric. Sometimes, at the edge of sleep, I remember shapes. Green light. Water moving underground. A voice so large it wasn't a voice, more like weather learning to speak. And it wasn't talking to me. It was talking to *itself*, dreaming aloud, and I was—" Her throat closed. "I was close enough to hear."

"In the deep places," Emeric said quietly, "the bindings were always thinnest. The old keepers sent children down sometimes. Not as sacrifice — as *vessels*. A child's memory is soft wax, girl. It takes an impression the way an adult's never can. They brought children to the dreaming edge so that something of the god would press itself into them and be carried safely up into the world, scattered among small lives where no army would think to look." He spread his hands. "And then the Spire learned what the keepers had done, and they went collecting. Bought the vessels' childhoods out from under them. Filed them as acquisitions. Locked the impressions in the sealed stacks, where they believed the name would stay put, inert, archived."

Wren's hands had gone numb. Or perhaps they had been numb for some time and she was only now noticing, the way you notice cold when the fire goes out.

"But the entries were sold," she said. "My childhood was *sold*. Someone with my mother's signature. And the ledgers move, Brother. Things get misplaced. Pages go missing. I found an entry in my own handwriting that I never wrote — the Guild has been shuffling those memories around for four hundred years like coins in a thief's pocket, and somewhere in all that shuffling—"

"—a fragment found its way home," Emeric finished. "Or never left. Or was never fully taken. The Spire's archives are not as perfect as the Spire believes. Nothing living stays perfectly filed." He picked up his cold tea and drank it anyway, grimacing. "The name is not in a vault, Wren Iversen. It has not been in a vault for twenty years. It has been walking around in borrowed boots, balancing other people's accounts, wondering why the sky feels wrong."

The bell above finished its counting. Silence rushed in behind it, thick as floodwater.

Wren became aware, very precisely, of her own heartbeat. Of the hum beneath it. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, and for one vertiginous instant she felt the pressure push *back* — patient, immense, sorrowful, like a hand resting against a door from the other side.

"It's in me," she said. Not a question. There was no question left in it. "The name. The true name. It's been in me my whole life and I didn't—"

"You weren't meant to know," Emeric said. "Your mother sold your memory to buy your safety, and the gods of irony being what they are, the sale is what kept the fragment free. If she'd known what she was carrying out of the deep places—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Grief enough for one night. You'll want the rest of it later, and later may not come, so listen now. The moon completes its thirteenth turn in nine days. When it does, every power in this city will come for the throat that can speak the name. The Guildmaster's hunters. The Garden zealots, who think waking the god means glory. Opportunists, prophets, fools. They will not ask nicely, and they will not ask once."

"Then I give it to them," Wren said. "If speaking it wakes it, and waking it burns cities—"

"If you speak it, you become the match and the powder both, and they will use you until there is nothing left to burn." Emeric's voice hardened, and for a moment the folded-up old man looked like something much older and less harmless. "But hear the rest, because there is a rest: a name can also be *unmade*. Silenced forever, as your friend's masters intend — but silencing requires the whole name gathered in one place, in one vessel, at the moment of the thirteenth turn. Do you understand what that means? It means that on that night, whatever carries the name becomes the most valuable object in the world. To wake it. To bury it. Either way, they need *you*, whole and breathing and delivered."

The candle chose that moment to drown in its own wax, and darkness took the chapel — total, velvety, absolute. In the dark, Wren's hum swelled until she could feel it in her teeth, in the bones of her skull, and beneath it, faint and far below the city's foundations, she felt something answer. Something turning over in its sleep. Something that knew she was listening.

When Emeric relit the candle from a taper, his face was grave.

"Nine days," he said. "And the Guild already knows a scribe went asking questions in the sealed stacks. You have until morning, perhaps, before Corvane's people stop searching the city and start searching the *records* — and your name, girl, is written in more of them than you know. Including one entry, filed twenty years ago, that names you as an acquisition." He reached beneath his stool and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, holding it out to her. Inside, she could see the corner of a page. Old paper. Guild paper. Her mother's signature slanting across the bottom like a wound.

"Take it," Emeric said. "Take the road out of the Spire before the bells ring prime. And Wren — when they come for you, and they will come — remember that the name in your blood is not a burden you carry." His clouded eyes held hers, and in them she saw reflected the tiny flame, and beyond the flame, impossibly, a suggestion of green light and moving water. "It is a *debt owed to you*. Act like a creditor."

She took the bundle. It was warm, though the chapel was freezing, warm as a living thing, and through the oilcloth her fingertips registered a pulse that was either her own heart or something much larger keeping time beneath the world.

Outside, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. Nine days to the thirteenth turn. And somewhere behind her, already, she could hear boots on the stair — many boots, climbing fast, with the particular rhythm of men who had been told exactly whom to find.
