## 6. Flight from the Spire

The bell began before dawn — or what passed for dawn now, in a city where no sun could be trusted to rise on schedule. Wren heard it from her cot in the scribes' dormitory: three strokes, a pause, three more. The Guild's oldest alarm. Not *fire*. Not *flood*. The third bell meant theft.

She was already dressed. She had not slept. The ledger page lay folded against her ribs, warm from her body all night, as if it were a living thing she'd smuggled beneath her shirt — and perhaps it was. Her mother's signature sat against her heartbeat like a second pulse.

*Sold by Maren Iversen. One childhood, complete. Condition: unspoiled.*

Footsteps in the corridor. Not the shuffling tread of half-asleep scribes. Boots. Evenly spaced. Deliberate.

Wren blew out her candle though there was nothing left to darken — the world outside had been dark for two days, starless and moonless and wrong — and pressed herself flat against the wall beside the window as the door swung open without a knock.

Guildmaster Corvane stood in the doorway, flanked by two hunters in grey. He did not look at the cots. He looked at the window, at Wren, with the mild disappointment of a man reading an entry that failed to balance.

"Scribe Iversen," he said. "You've been in the sealed stacks."

It was not a question, and that was the worst of it. Questions could be answered. Statements could only be survived.

"The ledgers were sealed by your own order," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I catalogued what I was assigned to catalogue."

"You catalogued what you were never meant to see." Corvane stepped into the room. The hunters remained in the corridor, and their stillness had a quality she recognized from the archives — the patience of things that waited to be written down. "Entry 4,417. Do you know why we seal certain pages, child? Not because they are dangerous. Because they are *incomplete*. A memory torn out of context is a knife without a handle. You have cut yourself already. I can see it in how you stand."

"I stand fine."

"You stand like someone holding something against her ribs." His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and terribly kind. That kindness frightened her more than cruelty would have. Cruelty you could hate. "Give me the page, and this becomes a disciplinary matter. Fines. Pen work. Your debt extended, but your hands kept. Refuse, and it becomes something else entirely."

Her hand moved before her mind agreed to it — flat against her sternum, the universal gesture of a liar protecting a lie. She saw him read it. She saw him sigh.

"Brand her," Corvane said quietly, turning away. "Thief of the Ledger. Standard mark. And send word to the gates."

The hunter nearest the door raised his hand, and Wren understood, in the half-second before the ink left his fingers, that Guild hunters did not carry whips or irons. They carried *writing*. A black sigil bloomed in the air between them, hanging like a drop of tar suspended mid-fall, and then it flew — fast, fast, faster than anything written should move —

— and struck the doorframe above her head because she had already thrown herself through the window.

Glass took a strip of skin from her forearm. The fall was one story onto the refuse-slope behind the dormitory, and she rode it the way she'd once ridden the laundry chutes as an apprentice, arms crossed, breath held, hitting garbage and old rainwater and rolling. Above her, the sigil hit the empty window frame and burst into letters that crawled across the brick like ants: *THIEF. THIEF. THIEF.*

The whole Spire would know within the hour. The whole Spire would know within the *minute*.

Wren ran.

* * *

Tam Vey had been waiting in the tannery district since the third bell, which was either devotion or stupidity, and he had stopped pretending to himself it was devotion somewhere around the fourth hour.

He crouched among the curing racks where the hides hung like flayed ghosts, breathing through his mouth because the lime pits stank of everything the city wanted to forget, and watched the Spire's upper terraces. From here you could see the whole grey mass of it, tier upon tier, the Ledger Guild's tower rising from its crown like a needle threaded with every secret in the kingdom. Two days ago there had been stars above it. Now there was only a ceiling of perfect, pressurized black, and Tam had begun to suspect the missing stars were not a symptom but a *countdown*.

The moon would make its thirteenth turn soon. Everyone felt it, even the ones who didn't know why. Dogs howled at nothing. Bread refused to rise. Old women in the undercity chalked warding spirals on their doors and spat on the corners.

And somewhere inside that needle-tower, a debt-bound scribe was deciding whether to trust him.

He'd told her the truth, mostly. Her history for the seller's name. What he hadn't told her — what he'd been ordered not to tell her — sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. *The Gardener sends greetings, little quill. Bring us the vessel. The name sleeps in her blood.* He'd had six days to decide whether he was a courier or a kidnapper, and he still hadn't decided, and the indecision was starting to feel like a decision itself.

Then the bells changed.

Three strokes, pause, three — and then a fourth stroke he had never heard before, long and low, a sound less like a bell than like a throat clearing after centuries. Every hide on the racks seemed to shiver.

*Thief of the Ledger,* said the wind off the Spire, carrying the crawling sigils' echo. *Thief. Thief.*

And a moment later, smaller, running footsteps on wet cobbles, coming fast along the tannery wall.

Tam moved before thinking, which for him was rare enough to be noted. He caught her arm as she stumbled past the rack-line — she nearly put a knife in him, good instincts, wrong target — and pulled her into the shadow between two curing sheds.

"They branded you," he whispered.

"They can take the brand back when they return my childhood," Wren breathed. She was bleeding from one arm, glass-cut, shallow, and she hadn't noticed. Her eyes had the particular shine he'd learned to dread in new Garden recruits: terror burning hot enough to pass for faith. "Corvane knew. Before I said a word, he *knew*. How does he know, Tam? Who told him?"

"Someone who reads the same ledgers you do." He tore a strip from his sleeve, spat on it, and pressed it to her cut; she flinched and let him. "Later. Right now there are four hunters sweeping the tannery district and a gate-ward with your face chalked on it. Can you run?"

"I've run from creditors since I was nine."

"Good. Because these aren't creditors." He peered around the shed. In the street beyond, a figure in grey walked with terrible unhurried calm, and where its boots touched the puddles, the reflections *forgot themselves* — water rippling with no image in it at all. Tam's stomach dropped. "That's not a hunter. That's Erasure."

"What's an Erasure?"

"Something the Guild says doesn't exist." He took her wrist. "Run anyway."

* * *

They went down.

Every instinct said up, over the walls, out of the city — but the gates were warded and the walls were watched and the sky itself had become a locked door, so Tam led her instead into the city's underside, through a hatch behind the glue-works that smelled of bone and horse-hoof, down stairs that predated the Spire and possibly the kingdom.

Wren had lived in the capital eleven years and had never known it had a basement. The undercity opened around them like the inside of a lung: vaulted dark, channels of black water, the ruins of streets built atop older streets. Someone had lived here once. Lamps still hung from hooks, generations dead, their oil long dried to amber tears.

"Hunters don't like the deep," Tam murmured. "Their ink needs light to set."

"And you know this because?"

"Because the Gardens have been hiding people from the Guild for three hundred years." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight underneath. "Keep your left hand on the wall. The water rises past the third junction."

She kept her hand on the wall. The stone was slick and strangely warm, and after a while she realized it wasn't stone — or not only stone. It was carved, everywhere, so worn by water that the carvings had become suggestions: leaves, vines, faces with their eyes closed. Sleeping faces. Hundreds of them, thousands, receding into the dark in both directions.

"Tam," she said slowly. "Who built this?"

His silence stretched long enough to answer.

"Gardeners," he said finally. "Before the Gardens were cults. Before we were exiled into orchards and herb-shops and called superstition. We were keepers. This whole city was built as a — " he searched for the word, hated it, used it anyway — "a *cradle*. Something underneath needed tending, and we tended it. Then the Spire came, and the Spire learned you could harvest a god like any other crop, and suddenly memory had a market price." He glanced back at her, and in the lamplight of some forgotten niche his face looked younger and more afraid than she'd ever seen it. "You asked me who sold your childhood. I'll give you the page. But understand what you're asking. Once you know the name on that line, you can't unknow it. Nothing down here can be unknown. That's rather the point of it."

Wren's fingers found the folded page against her ribs. Entry 4,417. Her mother's signature, small and careful, the same hand that had written *eat something, love* on a hundred lunch-wraps.

"She was protecting me," Wren said. It came out like an accusation aimed at no one. "Whatever she sold me to escape — she was protecting me."

"Or surviving," Tam said gently. "Those aren't always the same thing. Down here, we learn the difference."

Water sounds. Dripping, somewhere far off, keeping time like a patient clerk. And beneath the dripping — Wren stopped walking. Tam stopped with her.

Beneath the dripping, faint as a thought at the edge of sleep, something vast was breathing.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered.

Tam had gone very still. "Hear what?"

"That. Like — like someone asleep, but *underneath* everything. Underneath the sound."

For a moment his expression flickered through something complicated — hope and horror sharing a single face — and then boots echoed in the passage above, many boots, and grey light spilled down the stairwell like milk poured into ink.

"Erasure," Tam breathed. "Three of them. They followed the brand-sigil — it's still writing you, Wren, it leads them like a scent." He grabbed her arm. "There's a keeper's door past the flooded junction. We can lose them in the rootways. But you have to leave the page."

"Leave it?"

"It's marked. Everything the Guild writes is marked — that's how they found the sealed stacks breach, that's how they found *you*. The page is a lantern in the dark and you're carrying it against your heart." His eyes were urgent, honest, and she wanted with a ferocity that frightened her to believe him. "Throw it in the water. Memorize the signature first if you have to. But let it go."

The boots came closer. Somewhere above, a voice like dry paper read aloud coordinates, and the darkness flinched.

Wren unfolded the page one last time. Lamplight from a dead man's niche fell across her mother's signature — *Maren Iversen*, the loops of the M like birds taking flight — and beneath it, in the Guild's cold clerical hand, the buyer's notation she had somehow missed in the archive's panic:

*Acquired on behalf of the Office of the Guildmaster. Purpose: containment of the Name-bearer's formative years.*

Name-bearer.

The word went through her like a hook through a fish. She read it twice. Three times. The breathing beneath the city seemed to quicken, very slightly, as if it too had read over her shoulder.

"Wren," Tam hissed. "Now."

She memorized the line — she was a scribe, memory was her first language — and then she hurled the page into the black water, where it did not sink but *unwrote*, dissolving into pale threads that swirled once and were gone.

Above them, the reading voice faltered. Lost the scent.

"This way," Tam said, and pulled her down into the dark, toward the breathing, toward whatever she had just discovered she was.

Behind them, faint and fading, the hunters' boots turned back toward the light — and ahead, beneath everything, the sleeping thing exhaled, and the water in the channels stirred, and Wren Iversen ran toward the sound of her own name being dreamed.
