## 7. What the River Remembers

The undercity smelled of drowned stone and old prayers.

Tam Vey had grown up in the flooded quarter, back when it was merely poor instead of forgotten, and he knew its moods the way other men knew weather. Tonight the water ran high and black through the old processional streets, swallowing doorways that had once opened onto shrines, and the only light came from the fungus gardens the Gardeners had seeded in the vaulted ceilings — pale shelves of glowcap and ghostwort that turned every archway into a ribcage of soft green fire.

He moved through it with a courier's economy, one hand trailing the guide-rope strung between drowned lampposts, the other keeping Wren Iversen's wrist in a grip firm enough to steer but loose enough, he hoped, not to feel like a cage. She had stopped flinching from his touch an hour ago. That was either trust or exhaustion, and with scribes it was usually exhaustion.

"Mind the step," he said, which was a joke, because there were no steps anymore — only the suggestion of them beneath six feet of river.

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed since the Spire's bells had begun their hunt-song behind them, since the word *thief* had been nailed to her name in wax and iron. But she kept walking, and she kept asking questions, and Tam had learned enough about Wren Iversen in two days of flight to know that the questions were how she prayed.

"Why does the water glow?" she asked now, tilting her head back toward the fungal shelves. Her voice echoed strangely in the flooded nave they were wading through; the acoustics of dead churches did strange things to living voices.

"The Gardens planted them," Tam said. "After the Purges. When the Spire burned our surface temples and salted the ground, the keepers came down here with spores in their pockets and started over." He gestured at the luminous tiers overhead. "Every light you see is a memory someone refused to sell."

Wren stopped wading. "That's not — " She caught herself, and he watched her do what scribes did: reach for the ledger, the entry, the official version. "Memory isn't *plantable*. It's not a seed. It's an acquisition. It has provenance. It gets catalogued, appraised, stored under seal conditions — "

"In the Spire," Tam said. "Yes. That's one way to keep a memory. Cold, dark, owned." He turned to face her, water swirling around his knees. "Here's another."

He reached into the oilskin pouch at his belt and drew out a small clay vessel, no bigger than a fist, sealed with green wax stamped with the sign of the open hand. He held it out to her.

"This one's mine. My grandmother's, actually. A summer evening. The whole of it — the smell of the bread, the sound of my grandfather arguing politics with a neighbor, the exact weight of the light coming through a window that hasn't existed in forty years." His throat tightened, as it always did. "I've carried it since I was nine years old. It's the last thing of hers the Guild never got."

Wren stared at the vessel without taking it. In the glowcap light her face looked carved, all planes and shadow, the face of someone doing arithmetic with her whole world.

"You're telling me the Gardens traffic in memories too," she said quietly. "You just don't file paperwork."

"I'm telling you we *keep* them. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." He tucked the vessel away again, gently, the way you'd cover a sleeping child. "Come on. The keeper who can answer your real questions is another half-mile down, and the hunters' dogs lose our scent past the third sluice."

* * *

They traveled the rest of the way in silence, or near enough. The undercity unfolded around them like the interior of some vast drowned animal: aqueducts gone to grotto, cisterns the size of cathedrals, staircases descending into water that had no bottom anyone had plumbed. Once they passed a wall covered in handprints, hundreds of them, pressed in ochre so old it had become part of the stone. Wren dragged her fingers an inch from the surface as they went by, close enough to feel the cold radiating off it, and Tam saw her lips move — counting, probably. Scribes counted even when they wept.

At last they reached the place the Gardeners called the Weir: a great round chamber where three ancient channels met, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a black mirror of still water. Wooden walkways laced across it on stilts, and on those walkways hung lanterns of woven reed, and among the lanterns moved figures in gray-green robes who paused, at the sight of Tam, and then bowed — low, formal, unmistakably deferential.

Wren noticed. Of course she noticed. "You said you were a courier."

"I am a courier." He helped her up onto the nearest walkway, feeling the familiar guilt settle into his chest like sediment. "I also have a name in the order. Come on. Mother Ysolde will want to see you before you sleep. Or after. With her it's hard to say."

Mother Ysolde received them in a cell that smelled of river-mud and dried feverfew, lit by a single lamp whose flame burned a color Wren would later describe — to herself, in the privacy of her own skull — as *wrong*: too gold, too steady, a flame that behaved less like fire than like attention. The old woman herself was small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with hands knotted by decades of garden-work, and eyes that went through Wren the way a needle goes through cloth.

"So," Ysolde said. "The first entry walks into my house."

Tam felt Wren go rigid beside him. "You know what she is."

"I know what the ledgers say she is, which is a different thing entirely." The old woman lowered herself onto a stool with the care of someone whose joints had opinions. "Sit, both of you. This will take longer than your legs will want to stand for."

* * *

What follows, Tam thought afterward, was the moment the world turned over for Wren Iversen — though at the time she gave no sign of it beyond a certain terrible stillness, the stillness of a woman watching a ledger burn.

Ysolde told it plainly, without ornament, the way you tell a thing you have told a hundred times and expect to tell a hundred more.

"Before the Spire," she began, "there was the Deeping. You'll find it in no Guild record, because the Guild wrote the records, and the Guild was built on making sure no one remembered otherwise. But once, this city had no walls and no wards and no ledgers. It had a god."

"A god," Wren repeated. Flat. Testing the word for flaws.

"A sleeper. Older than the city, older than the kingdom, maybe older than the stars your Spire pretends govern everything." Ysolde's gnarled hands folded in her lap. "It dreamed beneath the earth, and its dreams leaked upward, and everything that grew grew rich on them. Our ancestors were not priests, girl. They were *gardeners*. Tenders. Their work was to sit by the god's dreaming and keep it sweet — to sing to it, to prune the nightmares before they took root, to carry its excess dreams up into the world like water from a well. Every story, every song, every impossible thing your Guild now bottles and sells — all of it was once freely given. Dreamed outward. Belonging to everyone."

"And the Spire took it," Wren said. Not a question.

"The Spire *invented itself* out of taking it." For the first time the old woman's voice sharpened. "Some centuries back — the dates are lost, deliberately lost — the men who tended the god's dreams decided that free gifts were wasteful. That a dream could be harvested, measured, sold. They built the first Ledger Hall on the god's own doorstep. They learned to cut memories loose from the dreamer without killing the dream, the way you'd tap a tree. And when the god stirred in its sleep — troubled, the way anything stirs when it's being bled — they called it wrath, and they called their theft *protection*, and they built walls above their sin and wards upon the walls and told the children the god below was a monster that must never be named."

Tam watched Wren's hands. They had gone white-knuckled in her lap, and her lips moved faintly — counting again, or reciting, some catechism of the Ledger Guild drilled into her so deep she probably couldn't name it as a catechism. *All acquisitions are accounted. All accounts are balanced. All balance is peace.*

"That's not true," she said finally. "The Spire teaches — the founding texts say — "

"The founding texts were written by the thieves, child. Would you trust a ledger kept by the man robbing the till?"

"But the wards. The border wards, the ones that fell — if the god is just a victim, why did the stars go out when it — "

"Because the wards were never protection." Ysolde leaned forward, and the wrong-gold lamplight filled the creases of her face until she looked like something very old drawn in fire. "They were a muzzle. A lid on a pot. For three hundred years the Spire has been bottling a god's dreams and selling them by the dram, and the god has been sinking deeper and deeper into a sleep that is starting to look like dying. And now the lid is off, and the moon is turning, and every soul in this kingdom is about to learn what their comfort was made of."

Silence. Somewhere far off, water dripped in the dark with the patience of water.

Wren stood up. It was abrupt, graceless, nothing like the composed scribe Tam had met in the archive stacks. She walked to the cell's narrow window slit — a slot of pure blackness, since there were no stars left to see through it — and stood there with her back to them both.

"My childhood," she said. "My missing years. The entry in my own handwriting. The signature that might be my mother's." Her voice was very controlled, which Tam had learned was worse than screaming. "You're telling me that wasn't just debt. That wasn't just the Guild buying up some poor woman's misfortune. You're telling me the Spire has been harvesting *me* since I was a child, because of what I carry."

"I'm telling you," Ysolde said gently, "that you were never a debtor. You were a crop."

Tam saw it hit her — saw it land somewhere below the ribs, below argument, in the place where a person keeps the load-bearing beliefs. Wren's hand rose to her own sternum, pressing flat, as if checking whether the ground floor of herself still held.

"All those years," she whispered. "I catalogued them. Bottled laughter. Years of grief. I wrote the entries. I *appraised* them. I sat at that desk and put prices on stolen dreams and called it honest work, and the whole time — " Her breath caught. "The whole time I was inventory."

"Wren." Tam started to rise.

"Don't." She didn't turn around. "Don't be kind to me right now. I need — " She pressed her palm harder against her chest. "I need to count something. Give me a moment to count something."

So they gave her one. Tam watched her shoulders rise and fall, rise and fall, the rhythm of a woman rebuilding herself out of numbers because numbers were the only material she trusted, and he felt the full weight of what he'd known since the archive, since before the archive, since the day the order had first shown him her name: that he had brought this on her. That *use* was the word Ysolde would use for him too, if she knew everything.

Not yet, he told himself, in the old coward's cadence. After the thirteenth turn. When it won't matter anymore.

* * *

Later — much later, in the small hours when the undercity's glowcaps dimmed to embers — Tam found Wren sitting alone on the walkway over the black mirror of the Weir, her boots off, her bare feet dangling a finger's width above the water. She didn't look up when he sat beside her.

"The river remembers," she said, after a while. "That's what your people say, isn't it? I heard one of the tenders muttering it when we came in."

"It's the oldest line we have. *What the river remembers, the gardens grow.*" He followed her gaze down into the water, where their reflections floated broken by ripples — two smudges of green light and shadow, indistinguishable from any other smugglers in any other age. "The river runs down from the mountains, over the god's dreaming, through every root in the valley. Everything it touches, it carries a little of. The Gardens learned to read it. That's half our craft, really. Memory isn't only in heads. It's in water, in wood, in the rings of trees. The Spire just never learned to look anywhere but inside people, because inside people is where you can put a price tag."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then, without looking at him:

"If everything you're saying is true — if the god is real, and sleeping, and bleeding — then why haven't the Gardens woken it already? You've had centuries. You've got keepers, and songs, and rivers full of memory. Why wait for me?"

And there it was. The question he'd been dreading, arriving with a scribe's precision, straight to the seam.

"Because waking it is war," he said. "Or might be. The last time it stirred — truly stirred, not just turned in its sleep — kingdoms burned. Even Ysolde doesn't pretend otherwise. The old stories disagree about whether the burning was the god's rage or the Spire's doing, whether the sickness came from the wound or the bandage. But ash doesn't care which story is true." He pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them. "So the order taught us patience. Keep the fragments safe. Keep the faith alive in small ways. Wait for a vessel clean enough to speak the name without shattering — or strong enough to choose not to."

"A vessel." Her voice had gone very quiet. "Is that what I am to you, Tam? Is that what this whole rescue was?"

"No." The lie came fast and smooth, worn smooth as river stone from years of rehearsal, and he hated himself for how easily it slid. "No. I found your page in the acquisition ledger and I — "

"*I found* it," she repeated softly. "A courier. Found a specific anomalous entry, in a sealed archive, belonging to a specific junior scribe. And happened to be caught stealing the very night I needed a way out." She finally turned to look at him, and her eyes in the glowcap light were the color of deep water, and just as hard to see the bottom of. "I'm a scribe, Tam. Reading entries is what I *do*. Your story balances on paper. It just doesn't balance in ink."

The silence stretched. Above them the glowcaps breathed their slow green light. Below them the black water held its counsel, carrying, as it always carried, its cargo of everything the city had ever tried to forget.

"Tomorrow," Tam said at last, standing, needing to stand, needing to not be seen lying from a seated position, "Ysolde will show you the reading rooms. The true histories. And there's a keeper coming upriver who knew your mother — who may know what she signed, and why, and what she thought she was protecting you from. Sleep if you can."

He walked three steps toward the walkway's end before her voice stopped him, quiet and precise as a knife laid flat on a table.

"Tam. Whatever you're not telling me — whatever you were sent to do — decide soon whose side of the ledger it goes on." A pause. "Because I've spent my whole life balancing other people's accounts. I'm very good at finding what's owed."

He didn't answer. He couldn't. He walked on into the green dark, and behind him he heard her turn back to the water, and the last thing he heard before the dark took him was the smallest sound of all:

Wren Iversen, alone over the black mirror, beginning to count.
