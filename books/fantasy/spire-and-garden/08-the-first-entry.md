## 8. The First Entry

The flooded undercity had a way of making silence feel like a held breath.

Wren sat on the edge of a dry stone shelf above black water, the ledger page spread across her knees. Tam had gone to fetch lamp-oil from one of the drowned market stalls — he moved through these sunken streets like a man walking through his own house, which unsettled her more than she liked to admit — and for the first time since the stars went out, she was alone with the thing that had been following her for days.

Not a hunter. Not yet. A page.

*Acquisition 1: One childhood, complete and unbroken. Seller's mark affixed. Buyer: The Ledger Guild of the Spire.*

Her mother's signature curled at the bottom in that looping hand Wren knew better than her own heartbeat — the hand that had written shopping lists and birthday notes and, once, a letter to a father who never came back from the border wars. *Maren Iversen.* Three syllables she had not spoken aloud in eleven years.

The page did not glow. It did not whisper. It simply lay there, ordinary as a receipt, and somehow that was worse. Somewhere in the Spire's sealed stacks, her childhood sat filed between someone's bottled laughter and a year of grief, catalogued, cross-referenced, *owned*.

She should have felt nothing. A scribe learns early that paper is paper, that ink is only ink, that the Guild's impossible acquisitions were rumors told to frighten apprentices. But the page was warm against her knees. Warm as skin.

And beneath the warmth, something stirred — a pressure behind her eyes, like a word waiting on the tip of the tongue for eleven years.

Tam returned with oil and a stub of candle, and paused when he saw her face. "You've figured it out."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You're touching your temple." He set the candle down and crouched across from her, water lapping somewhere below in the dark. "Everyone does that. When there's a hole in them and they can finally feel its edges." His voice was gentler than she'd ever heard it. "It's the page, isn't it. It wants to give it back."

"Pages don't want things."

"Garden pages do." He struck a light. The flame caught, threw their shadows up the dripping walls, and Wren saw that Tam was watching her with an expression she couldn't name — guilt, maybe, or its close cousin, pity. "The Gardens never sold memories, Wren. We kept them. Tended them. A memory read aloud in the right place, by the right person, comes home. That's all it takes. That's the whole secret."

"That can't be true."

"Why? Because the Spire taught you memories are commodities? That they're extracted, bottled, filed away, and the person they came from is supposed to just—" he made a small, dismissive gesture, "—carry on?" He slid the ledger page toward her across the stone. "Read it. Not the words. Read it the way you'd read a prayer."

"I'm not religious."

"No," Tam said quietly. "But something in you is."

* * *

She waited until he withdrew to the mouth of the passage, keeping watch on the stair, because some part of her still did not trust him — could not trust him, would be a fool to trust him — and even so, she wanted no witness for what she was about to do.

The candle burned low. The water below breathed in and out against the stones.

Wren put her palm flat on the page, over her mother's signature, and began to read aloud.

*Acquisition 1: One childhood—*

The words left her mouth and did not echo. They fell into the dark the way stones fall into deep wells, and then—

Then the well gave them back.

* * *

Rain. She was six years old, and it was raining the night everything ended, though she hadn't known it was ending; she had thought it was only another night, another argument behind her mother's closed door, another evening of counting coins on the kitchen table while the lamplight turned the copper dull as old blood.

"Mama, are we poor?"

Maren Iversen looked up from the coins, and Wren — the Wren who was watching, the Wren who was also somehow six years old again, standing in the doorway with wet boots and a cold nose — saw her mother's face do something terrible. It composed itself. Smile first, then eyes, then voice, each piece fitted into place like a scribe assembling a forgery.

"We're rich in other ways," her mother said. "Come here, little bird. Let me braid your hair before bed."

But her hands shook while she braided. Wren remembered that now — remembered feeling it through her scalp, tiny tremors traveling down each plait like messages in code. She had fallen asleep mid-braid, head heavy against her mother's knee, and woken past midnight to voices in the front room.

A man's voice. Smooth, unhurried, with the particular patience of someone who has never once been refused.

"The debt is called due, Mistress Iversen. The Guild has been generous. The Guild is done being generous."

"There must be another arrangement. There's always another arrangement. My husband died for this city—"

"Your husband died for a city that doesn't remember him. But the Guild remembers everything." A pause, and when the voice resumed it had softened into something almost kind, which was somehow more frightening. "There is a new acquisition program. Experimental. Very valuable material, properly harvested. A childhood — complete, unbroken, the whole of it — fetches enough to clear your account twice over. With a stipend besides. You'd live comfortably. She'd live comfortably. Better than comfortably."

"She's six years old."

"She won't miss what she can't remember. That's the beauty of it. No grief, no wound, no scar. She'll wake tomorrow and the years will simply... begin where we say they begin. Clean ledgers, Mistress. For both of you."

Silence. Long enough that six-year-old Wren, crouched at the top of the stairs in her nightgown, began to hope. Began to believe her mother would refuse, would throw the man out, would take her hand and run into the rain and keep running until the Spire was a smudge on the horizon behind them.

Then her mother said, "Will it hurt her?"

And the man said, "Less than debt will,"

—and Maren Iversen began to cry, silently, the way she did everything costly, without letting anyone see the price —

—and signed.

* * *

Wren came back to herself on the stone shelf with her cheeks wet and her throat raw and the candle burned down to a blue nub, though it had been tall when she began. Hours. She had lost hours inside her own head, and they had not been lost at all. They had been *returned*, every one of them, delivered like mail to an address that had stood empty for eleven years.

She remembered now.

She remembered the morning after, waking in a strange soft bed with a stipend-widow's breakfast beside her and no idea how she'd arrived, her mother kneeling over her with red-rimmed eyes saying *good morning, little bird, do you know my name?* And Wren — six years old, hollowed, re-hemmed — had looked at this kind stranger and said, politely, "Should I?"

She remembered being taken to the Guild orphanage with a tag tied to her wrist like luggage. *Iversen, Wren. Age: indeterminate. Prior record: none.* She remembered learning letters faster than any child in the ward, because ink was the only thing that felt familiar, because her hands remembered holding pens even if nothing else did. She remembered the Guildmaster — younger then, Corvane with brown in his beard instead of ash — visiting the orphanage and studying her a moment too long, the way a man studies an investment.

She remembered her mother visiting, once a year, on the false birthday the Guild had assigned her. Remembered sitting across a table from a woman who loved her visibly, desperately, and feeling nothing but a vague obligation to be polite. Remembered the last visit, seven years ago, when her mother had gripped her hands and whispered, "Do you feel anything? Even a little? When you look at me?" and Wren had said, "You seem nice," and watched something die behind her mother's eyes.

Her mother had stopped coming after that.

And two winters ago, a notice had come through the Guild channels, routine as weather: *Maren Iversen, deceased. No estate. No next of kin.* Filed by Wren herself, in her own neat hand, because that was the job. She had entered her own mother's death into a ledger and felt nothing at all, and had wondered, briefly, idly, whether there was something wrong with her.

There was something wrong with her. Someone had taken it out.

* * *

Grief came first, and it was enormous, and she let it come because she had spent eleven years with no room in her for it and now the room was suddenly, brutally furnished.

She cried the way her mother had taught her without teaching her — silently, costly, hiding her face in her arms — and the black water below caught the sound and gave back nothing.

Then the fury came, and the fury was worse, because grief spends itself and fury compounds.

They had *filed* her. Somewhere in the sealed stacks of the Spire, between a bottle of laughter and a year of someone's sorrow, her sixth birthday sat in a drawer. Her father's face — she could see it now, oh gods, she could see it, the crooked smile, the ink stains on his fingers, he had been a scribe too, that was why pens felt like home — her father's laugh, her mother's lullabies, the smell of their kitchen, the name she'd had for the stray cat under the stairs — all of it, boxed and shelved and stamped with the Guild's seal, and above it all, in the margin, in the neat clerk-hand she now recognized as Corvane's own:

*First entry. Proof of concept. Handle with care.*

Proof of concept. She had been the pilot program. The test case that proved a human being could be emptied and refilled and sent back into the world functional, productive, *indebted*. Every impossible acquisition that followed — every bottled grief, every stolen year — had been built on the template of her. The Ledger Guild's whole trade in souls had begun with a six-year-old girl and a mother with no other currency left.

And her mother — her mother had chosen it. Had weighed her daughter's childhood against debt collectors' knives and chosen the childhood, and Wren understood, she *understood*, protection was real, the Guild's collectors were not gentle with defaulters, her mother had bought her life with the only coin she had —

—and understanding did nothing, nothing at all, to stop the scream building in her chest, because love that sells you is still a sale, and forgiveness was a country she could not find on any map tonight.

"Wren." Tam's voice, low, from the passage mouth. "Movement on the stair. We need to go."

She didn't move. She sat with the ledger page crumpling slowly in her fist and the candle dying between them, and she said, in a voice she barely recognized as her own:

"They didn't just steal it. They built everything on top of it. The whole trade. I'm the first entry, Tam. Everything the Guild owns, it owns because of me."

"Then you're not just a fugitive anymore." He came closer, and in the guttering light his face wore something that might have been awe or might have been fear. "You're the foundation. If the first entry can be undone—"

"It can't be undone." She stood. Her legs were steady, which surprised her; she had expected to shake apart. "But it can be answered for."

Above them, faint through the drowned city's broken ceiling, the moon rose one turn fuller — twelve nights now, maybe fewer — and somewhere far overhead, in the sealed stacks of the Spire, Wren knew exactly which drawer held the rest of her life.

She intended to go get it back.
