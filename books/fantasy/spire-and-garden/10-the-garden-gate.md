## 10. The Garden Gate

The last free Garden did not want to be found, and Wren understood why by the third day of walking.

She had followed Tam's map — a scrap of vellum covered in his cramped courier's hand, directions written not in streets and landmarks but in things the city had forgotten. *Past the tannery that burned twice and was mourned once. Where the wall leans like a drunk against his neighbor. Follow the smell of wet stone until the smell of green overtakes it.* She had walked out through the starless dark of the undercity's western mouth, past the drowned chapel where the river remembered its dead, and up into hills she had never known existed, because no ledger she had ever catalogued mentioned them.

That was the thing about the Spire, she was learning. It did not simply hide what it took. It hid the fact that anything had been hidden. The Guild's ledgers recorded acquisitions with such meticulous care that the absence of an entry became its own kind of erasure — a blank page so clean it looked like nothing had ever been there to write about.

The hills were full of blank pages.

Grass grew over them in long silver waves, bending under a wind that came from nowhere she could name, because the sky above her was a smooth black lid. No stars. No moon yet either — the moon would come, the priest had said, waxing toward its thirteenth turn, and every night it failed to rise felt less like absence than like held breath. Wren walked through that breathless dark with a satchel of bread gone hard as slate, a knife Tam had pressed on her at the parting of their ways, and the memory of his confession still lodged under her ribs like a fishhook.

*I was sent to use you, not save you.*

She had believed him. That was the worst of it. Even now, three days alone, she could not decide whether believing him had been wisdom or just another kind of hunger — the hunger of a woman who had spent her whole life being someone's entry, grasping at the first hand offered to her, even a hand she knew was baited.

The green smell found her on the morning of the fourth day.

It came first as a wrongness — a sweetness threaded through the cold mineral air, green and growing and utterly impossible this far into the dying season. Then the hillside opened. Not dramatically; nothing about the Garden announced itself. The slope simply stopped being hillside and became terraces, descending in slow curves into a hollow where light gathered without any source Wren could find. Vines climbed trellises of pale wood. Fruit hung heavy on branches that should have been bare. And everywhere, threading between the planted rows like veins through a leaf, ran channels of water so clear they seemed less like water than like the idea of water, remembered perfectly.

Wren stood at the top terrace for a long time, doing the thing she did best: cataloguing.

*One garden, unrecorded. Light, source unknown. Water, flowing uphill in two places.* Her scribe's mind reached for ink and found none, and the reaching itself felt like grief. For ten years she had written down everything the Spire took from the world, and now, standing in front of something the Spire had never touched, she did not know how to hold it except by writing it, and there was no one left to write for.

"You're standing in the lettuce."

Wren spun, knife half-drawn. An old woman sat on a low stool between the rows, so still and so woven into the greenery that Wren had taken her for a heap of sacking. She was small, brown as a nut, wrapped in layers of patched wool despite the warmth. Her eyes were milky at the edges — cataracts, or something older — but they fixed on Wren with unsettling precision.

"I'm sorry," Wren said. "I didn't—"

"No one ever does. They stand in the lettuce." The old woman patted the earth beside her stool. "Well. Come down properly or don't. But if you've come all this way on Vey's little map, you'd better come down. He always did draw maps for people he wasn't sure he wanted to find the place."

Wren's stomach dropped. "You know Tam?"

"I know everyone who carries my seeds and steals my letters." The keeper — and Wren knew now, with a certainty that bypassed thought, that this was the keeper, the dying one the fragments of rumor had spoken of — smiled with most of her teeth. "Sit. You look like a page that's been carried too long in a wet pocket."

Wren sat. The soil was warm through her trousers, warm like a sleeping animal, and she snatched her hand back from it before she understood why.

The keeper laughed — a dry rustle, like wind through husks. "Yes. It does that. Warm because it remembers summer. All of them." She leaned forward, and the milkiness of her eyes seemed to recede, leaving something sharp and green underneath. "Now. Let me look at you, Wren Iversen. Daughter of Maren. Debtor of the Ledger Guild. First entry."

Each word landed like a stone dropped into still water. Wren made herself stay still. "How do you know my mother's name?"

"Because your mother wrote to me." The keeper reached into her wrappings and drew out a bundle of letters tied with faded green cord, handling them the way Wren handled first editions — with reverence, with fear of damage. "For eleven years. Every month, until her hands got bad. She wrote asking questions the Spire wouldn't let her ask. What happened to the childhood she sold. Whether what grows back can grow back wrong. Whether her daughter would hate her." The keeper's voice gentled. "I told her the truth, which is that I didn't know. I'm the last one who might have. Everyone else who tended the god is ash or archive."

"The god," Wren said. The word came out flat, defensive. "The priests say there's nothing beneath the city. The Guild says the 'impossible things' are curiosities. Bottled laughter. Grief by the year."

"And what do *you* say? You've catalogued enough of them." The keeper tilted her head. "You've held a bottle of laughter, girl. Did it feel like a curiosity?"

Wren thought of the small stoppered vial from her second year in the Guild, the one she had been forbidden to open but had opened anyway, alone in the stacks, because a scribe who doesn't sample her inventory is a scribe who can't verify it. The laughter had poured out around her like warm water. It had been someone's laughter — specific, particular, a woman laughing at something a child had said, and the love in it had been so dense and so helpless that Wren had sat on the archive floor and wept for a stranger.

"It felt like theft," Wren said quietly.

"*There* is your theology." The keeper thumped her stool. "Everything else is commentary."

* * *

They walked the terraces while the sourceless light dimmed toward evening, and the keeper talked, and Wren listened the way she had once listened to her mother's stories — knowing even then that some of them were true, and unable to tell which.

"The Gardens were not cults," the keeper said, snapping a dead leaf from a tomato vine with practiced fingers. "Whatever word the Spire uses. We were groundskeepers. That's all. When the god first went to sleep — and it went to sleep willingly, girl, remember that, it *chose* the long dream rather than burn the world again — our order kept the ground above it. Kept it soft. Kept things growing, because growing things remember. A tree doesn't need a ledger to know what it was last spring. It just knows."

"And the Spire?"

"The Spire was built on its back. Literally — you've seen the foundations, the black stone that never warms? That's it. That's the god's spine, dressed up in masonry. And when the founders realized what slept beneath them, they faced a choice." She held up two gnarled fingers. "Wake it, honor it, live beside a power that had once cracked continents. Or harvest it. Bottle its dreams, sell its memories by the measure, keep it dreaming and drugged and *useful*. The Gardens said honor. The Spire said use." She shrugged. "The Spire had soldiers."

They passed a bed of white flowers Wren didn't recognize, and as she leaned close she heard — faintly, unmistakably — humming. A lullaby, in no language she knew, coming from the flowers themselves.

"What are these?"

"Maren's favorites. Your mother asked about them in her third letter." The keeper watched Wren's face carefully. "They grow from a memory. One of the first the Spire ever took. A song a mother sang. The god dreamed it, once, and the dream fell into the soil here, and it grew." She paused. "We plant what escapes them. Every scrap that slips the ledgers, we put in the ground and tend. That's why they call us thieves. We're composting stolen goods."

Wren stood among the humming flowers for a long moment. Somewhere in the Spire's sealed stacks, she knew, there was a ledger entry for that song. *Acquired:* a date. *Seller:* a signature. *Price:* whatever price mothers get for their singing.

Her hands had curled into fists without her permission.

"You said the god chose to sleep," she managed. "Then why does everyone want to wake it? Corvane wants it silenced forever. The Garden zealots want it awake and angry. Nobody seems to care what it wants."

"Ah." The keeper lowered herself onto a bench beside the flowerbed, breathing harder now, and Wren saw for the first time how thin the old woman's wrists were inside the wool, how the skin lay on them like paper. "Now we arrive at the reason Vey's map brought you to me, though I suspect he didn't know it himself. Sit. This is the part where I tell you what you are, and you will want to be sitting."

Wren sat.

"The god sleeps, but a sleeper still dreams, and a dreamer still has a name. Its true name — not the hundred false ones the Spire's founders invented to bind it, not the holy syllables the zealots chant — its *real* name is the lock and key of everything. Speak it whole, and the god wakes exactly as the speaker intends: healed, if the speaker means healing; weaponized, if they mean war. Erase it entirely, and the god doesn't die — gods don't die — but it forgets itself, and a god that forgets itself dreams nightmares, and its nightmares leak." She looked at Wren steadily. "Corvane wants the second thing. He thinks silence is safety. He's wrong, but he's honest about wanting it. The zealots want the first thing, the waking, and they're wrong too, and dishonest besides."

"So the name has to be protected. Hidden. I understand why the Guild seals its archives—"

"No." The keeper's voice cracked like a whip. "No, child. Stop thinking in ledgers. The name was never *hidden*. Hiding is a Spire concept — put the dangerous thing in a box, guard the box, charge admission. The name was *given away*. Do you understand? Before the god sank into its long sleep, it broke its own name apart and scattered it — into bloodlines, into bloodlines' bloodlines, thinned and diluted across thousands of ordinary people, so that no single throat could ever speak it whole again. That was its own final act. Its own safeguard. It made itself unspeakable."

The evening light had gone amber and low. The humming flowers sang their borrowed lullaby. Wren heard her own pulse in her ears.

"But names thin," the keeper went on softly. "Generations pass. Fragments drift, combine, recombine. And very rarely — once in an age — the scattered pieces gather again in one person. Not the whole name. Never the whole name, not naturally. But enough of it. Enough that such a person stands at a crossroads no one else can stand at. They could gather the rest. Call the pieces home. Speak the god awake — or speak it silent. They are, whether they wish it or not, the hinge."

The old woman reached out and took Wren's wrist. Her grip was astonishingly strong for hands so frail. She turned Wren's arm over and pressed her thumb hard into the soft flesh below the palm — and Wren gasped, because the touch *lit*. Under the skin, spreading out from the keeper's thumb like ink dropped in water, gold-green light bloomed in branching lines, tracing patterns up Wren's forearm that looked disturbingly like script. Like handwriting. Like entries.

"The name in your blood," the keeper said. "Written where no ledger can reach it and no hunter can erase it. Your mother carried a fragment. Her mother before her. But you — you're a confluence, girl. The river came home to you." She released Wren's arm, and the light sank slowly back beneath the skin, leaving the faint ache of a bruise that wasn't there. "That's what the Guild really bought, all those years ago. Not your childhood. Your *proximity*. Someone told them what you might become, and they've been keeping you close ever since — debt instead of chains, ink instead of iron. You were never a scribe who stumbled onto a secret. You were the secret, shelved."

Wren's mouth was dry. "My mother. Did she know? When she signed—"

"She knew she was buying your life with your history. The Guild was going to take you outright — a confluence is far too valuable to leave running loose — and she negotiated. Sold the years they wanted most, bought their patience, bought you a childhood of ordinary poverty instead of a cage." The keeper's eyes softened. "She hated herself for it every day of her life. I have eleven years of letters that say so."

Wren looked down at her arm, at the ghost-faint traceries already fading. *First entry,* the Guild's records called her. She had thought it meant her debt. It meant her *self*.

"Why tell me?" she asked finally. "If waking it means war — if I'm the hinge — why arm the hinge? Why not let Corvane have his silence?"

"Because silence isn't peace, it's just a slower war." The keeper gripped her bench, and a spasm crossed her face — pain, deep and structural. "And because I'm dying, girl, and dying people don't have time for comfortable lies. So here is mine to give you, straight: if you wake the god whole, there *will* be war. Not maybe. Not possibly. The kingdoms above remember what it did the last time it woke, even if the ledgers burned the memory. Cities of ash. Rivers boiled. And every power in the world will fall on whoever woke it, and on each other, and the god's sorrow at what it caused will crack the ground again. Waking it is not salvation. It is choosing which catastrophe, and whose."

"Then what do I do?"

"That," the keeper said, "is the question I cannot answer, and the reason I sent for you anyway." She fumbled in her wrappings again and produced a small clay pot, sealed with green wax, cool to the touch. "A seed. The last one from the god's own garden, grown from a dream it dreamed about *peace* — do you understand, it dreamed of an ending to its own wars, and the dream survived. Plant it where you make your choice. Whatever you choose, let it grow from that spot. And—" she caught Wren's hand, pressing pot and seed both into it, folding Wren's fingers closed with hers, "—forgive your mother. Not because she deserves it. Because carrying both the name and the hatred will split you, and the world cannot afford for you to split."

Far off, beyond the hollow, beyond the hills, the black lid of the sky shuddered.

Both of them felt it — a tremor in the sourceless light, a stutter in the water channels, the humming flowers faltering mid-phrase. The keeper's head came up sharply, her blind-milky eyes suddenly clear and terrified.

"That's not the moon," she whispered. "The turn isn't complete. Something's *pulling* at it. Someone is trying to force the turn early." She struggled upright, swaying. "Girl — the Guild's hunters aren't only behind you. There's a company of them moving on this valley right now, and they're not coming for the seed."

Wren's hand closed around the clay pot. Below them, at the edge of the lowest terrace, the green light of the Garden began to gutter like a lamp running dry — and out in the dark hills, torches were kindling, one, then five, then dozens, closing in a ring.

"They're coming for *me*," Wren said.

"No," the keeper said, and smiled, and it was the saddest smile Wren had ever seen. "They're coming for the name. And you, my dear, are where it lives."
