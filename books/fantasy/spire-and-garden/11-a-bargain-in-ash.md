## 11. A Bargain in Ash

The ash came down like snow that had forgotten how to be beautiful.

Tam Vey crouched beneath the ribs of a burned-out granary at the edge of the flooded undercity, watching it settle on the black water in gray flakes that hissed when they touched the surface. The Spire's hunters had torched the eastern terraces two nights past — not to kill anyone, he suspected, but to burn away the paths, the hiding places, the soft green corridors the Garden cults had threaded through the city's bones for a century. Fire was Corvane's way of editing. The Guildmaster could not erase what people remembered of a place until there was no place left to remember.

Tam knew about erasure. He had been trained in it since he was nine years old, though they had never used that word in front of him. They had said *retrieval*. They had said *acquisition*. They had said, in the gentle voices the Garden used for its children, *you will help us take back what was stolen*.

He turned the ledger page over in his hands for the hundredth time. He had taken it from Wren's satchel while she slept — a small theft, practiced, almost tender — and now he sat with it the way a man sits with a knife he has decided not to use and cannot put down.

Her name was written there in her own hand, or a hand indistinguishable from it: *Wren Iversen, first acquisition, age six. Seller: Maren Iversen.* And beneath it, in the cramped cipher of the Garden's inner circle, the notation that had been his orders for three years, copied and recopied until he knew it better than his own prayers:

*The vessel carries the name intact. Confirm by proximity. Deliver to the Deep Gate before the thirteenth turn. The keeper's consent is irrelevant.*

*The keeper's consent is irrelevant.*

He had read those words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, and only now, sitting in the ash-fall with Wren sleeping twenty feet away on a pallet of dry reeds, did he finally hear them.

She stirred. He slid the page into his coat and made himself look at the water instead, at the drowned staircases descending into green-black nothing, at the pale fish that drifted through the windows of submerged houses like thoughts moving through a sleeping mind.

"Is it morning?" she asked. Her voice was rough with sleep and smoke. "I can't tell anymore. Without the stars."

"It's morning somewhere," Tam said. "Probably."

She laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real — and sat up, pushing hair out of her face. In the gray light filtering down through the granary's broken roof she looked younger than she was and older than she should have been, both at once, the way everyone looked now that the sky had gone blank. Three days without stars. Three days since the wards fell, since the world had lost its ceiling, and every hour the moon swelled a little fuller toward its impossible thirteenth turn.

"We should move before the water rises again," she said. "You said the Deep Gate is reachable from the flooded quarter. Through the old processional stairs."

"I said that," Tam agreed.

"You've gone quiet." She studied him with those ledger-keeper's eyes, the eyes of a woman who had spent ten years cataloging other people's impossible things and had learned, somewhere along the way, to weigh a soul by its silences. "Quieter than usual. What aren't you telling me?"

Everything, he thought. I am a man made entirely of things I'm not telling you.

"The stairs are guarded," he said, which was true, and which was also the smallest coin in a very large debt.

* * *

They walked the drowned streets in single file, Wren ahead because she insisted, Tam behind because it let him watch her without being watched. The water came to their knees, then their thighs, warm as bathwater and smelling of iron and rot and, strangely, of roses — the Gardens' old irrigation channels still fed something down here, some root system older than the Spire itself, and flowers bloomed in the dark where no one would ever see them.

Wren kept stopping to touch them.

"You do that a lot," Tam said.

"Do what?"

"The flowers. The moss on the walls. Anything growing." He meant it as an observation. It came out sounding like an accusation, and he hated himself a little for that.

She glanced back at him, wading, one hand trailing in the water where white blossoms opened around her fingers. "My mother had a garden," she said. "Before. I don't remember it — that's the whole cursed point of me, isn't it, I don't remember anything before the Spire took me — but my hands remember. Isn't that strange? They reach for things on their own. Like they know something the rest of me doesn't."

*Your hands remember,* Tam thought, *because your childhood is folded into a god's dream beneath this city, and dreams leak.* He said none of it. He had been saying none of it for three years, and the silence had grown so heavy inside him that he sometimes felt it in his teeth, a pressure like a second skull.

At midday they reached the Hall of Ten Thousand Steps — the old processional descent, carved before the Spire, before the ledgers, before anyone had thought to write down what a god dreamed. The stairs went down into the floodwater and kept going, swallowed, each step marked with a glyph that glowed faintly when Wren passed it. She didn't seem to notice. Tam noticed. The glyphs were waking for her, one by one, like lamps lit for a returning daughter.

That was when he understood that he was out of time. That whatever he chose, he had to choose it here, in this drowned hall, with the ash still falling somewhere far above them and the moon climbing toward its final turn.

"Wren." His voice came out wrong. Too loud in the echoing dark. "There's something you need to hear. From me. Before we go any further."

She stopped on a half-submerged step and turned, and the glyph-light made her face pale and strange. "You're frightening me, Tam."

"Good," he said. "You should be frightened. You should have been frightened since the night I pulled you out of the archive, except you were too busy running to ask the right questions, and I was—" He stopped. Breathed. The water lapped against the stone, patient as a tide. "I wasn't going to let you ask them."

"What questions?"

"Why me," he said. "Why a Garden courier just happened to be caught stealing from the Ledger Guild the same week a scribe found her own name in a sealed acquisition. Why I just happened to know every safe road out of the Spire. Why I just happened to be there, Wren, every single time, at every turn, exactly when you needed me." He made himself hold her eyes. It was the hardest thing he had done in three years of hard things. "Because I was sent. Not to save you. To use you."

The words fell into the water between them and sank.

"Sent by whom?"

"My order. The inner circle — the ones even the keepers don't speak of. The Gardens aren't what you think they are. They're not all herb-wives and candle-singers mourning a stolen god. There's a faction that wants the god awake the way a man wants a weapon awake. They've known for years that the name survived — that it wasn't harvested with everything else, that it hid itself somewhere the Guild's ink couldn't reach." He swallowed. "In blood. In a bloodline. In a child sold to the Spire before she was old enough to remember being sold."

Wren had gone very still. Stillness, Tam had learned, was worse than screaming. Screaming meant the wound was open; stillness meant it was going septic.

"Say it plainly," she said.

"You're the vessel. You've always been the vessel. I was sent to confirm it and to deliver you to the Deep Gate before the thirteenth turn, and the page in my pocket — the one naming your seller — the one I traded you for your trust — I was authorized to give it to you. It was bait. Your mother's signature was bait. Everything I have given you on this road was measured out for me by people who have never met you and do not care whether you live, so long as you arrive."

The hall was silent except for the water. Somewhere below them, deep beneath the flood, something vast exhaled in its sleep, and the surface of the water shivered with rings that spread outward and outward and outward, like the god turning over in a dream it could not wake from.

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — one note, flat, terrible.

"The archive," she said. "The night we met. You were caught stealing. Was that staged too? Did you let them catch you?"

"No. That part was real. I was stealing for myself, by then." He heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway. "I'd been watching you for two years, Wren. Two years of watching you catalog bottled laughter and bottled grief and go home to a room you owed the Guild for, and somewhere in there the mission stopped being the mission. When they caught me I told myself it was providence. A sign. The Gardens love signs." His mouth twisted. "It wasn't providence. It was just a thief getting caught. But I used it. I walked up to you with a trade I'd been handed like a knife, and I watched you take it, and I told myself I'd find a way to make it mean something else by the end."

"And did you?"

"I don't know," he said, and it was the truest thing he had said in three years. "I don't know if a man can steal a person's trust honestly."

* * *

She walked away from him then — not running, just walking, up out of the water onto the dry upper steps, her satchel banging against her hip, her shadow long and crooked in the glyph-light. He followed because following was all he knew how to do, and she rounded on him with a fury he had not seen in her before, a fury that seemed to come up out of the ground itself.

"Don't," she said. "Don't follow me. Don't stand there looking like a kicked dog. You don't get to be sorry in a way I have to comfort."

"I'm not asking you to—"

"You gave me my mother's name." Her voice broke on *mother*, and she hated that it broke, he could see her hating it. "Do you understand what that was? I have nothing, Tam. No childhood. No before. Every memory I own was assigned to me by the Guild, stamped and shelved and taxed. And you handed me one true thing — one — and let me build three weeks of hope on it, knowing it was bait. Knowing you were walking me to a gate like a lamb to—" She stopped herself. Drew breath. When she spoke again her voice was low and cold and worse than the fury. "What happens at the gate, Tam? After I arrive. After I'm delivered. What do your handlers do with a woman who can wake a god?"

"They need someone to speak the name."

"And after that?"

He said nothing.

"After *that*," she repeated. "After the name is spoken and the god is awake and the war starts — the war your priest warned me about, the war that burned cities to ash last time — what use is the vessel then? Do they need her alive? Do they need her at all?"

The water dripped somewhere in the dark. The glyphs pulsed faintly, patiently, lighting her path downward whether she wanted to walk it or not.

"That's what I thought," Wren said.

"It doesn't have to be them," Tam said desperately. "The zealots aren't the Gardens. The keeper at the western gate — the dying one — she told you the truth, she warned you about the war, she—"

"She's one woman, and she's dying, and her order sent *you*." Wren's laugh was awful. "You know what the worst part is? I believed you. Out of everyone — out of the Guild and the priests and the zealots and Corvane with his kind, reasonable voice — I believed *you*, because you were the only one who wanted nothing from me. Because you were just a courier with debts of your own." She shook her head slowly. "I made you honest because I needed you to be. That's not your sin, Tam. That's mine. But this—" she gestured at him, at all of him, "—this is yours."

"I was going to tell you," he said. "I was going to tell you at the gate. I was going to stand between you and them and—"

"*Was*," she said. "Everything you have to offer me is in the past tense."

She turned and started down the stairs.

"Wren." He said it to her back, to the glyph-light rippling across the water around her ankles. "You can't go alone. The Erasure is hunting you. Corvane's hunters unmake memories — you've seen what they did to the ferryman. Alone, you won't—"

"Then I'll be unmade alone," she said, without turning, "which is more than I'd be with you."

And she went down, step by step, the glyphs waking ahead of her and dimming behind her, until the dark took her entirely and the only sound was the water settling into the shape of her absence.

* * *

Tam stood in the Hall of Ten Thousand Steps for a long time after she was gone.

The ash found its way down here eventually — it always did, sifting through cracks and broken roofs, settling on the black water in a thin gray skin, so that the flooded hall looked like the surface of a dead star. He fished the ledger page from his coat and held it over the water, meaning to let it go, meaning to drown his orders the way he should have drowned them three years ago.

He couldn't. His hands wouldn't open. They were courier's hands, trained hands, hands that delivered what they were given, and they held the page the way they had once held messages that got people killed, and Tam Vey stood alone in the drowned dark and understood that he was exactly what Wren had named him: a man whose every gift arrived in the past tense.

Below him, far below, the god breathed, and the water trembled.

Above him, through the shattered roof, the sky showed its blank gray face — and at its edge, where the horizon should have been empty, he saw it now for the first time: a smudge of movement on the ruined terraces. Lantern-light. Many lanterns, moving in formation, disciplined as ink lines down a ledger page.

Not the Gardens. The Gardens scattered; these moved like a single hand writing.

The Erasure had found the flooded quarter. And they were heading for the stairs.

Tam looked down into the dark where Wren had gone, then up at the lights descending toward him, and felt the oldest truth of his training settle over him like the ash: *the mission is the mission, and the mission is not finished.*

He ran down into the dark after her.
