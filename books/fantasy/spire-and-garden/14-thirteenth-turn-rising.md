## 14. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The rope came out of the dark like a serpent deciding to be kind.

Wren had been counting her remaining hours by the drip of water through the ruined aqueduct — a habit she'd picked up in the Ledger Guild, where everything was measured or it didn't exist — when the knot dropped past her shoulder and Tam's voice followed it down, rough and low and impossibly alive.

"Up. Now. Before I lose my nerve again."

She stared at him. Three days since he'd confessed what he was — a courier sent to use her, a leash with a face — and walked away into the ash-fog of the low quarter without looking back. She had rehearsed every cruel thing she would say if she ever saw him again. She had imagined his death more than once, and felt guilty about how little it moved her.

What she had not imagined was that he would come back.

"You're supposed to be gone," she said.

"I was." His hands were shaking on the rope. In the grey light leaking through the aqueduct's broken crown she could see new cuts on his knuckles, a bruise blooming along his jaw like ink dropped in milk. "Gone is a large place, Wren. It turns out I couldn't afford the rent."

"Is that meant to be an apology?"

"It's meant to be a warning." He glanced up at the sliver of sky, and she watched his throat move as he swallowed. "The Erasure found my old cell. They found my handler. She's not going to be finding anything anymore."

Wren took the rope. Her palms were still raw from the last climb, and the hemp bit into the wounds with a familiarity that felt almost domestic now — pain as punctuation, pain as the only honest ledger left to her.

"Why?" she asked, before she climbed. "Why come back? You got your orders. You got out."

Tam looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he said next, he seemed to have to dig for it, hauling each word up from somewhere deep and flooded.

"Because I stood in a doorway for two days deciding whether you were a job," he said, "and then I realized the doorway was closing, and I still hadn't decided. That's not loyalty. I know that. But it's the closest thing I own."

Above them, through the fracture in the aqueduct's spine, the moon hung in its starless sky like a hole cut in black cloth — and it was fuller than it had been yesterday. Fuller than it should have been.

* * *

They traveled by night, which no longer mattered, because every night was the same color now. Since the wards fell and the stars went out, the kingdom had become a country of one long dusk, lit only by the moon's slow, obscene completion. Farmers burned their fields rather than let them stand unwatched. Pilgrims clogged the roads walking toward the Spire, toward the thing beneath it, drawn like water finding a crack.

From the ridge above the river town of Marrow's Ferry, Wren could see all three armies.

The Spire loyalists came from the north in columns of grey and silver, their banners showing the closed book of the Guild, their wagons heavy with ledgers bound in iron. Even at this distance she could pick out the Erasure among them — they walked differently, she'd learned that much. The hunters who unmade memories moved like people listening to something behind the world, heads tilted, faces empty as unwritten pages.

To the south, the Garden zealots came singing. That was the worst part. Their voices carried up the ridge in fragments — hymns Wren half-remembered from childhood, songs about the Gardener waking, about the harvest of names — but the melodies had been bent into something martial, something with teeth. They carried sickles and seed-sacks and staves crowned with thorns, and they sang as though the war they wanted were already won.

And between them, in ones and twos and small desperate companies, the opportunists: relic-hunters, mercenary bands, prophets with painted faces selling protection from a god nobody had seen in a thousand years. One enterprising crew had set up a market on the bridge itself, hawking charms against remembering and charms against forgetting, and doing brisk trade in both.

"They're all coming for the same door," Tam said beside her. He crouched in the bracken, chewing a stalk of something bitter, his eyes tracking the columns below with a courier's flat assessment. "Spire wants to seal it forever. Garden wants to kick it open. And everyone else wants to sell tickets."

"And us?"

"We want to get there first." He spat the stalk out. "Or at least get there loud enough that first stops mattering."

Wren looked at the moon. It hung over the converging armies like a coin about to be spent, and she could see now — had been able to see for two nights — the thin ring of shadow beginning to gather at its edge. The thirteenth turn. The old priest's words came back to her in the dead man's voice he'd used to say them: *When the moon completes its thirteenth turning, the name can be spoken whole, or buried whole. There is no third option.*

There was a third option. It lived in her blood, and it was made of ten thousand pieces, and no one below knew it yet.

She intended to keep it that way until the very last moment possible.

* * *

Marrow's Ferry itself was a lesson in what fear did to ordinary people, and Wren catalogued it despite herself — old habits dying hard, the scribe in her still reaching for ink whenever the world grew too large. A woman had nailed a list to the ferry-house door: names of neighbors suspected of dreaming too vividly. A boy sold cups of riverwater sworn to be blessed, though the river ran black with silt these days and blessed nothing. In the tavern, a Guild clerk — she recognized the ink-stains, the nervous way he held his hands like birds he was afraid of — read aloud from a proclamation while the room listened with the particular silence of people deciding whether to obey or burn something.

*"By order of the Ledger Guild and the Office of Sealed Things: all persons harboring knowledge of the Unwritten Name will present themselves for voluntary redaction. Voluntary redaction carries full debt forgiveness. Involuntary redaction carries—"*

He never finished. A cup hit the wall behind him, and then the room remembered it was a room full of frightened people with knives, and the clerk went out the window instead of the door.

Wren and Tam watched from the shadows across the street, and neither of them laughed, because there was nothing in it to laugh at. This was what the tide looked like from underneath. Not armies — armies were just the foam. The tide was this: a clerk reading proclamations to a tavern, a woman nailing lists to doors, a boy selling poisoned water as mercy. Everyone reaching for someone else's memory to hold between themselves and the dark.

"The ferry's burned," Tam murmured. "We go upstream. There's a weir at Coll's Mill — old Garden country. We can cross on the sluice gates if the water's low."

"If it's high?"

"Then we learn to breathe water, and honestly, after everything, it wouldn't surprise me."

It was almost humor. She looked at him sideways, at the bruise and the shaking hands and the way he kept checking the rooftops without seeming to move his head, and she understood that he was terrified, and that he was staying anyway, and that this — not his confession, not his guilt — was the first true thing he'd ever given her.

They crossed the town's edge in single file, hugging the wall of the tannery where the reek covered their scent from dogs, and that was where the bell began to ring.

* * *

Not the town bell. The town bell had cracked years ago; she'd heard its broken voice earlier, a wheeze instead of a peal. This was deeper, older, coming from everywhere and nowhere, rising out of the ground itself, out of the river, out of the bones of the bridge — a sound Wren felt in her back teeth and behind her eyes, a sound like a word being pronounced very slowly by something with a very large mouth.

Every person in Marrow's Ferry stopped moving at once.

The clerk picking himself out of the gutter froze mid-curse. The boy with his cups froze mid-sale. Down by the water, the opportunists' market went silent, forty hucksters and forty buyers standing like figures in a waxwork, and above them all the moon pulsed — actually pulsed, Wren saw it, a slow dilation like a heartbeat seen through skin — and the ring of shadow at its edge crept visibly inward.

"The turn," Tam breathed. "Gods below, it's early. The priest said we had—"

"He said turns don't keep honest time anymore." Wren's own voice sounded far away to her. "He said the god was pulling time toward itself. Like water toward a drain."

The deep bell tolled a second time, and this time Wren heard something inside it — syllables, almost, folded into the sound the way a seed is folded into fruit. Her blood answered. That was the only way she could describe it: a warmth starting under her sternum and spreading outward, and with it a pressure behind her lips, the name rising in her like floodwater rising in a cellar, patient, inevitable, indifferent to her plans.

She clamped her mouth shut. Breathe through the nose. Count the drips. *Everything is measured or it doesn't exist.* For once, the Guild's catechism served her.

Around the frozen square, others were less fortunate. A woman near the well had begun to speak — rapid, involuntary, a stream of syllables pouring out of her that no one had taught her, and Wren felt the fragments in her own blood lean toward the sound like plants toward sun. *Rememberers,* the keeper had called them, back in the last free Garden, with his hands like autumn leaves. *The name has always leaked, child. It leaks into whoever stands close to the wound. That's why the Spire harvests. That's why the Gardens hoard. And that's why, when the turn completes, every rememberer within a hundred miles will become either a vessel or a weapon, depending entirely on who reaches them first.*

The woman by the well stopped as suddenly as she'd started, blinking, wiping her mouth, looking around at the frozen crowd with the expression of someone who has woken on a train and cannot remember boarding.

Then, from the north road, horns. Grey and silver. And from the south, answering, the zealots' song swelling to drown the silence the bell had left behind.

"They felt it too," Tam said. "Both sides. The turn's accelerating — Corvane will march through the night now, and the Garden's maddest will run ahead of their own army to reach the descent." He grabbed her wrist. "Wren. We have maybe two days. Maybe less. The moon isn't waiting for anyone's plan."

* * *

They ran.

Coll's Mill was four miles upstream, and they covered them in the grey half-light with the moon pulsing overhead like a wound that refused to close, and twice the deep bell tolled again, and each time Wren had to stop and press her fist against her mouth and ride out the flood-pressure of the name. The second time, Tam held her shoulders while she shook, and said nothing, and stayed, and she filed that away in the one ledger she was keeping now — the private one, written in nothing, kept nowhere, the ledger of small debts she might actually be able to repay.

At the mill, the sluice gates stood open and the water was low — black and slow over the stones, carrying silt and, once, a dead crow, and once something larger that she chose not to identify. They crossed on the gate-beams, arms out, the whole drowned valley spread below them, and from the height of the crossing Wren looked back the way they'd come and saw the world arranged like a diagram in one of the Guild's old tactical folios.

North: the Spire's columns, torches now, a river of grey fire flowing south through the dark. She could see the sealed wagons, could imagine the ledgers inside — every impossible thing the Guild had ever taken, bottled laughter and grief by the year and, somewhere in the deepest vault, a child's entire childhood, purchased with a mother's signature. Corvane would be with them. Somewhere in that column, a man who had seen cities of ash and built his whole soul into a dam against them, marching to bury a name he didn't know was already broken.

South: the Garden's fires, orange and wild, and the song still rising, and among the banners she could make out the thorn-crown sigil of the zealot orders — not the keeper's gentle sect, not the caretakers of the old stories, but the ones who believed a god was a sword if you held it right.

Between and behind and beside: everyone else, drawn up the roads like iron filings toward a magnet, every grifter and prophet and desperate pilgrim in a starless kingdom, all of them flowing toward a door beneath the Spire that only she knew how to open — and only she knew was already, secretly, quietly, gloriously broken.

"They think it's a key," she said aloud. The wind off the water took the words and shredded them. "Corvane thinks it's a key he can melt down. The zealots think it's a key they can steal. And it's—" She pressed a hand flat against her sternum, against the warmth, the pressure, the ten thousand pieces sleeping in her blood like seeds in winter soil. "It's a handful of grain, Tam. It was always a handful of grain. You can't lock up a handful of grain. You can only plant it or burn it."

Tam stood beside her on the beam, silhouetted against the pulsing moon, and for a moment neither of them moved, and the whole converging world held its breath below.

"Then we'd better get you to the planting," he said.

Far to the north, above the Spire's distant spires, the ring of shadow slid another degree inward, and the deep bell tolled a third time — closer now, or louder, or simply more impatient — and in the space between one toll and the next, Wren felt one small fragment of the name stir against her heart like a sleeper turning over, and whisper, in a voice like her mother's, like the river's, like her own:

*Soon.*
