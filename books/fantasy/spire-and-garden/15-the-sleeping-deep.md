## 15. The Sleeping Deep

**Wren**

The way down began where the ledgers ended.

Wren had always assumed the Spire's archives went on forever — that somewhere below the lowest shelf, below the sealed stacks and the drowned sub-basements where the oldest acquisitions were kept in lead-lined boxes, there was simply more. More paper. More ink. More of everything the Guild had ever taken and called its own.

She had been wrong. The ledgers ended, and beneath them was a door that had no lock because it had never needed one. It was carved from a single slab of black stone, and it was warm to the touch, like the flank of a sleeping animal.

"Nobody guards it," Tam said quietly behind her. His voice echoed strangely down here, flattened, as if the walls were drinking sound. "Why would they? Who would come? Everyone who knows what's below is either dead or lying about it."

The stairwell beyond the door was not built. Wren understood that within the first hundred steps. Built things had corners, had mortar, had the honest geometry of human hands. This was a spiral bored through living rock, smooth as a throat, wide enough for six abreast at the top and narrowing as it descended, so that by the time they had walked an hour she could touch both walls with her elbows. The stone was veined with something pale and slow-moving, and when she pressed her palm against it, the vein pulsed once beneath her fingers — a heartbeat, or something pretending to be one.

She snatched her hand back.

"It knows you're here," Tam said. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at his feet, at the steps worn glassy-smooth by centuries of descent. "It's known since the Garden Gate. Maybe since the Erasure tried to take you. You're carrying a name, Wren. Names are loud, even unspoken. Especially unspoken."

Her hand still tingled. Not unpleasantly. Like standing too close to a hearth.

They descended. There was nothing else to do. Above them, the moon hung three turns short of its thirteenth completion, and every faction in the darkened kingdom was marching toward this city, toward this Spire, toward whatever waited at the bottom of this throat of stone. Corvane's hunters. The Garden zealots with their ash-stained banners. Opportunists with knives and no convictions at all. Wren had seen the roads from the ridge two nights ago: rivers of torchlight all flowing one direction, patient as water finding its level.

Here, though, there were no torches. They didn't need them. Since the third hour of descent, the walls themselves had begun to glow — faintly, reluctantly, the pale veins brightening wherever Wren walked and dimming behind her, so that they moved inside a bubble of light that traveled with her and died at their backs. Tam noticed it before she did.

"You're lighting the way down," he said. "Or it's lighting you."

"I don't want it to."

"I know." A pause. "That might be why it does."

* * *

**Tam**

Tam Vey had grown up on stories of the deep. Every Garden child did. The old keepers told them at night, when the fires burned low: that beneath the Spire lay the First Root, the body of the god who had dreamed the gardens green and the rivers sweet, who had been drugged and bound and flayed of memory by men who wanted its dreams bottled and sold. That the god did not sleep the way people slept. It slept the way a mountain sleeps, the way a season sleeps — massively, unwillingly, waiting.

He had believed the stories the way children believe things: completely and without consequence. Now, descending through the god's own throat, he found belief rearranging itself into something heavier. Something with weight and temperature.

Because the stories hadn't mentioned the smell.

It hit him at the fourth hour. Sweet rot, green and ancient, like a garden left untended for a thousand years — loam and lilies and underneath it something coppery, something like blood left too long in the sun. The Gardens above ground smelled of growth. This smelled of growth interrupted. Of a thing that had been mid-becoming when the binding came, and had been held there ever since, neither dying nor continuing.

And the sounds. He didn't tell Wren about the sounds. Far below them, at the very edge of hearing, there was a rhythm in the stone — not the pulse she'd felt, but slower, vaster, a breathing measured in minutes rather than seconds. Each exhalation moved air up the spiral past them, warm and damp, smelling of that interrupted garden. Each inhalation pulled faintly at their clothes, at their hair, gentle as a tide.

They were walking down a windpipe. Tam kept the thought to himself and put one foot in front of the other.

At the fifth hour, Wren stopped walking so suddenly that he nearly collided with her.

"What is it?"

She was staring at the wall. In the light of her own passage, carved into the stone at eye height, was a line of text. Old Guild script, formal, the kind used only in acquisition ledgers:

*Item 1. One childhood, complete. Seller's mark affixed.*

Below it, a signature. Wren's mother's name, written in a careful, trembling hand.

Tam watched her read it twice. Watched her reach out and touch the letters, and watch the letters dim under her fingers like something ashamed.

"It's everywhere," she said. Her voice was very flat, which he had learned was worse than crying. "The whole wall. Look."

She was right. As far as the glow reached, the stone was inscribed — column after column of acquisitions, thousands of them, tens of thousands, each entry cut into the living rock as if the ledger had been written directly onto the god's skin. *One grief, aged eleven years. One first love, unused. One mother's voice, forty hours. One courage, battlefield-grade.* On and on, spiraling down into the dark, the entire history of the Guild's harvest carved into the flesh of the thing they'd harvested it from.

"They didn't just take from it," Tam said. "They wrote the taking on it. So it would know. Every entry. Every theft. Recorded on its own body, in its own presence, forever."

"Why?" Wren whispered. "What kind of cruelty—"

"Not cruelty." He hated that he knew this. Hated that his years among the Garden's scholars had taught him to think like the people he despised. "Accounting. The old Guildmasters believed a debt unwritten is a debt unforgiven. They wrote everything down so that nothing could ever be forgiven. So the god could never forget what was taken, and never pretend it hadn't been taken willingly — see, the seller's marks, the signatures. Consent, carved in stone. They made the victim co-sign its own robbery."

Wren stood very still. Then she started walking again, faster now, and Tam hurried after her, and the light moved with her down through ten thousand entries, illuminating theft after theft after theft, until he lost count entirely.

* * *

**Wren**

The stair ended without warning.

One step Wren's foot found stone; the next it found nothing, and she pitched forward into a vastness that caught her — not floor, exactly, but a surface that gave like moss, like turf, like the memory of a meadow. She landed on her hands and knees and looked up, and forgot how to breathe.

There was no ceiling. There was no wall. There was no distance she could measure, because the chamber — if it was a chamber — curved away beyond the reach of her light, and her light was failing anyway, the veins in the rock gone dim here, hoarding their brightness. What illumination remained came from the god itself.

It was everywhere. That was the first thing. There was no standing before it, no approaching it, because it was not an object in a room; the room was an object in it. The floor she knelt on rose and fell with its breath. The darkness overhead was the underside of its dreaming. When Wren turned in a slow circle, she saw the shape of it only in fragments: a curve like a shoulder, ridged and pale, taller than the Spire. A slope that might have been a cheek, furred with grey growths like frost or lichen. And everywhere, everywhere, the ledgers — entries carved into every visible surface, dense as bark, the whole sleeping body lettered over with its own inventory.

Half-harvested. She understood the phrase now, the way the dying keeper had used it. Whole regions of the vast body were smooth and dark and unmarked — original, untouched, dreaming still. Other regions were bare in a different way: scraped, scarred, the stone gone thin and grey where memory had been taken and taken and taken until almost nothing remained. The god lay beneath its city like a field half-plowed, and the plow had been writing down its work for a thousand years.

"It's so quiet," Wren said. Her voice came out small and absurd. "Something this size should be screaming."

"It tried that first," Tam said softly, beside her. "For the first few centuries, I think. Then it learned what the Guild learned about everyone else: that noise gets you erased faster. So it went quiet. It's been quiet for eight hundred years." He swallowed. "This is what silence looks like when it's this big. It looks like peace. It isn't."

Wren got to her feet. Her legs shook. She walked — she couldn't say why, except that some current in her blood pulled her forward like a fish on a line — across the breathing turf, toward the nearest stretch of unmarked stone. Up close, the god's skin was warm and faintly damp, and beneath the warmth she could feel movement, glacially slow, like weather happening underground.

She pressed both palms against it.

The dream came into her like water into a cracked vessel.

*A garden. No — gardens, plural, numberless, terraced down hillsides that no longer existed, tended by hands that sang while they worked. She stood in the middle of the dreaming and it was summer everywhere at once, and the god — she could feel it around her, through her, a presence like sunlight made patient — was happy. Not triumphant, not powerful. Happy, in the way of a gardener watching something grow that it had grown. It loved the small things most. The beetles in the compost. The moss on the north walls. The children who came to steal apples and were always allowed to escape.*

Then the dream turned, the way dreams do, and she was in the binding: cold iron driven into warm earth, chains of doctrine, voices reciting entries. *Item. Item. Item.* And the god did not fight, even then. It grieved. It lay down under the ledgers the way a mother lies down under a collapsing roof, curled around the small things, trying to keep the beetles and the moss and the apple-thieving children safe inside its own diminishing dream.

Wren came back to herself on her knees, weeping, her hands still pressed to the warm stone.

"It's not angry," she managed. "Corvane thinks it'll burn the world if it wakes. The zealots think it'll make them kings. It's not — it doesn't want any of that. It wants—" She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "It wants its gardens back. It wants to be allowed to finish growing. That's all. Eight hundred years, and that's all it wants."

Tam crouched beside her. For once he had nothing clever to say. His eyes were wet too, though he'd deny it later.

"And the name?" he asked finally. "Is it here? In it?"

Wren shook her head slowly. She already knew, had known since the Garden Gate, since the Erasure burned backward off her skin. She opened her mouth to say it —

and the god breathed.

Not the long tidal breath it had been taking since they arrived. A short one. Startled. The whole vast chamber shuddered, turf rippling underfoot like water struck by a stone, and far above them, transmitted down through miles of spiral stone, came a sound that did not belong to the deep at all:

Boots. Thousands of them. Doors breaking. The particular thunder of an army finding the entrance it had marched a kingdom to find.

The god's breath caught — and Wren felt it catch, felt the hesitation move through her own chest as if the lungs were shared — and in that hesitation, pressed against its skin, she heard it for the first time. Not words. Older than words. A question, asked of her alone, in the voice of something that had been silent for eight hundred years:

*You brought them. Will you also bring the name?*

The army was coming down.
