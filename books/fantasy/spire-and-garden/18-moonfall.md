## 18. Moonfall

The descent had taken them three days through the god's body, and by the last hour Wren no longer thought of it as a place.

It was a throat. A long, warm dark that swallowed the lamplight whole and gave back only echoes — not of their footsteps but of older things: a lullaby in a language with no living speakers, the creak of a loom, rain on leaves that had not fallen in nine hundred years. The walls were not stone. She had stopped pretending they were. They flexed faintly beneath her palm like the flank of an enormous sleeping animal, and when she pressed her ear to them she could feel a pulse so slow it took a full minute to complete itself, and each beat moved through her own chest and answered there, in the name coiled behind her ribs.

Tam walked half a step ahead, his lantern held low, his face gray with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion. He had not spoken since the zealots' torches had appeared above them, hours ago, and he had turned his back on his own order to stand between Wren and their blades. The cut along his forearm had been bound with strips of his coat. Blood had soaked through twice.

"Tell me again," Wren said, because silence was becoming unbearable, "what happens if I say it."

"You know what happens." His voice came out scraped raw. "The moon finishes its turn. The wards that have kept this city asleep for nine centuries finish theirs. And whatever you speak becomes true."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Corvane's people get here first, and they carve the name out of you syllable by syllable, and they put it in a ledger, and it sleeps another thousand years while they harvest its dreams to sell bottled laughter to merchants." He glanced back at her. In the lantern light his eyes looked very old. "Or the Gardens get here first, and they put it in your mouth whether you want it there or not, and we learn what war looks like when a god fights on one side of it."

Above them — impossibly far above, past the sealed stacks, past the Ledger Hall, past the Spire's crown — the thirteenth moon was rising toward completion. Wren could feel it the way she felt the pulse in the walls: a slow tightening, a wheel coming around to the notch it had been carved to fit. Thirteen turns. The old priest had told her that twelve made a year, and thirteen made an ending.

The passage opened.

* * *

Corvane stood at the edge of the Spire's highest terrace and watched the sky come apart.

He had seen the stars go out weeks ago, and he had told himself it was temporary, a ward-failure, a wound the Guild could stitch closed. But this was different. This was the moon — swollen far past its proper size, hanging low over the city like a lamp about to be snuffed, its light gone the color of old bone. Around its rim, faint as breath on glass, a ring of darkness was closing. The thirteenth turn. Every text he had ever suppressed described it, and every text agreed: when the ring closed, the deep thing under the city would be *nameable*.

Below him, the terraces burned.

Not with fire — with torchlight, thousands upon thousands of torches, carried by the Garden faithful who had come up out of the hills and the flooded districts and the forgotten places where his hunters had never quite managed to plant informants. They filled the great processional avenue from the river gates to the Spire's foot, a river of candle-flame chanting in the old tongue, and above their song he could hear the bells of his own Guild ringing the alarm, and beneath both, felt more than heard, the slow pulse of the god waking in its sleep.

And beyond the Gardens, drawn up in ranks along the boulevards, his own army. Scribes with crossbows. Ledger-masters with warding-ink still wet on their hands. Boys he had personally signed into service three years ago, standing now in formation because he had told them that silence was the only wall between civilization and ash.

*You are doing this,* he thought, watching the two hosts regard one another across four hundred yards of empty avenue. *Every death tonight is yours, purchased years ago, accruing interest.*

He had been twenty-two when he first descended, an idealist with ink-stained fingers, certain that the Guild's work was mercy. Someone had to hold the memories. Someone had to keep the impossible things catalogued and quiet, because the last time the god had been awake, cities had burned in a single night — he had walked through those ashes as a boy, sifting them, finding a child's sandal fused to a paving stone. He had built his entire life on the certainty that no one, ever, should hold that much power unguarded.

And now a debt-scribe named Wren Iversen was somewhere beneath his feet carrying exactly that power in her blood, and two armies were about to kill each other over the privilege of owning her.

"Master Corvane." The Erasure captain stood at his shoulder, her face unreadable, her hunters arrayed behind her in their gray, unremarkable coats. "The descent is breached. The zealots went down ahead of us. We can follow."

"No." Corvane did not take his eyes off the avenue. Below, someone had thrown the first stone. He heard it crack against a shield, and then he heard everything else — the roar, the surge, the terrible sound of ten thousand people deciding at once that the others were the enemy. "Send six down. Only six. Bring me the girl alive, and bring me Tam Vey's head if he resists."

"And the Gardens below?"

"The Gardens below are not my problem tonight." He watched the armies collide, torchlight and steel, and felt the moon's ring tighten another degree, and understood with perfect clarity that every choice he had ever made had led him to this terrace, to this hour, to this arithmetic in which any number of deaths tonight was cheaper than one waking tomorrow. "My problem is up here. Hold the avenue. Buy the girl time."

The captain hesitated. "Time, Master Corvane? Or silence?"

Corvane finally turned to look at her. She was young. They were all young. That had seemed like an asset once.

"Whichever she chooses," he said. "Go."

* * *

The chamber at the bottom of the world was not a chamber. It was a hollow the size of a cathedral, ribbed with arches of bone-pale substance that might have been grown or might have been dreamed, and in its center, filling most of it, lay the god.

Wren had imagined — she realized now — something monstrous. Coils, teeth, an eye like a lake. What she saw instead was sorrow given scale. The god lay curled around its own emptiness, vast and dim, and great sections of it were simply *missing*, scooped away in clean geometric planes where the harvesters had cut, the wounds edged with scar-tissue that glittered faintly like mica. Where its flesh remained, it rose and fell with that minute-long breath. Its face — and it had a face, worn smooth and gentle as a river stone — was turned toward the ceiling of its hollow, toward the city, toward the moon it could not see.

"It's been dreaming us," Tam said quietly. "All this time. Everything it couldn't remember, it dreamed instead. The rivers. The gardens. Us."

Wren stepped closer. The air near the god tasted of rain and cut grass and her mother's kitchen, all at once, all impossible. The name behind her ribs had begun to warm.

That was when the torches came down the passage behind them.

There were perhaps thirty of the Garden zealots, and their leader was a woman Wren recognized from the last free Garden — Sister Haleth, who had knelt at the dying keeper's feet, whose devotion had frightened Wren even then, because it had no floor to it. Her eyes found Wren across the hollow and lit like lamps.

"There," Haleth breathed. "The vessel. The thirteenth turn closes within the hour — bind her hands if she resists, but do not mark her. The name must leave her lips unbroken."

"She isn't a vessel," Tam said, stepping forward, and Wren heard in his voice the sound of a man finishing a break that had been running through him for days. "She's a person. She decides."

Haleth's expression did not change. "Brother Vey. You were sent to bring her to us. You have done half your work."

"I was sent to *use* her." Tam's hand went to his knife. "I've resigned."

What happened next happened fast. Six zealots moved; Tam met the first two; Wren stumbled back against the god's flank, which was warm as a living cheek, and felt the name surge up her throat like floodwater — and then the passage behind the zealots filled with gray coats, and the Erasure came down into the dark with their memory-blades drawn, and the hollow became a battlefield.

Wren pressed herself into the crook of the god's shoulder and watched people fight over her soul. Blades rang against warding-charms. A zealot screamed and forgot his own name mid-shout, collapsing into a confused child. An Erasure hunter took a spear through the chest and died mouthing the alphabet, holding onto letters the way drowning men hold onto rope. And through it all the god breathed, and breathed, and its missing places glittered, and Wren understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that none of these people wanted what she wanted.

Corvane wanted silence. Haleth wanted a weapon. The Guild wanted inventory. The Gardens wanted victory. Everyone wanted to own the name, and no one — not one soul in this hollow or on the burning avenue above — had asked the god what *it* wanted.

She put her hand flat against its scarred flank.

*What do you want?*

The answer did not come in words. It came the way her recovered memories had come, whole and sensory and unbearably intimate: green light through leaves. Hands in soil. The feeling of being *many* — of being a hillside, a watershed, ten thousand small attentions, none of them sovereign, all of them free. It wanted, she understood, to be broken. It had been trying to be broken for nine hundred years, and everyone who found it had tried to make it whole instead, because wholeness could be owned and fragments could not.

"Oh," Wren said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word.

Above them, muffled through nine hundred feet of dreaming flesh, the armies clashed. Above them, the moon's ring narrowed to a thread. The name rose in her throat, all of it, the whole unspeakable architecture of it, hot as swallowed starlight, and she felt the thirteenth turn complete itself like a key turning in the lock of the world —

— and the god's eye, the one intact eye, opened and looked at her.

Not with command. With *hope*. Terrible, patient, nine-hundred-years hope, the hope of something that had been waiting to be set down.

Wren opened her mouth.

* * *

On the terrace, Corvane felt the moment arrive before any messenger could bring it: a pressure in the ears, a taste of copper, every warding-ink sigil in the Spire flaring white and then going dark. The ring closed. Somewhere below, a girl was speaking or not speaking, and the fate of everything he had spent forty years protecting hung on the width of a syllable.

He gripped the balustrade. Below, the battle on the avenue had stalled — both armies frozen, looking up, as the dead moon began, very slowly, to shine.

* * *

In the hollow, the name reached Wren's lips.

Tam broke free of the melee, blood streaming down his arm, and saw her standing against the god's flank with her mouth open and her eyes full of tears, and he understood — the way you understand lightning just before it strikes — that whatever she did in the next three seconds would remake the world, and that nothing he could shout would reach her in time.

"Wren!" he screamed anyway.

Her lips began to move.
