## 21. The Unwritten Page

The city did not know her anymore.

Wren felt it first as a smallness, a thinning of the air where her name should have been. She stood in the queue outside the Guild's debt office — the last time she would ever stand in that line — and when the clerk looked up and asked for her name, she said *Wren Iversen*, and the clerk's quill hovered over the ledger and found nothing.

"No record," the clerk said, not unkindly. Clerks were never unkind; they simply had no room in their ledgers for kindness either. "No record under Iversen. No debt. No entry. Are you certain of the spelling?"

"I'm certain," Wren said.

"Then you don't exist." The clerk said it the way one says *the rain has stopped* or *the bread is fresh* — a fact of no particular weight. "Next."

She stepped aside and let the queue flow past her like water around a stone, and she stood there in the gray morning light while clerks behind clerks bent over their books, and understood what Corvane had done, or what the shattering had done, or perhaps what both of them together had done between them: every page that had ever held her name was blank now. Her debts were gone because her debts had been written down, and nothing written about Wren Iversen had survived the night the moon completed its thirteenth turn. She was free the way a word is free after it is scraped from vellum — still shaped, still hers, but no longer legible to anyone else.

It should have frightened her. For eleven years the ledgers had been the walls of her life, the architecture of every day: rise, ink, catalog, sleep, owe. She had hated those walls and leaned against them all the same. Now they were gone and she felt the wind on all sides at once.

But underneath the vertigo, quieter than the vertigo, was something else. Something that felt almost like the moment after a fever breaks.

She walked out through the Spire district without looking back. The great tower rose above her, its windows dark, its wards still humming faintly with the residue of ten thousand syllables that had passed through it and scattered like seed on wind. People moved through the streets below it carrying their fragments without knowing what they carried — a baker who hummed a note of the god's patience while he kneaded; a child who held, in the back of her mind, a single syllable of its sorrow and cried sometimes at dusk for reasons no one could name. The whole city was quietly haunted now, gently, the way a garden is haunted by everything planted in it.

Wren touched her own chest, where the last and largest fragment had settled after she spoke — the silence at the center of the name, the part that meant *I am here, I am waiting, I am not angry*. It was warm, the way a stone is warm long after the sun has left it.

Then she turned west, toward the Garden district, toward the grave she had been walking toward in her mind since the night the stars went out.

* * *

Her mother's grave was not where the records said it was, because her mother had never been in any records worth trusting. Wren knew this only now, standing at the rusted gate of the paupers' yard behind the tannery, reading the ground itself the way she had once read ledgers — by what was absent.

There were no stones. There never were, in this yard. The dead of the debt-districts went into the earth marked by whatever the living could spare: a wooden stake, a strip of cloth, a handful of river pebbles arranged in a pattern only the family would recognize. Wren's mother had been buried by no family at all, if the old priest had told the truth — and Wren had learned, in these last terrible weeks, that the old priest told the truth more often than the ledgers ever had.

But the priest had also given her the pattern. *Three pebbles and a sprig of ash*, he'd said, *because she asked me to remember it that way, and I am the only ledger left that keeps such things.* He had died two days later, gently, in his chair, with the moon full and the god stirring beneath them all, and Wren had thought: *now there is a man who was erased the honest way.*

She counted rows by the tannery wall. Fourth row, seventh plot, near the drainpipe where the water sang softly even in drought. And there — half-swallowed by moss, tilted like a drunkard — three river pebbles, gray and patient, and beside them the dry skeleton of an ash sprig that someone, sometime, had pressed into the dirt.

Wren knelt.

The ground was cold through her skirts. The smell of the tannery hung over everything, sharp and animal, and beneath it the green smell of moss and the mineral smell of wet stone, and beneath all of it, so faint she might have imagined it, something older — the deep, slow breath of the earth itself, which she knew now was not empty. Nothing was empty. Everything remembered.

"I brought ink," she said aloud, and heard how foolish it sounded, and said it anyway. "I don't know why. Habit."

She set the little pot on the grass beside the pebbles. Black ink, ground herself, from soot and river water and a drop of gum arabic bought with coins that belonged to no ledger anywhere. Eleven years she had written other people's acquisitions. Bottled laughter. A year of grief. The color of a dead man's last sunset. She had cataloged the impossible until the impossible seemed ordinary, and all the while the most impossible thing in the Spire had been sleeping in her own blood, sold before she could walk, signed away in a hand she had spent her childhood learning to imitate.

Her mother's hand.

"I used to practice your signature," Wren said. The words came out low and steady, which surprised her; she had expected weeping, had braced for it the way you brace for cold water. "When I was small. Before. I didn't know whose it was. You used to write lists — market lists, mending lists — and I'd copy them on scraps and hide them under my bed. I thought your letters were beautiful. I thought everyone's mother wrote like a scribe."

A crow landed on the tannery wall and regarded her with one flat eye.

"And then you sold me." Not accusation. Just the shape of the thing, spoken into the air so it could stop living inside her. "You sold my childhood to the Guild. My memories. The first entry in their whole impossible catalogue, and it was me — it was us, it was the kitchen and the song you sang and the way you held my hand crossing the bridge. They took it and put it in a bottle and filed it, and you took the money and bought—" she stopped. Swallowed. "Bought protection. Bought time. Bought whatever the Guild promised you for a daughter's years, and I have spent six weeks wanting to hate you for it, and I have discovered that I cannot."

The crow shifted its feet. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city went on — cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, the ordinary music of the unremarkable morning.

"Because I know now what you knew," Wren said. "I know what was coming for us. I've seen the Erasure work. I've felt them reach into my head and pull, and I know that the only thing worse than selling a memory is watching it be taken. You didn't sell me, Mother. You saved what you could of me the only way the world allowed. You paid the Spire with the past so that I would have a future. And the future turned out to be a cell and a debt and a quill, and I'm sorry — I'm sorry that's what your sacrifice bought. But it also bought this. This morning. This grave. This choice I got to make, at the end, with my own mouth and my own name. You gave me the years I needed to become someone who could choose."

Her voice finally broke then, on *choose*, and she let it. She knelt in the cold grass and wept the way she had not wept in the flooded undercity, not in the dying Garden, not even in the deep place beneath the Spire when she had laid her palm against the vast sorrowful flank of the sleeping god. That had been awe. This was only grief, plain and human-sized, and it was hers, unwritten, belonging to no catalogue.

She wept until the crow grew bored and flapped away, and until the sun climbed high enough to warm the back of her neck, and then she wiped her face with her sleeve and did the thing she had come to do.

* * *

She had thought about it for three days, walking out of the city and back in again, sleeping in doorways and haylofts, invisible and unrecorded and freer than she had ever been. Three days of turning the question over: *what do you give a mother who cannot receive it?*

Not forgiveness — she had already forgiven her, somewhere on the road, without deciding to, the way a wound closes without asking permission. Forgiveness was done. What remained was something else. A return. A restitution owed in the only direction that mattered.

She uncorked the ink pot.

Then she reached into the satchel Tam had given her before they parted at the city gate — *go find your mother's grave, I'll find the others, we are not finished, you and I* — and drew out the blank book. Good paper, sewn binding, no guild stamp, no watermark, no history. A book that had never been anyone's ledger.

And beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the page she had taken from the sealed stacks on the night everything changed. The first acquisition. Entry one. *One childhood, female, aged four years, complete. Seller's mark affixed.* And below it, in faded brown ink, the signature she had practiced as a child without knowing why: *Maren Iversen.*

She had stolen it from the archive during the panic, when hunters ran the halls and ledgers burned in the courtyards, and she had carried it through every mile since — through the undercity, the Gardens, the descent, the shattering — telling herself she would destroy it when the war was won. Burn the contract. Unmake the sale.

Now she knelt at the grave and understood that burning it would be another kind of erasure, and she had sworn, on the god's warm silence in her chest, that she was finished with erasure. Erasure was the Spire's whole religion. She would not convert to it in grief.

Instead she dipped her pen.

She wrote across the bottom of the contract, beneath her mother's signature, in her own careful scribe's hand — the hand trained by eleven years of servitude, the hand that had copied her mother's letters under the blankets as a girl:

*Paid in full. Received: one life, returned to its owner. Witnessed by the earth, the ash tree, and the god who remembers everything and holds nothing.*

Below that she wrote the date — not the Guild's date, not the year of any reign, but the count of turns since the moon had finished its thirteenth circle. First turn of the new reckoning. Let the calendars start here, she thought. Let this be year one of the world after ledgers.

Then she folded the contract along its old creases, dug a small hollow in the soft earth beside the pebbles with her bare hands, and buried it. Contract, pen-stroke, witness-line — all of it returned to the ground that held her mother, so that the sale and its ending lay together, the way a scar lies over healed flesh.

"There," she whispered. "It's done. You're free too."

The moss on the pebbles glistened where her tears had fallen. The drainpipe sang its small water-song. And beneath her knees, beneath the yard, beneath the tannery and the street and the foundations of the whole forgetful city, Wren felt — distinctly, unmistakably, the way you feel a sleeper turn over in the next room — something vast shift its weight and settle, comfortable, unhurried, *content*.

Ten thousand fragments, dreaming in ten thousand skulls, and not one of them owned.

* * *

She stayed until the light went long and gold, talking to the grave about small things — the taste of the bread in the western villages, the way Tam argued with his own reflection, the school she was beginning to imagine and had not yet dared say aloud to anyone. When the shadows of the tannery wall reached the fourth row, she stood, brushed the dirt from her skirts, and pressed one last gift into the earth: the ash sprig's dry stem, snapped cleanly, replaced with a green cutting she had carried from the last free Garden, roots wrapped in wet cloth. It would live or it wouldn't. Gardens, she had learned, were mostly faith.

At the gate she paused and looked back once. Three pebbles, a young ash, a patch of turned earth. No stone, no name, no record. An unwritten page in the world's great book.

It was enough. It was, she realized with a quiet shock, more than enough — it was the only kind of memorial she trusted anymore.

The streets were lighting their lamps as she walked east. In a doorway near the tanner's row, a figure detached itself from the shadow and fell into step beside her, and she did not startle, because she had smelled the river-mud and cheap tobacco half a street ago.

"You buried it," Tam Vey said. Not a question. He had a new scar along his jaw and the same infuriating half-smile underneath it.

"I buried it."

"The zealots are asking for you. The ones you didn't break. They want the fragments gathered, organized, *kept*." He spat the word like a pit. "They're calling it stewardship."

"They would."

"And Corvane resigned this morning. Walked into the Guild hall, set his seal on the table, and walked out. Nobody knows where he went." Tam glanced sideways at her. "Some people are saying a woman with no records talked him into it."

"Some people should mind their ledgers," Wren said, "since they're all blank anyway."

They walked together through the lamplight, two unrecorded people in a city full of sleeping gods, and ahead of them, in a narrow street off the Garden district, a building waited with a broken sign and good bones and enough rooms for desks, and ink pots, and children — children who would learn letters the way her mother had written them, beautiful and free, on pages that belonged to no one but themselves.

Wren had a blank book in her satchel, and for the first time in her life, every page of it was hers to fill.
