## 22. Garden, Growing

### Wren

The house at the edge of the flooded quarter had been a counting-house once, back when the river still ran where it was supposed to. Its vaults were dry now, its iron doors rusted open like mouths that had forgotten how to shut, and the great ledger-room on the ground floor had windows — actual windows, tall and cracked and full of morning — which no room in the Spire had ever possessed.

Wren stood in the doorway with a broom in her hands and watched the light come through the glass in long amber bars, laying itself across the floor like something patient.

"You're sweeping dust into patterns again," Tam said from somewhere behind her.

"I'm deciding where things go."

"You're sweeping dust into patterns."

She smiled and didn't argue, because he was right. For three weeks they had done nothing but clean, and for three weeks she had found herself arranging the debris of the old world into shapes: drifts of plaster raked into spirals, broken ledger-boards stacked like garden beds. Tam teased her about it. He did not know — or perhaps he knew too well to say — that she was not decorating. She was planting. Every corner she cleared felt less like an emptying and more like a tilling, as if the room remembered what it wanted to grow.

Outside, the city was learning to live without stars. That was the phrase people used now, *without stars*, spoken the way one spoke of weather, or of a hard winter past. The sky had never refilled. Whatever the god had given back when Wren shattered the name had not included the lights it had borrowed to dream by, and so the nights remained black and close and strangely intimate, lit instead by ten thousand small lamps that people set in their windows without quite knowing why. The lamplighters' guild had tripled. Children who had never seen a constellation invented their own out of chimney smoke and candle-glow, and named them after bakers and dogs and dead grandmothers, which Wren thought was better than anything the old sky had managed.

And beneath all of it, distributed through the city like water through soil, the fragments slept and woke and slept again.

She felt hers sometimes. A single syllable of a name older than the kingdom, lodged behind her sternum like a seed behind a shell. It did not speak. It did not command. On certain evenings — when the lamps were lit, when someone laughed in the street below — it warmed, faintly, the way a stone warms that has sat all day in sun. Once, in the market, a woman dropped a basket of plums and swore with remarkable creativity, and Wren's fragment hummed so hard her teeth tingled, and she had to stand very still among the rolling fruit until it settled. Joy, she had decided. It liked joy. Ten thousand syllables scattered through ten thousand ordinary lives, and every one of them apparently partial to laughter and ripe fruit and nothing else anyone could determine.

No army could collect them. No ledger could hold them. She had made sure of that with the last true act of her old life, and some mornings she still woke expecting Corvane's hunters at the door, expecting the price.

Most mornings, though, she woke to the smell of bread, because Tam baked now, badly, at hours no reasonable person would call morning, and the whole house filled with the smell of almost-burnt grain and woodsmoke, and that was the world now. That was what she had bought with everything she'd spent.

"Wren." Tam's voice came from the front room, careful in the way he got careful when strangers arrived. "We have visitors."

"How many?"

"Seven. Maybe eight. One of them is a child."

Wren set down the broom. She took a breath, the way she'd learned to take breaths before doors opened — not bracing anymore, just ready — and went to see who the dark had sent them.

### Tam

They came up from the undercity paths, seven adults and a girl of maybe nine, mud to their knees and wariness in their shoulders, the particular posture of people who had learned that every gift had a hook in it. Tam recognized the posture because he had worn it for most of his life. He recognized other things too: the woman at the front had Garden tattoos gone grey and blurred with age, the vine-and-root sigil inked in the old style, before his order turned its care into hunger. She looked at him and her mouth went flat.

"The courier," she said. "The one who was going to use the girl."

"That was me."

"And now?"

"Now I bake bread and sweep floors and get shouted at by children." He held her gaze. "You've heard what happened at the Spire. You've heard what she did. If you've come to take something, you should know there's nothing here to take. That was rather the point."

The woman studied him for a long moment. Then, slowly, she unslung the bundle from her back and unwrapped it on the counting-house steps, and Tam's breath caught, because he knew what he was looking at even before he understood it: pages. Dozens of them. Ledger leaves, Guild stock, the heavy cream paper with its faint watermark of the tower — but written on in a hundred different hands, cramped and looping and childish, each page holding a memory pressed into ink the way flowers are pressed into books.

"We started keeping our own records," the woman said. "After. Not acquisitions." She said the word like she was spitting out a pit. "Tending. My grandmother kept the last free Garden until it burned. She used to say a memory isn't a thing you own. It's a thing you keep alive, like a cutting, like a graft. You give it away and it grows." Her voice roughened. "I have my mother's laugh in here. Someone bottled it forty years ago and I paid a season's wages to get the page back, and I don't even remember her face, but I can read this and hear her laughing and—" She stopped. Steadied. "We heard there was a school."

"There's a school," Tam said.

"There's a school," said Wren, from the doorway, "that hasn't decided yet whether it's a school or a garden or a disaster. Come in. All of you. Especially the child — she looks like she hasn't eaten since the moon went strange, and we have bread that is only slightly burnt."

The girl peered around the tallest adult's legs. "Is it true," she asked, in the piercing way of children everywhere, "that you broke the name? That you're the one who—"

"I'm the one who swept a lot of floors," Wren said. "Yes."

Tam watched the strangers cross his threshold, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the descent, since the zealots, since the moment he'd confessed what he was sent to do — something in him loosened, one turn of a key. He had betrayed her once, in the small cowardly way of a man following orders he hated. She had let him come back anyway. He intended to spend whatever years he had proving the arithmetic of that mercy sound.

He went to check the bread. It was, as predicted, burnt on one side.

### Wren

The school grew the way gardens grow, which is to say unevenly, and mostly in the dark before anyone saw it coming.

At first it was only the seven from the undercity, then their cousins, then a lamplighter who brought a fragment-page his father had left him and asked, red-faced and desperate, if someone could teach him to read it. Then a former Ledger clerk — an actual clerk, guild-trained, who flinched whenever Tam moved too fast — arriving with a satchel of acquisition records she had rescued from the sealed stacks before Corvane's successors could burn them. *Rescued*, she said, though Wren suspected *stolen*, and found she approved entirely.

They called the students rememberers, at first, and then simply gardeners, because the name stuck the way names do, because the old keeper's words had traveled farther than the old keeper ever had: *a memory tended is a memory alive; a memory owned is a memory dying in a jar.*

Wren taught the reading of pages. It was harder than it sounded. A harvested memory in ink was not a story; it was a pressure, a shape of feeling folded flat, and to open it you had to do more than decipher the words. You had to offer yourself as soil. The first time she demonstrated, pressing her palm flat against a stranger's page — a fisherman's memory of his daughter's first steps, acquired by the Guild in settlement of a debt — the whole class went silent as the man's voice rose out of the paper, thin and astonished, saying *look at her go, look at her go*, and half the room wept, including, to her considerable irritation, Wren herself.

"That's the lesson," she told them, wiping her eyes with her sleeve while thirty students pretended not to notice. "You don't take a memory. You receive it. And when you've received it, you copy it out in your own hand, and you give your copy away, and the person who takes yours gives theirs away, and so on. No one holds more than one. No one holds it alone. That's the whole law. Everything else is technique."

"And if someone breaks the law?" asked the child, whose name was Sefi and who missed nothing.

Wren thought of Corvane, of ash, of a lifetime spent gripping silence so tightly it cut his hands. She thought of the zealots in the deep places, reaching for a god the way misers reach for coin.

"Then they're lonely," she said. "That's the punishment. They end up the only person in the world holding their piece, and pieces are too small to matter alone. We don't punish. We just let the loneliness do the work."

Sefi considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My fragment hums when I eat honey," she announced. "Is that normal?"

"It hums when *anyone* nearby eats honey," said her aunt. "It's a menace."

"It likes sweetness," Wren said, and caught Tam smiling at her across the room, and did not smile back fast enough to hide that she was smiling too.

### Tam

In the fourth month, the Spire sent a letter.

Tam found it wedged in the door at dawn, sealed with grey wax — not the Guild's tower sigil but a plain blank seal, which somehow frightened him more. He carried it to Wren where she sat in the old vault-room, which they had cleared of everything except a table, two chairs, and light from the high grating, and they opened it together the way you open a letter you already half-know.

It was from Corvane.

Not Guildmaster Corvane; the letter was signed with a name and no title, and the handwriting had changed, looser than the copperplate Wren remembered from her debt-notices, as if a hand had set down a weight it had carried for decades. It was short. Tam read it twice over her shoulder.

*I have resigned. They offered me the archives and I declined; I have seen what keeping does to a man. I write to ask one question, and you may refuse to answer it. In the deep place, before the end, you told me the god was not sleeping but dreaming us — that we were what it dreamed when it dreamed gently. I have thought of nothing else since. Was that true, or was it a kindness meant for a condemned man?*

*Either answer will do. I am learning to plant things. I am poor at it.*

*— C.*

Wren read it three times. Then she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright in the vault-room — and Tam realized he hadn't heard it enough, not ever, not in all the starless miles between the Spire and the sea.

"He wants to know if I lied to save his feelings," she said. "Corvane. The man who erased a thousand memories to keep the peace."

"What will you tell him?"

She was quiet a moment. Outside, faintly, came the sounds of the evening lesson: Sefi's voice leading the younger ones through a copying exercise, the scratch of pens, someone's fragment humming low and contented as a hive.

"I'll tell him the truth," she said. "Which is that I don't know. Which is that nobody knows, and that's the point — that's what he never understood and the zealots never understood. A name split ten thousand ways can't be answered, only lived with. The god isn't awake and isn't asleep. It's *among*. Like weather. Like bread." She pulled a pen toward her. "And then I'll tell him his beans are probably failing because he's planting them too deep, because of course he is, the man erases memories and buries seeds, it's the same gesture—"

Tam left her writing, because some letters deserved privacy, and went to pull the evening loaves. But at the door he paused.

"Wren. The question underneath his question. Are we — is this —" He gestured vaguely at the house, the school, the whole improbable warm edifice of it. "Is this what you wanted? After everything it cost?"

She looked at him for a long time. The lamplight through the grating laid its bars across her face, gold instead of iron.

"When I was a child," she said, "someone sold my years, and I grew up thinking a life was a thing that happened to a ledger. Now there are thirty people upstairs learning to hand each other their hearts in ink, and a burnt loaf, and a letter from my worst enemy asking how to garden." She shrugged, and her eyes were wet and shining. "I don't know if it's what I wanted. I know it's what I planted. Come taste the bread."

### Wren

On the last night of autumn, when the year's first copies had been given away in their dozens and the walls of the counting-house were hung with drying ink-pages like herbs, Wren climbed to the attic with a lamp, a pen, and a single blank book.

It was Guild stock — good cream paper, tower watermark — salvaged from the clerk's satchel, and she had saved it for months without letting herself know why. Now she sat on the floorboards with her back against the cold chimney and opened it to the first page, and for a long while wrote nothing at all.

A ledger of her own. Not acquisitions. Not debts. One book, hers, in which nothing would be owned.

She began the way she had begun ten thousand entries in the Spire, with date and hand and provenance, and then she broke the form deliberately, lovingly, the way you break ground.

*Entry the first. Memory of: my mother, singing in a kitchen I no longer remember, in a house I paid for with years I cannot recover. Source: myself, what remains. Provenance: stolen, sold, survived. Disposition: to be copied out and given away, and given away, and given away, until no one remembers whose mother it was, only that somewhere a woman sang, and it was good.*

Her pen hovered. Then, beneath it:

*Entry the second. Memory of: a courier named Tam Vey, coming back when he had every reason not to. Source: witnessed firsthand. Disposition: kept. Some things may be both shared and kept. I have decided this is allowed. I have decided a great many things are allowed.*

She wrote until the lamp burned low. She wrote down the fisherman and his daughter's first steps. The plum-woman's swearing. Sefi and the honey. The keeper of the last Garden, dying, saying *tend it, don't own it.* She wrote Corvane's beans. She wrote the black sky and the ten thousand lamps and the children naming constellations after dogs. Page by page the book filled with small mortal brightnesses, none of them hers alone, all of them hers to pass on.

When she finished, she blew out the lamp and sat in the dark a moment, and behind her sternum her fragment of the god's name gave one slow, warm pulse — approval, or greeting, or simply the contentment of a seed in soil — and settled.

Below her, muffled through the floors, she heard Tam moving in the kitchen, setting the dough for tomorrow, humming something tuneless and half-remembered. From the street, a lamplighter's bell. From the rooms where the students slept, the soft collective breathing of people who had learned to carry pieces of something vast and had discovered the carrying weighed almost nothing, shared.

Wren closed the ledger. Tomorrow she would copy the first entry out in a fair hand and give it to whoever came through the door, and they would copy it, and give it away, and somewhere down the long chain of hands a woman would sing in a kitchen none of them could see, forever, belonging to everyone.

She rose, stiff-kneed, smiling in the dark, and went downstairs toward the smell of tomorrow's bread — and stopped on the third step, because through the window, above the rooftops where no stars had shone for a year and a day, one light had appeared.

Small. White. Unmistakable.

A star.

It hung there alone in the black, trembling, like the first green shoot through ash — and far below, all through the sleeping city, ten thousand fragments stirred at once, humming, like seeds remembering spring.
