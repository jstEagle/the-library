## 1. The Date That Wasn't His

The rain in Vell fell the way it always did in the month of Ashfall — sideways, gray, tasting faintly of the tanneries downstream. Ansel Okonkwo had learned to love it, or at least to rely on it. Rain meant hoods up, collars high, faces turned inward against the wet. Rain meant that a man could stand three feet from a magistrate in a crowded colonnade and lift the contents of the man's head without anyone noticing the small silver needle of his touch.

Tonight's mark was Magistrate Corvane Dull, a soft-bellied functionary with a taste for illegal brandy and a memory like an unlocked drawer. Ansel had cased him for six days: the evening walks, the bodyguards who peeled off at the winehouse, the route home along the Lantern Canal where the lamps doubled themselves in black water and no one watched anyone else.

He pressed close behind Dull beneath the colonnade arch, two fingers finding the warm skin below the magistrate's ear, and whispered the thief's litany under his breath — *what is yours I borrow, what I borrow I keep* — and felt the memory come loose like a ripe fruit from its stem.

It was good stock. He could feel it through the needle even before he decanted it into the wax vial at his wrist: ledgers, bribes, a judge's name spoken in a bathhouse steam. Enough to sell twice over to two different buyers who would never compare purchases. A clean night's work. He was already composing the lie he'd tell Sefa about how easy it had been when the magistrate stumbled, caught himself on the colonnade rail, and laughed — a strange, hiccuping laugh — and said, to no one, to the rain:

"Strange. I feel as if I've forgotten something important."

They always said something like that. Ansel had heard a hundred variations and never once flinched at them. But tonight the words landed wrong, the way a stair lands wrong in the dark, and he felt the vertigo before he understood it.

Then Dull straightened, blinked rain from his eyes, and walked on whistling, and Ansel told himself he was tired, that was all, that he'd been working too many nights.

* * *

He should have gone home. Home was a rented room above a chandler's shop in the Tallow Quarter, with a cot, a kettle, and forty-one stolen memories sealed in wax and hidden under the floorboards like a dragon's hoard of other people's lives. He should have gone home, slept, sold the magistrate's secrets in the morning.

Instead he took the long way, through the Scriptorium district, because one of the memories he carried — not tonight's, an older one, bought cheap from a dying bookbinder two winters past — kept tugging at him. The bookbinder's memory held a room: shelves of forbidden texts, and among them a slim volume bound in gray leather, unlettered, which the binder had been paid to hide inside a false cover and never speak of again. The binder had never known what was in it. But Ansel knew the address, and he knew the binder was dead now, and dead men's hiding places belong to whoever finds them first.

The shop was still there, shuttered, its sign reading FINE BINDING & GILDING in peeling gilt. The lock surrendered to his picks in under a minute. Inside, dust and the ghost-smell of glue. He found the false spine on the third shelf, exactly where a dead man's memory said it would be, and slid the gray book free.

It was lighter than it looked. Cold, too, in a way paper shouldn't be — cold like river stones, like the underside of a bridge where something waits. His hands wanted to put it down. He didn't.

He struck a match and opened it by that small burning light.

The pages were dense with a script he half-recognized — old Vellish, the ceremonial hand they used on courthouse lintels — and interleaved with diagrams: a tower drawn as a vertical wound, a gate drawn as a broken circle, seven small symbols arranged in an arc. Prophecy, then. The real kind, the outlawed kind, inked a century ago by someone who'd paid for it with more than money.

He turned pages quickly, skimming, a thief assessing value. *...the Hollow Covenant shall lie sundered until the tokens are gathered... the heir shall come unnamed, wearing another's face...*

And then the match burned down toward his fingers, and he turned one page too far, and stopped.

There was a date written there. Not a year. A day and a month, precise as a debt ledger entry, in ink so fresh-looking it might have been set down that afternoon:

**17th of Ashfall.**

Tonight.

His breath made the flame gutter. Below the date, in the same patient hand, a single line:

*The heir returns to himself. Ansel Okonkwo, son of the Covenant, claimant of the Hollow Gate.*

He read his own name four times. The match died. In the dark, the words stayed printed on the inside of his eyes, glowing faintly, like a memory he'd swallowed.

Someone had written this a hundred years ago. Someone had sat with pen and prophecy and written *Ansel Okonkwo* the way you write a thing already finished — and beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, a phrase that made his scalp tighten: *he will have taken it from himself, and must take it back.*

* * *

He ran.

Not immediately — first there were three full seconds in which he stood perfectly still in a dead bookbinder's shop, listening to the rain and to his own heart, doing the arithmetic of impossibility. Then somewhere out in the Scriptorium district a whistle blew, short and sharp, and was answered by another, and a third, triangulating, and Ansel's body understood before his mind did that the sound was for him.

Inquisitors. The Church of the Unwritten Word kept them lean and fast and utterly without humor, and they did not blow whistles for pickpockets.

He went out through the back, over a wall slick with moss, dropping into the alley behind the chandlery row. Rain hammered the tin roofs into a white noise that swallowed footsteps — his and everyone else's. He ran the way he'd learned to run in this city: low, fast, changing rhythm, using the crowds of late drinkers as moving cover, trusting the map of Vell that lived in his feet rather than his head.

Left at the Broken Saint. Down the weaver's stair. Through the covered market where the tarps drummed like war shields overhead. Behind him, once, he glimpsed them: two figures in oilcloth gray, moving with terrible economy, not running so much as *arriving*, each place he'd been a moment ago being the place they were a moment later. They weren't following his trail. They were following the book. He could feel it against his chest where he'd stuffed it inside his coat, cold as a stolen organ, beating some slow private pulse.

*They knew.* That was the thought that kept arriving and refusing to be processed, circling back like a gull over a kill. They knew tonight. They knew the date. A hundred years before he was born, someone had written down that on the 17th of Ashfall, Ansel Okonkwo would walk into a dead man's shop and open a dead man's book — and the inquisitors had read the same page and marked their calendars accordingly.

Which meant the safe, small life he had built — the rented room, the careful thefts, the forty-one vials under the floorboards, the anonymity he'd polished like armor for seven years — had never been safe at all. It had been a stage set, and somewhere a playwright had written his exit long before he learned his lines.

He cut down an alley between the tannery and the canal, boots skidding in runoff, and pressed himself into the recessed doorway of a shuttered dye-house. Breathed. Listened. The whistles had fallen behind, or gone quiet, or gone clever.

In the doorway's dark, with rain sheeting past a hand's breadth from his face, he finally let himself look at the thing he'd been refusing to look at since the match went out.

*He will have taken it from himself, and must take it back.*

Seven years ago — he checked the shape of the memory and found it smooth, featureless, like a stone worn blank by water — seven years ago something had happened to him. He knew this the way amputees know weather. There was a gap in him shaped like a life. He had woken at nineteen with skills and no history, a thief's instincts and no teacher he could remember, and he had assumed grief, assumed trauma, assumed the ordinary cruelty of the world. He had built himself on top of the gap and called the building a life.

But the book said the gap wasn't damage. It said the gap was *architecture*. That he had done it deliberately. That whatever he'd erased — whatever inheritance, whatever covenant, whatever hollow gate — he had chosen oblivion over bearing it, and then hidden the choice inside itself, a lock whose key was also locked away.

And tonight, of all nights, the date had come due.

A lantern swung into the far end of the alley. Ansel flattened deeper into the doorway. Two inquisitors passed, close enough that he could hear the wet click of their boot buckles, and one of them spoke quietly to the other, and the words drifted across the rain to him like a blade slipped under a door:

"—not just the book. The Okonkwo man. Malvane wants him breathing."

Malvane.

The name went through him colder than the rain. Everyone in Vell knew that name the way you know a storm front — High Chancellor Malvane, who had never lost an election because no one dared run, whose armies were massing in the east beyond the free cities' borders, whose inquisitors burned prophecy-books by the cartload. He had never wondered why she hated prophecy so much. Now, shivering in a dye-house doorway with a century-old book against his ribs, he began to wonder.

She wanted him breathing. Which meant that to her, he was not yet a threat — he was a *loose end*. A claimant who hadn't claimed. And loose ends, in Malvane's ledger, only stayed breathing until they were useful.

The lantern light moved on. Ansel counted to a hundred, then slipped out into the rain.

There was only one person in Vell who dealt in things the law had outlawed and asked no questions loudly enough to matter. Only one person whose back room held charms, false papers, and a lifetime of looking the other way. Sefa would curse him, charge him double, and hide him anyway.

He ran west, toward the Tallow Quarter, the gray book cold against his chest, and behind him the city hissed and glittered and did not know that its hundred-year-old secret had just started walking.
