## 2. What I Stole From Myself

Sefa's shop smelled of tallow and old iron, the way all her shops had smelled for as long as Ansel had known her, which was longer than either of them admitted. She locked three bolts behind him, drew a salt line across the threshold with her heel without looking at it, and only then turned to study his face in the lamplight.

"You look like a man who read something," she said.

"I stole something." His voice came out wrong. Flat. He set the magistrate's memory-vial on her counter between a jar of pickled wraiths-eyes and a saint's fingerbone worn smooth as river stone. "The book wasn't on the ledger. It was in his private case, under warding I've never seen outside the Inquisition. A hundred years old. And Sefa—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a page with tonight's date on it."

"Dates repeat every century. Coincidence."

"And my name beneath it."

Sefa went very still. He had seen her face down an inquisitor with a drawn brand-iron; he had seen her haggle a ghost down from possession of her own hand. He had never seen her go still like this.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The book opened to the marked page as if it remembered being asked. The vellum was soft as skin, the ink brown-black, the script cramped and urgent — a prophet writing fast because the vision would not wait. Ansel read aloud, because reading silently felt too much like letting it happen to him in the dark.

*"When the seventh bell drowns and the rain comes up from the river, the heir of the Hollow Covenant will stand unmarked among thieves, having made himself a stranger to his own inheritance. On this night he will learn his name again."*

Below it, the date. Below that, two words in a different hand — newer, sharper, pressed hard enough to score the page:

**FIND HIM.**

"Unmarked among thieves," Sefa repeated. "Having made himself a stranger." Her eyes lifted to his. "Ansel. What did you do before you came to Vell?"

"I told you. Nothing. I walked out of the eastern fens seven years ago with a kit of thief's tools and no past worth mentioning."

"No one has no past. They have only pasts they've paid good money to lose." She turned the page. "And there is no one in this city better at taking memories than you. So ask yourself the question you're afraid of. If someone wanted to hide an heir so thoroughly that even prophecy could barely find him — where would they put the proof?"

He knew. He had known since the alley, since the rain, since the moment he'd read his own name in a dead man's handwriting. He just hadn't let himself say it.

"In me," he said. "In my head. Where nobody steals from but me."

* * *

The memory came out badly.

That was the first sign it was wrong. A clean extraction slid free like a fish through oil — he'd done ten thousand, he knew the feel. This one caught. This one *held*, the way a splinter holds, grown into flesh around its edges. He sat in Sefa's back room with the extraction needle trembling above his temple and sweat running cold down his spine, and for the first time in seven years of professional thieving, Ansel Okonkwo was afraid of what he might find.

"Do it slow," Sefa said. "If it fights—"

"It won't fight." He almost laughed. "It's mine. It's been waiting."

He touched the needle to the place behind his ear where the scar tissue was older than any scar he could account for, and pulled.

* * *

Rain. But not Vell's rain — fen rain, warm and wide, falling on a causeway of black stone that ran out across drowned country toward a gate the size of a cathedral door. The Hollow Gate. He stood before it wearing clothes he did not recognize: grey wool, silver thread at the collar, the stitching of a house he couldn't name but whose sigil rose in his chest like a second heartbeat. Around his neck hung a token on a chain — a small ring of hollow metal, cold as a held breath.

*The covenant token,* said a voice beside him, and it was his own voice, younger, steadier, afraid. *First of five. The others are scattered, hidden when the pact broke. You are the last of the line. When you come of age, you carry them to the Gate and swear the oath, and the realms open, and everything your family bled for is finished.*

*I don't want it,* said the younger voice, and now Ansel recognized the fear in it, because it was the same fear sitting in his chest right now, watching. *I didn't ask to be born into a debt.*

*Nobody asks. That's what makes it an oath and not a wish.*

Then other voices, harsher. Men with lanterns and knives — Malvane's people, though the name meant nothing yet, only *the usurper's men, the ones who burned the fen-holds*. They had come for the heir. And the younger self, seventeen years old and drowning in a legacy he never chose, did the only thing he could think of.

He found a memory-thief in a fen-market town. He paid everything he had. He pointed at his own head and said:

*Take it. Take all of it. The name, the line, the token, the Gate. Take it so deep I'll never dig. Make me a stranger to myself. I'd rather be no one than be hunted for what I am.*

And the thief — a fen-witch with kind eyes and steady hands — had hesitated, and said, *Child, this will hurt worse than dying,* and he had said, *Do it anyway,* and she had done it, and the pain —

The pain was white. The pain was a bell drowning. The pain was seven years long and he had been living inside it the whole time without knowing, the way a man lives inside a scar.

* * *

Ansel came back to himself on the floor of Sefa's back room, on his hands and knees, weeping without any sound at all, which was somehow worse than screaming. The extracted memory hung in its vial before him, glowing faintly fen-green, and inside it he could see the shape of the token — the hollow ring, the covenant's first key — turning slowly like a thing alive.

"There it is," Sefa said quietly. "Seven years you've been walking around with the crown jewels in your skull and charging people by the hour."

He laughed. It came out broken. "I stole from myself. Do you understand? I'm the victim of the best theft I ever — " He couldn't finish. He picked up the vial and held it to the lamp and watched his own seventeen-year-old cowardice swim in the green light. "I didn't escape the burden. I just made sure someone else would have to find it. Someone like me. Someone who'd dig."

"Prophecy says tonight you learn your name again." Sefa crouched beside him, and her voice lost its fence's irony entirely. "So learn it. What is it?"

He closed his eyes. Behind them, the causeway stretched out over drowned country, and the great Gate waited, and somewhere out in the dark the usurper's armies were burning towns whose names were starting, painfully, to mean something again.

"My name," he said, "is Ansel Okonkwo. Of the line of the Hollow Covenant. Heir to a debt I tried to bury." He opened his hand; the vial's light spilled between his fingers. "And there are four more tokens, and I know where the second one is — because I hid it myself, the night I forgot. I left myself a map. Inside another memory."

Whose memory, he did not say. Because he already knew, and the knowing was a knife: the map was hidden in a memory he had stolen from someone else, years ago, casually, professionally — a stranger whose face he had never bothered to learn.

Someone in Vell was missing a piece of their life and didn't know why.

And Ansel was going to have to give it back.
