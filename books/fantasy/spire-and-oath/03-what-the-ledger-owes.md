## 3. What the Ledger Owes

The dead man was still lying in the mortuary chapel when the High Scribe called the household to account, which struck me as a particular kind of efficiency: interrogate everyone before the corpse has even cooled enough to embarrass anyone with details.

We were assembled in the Scriptorium at dawn bell—all forty-one of us debt-bound scribes and the dozen free clerks who looked down their noses at us for having been foolish enough to be born owing money. Brother Anselm stood at the head of the room in his grey robes, hands folded into his sleeves like a man preparing to say grace over a meal he intended to refuse us.

"Last night," he said, "a stranger died at our gate."

Nobody breathed. In the Quiet Valley, strangers did not die at gates. Strangers did not, strictly speaking, *arrive*. The valley had three roads out and none of them were roads so much as suggestions the mountain made when it felt generous, and no one came in who wasn't born here or bound here. A stranger dying on our doorstep was roughly as plausible as snow falling upward.

"The temple's physicians are examining the body," Anselm continued. His voice was the voice of a man reading an inventory of someone else's sins. "Until they conclude, the household will conduct itself as normal. You will copy your ledgers. You will attend your offices. And if any of you saw anything—" his gaze moved across us like a slow blade, "—anything at all that bears on how this man came to be at our gate, bleeding, you will bring it to me before the noon bell. Not after. Before."

He paused.

"There is no shame in knowledge," he said. "There is only shame in hoarding it."

I kept my eyes on the middle distance, which is a skill every scribe learns by age ten, and I thought about the map currently sewn into the binding of Ledger 114, *Grain Tithes, Western Terraces*, where it lay against the spine like a second nervous system. I thought about how Brother Anselm would look if I walked up right now and handed it over. I imagined him saying *there is no shame in knowledge* while the enforcers took me down to the undercroft, and I decided that the shame of hoarding sounded considerably more survivable than the alternative.

"Dismissed," said Anselm, and forty-one people exhaled at once, a sound like wind through dry reeds.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding something: the hiding is easy. It's the *carrying* that wears you down.

The map had gone into the ledger two nights ago, in the dark hours after the courier died, when my hands had finally stopped shaking enough to hold a needle. I'd taken Ledger 114 from the binding rack—a fat, dull volume nobody had requested in four years—and slit the leather along the spine with a bookbinder's knife, laid the folded map flat against the cords, and glued it down with wheat paste. Then I rebound the whole thing properly, because I am, whatever else is true, a good scribe, and there is a certain grim comedy in doing excellent work on your own destruction.

Ledger 114 went back on the shelf between *Grain Tithes, Eastern Terraces* and *Orchard Levies, Third Register*, and it has never once been requested, because grain tithes from the western terraces are the literary equivalent of watching paint freeze.

But now, standing in the Scriptorium with Anselm's words still hanging in the air, I understood the flaw in my plan: the interrogation wouldn't be about the map. It would be about *me*. And I had spent the night of the courier's death being exactly the wrong kind of visible.

"You." Sister Maren caught my sleeve as we filed toward our desks. She was sixty, half-blind, and missed nothing, which is a combination the temple has never learned to fear properly. "You were on gate duty when he came."

"I was."

"And you've said nothing to the High Scribe."

"I've said everything I have to say. He collapsed. He bled. He died. It was very sad and very quick."

Maren looked at me over her spectacles the way she looked at a page with a smudged character—patiently, and with the certainty that she would find what was underneath. "Your hands," she said.

"What about them?"

"They're still shaking."

"They shook all through the copying of the funeral registers last spring. Grief takes me strangely."

"That wasn't grief you copied." But she let go of my sleeve, and that was its own message: *I'm not pressing now. I'm pressing later.* Maren had been indentured longer than I'd been alive; she knew that debts come due on schedules nobody posts.

I sat at my desk and pulled the morning's work toward me—an inventory of candle wax, because the temple believed in punishing its scribes with tedium as a form of grace—and I made myself write *wax, tallow, forty pounds* in a hand so steady it looked machine-made, and inside my chest my heart was doing something much less dignified.

Because here was the problem, the one I'd been avoiding since the gate: the courier hadn't just given me a map. He'd known my face. Or—he'd known *something*. *The Spire remembers*, he'd said, and then he'd looked at me with an expression I keep trying to file away and failing, an expression like recognition, like relief, like a man finding the one door in a burning house that isn't locked.

Strangers don't recognize scribes. Scribes are furniture. We are the ink-stained background against which the temple performs itself. If a dying man looked at me like that, then either he was delirious—and delirious men don't carry wax-sealed maps to nowhere—or he knew precisely what he was doing.

And if he knew what he was doing, then someone else might know too.

* * *

They found the insignia at midday, and the whole valley heard about it by evening, because the temple has no secrets except the ones it keeps badly.

Brother Colm told me, in the way Colm tells everything—sideways, in the refectory, over soup thin enough to read through. "They cut the coat off him," he murmured. "Under the travel leathers. Temple grey underneath. Full insignia."

"That's impossible," I said, and then wished immediately that I'd said *how strange* instead, because *impossible* was a word with weight, and weight draws attention.

"That's what I said." Colm stirred his soup without eating it. "But there it was. Grey cloth, the nine-pointed star, the border knotwork. And here's the part—" he leaned in, and his breath smelled of the cheap wine the free clerks traded for among themselves, "—the knotwork was wrong. Old style. Pre-Settlement pattern. Archivist Veyne looked at it and went white as a tithe notice."

Pre-Settlement. The knotwork from before the valley closed. From before, if you believed the histories—and I had copied those histories eleven times, so I believed them the way you believe a wall you've leaned against all your life—from before the Covenant sealed the Quiet Valley away from the world and its magic, and sealed the world away from us, and declared that this was mercy, and that no one left, and no one entered, and the ledger of the world balanced forever.

No one leaves. That was the first law written in the children's primer, before letters, before numbers: *no one leaves.* And yet a man wearing temple grey in a style older than anyone living had walked out of somewhere, across mountains, through the pass, carrying a map, and died at our gate whispering about a spire that appears in none of our records except as a child's rhyme:

*Ember high and ember low,*
*what the fire forgot, the snow will know.*

I'd sung that rhyme skipping rope when I was six. I'd thought it was about hearths.

"So either the temple's been lying to us," Colm said, "for a hundred years, or two hundred, or however long—"

"Colm."

"—or somebody's been making coats. Which is worse, honestly, because why would anybody—"

"*Colm.*"

He shut up. Across the refectory, two of the enforcers had come in—not the ordinary guards, but the tall quiet ones in undyed wool who answered only to the High Scribe and whose names nobody used because using a name implies the named person can be addressed. They stood by the door and did not eat, and the temperature of the room dropped by several degrees of conversation.

One of them looked at me. Just looked. Two seconds, maybe three. Then he looked away, and I spent the rest of the meal performing the most convincing bowl of soup ever consumed by a guilty woman.

(Or man. Or whatever the temple had decided I was this year—the ledgers disagree, and I've stopped correcting them, because correcting them requires explaining, and explaining requires being seen, and I have built a whole life on not being seen.)

* * *

That night I went back to the binding room, because paranoia is a discipline and I am nothing if not disciplined.

The binding room sits off the Scriptorium proper, a low chamber smelling of paste and leather and the tallow candles the temple begrudges us. I lit one candle, no more, and took Ledger 114 down from the shelf, and turned it over in my hands looking for what I already knew I'd find: nothing. The spine was smooth. The stitching matched. The paste had dried clean. It was a perfect piece of concealment, and it was also, I realized as I held it, a death warrant with nice craftsmanship.

Because here is what I know about searches, having performed them myself—temple protocol requires quarterly audits of the archives, and I have run three of them. You don't search shelves. You search *anomalies*. A ledger that hasn't moved in four years is an anomaly. A scribe whose hands shake is an anomaly. A courier who dies at the gate wearing impossible insignia turns the entire household into a field of anomalies, and somewhere in that field, sooner or later, someone methodical was going to kneel down in front of the western terrace grain accounts and think: *why is this binding newer than its neighbors?*

I put the ledger back. I sat down on the binding bench in the dark and let the panic move through me like weather, because fighting it early only makes it last longer—that's another thing scribes learn.

And in the middle of the weather, a thought arrived, cold and clear as the first snowfall:

*The High Scribe didn't ask what the man said.*

Think about it. A stranger dies at the gate. The household is assembled. Anselm asks if anyone *saw* anything. Not *heard*. Not *received*. Saw. As though the interesting question were visual. As though the temple already knew the man could talk, and wanted to know whether anyone had let him finish.

I had let him finish. I had knelt in the snow with his blood soaking through my sleeve and listened to a dying man tell me that the Spire remembers, and I had taken his map, and I had hidden it, and every hour that passed with it hidden I was not a scribe anymore. I was evidence.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above me, footsteps crossed the Scriptorium floor—slow, unhurried, the tread of someone checking on things at an hour when honest people sleep. I pinched out the flame and stood perfectly still between the binding racks, listening to the footsteps cross the ceiling of my life, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that comes to people in dark rooms holding other people's secrets, that the interrogation hadn't started at dawn.

It had started the moment the snow touched his blood. Everything since—the assembly, the soup, the enforcers' two-second glance—had been the temple letting the household hang itself, thread by thread, the way we bind books: patiently, cord over cord, until the whole thing holds together and can be carried anywhere.

The footsteps faded. I stayed in the dark a while longer, doing sums I didn't want to do.

Three years left on my bond. That was the arithmetic I'd lived by my whole life—three years, and then freedom, such as freedom is, a cottage and a garden and the right to be ignored by the temple instead of owned by it. Three years was survivable. Three years was *nothing*; I'd already survived twenty-two.

But the arithmetic had changed. The dead man had carried a zero into my ledger, and zeros multiply.

If they found the map on me, I would not be beaten and fined. Beating is for stolen candle wax and read letters. This was different. This was a stranger with impossible insignia and a dying message, and the temple does not beat people who know things; it files them. There is a shelf in the undercroft for filed people. Sera Voss has been on it for seven years—I'd seen her once, through the grate, chained and calm as a closed book, and I'd thought *there but for the grace of solvency go I.*

I took Ledger 114 down again. I held it. And then, because I am a good scribe and good scribes do not destroy records, I did not burn it. Instead I did something better.

I unbound it. All of it—cords cut, boards off, the map lifted free and dried and folded small, small, smaller than a prayer. And I began rebinding the ledger with a deliberate flaw: a loose gathering, a slipped signature, the kind of error a tired scribe makes at the end of a long day. Because flawed volumes get pulled for correction. Flawed volumes get *requested*. And a requested volume travels through the archive under a clerk's name and a return date, and a volume that moves looks exactly like a volume that has nothing to hide.

By the time I finished, the bell for midnight offices had come and gone, and my hands were steady for the first time in two days.

Tomorrow, Ledger 114 would go to the correction queue under Sister Maren's authority—she owed me a favor from the funeral registers, and favors are the only currency the temple doesn't tax. Tomorrow the map would begin moving, quietly, invisibly, in plain sight.

And tomorrow, too, I would have to decide what to do about the woman in the undercroft. Because the courier's map showed a road, and the road went over the pass, and no one crosses the pass alone in winter unless they are very lucky or very dead, and I have never once in my life been lucky.

Sera Voss had looked at me through the grate with the eyes of someone counting exits. I had a feeling she'd already counted mine.

I blew out the candle and went up to my cell, and behind me in the dark the archive held its breath around one small, folded secret, waiting—like everything in the Quiet Valley—for someone to finally ask the right question.
