## 4. Sera Voss, Exile

There are three hundred and eleven stones in the ceiling of my cell. I know because I have counted them four times a day for six years, and on the days when the counting comes out wrong—which happens more often than I'd like—I start over rather than accept that my mind is going soft in this hole.

Three hundred and twelve today. Either the temple has added a stone or I have lost one somewhere between breakfast and noon. Breakfast is gruel. Noon is gruel. The temple of the Quiet Valley believes in consistency the way other churches believe in gods.

My name is Sera Voss. Once Knight-Captain of the Third Lance, Winter Court of Aldermere, sworn shield to a queen who is either dead or has very good reasons to pretend she is. Now I am the woman who deserted, and desertion in the winter court is not a crime so much as an insult—a wound given to the whole idea of loyalty—and wounds like that are punished slowly, ceremonially, and preferably somewhere cold.

The valley was their idea of somewhere cold. Not cold at all, as it turns out. That's the joke. They sent me to the one place in the world where nothing works—not magic, not escape, not even hope, if hope runs on the same fuel as everything else. The Quiet Valley eats power the way sand eats water. I felt it go out of me on the first night, six years ago, like a lamp guttering: one moment the old warmth in my blood, the next just a body. A big body, scarred, increasingly good at counting stones.

They keep me in the undercroft beneath the scriptorium because the High Scribe is a careful man and careful men keep their dangerous things close. I am a dangerous thing. So, apparently, are the ledgers above me, which the scribes copy out by hand day after day, year after year, records of grain tithes and candle wax and who owes whom how many chickens. A kingdom built on chicken arithmetic. Somewhere north, my old comrades are freezing on a wall, and here I lie beneath a room full of people arguing about eggs.

I hear them through the floor. Feet, mostly. One set of feet I have come to know better than I'd like: light, quick, always slightly hurried, with a drag on the left step—someone favoring an old injury, or new ones, regularly renewed. The scribe. The quiet one. Paloma, I learned eventually, from a guard who talked too much when he thought stone walls kept secrets. Stone walls don't keep secrets. Stone walls are where secrets go to be counted alongside the ceiling.

* * *

The courier died on a Tuesday. I know it was Tuesday because the bells above me rang the penitential hour twice, which they only do on Tuesdays, and because the undercroft filled with boots—six pairs, maybe eight, moving fast, which never happens. Nothing fast ever happens down here. Fast things happen up there, in the world, and then their consequences trickle down to me like water through a cracked roof.

I heard the gate bell first, muffled, urgent. Then shouting—the particular flat shouting of men who have found something they didn't expect and are frightened of it. Then the boots. Then, later, quieter, the voice of the High Scribe himself, coming down the stairs he almost never descends, pausing outside my door.

He didn't come in. He stood there for the length of ten breaths, and I lay on my pallet and looked at the ceiling and did not count anything, and then his footsteps went away again, slower than they'd come.

That night the guard brought my gruel and forgot to lock the slot properly, which had never happened before. I could have pushed it open. I could have done nothing else, but I could have done that much. I lay there looking at the unlocked slot until dawn, and in the morning it was locked again, and neither of us ever mentioned it.

Something had changed upstairs. You learn to read a prison the way sailors read weather.

* * *

She came down herself four days later.

I heard her before I saw her—that left-dragging step, quicker than usual, and a second set of feet behind her, heavy, bored, the guard named Oskar who smells of onions and fear in roughly equal measure. The key turned. The door opened. And there she stood in the doorway with a lantern held too high, blinking into the dark like a person who has rehearsed being brave and found the rehearsal insufficient.

"Knight-Captain Voss." Oskar said it with the flat contempt of a man reading from a list. "The scribe wants to see your hands."

"My hands," I said.

"Show her your hands," Oskar said, and then, because he was fundamentally a lazy man, he leaned against the doorframe and stopped caring what happened next.

So she stepped inside—into my cell, the first visitor in six years who wasn't delivering gruel or a beating—and raised the lantern, and I got my first proper look at Paloma Nakamura.

Young. Younger than I'd expected from the footsteps; the drag made me think older, worn. Fine-boned, ink-stained to the elbows the way some people are sunburned, hair pulled back in a style that suggested someone else had told them to do it. And eyes that did the thing I recognized immediately, the thing you only see in two kinds of people: soldiers before a battle they expect to lose, and prisoners deciding whether to trust you. Eyes doing arithmetic. Weighing what it will cost to say the true thing versus the safe thing.

"Hold out your hands," they said. Their voice was steadier than their eyes.

"Why?"

"The temple requires—" they began, and stopped, and I watched them discard the sentence like a card played badly. "Please. There was a man at the gate. Before he died. I want to know if you knew him."

Well. That was more interesting than gruel.

I held out my hands, palms up, the way you do when surrendering or begging, and they took them—actually took them, turned them over, ran ink-soft fingers across the calluses. My sword calluses. My frost-scars. In the valley's dead air those scars were just scars, but a trained eye can still read them, and this was a trained eye, whatever else it was. Scribes don't learn to identify weapon calluses. Scribes don't flinch, very slightly, at the burn pattern on my right palm—the mark the winter court puts on its oath-takers, a snowflake seared into the skin, which the valley's null air had faded to a ghost of itself but not erased.

"You were real," Paloma murmured. Not to me. To themselves. Like a scholar confirming a citation.

"I'm generally considered real," I said. "Though the winter court prefers to think of me as a cautionary tale."

Oskar shifted by the door. Paloma dropped my hands as if they'd been caught touching something hot.

"The man at the gate," I said, before they could retreat behind protocol. "Describe him."

They hesitated. I watched the arithmetic again—cost of truth versus cost of safety—and I thought: *they're going to lie to me*, and I couldn't even blame them, because I'd have lied too, in their position, to a convicted deserter in a hole.

"He was wearing temple gray," they said instead. Which was true, probably, and also not the answer to my question, and we both knew it.

"Temple gray from *this* temple?" I asked, mild as milk. "Because I've been under this particular pile of stones for six years, scribe, and in all that time no one has gone out through the pass and come back. No one leaves the valley. Isn't that the first thing they teach you? Along with the chicken ledgers?"

Their face did something complicated. Fear, mostly, but underneath the fear a flash of anger—good, anger is fuel, anger means the fire isn't out—and underneath the anger, worst of all, recognition. They knew I was right. They'd known it since the man died, and it was eating them alive.

"Your hands are shaking," I said quietly.

"They're not."

"They've been shaking since you walked in. It's not the cold. This is the warmest room in the valley—it's directly over the ovens, did you know that? Six years, and the one luxury they gave me is warm floors." I folded my hands in my lap, deliberately slow, deliberately harmless. "You're shaking because you're carrying something you shouldn't be carrying, and every hour you carry it, the weight doubles. I know the feeling intimately. I once carried a sealed order I wasn't supposed to read. Reading it cost me everything. Not reading it would have cost me worse."

Paloma stared at me. Behind them, Oskar scratched his neck and gazed at the corridor with the profound disinterest of a man being paid by the hour.

"What order?" they asked.

"That's not the story I'm selling today." I leaned back against the wall. Three hundred and twelve stones overhead, and for the first time in years I didn't care whether the count came out right. "Here's what I'll give you for free, scribe, since you came all this way and showed me your frightened eyes when you meant to show me your official face. Whatever that man pressed into your hands—and don't bother denying it, I can see the shape of it under your robe, everyone in this temple can see the shape of it if they look, and *they are looking*—whatever it is, it's already killed one person, and the people who killed him are not finished. The High Scribe stood outside my door four nights ago and listened to me breathe. He hasn't done that since my sentencing. Careful men only check on their dangerous things when they're planning to use them or destroy them."

The lantern trembled. Just slightly. Just enough.

"You think they'll kill me," Paloma said. Flat. Testing it in their mouth like a coin bitten for gold.

"I think," I said, "that in six years, no one has ever come down here to ask about my hands. And I think a scribe who copies egg tallies doesn't suddenly develop a professional interest in a disgraced knight unless they need to know something only a disgraced knight would know." I let that sit. Let it get comfortable. "And I think you should ask yourself why the temple keeps a winter court soldier locked beneath its scriptorium at all. Deserters get ransomed back, usually. Executed, sometimes. Kept? For six years? Kept the way you keep a tool you might need?"

"They said you were a warning."

"I'm a lot of things. A warning is the cheapest of them." I tilted my head toward the ceiling, toward the scriptorium, toward the whole pious, suffocating, candle-counting edifice of the Quiet Valley. "Ask yourself what a temple that worships the absence of magic wants with a knight whose whole life was magic. Ask yourself why the valley exists. Not the hymn version. The real version."

For a long moment they just breathed. I could hear the ledger-paper rustle of their thoughts, the frantic re-sorting of a life's assumptions. I remembered that sound. I'd made it myself once, on a frozen rampart, holding a sealed order and a knife and the wreckage of everything I'd believed about my queen.

Then Paloma did something I did not expect. They reached into their robe—Oskar stirred, then didn't—and pressed their hand flat against their own chest, over the hidden shape, over the thing that was doubling in weight every hour. Not showing me. Just acknowledging it. Telling me *yes, it's here, yes, I know you know.*

"If I needed to know something," they said carefully, "about the winter court. About what lies beyond the pass. Would you tell me?"

"Depends what you're offering."

"Nothing. Yet." A ghost of something crossed their face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might build later, if the materials arrived. "I'm a debt-bound scribe, Knight-Captain. I don't own anything. Not my time, not my name, not three more years of my life. All I have is questions."

"Questions are worth more than you think," I said. "I've been asking the same one for six years. *Why am I still alive?* Nobody's answered it yet. Maybe you'll have better luck with yours."

Oskar cleared his throat. The audience was over; the onion-scented clock had run down. Paloma straightened, and the official face came back up, smooth as fresh vellum, and they turned to go.

At the door they paused. Didn't look back. Spoke to the corridor rather than to me, which was wise, because Oskar was watching.

"The man at the gate," they said. "Before he died. He said something. Two words."

I waited.

"He said, *the Spire remembers.*"

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the valley's engineered seasons. Six years of dead air, six years of numbed blood, and two words from a dying stranger reached down through all of it and found the old warmth and pinched it, hard.

The Ember Spire. The place the winter court whispered about when it thought its knights were asleep. The place the war ended—or didn't end, depending on which version of the ending you were allowed to hear. The place my queen sent half her Lance to die securing, the winter I stopped believing in her.

"Guard," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because nothing else in me was. "The scribe asked about my hands. Tell whoever needs telling that my hands are fine. Old, scarred, useless. Tell them exactly that."

Paloma glanced back at me then—one heartbeat, lantern-light, two sets of trapped eyes finding each other across six years of stone—and I saw that they understood. Understood that I'd just lied to the temple for the first time, on their behalf, without knowing why. Understood that whatever they carried, I was now carrying a piece of it too, whether I wanted it or not.

Understanding went both ways. That was the trouble.

The door closed. The key turned. The boots went away up the stairs, the light step dragging on the left, the heavy one following, and I lay back on my pallet and stared at the ceiling and did not count a single stone.

*The Spire remembers,* I thought. *And apparently, so do I.*

Above me, faint through the floor, the scriptorium bells began to ring—and they rang the penitential hour twice.

On a Friday.

Someone upstairs had decided it was time to start hunting.
