## 6. Ash Wednesday Rites

The undercroft smelled of wet stone and old penance, which I suppose is what most prisons are made of when you strip away the iron.

I had been in the Temple of the Quiet Valley for six years by that winter, and in that time I had learned three things with any certainty. First: the brothers and sisters of the Quiet Valley were the most quietly vicious people I had ever met, and I had once dined for a week at a winter court table where the soup course alone claimed two careers. Second: the valley's magiclessness was not an accident of geography but a discipline, though it would be years before anyone told me why. Third: the scribe with the ink-stained cuffs and the shaking hands was hiding something, and whatever it was, it was going to get us both killed.

I want to be clear that I did not like Paloma Nakamura when we began. Liking is a luxury for people whose futures extend past Thursday. But I watched them the way you watch weather — because your life depends on reading it correctly — and over the years I had catalogued their habits the way a soldier catalogues patrol routes. They came down to the undercroft every third day with the temple's ledgers of debt, because even prisoners owed the Quiet Valley money, and they always, *always*, kept their left hand curled around something when they thought no one was looking.

No one was ever looking. That was the whole point of the valley. But I had been a knight of the winter court before I was a prisoner, and knights are trained to notice the thing that doesn't fit, the loose thread, the door that's been oiled too recently. The scribe's left hand didn't fit.

So on the morning of the Ash Wednesday rites, when Brother Cassian came down the stairs two steps at a time — which he never did; Cassian moved through the world like honey poured uphill — I knew before he opened his mouth that the weather had turned.

"Voss." He stopped at the bars. He never called me Voss. He called me *the exile*, or *sister* when he wanted to wound me, because using my rank against me was the only cruelty the Quiet Valley permitted itself. "You will attend the rites this year."

I looked up from the floor where I'd been counting stones. Four hundred and eleven. It had been four hundred and ten for three years; I had added one out of spite. "Attending is a strong word."

"You will kneel in the rear gallery. You will keep your eyes lowered. And you will be seen by the whole congregation, so that no one can say the temple hides its failures." He smiled the way frost forms — slowly, and all at once. "The High Scribe believes in transparency during holy days."

Which meant, translated from temple-speak into the language I actually spoke: *something is happening today, and we want our dangerous things where we can see them.*

"I'm honored," I said.

"You're nothing," Cassian said pleasantly, and unlocked the cell.

* * *

The temple on Ash Wednesday was a different animal wearing the same skin. The great hall had been swept and hung with white banners so clean they hurt to look at, and the congregation filled the benches in their grey wool, breath steaming, faces tilted toward the brazier where last year's palm fronds waited to become this year's ash. Incense crawled along the ceiling. Somewhere a choir of children was rehearsing a hymn in a key that suggested none of them had volunteered.

They put me in the rear gallery as promised, kneeling on stone with my hands visible, flanked by two enforcers who had the relaxed posture of men who had been told to relax and resented it. From there I could see the whole hall, and more importantly, I could see the doors.

Enforcers do not attend rites. Enforcers stand in corridors and check seals and ask questions in low voices. There were eight of them that I counted in the first five minutes, moving through the side aisles with censers they had no business carrying, and every one of them paused at the scribes' bench.

Paloma sat third from the end, bent over a prayer book they clearly weren't reading. Their left hand rested flat on the wood, empty, deliberately empty, and I felt something loosen in my chest that I refused to name as relief. Then Sister Maren stopped behind them, leaned down, and said something into their ear, and Paloma went still in a way I recognized from interrogations. Not the stillness of innocence. The stillness of a rabbit that has heard the hawk and is betting everything on being mistaken for stone.

The hymn ended. The High Scribe rose, robed in ash-grey, and began the litany of debts — the Quiet Valley's central sacrament, the recitation of everything owed and everything forgiven, which in my experience forgave considerably less than it recited. The congregation murmured responses. The children's choir found its key at last. And under all of it, the enforcers began to move.

Not toward Paloma. Toward the scriptorium. Two of them slipped out the west door while every face in the hall was bowed, and a minute later two more followed, and I understood the shape of it the way you understand a battle plan from the artillery: they weren't hunting the scribe in public. They were tearing apart the scribe's life in private, and the rites were the noise that covered the sound.

They had searched my cell yesterday. I knew because the straw was wrong — I sleep on the left side of it, always, a habit from campaign tents, and someone had rearranged it right-handed. They had found nothing, because there was nothing to find, because whatever Paloma carried, they carried it on their body or nowhere. But searches escalate. Searches have a grammar. Yesterday the cells, today the scriptorium, tomorrow—

Tomorrow, I thought, watching Sister Maren whisper to a man in enforcer grey near the vestry door, tomorrow there won't be a search at all. There will be a report. *Tragic. Bandits in the pass took two souls, a scribe and the exile both, may the Quiet Valley forgive them their debts.* The valley hadn't seen bandits in a generation — the mountains saw to that — but grief needs no evidence, only a grave, and the valley had plenty of ground.

I had run this arithmetic before, in other temples, other courts. It always came out the same way: when they start searching quietly, the killing has already been scheduled. The search isn't to find the contraband. It's to establish the timeline.

Paloma raised their head during the response — *and what is owed shall be paid* — and looked directly up at the gallery. At me. Their face did something complicated, fear and decision fighting for the same ground, and then they mouthed two words across forty feet of incense-hazed air.

*I know.*

Well. Good for the rabbit.

* * *

The rites ended at noon, as rites do, and the congregation filed out into the snow in their twos and threes, and the trap began to close with the gentle efficiency of everything the Quiet Valley did.

I was returned to my cell — escorted now by four enforcers instead of two, which told me my own timeline had been moved up — and I lay on my straw listening to the temple breathe. Footsteps above. Doors. Once, the scrape of heavy furniture in the scriptorium directly overhead, and the muffled voice of the High Scribe saying, very calmly, words I couldn't make out to someone who was presumably not answering satisfactorily.

I had escaped from three prisons in my life. A court dungeon in Vhale, a river hulk during the border war, and a monastery in the Greyfells that had made the mistake of hiring me to guard its own walls. Each escape had begun the same way: with the realization that the people holding you had stopped thinking of you as a person and started thinking of you as a problem, and that problems, unlike people, can be disposed of without paperwork.

The Quiet Valley had reached that stage somewhere between the rites and vespers.

Here is what I had going for me: six years of counting stones, and a body that the temple fed adequately because dead exiles make poor examples, and a door lock older than the temple's current administration. Here is what I had against me: everything else.

But I had also spent six years watching the novices come down to scrub the undercroft floors, and novices are the same everywhere — careless with keys, sentimental about each other, and constitutionally incapable of resisting a pretty story. Brother Tam, seventeen, chin like a shovel, had been bringing me extra bread since his grandmother died because I had once told him his grandmother was surely forgiven her debts, which was a lie, because I had no idea what she'd owed and neither did he, and it was the kindest lie I'd told in years.

At the changing of the watch, I coughed. Then I coughed worse. By the time Tam came down the stairs with his lamp, I was folded against the wall doing my best impression of a woman drowning on dry land, which is a skill the winter court trains into you whether you ask for it or not.

"Sister Voss?" His lamp shook. Good lad.

"Pneumonia," I wheezed. "Or the lung-rot. Or—" another convulsion, artistically timed— "go get the infirmarian."

"The infirmarian's at evening office—"

"Then get *anyone*, boy, unless you want to explain to the High Scribe why the temple's example-prisoner died unattended in the dark." I let my head thunk against the stone for punctuation. "Unless that's already decided."

That landed. I watched it land. Tam stood in the doorway torn between doctrine and panic for exactly three seconds, then bolted up the stairs shouting for help, and in his haste he set his key ring on the bench outside my cell rather than fumbling with it in the dark, and I want it noted for posterity that I did not steal the keys.

I stole one key. The cell key. The rest I left swinging gently on their ring like a promise that I was a thief of limited ambition, which in a way I was. All I wanted was out of the cage. What I did after that was between me and the gods, and the gods of the Quiet Valley weren't speaking.

* * *

Finding Paloma was easier than it should have been, because Paloma wasn't hiding. They were standing in the cloister walk between the chapel and the dormitory, in the falling snow, with a lantern, waiting — for me, I realized, with a jolt, though how they'd known I'd come I never asked. Perhaps rabbits and soldiers read weather the same way.

"We need to talk," they said, "and we need to do it walking."

"Talking while walking is how I got exiled," I said. "But fine."

We walked. Snow came down in that fat, silent way it does in the valley, erasing the courtyard edges, muffling the world. Paloma kept to the shadowed side of the colonnade and spoke low and fast, and everything they said confirmed the arithmetic I'd already done, plus interest.

"They searched the scriptorium during the rites. All the bindings, all the ledgers. They didn't find it." Their hand touched their ribs, briefly, unconsciously — so it was on their body, sewn into clothing probably, clever little archivist. "And then Sister Maren took me aside and told me the temple was arranging an escort for me. To the pass. Tomorrow. To 'deliver testimony' about the dead courier to the garrison at Hollowbridge."

"There's no garrison at Hollowbridge that takes testimony," I said. "There's a road, a bridge, and a lot of trees."

"Yes," Paloma said. "I've been reading the temple's outbound correspondence for nine years. I know exactly what's at Hollowbridge." They stopped walking. In the lantern light their face was terrible and calm, the calm of someone who has finished crying in private and budgeted no further tears. "And they've assigned Brother Cassian to escort me. Cassian, who has never left the valley in his life. And they've reassigned my dormitory bunk as of tonight. And the courier's body was burned this afternoon without rite or record, which violates eleven sections of the temple's own mortuary code — I checked."

"Burned," I repeated. "Fast."

"They're erasing him. And tomorrow they'll erase me, on the road, and blame bandits, and burn whatever's left, and the ledger will show I died honorably in service." Paloma looked at me, and here was the thing that undid me, though I hid it well: they weren't asking me to save them. They were reporting facts. "And you're next. After me. Because you saw the courier die too, and because the High Scribe has wanted a clean excuse to close your account for three years. Bandits are generous that way. They'll take two."

The snow fell. Somewhere across the courtyard, an enforcer's boots crunched past on a route that was definitely not the usual route.

"So," I said. "Staying is the plan that gets us buried."

"Staying was always the plan that got us buried," Paloma said. "We just didn't used to know the date."

I should tell you what I thought about in that moment, because it wasn't heroism. I thought about my cell, four hundred and eleven stones, six years of cold and bread and counting. I thought about the fact that I was a convicted deserter with a bounty on my head in three provinces, and that outside the valley's shimmer there was a whole world of people who wanted me dead for reasons with actual paperwork behind them. Every rational bone in my body said: go back to the cell, Voss. Die in the straw you know. It's warmer than the pass.

But I had spent six years watching this ink-stained, trembling creature carry ledgers up and down those stairs with their left hand curled around a secret, and I had watched them take a beating in chapter house for reading a sealed letter and come back the next day with their spine straighter, and I thought: if this person has decided to stop waiting to be buried, then the least I can do — the very least, the debt-floor minimum — is not let them walk into the mountains alone.

Also, I'll admit it: I was tired of counting stones.

"The pass closes with the deep snow," I said. "If we go, we go tonight, before the watch changes at second bell. And we go over the shoulder of the mountain, not through the gate — the gate has a wardens' post, and wardens ask questions, and I'm done answering questions for institutions." I held out my hand. "Give me the map."

Paloma went very still again. Hawk-stillness. "How did you know there's a map?"

"Scribe. You've been guarding a document for days. The courier died pressing something into your hands — I was in the undercroft, not deaf. Documents that get people burned are usually maps, and maps lead somewhere, and somewhere is better than here." I kept my hand out. "I'm not asking to keep it. I'm asking to see the route, because I've crossed these mountains in war and you haven't crossed them at all, and if we take the wrong col we'll be found in spring, frozen together, a cautionary tale."

A long moment. Snow accumulated on both our shoulders like the valley itself trying to hold us in place. Then Paloma reached under their collar and drew out a small oilskin packet, and inside it — I saw only the edge of it, the wax seal broken, the ink fine and precise — a map, and something written on it in a hand that made the scribe's breath catch even now, weeks after they'd first read it.

I didn't see what was written. I saw their face. And their face told me that the map said something about *them*, something that had cracked their whole life open like ice on a pond, and that they had been carrying the crack around against their ribs, keeping it warm, feeding it.

"Tonight," they said. "Second bell. The undercroft grate — it opens onto the drainage channel, and the channel runs under the wall. I've known about it since I was twelve. I copied the maintenance ledgers." A ghost of something that was almost a smile. "Nobody reads the maintenance ledgers."

"Except you."

"Except me."

Above the cloister, a bell began to toll the evening office, and the sound rolled out over the snow, and I understood that we had just crossed a line that could not be uncrossed — that somewhere in the temple above us, a report was already being drafted with two names on it and the word *bandits* in the margin, and that the only question left in the world was whether we would be corpses in the pass or ghosts in the mountains.

"Second bell," I said. "Don't be late, scribe. I've broken out of three prisons, and I refuse to make the fourth one wait."

Paloma nodded once, snuffed the lantern, and vanished into the snow like a debt forgiven — completely, and against all expectation.

I stood alone in the falling white and listened to the bell count down the hours of my old life, and I found, to my considerable surprise, that I was smiling.

Four hundred and twelve stones, I thought. I'd add one more tomorrow. If there was a tomorrow, and if there was a wall to count from.

The bell tolled. The snow fell. Somewhere overhead, in a warm room that smelled of ink and candlewax, the High Scribe was writing the ending to a story that Paloma Nakamura and I had just decided to survive long enough to rewrite.
