## 7. Over the Pass

*Paloma*

The undercroft key was where Sera said it would be: in the High Scribe's belt pouch, which was hanging on a hook outside the bathhouse like the most trusting object in the world.

I want it noted that I had never stolen anything before that night. Not so much as a candle stub. Three years of indenture will teach you to covet quietly and take nothing, because taking is how they extend your bond by a season, then two, then however long it takes for you to forget there was ever a number attached to your name. But tonight the ledger of my debts was about to be closed by other means, and apparently other means required larceny.

The key was heavier than it looked. Iron, cold enough to bite, with a symbol stamped on the bow that I recognized from the temple's oldest records — not the Quiet Valley's dove-and-sheaf, but something older, a circle broken by a line. The kind of mark you find on things people wanted very badly to keep shut.

"Paloma." Sera's voice came through the gap beneath the bathhouse door, low and even. "You're standing in snow making noise with your teeth. Either commit or go back to bed."

"I'm committing," I whispered.

"You're hesitating with extra steps."

She wasn't wrong. I stood there in the falling dark with the first real snow of the season coming down early — weeks early, thick and silent, the kind of snow the elders called *heaven holding its breath* — and I thought about how easy it would be to put the key back. To return to my cot, my ink-stained fingers, my three remaining years. To be small and safe and slowly erased.

Then I thought about the courier's blood soaking into the gate stones. About the map hidden inside the binding of Ledger 114, *Grain Tithes, Years Unrecorded*, bearing a name I'd been told never existed. About the way Brother Cassian had looked at me during evening rites — not with suspicion exactly, but with the particular gentleness one reserves for things already scheduled to die.

I went to the undercroft.

* * *

*Sera*

Seven years in a cell teaches you the acoustics of your own prison better than any map could. So when the lock turned — really turned, the tumblers giving up their argument after a lifetime of winning it — I knew before the door opened that it was either freedom or an executioner, and I'd stopped being able to tell the difference years ago.

It was the scribe. Of course it was the scribe.

They stood in the doorway with a stolen lamp and a face doing its best impression of resolve, which is to say: terrified, stubborn, and trying to hide both behind the other. Their hands were shaking. Their hands were always shaking, but tonight the shaking had a direction to it, and direction counts for more than steadiness in my experience.

"You brought a lamp," I said.

"The pass is dark."

"The pass is also full of temple hunters by dawn, once they find my cell empty and yours colder than it should be. A lamp says *here we are, please collect us*."

"Do you have a better idea?"

"Yes. Walking fast." I took the lamp anyway, because if we were going to die on a mountainside I preferred to see it coming. "How far to the valley's edge?"

"Half a night, if the snow doesn't slow us. Then the boundary." They said the word the way children say the names of monsters — carefully, from a safe distance. "The shimmer. Where magic starts again."

"And you've never seen it."

"No one has. No one leaves." A pause. "No one left."

I stepped out of the cell that had been my whole world since the winter court put me in it, and my legs nearly buckled at the novelty of a floor that went somewhere. Seven years. I had measured out seven years in cracks in the stone and the sound of rites through the ceiling, and now here was a corridor, and beyond it a door, and beyond that —

Beyond that, everything.

"Scribe," I said. "Your name. You've never given it."

"Paloma." They hesitated. "Just Paloma. The family name isn't mine to use anymore. It went into the bond ledger when I was nine."

"Paloma, then. When this is over, if it's ever over, someone should give it back to you."

They didn't answer, but they walked a little straighter, and I've learned to count that as an answer.

* * *

*Paloma*

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing the only home you've ever known: it looks exactly like home until suddenly it doesn't.

For the first hour it was almost pleasant. Snow falling soft as forgiveness, the temple lights shrinking behind us, Sera setting a pace ahead of me like a compass needle that occasionally swore. She moved through drifts the way water moves downhill — no wasted motion, no complaint, just inevitability. I floundered. I floundered magnificently. At one point I went in up to my hip and she hauled me out by the collar without breaking stride, saying only, "Walk where I walk," which struck me as both practical advice and a fair summary of my entire situation.

But somewhere past the terraced fields, past the last shrine with its little unlit lantern — nothing burns in the Quiet Valley; fire won't catch where magic can't breathe — the valley began to feel less like a place and more like a held breath. The trees grew sparser. The snow fell thinner. And the silence changed quality. In the valley, silence is comfortable, upholstered, the silence of a room you know. Out here it was attentive. It listened back.

"We should stop," Sera said, "only if we must. Cold is patient. Hunters are not."

"What were you, before?" I asked, mostly to fill the listening silence. "In the court. Before the desertion."

Her jaw did something complicated. "A sword," she said finally. "A very good one, for a while. And then a bad one, for reasons that seemed excellent at the time." She glanced back at me. "Ask me again when we're somewhere with wine. Or don't. Most people who ask regret it."

"Noted."

We climbed. The pass narrowed between shoulders of black rock, and the wind found us there and made its opinions known. My borrowed coat — Sera's suggestion, taken from the bathhouse stores, another crime added to my rapidly thickening file — was meant for temple clerks, not mountain flights, and by the time the sky began to bruise toward dawn I couldn't feel my feet and had begun negotiating seriously with the idea of lying down.

That was when Sera stopped so abruptly I walked into her back.

"There," she said.

Ahead of us, maybe fifty paces, the air itself was wrong.

I don't know how else to describe it. It was as though someone had hung a curtain across the pass made of heat-haze and faint light, a vertical shimmer that moved without wind, silver-edged and utterly silent. Beyond it the mountains continued — same rock, same snow, same grey dawn — but seen slightly differently, the way a face looks different when it smiles. The world on the far side of the shimmer was *more*. More color in the stone. More depth in the shadows. More of whatever it is that ordinary eyes aren't supposed to notice.

"That's it," I breathed. "That's the edge."

"That's the cage door," Sera said, "and we're on the inside of it. Go on. Touch it."

"Why me?"

"Because if it melts the skin off your hand, I'd rather know before I walk through."

There was, I reflected, a certain grim honesty to her that I was beginning to rely on. I raised my hand and reached toward the shimmer.

And my blood woke up.

I don't have better words than that, though I am, professionally, a person of words. It started in my fingertips — warmth, sudden and enormous, like plunging a frozen hand into summer — and rolled up my arm and into my chest and out to every border of me, and for one impossible moment I could *feel* the shimmer the way you feel a drum through a floor. It wasn't a wall. It was a scar. Something had been cut here, long ago, and stitched shut, and the stitches were singing.

"Paloma." Sera's voice, sharp with alarm. "Your eyes are—"

The light bent around my hand. That's the truth of it, strange as it sounds: the shimmer leaned toward me, curved inward like water finding a drain, and where my fingers hovered the silver brightened to gold, and somewhere deep below thought, below fear, something ancient and patient in my blood sat up and said, with unmistakable recognition:

*Oh. There you are.*

I snatched my hand back. The shimmer settled. My heart was going like a temple drum at festival, and my breath came out in ragged clouds, and I felt — gods help me — I felt *wonderful*. Alive in a way three years of copying tithes had never once managed. Every nerve sang. The exhaustion was gone. The cold was gone. I could have run to the Spire and back.

That was the part that frightened me. Not the power. The *appetite*.

"It knows me," I said. My voice didn't sound like mine. "Sera, the boundary — it recognized me."

She was looking at me the way you look at a fire that has jumped the hearth. Not fear, exactly. Recalculation. "Then it's true," she said quietly. "What the old stories say. What they sealed away." She shook her head. "I thought you were a scribe who'd read too many sealed letters."

"So did I."

"Well." She pulled her cloak tighter and faced the shimmer, and I watched her make peace with it the way soldiers do — quickly, completely, without ceremony. "The valley wants you dead, the winter court wants you as a weapon, and the thing in your blood wants *something* it hasn't told us yet. As prospects go, I've had worse." She glanced sideways at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, Sera Voss smiled. It was not entirely reassuring. It was, however, entirely sincere. "Coming, vessel-of-the-old-covenant? Or would you rather stand here admiring the view until the enforcers arrive?"

I looked back, once, at the valley. At the little cluster of temple lights below, asleep and safe and built on a lie I had spent my whole life keeping warm. Somewhere down there, Brother Cassian was probably already awake, already reading ledgers, already looking for my name.

I turned around.

Crossing the shimmer felt like stepping through a curtain of cool water. For half a heartbeat the world doubled — valley and not-valley, silence and song — and then I was through, and the air tasted different, sharper, sweeter, like the first breath after a fever breaks. Magic wasn't a roar out here. It was just *present*, the way gravity is present, the way debt is present. Simply part of how things were.

Behind us, far to the south, the sky flickered — once, twice — a pale pulse along the horizon, like a great eye opening.

Sera saw it too. Her smile died.

"That's not dawn," she said.

"No," I agreed, though I had no idea how I knew. I only knew that the thing in my blood had felt it too, and had stirred, and had *leaned toward it* the way a dog leans toward a whistle. "The winter's starting early. And I think—" I swallowed. "I think it's starting because of me."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Snow began to fall again, fine and dry, the wrong snow somehow, snow with intent behind it.

"Then we'd better get to the Spire before everyone else does," she said, and started walking north, and I followed — walk where she walks, after all — into a country I had never seen, wearing a name I had never been allowed to say, carrying a fire in my veins that had waited my entire life for a door to open.

Behind us, the shimmer of the valley's edge glowed softly in the new light, patient as a scar.

Ahead, the pass climbed toward the Spire, and the long winter came down to meet us.
