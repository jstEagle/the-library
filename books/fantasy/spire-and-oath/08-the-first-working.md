## 8. The First Working

I have copied ten thousand pages of temple ledgers in my life, and I can tell you with authority that nothing prepares you for the moment the numbers stop meaning anything.

We had made it perhaps four miles past the shimmer—that curtain of faint light at the valley's edge that Sera called a *barrier* and I called, for the first hour of walking through it, *nothing at all*, because crossing it felt like stepping from one stuffy room into another stuffy room. All my life I'd been told the world beyond the valley was a place of wonders and horrors, of sorcery and ruin, and the great revelation of my twenty-third year was that the other side of a miracle is just more snow.

Then Sera collapsed.

Not dramatically—Sera Voss does not do anything dramatically if she can help it. She simply stopped mid-stride, said "huh," in the tone of someone noticing they've run out of ink, and sat down in a drift like a puppet whose strings had been quietly cut.

"Sera?"

"Hand," she said, by way of explanation, and held it up.

The hand was wrong. That's the only word I have for it. The fingers had gone the color of candle wax, waxy and stiff and faintly translucent at the tips, and when she tried to close them they moved like something borrowed rather than owned. Frostbite, she said later—the river crossing two nights back, the wet glove she'd never mentioned, the particular stubbornness of exiles who consider their own bodies a low-priority expense.

"We should turn back," I said. "There are healers in the valley."

"There are healers who will ask why we left," she said. "And enforcers who already know." She flexed the bad fingers, winced, and did that thing she does where she converts pain into arithmetic. "It's not so bad. People lose fingers all the time. It builds character."

"You don't have any character to spare. You're an exile."

"That's exactly how much character I have. One exile's worth. Don't make me spend it on jokes."

I knelt beside her in the snow, because there was nothing else to do, and took her ruined hand in mine—and that is when everything went strange.

*

Here is what I knew about magic before that afternoon: nothing. Less than nothing. In the Quiet Valley, magic isn't forbidden; it's *impossible*, the way a stone is impossible to eat. We didn't have wards or prohibitions. We had ledgers. The temple taught that the world outside was full of fire-callers and storm-speakers and worse, and that the Quiet Valley was blessed precisely because none of it could reach us. Magic slid off the valley like rain off oiled canvas. I had spent my whole life inside that oilcloth, and I had assumed—I see now how completely I had assumed—that whatever made me *me* would stay on the same side of the barrier as my fear of the High Scribe and my talent for copying small numbers neatly.

I was wrong about that. I was wrong about almost everything, but let me be precise about this one.

When my skin touched Sera's frostbitten hand, I felt something *open*. There is no gentler way to say it. It wasn't a door and it wasn't a floodgate and it wasn't any of the metaphors the temple used to make the outside world sound manageable. It was more like a knot coming undone—a knot I hadn't known was tied, pulled loose by contact, by warmth, by the simple fact of another person's hurt pressed into my palm. And then the knot came out, and what rushed through the hole was not power, whatever the ballads call it. It was *attention*. Total, merciless attention. For the first time in my life I perceived every part of Sera's hand at once: the dead tissue in her fingertips, the capillaries collapsed like roads after a flood, the cold sitting in her bones the way debt sits in a ledger—patient, compounding, indifferent.

And I understood, without being taught, that I could pay it.

That's the only honest description. Something in me understood the shape of the debt and reached for it, and I thought—briefly, stupidly, in the last clear thought I would have for some time—*oh, so this is what the courier meant*—and then I stopped thinking in words altogether.

Heat poured out of me. Not from my hands—from somewhere behind my sternum, some room in me I had never been given a key to, and the heat went down my arm and into hers and began doing work I could feel but not follow. I felt it knit. I felt it *argue* with the cold, cell by cell, waking things that had already begun to die. And every bit of that work came out of me. I don't know how else to explain it. It was like watching someone copy a ledger using your own blood for ink. Each repaired vessel in her hand cost me something—not pain exactly, though there was pain, but *substance*. Memories flickered as they went. My mother's kitchen. The smell of the temple's binding glue. The name of the boy who'd shared my dormitory bench when I was nine. They surfaced and were skimmed, like coins taken from a purse by fingers too quick to catch.

I remember thinking: *stop.*

I remember being unable to stop, which is different.

The last thing I saw clearly was Sera's face above mine, going from gray to horrified, her mouth shaping my name—my real name, the childhood name from the map, the name I'd only just learned existed—and then the snow came up to meet me, and the world folded shut like a book.

*

I woke to firelight and the smell of burning pine, which is a smell I had no right to know and yet knew perfectly, and to Sera sitting over me with her whole hand wrapped around a tin cup, all five fingers flexing, working, *alive*.

"You absolute idiot," she said, with feeling.

"Good morning to you too."

"It's night. You've been out since noon." She pressed the cup into my hands. Broth, thin and salty and the best thing I had ever tasted, though I want to be clear that my standards had recently collapsed along with most of my certainties. "Your lips were blue. Your nose was bleeding. You were muttering ledger entries in your sleep, which, incidentally, is the most disturbing thing I've ever heard a person do while dying."

"I wasn't dying."

"You were absolutely dying." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I've seen men die of less than what you did. So before you say anything else: thank you. And never do it again."

I looked at her hand. Whole. Pink at the fingertips. She caught me looking and turned it over, examining it herself, the way you'd examine a returned letter.

"It doesn't even scar," she said quietly. "Do you understand that? Frostbite like that takes fingers. Takes the whole hand, sometimes. And you—" She stopped. Started again. "What *are* you, Paloma?"

"I'm a scribe."

"You know that's not an answer anymore."

I did know it. I drank the broth and bought myself time, and looked up at the sky through the pine branches, and the sky was wrong—or rather, it was *right*, and I had never seen it right before. Stars. Hundreds of them, sharp as nailheads, in patterns I had read about in books imported at ruinous expense and never once believed. The Quiet Valley's sky was a soft gray ceiling, always, clouded by something no one ever named. Out here the ceiling was gone, and underneath it the world went on forever, and I had healed a knight's hand with a room behind my sternum, and both of those facts were equally impossible and equally true.

"The map says the Ember Spire," I said finally. "You've heard of it. You went quiet when I first showed you, back in the undercroft. You went quiet the way people go quiet around a grave."

Sera fed a branch to the fire. She has a habit of answering questions with chores, buying time the way I buy time with broth.

"There was a war," she said at last. "Before your war, before my war, before any war anyone alive remembers clearly. Mages against the winter courts. Not armies at first—just... escalation. The mages could unmake things. The courts learned to freeze what was unmade, hold it still, keep it from ever having happened properly. Villages stopped existing in between states. Whole valleys got locked in a single season for a hundred years." She glanced at me. "Sound familiar?"

My mouth had gone dry. "The valley doesn't have seasons. It has weather. Gray weather. Forever."

"Mm." She poked the fire. "Nobody won. That's the part the songs leave out. Wars like that don't end, they just get *expensive*, until both sides are spending things they can't afford to lose. And then someone—no one knows who, or they pretend not to—did the unthinkable. Instead of winning, they ended it. Gathered every vessel, every person born with the talent, every living conduit of the old covenant, and sealed them away. Somewhere magic couldn't reach. Somewhere it couldn't wake."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, a bird made a sound I didn't know and would spend the next week learning.

"They built a valley," I said.

"They built a prison," Sera said, "and called it a blessing, and filled it with people who'd never know. And either the sealing failed after all these years, or—" she looked at me across the fire, and her face did something complicated, "—or they missed one."

The wind moved through the pines. I held the cup in both hands and watched my own reflection shiver in the broth, and I thought about the High Scribe's face whenever a child asked too many questions. I thought about the sealed letter, the beating, the three years remaining on a bond I now suspected was never about money at all. I thought about a courier dying at the gate with a map bearing my stolen name, and the words he'd spent his last breath on.

*The Spire remembers.*

"Sera," I said. "If I'm what's left of that—if I'm a vessel, if I'm the last of whatever they sealed away—then everyone who kept me in that valley knew. The temple. The High Scribe. They raised me in a cage and charged me rent for it."

"Yes."

"And the winter court wants me because I'm a weapon."

"Yes."

"And the Spire is where the weapon is kept loaded."

She didn't answer that one, which was its own answer, and I set down the cup because my hands had started shaking and I didn't want to spill the broth, waste being a sin even at the end of the world.

*

Here is the thing nobody tells you about wonder: it curdles.

I lay awake long after Sera took first watch, staring up at those ferocious stars, and I turned my own hands over and over in the firelight looking for the room behind my sternum. It was still there. Quieter now, closed, but there—a pressure, a potential, the way a ledger is full of money that isn't yours. And beneath the awe of it, beneath the child's delight of *magic is real and I am magic*, something colder had begun to grow.

Because I remembered what it had cost. Not in the abstract. I remembered reaching for my mother's face while I burned, wanting to give Sera something warm to hold onto, and finding—nothing. A shape where a memory should be. I know my mother loved me. I know it the way I know sums: provable, recorded, empty. Her voice is gone. Her face is going. Whatever paid for Sera's five fingers took the coin that mattered most and gave me change I couldn't count.

And the truly frightening part—the part I haven't said aloud, the part I am only admitting now, here, to this page—is that when I touched her hand and felt the knot come undone, for one half of one heartbeat, I *enjoyed* it.

Not the healing. The *power*. The sense of debts I could pay, of a world full of broken things arranged before me like blank pages waiting for my hand. It felt like competence. It felt like purpose. It felt like everything the temple had starved me of for twenty-three years, served up in a single swallow, and I wanted more of it before I even knew what it was.

Sera says power corrupts. I used to think that was a thing comfortable people said about uncomfortable things. Now I think it's simpler and worse than that. Power doesn't corrupt you. Power *pays* you. It pays you in exactly the currency you're poorest in—purpose, importance, the feeling of mattering—and it lets you set your own price, and it never, ever shows you the bill until afterward.

I fell asleep rehearsing rules for myself. Never touch anyone's hurt without asking. Never work alone. Never, whatever happens, start enjoying the paying.

Near dawn I woke to Sera shaking my shoulder, her restored hand gripping hard, her face lit orange and grim by a fire she'd already kicked apart.

"Wake up," she whispered. "Smoke. Downhill, maybe two miles. Too much of it for a campfire." She paused, and I watched her decide whether to say the rest. "And Paloma—there's a road down there now. There wasn't one yesterday. Somebody's building one fast, and there's only one reason I know of to build a road toward a mountain pass in the dead of winter."

She looked north, toward the pass, toward the Spire neither of us had named aloud since the fire.

"They're not chasing us," she said. "They're racing us."
