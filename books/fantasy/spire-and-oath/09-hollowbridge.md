## 9. Hollowbridge

The town announced itself before we saw it, the way grief announces itself: a smell on the wind, a sound under everything else. Woodsmoke and wet wool and too many people breathing in too small a space. Then the walls rose out of the frozen mist—old stone, older than the war that had never quite finished happening—and I felt my shoulders drop an inch for the first time in six days.

"Is that it?" Paloma asked. They'd been walking like a marionette with two strings cut since the river crossing, and their voice had the flat, careful quality of someone rationing their own words.

"Hollowbridge," I said. "Last town before the pass. Last place where a person can buy soup without explaining herself."

"And after that?"

"After that, mountains. And after the mountains—" I didn't finish it. The Ember Spire sat at the end of that sentence like a stone at the bottom of a well, and I wasn't ready to say its name out loud yet. Names have weight. I'd learned that much from watching Paloma flinch every time they touched the map through their coat.

We joined the road's slow current of refugees. That was the thing I hadn't anticipated—the sheer number of them. I'd known the long winter was coming early; I'd felt it in my knees for a month before we ever left the valley. But knowing a flood is coming and seeing the first families carrying their furniture uphill are different animals entirely.

There were carts stacked with children and chickens, old men leading horses too thin to be worth stealing, a woman walking with a loom strapped to her back like a shell she refused to abandon. Nobody was running. That was what struck me. They were all walking at the pace of people who had decided, collectively and without discussion, that panic was a luxury the road didn't sell.

"They're fleeing the war," Paloma said quietly. "The one that hasn't started."

"The one that's always about to start." I kept my hood up and my eyes down. "The winter court mobilizes, the temple counter-mobilizes, and everyone who lives between them discovers that geography is not protection. It happens every generation or so. Usually somebody talks everybody back down before the arrows fly." I glanced sideways at the scribe trudging beside me, wrapped in a borrowed cloak three sizes too big, carrying a sealed covenant inside their skin like a swallowed coal. "This time, apparently, we're the negotiation."

"I didn't ask for—"

"I know what you asked for. You asked to not be murdered by your own temple. Very modest request. Tragically expensive."

Paloma almost smiled. Progress. Six days ago they would have apologized.

* * *

Hollowbridge earned its name honestly. A great arched bridge crossed the river at the town's heart—or half of it did; the other half had come down sometime in some earlier war and nobody had rebuilt it, so travelers crossed on the standing arch while the broken one leaned into the water like a drunk against a wall. The gate guards weren't checking papers. They were checking faces, but lazily, the way men check faces when they're being paid by the town and not by anyone with a real interest.

I got through on the strength of looking boring. Hood up, shoulders rounded, the walk of a hired sword escorting her employer's clerk. Which, in fairness, is more or less what I was now. Life has a sense of humor like that.

Paloma got through because they looked like exactly what they were—a tired scribe—and because the guard was more interested in the cart behind us, which contained four barrels of something the driver swore up and down was pickled herring and which smelled, even through the wood, like it could strip paint.

Inside the walls, the town was drowning politely. Every inn had tripled its prices and halved its portions. Every alley held a family with a cookfire. Refugees slept in the doorways of shuttered shops, and the shop owners had mostly stopped complaining, partly out of charity and partly because a doorway full of sleeping bodies is warmer than an empty one.

And everywhere—pasted to walls, nailed to posts, layered over each other like scabs—placards.

I saw the first one outside the chandlery and made the mistake of actually reading it. REWARD, it said, in the winter court's crisp blue ink. INFORMATION LEADING TO THE CAPTURE OF DESERTERS OF THE SEVENTH OATH. Below the text, rendered by a court artist with a genuinely cruel gift for likeness:

Sera Voss.

They'd given me my scar. Most artists flatter you or forget the details, but this one had drawn the white line across my jaw with loving precision, as if he'd been there when Aldric's blade opened it. Maybe he had been. The court brought chroniclers to executions. Why not to tribunals?

Below my face, a second placard, newer paper, different hand. A sketch of a slight figure in a scribe's robe, features vague, almost apologetic. WANTED BY ORDER OF THE TEMPLE OF THE QUIET VALLEY. FUGITIVE FROM DEBT-BOND. RETURN ALIVE. The word *alive* was underlined twice.

"Well," I said. "At least they spelled your name right."

Paloma had gone very still beside me. "That's me."

"That's you. Badly. You should be insulted." I took their elbow and turned us both away, walking at the pace of people with nowhere urgent to be. "Don't run. Running is how you turn a rumor into a certainty."

"They want me alive."

"Yes. Notice mine doesn't specify. The temple needs a vessel intact. The court just wants their traitor back, and they're flexible about condition." I said it lightly, because if I said it any other way Paloma would start apologizing again, and I'd discovered over six days of hard road that Paloma's apologies were like snowfall—harmless individually, fatal in accumulation. "It does mean our smuggler problem just became complicated."

"What smuggler problem?"

"The one where neither of us can show our face to arrange passage, and the pass closes within the week, and the only man who runs it in winter is a smuggler who knows everyone." I steered us toward the river district, where the taverns grew shabbier and the prices grew honest. "Fortunately, I know him too. Unfortunately, that cuts both ways."

* * *

Ivo kept his office above a tannery, which told you most of what you needed to know about his relationship with respectability. The stairs smelled like a dead animal's ambitions, and the door at the top was unlocked, because Ivo's philosophy was that anyone willing to climb those stairs deserved to rob him.

He was sitting behind a desk buried under charts and unopened letters, a big soft-bodied man with a beard going to iron-gray and hands that were surprisingly delicate for their size. He looked up when we came in, and I watched recognition move across his face in stages—first the polite blankness of a businessman greeting strangers, then the flicker of memory, then the slow spreading grin of a man watching money walk through his door wearing a wanted poster.

"Sera Voss," he said, "in my tannery. The world has gone strange."

"Ivo. Still hiding your best brandy behind the tax ledgers?"

"Still assuming everyone who asks about the brandy is a tax man." He came around the desk, wiped his hands on his apron out of habit rather than need, and embraced me briefly—a smuggler's hug, all back-slapping and no sincerity. Then he held me at arm's length and looked at me properly, and the grin faded into something more careful. "You look like hell, Voss."

"You should see the other guy. There were several other guys."

"There usually are." His eyes went past me to Paloma, who stood near the door with the particular tension of someone calculating exits. "And this is the reason for the other guys, I'm guessing. The temple's little runaway. Half the river towns are talking about it. Scribe vanishes from a valley that nobody leaves, taking nothing but a map." He smiled at Paloma, and it was a good smile, warm as a hearth, and I knew from long experience exactly how little it meant. "You're famous, friend. Well. Infamous. In this economy it's the same coinage."

"They're not 'the temple's runaway,'" I said. "They're my employer."

"A knight errant and a fugitive clerk. Very romantic." Ivo dropped back into his chair, which groaned like a ship in weather. "So. You want the pass. Winter route, no questions, no checkpoints. I can do it. Question is what you've got."

"We've got coin."

"You've got coin enough for the summer route. The winter route costs double, and half up front, and I don't leave till the moon turns, because the ice on the high trail isn't safe before then and I like arriving alive." He laced his fingers over his belly. "Unless you've got something else to sweeten it."

"We've got nothing else."

"Mm." He looked at Paloma again, longer this time, and I saw the shape of the thought forming behind his eyes before he spoke it, the way you see lightning before thunder. "Word is the runaway carries a map. Word is the map's worth more than the person carrying it. Word is three separate parties are paying good money just to *see* it."

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the price yet."

"I don't need to. The answer's no."

"Sera." Paloma's voice was quiet. I hated that they used it. "Let him talk."

"He'll talk all day. Talking is free. Everything else he sells costs your life."

Ivo spread his hands, wounded. "Voss. You wound me. All I want is a *look*. One look. I copy nothing, I keep nothing, I tell no one. But I look at it, and I know what I'm carrying, and knowing what I'm carrying changes my price. That's not betrayal, that's due diligence. Any merchant in the valley would tell you the same."

"Any merchant in the valley would also sell his grandmother for a favorable tariff," I said. "Your reputation precedes you, Ivo, and it arrives slightly ahead of you, whispering."

"My reputation keeps me alive." For a moment the warmth drained out of his face and I saw the actual man underneath—the one who'd survived twenty years running the passes between three powers that would all happily hang him. "Look. I like you, Voss. We've done business. You paid fair and you didn't ask what was in the crates, and I appreciated that. So I'll be honest with you, because honesty between professionals is cheaper than knives. If I take you across that pass blind—if I don't know what I'm smuggling—I might be carrying the thing that gets my throat cut in some waystation by whoever's following you. And people *are* following you. I count at least two groups asking after a knight and a scribe, and one of them wears court colors and doesn't haggle."

Behind me, Paloma moved. I heard the soft sound of the coat opening, the wax seal cracking free of whatever hiding place they'd sewn for it.

"Paloma." I didn't turn around. "Don't."

"It's a look," they said. "He's right. Knowing what he carries will keep him loyal better than coin will. Isn't that what you taught me? On the river? Trust is a ledger, and every entry has to balance?"

"That is *not* what I taught you."

"It's close enough." They stepped past me, and I watched them lay the map on Ivo's desk with both hands, carefully, the way you set down something sleeping. The candlelight caught the old vellum, the faded lines of the pass, the spire drawn at its heart like a wound.

Ivo leaned forward. And I swear on every oath I'd broken and every one I hadn't, for one moment—for the length of a single breath—he simply *looked*, the way he'd promised, with a craftsman's eye and nothing else in his face.

Then his fingers twitched toward the charcoal stub lying among his charts, and I saw the whole transaction complete itself behind his eyes, quick and clean as a pickpocket's lift.

He was fast. I'll give him that. Faster than I expected, faster than he needed to be, his hand darting for the charcoal and the scrap of vellum beside it—

I put my knife through the space between his knuckles and into the desk, pinning nothing but his shadow, and the crack of it was very loud in the little room.

We all stood still. Downstairs, the tannery went on with its reeking business, oblivious.

"Ivo," I said pleasantly, "we're going to play a game called Remember Our Friendship."

"That was reflex," he said. His voice had gone carefully light. Sweat stood out at his hairline. "Professional habit. You put a rare document in front of a man who deals in documents, Voss, he's going to reach. Doesn't mean anything."

"It means everything. It means the moment I walk out that door, you sell the description to whichever buyer pays first, and by tomorrow there are three parties waiting for us at the top of the pass instead of two." I twisted the knife free. "So here's the new arrangement. You take us across. You get paid double, half now. And you forget everything you saw in the last thirty seconds, or I come back down off that mountain, and I won't be alone, and what's with me doesn't negotiate."

I didn't look at Paloma when I said it. I didn't have to. I felt them go still, and I felt the temperature of the room change—not literally, not magic, just the specific chill of a person realizing what they'd been about to become party to.

Ivo looked at me. Then at Paloma. Then at the map, still lying on his desk, patient as a trap.

"Double," he said finally. "Half now. And Voss—you watch yourself. Because I'm not the only one who's heard the description of your cargo, and I'm the *friendly* option."

* * *

We found a room above a dyer's shop, cheap because the fumes made sleep a gamble. Paloma sat on the narrow bed with the map back inside their coat, arms wrapped around their knees, and didn't speak for a long time.

"You think I was wrong," they said eventually.

"I think you were desperate, and desperation makes excellent arguments."

"He only looked."

"He reached for the charcoal."

"But he *stopped*." Paloma's voice cracked, just slightly. "Because you stopped him. Maybe that's—that's how it works, maybe. People aren't good or bad, they're just—leaning. And you catch them." They looked up at me, and in the lamplight their face was young and exhausted and terribly sincere. "That's what you did. You caught him leaning."

I sat down on the floor across from them, back to the wall, because the chair had a broken leg and because sitting below the window felt prudent. "Paloma—"

"Everyone in that square today," they went on, as if I hadn't spoken, "all those refugees. They looked at us and they didn't see anything. Just two more travelers. Two more people walking away from something. And for about ten minutes, buying bread, I felt—" They stopped. Started again. "I've spent my whole life invisible, Sera. Debt-bond. Robes. Head down. And today, in a crowd of strangers, invisible was just—ordinary. Everyone was invisible together. It was the closest thing to belonging I've ever—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "And then I saw my face on that wall. Twice. Wanted by everyone who raised me. And I understood that the belonging was a loan, same as everything else in my life. Same as the magic. Somebody's going to call it due."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below us, the dyer's dogs started up at something in the street, and didn't stop.

I should have said something wise. I was, allegedly, a knight; wisdom was supposed to be part of the inventory. Instead I said, "Eat something. Believing the worst is easier on a full stomach."

Paloma laughed—a wet, surprised sound—and reached for the bread.

I stayed awake after they slept, listening to the dogs. They'd stopped barking at carts and cats an hour ago. Now they were barking at intervals, steady, directional, the way dogs bark at men who stand still in courtyards and wait.

Through the shutter slats I could see the bridge, black against the gray water. And on the far side of it, where the lamplight ended, shapes in the dark that were too patient to be refugees.

Court colors didn't show at night. But I'd know that stillness anywhere. I'd drilled it into recruits for six years.

Aldric had found the river towns.
