## 10. Ivo's Price

I want it on the record—my record, the one I keep in my head now because paper can be searched and heads, so far, cannot—that I never trusted Ivo. Not for a moment. I distrusted him the way you distrust a bridge made of rope and optimism: you still cross it, because the alternative is the river, but you cross it *quickly*, and you do not stop in the middle to admire the view.

He found us at Hollowbridge's south gate on our second morning, a wiry man with a smuggler's smile and a boat he called *the Honest Woman*, which should have told me everything. He quoted us a price to take us downriver past the toll stations. He looked at Sera's sword, at my scribe's ink-stained fingers, at the way we stood too close together like people carrying something breakable, and his price was not coin.

"The map," he said. "Just a look. I've been running this river eleven years and nobody's ever asked me to go where you're going. A man likes to know what he's ferrying."

"A look," Sera said.

"A look," Ivo agreed, hand over his heart, which I noted was positioned conveniently over a coat with very deep pockets.

Sera looked at me. I looked at the map inside my coat, against my ribs, where it had lived so long it had begun to feel like an organ. The courier had died pressing it into my hands. The High Scribe had beaten a household half-blind looking for it. Aldric's hunters had drowned in a frozen river chasing the rumor of it. And here was a man with a boat asking to see it because he was *curious*.

"Fine," I said, before Sera could negotiate, because I have always been the sort of fool who resolves tension by making it worse.

I showed him the map. I held it up in the grey light of the boathouse, far enough away that he couldn't touch it, and I watched his eyes move across it—the valley pass, the river road, the black mark of the Ember Spire at its western end—and I watched something flicker behind them that was not curiosity. Curiosity is open. This was a door closing quietly.

But by then we were already on the water, and the alternative was the river.

* * *

Three days downriver, I started counting the things that didn't add up.

The first: Ivo knew the patrol schedules of the winter court along the northern bank. Not approximately. *Exactly.* He'd steer wide of a bend an hour before a gray-cloaked rider appeared there, and he'd whistle while he did it, like a man avoiding puddles he'd mapped himself.

The second: at the ferry station at Marlow's Crossing, the toll-keeper waved us through without payment and called Ivo "cousin," and Ivo's family, as far as I could tell, came from nowhere near Marlow's Crossing.

The third—and this is the one I should have led with, because I am a scribe and scribes are supposed to understand that documents matter more than words—Ivo asked me, on the second night, what name was written in the map's lower corner.

I told him it was smudged.

It wasn't. It said *Paloma Ashkettle*, in a child's careful hand, the name I'd worn for six years before the temple took me and unmade it. But I lied easily, smoothly, and I felt the lie come out of me like breath in cold air, and I thought: *this is what fleeing does to you. You get good at this.*

I lay awake that night in the boat's shallow hold, listening to Sera sleep sitting up against the mast with her sword across her knees, and I did the arithmetic I should have done at the gate. Eleven years running this river. Comfortable with patrols. Cousin to toll-keepers. Asking after names instead of routes.

A man who sells passage does not need your name. A man who sells *you* does.

I didn't tell Sera. That's the part I keep turning over, even now. I had three days of evidence and a scribe's training in exactly this kind of forgery—documents that say one thing and mean another—and I said nothing, because I was tired, and because part of me wanted to be wrong, and because the ugliest truth of all: I didn't want to admit I'd been the one who said *fine* at the boathouse. If Ivo betrayed us, then some portion of that betrayal belonged to me, signed in my own hand.

So I kept watch and told myself stories, which is, if nothing else, excellent practice for a novelist. Though I wasn't a novelist yet. I was just a liar on a boat.

* * *

It happened at the weir below Thornmouth, where the river narrows and the banks rise up steep and pine-dark, and where Ivo said we'd have to portage around the locks until morning.

"We'll camp here," he said, beaching the *Honest Woman* on a shingle bank. "Roads are bad through the town. Better at first light."

There was no reason to camp. There was every reason not to—we were two days ahead of the thaw-flood, Ivo said, and every hour mattered—but I was so tired of suspecting that I let myself be managed. Sera built a fire small enough to hide under a blanket. I sat with my back against a stone and rehearsed, for no reason I understood at the time, the route from memory. Pass, river road, the high trail above Thornmouth gorge, the Spire. Over and over, like a prayer, or like a scribe copying out a text he suspects will be burned.

Ivo said he was going to check the lines. He was gone perhaps twenty minutes.

When he came back, he wasn't alone in his manner, if that makes sense. His body came back alone, but something else had joined it—a looseness, a brightness, the particular cheer of a man who has recently been paid. He brought dried fish and a bottle of plum brandy and pressed the bottle on Sera like a gift between old friends.

Sera drank a little. I pretended to drink more.

And in the middle of the night, when the fire had gone to coals and Sera's breathing had gone long and heavy—which I told myself was exhaustion, which I *begged* it to be exhaustion—Ivo crouched beside me in the dark and said, conversationally, like a man discussing weather:

"They'll come up the gorge road at dawn. Court riders. You'd do well to be gone before then, scribe. Take the high trail. They won't think of it—they never do." He paused. "Consider it a refund."

My whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the river air.

"You sold the route," I said. My voice came out flat. Isn't it strange, the moments when terror sounds like bookkeeping?

"I sold *a* route," Ivo said. "The one they expected me to sell. Downriver, past the tolls, straight into their arms at Grayford. That's what the agent paid for." He grinned, and in the dark his teeth were very white. "Whether you're on it is another question entirely."

"You copied the map."

"I looked at the map. There's a difference." He stood, brushing off his knees. "Though I'll grant you, I've got a good memory. Comes of having nothing else worth keeping."

"Why?" It came out of me almost as a child's question. *Why would you do that.* As if the world ran on reasons.

Ivo looked down at me, and for one moment the smuggler's grin fell away and underneath it was just a thin, tired man on a cold riverbank, and that was somehow worse than the grin.

"Because the court pays in gold and doesn't ask what you spent," he said. "Because everyone I've ever ferried who was *running* ended up dead anyway, and at least this way somebody profits. Because—" he shrugged, and the shrug contained entire continents of resignation— "because it's what I'm for, scribe. Nobody hires the Honest Woman expecting honesty. You knew that at the gate. You crossed anyway."

He walked back toward the boat. And I sat there with the coals dying in front of me and something rising in my chest that I had no name for yet, except that it was hot, and it was mine, and it was awake.

* * *

Here is what I remember about the next few minutes, in the order my body remembers them rather than the order they happened:

The heat first. It started under my sternum, the same place the cold had been, and it spread outward like ink dropped in water. The coals of the fire brightened without being fed. Frost on the stones around me began to sweat.

Then the sound—the river getting louder, though the river hadn't changed, only my hearing of it, as if someone had opened a door in my skull onto a room where everything was shouting.

Then Ivo, halfway to the boat, turning around because he felt it too. Everyone feels it. That's the thing nobody tells you about magic—it has no manners. It announces itself. It walks into the room wearing your face and takes up all the air.

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. The last time this happened—on the ice, with Aldric's hunters—I was terrified and it moved through me like weather. This time I wasn't terrified. This time I was *furious*, and fury fit the power like a key fits a lock, and that frightened me more than anything else, that it fit so well.

"Ivo," I said, and my voice came out wrong. Doubled. Like two people speaking slightly out of sync, and one of them was not me.

He stopped. Smart man. Even smart men freeze when the frost starts crawling *uphill*.

"Scribe," he said carefully. "Whatever you're doing—"

"What did you tell them?" The heat was climbing my throat. "About us. About *me*. What did you tell them I am?"

"That you're cargo," he said. "That's all I told them. Cargo and a knight with a bounty. I swear it on the river—"

The river answered. I swear this is true: the water rose against its own current, a hand's width, a foot, standing up out of its bed like something waking, and the sound it made was the sound the room behind the door in my skull had been making all night. The pines on the bank groaned. Snow shook loose from the branches and hung in the air, falling upward, and I stood in the middle of it with my hands burning and I thought, with terrible clarity: *I could stop his heart. It would be easy. It would be like snuffing a candle.*

And the worst part—the part I have never told anyone, the part I am telling you now because you are paper and paper keeps secrets—is that some part of me *wanted* it. Not justice. Justice would have been tying him up, taking the boat, leaving him for the court riders he'd sold us to. This was not justice. This was the simple animal pleasure of being, for once in my indentured, beaten, invisible life, the most powerful thing on the riverbank. Three years left on my bond, the High Scribe used to say, as if my life were a debt someone else held. Well. Here, on this bank, I owed nothing to anyone, and something in me had done the math and found the balance irresistible.

I raised my hand.

Sera caught my wrist.

She must have woken minutes before—she fakes sleep better than anyone I've ever met, a useful skill for a woman with a bounty on her head—and she didn't shout, didn't grab the sword, didn't do any of the dramatic things. She just closed her hand around my wrist, warm and callused and utterly solid, and said my name. My real one. The one from the map. She was the only person alive who used it.

"Paloma. Look at him."

I looked. Ivo was on his knees in the snow—not cowering, exactly. Watching. With that same awful attentiveness he'd given the map, memorizing me, and I understood with a lurch that whatever I did next would be carried out of this riverbank and sold somewhere too, that even my rage had a market value, that I could not perform a single act in this world without it becoming currency in someone else's ledger.

"This is how it happens," Sera said quietly. Her grip didn't tighten. It didn't need to. "This exact moment. I've seen it. I *was* it." Her voice caught, just barely, on the edge of something old. "You tell yourself he deserves it. Maybe he does. That's not the question. The question is who you are when you're finished. And the answer, every time, is *someone who finds the next one easier.*"

The heat under my sternum fought her words. It wanted to burn them as fuel. Everything I felt, it wanted to eat—grief, fear, love, all of it convertible, all of it coin. I thought of the drowned hunters on the frozen river, the way the ice had taken them, the way I'd slept afterward without dreaming, as if some part of me had filed the whole thing under *not my fault* and locked the drawer.

If I killed Ivo, the drawer would get bigger.

The snow fell back down. The river settled into its bed with a long, grudging sigh, like a dog told to lie down. The heat drained out of me and left behind the familiar hollow ache, and beneath the ache, faint but unmistakable, a small absence—something gone that I couldn't name, the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue. I would learn later what it was. That night I only knew the bill had come due again, and that I had, by inches, chosen not to pay the largest installment.

I let my hand fall.

"Get off my boat," I said. My voice was just my voice again, hoarse and ordinary and twenty-two years old.

"It's my boat," Ivo said.

"Then swim."

* * *

We took the *Honest Woman* upriver ourselves, badly, arguing with the current and each other, while Ivo stood on the shingle bank growing smaller behind us. He didn't beg. I'll give him that. He stood with his hands in his deep pockets and watched us go, and just before the bend took him, he called out—one last transaction, one last piece of information tossed across the water like a vendor's cry:

"High trail above the gorge! Dawn, scribe! Don't make my gold wasted!"

Sera handled the tiller. I sat in the bow with my arms around my knees and watched the pines slide past, and I did not feel cleansed or noble or any of the things the temple's liturgies promised waited on the far side of temptation. I felt *split*. Like a document with two texts written over each other, both in my hand, both legible if you knew how to look.

"You stopped me," I said finally. "Thank you. I think."

"Don't thank me." Sera's eyes stayed on the water. "Thank yourself, in about ten years, when you find out who you turned out to be." She paused. "But Paloma—there's something you need to hear, and you're going to hate it."

"Those are usually your best speeches."

"He sold the route they *expected*. Which means the court already knows roughly where we are, and now they know we know." She nodded upstream, toward the dark throat of the gorge, where the high trail climbed into country no boat could follow. "And there's a third thing. The agent who bought your route from Ivo." Her jaw tightened around the words like they cost her. "I recognized the description. Gray cloak, silver chain of rank, a scar through the left eyebrow."

The river roared beneath us. I waited.

"It was Aldric," Sera said. "My commander. My—" she stopped, restarted. "The woman who trained me. She's not hunting us anymore, Paloma. She's *herding* us. And I need to figure out why before we walk into the gorge."

Above Thornmouth, the first light found the high trail, narrow and white and waiting, and it looked exactly like a road someone wanted us to take.
