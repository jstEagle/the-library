## 11. The Winter Court's Hounds

There is a particular sound a frozen river makes when it's about to betray you—a high, thin singing, like a wineglass rubbed at the rim. I learned it as a girl on the border marches, and I had hoped never to hear it again, because the last time I heard it, three men went into the water and only their sergeant came out, and he was never quite right afterward.

The Vessa was singing now.

"Walk wide," I said to Paloma, for the fourth time that morning. "Wide as you can. The middle of the ice is where the current still runs underneath, and the current is what thins it."

"The middle is the shortest way across," Paloma said. They had developed a habit of arguing with me in the flat, reasonable tone of someone who has spent years copying other people's numbers and believes, with religious conviction, that arithmetic applies to everything.

"And the shortest way across a frozen river is how people end up in the ledger under *drowned, avoidable*."

That shut them up. Scribes respect ledgers.

We had been two days out of Hollowbridge, running north along the river road because Ivo's map—his copy of our map, the traitor's copy, which meant half the winter court probably had one by now—showed a ford where the Vessa bent east toward Greyfell. Two days of hard walking, Paloma going greyer and quieter by the hour. They hadn't slept properly since Hollowbridge. Since Ivo. Since they'd stood in that smuggler's back room with frost-light crawling up their arms and murder in their eyes, and I'd put my hand on their shoulder and said *no*, and they'd listened, and I think listening had cost them more than the magic would have.

Guilt is like that. It doesn't burn quick. It smolders.

I knew the look because I'd worn it myself for six years after Marren fell. You carry a thing like that the way you carry a bad sword arm—you compensate, you favor the other side, and everyone can tell.

So: the river, singing. Grey sky pressing down like a lid. And me with the itch between my shoulder blades that has never once been wrong.

"We should go around," I said. "Through the trees. It'll add half a day."

Paloma looked at me. They'd gotten better at reading me, or maybe I'd gotten worse at hiding. "You heard something."

"I didn't hear anything. That's the problem." I turned a slow circle, scanning the eastern bank—the birches standing bare and white as bone, the drifts unbroken, the road empty to the horizon. Too empty. Two days out of a refugee-choked town, and not one cart, not one beggar, not one child throwing stones at crows. "Where is everybody?"

"Frozen?"

"Frozen doesn't explain the absence of crows." I nodded at the birches. "Crows follow armies and refugees. There are neither. So either we've walked off the edge of the world, or something made everything with wings decide to be elsewhere."

Paloma's breath came out in a long plume. "Hounds," they said quietly. Word travels fast when you sell it cheap.

"Hounds," I agreed. "Or worse. Hounds have handlers."

* * *

We cut inland, into the birchwood, moving fast over the crusty snow. I gave Paloma my pack to slow them down—not out of cruelty, but because a scribe carrying weight walks with purpose, and a scribe walking light starts thinking, and a thinking scribe asks questions like *why are we stopping* at exactly the wrong moment.

It didn't help. Half an hour in, Paloma caught my sleeve.

"Sera. If they're court hunters—if they're *your* court's hunters—they're here for me. Not you. You could—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"You could walk out there and tell them where I am, and they'd take you back. Aldric would—" They stumbled over the name. They'd heard me say it once, drunk on Ivo's terrible plum brandy, and they'd held onto it like a coin found in the street. "She was your commander. She could restore you."

I stopped walking. The birches ticked in the wind, dry branches knocking like teeth.

"Do you know what desertion means in the winter court?" I said. "It doesn't mean running away. Anyone can run away; running away is practically a national pastime above the snowline. Desertion means you were *given an order* and you chose otherwise. It means the court looked at you and said, this one has a will of her own, and wills are dangerous, so we'll make an example." I started walking again, faster. "Aldric doesn't want me back. Aldric wants me brought back—in chains, on her own rope, so the court can see what loyalty costs and what treachery pays. If she offered me reinstatement, it would be bait, and I'd sooner eat my own boots."

"That's very dramatic."

"I'm a knight. Drama is load-bearing."

That got a ghost of a smile out of them, which had been the point. But the smile died fast, because that was when the horn sounded.

One note, low and long, from downriver. Then another, from upstream. Then a third, from ahead of us, deeper in the trees—which meant they'd flanked us, which meant they'd known our line of flight before we did, which meant someone had done arithmetic, and I suddenly understood why Paloma trusted sums.

"Off the road," I said. "Down to the river. Now."

"The river's—"

"The river's the only place they can't surround us. Ice holds six abreast at most, and riders can't charge on it. Move!"

We ran. Behind us, through the trees, I heard the jingle of harness and the crunch of many boots finding a rhythm, and I thought, with the strange calm that used to come over me before battles: *six years, and they finally came.*

* * *

They came onto the ice behind us in a wedge—eight riders leading their horses, walking the animals carefully, and afoot behind them perhaps a dozen more in grey-and-white court livery, spears slung, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have already decided how this ends. At the point of the wedge rode a woman on a black gelding, and even before I saw her face I knew her by her seat in the saddle, by the way she sat a horse the way other people sit thrones.

Aldric Vane. My commander for nine years. The woman who taught me the spear, who pulled me out of the Marren marshes with a rope and her own two hands, who read me the sentence of exile herself because she refused to let anyone else do it. Her face hadn't changed. That was the worst of it. Six years, and the court had left her face alone, as if it were a monument too expensive to renovate.

"Sera Voss." Her voice carried flat across the ice, no echo, the cold eating the sound. "You look terrible."

"Aldric." I stepped in front of Paloma, spear—Ivo's smuggler-grade spear, ash shaft, chipped head—coming up into guard. "You look exactly the same. Don't you ever eat?"

"Discipline." She dismounted, handed her reins to a trooper, and drew her sword with the same unhurried economy she'd always had. Court steel, pattern-welded, older than both of us. "Give me the vessel, Sera. Walk away. I give you my word you'll be treated fairly."

"Your word." I laughed, and it came out uglier than I intended. "Last time I took your word for something, I spent six years shoveling temple muck in a valley where the gods themselves aren't allowed to visit."

"Last time, you broke your oath." She began to walk toward me, boots ringing on the ice, and her troopers spread out behind her in a line, closing the river behind us. Ahead, the ford, and beyond it the far bank, and beyond that Greyfell and the hermit and answers—but between us and all of it, twelve spears. "This time I'm offering you the chance to unbreak it. That's more than you deserve, and we both know it. So let's not pretend this is a negotiation. It's a courtesy. I'm being courteous because I knew your mother."

Something went through me at that, cold and old, but I kept my voice level. "You knew my mother. And you're hunting a child in front of me."

"They're not a child. Look at them." Her eyes flicked past my shoulder, and I felt Paloma trembling there—or not trembling. Shivering. No. Something else. The air around the scribe had begun, very faintly, to shimmer, the way it does over a forge. "Look at what they are, Sera. A weapon that size doesn't get to be innocent. Neither do we. Bring them in, and maybe the court uses them gently. Leave them out here, and they'll crack on their own, and everything within fifty miles cracks with them. Is that mercy? Is that your great redemption?"

And the hell of it, the true hell of it, was that part of me wanted to agree. Part of me had spent six years wanting nothing more than to hand my guilt to someone qualified and walk away clean. I looked at Aldric—at the rope-scar on her jaw from Marren, at the ring she still wore that had belonged to my dead friend Bren—and I felt the old obedience rise in me like floodwater.

Then Paloma said, from behind me, very quietly: "Don't listen to her."

Not *save me*. Not *she's lying*. Just: don't listen. As if my judgment were mine to make. As if I were a person and not a piece on a board.

Well. That decided it, didn't it.

"No," I said.

Aldric sighed, genuinely sad, and raised her sword. "Take the knight alive if it's easy," she told her line. "The vessel undamaged. Go."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting on ice: it is less a battle than an argument with gravity that you are losing from the start. Every lunge slides. Every parry skates. You fight with your knees and your ankles as much as your arms, and the man who stays calm wins, and the man who tries to win fast falls down.

Twelve spears came at us across the Vessa, and I had one smuggler's spear and a scribe, and I was calm, because I had decided somewhere in the last minute that I was going to die here, and that decision is the most restful thing in the world.

"Stay behind me," I said. "If I fall, run for the far bank and don't look back."

"You always say things like that," Paloma said. "Like you're the one in the story."

"I'm the one with the spear. That makes me the one in the story."

The first two troopers reached us together, the way they're trained, one high one low, and I gave the high one my spear-point and the low one a boot to the knee, and the ice did the rest—he went down hard and slid ten feet, and his partner overbalanced reaching for me and I broke his collarbone with the butt-end. Simple. Ugly. Effective. Around us the wedge dissolved into a scrum of sliding men, and for perhaps thirty seconds it was going to work, it was actually going to work—

Then Aldric came through the middle of it like winter itself, and the argument stopped being with gravity.

She fought the way she'd taught me to fight, which meant she fought me, in a sense—every feint was one I'd drilled a thousand times, every counter was a lesson with her voice attached. *Economy, Voss. Never spend a motion you can't afford.* Our blades met and rang and rang again, and the whole world narrowed to her grey eyes and the singing of the ice beneath us, and I thought, absurdly, *the river's been singing this whole time, hasn't it. Singing louder.*

Because Paloma had stopped hiding behind me.

I saw it in flashes, between exchanges—Aldric driving me back a step, a step, another—and each flash showed me more. The scribe standing with arms half-raised, hair lifting in a wind that wasn't blowing, frost-light crawling up their forearms in branching veins like lightning deciding to grow roots. Their eyes had gone pale. Their lips were moving. I don't think they were saying words. I think they were doing arithmetic.

"Voss!" Aldric snarled, pressing me. "Call it off! Call it off NOW—"

"It's not mine to call!" I shouted back, and it was true, and it was the most terrifying sentence I have ever spoken.

The river answered.

The singing rose to a shriek. A crack ran down the length of the Vessa like a whip-lash, white and jagged, splitting the ice from bank to bank—and then the water came *up*. Not a hole opening. The river heaving, bulging, a swell rolling under the ice like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and the surface buckled in plates, and the plates tilted, and men began to slide.

I dropped flat. Old instinct—when the world goes sideways, become small. Aldric dropped too, faster than I did, damn her. All around us, troopers clawed at tilting ice, spears clattering loose and skating away, horses screaming that awful screaming-horse sound. And the water came through the cracks in black ropes, coiling, climbing, wrapping ankles and wrists and dragging, and it was not behaving like water. It was behaving like a hand.

Two of them went under before anyone could reach them. I saw the first go—a young trooper, hardly more than a boy, his fingers scrabbling at the ice edge, and then the plate he held tipped and swallowed him without a splash, the way the river takes things it has decided to keep. The second was a sergeant I half-recognized from the old garrison at Coldford. He looked at me as he went down. That's the part I'll keep. He looked at me, and he didn't cry out, and the water closed over his face like a door.

Then it stopped. All at once, like a held breath released. The ice settled, groaning, into rough new geometry. The wind that wasn't blowing died. And Paloma folded up like a puppet with cut strings, and I caught them before their head hit the frozen river, and their skin was burning hot and grey as ash, and blood was coming from their nose in a thin steady line.

Around us, the surviving troopers scrambled back toward the bank, and Aldric—

Aldric knelt ten feet away in the wreck of her perfect formation, sword still in hand, staring at the black water where her sergeant had gone. When she looked up at me, there was something in her face I'd never seen there in nine years of service and six years of enmity.

Fear.

"Six years ago," she said, hoarse, "you ran from one order. One. And I told the court you were the best soldier I ever trained." She rose slowly, backing toward her horse, toward her remaining men, toward the shore and sanity. "I was wrong. You're the worst thing I ever trained. Because you don't follow orders, Voss—and apparently neither does *that*."

"Go home, Aldric," I said. I was holding Paloma and I couldn't hold a spear too, and we both knew it, and she knew I knew, and she went anyway. Whatever she'd seen on the river had rearranged her arithmetic. "Tell the court what you saw. Tell them the vessel isn't a weapon you can pick up. It's a storm you can only point."

She paused at the bank, one boot on stone. "I'll tell them something better. I'll tell them it's drowning its own protectors." And she was gone into the trees, and the grey-and-white line went with her, and the river lay quiet and guilty under the grey sky, holding two men of the winter court in its cold dark hands.

* * *

I built a fire on the south bank, in the lee of a fallen birch, because Paloma was shaking and blue-lipped and I could not carry them another mile without rest, and because fire is the oldest answer to fear and I needed to ask it something.

Paloma woke once, briefly, eyes unfocused. "Did I—" they started.

"Sleep," I said.

"Did I kill them?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upriver, a crow had finally found its courage and was complaining about the weather.

"Sleep," I said again, gentler, and they went under.

I sat watching the flames and doing my own arithmetic, the kind that doesn't fit in ledgers. Two men dead. Not by my hand, not by Paloma's intent—by the thing inside Paloma that wakes up when we're cornered and solves the problem the fastest way it knows. And the question that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone was the one I'd asked Ivo's back room, the one I'd answered so easily with a hand on a shoulder and a single word: *no*.

Power corrupts exactly this way. I'd said it. I'd believed it. I'd watched it happen to better people than me.

But Ivo had sold us to be flayed for information, and the river had drowned a boy who was only following orders, and I could not, try as I might, find the symmetry in it. One was a choice. The other was a reflex. And if I couldn't tell those apart anymore—if survival was going to keep buying itself with other people's lives—then the corruption wasn't waiting in Paloma's blood.

It was already in mine. It had been since Marren. I'd just been calling it experience.

Paloma stirred against my shoulder, feverish, murmuring fragments—numbers, mostly, ledger-columns of some dream, and once, clearly, a name I didn't recognize: *"Wren."* I filed it away. People's dreams are their own.

Greyfell was a day north. The hermit, the archivist, the truth about the valley and the vessel and whatever Paloma was becoming. A day north, and behind us a commander who had seen the storm and lived, carrying word to a court that would stop sending twelve spears and start sending something else entirely.

I fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb, and I made myself a promise I suspected I wouldn't keep:

Whatever Paloma was turning into, I would not let them turn into it alone.

Even if the next thing the water dragged under was me.
