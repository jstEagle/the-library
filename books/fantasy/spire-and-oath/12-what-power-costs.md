## 12. What Power Costs

The river gave back two bodies at dawn.

I watched them from the bank while Sera built a fire that smoked too much and warmed too little. The hunters floated face-down in the shallows where the current had parked them, their grey cloaks spread around them like wings, like they'd meant to fly somewhere. One of them was the young one—the one who'd hesitated on the ice before the water took him. I kept looking at his hands. They were ordinary hands. Chapped knuckles, a healing cut across one palm. Hands like mine.

I had drowned him with a river.

Not with my hands. With whatever lived in my blood that I hadn't asked for and didn't understand and could not, apparently, be trusted near open water. I sat with my knees pulled to my chest and tried to feel something other than the cold, and mostly failed.

"You should eat," Sera said. She handed me hardtack without ceremony. That was one thing I'd learned about her: she believed ceremony was for people who had time to die slowly.

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat anyway. Grief burns fuel." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *rain falls downward*, and I realized she'd said versions of it before—to soldiers, probably, after battles I couldn't imagine. "It's not a metaphor. It's logistics."

I ate. It tasted like wet chalk, which was an improvement over how everything else tasted that morning.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about magic: it has a smell.

Or maybe it doesn't, and what I smelled was myself burning from the inside—something like hot copper and old paper, like a ledger left too close to a candle. When the power moved through me on the ice, when it flooded out of my chest and into the frozen water and cracked it wide, I had felt *full*. That's the obscene part. In the moment, drowning those men, I had felt like a cup finally being poured into instead of endlessly poured out of. Three years of copying other people's debts in a valley where nothing magical could survive, and then one night the debt collector came to me wearing my own face, and I felt *rich*.

I wanted to be sick thinking about it. I did get sick, once, behind a stand of pines while Sera pretended not to hear. Then we walked.

We walked because Aldric would regroup, and because the river road bent south toward Greyfell, where Sera claimed there was a man who knew things—though when I asked what things, she said, "Old things," in a tone that suggested the conversation was over before it started. So we walked, and I catalogued what I'd done, item by item, like entries in a ledger. Old habit. You can take the scribe off the bond, but the bond stays in the hand.

*Entry the first:* healed Sera's hand. Cost unknown.

*Entry the second:* flooded the river. Cost unknown.

I hated unknowns. Unknowns were how temples got you. Every contract I'd ever copied had fine print, and the fine print always came due later, with interest, and by the time you understood the terms you'd already signed.

* * *

It happened on the second night, in the ruin of a shepherd's hut with a collapsed roof and one wall still standing against the wind. Sera slept first—I insisted; her hand was healing wrong, stiff at the knuckles, and exhaustion made her careless—and I sat up keeping watch over a fire the size of a cupped palm, doing what I always did when I couldn't sleep: reciting things. Lists. The temple's inventory of tallow candles, forty-one gross. The names of the High Scribe's subordinate clerks in order of seniority. My mother's—

My mother's voice.

I reached for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't where your foot remembers. Nothing there. Just a hole shaped like a sound.

I tried again. This is what I do; I am thorough; I once re-copied an entire misbound ledger out of spite. I went backward through what I knew. Her name: Mirei. Her hands, flour to the wrist on baking days. The way she hummed when she thought no one listened. The song itself—gone. Not faded, not muffled. *Gone*, the way a page is gone when someone has cut it cleanly out of a binding, leaving only the ghost of glue and a sliver of the next sheet.

She used to sing me a song about a boat. Or a bird. Something that crossed water. I could remember that it existed. I could remember that I loved it. I could not remember one note of it, or one word, or whether her voice had been high or low, warm or thin, whether she sang like she was happy or like she was remembering.

"Paloma." Sera's voice, low. "You're crying."

"I'm not," I said, which was a lie so obvious that even the fire didn't believe it.

She sat up. She didn't ask what was wrong, which I appreciated, because I didn't have words yet—only the hole. Instead she fed a stick to the flames and waited, because she was very good at waiting. Knights are, I think. Waiting is most of any siege.

"The men in the river," I finally said. "And before that, your hand. Every time I—" I made a gesture that was supposed to mean *the power* and probably looked like I was shooing a chicken. "Something goes missing. Afterward. Small things. Yesterday I couldn't remember which pocket I keep my needle in. Last night I forgot the name of the clerk who trained me—just for a minute, and then it came back, but slow, like wading through deep snow. And now—" My voice did something humiliating. I pushed through it. "My mother sang to me. I know she did. I can't hear it anymore. Any of it. It's not like forgetting. Forgetting is soft at the edges. This is a *cut*."

Sera was quiet for a long time. Firelight moved on her ruined hand, on the scar tissue that the frostbite had left even after my magic had done its violent, half-competent repair.

"There was a mage in the war," she said at last. "Before your time. Before mine, really—I was a child when the last of them were broken. The stories the court told were nonsense, mostly. But one detail stayed. They said the vessels paid for every working out of themselves. Not blood. Not years." She looked at me. "*Memory.* The old ones called it spending. A great working could burn a whole life down to the wick. The small ones took small coins." She paused. "Nobody ever told me what counted as small."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, more wound than humor. "So I'm paying for the river with a lullaby. That's the exchange rate? Two men's lives cost me my mother's voice?"

"That's not how I'd price it."

"How *would* you price it?"

"Honestly?" She turned the stick in the fire. "I'd say the river priced itself. The water was already going to break—we both heard it groaning under all that weight. You just... chose where." She said it carefully, the way you handle a blade you're offering hilt-first. "You didn't murder anyone, Paloma. You saved us. There's a difference, and I've stood on both sides of it, and I'm telling you there's a difference."

But that wasn't the part that frightened me, and I think she knew it wasn't, because she stopped talking and let me find it myself, out loud, in the dark, which is the only place I can ever find anything.

"It's not even the men," I said. "They came to kill us. They'd have killed us. I can grieve that and live with it—that's just... war, or whatever this is. What frightens me is the arithmetic." I held up my hands and looked at them, these traitor hands that had held a quill for eleven years and now held lightning. "If a needle's worth of power costs me a pocket. If saving your hand costs me—I don't even know what it cost me yet, that's the horror, I won't find the gaps until I go reaching for something and fall through. Then what does the Spire cost? Whatever's at the end of this map, whatever everyone is willing to kill each other to control—it'll want to be spent, Sera. It'll pour through me like I'm a channel, and I'll feel *rich* the whole time, right up until there's nothing left of me to feel anything."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind worried at the standing wall.

"And the worst part," I said, quieter now, "is that I can't even miss what I've lost properly. Because to miss it I'd have to remember it, and I don't. I'm grieving a hole. How do you mourn a hole?"

"You don't," Sera said. "You mark it. Like a soldier marks a fallen comrade whose body they never recovered." She said it without softness, which is why I believed her. "You put a stone somewhere. You say a name over it. And then you learn the shape of the absence well enough that nothing else gets taken while you aren't watching."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I did the only thing I know how to do with catastrophe, which is write it down.

I still had the courier's map, wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, and beneath it, folded smaller, three sheets of cheap paper and a nub of charcoal I'd traded for in Hollowbridge before everything went wrong there too. By firelight I began a new kind of ledger. Not debts owed to the temple. Debts owed to *me*—an inventory of everything I could not afford to lose.

*Mirei. Mother. Flour on her wrists. Hummed when she thought no one listened.*

*I cannot recall: the song. Her voice. Whether she was tall. (Ask—no. No one left to ask.)*

That last line I wrote and struck through and wrote again, and the striking-through hurt worse than the loss, because it made it official. Sealed. A record closed by the only clerk authorized to close it.

Then, because grief makes you practical or it makes you nothing, I kept going.

*Sera Voss. Left-handed except with a knife. Sleeps facing the door. Calls hardtack "bread" as a joke she thinks no one notices is a joke. Saved my life four times that I count, possibly more that she wouldn't admit to.*

*Aldric. Her old commander. Hunts us. Wears grief like armor worn so long it's grown into the skin.*

*The valley. The shimmer at the pass. The feeling of waking.*

I filled both sides of all three sheets. Names, details, small stupid precious things—the way Sera swore in three languages when her hand ached, the exact blue-green of the shimmer at the valley's edge, the taste of the temple's terrible winter tea, which I had complained about for eleven years and would apparently walk barefoot through the long winter to drink again. I wrote until the charcoal wore down to a smear and my fingers were black to the second knuckle.

Because here is what I understood, sitting in that roofless hut with my mother's voice already gone and more workings certainly ahead of me: if the magic was going to spend me like coin, then I would become the mint. I would make the record faster than the fire could eat it. Every memory written down was a memory with a copy outside the vault of my skull—a copy that no working could touch. It wasn't a solution. It was a dam of paper against a flood of light. But I had been a scribe my whole miserable bonded life, and if the universe was going to insist that I pay for power in selfhood, then the universe had picked exactly the wrong victim, because I *kept accounts*.

"Writing letters to yourself?" Sera murmured. She wasn't asleep after all. Of course she wasn't.

"Ledger," I said. "Of small debts. Owed to me, payable by me, collected by no one."

She looked at the pages a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes that I couldn't read—pity, maybe, or recognition, or the particular ache of a woman who had once sworn oaths and learned what oaths cost when the institution holding the other end decides to collect.

"Keep two copies," she said finally. "One on your body. One hidden. Never carry both in the same place." And then, before I could ask how she knew to advise that: "Deserters learn paperwork too. The court strips you sentence by sentence. Name, rank, honors. By the end you're a number in a cell. I wrote mine on a wall with a nail for two years." She lay back down. "Walls get left behind. Paper travels."

* * *

We reached the foothills of Greyfell on the fourth day, under a sky the color of dishwater, and that was where the gap opened under me for the second time.

We'd stopped to rest, and Sera asked me—casually, passing me the waterskin—how long I'd known the courier. The dying man at the gate. The one who started all of this.

"A few minutes," I said. "He was dying. It wasn't a long acquaintance."

"No—I mean before. You said once he seemed familiar. That you almost recognized him."

Had I said that? I searched. On the ice, afterward, walking—I remembered telling her, or meaning to tell her, that the courier's face had snagged at something, that there had been a flicker of *I know you* before he died. I remembered the intention of the memory. The memory itself was a doorway painted on a wall.

"I don't know," I said, and my voice came out very steady, which is how I sound when I am most afraid. "I think I knew him. I think I told you that. I can't find it anymore."

Sera went still in the way she did—total stillness, a held breath with a sword attached. Then she looked south, toward the grey shoulder of Greyfell rising out of the mist, where the hermit was supposed to live. The archivist. The exile who knew old things.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

And I stood there in the falling snow with my pockets full of charcoal-stained paper, doing sums I didn't want to finish: two workings, two confirmed losses, and a stranger's face already spent without my ever knowing what it bought. The magic inside me sat quiet and patient as a creditor, and for the first time I understood that it wasn't waiting for me to grow stronger.

It was waiting for me to run out of things worth taking.
