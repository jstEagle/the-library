## 13. The Hermit of Greyfell

**Sera**

The mountain had a name on no map I'd ever been shown, which was fitting, because everything that mattered in my life had lived off the maps.

Greyfell rose out of the treeline like a broken tooth, grey stone streaked with old snow, and halfway up its flank there was a hut with a crooked chimney smoking against the white sky. Smoke meant a fire. A fire meant a person. A person meant either shelter or trouble, and after eleven days of frozen rations and Paloma's worsening silences, I'd have taken either.

"Who lives on a mountain?" Paloma asked. Their voice had gone thin since the river. Since the ice took those two hunters down and didn't give them back. They hadn't said their names aloud, but I'd caught them mouthing them at night like penance, two syllables each, over and over, as if repetition could pay a debt the dead couldn't collect.

"A hermit," I said. "Or a bandit with poor taste in real estate."

"You're very reassuring."

"I'm a knight. Reassurance is extra."

I knocked on the door with the flat of my sword—old habit, keeps your fingers attached—and a voice from inside said, "If you're from the temple, I've been dead for six years and this is a very committed haunting. If you're from the court, I've eaten something disagreeable and you'll catch it. If you're neither, come in before the cold does."

The voice was dry as old vellum and twice as brittle. I pushed the door open.

The man inside was small and folded up in a chair like a letter someone had given up trying to seal. White hair to his shoulders, ink-stained fingers—I knew those stains, knew the particular bruise-purple of temple ink versus the brown-black of common oak gall—and behind him, shelves. Shelves and shelves and shelves, sagging under books, scrolls, folios bound in leather gone soft as cloth. More writing than I'd seen outside the temple's great archive. More than I'd seen anywhere.

Paloma made a sound I'd only heard from them once before, at the sight of the courier's blood on the snow. It was the sound of someone recognizing home in an unexpected place.

"Brotan Aldous," I said slowly, reading the name off a memory twenty years deep. "Third Archivist of the Quiet Valley. You signed the inventory ledgers my escort carried when they brought me down the pass in chains."

The old man's eyes found me, then widened with genuine pleasure, which was somehow worse than fear would have been.

"The desertion case," he said. "Sera Voss. Oh, I *remember* you. Your trial transcript was one of my favorites. Terrible thing to say about a person's life, but there it was—the clerk who wrote it down kept crossing out her own adjectives because you kept making them sound insufficient." He waved us toward stools. "Sit. Tea's terrible but hot. And you—" his gaze slid to Paloma, and everything pleasant drained out of his face like water through cracked clay, "—you must be the reason the mountain birds have been flying in circles for a week."

Paloma went still. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Brotan said, pouring tea with hands that trembled only slightly more than Paloma's did, "that magic has a smell, child, if you know what to smell for. And you reek of it. Sit down. Both of you. What I have to tell you is best absorbed seated, because standing people tend to run, and running people tend to die on my doorstep, and I've only just gotten the last stain out."

**Paloma**

He talked the way scribes read aloud when they think no one important is listening—flatly, precisely, without mercy.

"The valley was not always silent," Brotan said. "Before the Covenant, the Quiet Valley was called the Verdant Vale, and it was the richest wellspring in the northern reaches. Every mage of consequence trained there. The Spire drew power from it the way a mill draws from a river. Then the war came—the real war, the one your histories call the Border Disputes, forty years of it compressed into a footnote—and when it ended, the victors faced a problem."

"Which was?" Sera asked.

"What to do with all the weapons." He sipped his terrible tea. "You can't unmake magic, Knight Voss. Power doesn't die; it only changes hands or goes to sleep. The Covenant chose sleep. They built a barrier around the valley—a working so large it isn't a spell anymore, it's a *law*, stitched into the ground itself. Inside the boundary, magic cannot exist. Cannot form, cannot flow, cannot wake. They didn't banish the mages. They buried the wellspring and salted the earth above it, and they told everyone who moved in afterward that the valley had always been quiet. That stillness was natural. That it was even holy."

My mouth was dry. "The Temple teaches that the valley is blessed. That the gods emptied it of sorcery to keep us pure."

"The Temple teaches what the Covenant paid it to teach," Brotan said, not unkindly. "Someone had to maintain the barrier. Someone had to keep the ledger of the working—renewals, repairs, the slow arithmetic of keeping a law alive. Who better than an order of scribes? We were never priests, child. We were *wardens*. Every ledger you ever copied, every column of figures that meant nothing—that was maintenance. You were turning the gears of a prison and calling it prayer."

I thought of the ledgers. Thousands of them. The way the numbers never resolved to anything, the way the High Scribe grew angry whenever anyone asked what the columns *were for*. Three years left on my bond. I had counted the days like a prisoner counts bars, and the whole time I had been one of the bars.

"And the courier?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "The man who died at the gate. He wore temple insignia from *outside*."

Brotan set down his cup. "There are temples beyond the valley, Paloma. Branch houses. The order has always had hands on both sides of the barrier—one set to maintain the prison, one set to watch for escapees. Or for the day the prison stops being enough." He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something, watch the decision cost him. "You should understand what you are. Not what the map says. What *you* are."

"I'm a scribe. Debt-bound. My parents sold me to the temple when I was six because the winter took the crops and—"

"Your parents," Brotan said gently, "were temple servants assigned to a farmstead outside Hollowbridge. You were born inside the barrier's shadow because that is where they were ordered to be. You were not sold to the temple, child. You were *placed*. There is a difference between a debt and a duty, and you have spent your whole life paying the wrong one."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves.

"Placed," Sera repeated. Her hand had drifted to her sword hilt—not drawing, just resting there, the way she did when the world needed holding down. "Explain that word very carefully, archivist."

"The vessels," Brotan said. "When the Covenant sealed the valley, they could not destroy the great workings—they could only bind them, and bindings need anchors. Living anchors. Blood that carries the covenant-mark, bodies that can hold a working the way a lamp holds flame. They made seven. Vessels. Children raised inside the silence where their own power could never wake, never be found, never be used—until such time as the Covenant needed them." He met my eyes. "Six are dead. Illness, accidents, one drowning that I have never believed was an accident. You are the seventh. The last. The courier who reached your gate was a branch-house archivist named Denner, and he spent the last four years of his life making sure the winter court never learned where you slept. He failed by three days. But he got the map to you, and the map got you here, and here—" he spread his ink-stained hands, "—is the only place left where you might learn to survive what you are instead of being spent by it."

I don't remember standing. I remember the room tilting, Sera's hand catching my elbow, the tea going cold in its cup.

"My mother," I said. "Her name was Maren. She sang while she worked. She—"

"Was a servant of the Covenant doing her duty," Brotan said, "and I believe, from everything I read before I left, that she loved you. Duty and love are not enemies, whatever the temple taught you. But the placement was not hers to refuse. None of it was yours to refuse. That is rather the whole horror of the Covenant, child—it built a world where nothing needed to be cruel, and everything came out cruel anyway."

**Sera**

I had heard confessions before. I had given one, once, at my trial, in a voice that didn't sound like mine. But I had never watched someone receive the confession of their entire life secondhand, and I hope never to again.

Paloma sat down on the floor. Not a stool—the floor, back against a shelf, knees up, arms wrapped around them like they were holding a door shut against something enormous. I crouched next to them because there wasn't anything useful to say and because staying close was at least a kind of sentence.

"They gave me a name," Paloma said finally. "At the temple. They said my parents hadn't named me yet, that I was too young, so the temple named me. Paloma. Dove. Peace." A laugh came out of them with no humor in it at all. "They named me *peace* and put me in a cage and called the cage a blessing."

"You were an infant," I said. "You wouldn't have remembered any other name."

"That's not the point." Their eyes came up, and they were wet and furious and the most awake I'd ever seen them. "The point is they decided. Before I could walk, before I could speak, before I was anything at all—they decided what I was for. My whole life. Every ledger, every beating, every year I counted down like it was freedom waiting on the other side of my bond—none of it was mine. Even my *escape*—" their voice cracked, "—even running, even the river, even this. Was I ever choosing anything, Sera? Or was I just following a script some archivist wrote before I had teeth?"

I looked at Brotan. He looked back at me with the weary patience of a man who had asked himself harder versions of the same question for thirty years.

"For what it's worth," I said, "the script didn't include me. Or the courier dying at your gate. Or Ivo selling you out, or Aldric hunting us across a frozen river. Whatever they planned, it's been coming apart since the moment you opened that sealed letter. That's not obedience, Paloma. Obedient vessels don't get hunted by three separate powers. They get *collected*."

Something in their face shifted—not comfort, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone checking a lock and finding it broken in their favor.

Brotan cleared his throat. "There is more, and it is worse, and you may throw things at me afterward. The barrier is failing."

I straightened. "Failing how?"

"Slowly, then quickly. It was built to hold for a thousand years and it has held for nine hundred and change, but bindings decay, and the anchor-points decay faster, and every vessel that dies weakens the lattice. Six dead, Knight Voss. The valley's edge you crossed—you felt the shimmer?"

"We felt it," Paloma said quietly. "It felt like walking through cold water."

"That was a seam. There are dozens now. Within ten years—five, if the court learns to push—the barrier fails entirely, the wellspring wakes, and every power in the north descends on the Quiet Valley to fight over what rises out of it. The temple wants its empire. The winter court wants its weapon. The refugees want a homeland. And the Spire—" he tapped a shelf of books behind him, spines worn blind, "—the Spire wants a vessel. It has always wanted a vessel. It is patient in a way that makes courts look hasty."

"So we run farther," I said. "South. Over the sea. There are places—"

"There are no places," Brotan said simply. "There is only distance, and distance runs out. You cannot outrun what you *are*, child. Believe me. I tried for six years and all I managed was a very good view of a mountain." He leaned forward, and for the first time the dry irony fell away entirely, and underneath it he looked ancient and frightened and honest. "But there is a third road. Not the court's road, not the temple's. The Spire itself. If a vessel reaches the heart of the covenant-working with knowledge instead of ignorance—with *choice* instead of command—the working can be remade. Renewed properly, or ended cleanly. Sealed forever, or unmade. Either way, done by consent. Done by *you*, rather than done *to* you. It is the only version of this story where anyone gets to write their own line."

"And the price?" Paloma asked. There it was—the scribe's instinct, always asking what the entry costs.

Brotan didn't answer right away. He looked at the fire, and at his books, and at something a long way past both.

"The working is held together by memory," he said at last. "That's what anchors are, in the end—not blood, not body. *Remembering*. Whoever goes into that heart will spend themselves to remake it. Not die, perhaps. But be... edited. Down to whatever remains when the account is settled." He met Paloma's eyes. "I won't dress it. The Covenant built you to be spent. The question in front of you—the only question that has ever actually been yours—is whether you spend yourself *on purpose*."

Silence. Wind. The fire eating its fuel the way fires do, without apology.

Then Paloma laughed—short, cracked, real—and said, "You know what the worst part is? Three years ago I got beaten half-senseless for reading a sealed letter. One letter. And now the entire shape of my life turns out to have been a sealed letter, addressed to me, that everyone else had already read."

"Welcome to the archive," Brotan said. "We keep the truth in the restricted section. It's safer there. Usually."

I was about to ask him what he meant by *usually* when the dogs started.

Not his dogs—he had none. Wild dogs, maybe, or wolves driven down by the early winter. But I'd been a soldier long enough to know that animals fleeing downhill in a straight line aren't fleeing predators. They're fleeing *people*. I went to the window, and far below, where Greyfell's slope met the forest road, torchlight moved in formation. Not a hunting party's scattered lights. Ranks. Discipline.

And at the head of the column, riding a grey courser with the easy seat of a man who had never once doubted a saddle, a figure in winter-court blue whose silhouette I would have known in the dark, in death, in the next world.

Aldric.

"He followed us," I said. My voice came out flat, which was good, because underneath it everything was screaming. "He's known our route since Hollowbridge. Maybe since the river." I turned to Paloma, and Brotan, and the shelves full of truths that had cost a lifetime to collect. "How many ways off this mountain?"
