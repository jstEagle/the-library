## 14. Aldric's Offer

I have been captured before. Twice, if you count the wedding I attended at nineteen where the groom's family objected to my presence on professional grounds. But being captured *well*—that was new.

They didn't drag me. They didn't even bind me at first. They simply closed around me on the road below Greyfell like a hand closing around a moth, polite and inevitable, and Aldric Vane dismounted and said, "Voss. You look terrible," in exactly the tone she'd used across a decade of shared campfires, and my body—the traitorous, well-trained thing—almost saluted.

That was the worst part. Not the six hunters in grey furs with drawn bows. Not the knowledge that Paloma was half a mile behind me at the hermit's cottage, asleep, trusting me to walk the perimeter and come back. The worst part was that hearing Aldric say my name felt like coming home, and I hated how much of me leaned toward it.

"Commander," I said. Old habit. She'd stopped being my commander the day I deserted, but titles die harder than men.

"Aldric," she corrected, as she always had. "We're not on duty."

"We're always on duty. That's rather the whole disease of us."

She smiled. Gods help me, she smiled, and it was the same crooked smile she'd worn when we'd stolen wine from the quartermaster's stores at fifteen, when we'd buried her first horse together in the rain, when she'd stood beside me at my oath-taking and gripped my shoulder hard enough to bruise because that was how Vane women said *I'm proud of you*.

Six hunters. Bows loose but strung. Snow falling in that fat, deliberate way it does when winter means business. And somewhere behind me, a scribe with borrowed magic sleeping in a cottage that smelled of old paper and older regrets.

"Walk with me," Aldric said.

"I'd rather not."

"You'd rather not do a great many things, Sera. You deserted rather than do them." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "Walk anyway."

So I walked. Because the alternative was making six archers decide things, and because—and this is the confession I'll carry to whatever grave I earn—I wanted to hear what she had to say. Some part of me, some starved garrison-corner of me, had been waiting three years for someone from the old life to say my name without spitting.

* *

The road below Greyfell runs along a frozen stream, and we walked beside it while the snow came down and the hunters followed at a distance that pretended not to be an escort. Aldric walked the way she always had: like the ground owed her money and she intended to collect.

"Do you know what they're calling you back at court?" she asked.

"Traitor has a certain classic ring."

"*The Oathbreaker.* With capitals. There are songs." She glanced sideways at me. "Bad ones. You rhyme with 'loss,' which the court poets find irresistible."

"Charming. And you've ridden two weeks through the long winter's teeth to quote poetry at me?"

"I've ridden to make you an offer." She stopped walking. The stream muttered under its ice, and somewhere up-slope a branch cracked under snow weight, loud as a musket. "Full reinstatement. Rank restored—captain, actually; Havel died last spring and no one else wants the border command. Pardon sealed by the Queen Regent's own hand. Your name struck from every bounty roll from here to the salt coast."

I kept my face still. It's the one skill the army taught me properly.

"And in exchange for this miracle of paperwork?"

"You bring us the vessel. Alive. Unharmed." A pause, precise as a surgeon's knife finding the gap between ribs. "Willingly, ideally. The court would prefer the vessel cooperative. Dead vessels are notoriously poor weapons—we learned that at Cael Marsh, though the histories skip over it."

The cold went through me then, real cold, the kind that starts in the understanding and works outward. "You know what the vessel is."

"I know what the court believes it is. A key with legs. A weapon that walks and talks and can be pointed." She turned to face me fully, and I saw that she was tired—not the performative tiredness of commanders angling for sympathy, but the deep, geological tiredness of someone who has been carrying something heavy for years and has stopped expecting to set it down. "Sera. The long winter is coming early. You've seen it. Everyone's seen it. The harvests are failing from here to the coast, the rivers aren't freezing right, and the court's diviners are reading omens in their own tea leaves because the proper instruments have all gone dark. The court believes the vessel is the answer. The only answer. Deliver it, and the winter ends—or so they claim—and everything you burned down at your desertion gets unburned."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take you back in chains, and my hunters collect the vessel anyway, and you get to watch whatever happens next from a cell." She said it gently. That was the obscenity of it—she said it the way you tell a patient about the amputation, kindly, because kindness is what's left when mercy has left the building. "I'd rather not. You know I'd rather not."

And gods forgive me, I believed her. That was the trap of Aldric Vane: she never lied to you. She just arranged the truth into shapes that did the lying for her.

* *

We stood by the frozen stream and I made myself ask the question, because there was a version of me—one I recognized, one I'd been trying to drown for three years—that genuinely wanted the answer.

"The pardon. Is it real? Sealed? In your saddlebag right now?"

"It's real." She didn't reach for it. Smart. If she'd produced it, I might have touched it, and if I touched it, some animal part of me might have refused to let go. "Parchment, wax, ribbon, the Regent's signet. It even forgives the *manner* of your leaving, which took some doing, because what you did at Thornholt was—" she exhaled through her nose, "—it was spectacular, Sera. You didn't just desert. You humiliated the Fourth Column in front of the northern levies. Half the court wanted you drawn and quartered. The other half wanted to buy you dinner."

"I've had worse reviews."

"So has everyone who ever told the truth about a bad order." Her voice dropped, and for a moment the commander fell away and it was just Aldric, twenty-nine years old, frost in her eyelashes, standing in the snow beside a dead stream talking to the ghost of her oldest friend. "Thornholt broke something in me too. You know that. I signed the report that covered it up. My name is on the lie, and it has been on the lie for three years, and every morning I wake up and it's still there." She looked at the ice. "Bring the vessel in. End the winter. And maybe—maybe—things like Thornholt stop happening. Maybe the machine gets better. That's what I tell myself at night. Give me something better than what I tell myself at night."

There it was. Not a blade at my throat—a mirror. She knew me too well for blades. She knew that I had lain awake in a hundred barracks hating myself for the order I'd refused and the friends I'd abandoned and the coward's arithmetic of my survival, and she stood in the snow offering me absolution with a bowstring attached.

For perhaps ten heartbeats, I wanted it. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left. Ten heartbeats where I saw the parchment, the seal, the cell door opening, my name made clean again, and all it would cost was one sleeping scribe with shaking hands and a map hidden in their coat.

Ten heartbeats. Then I thought of Paloma asking me, three nights ago, in the dark of the hermit's cottage, in a voice small enough to fit inside a teacup: *"If I'm a weapon, whose hand is holding me?"*

Nobody had ever asked me that. The army certainly hadn't. The army assumed the hand was self-evident.

"No," I said.

Aldric closed her eyes briefly, like a woman receiving news she'd already read in the dispatches. "No to the offer, or no to the arrest?"

"That depends entirely on your hunters' marksmanship."

"Sera—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't do the speech where you explain that you're doing this for my own good. Don't do the speech where the court's need becomes the realm's need becomes God's need. I wrote those speeches. I delivered them to deserters in the stockade, and I watched their faces while I did it, and I remember exactly what it felt like to be reasonable at someone while their world ended." The snow came down between us. "Paloma Nakamura is not a weapon. They're a person who was put in a cage as an infant by people who are all, presumably, very sorry about it in official memoranda. I spent three years learning what it costs to be the instrument of someone else's necessity. I will not sell them into it. Not for a captaincy. Not for a crown. Not for a pardon written in the Regent's own blood."

Aldric studied me for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off, and that, more than anything, told me how much this cost her—Aldric brushed snow off. Aldric maintained equipment, posture, appearances, always.

"You've changed," she said finally.

"You haven't. That's the tragedy of both of us."

Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, or its military equivalent—and then the commander came back up like a visor dropping. "Take her," she said quietly, to the hunters. "Gently. If she resists, don't kill her. The court wants her breathing. Apparently she's worth more alive." A pause, and the ghost of the old crooked smile, aimed at me like an apology fired from a siege engine. "Told you the poets found you irresistible."

Six bows came up. Six arrows found me in the grey light.

I could have fought. I want that recorded too: I considered fighting, ran the arithmetic, and lost. Six against one, on open ground, with Paloma half a mile away and asleep. If I died here, nothing stood between the court and the cottage but a scribe's borrowed magic and a hermit's bad knees. So I let them take the sword off my hip, and I let them bind my wrists with cord instead of iron—Aldric's order, that; she remembered I hate iron—and I let them march me up the road, away from Greyfell, away from the cottage, away from the sleeping person whose name I had just made myself responsible for.

But I did one thing first. As they turned me, as the hunters closed in, I looked back down the road—at nothing, at snow, at the empty track winding toward the hermit's ridge—and I said, clearly, loudly, in the flat parade-ground voice that carries:

"COTTAGE'S EAST WINDOW HAS THE LOOSE SHUTTER. FIX IT BEFORE THE FROST GETS IN."

Nonsense to anyone listening. An old soldier's grumble about carpentry. But Paloma, if awake, if watching, if clever—and Paloma was all three more often than anyone gave them credit for—would know what it meant. *East window. They're taking me east. Run west.*

Aldric heard it. Of course she did. She rode beside me for the first mile without speaking, and then, without looking at me, said: "Loose shutter."

"Dreadful state of the local architecture."

"You told them to run."

"I told them to mind the property. I'm very house-proud these days."

She sighed, a long white plume of breath, and for a moment she sounded less like the winter court's hound and more like the girl who'd buried a horse with me in the rain. "They'll catch them, Sera. You know that. The court doesn't send six hunters for a scribe. It sends six for the escort."

"Then I hope your hunters enjoy the weather."

"They won't." She nudged her horse forward. "Neither will you. The Regent's dungeons are warm, at least. Small mercies."

* *

They bound me that night in a way that was almost respectful—wrists in front, blanket provided, fire built close enough to feel. Aldric's orders again. She sat across the fire from me while the hunters slept in shifts, and for a long time neither of us spoke, and the silence had ten years of friendship in it, which is heavier than any chain.

"Why?" she asked eventually. "Not the noble answer. The true one. Three years ago you threw away everything—rank, name, honor, home—for a principle. Principles cool, Sera. I've watched it happen a hundred times. What keeps yours lit?"

I looked at the fire. I thought about lying, and then I thought about how tired I was of lies, mine included.

"Because I met someone worse off than me," I said. "Someone who never got to choose anything. Not their name, not their home, not their work, not their god. Everything chosen *for* them, by institutions that called it care." I looked up at her. "And I realized that's what I was. A well-treated instrument. The difference between me and Paloma is that my cage had a view."

Aldric fed a stick to the fire. Her face did something complicated in the light.

"At Thornholt," she said quietly, "the order was wrong. I've known it for three years. I signed the cover-up anyway, because the alternative was becoming you, and I wasn't brave enough." She looked at me across the flames, and for one unguarded moment my oldest friend looked out of the winter court's commander like a prisoner looking out of a window. "Was it worth it? All of it? The losing?"

I thought of Paloma's hands steadying as they learned to hold power instead of being held by it. I thought of a hermit's cottage, bad tea, a scribe laughing for the first time since I'd known them. I thought of the shutter, the east window, and prayed to every indifferent god that they were running.

"Not yet," I said. "Ask me again when they're safe."

She nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd already suspected, and rose to check the pickets, and left me bound by a warm fire under the falling snow—captured, compromised, and more certain than I had been since the day I deserted.

Because here is what Aldric never understood, and what the court will never understand, and what I only understood standing in the snow with a pardon waiting in her saddlebag like a bribe from heaven:

You cannot offer a person their chains back and call it freedom. Even gilded. Especially gilded.

Somewhere west of us, a scribe was running. And I had given them the only thing I had left to give: a direction, and a head start, and the knowledge that whatever came for them next would have to go through the memory of me first.

I just hoped—prayed, argued, bargained with the dark—that it would be enough.

It was near dawn when one of the pickets shouted from the treeline, and the camp came up out of its blankets with steel already drawn, and I knew, with the terrible clarity of the truly doomed, that the weather had found us after all.
