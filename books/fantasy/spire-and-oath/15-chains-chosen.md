## 15. Chains Chosen

I want it on the record—my record, the one I keep in my head now that I trust no ledger—that I did not cry when I saw the bounty notice. I merely stood in the doorway of a tannery shed outside Greyfell and read my own face rendered in cheap ink, and felt something in my chest go very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone you love says your name in a certain tone.

*WANTED BY ORDER OF THE TEMPLE OF THE QUIET VALLEY*, the placard said. *PALOMA NAKAMURA. DEBT-BOND SUSPENDED. TO BE TAKEN ALIVE.*

Alive. That was the detail that should have comforted me. It didn't. The temple had never once in my twenty-two years wanted anything alive that it couldn't use.

Below the notice, in smaller print, was a second placard, older paper, court-stamped with a snowflake sigil: *SERA VOSS. DESERTER. DELIVER TO THE WINTER COURT.*

And beneath both, in a hand I knew too well because I had spent three years copying its elegant, bloodless loops into temple ledgers—the High Scribe's own hand—a note pinned for whoever tore these down:

*The vessel travels with the deserter. The deserter has been offered terms.*

I read it four times. The words didn't change. That's the trouble with words; they're stubborn things, they hold their shape no matter how hard you wish them soft.

Sera had been gone since dawn. *Scouting the river road*, she'd said. She'd kissed my forehead—she had started doing that somewhere past Hollowbridge, casually, like it cost nothing—and she'd taken the longer knife instead of the shorter one, and I hadn't thought anything of it because I am, apparently, an idiot who deserves everything that happens to him.

*Terms.* You don't offer terms to someone you intend to fight.

* * *

Let me tell you what surrender looks like when you choose it, because I've seen the other kind, on the faces of refugees at Hollowbridge, and mine looked nothing like theirs.

I walked back to the crossroads where the enforcers' fires were visible through the trees—temple fires, banked low and disciplined, nothing like the sprawling chaos of the court hunters—and I took off Sera's spare sword belt, which I had been wearing like a child wearing his father's coat, and I laid it on a stone. Then I sat down on the frozen ground and waited, hands open on my knees, palms up, the way we knelt for inspection in the scriptorium.

It took them half an hour to find me. Enforcers are thorough but not clever; they search the way ledgers balance, line by line, expecting the world to cooperate.

There were six of them. Their captain was a woman named Brecht whom I had last seen beating a kitchen boy for stealing honey, and her face when she saw me sitting there was genuinely puzzled, which I found almost funny.

"Nakamura," she said.

"You're looking for me."

"We're looking for—" She stopped. Recalculated. "You're supposed to run."

"I'm aware." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "I'm not going to. Take me back."

She looked at my empty hands, then at the sword belt on the stone, then at my face, hunting for the trick. There wasn't one. That was the whole point. A trick is something you do when you still believe there's a version of this where you win.

"Where's Voss?" one of the others asked.

"Gone," I said, and it wasn't even a lie, and the word went through me like a splinter working deeper. "You'll find her or you won't. It doesn't matter anymore."

Brecht cuffed my wrists with iron that hummed faintly against my skin—I would learn later that suppression iron does that only to people like me, people who are *something*—and pulled me up by the collar.

"For what it's worth," she said gruffly, "the High Scribe said you'd come in on your own eventually. Said you were a scribe first and a runaway second. Obedience in the bone."

The worst part, the part I keep turning over like a stone with something crawling under it, is that she meant it as a compliment.

* * *

They did not take me back through the pass. They took me south, down out of the hills, to a convoy waiting on the low road: two wagons, thirty enforcers, and a black-lacquered carriage with the Quiet Valley's seal—a valley between two mountains, painted in silver—on its door.

A prison wagon, essentially, but a polite one. Everything about the temple was polite. That was always the horror of it, if you grew up inside it the way I did: the violence arrived dressed as procedure, the cruelty wore a clerk's neat handwriting, and you could be destroyed entirely without anyone ever raising their voice.

They put me in the carriage alone. There was a desk bolted to the floor. Of course there was. There was ink, and paper, and a lamp, because the High Scribe could not imagine a circumstance in which I might not want to be useful.

He came to me an hour after dark.

I had prepared myself for him. I had spent the ride composing little speeches, dignified ones, bitter ones. And then the door opened and he stepped in—small, grey-robed, spectacles catching the lamplight, smelling of the same cedar-and-vinegar ink I had bathed in my whole life—and every speech fell out of me at once.

"Paloma." He sat across the desk as though we were about to review quarterly grain totals. "You look terrible. Have you been eating?"

"You sold me to the winter court," I said. Wait. No. That wasn't— "No. Sorry. Let me start over. *Did* you sell me to the winter court? Because the notice said—"

"The notice was bait." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried. "For Voss. Yes, I'm aware you saw it; yes, I'm aware of what it implied. Deception is a tool, Paloma, not a sin. You of all people should appreciate that—you hid a document in a ledger binding for six days before we found it. Good workmanship, incidentally. I said so at the time."

"You beat me for it."

"I corrected you for it." He set the spectacles back on. Behind them his eyes were the same mild, bottomless grey as always. "You seem to be under the impression that something has changed between us. That you ran, and therefore became a different person, and I became a different person, and now we meet as enemies. But nothing has changed. You are still my scribe. You are still indentured—your bond was never dissolved, merely suspended. And I am still responsible for you." He folded his hands. "So let us dispense with the theatrics and discuss what happens next."

"Where's Sera?"

"Ah." Something flickered behind the mildness—not quite a smile. "Yes. Voss. I imagine right now she's standing in front of Commander Aldric, negotiating. The court offered her reinstatement, full pardon, her name restored to the rolls. All she has to do is deliver you. I expect she'll take it. Deserters generally do; guilt makes people terribly predictable." He tilted his head. "Does that distress you?"

"No," I lied.

"Liar," he said pleasantly. "But it doesn't matter whether she takes the bargain, because the court will never receive you. Neither will anyone else. You are temple property, Paloma, and the temple has finally remembered what it owns."

The lamp guttered. Or maybe it didn't; maybe that was just the floor tilting under my understanding of things.

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said, "that you have been asking the wrong question your entire journey. You and Voss and even that fossil Herrod at Greyfell—you've all been telling yourself a story about the valley. That it's a prison. That the barrier exists to keep magic *out*. To protect the world from what's inside." He shook his head, gently, the way one corrects a promising student. "Ask instead why an order of scribes—an order of *accountants*, Paloma, we are bookkeepers, that is our whole sacred mystery—would spend nine hundred years maintaining the largest working of magic on the continent. Ask what a barrier that size costs to run. Ask who pays."

I couldn't speak. My mouth had gone dry as old vellum.

"Magic cannot exist in the Quiet Valley," he continued, in the tone of a man reciting scripture, "because the valley *eats* it. Every hour of every day, the barrier drinks the raw stuff of the world and refines it, and stores it, and we have been sitting on nine centuries of the distillate like misers sitting on gold." He leaned forward. "And do you know what the barrier is made of? What holds it? Not stone. Not wards. *Blood.* Vessel blood. One vessel per generation, seeded into the valley as infants, raised ignorant, raised obedient, their lives poured silently into the work without ever knowing they were paying for it."

"My mother," I said. The words came out flat, strange. "The woman who raised me. Was she—"

"A vessel, yes. Spent by forty. Yours truly signed the expenditure reports." He said it without flinching, without anything. "You were the reserve. The last of the line. We kept you copying ledgers because a mind occupied with debt figures asks fewer questions, and because—forgive the poetry—it amused me, keeping the key to the empire filing its own chains."

"The empire," I repeated.

"The valley is coming down, Paloma. Not today, not this year—but soon, and *on our terms*. When it does, the temple walks out holding nine hundred years of refined power while the winter court and every hedge-witch and warlord on the continent stands outside the shimmer with empty hands. We will not need armies. We will need clerks." He smiled, small and genuine, and it was the most frightening thing I had ever seen. "Your blood reopens the barrier from the inside. That is all I require of you. A few pints, drawn properly, over some months. You'll live. You may even find it meaningful. You'll be the man who ended the age of hiding."

"And everyone the barrier falls on," I said. "Every village in the borderlands. Every refugee camp. Magic comes back all at once after nine hundred years, into a world already burning toward war—"

"Yes," he agreed. "There will be disorder. There is always disorder during a transition of power. We have contingency plans. We have very good plans, Paloma; I wrote most of them myself." He rose, smoothing his robe. "You'll be kept comfortable until we reach the valley. Ink and paper, if you like. I know how you settle your nerves."

He was at the door when I finally found my voice, and it didn't sound like despair anymore. It sounded like something colder, and I barely recognized it as mine.

"You said the barrier eats magic," I said. "Refines it. Stores it."

He paused. "Yes."

"And I'm a vessel. I *hold* it. That's what vessels do." I watched him in the lamplight, this small grey man who had filed my entire life under *expenditures*. "Then you haven't thought it through, sir. You've never thought anything through, because you've never once imagined that the thing in the cage could read."

His head turned, slowly.

"Nine hundred years," I said, "is a great deal of power to leave lying next to someone with nothing left to lose."

He studied me for a long moment. Then, mildly, horribly, he smiled again.

"There he is," the High Scribe said. "There's the vessel." And he locked the door behind him, and left me alone with the lamp, and the ink, and the beginning of a plan.
