## 16. Sera's Choice

There is a particular flavor of humiliation in being escorted by your own people. I had imagined capture a hundred times over the years—crossbow bolts, a blade in the dark, the cold dignity of dying with my sword out. I had never once imagined being walked through the snow at Aldric Vane's elbow like a dog that had wandered home and needed fetching.

"Keep up," she said, without looking at me.

"I'm keeping up," I said. "I'm also hating it. The two are not mutually exclusive."

Her jaw tightened. Good. Somewhere under that polished commander's mask there was still a person who remembered me, and I intended to irritate her until she surfaced.

The temple convoy stretched along the frozen road below Greyfell: six wagons, forty enforcers in their grey-and-white livery, and one iron-barred cage on wheels where Paloma Nakamura sat with their wrists bound and their face arranged into an expression of perfect, terrible calm. I knew that expression. It was the face of someone who has stopped hoping and started planning, and I did not think the plan involved us both walking away.

They'd taken Sera's sword, of course—my sword, the one I'd carried since I was nineteen—and replaced my cloak with a blanket that smelled of wet wool and defeat. They'd left my hands free because Aldric had ordered it, which told me everything about how much she trusted me, which was exactly as far as she could throw a horse.

"You could have said no," she said quietly as we walked.

"To what? You had four hunters behind me and a crossbow trained on my spine. I've read enough battle reports to know how that arithmetic ends."

"You always were good at arithmetic."

"And you were always bad at lying to me," I said. "So let's skip to the part where you tell me what the court actually wants with them."

She was silent for ten paces. Twenty. The snow creaked under forty pairs of boots.

"Aldric."

"The vessel is to be delivered intact to the Winter Court," she said finally, in the flat voice of a woman reciting orders she wished she'd never heard. "Alive. Unspoiled. The Spire answers to its bloodline, and Her Majesty intends to be standing next to it when it opens."

"And after?"

"After, the vessel will be... maintained."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me ugly and loud, and a couple of the younger enforcers flinched. "Maintained. Like a well. Like a broodmare. You know what they did to the last vessels, Aldric? The hermit told us. They hollowed them out and used the shells for kindling."

"I know what war costs," she said, and for just a moment her voice cracked down the middle. "I have paid it. You paid more. That is why I am offering you this, Sera. Deliver them, stand before the court, and every charge against you dies. Your name restored. Your oath restored. Everything they took from you at Hollowbridge—" she gestured vaguely at my ruined reputation, my exile, seven years of sleeping in ditches, "—returned."

I looked at her. Really looked. There was frost in her eyelashes and something desperate underneath the discipline, and I understood, with a lurch, that she genuinely believed she was offering me salvation.

"That's the thing about oaths," I said. "You all keep treating mine like a coat I misplaced. Something that can be handed back if I say the right words to the right people." I shook my head. "My oath wasn't to the crown, Aldric. It was to the people behind me. And right now the person behind me is sitting in a cage because I hesitated."

* * *

I want it recorded—I want *something* recorded, even if it's only this—that I did not decide lightly.

I spent the afternoon doing what soldiers do before a bad night: checking knots, counting boots, memorizing the rotation of the watch. The convoy made camp in a bowl beneath the pass, wagon circle tight, fires low. Temple discipline. Efficient, predictable, and built on the assumption that everyone inside it wanted to go home alive.

That assumption was going to cost them.

Paloma saw me coming to the cage and stood, slow, hands still bound. Up close they looked awful—hollowed, sleepless, a bruise blooming along one jaw where an enforcer had been enthusiastic during the surrender. But their eyes were clear. Clearer than mine felt.

"They offered you a pardon," they said. Not a question.

"They offered me a parade."

"And you're here instead. Why?"

I gripped the bars. "Because I spent seven years learning exactly how much a person can lose before they stop being a person, and I'll be damned if I watch it happen to you while I collect my reward."

Something moved behind their eyes—hope, maybe, or the memory of it, stiff from disuse. "Sera. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong."

"Liar."

"Obviously," I said. "But I'm a knight. Lying is one of the few courtly arts I mastered."

* * *

Ivo found me at the second watch.

He should not have been anywhere near that camp. He was supposed to be three towns south, spending whatever he'd been paid for selling our route, drinking himself stupid on blood money. Instead he was crouched in the shadow of the supply wagon wearing a stolen enforcer cloak two sizes too small, grinning at me like sin itself.

"You're supposed to be gone," I hissed.

"Yeah, funny thing about that." He scratched under the borrowed hood. "Sold your route, collected my coin, sat down with the money, and couldn't drink a drop of it. Kept seeing the scribe's face when they figured out what I'd done. Kept thinking, *that's the look of someone who trusted me, and I am a man who has never once deserved trusting.*" He shrugged. "Turns out I hate myself sober. Who knew."

"Ivo—"

"So here's the arrangement." He talked fast, the way he did when honesty embarrassed him. "The temple boys are expecting trouble from the winter court, not from inside. Your commander's got half the watch pulled toward the pass road. The cage lock is temple work—good iron, bad hinges. Hinges are on the outside, left side, third bolt's loose already because I loosened it an hour ago while pretending to check the axle." He pressed something into my hand: a short knife, ugly and honest. "Fire the supply wagon, everyone runs to the fire, you get the scribe, we run east into the trees. Simple."

"Nothing is ever simple."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "Which is why I'll be the distraction. A smuggler of my talents, caught skulking around a military camp? They'll chase me for miles out of pure professional pride."

"Ivo, no. Get out. Take the east road, take anything—"

"Sera." For once the grin dropped, and underneath it his face was older and kinder than I'd ever let myself see. "I sold a scared kid to people who put them in a cage. There isn't enough running in the world to outrun that. Let me carry something heavier than guilt for once." He squeezed my shoulder. "Tell the scribe the map was worth copying. Best thing I ever stole was the chance to know them."

Then he was gone, melting between the wagons, and I stood there holding a cheap knife and the terrible knowledge that some debts can only be paid in one currency.

* * *

It went wrong immediately, the way things do.

The fire went up beautifully—the supply wagon caught with a roar that lit the whole bowl orange, and forty enforcers became forty panicking people shouting contradictory orders, which is the most dangerous animal there is. I got to the cage. Third bolt, loose hinge, the door swung wide, and Paloma stumbled out into the snow with their bound hands raised against the light.

"This way—"

And then Aldric stepped out of the smoke.

Of course she did. She'd been waiting for me to move; I'd have been insulted if I weren't so busy being terrified. Her sword was drawn, and behind her a ring of enforcers was forming, and somewhere in the chaos Ivo's voice rose, high and theatrical, leading a dozen pursuers off toward the tree line like the world's most reckless pied piper.

"Sera." Aldric's voice carried over the crackle of the burning wagon. "Last time. Stand down. Come home."

"Home," I repeated. I drew the ugly little knife and almost laughed at the size of it against her blade. "You know, seven years ago I would have. Seven years ago I thought home was a place with banners."

I moved first. That surprised her—I could see it, the flicker of a commander whose intelligence report had failed her. We'd trained together; she knew every trick I knew, and I knew hers, and so the fight was short and brutal and mostly elbows and desperation in the trampled snow. She got my arm twisted halfway to breaking. I got the knife flat against her throat and froze there, both of us breathing hard, close enough to count each other's heartbeats.

"Move," I whispered, "or don't. But I'm not killing you, Aldric. Not even now. Especially not now."

Her eyes searched mine—for what, treachery? Regret? The girl she'd trained with, maybe, the one who'd died at Hollowbridge in every way but the body. Whatever she found, it made her step back. Just one step. Just enough.

"Let them go," she said to her enforcers, hoarse. "*Let them go.*"

They stared at her. She was, after all, their commander, and commanders do not say such things. But then Paloma raised their bound hands, and light came off their skin—not the wild flood I'd seen on the river, but something quieter, colder, like moonlight deciding to have opinions—and forty veterans of two armies collectively decided that whatever was in that cage was above their pay grade.

We ran.

* * *

We ran east, toward the trees, toward where Ivo had led the pursuit, and it wasn't until the camp fires were small orange stars behind us that I understood why the forest ahead was so quiet.

No shouting. No crashing through brush. No Ivo.

I found him a quarter mile in, at the bottom of a slope where the snow lay undisturbed except for the story written across it—a chase, a turn, a crossbowman who hadn't bothered with a warning shot. He was on his back with his arms flung out, and his face, thank all the patient gods, was peaceful. Grinning, almost. As if he'd finally gotten the last word in a negotiation.

Paloma knelt beside him. Their bound hands hovered over his chest, trembling, and I saw the magic gather in them—saw the air bend, saw the frost on the branches shiver—and I caught their wrists.

"Don't."

"He's—" Their voice broke. "I can fix him. I fixed your hand. I can—"

"You'd burn yourself hollow for a dead man, and he wouldn't thank you for it." I kept my voice steady, which cost me everything I had. "Paloma. Look at him. He chose this. He chose it with his eyes open, which is more than most of us ever manage."

They knelt there a long time, snow settling on their shoulders, and then they did the strangest, most human thing: they untied their own bindings with their teeth, and they closed Ivo's eyes, and they said, "Your debt is paid. All of it. Every ledger in the world—you're clean."

Then they stood, and turned to me, and their face was wet and set.

"The Spire," they said. "Before anyone else can reach it. Before the temple regroups, before the court moves, before anyone else decides what I'm for." They looked back once at the small still shape in the snow. "He bought us this. We don't waste it."

* * *

We walked through the rest of that night and all of the next day, north and east, toward the black tooth of the Ember Spire rising on the horizon like a wound in the sky. The long winter deepened around us with every mile—snow falling straight and endless, the light gone the color of old pewter. Neither of us spoke much. Grief makes poor conversation.

But somewhere past the second dawn, Paloma fell into step beside me and said, without preamble: "You gave up your pardon. Your name. Everything they promised you."

"Yes."

"Why? Truly. Not the speech version."

I considered lying, out of habit. Then I thought of Ivo, grinning his way into eternity, and decided the dead deserve better than my reflexes.

"Because when I deserted, everyone called it betrayal," I said. "Seven years I half believed them. But watching you in that cage, watching them plan to *maintain* you—" I exhaled, breath steaming. "Loyalty isn't obedience, Paloma. I finally learned the difference, and it only cost me everything. Cheap, really."

Ahead of us, the Spire caught the light—or made it; it was hard to tell anymore—and the snow around its base seemed to fall upward, slow and wrong, like the world holding its breath.

Paloma watched it and said, very quietly:

"There are voices coming from inside it. They've been calling my name since yesterday."

And they started walking faster.
