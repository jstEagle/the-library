## 17. The Ember Spire

**Paloma**

I had spent my whole life believing the world ended at a shimmer in a mountain pass. It was a reasonable belief. The temple said so, the ledgers said so, and the ledgers were the closest thing to scripture I'd ever been allowed to touch.

The world, it turned out, did not end at the shimmer. The world went on for weeks of frozen road and smugglers' barges and one memorable night hiding in a root cellar while hunters with lanterns passed overhead. And at the end of all that road, rising out of the white like a wound that refused to close, stood the Ember Spire.

Sera saw it first. She always saw things first—ambushes, lies, the exact moment Ivo's smile stopped being honest. She reined the sledge horse to a stop and just looked, and I followed her gaze up, and up, and kept going until my neck ached.

It was black stone shot through with veins of dull red, like coals banked under ash. Not ruined, exactly. *Waiting.* The kind of architecture that makes you understand, viscerally, that the people who built it thought in centuries the way I thought in ledger entries.

"That's it," Sera said. "That's the thing everyone wants."

"It's very tall," I said, because someone had to say something stupid, and I'd found that if I got there first it took the sting out of everything else.

"You're doing the thing where you make jokes so you don't scream."

"I'm aware. It's a coping mechanism. It's in my file."

She almost smiled. Almost. Her face had been doing that more lately—the almosts—and I'd decided not to examine what that meant, because examining it felt like holding a candle too close to something flammable.

We left the horse tethered at the tree line, where the trees gave up on being trees and became skeletons. The snow came down thicker here, fat and slow, and it didn't land on the Spire. I noticed that from about half a mile out: flakes drifting toward those black walls and simply ceasing, as if the building ate weather.

"Paloma." Sera's voice had gone flat, which was her version of shouting. "Your hands."

I looked down. My fingers were glowing. Faintly, guttering, like embers breathing—but glowing, in broad winter daylight, casting orange light up into my own face.

"I know," I said. "They've been doing that since the river."

"Since you drowned two men, you mean."

"Since the river," I repeated, and she let it go, because we had an agreement now, unspoken but load-bearing: some things could be named once and never again.

The doors were thirty feet of black iron, and they opened before Sera could find a way to force them. No groan of hinges. No resistance at all. They swung inward with the courtesy of a host who has been expecting you for twenty years, and warm air rolled out smelling of hot metal and old paper—which is, incidentally, the smell of every place I have ever been happy, so I suppose some part of me relaxed right then, which should have been a warning.

"Inviting," Sera muttered. She drew her sword anyway. "Nothing good has ever invited me inside."

* * *

**Sera**

The inside of the Spire was a lie told by scale.

From outside it was a needle. Inside, the entry hall alone could have swallowed the temple undercroft where I'd spent six years learning the exact taste of stone floors. Pillars rose into darkness. The red veins in the walls brightened as we walked, dimming behind us, and I understood after a while that they weren't following our footsteps.

They were following *him*. Him-them. Paloma. Whatever pronoun you gave to a person who glowed.

"This place knows me," Paloma said quietly.

"Places don't know people."

"The valley didn't know people either, and look how well that worked out." They touched a pillar and the vein beneath their palm flared like a struck match. "Sera. It's *glad* I'm here."

I didn't like that sentence. I didn't like any word in it. But there was nowhere to go but forward—the doors had shut behind us without ceremony—and so forward we went, up stairs that appeared when needed and weren't there when we looked back, through galleries hung with banners from armies whose sigils matched nothing in any history I'd been taught, past statues of robed figures whose faces had been deliberately smoothed blank.

"They erased them," Paloma said, stopping at one. "On purpose. Every face."

"Covenant," I said. "The archivist said they sealed themselves away with their work. Maybe this is what sealing looks like."

"No. Sealing looks like a valley with no magic in it and a child who doesn't know why she can't remember her mother singing." Their voice cracked on *singing*, and I pretended not to hear, because pretending not to hear was the only kindness I had left to offer that particular grief.

At the top of the last stair was a round chamber with no ceiling—or a ceiling so high the dark above it might as well have been sky. In its center floated a heart.

There's no other word. A mass of ember-light the size of a curled-up child, turning slowly, pulsing in a rhythm I recognized before I wanted to: it beat in time with Paloma's pulse. I knew because I'd fallen asleep listening to that pulse across three miserable nights in Ivo's hold, counting it like a rosary, telling myself it was just keeping watch.

"Oh," Paloma said. Small voice. "Oh, hello."

And the chamber answered.

* * *

**Paloma**

The ghost-echoes came out of the walls like breath on cold glass.

They were not ghosts, exactly. They were *possibilities*—the covenant's dead, or the covenant's mathematics, wearing shapes. Figures of smoke and ember-light arranged themselves around the floating heart, and each one spoke with a voice that was almost mine, pitched slightly wrong, like a memory of my own voice played back to me.

*Vessel,* they said. *Last vessel. You are come home.*

"I'm not home," I said. "Home is a valley full of people who lied to me. This is—" I gestured at the impossible room, the beating heart, the audience of smoky not-mes. "This is a trap with excellent acoustics."

One echo stepped forward. It wore my face, but older, harder, crowned in something that wasn't metal so much as *authority made visible.*

*See,* it said. *Choose.*

And the room showed me.

Not pictures exactly—it was more like being handed ledgers and forced to read them at speed, whole futures entered in columns. In the first, I held the heart and the heart held me, and the long winter broke like ice in spring, and the armies at the gates knelt—not to the Spire, to *me*—and I rebuilt the covenant with myself at its center, and the valley's barrier became a wall instead of a cage, and I ruled, and I was wise, and I was fair, and somewhere around year forty of the vision I noticed that I never laughed again, and that the faces on the statues below had been smoothed blank because power does that, it sands you down to function—

*I could stop the war,* I whispered. *Really stop it. Feed people. End the winters.*

*Yes,* said the echoes, in my voice, in chorus. *Rule.*

The second ledger opened.

In it, I took the heart and broke it. Unmade it. Spent it. And the spending was—there isn't a clean word. It was like the healing on the river, when Sera's frostbitten hand mended and I lost the sound of rain on the temple roof. Multiply that by everything. Every working I'd ever need to do this would take payment, and the payment was *me*, memory by memory, self by self, until whatever walked away from the broken Spire had my face and none of my life.

In the second future, the war died unborn. The winter came down gentle as mercy on both armies. And a hollow-eyed person who used to be Paloma Nakamura stood in falling snow remembering almost nothing, and Sera Voss looked at them like a woman reading a letter written by someone she'd loved, in handwriting that had changed.

*I disappear,* I said.

*You are erased,* the echoes agreed, without judgment, which was somehow worse. *Sealed as we were sealed. The covenant ends in you.*

"And if I do neither? If I turn around and walk out?"

The echoes rippled, amused. *Then the temple takes your blood and builds its empire. Then the court takes your body and aims it like a cannon. Then the militias burn the Spire and the magic inside scatters into ten thousand desperate hands. There are many futures, vessel. None of them are yours.*

The heart pulsed. My pulse. Waiting.

I sat down on the floor of a chamber older than every nation I'd ever copied records about, and I laughed—a short, ugly, honest laugh—because of course. Of course. My entire life had been a debt. Three years left on my bond, the High Scribe said, as though time were the currency. And here at the end of the road, the universe itself turned out to be a creditor, holding out a contract in two columns: *everything, paid for with yourself; or everyone else's freedom, paid for with your absence.*

I had spent twenty-two years learning to copy other people's words accurately.

Nobody had ever taught me what to write.

* * *

**Sera**

I found him sitting on the floor with his knees drawn up, talking to the air, and the air talking back—I could hear it now, that chorus of almost-his-voice, patient as tax collectors.

"—and you're certain there's no third option. You're certain. You've had centuries to work on this problem and the answer is rule or vanish."

*The answer has always been rule or vanish,* the echoes said.

"Well," Paloma said, "with respect, your answer got your faces sanded off."

I cleared my throat. Both of us—me and the entire haunted architecture—turned to look.

"How much did you hear?" Paloma asked.

"Enough. Rule or vanish." I leaned against a pillar, arms crossed, sword still drawn because I am not an idiot. "For what it's worth, I've met rulers. Deserted most of them. They start out wanting to fix things and end up wanting to be right, and being right feels better than fixing ever did."

"You're saying vanish."

"I'm saying I've watched you spend memories already. The rain on the roof. Your mother's voice." I said it plainly, because he deserved plainness even though it cost him visibly. "Every time you use it, you pay. And you're asking me whether you should write a check for *all of it.*"

"Yes."

"Then here's my professional opinion, as the only person here who's ever sworn an oath and regretted it." I crouched to his level. "An oath given freely is worth more than one you're born into. You were born into this—planted in that valley like a seed in a vault. If you break the Spire because the echoes guilted you into it, that's just another bond. Another indenture. Temple, court, covenant—they're all the same document with different seals."

"But if I choose it—"

"Then it's yours. Even the erasure. Especially the erasure." My throat tightened, and I pushed through it. "I broke one oath to save myself. Don't you dare make breaking this one about anyone but you."

He looked at me for a long moment. The ember-light moved in his eyes like weather.

"When it's done," he said carefully, "I won't know you. I'll look at you and see a stranger with a sword."

"I know."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

It frightened me more than Aldric, more than the frozen river, more than anything I'd faced since the day I walked away from the winter court and let them call it desertion. I told him the truth anyway, because he'd earned it and because I was apparently the sort of fool who paid her debts.

"I've been a stranger to myself for six years," I said. "It's survivable. We'll manage. I'll introduce myself."

He laughed—wet, cracked, real—and pressed his palms flat against the floor.

Outside, far below, horns began to sound. Three distinct calls, three directions: the temple's low bronze note, the court's silver shriek, and the ragged human clamor of the refugee militias. All of them arriving at once, the way disasters do.

The echoes stirred. *They come for you, vessel.*

"No," Paloma said, standing, light climbing his arms like ivy. "They come for what they think I am. That's different. That's finally, *finally* different."

The heart above us beat faster.

"So do I," he said—to the echoes, to the heart, to me, maybe to himself—and the whole Spire drew breath.

* * *

Below, the first ladders touched the walls, and the long winter leaned in to watch.
