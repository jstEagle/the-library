## 20. After the Falling Snow

I have stood in the aftermath of battles before. I know the particular silence that follows them, the way it presses on your ears like deep water. I know the smell—blood and scorched earth and the sour stink of men who have soiled themselves and will never admit it. I thought I knew everything a battlefield could do to a person.

I was wrong.

The Spire did not fall the way towers fall. There was no slow groan of masonry giving up, no rolling thunder of collapse. It simply *unmade* itself, from the heart outward, like frost retreating off a windowpane in reverse. One moment it stood against the grey sky, black and impossible, veins of ember-light crawling its length; the next, the light went out of it all at once, and what remained crumbled into ash so fine that the wind took it before our eyes could finish believing.

And then the snow came down.

Not the long winter's snow—that had been falling for days, hard and mean, the kind that kills livestock and old men. This was different. This fell soft. Gentle. It drifted down through the smoke in fat, unhurried flakes, and where it touched the frozen churn of the battlefield—the trampled ice, the abandoned siege lines, the bodies we hadn't yet reached—it did not pile up. It *melted*. Everywhere it landed, the killing cold loosened its grip, just a little, just enough.

Around me, three armies stopped being three armies.

I watched a temple enforcer—a man who two hours ago would have run me through for the crime of existing near his High Scribe's ambitions—drop to his knees beside a refugee militiaman with an arrow in his shoulder and press his hands over the wound without knowing why. I watched winter court soldiers, Aldric's people, stand in their perfect ranks and slowly, one by one, break formation, because there was suddenly nothing to hold rank *for*. The war they'd all marched here to fight or prevent or profit from died unborn, and none of them could say exactly how they knew it. They just felt it go out, the way you feel a fire die in a room when you've been sleeping beside it.

Paloma had done that. Paloma had spent themselves doing that.

"Where are they?" The question came out of me raw, and I didn't recognize my own voice until the nearest militiaman flinched at it. "The scribe. Where is the scribe?"

No one answered. No one knew. Of course no one knew. In the last hour I had held a shield line against temple cavalry with nothing but a broken sword and a great deal of anger, and somewhere in the middle of it the world had ended, and I had lost sight of the only person I—

I didn't finish the thought. I started walking instead.

* * *

I found them at the base of what had been the Spire, which sounds straightforward and was not. The ruin field was a mile of drifting ash and half-melted snow, littered with the debris of three armies' ambitions: shattered ladders, burned banners, a siege engine lying on its side like a dead insect. I turned over two bodies that weren't them and felt my heart do something ugly and grateful both at once, which is not a feeling I recommend.

The third shape wasn't a body at all.

They were sitting cross-legged in the ash, small and still as a stone marker, snow gathering on their shoulders because they weren't brushing it off. Their hands lay open in their lap, palms up, and even from ten paces away I could see the damage—skin cracked and greyed, the fine scribe's fingers burned down to something raw and new, as if the magic had used them as a wick and burned all the way through.

"Paloma."

Nothing. Not a flinch. Snowflakes settled in their lashes and melted there.

I crossed the distance fast and dropped down in front of them, taking their face in my hands—gently, gently, I'd learned gentleness the hard way, holding broken things—and tilted it up toward mine. Their eyes were open. They were looking at me. And there was nothing behind the look. No recognition, no fear, no wariness, none of the quick flickering calculation I'd spent months learning to read across a campfire. Just a polite, exhausted blankness, the look you give a stranger who has addressed you on a road.

"Do you know me?" I asked. Stupid question. I asked it anyway.

They considered it with the seriousness of someone very tired trying very hard. "You're... the knight," they said at last. "Sera Voss." A pause. "Someone told me your name. Recently. Or—" their brow creased, and I watched them reach for something and find the shelf empty, watched the confusion turn to a kind of quiet distress, "—or I read it. Did I read it? Why would I—"

"It doesn't matter," I said quickly. "It doesn't matter how."

"But it does." Their voice cracked on it, and they looked down at their ruined hands as if the answer might be written there. "Everything matters and I can't—I know there was a reason I'm here. I know I did something. Everyone out there—" a vague gesture toward the armies dissolving in the snow, "—everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a story they heard. But I don't remember being in it. I don't remember *any* of it. I don't remember—" They stopped. Swallowed. Tried again, smaller: "I don't remember why I can't remember."

The Spire had shown them every future, they'd told me before we went in. Every future except the one where they paid for the choosing with the chooser. I had known, going in, what the working might cost. I think they had too. Knowing a thing and watching it sit across from you in the snow, asking you its own name, are different countries entirely.

"You saved everyone," I said. "That's the whole of it. You can learn the rest later. Right now we need to get you warm."

"I don't know if I saved everyone." They said it flatly, factually, checking an account against a ledger that no longer existed. "I feel like I should know whether I saved everyone."

"You did. I was here. I'm telling you."

They looked at me then, really looked, and something moved behind the blankness—not memory, but the ghost of a habit. Trust, maybe. The body remembering what the mind had spent. "You were here," they repeated. "With me."

"Every step," I said, which was true, and also not, because the last hundred steps I'd been holding a line fifty yards away while they unmade the world's oldest weapon, and I would carry the sound of that working until I died. "Come on. Up you get."

They couldn't stand on their own. That frightened me more than anything else—their legs worked, but they rose like a puppet remembering strings, uncertain, testing each joint. I got my shoulder under their arm and took most of their weight, and they let me, which Paloma Nakamura would never have done. Paloma Nakamura had been indentured since childhood and trusted no one's help, because help always came with interest.

This person leaning on me was not quite Paloma Nakamura anymore. I understood that. I walked anyway.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the ruin, because no one was marching anywhere. Half the temple force had simply sat down where they stood when the Spire fell; the rest had looked to their officers, and their officers had looked at the sky, and no orders had come, because what order covers this? The winter court withdrew a mile east in good order, because discipline dies last, but their scouts reported back that the long winter was breaking—that the cold was lifting along the whole front, season by season, as if someone had opened a door in the world and let the years start moving again.

Aldric found us by the fire. Of course she did.

She came alone, unarmed, hands visible, which from Aldric Vane was either surrender or theater, and after twenty years I still couldn't always tell which. She stopped at the edge of the firelight and looked at Paloma—dozing against my pack, hands bandaged with strips of my own cloak—and something in her face that I had never once seen there in all our years together came and went.

"The court is standing down," she said. "Formally, by morning. Whatever they did in there—" a nod at the ash-field, "—there's nothing left to fight over. The vessel's empty. The covenant's gone. My queen's advisors will take a decade to accept it, but the soldiers already know." She looked at me. "You're free, Voss. Fully. I'll carry the word myself. Desertion pardoned, exile lifted. You could come home."

Home. The word sat in the fire between us like a coal.

Twenty years ago I would have crawled home on my knees for it. I had dreamed of it in the undercroft of the Quiet Valley, in exile, in disgrace—dreamed of white halls and clean snow and my name spoken without spit. And now it was offered, freely, earned twice over, and I found I was looking past it at a hollow-eyed scribe who didn't know my name except as words someone had told them.

"No," I said.

Aldric studied me for a long moment. "They won't remember what you gave up," she said quietly. "People like that. They never do. You'll spend the rest of your life being a stranger to them."

"I know."

"And you're staying anyway."

"I'm staying anyway."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Then you were a better knight than the court ever deserved, and I'll say so at your pardon hearing, and you'll be insufferable about it forever." She turned to go, then paused. "For what it's worth—the valley barrier came down with the Spire. All of it. My scouts rode through the pass this afternoon. There's a town growing there already. People walking in and out of the Quiet Valley like it's any other place." She glanced back at Paloma. "Whatever debt they were bound under—it's gone. Burned with the rest."

She left. I sat with the fire and the sleeping stranger who had once been the most stubborn, curious, infuriatingly decent person I had ever known, and I grieved, and I did not let myself stop grieving, because they deserved at least one person who remembered what had been spent.

* * *

Paloma woke near dawn, screaming.

Not loudly. That was the worst of it—a thin, choked sound, like someone trying to be quiet about terror, some old habit of servitude surviving even the loss of everything else. I was beside them before I was fully awake, and they shrank back against the pack, hands up, and then froze, staring at their own bandaged fingers as though they belonged to someone else.

"I was dreaming," they said. "There was a ledger. I was copying it. Someone was beating me for reading a letter, and I woke up before I could see who, and I don't know if that happened, or if I dreamed it, or—" Their breath hitched. "Was that real? Was I beaten? Who beat me?"

"The Temple," I said. "It's over now. They can't touch you."

"Did I deserve it?"

The question hit me like a fist. "No. Gods, no. You read a letter. That was all. You were curious, and they broke you for it, and it was the worst crime they ever accused you of, because curiosity is the only thing they couldn't tax."

They absorbed that, staring into the fire. Then, in a voice so small I nearly missed it: "I keep reaching for things. Names. Faces. There was a mother—I know there was a mother, I can feel the shape of where she was, but there's no one in it. I spent her. Didn't I. Whoever I was, I spent her to do—to do whatever I did."

"Yes," I said, because they deserved truth more than comfort. "But listen to me. Listen." I took their face again, made them look at me. "What you bought with it is real. The war is dead. The winter is ending. Ten thousand people are going to live who would have died, and a valley full of prisoners just walked free, and none of it gets returned. Nothing you spent comes back, and nothing you bought goes away. Both of those are true forever."

They were crying now, silently, the way people cry who learned early that noise costs extra. "I don't even know why I did it. Everyone keeps saying I chose it. How can I have chosen it if I can't remember choosing?"

"Because I remember," I said. "I watched you choose. I'll hold it for you. As long as it takes."

They looked at me for a long time through the tears, and then they said, hesitantly, like a child testing ice:

"Will you tell me? About them? About—whoever I was?"

"Every day," I said. "If you want. Every single day, as many times as you ask."

* * *

We started south the next morning, toward the pass, toward the valley that was no longer a prison. The roads were filling with people going the same direction—soldiers without wars, refugees without walls, all of them drawn toward the rumor of a shimmer falling, a boundary dissolving, a place called Quiet that was quiet no longer.

Paloma walked beside me, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, moving carefully, like someone relearning their own body. Around midday they stopped on the road and stared at their own hands, flexing the bandaged fingers.

"They hurt less today," they said. "Healing. Is that—is that something I do? Did I used to heal things?" A pause, and then, wry despite everything, despite everything: "Please tell me I wasn't one of those people who was *good* at everything. I couldn't bear meeting myself and finding out I was insufferable."

I laughed. It startled us both. "You were insufferable," I said. "Constantly. You corrected my grammar in the middle of a river ambush."

Their face lit—faintly, cautiously, the first light I'd seen in it. "Did I win?"

"You were right, which for you was better than winning."

They smiled. It was a stranger's smile, but it sat on a familiar face, and I thought: we will build from here. From small things. Grammar and wounds and the names of mothers, given back one at a time, chosen instead of owed.

That night, making camp within sight of the pass, Paloma sat close to the fire and watched me with that careful, hungry attention they brought to everything now, cataloguing, learning.

"Sera," they said—my name, offered up like a question. "Tell me the beginning. Tell me about a courier at a gate."

So I began.
