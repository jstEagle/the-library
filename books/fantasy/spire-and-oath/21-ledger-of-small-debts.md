## 21. Ledger of Small Debts

The first thing I wrote after the Spire fell was my own name.

Not because I'd forgotten it—though give it time—but because I wanted to see what it looked like when no one was charging me for the ink. *Paloma Nakamura.* Two words, eleven letters, and not one of them owed to the Temple of the Quiet Valley, the winter court, the covenant, or any god who'd ever kept a ledger of their own. I wrote it at the top of a page in a book Sera had looted from a dead man's saddlebag, which felt appropriately like inheritance, and beneath it I wrote: *Day one. You are alive. Do not lose track of that.*

Then I sat back and looked at it and thought: well. That's a start.

Here is what nobody tells you about spending your memories like coin: the spending is painless. It's the audit afterward that kills you. I came down from the ruin of the Ember Spire with frost in my hair and a hole where my life used to be, and the hole didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. A wound you can feel is a wound that's healing. This was just absence, smooth as river stone, and I kept running my thumb over it the way you worry a missing tooth.

I knew I had done something enormous. Everyone told me so. The battlefield outside the Spire had been three armies' worth of people with very strong opinions about who should own me, and then there had been light, and snow, and silence, and now those armies were mostly just cold men walking home. I knew I had chosen it. Sera said so, again and again, in her flat way that meant she meant it: *You chose this. Nobody took it from you.*

But I couldn't remember choosing. I couldn't remember the Spire's heart, or the ghost-echoes, or the moment I decided that unmaking was better than ruling. I had a shape of a decision without its contents, like a jar that once held honey. And somewhere in all that spent coin, apparently, had been Sera's name—I learned this from watching her face when I asked her, politely, the way you'd ask a stranger at a well, what I should call her.

She hadn't flinched. That's the thing about Sera Voss; she treats other people's catastrophes like weather. She'd just said, "Voss. Sera Voss," and then turned away so I wouldn't see whatever her face did next, which was considerate of her, and also completely failed, because I saw it.

So: the ledger.

It was Sera's idea, actually, though she'd deny the sentimentality of it. "You were a scribe," she said, the second week, when I'd asked her the same question about the pass twice in one morning and watched her decide, visibly, not to be wounded by it. "Scribes write things down. So write them down. Everything you learn about yourself, everything anyone tells you, put it in the book. Then when you forget, you can check."

"It'll be a stranger's diary," I said. "Reading it will be like reading someone else's mail."

"Better than being a stranger to yourself." She shrugged. "Trust me. I've been both."

That's the other thing about Sera: she says things like that and then walks off before you can ask what she meant.

* * *

The settlement went up where the valley had been, which sounds more poetic than it is. What it means is: there was a wide green bowl of land between two mountain shoulders, formerly sealed behind a shimmer that made magic die on approach, and now the shimmer was gone and the land was empty and free, and free land attracts people the way spilled grain attracts birds.

They came in trickles first. Refugees from Hollowbridge who'd never gone home. Militia folk whose war had died unborn and who didn't know how to stop marching. A family of potters from the eastern lowlands who claimed they'd heard the valley "was quiet," which it was, gloriously, except for hammering. By midwinter there were forty of us. By thaw, a hundred and sixty, and someone had started calling it Nakamura's Rest, which I objected to on the grounds that I was still alive and could hear them, and which they ignored entirely, because naming things after half-remembered heroes is cheaper than building monuments.

I did not tell them I was the hero. Half of them knew anyway. Word travels strange in a world where a girl unmade a Spire; it arrives distorted, wearing extra limbs. In some versions I was seven feet tall. In one memorable account I had burned the entire winter court to ash with a word, which would have been news to the winter court, who were, last anyone checked, still sulking in their frozen capital and pretending none of this had happened.

What I actually did, most days, was teach children their letters.

This had come about by accident. I was sitting on a stump outside my tent copying out yesterday's memories—the ledger work, always the ledger work—and a boy named Tam, the potter family's youngest, stood watching me for so long that eventually I said, "Do you want to learn?" and he said, "Ma says writing is for temple-folk and liars," and I said, "Your ma is half right," and that was that. Within a month I had nine students and a classroom that was a wagon bed, and within two I had a waiting list and a grudge against whoever invented the letter *h*, which is silent more often than it isn't and teaches children nothing but distrust.

There was an irony in it that I chewed on nightly. The Temple had taught me letters as a leash—*you owe us for every character we put in your hand*—and here I was handing characters out for free, and it turned out that was the whole difference between a bond and a gift. Same skill. Same ink. One makes you property. The other makes you a person who chose.

I wrote that down, too. *Difference between bond and gift: choice. Nothing else.* Underlined twice.

* * *

The magic came back slowly, the way feeling returns to a frostbitten hand—pins and needles first, then ache, then something almost like use.

I say "almost" because I was terrified of it, and terror is a poor teacher. Every working cost memory; that was the price I'd learned on the frozen river, drowning hunters I still dreamed about, and confirmed later at costs I catalogued with grim precision. My mother's voice: gone, spent before I even knew it was currency. My tenth winter: gone. The taste of the Temple's barley soup, which I had hated with my whole soul and now mourned like a friend: gone, gone, and honestly, good riddance, but it left a gap shaped like hating something, and gaps don't care whether what filled them was pleasant.

So I rationed myself. A splint set with a touch of warmth for Tam's sister when she fell from the cart—a small thing, a candle-flame of a working, and I paid for it with the name of a street in Hollowbridge I'd walked exactly once. I wrote the street name down beforehand, which felt like cheating, and afterward read it back and felt nothing, which proved it wasn't.

"You could just not," Sera said one evening, watching me record the payment in the ledger. We'd built proper cabins by then; mine had a window, and I was inordinately proud of the window. "Use it. People here got along without miracles for a long time."

"I know."

"And yet."

"And yet." I dipped the pen. "Because if I never touch it, it stays this huge frightening thing that owns me. And if I use it carelessly, it eats me. So I'm going to touch it carefully, on purpose, and write down the price, and that way—" I tapped the page, "—it works for me instead of the other way around."

Sera considered this for a long moment. Firelight did something kind to her face that she'd have denied enjoying.

"That's the sanest thing anyone's ever said to me about power," she said finally. "And I served a court full of mages for six years."

"Low bar."

"Astoundingly low bar." She reached over and turned my ledger toward herself, scanning the entries with the efficiency of a woman auditing supply requisitions. *Paid: the color of the door of the house I grew up in. Paid: the smell of temple incense, thanks the gods. Paid: how to whistle.* Her finger stopped on that last one. "You spent knowing how to whistle?"

"It was an emergency."

"What emergency requires whistling?"

"The emergency was that Tam's dog was stuck under the granary and Tam was crying and I needed him to go get his father, and 'go get your father' wasn't working, and I thought, a whistle will call the dog, the dog will follow me—" I gestured helplessly. "It worked. The dog is fine. I have since relearned whistling badly. There's a whole entry about it."

Sera laughed. It was a rare enough sound that I stopped and stared, and she caught me staring and made the laugh into a cough, and we let it lie there between us, neither of us picking it up, both of us aware of it.

Later that night I added an entry I didn't show her:

*Sera laughs maybe once a month. When she does, she converts it immediately into a cough. I am keeping a count. Current total: four. I do not remember why this matters to me, but the hand that wrote it down seems to think it does, and the hand has been right before.*

* * *

Spring came to the valley the honest way, mud first, then green, then the unbearable optimism of blossoms. The settlement grew a mill. Then a shrine—not to anything in particular; we'd all seen what happened when institutions got particular—and then, inevitably, a council, because a hundred and sixty people cannot agree on where to put a well without governance, and governance cannot happen without minutes, and minutes require—

Well. They required me.

"You're the only one who writes fast enough," said Marta Osei, the potter matriarch, who had appointed herself head of the council by the ancient and unimpeachable method of being the loudest person in favor of it. "And you're honest. Temple-trained, but honest. There's a difference, I've decided."

"There is," I said. "The Temple taught me that honesty is a debt you owe your superiors. I'm trying to learn it as a habit instead."

Marta squinted at me. "Say that again slower. I want to put it on the meeting-house wall."

"Don't you dare."

She dared. It was up within the week, painted in Tam's wobbly hand, misspelled. I left the misspelling. Some part of me—the part that kept the count of Sera's laughter—insisted that a misquoted maxim on a crooked wall was worth more than a perfect one in a sealed archive, and I have learned to trust that part. It's the only part of me that survived the Spire intact, as far as I can tell: the part that writes things down. Whatever else they burned out of me in there, they left the scribe.

Some days that feels like a mercy. Some days it feels like they took everything else and left the tool, the way you'd leave a hammer in a burned house. On those days I do what the ledger says to do: I make an entry. *Bad day. Reason unclear. Ate eggs. Eggs helped.* And it's absurd, and it's small, and it is *mine* in a way that nothing in the Temple ever was, because the Temple's ledgers recorded what I owed. This one records what I have.

*Debt of gratitude: Sera, for standing watch the night I woke screaming and didn't remember why. She didn't ask. She just sat outside my door until dawn, and in the morning she said "nightmares are weather too" and went to fix the mill gate.*

*Debt of gratitude: Marta, for misspelling my philosophy on a wall where children point at it.*

*Debt owed: the two hunters on the river. Names unknown. If I ever learn them, their families get whatever I can carry to them. Entry open.*

*Debt owed: the courier at the gate. Name unknown. Face unknown—he's one of the holes. But he gave me a map and a sentence and it cost him everything, and I built a life on it. Entry open. Interest accruing.*

That last entry was the one I thought about most. Because there was a box, in my cabin, under the window I was proud of, and in the box was a map with a broken wax seal, and I could look at it and know—it in the bone-way, below memory—that this object had started everything. The map to the Ember Spire. The words *the Spire remembers*, which someone had whispered to a scribe who was supposed to be invisible, and which had turned out to be the truest sentence ever spoken to me.

I couldn't remember him. His face, his voice, whether his eyes were kind or frightened or both. He was a hole shaped like a beginning.

But the map was real. And the valley was real, and the school was real, and Tam could now spell his own name and several insults, and Sera's laugh-count stood at seven, and every single day I sat down and wrote the day into existence before it could slip away from me—and somewhere in the middle of all that, I realized the debt had changed shape. It wasn't a bond anymore. Bonds are held over you. This was something else. This was a story I'd been handed by a dying man, and stories don't collect interest. They just ask to be continued.

I was still thinking about that—still sitting at my table with the pen uncapped, spring rain starting against my proud window—when Sera knocked, which she never did, because Sera's version of knocking was opening the door.

So I opened it myself, and found her standing on my step with rain in her hair and a look on her face I'd only seen once before, on a frozen river, right before everything went wrong.

"Riders coming up the pass," she said. "Six of them. Temple grey."

My hand, without consulting me, closed around the pen.

"They're asking for you by name," Sera said. "Paloma Nakamura. And Paloma—they're carrying a ledger."

* * *

Rain on the roof. Rain on the new-planted fields. Somewhere across the settlement, Tam was teaching his dog to come to a badly-whistled note, and the dog, bless it, was learning.

"Whose ledger?" I asked.

Sera's jaw tightened. "That's the thing," she said. "The sigil on the cover. It's yours."
