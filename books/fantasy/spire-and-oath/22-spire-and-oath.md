## 22. Spire and Oath

### Paloma

The first snow of the year came on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Catastrophes, in my experience, prefer dramatic days—the solstice, the equinox, the feast of some martyred saint—but the quiet things, the good ones, they arrive like a scribe arriving at a desk: unremarkably, and on schedule.

I was mending a fence when it started.

Not my fence. Nobody's fence, technically, though Sera had opinions about that. The settlement we were building where the valley used to be had reached the stage where fences mattered—where *property* mattered, which is a word I once would have spelled correctly and resented deeply. Now I spell it correctly and feel something complicated and warm about it. Progress takes strange forms.

The snow came down soft and sideways, the way snow does when it has nowhere urgent to be, and I stood there with a hammer in one hand and a fence post leaning against nothing, and I watched it fall over the black ribs of the Spire's ruin half a mile east, and I thought: *one year.*

One year since the ice. One year since the battlefield went silent mid-scream, since three armies looked up at a falling tower and understood, all at once, that they had already lost—that whatever they'd come to take had just been spent by a debt-bound scribe who couldn't remember their own mother's face. One year since I forgot why I did it.

That's the part nobody warns you about. Not the power—I've written enough about the power. The cost. Every working took a memory, and I spent myself down to the studs to break the covenant, and what's left is a person who knows the shape of their own life without the furniture inside it. I know I had a mother. I know her voice was the first thing to go, burned to keep Sera's frostbitten hand from becoming a stump. I know I loved someone once before all this, or thought I did; there's a hollow where that should be, smooth-walled, like a room swept for guests who never came.

But here is the thing I have learned, the thing I would carve into the temple doors if the temple still had doors: a self is not what you remember. A self is what you *choose*, again and again, in the absence of proof.

I chose to walk back here. That much survived.

"Paloma." Sera's voice, behind me, carrying across the frozen field the way her voice always does—like it expects to be obeyed by weather. "You're letting the post freeze."

"The post is fine."

"The post is lying in a drift."

"That's where posts live, Sera. Before we cruelly uproot them."

She came around the fence line with snow in her hair and that expression she wears when she's decided not to smile yet but is holding the door open for it. Even now, a year on, sometimes I look at her and feel the ghost of a panic—not remembering how we met, not remembering the river, not remembering Aldric's blade coming down and Sera stepping into its path with nothing but her shoulder and her stubbornness. I know these things because she told me, and because I wrote them down, and because I read what I wrote every morning like scripture, like a ledger of small debts I am slowly paying back to myself.

But the *feeling* of those days is gone. And some mornings the man standing next to me is a stranger with kind eyes and old scars, and I have to choose her again from scratch.

I always do. It gets easier. Or maybe it doesn't get easier and I get better at not minding, which might be the same thing.

"Come on," she said. "You promised."

"I promise a lot of things. It's a character flaw."

"You promised the children you'd tell the story of the falling tower if it snowed. They've been waiting since dawn. Mira organized them. There's a *rota*."

"Mira is eight."

"Mira is eight and terrifying, yes. Where do you think I learned it?"

I set the hammer down. The snow kept falling, patient as ink drying, and out east the Spire's bones wore their first white of the season, and I felt—this is the honest word for it, the word I'd have been beaten for writing in a temple ledger—*content.*

Not happy. Happy is a fire; it eats its fuel. Content is a hearth. You can live beside a hearth.

We walked in through the settlement gate together, and the children swarmed, and I told the story of the falling tower for perhaps the hundredth time, and I got every detail right, because I checked my own book that morning, and because some stories you keep the way you keep a vow.

* * *

After, when the children had been extracted by their various exhausted parents and the long hall smelled of woodsmoke and spilled cider, I went to my rooms—the small stone house Sera and I share, though "share" undersells the negotiation—and I did the other thing I do when it snows.

I sat down in front of the map.

It hangs framed above the desk, under glass Sera traded three weeks of fence duty to get from a caravan out of Hollowbridge. The courier's map. The dead man's map. Wax seal broken, vellum gone amber at the folds, the route to the Ember Spire drawn in a hand that shook with cold and blood-loss. I cannot remember him. I want that stated plainly, because everyone in the settlement has decided the story is more romantic if I can—they call him *the stranger who started it all* and give him a tragic jawline in the retellings—but the truth is he is a blank to me. A name I never learned. A voice I never heard. He pressed a map into my hands and said words I have only ever known secondhand, and then he died at the temple gate, and everything after grew from him like a tree from a seed I never saw planted.

Sera says his last words were *the Spire remembers.* She was there. She heard it through the door.

I keep the map anyway. Not as a relic. As an oath.

Here is the oath, and I will write it here so that future-me—who may be reading this with fewer memories than present-me, who may be anyone at all—understands:

*I do not know the man who gave me this map. I choose to honor him anyway. I do not remember why I broke the Spire. I choose to believe it was worth it. I do not remember my mother's voice, or my first love, or the taste of the bread the temple kitchens made on feast days. I choose, every morning, to be the person who spent those things well. This is the whole of my creed: that a life is not a debt you inherit but a ledger you keep, and I am the one holding the pen.*

I used to be a scribe indentured to the Temple of the Quiet Valley. I copied ledgers for eleven years and was beaten for reading a sealed letter, and I believed, the way you believe the walls of a cell are the edges of the world, that I was small.

I was never small. I was *stored.* There's a difference, and the difference cost three armies their war and me nearly everything, and I would pay it again. I think I would. I've written that down too, so that whichever version of me wakes up tomorrow can decide for himself whether it's true.

The snow thickened against the window. Somewhere below, Sera was arguing cheerfully with the miller about load-bearing walls, her voice rising through the floorboards like a pulse. I lit the lamp, took down my book, and began to write today's page, because that is the ritual, and rituals are how the hollow places learn to hold their shape.

*First snow,* I wrote. *Told the tower story. Got the details right. Sera laughed at the part where I insulted Aldric's horse, which I apparently insulted, which sounds like me. Tomorrow: fences. Tomorrow: choosing.*

Outside, over the ruin of the Spire, the snow fell on everything equally—the stones I broke and the fields I saved and the graves of people whose names I am teaching the settlement to say aloud. It fell like forgiveness. It fell like paper.

It fell like a blank page, waiting to see what I would write.

### Sera

There is a particular silence that follows a battle, and I have stood in most of its varieties: the silence after victory, which rings; the silence after defeat, which hums; the silence after a rout, which screams. But the silence after the Spire fell was none of these. It was the silence of a held breath finally let go—a whole world exhaling at once—and I have spent a year trying to describe it to Paloma, who cannot remember it, and failing every time, and discovering that I don't mind failing. Some things you carry for two.

I knew Paloma for exactly one day before I understood I would follow them anywhere. I have never told them this. They'd only write it down, and then I'd have to live with seeing it in my own handwriting of the soul, which is worse.

A year ago tonight—or near enough; the anniversary moves with the snow, and the snow does not consult calendars—I stood in the ruins of my oaths with a sword I no longer believed in and watched a scribe climb a tower of ghosts to unmake the oldest covenant in the world. I watched Aldric die reaching for a crown. I watched the High Scribe kneel in the ash and weep for an empire that had died before it was born. I watched Ivo's grave fill with snow, and I thought: *this is what redemption costs, and it is more than I have, and I will spend it anyway.*

And then I watched the snow come down on three armies, gentle as a benediction, and I understood that the long winter had never been a punishment. It had been a mercy, waiting all those centuries for someone brave enough and foolish enough to open the door.

Paloma opened it. Paloma paid.

They don't remember paying. That's the cruelty of it, and I have made my peace with being the keeper of the receipt.

Tonight I found them at the fence line watching the first snow, hammer in hand, looking at the Spire's bones with an expression I have learned to read the way soldiers read weather. It's not grief. Grief requires the lost thing. It's something adjacent—recognition of a hole without memory of what filled it. I stood beside them and said something about the post freezing, because banter is a bridge, and you build bridges even when the person on the far side changes daily. *Especially* then.

They came inside. They told the story to the children—all of it true, all of it checked against their book, because Paloma Nakamura lies about nothing anymore, having paid too much for the truth to fritter it on comfort. I listened from the doorway with cider going cold in my hand and thought, not for the first time: *I was exiled for desertion, and it turns out I simply deserted the wrong army early.*

Then they went upstairs to the map, and I let them go, because that hour is theirs. I hear the lamp, the scratch of the pen, the long pauses. Once, months ago, I asked what they wrote up there, and they showed me the oath, and I read it twice, and I could not speak for a while, and they pretended not to notice, which is its own kind of love.

Tonight, though, when I climbed up, they weren't writing. They were standing at the window with the map glowing gold under the lamplight, watching the snow bury the Spire.

"He was afraid," Paloma said, without turning. "The courier. Wasn't he."

"Yes."

"But he came anyway. Through the pass, past the boundary, all the way to a valley where magic couldn't touch him—where *nothing* could help him—just to put a piece of paper in the hands of a stranger."

"Yes."

Paloma was quiet a moment. Snow ticked against the glass. "Why do you suppose?"

I considered lying, which I no longer do, mostly. "Because someone once chose him," I said. "When he had nothing left. And he wanted to spend the rest of his life the same way. Passing it on."

Paloma nodded slowly, the way they nod when a sentence has landed somewhere structural. Then they turned, and the lamplight caught the scar along their jaw—the one from the temple enforcers, from the beating in chapter one of all this, though they don't know it was chapter one; to them it's just a scar with a story attached—and they smiled the small dry smile that I crossed a frozen country to protect, and they said:

"Well. Then I'm doing it right."

"You're doing it right," I agreed.

Below us, the settlement settled into its evening—doors latching, fires fed, Mira's voice raised in triumphant negotiation over bedtime. Out east, the Spire wore its snow like a shroud or a blanket, depending on your theology, and I have decided, after a year of deciding, on *blanket.*

"One more year," Paloma said, "and the schoolhouse will be finished. And maybe a proper archive. Someone should keep records here. Real ones. Chosen ones."

"Someone should," I said. "Terrible idea, trusting you with a pen."

"I'm told I have a gift."

"You're told that by yourself. Daily. In writing."

They laughed—really laughed, the sound I hoard against winters—and blew out the lamp, and we went down together into the warm noise of the house we built on ground that used to be a prison.

I do not know what comes next. That is the whole miracle of it. For thirty years my life was written for me—oath, order, exile, sentence—and now the pages are blank, and the snow keeps falling on them, and every morning I wake up and choose: her, this place, this peace, again.

The Spire is fallen. The oath stands.

And outside, softly, without hurry, the first snow of the second year began to fall on everything we had chosen—and on everything we would choose tomorrow.
