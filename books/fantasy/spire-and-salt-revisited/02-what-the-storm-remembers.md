## 2. What the Storm Remembers

The storm came before the enforcers did. August felt it the way a man feels a knife between his shoulder blades — not as weather, but as intention.

He stood at the caravan's head on the switchback above Greywater Pass, one hand raised, and the clouds answered him like hounds answering a whistle they'd been bred to. That was the obscenity of it. Ten years of exile, ten years of pretending he was only a guide with good instincts and a lucky charm of iron nails sewn into his coat hem, and still the sky came when he called without meaning to call.

"Storm's turning," he said to Marta Voss, who drove the lead wagon and paid him for lies exactly this shape.

"Turning where?"

"Away." He made his voice flat, professional, bored. "We'll have clear passage through the pass by midnight."

Marta grunted and flicked her reins. Behind her, nine wagons groaned under salted hides, untaxed brandy, and the particular silence of people who had learned not to ask their guide too many questions. Good cargo. Good silence. August kept walking.

But the pressure in his back teeth didn't ease. It deepened.

Storm-callers felt weather the way other men felt mood — as a texture in the blood, a pressure behind the eyes. And what August felt now wasn't a mountain squall. Mountain squalls were messy things, all elbows and temper. This was *arranged*. The clouds overhead had been combed into ranks. The wind that should have come screaming down from the high snowfields was being held back, banked like a fire, and everything it wanted to be was coiled instead around the pass ahead of them, waiting.

Waiting for them.

*You're paranoid,* he told himself. *Ten years of looking over your shoulder will do that.* But his hands had gone cold, and his hands never went cold. His hands were the warmest part of him; they were full of sky.

Behind him, at the rear of the column, Wren Corvane walked with the prophecy book strapped to her chest like an infant, muttering to herself in the cadence of someone reading and arguing with what she read simultaneously. She'd barely slept. Neither had he. He'd spent the night telling himself the book was a forgery, or a madwoman's game, or — most likely — a coincidence so old it had curdled into something that looked like fate. A century-old text with today's date circled. His name inked beside it in faded brown gall-ink: *August Dravenmoor, thief of souls.*

His mother hadn't named him anything so dramatic. Nobody had called him a thief of anything until the spires put the word *exile* next to it.

The pass narrowed. The walls rose on either side, grey granite streaked white with old avalanche scars, and August stopped liking any of it very much indeed.

"Marta," he said quietly. "Halt the column."

She looked at him. "It's another hour to the waystation—"

"Halt the column."

Something in his voice must have carried, because she hauled the reins without another word, and the wagons ground to a stop in a chain of creaks and curses. August turned to face back down the line and found Wren already hurrying forward, book clutched against her ribs, her grey eyes enormous behind the smudged lenses of her spectacles.

"You feel it," she said. Not a question.

"I don't feel anything. I want you to walk at the front with me, and I want everyone ready to run the wagons if I say the word."

"You feel it," she repeated, with the maddening satisfaction of a woman whose marginalia had just been vindicated. "The book says—" she fumbled it open one-handed, "—the ninth annotation, grandmother wrote, *'the sky will hold its breath for the thief.'*"

"The sky is holding its breath because there's weather coming over the saddle, and I'd like us not to be in the narrowest part of the pass when it arrives."

"August." Wren lowered the book. Her voice dropped into something stripped bare, no scholarship in it at all. "I've read that page four hundred times. I dreamed about it before I ever met you. Whatever comes down that pass, it isn't weather."

He might have argued. He would have, if the sky hadn't chosen that moment to exhale.

Lightning, out of a cloudless gap — a single white vein splitting the air above the saddle, and riding down inside it, figures. Three of them, then five, then more, descending the rock face in long controlled falls, robes snapping, staves trailing blue-white corona. Spire enforcers. August knew the silhouette the way he knew his own heartbeat: he'd worn it once. The storm-grey mantles. The lightning-crowned sigils. The particular arrogance of people who believed the sky belonged to them by right of employment.

"Caravan!" August's voice cracked across the pass like a whip. "Off the road! Scatter to the rocks — *now!*"

They scattered. Marta Voss, to her eternal credit, didn't waste breath asking why; she wheeled her team toward the scree and the whole column broke apart behind her like a snapped necklace. And August stood alone in the middle of the road with his arms slightly away from his body and the storm gathering in his palms whether he willed it or not.

The first enforcer landed thirty feet away, boots cracking frost-rimed stone. He pushed back his hood, and August's stomach dropped through the mountain.

"Veylan." The name came out of him like a splinter. "You've come a long way to collect a smuggler's tithe."

Veylan Ashgrave. They'd been novices together. They'd climbed the same spire stairs, sworn the same oaths to the storm, drunk from the same cisterns of rainwater. Veylan's face hadn't changed much — still sharp, still handsome in the way of well-maintained weapons — but his eyes had the flat shine of a man who had been given orders he'd agreed to stop questioning years ago.

"We're not here for tithes, August." Veylan's voice was almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty. Gentle meant pity, and pity meant the blow was bad. "We're here for the archivist. And for you."

"For me." August laughed, and hated how thin it sounded. "Ten years. You let me walk the world for ten years, and tonight of all nights—"

"Not tonight." Veylan glanced upward, at the held-breath sky, and something flickered across his face that August had never seen there before. Fear. "Every night since you left. We've been watching you for ten years, August. Waiting for the date."

*The date.* The circled ink. The brown gall letters. August's mouth went dry. "What date? What are you talking about?"

"The bargain." Veylan took a step forward, and the other enforcers fanned out behind him with the smooth choreography of a thing done many times. "Your bargain. You thought the spires exiled you for trading with the Salt Court. For selling a piece of yourself to buy your sister's cure." He shook his head slowly. "That's the story we let you keep. It was kinder."

The wind moved through the pass, and it was cold in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"What story is true, then?" August heard himself ask, from very far away.

"The Court doesn't take pieces, August. It takes wholes, or it takes payment in kind. Your little bargain — the fever-cure, the whispered contract under the mountain — it was never valid. Too small. Too desperate. The Court rejected it." Veylan's jaw tightened. "But contracts with the Salt Court don't simply lapse. They *migrate*. The debt went looking for collateral. And the spires..." He paused, and August watched him choose, deliberately, to say the rest. "The spires provided it. Isolde's soul. Paid in full, transferred, sealed. To cover your account."

The pass went very quiet. Even the held-back storm seemed to lean in.

"No," August said.

"The exile was engineered. Not as punishment. As *containment*. A debtor who doesn't know he has a creditor can't come looking for the vault." Veylan spread his hands, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness. "You were never thrown out, August. You were *filed*."

August's sister. Isolde, who had survived the fever that should have killed her because her brother had crawled into the dark places beneath Thornfell and begged. Isolde, who had woken whole and laughing and lived eleven more years before the wasting took her — the slow, hollowing sickness the healers had never been able to explain.

Eleven years. He'd mourned her body. He'd never once wondered where the rest of her had gone.

The storm answered before he did.

It came up out of him the way water comes through a cracked dam — not all at once but *inexorably*, with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Blue-white light sheeted down his arms. The temperature of the pass dropped twenty degrees in a breath. Above, the combed and waiting clouds finally broke rank and came down the saddle like cavalry, and every enforcer's stave lit at once in answer, and for three glorious, terrible seconds August Dravenmoor stood in the center of a war between the sky and himself and could not have said which side he was on.

"Take him alive!" Veylan shouted. "The Regent needs him breathing!"

*The Regent.* The words reached August through the roar, and they were the last straw. The Regent. The Salt Court. They hadn't sent enforcers to punish him. They'd sent them to *deliver* him.

The lightning went out of him sideways.

He didn't aim it. He didn't have to. Ten years of dammed grief found the path of least resistance and took it — through Veylan's stave, shattering it to smoking crystal; through the rock wall, blasting a scar of fused glass across the granite; through the ranks of enforcers, hurling them like dolls, robes burning, corona scattering. The pass filled with thunder and the smell of ozone and hot stone. Somewhere a wagon horse screamed and was answered by its driver. August stood in the eye of it with his arms flung wide and tears freezing on his face, and the storm used him like a throat uses a scream.

Then it was over. The clouds, spent, tore open onto indifferent stars. Five enforcers lay groaning among the scree. Veylan knelt in the ruined road, half his mantle charred, staring at August with an expression that had finally, finally lost its gentleness.

"Marked," Veylan rasped. Blood at the corner of his mouth. "You've marked yourself. Every wight under the mountain just felt that. Every ledger-page with your name just grew a new line. You think we were the hunt, August?" He laughed, wet and broken. "We were the *escort*."

August walked toward him, and each footstep left a print of frost that steamed. He stopped close enough to see the fear plainly now, and he found he couldn't even enjoy it.

"Tell the spires whatever you like," he said. "Tell them I'm coming. Tell them I'm not theirs anymore, and I never was."

He turned away. Wren Corvane stood at the edge of the light, book clutched to her chest, having watched all of it with the expression of a woman watching a prophecy learn to walk.

"They'll regroup," she said quietly. "And the Court knows you're coming now. Both of them hunting us, the whole mountain between us and the gate." Her hand found the book's cover, gripped it. "August. I know what you're thinking. I can see it. You're thinking you'll turn around, go back to the roads, lose yourself in the low country, and spend the rest of your life not knowing. Because not knowing is survivable. Knowing is the thing that kills you."

"Isolde's soul is in a vault under this mountain," August said. His voice came out scraped raw, unrecognizable. "Because of me. Because I signed a piece of paper in the dark because I couldn't watch her die." He looked down at his hands — still crackling faintly, still full of sky, still the hands of a man the spires had built and then sold. "I spent ten years believing my exile was my punishment. It was my *receipt*, Wren."

Wren stepped closer. In the starlight her face was pale and certain, and she pressed the book into his hands — the whole impossible weight of it, vellum and gall-ink and a century of a dead woman's handwriting.

"Then stop running from the map," she said. "The margins mark a descent gate. Sealed. Storm-sigil lock — it opens for *you*, and no one else. Two days' climb from here." Her eyes held his, fierce and frightened and utterly resolved. "It's the only door in the world that leads to her. And it's the only map we have."

Far below them, down in the roots of Thornfell where no light had ever reached, something ancient turned a page of its own — and began, patiently, to count.
