## 3. Marginalia

The book had been in Wren Corvane's family for ninety-one years, and for forty of those it had been hers.

She thought about that as she watched August Dravenmoor sleep by the waystation fire — or pretend to sleep, which amounted to the same thing with a man like him. His breathing was too even. His hands were folded over his chest like a man lying in a coffin who expected to be asked to get out of it soon. Storm-callers, her grandmother had written, do not rest; they only wait for weather.

*Grandmother.* The word opened the first door, and Wren, as she always did, walked through it.

* * *

She was nine years old, sitting cross-legged on the cold flagstones of the archive undercroft in Vell's Hollow, watching her grandmother bind a new cover onto the old book. Her grandmother's hands were stained black to the second knuckle — ink and tanning solution and something else, something that smelled faintly of the sea though they were three hundred miles from any coast.

"Salt," Grandmother had said, catching her sniffing the air. "Everything true smells of salt eventually. Remember that."

"Why?"

"Because everything false smells of honey." She pressed the leather down along the spine, working it with her thumbs, and the book lay open between them to a page Wren had never been allowed to touch: dense columns of prophecy in a hand so old the letters had begun to eat themselves, and around them, in the margins, annotations in three different inks, three different centuries of a single family arguing with the text.

"What does it say?" Wren asked.

Her grandmother was quiet a long time. Outside, wind worried the shutters of the archive house, and somewhere above them the bells of Vell's Hollow rang the hour wrong, as they always did, two minutes fast, so that the town lived perpetually in the future.

"It says a man will come," she said at last. "A storm-caller without a spire. It says he will walk into the mountain on this date, and it gives the date." She tapped the margin, where someone — her grandmother's own mother, Wren would learn much later — had written in a careful, frightened hand: *and if you are reading this before it happens, know that the ending is a lie.*

"The ending is a lie," Wren repeated.

"All endings are lies, child. They only tell you where the page stops, not where the story does." Her grandmother closed the book and put her hands flat on its cover, the way other people laid hands on the dying or the newly born. "But this one lies on purpose."

* * *

In the present, the fire popped, and August's eyes opened — not all the way, just enough. A man checking his exits even in half-sleep. Wren pretended not to notice, and he pretended to believe her, and that mutual fiction felt oddly like the beginning of something.

"You're staring at me," he said.

"I'm staring at the book." She patted the satchel beside her. "You're incidental."

That earned her a huff that might have been a laugh in a more generous man. "You dragged me off a caravan route to show me a hundred-year-old fortune-telling, archivist. Forgive me if I don't feel incidental."

"You will." She pulled the book out and set it on her knees. The leather cover was cracked now, mended twice, once by her grandmother and once by herself, badly, at nineteen, with thread that was the wrong weight because grief makes poor seamstresses. "I want to show you what's in the margins."

* * *

She was twenty-three when she understood that her grandmother had not merely annotated the prophecy but had spent her whole life decoding it, and that the decoding had killed her slowly, the way water kills stone.

They had fought about it, near the end. Wren wanted to take the book to the spires — let the storm-callers read it, let their archivists confirm the date, the name, the descent gate. Her grandmother had struck the table hard enough to rattle the ink pots.

"The spires wrote the first draft of this," she said. "Do you understand? Every prophecy is written by someone who wants an outcome. The question is never *is it true*. The question is *who profits if you believe it*."

"And who profits here?"

Her grandmother looked at her then with an expression Wren would spend the next seventeen years learning to read — pity and terror braided together, the look of a woman watching a child walk toward a cliff edge she could describe but not close.

"A court beneath the mountain," she said. "One that trades in memory. One that has been hungry for a very long time. And a family of archivists foolish enough to write down what we saw." She turned the book to its final leaf, which Wren had never seen unbound. There, in ink gone brown as dried blood, four words:

*THE ENDING IS FALSE.*

"And below that," her grandmother said, "in my mother's hand: *the thief must refuse the script. Pray he is stubborn enough.*"

* * *

Now Wren turned the pages by firelight, past the prophecies of harvests and wars that had come true or hadn't, past the genealogies scrawled by great-aunts, until she reached the leaf she had been carrying toward for forty years.

August leaned forward despite himself. She watched him fight the leaning and lose. Good. Curiosity was a hook; she'd baited it carefully since the waystation door.

"There," she said. "Read it."

He read aloud, slow, the way men read when they don't trust the words to stay still. "*On the eve of the deep winter, the exile Dravenmoor comes down from the high road. He carries no banner and no spire-key. The gate beneath Thornfell knows his sigil. He goes to steal back what was sold in his name.*"

"In your name," Wren said quietly. "Not *for* you. In your name. Someone sold something using your name as the coin."

His jaw worked. "My sister," he said, and stopped, and started again. "Isolde. When I made my bargain with the spires — I paid a debt to save her life. I thought—" He shut his eyes. "I thought the debt was mine alone."

"The margins say otherwise." Wren turned to a passage near the binding, where her grandmother's hand had grown cramped and urgent. "*The spires balance their ledgers with flesh. The Court accepts souls as tender. The bargain the exile believes he made is not the bargain that was made.*"

August stood up. Walked to the broken window of the waystation, where the Thornfell peaks stood black against a sky that was already, impossibly, in early autumn, going the color of pewter. The mountain hummed — Wren couldn't hear it, but she could see it in him, the way his shoulders tightened, the fine tremor in his fingers, a tuning fork held against the world.

"How long have you known my name was in there?" he asked without turning.

"Since I was nine years old."

"And you waited. You found me on a smuggler road and you *waited*."

"I followed the book," Wren said. "The book said the high road, the third week of the waning moon, a caravan master with a storm-scar. I've been walking these roads for six years looking for you, Dravenmoor. Waiting wasn't the word for it."

He turned. Firelight caught the scar at his temple — lightning-written, the mark every storm-caller bore, though his had been struck through deliberately, a brand of exile. "Why you? Why does some archivist's granddaughter care whether one exiled smuggler walks into a mountain?"

And there it was — the real question, the one underneath all questions, the one Wren had been avoiding since she'd first seen him argue prices with a caravan boss in a language that mixed three dialects and pure stubbornness. She hugged the book to her chest.

"Because my grandmother died waiting for this date," she said. "And her mother died waiting for it. Three generations of women writing warnings in margins for a man none of us would ever meet, hoping he'd be stubborn enough to listen." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "I have spent my entire life talking to dead people through a book. You're the first living thing those words have ever touched. So yes, Dravenmoor. I care."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, thunder rolled with no storm to carry it — or rather, with no storm except whatever the mountain was becoming.

* * *

They found the gate two days later, and Wren would remember afterward that neither of them spoke for the last hour of the descent, as if the mountain itself had asked for quiet.

It was buried in a fold of the range where the maps went vague — a cirque of black stone, snow already gathering though winter was weeks away, and at its center a wall of basalt that predated every kingdom whose coins Wren had ever catalogued. No door. No seam. Just stone, and carved across its face in letters ten feet tall, worn nearly smooth: words in Old Spire-tongue that Wren had spent her girlhood translating by lamplight.

"*What was given may be reclaimed,*" she translated now, aloud, her breath fogging. "*Nothing is owned. Everything is owed.*"

"Cheerful," August muttered. But he stepped forward, and Wren saw him raise his right hand, palm out, and saw the scar at his temple begin to glow — a thin white line, brightening, the brand of exile burning like a fuse.

"That's it," she breathed. "The sigil. The book says the gate knows his—"

"I heard you the first forty times."

Lightning answered him. Not from the sky — from *inside* him, Wren realized, from whatever prison the spires had built around his gift when they cast him out. The air went sharp and metallic. Snow lifted off the ground in a slow spiral. And in the basalt face, seams appeared — hairline cracks spreading outward from a point at the height of a man's heart, widening, groaning, until a doorway stood open on darkness and a stair descending beyond the reach of any light August raised.

Cold poured out of it. Not winter-cold. Older. The cold of deep places, of vaults, of things kept.

Wren's hands shook as she opened the book to the final leaf — the false ending, the warning, her grandmother's brown ink. But below the warning, in a margin she had never been able to read by any lamp, the characters now stood out faintly luminous, drinking the gate's light, legible for the first time in a century.

She read them. Read them again. Felt the words go through her like a fishhook.

"Dravenmoor," she said. "There's more. Something my grandmother added after the warning." She swallowed. "It says: *the gate opens both ways. Whatever you trade below, bring nothing back that isn't yours.*"

August looked at the stair. At her. At the book in her shaking hands.

"And if the thing I want most," he said quietly, "was never mine to begin with?"

Below them, in the dark, something vast shifted — the sound of a ledger being opened, of a page being turned, of a hunger older than kingdoms noticing, at last, that dinner had arrived.

Wren closed the book. Chose. Stepped to the threshold beside him.

"Then we go get it anyway," she said, "and steal it properly."

Together, they started down.
