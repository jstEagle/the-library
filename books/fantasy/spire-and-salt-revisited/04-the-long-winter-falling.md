## 4. The Long Winter Falling

The snow came down like a verdict.

August had walked the Thornfell passes in every season the mountains cared to offer — the wet springs when the trails ran like veins, the dry summers when dust rose off the switchbacks in gold plumes, the autumns that smelled of pine rot and woodsmoke. He had never seen winter arrive like this. Not falling. *Descending*, as if it had been waiting above them all along and had simply decided, tonight, to come down.

It was three weeks before the solstice. The larches should still have been burning amber. Instead they stood along the trail already stripped and silvered, their branches bent under snow that had no business existing yet, and the cold that came off the heights was not the clean cold of altitude but something older and hungrier, a cold with intent behind it.

He felt it in his teeth first. Then, worse, in the old scar tissue along his palms where the storm-gift lived closest to the skin.

*Humming.*

The same frequency he'd felt in the ruined waystation, the night Wren Corvane had opened a century-old book and shown him his own name inked beside today's date. He'd laughed then. He wasn't laughing now. The hum had followed him for nine days like a second heartbeat, rising whenever they climbed, fading in the valleys, and August Dravenmoor — who had once called lightning down the length of the Spires' great conductors, who had bargained with powers whose names were not spoken above the snowline — knew what it meant when a mountain began to sing.

Something below was pulling.

"Dravenmoor." Tam Vess, his caravan master, reined up beside him, breath steaming. Behind her, twelve wagons creaked under canvas, laden with salt-pork, iron nails, and the kind of cargo nobody asked about. "Horses don't like it."

"Horses are smarter than we are."

"The mules are refusing the north fork. Flat out. Kestrel's been whipping them and they just—" She stopped, because ahead of them the lead mule had planted all four hooves and was staring down into the ravine with its ears flat, trembling like a struck bell.

August dismounted. Walked to the edge of the trail. Below, where the ravine bottom should have been lost in ordinary dusk, there was light — a pale, sourceless glow rising out of the dark like heat shimmer inverted, like cold made visible. Frost crept up the rock face toward him in delicate ferning patterns, and where the frost touched stone, the stone sang.

He pulled his hand back from the railing of an old guard-post. His palm had gone numb to the wrist.

"We're not taking the north fork," he said.

"It's the only road through to Greywater—"

"Look at it, Tam." He nodded at the glow. "That's not weather. That's a *throat*. Something's drinking the warmth right out of this mountain, and I'd rather not be standing on its tongue when it swallows."

Tam looked. Her face did what good caravan masters' faces do — went carefully blank, filed the fear away for later, moved to logistics. "Then where?"

August turned south, toward the high shepherd roads, and felt the hum shift against his ribs like a compass needle finding north. Or south. Or *down*.

"There's a village," he said slowly, because he hadn't known he was going to say it until the words were out. "Cinderhollow. Off the maps since the last war. If anything warm is left on this mountain, it'll be crowded with everyone running from whatever that is."

* * *

They reached Cinderhollow at dusk, or what passed for dusk beneath a sky the color of dirty wool.

The village sat in a bowl of the southern hills, and it was full — not with its own people, who numbered perhaps sixty, but with hundreds more, refugees from the high valleys, from Greywater, from places further north whose names came out of the survivors' mouths like splinters. They had packed themselves into the village's stone longhouse, its byres, its shrine, its streets. Cookfires burned in every doorway. And over all of it lay a silence August recognized from funerals: the silence of people listening for something they hoped not to hear.

The snow had stopped at the rim of the bowl. It fell everywhere else on the mountain — he could see it hanging in the air above the ridgelines like curtains — but here, above the village, the clouds simply *ended*, sheared off in a perfect circle, as if something enormous pressed down upon Cinderhollow from a great height.

"They say it started eight days ago," said the headwoman, a broad-shouldered woman named Maren Ashka whose hands were stained blue with woad and whose eyes hadn't slept recently enough to lie well. "First the birds went quiet. Then the wells froze from the bottom up — not the top, mind, the *bottom*, like the cold was coming up out of the ground. Then Old Ferro's boy touched a stream and the water cut him like glass. We sent runners north. Two came back. One of them had stopped shivering and couldn't start again." She looked at August's caravan, at the wagons, at the armed and road-hardened faces. "You're going down, aren't you. Into the deep roads."

"I'm going wherever the answer is," August said. "And I think the answer is down."

"Then you'll take them." Not a question. Maren's voice cracked on the last word and she hated herself for it; he could see that too. "Anyone who can walk. There's four hundred souls in this hollow and the warmth is leaving it. You can feel it, can't you? You've got the look of one who feels things." She didn't wait for confirmation. "Every hour the fires burn lower. Wood doesn't want to catch anymore. Bread won't rise. The sick aren't getting better, they're getting *quieter*, and quiet is how it starts, I've seen it, quiet and then—"

"Maren." He put his hand over hers. Her skin was ice. "I'll take every soul who can stand. That's a promise. But I need to know what you know about what's below, because I've read a book lately that says a great deal, and books lie."

She told him what the refugees had carried down with them: stories of lights in the salt caves under Greywater, of figures walking out of mine shafts with white faces and patient smiles, asking after debts — *old debts, family debts, debts the living had forgotten but the dead had kept careful record of.* Stories of whole farmsteads found frozen mid-motion, a man half-risen from his chair, a woman with a ladle still in her hand, preserved like insects in amber, and no wound on any of them, no terror, just a terrible *contentment*, as if they had been paid for.

As if they had been *bought*.

August listened with the storm-gift humming against his palms and thought of Isolde. Twelve years dead to him — sold, though he hadn't known the word for what he'd done until nine days ago, until enforcers with spire-sigils had come out of a snowfield and told him the price of his bargain had been paid in his sister's soul, and the Salt Court had held it ever since, drawing interest.

Drawing *warmth*.

"Oh," he said quietly, and Maren Ashka stopped talking and looked at him, because something in his voice had changed. "It's not hunger. It's accounting. It's keeping itself warm on what it's owed."

* * *

They left two days later, three hundred and eleven refugees and one caravan that now resembled a city in flight. The high shepherd road wound south and then east, climbing before it descended, and August walked the whole way at the column's head because he could feel the cold following them like a tide follows the moon, and someone needed to watch it.

It was on the third evening, at a shrine to a road-god whose name had worn off the stone, that the dying woman found him.

She was old — older than the mountains looked, older than the road — and she had walked the entire distance from Greywater on feet wrapped in sacking, which should have killed her in a day and had instead taken three. Now it had finished its work. She lay in the lee of the shrine while her granddaughter, a girl of perhaps fifteen with chapped hands and furious eyes, knelt beside her pretending to adjust a blanket that did not need adjusting.

"You're the storm-caller," the old woman said. Her voice was thin as paper but steady. "The one from the book."

August went very still. "What book?"

"The Corvane book. My grandmother copied margins for a Corvane, back before the war. Everyone who copies for the Corvanes knows the shape of the story, even if the words change." She smiled, and her teeth were very white in her gray face. "You have your name in it. Lucky man. Most of us aren't written anywhere."

"I'm not sure I'd call it luck."

"No," she agreed. "I'd call it a bill." She struggled upright, and the girl moved to stop her and the old woman pressed her down with one hand, gently, the way you press down something that has decided. "Listen to me, storm-caller. I'm dying. Not of the cold — of the walking. It's in my chest now, a rattle like a door in wind. I'll be done by morning, and I'm not sorry, because I got my granddaughter out, and that was the whole errand." Her eyes were bright and terribly clear. "But I have something I need delivered. Down. Underneath."

"You want me to carry your memories," August said. Wren had explained it to him on the second night, hunched over the prophecy book by firelight: how the Court traded in memory, how the living could pay passage through the deep roads with their recollections, sealed in salt, carried by a bearer. How most bearers came back lighter. How some didn't come back at all.

"I want you to carry my *grief*," the old woman said. "That's the payment. Memory's the coin, but grief's the weight, and the Court likes weight, it *eats* weight." She fumbled at her neck and drew out a small leather pouch, and inside it, when she shook it into his palm, were grains of coarse gray salt, each one different, each one humming faintly against his skin the way the mountain hummed. "Sixty years. A husband, two sons, a daughter who chose the sea. Every hard day and every sweet one, pressed into salt the way the old women taught me. It's heavy, storm-caller. Heavier than it looks. Are you strong enough to carry a stranger's life?"

And August, who had carried his guilt for twelve years without knowing it was freight, who had learned nine days ago that his love for his sister had been spent like currency without his consent, looked down at the salt in his hand and understood exactly what was being asked. Not to hold her memories. To *feel* them. To let a stranger's grief settle into his chest alongside his own and walk with it, mile after mile, down into the dark, and give it away at the door of the thing that ate such things.

It would hurt. That was the point. Survival had a price, and down here, the price was always paid in what you could least afford to lose.

"Yes," he said.

The old woman exhaled, a long rattling breath that was half relief and half surrender, and closed his fingers over the pouch with both her hands, cold as riverbed stones. "Her name is Sefa," she said, nodding at the granddaughter. "When it's done — when you've paid my passage — you tell her the debt was mine, not hers. Tell her she walks free. Promise me."

"I promise."

She died an hour before dawn, quietly, between one breath and the next, and the strange thing — the thing August would remember longest — was that the cold seemed to pause around the shrine, for just a moment, like a clerk looking up from a ledger to mark an entry received.

* * *

They buried her above the snowline, in ground so hard the digging took two hours and cost them a wagon axle pried apart for levers. Sefa did not cry. She stood at the graveside with her fists at her sides and her jaw set like her grandmother's had been, and when August offered her a place in the wagons she shook her head.

"I walk," she said. "She walked. I'll walk."

So the column moved out at first light, three hundred and ten souls now, and August walked at its head with a stranger's grief riding in the pouch against his chest and, within the hour, riding somewhere far less convenient — because the salt had begun to speak to him.

Not in words. In *weather*. A kitchen in Greywater with steam on the windows. A man's laugh, low and frequent, gone thirty years. A son's small hand in hers on a winter road much like this one. A daughter's sail shrinking on a gray horizon. Sixty years of loving people and losing them, one by one, until the losing was the shape her life had made — and all of it pressed into six ounces of salt that warmed against his sternum like a second heart, beating with someone else's sorrow.

By noon his eyes stung constantly and he didn't know whose tears they were. By dusk he had caught himself reaching for a wedding ring he had never worn, on a hand that was his own, grieving a man he had never met, and Wren Corvane fell into step beside him and said nothing for a long time, because she understood, better than anyone alive, what it was to be haunted by other people's words.

"It gets heavier as you go down," she said finally. "The annotations say so. The Court draws on the bearer. It wants you to arrive *full*."

"Then it will get what it wants," August said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "That's the arrangement."

Wren glanced at him — that quick, decoding glance she gave everything, as if the world were marginalia and she alone had learned to read it. "You're not afraid?"

He looked back down the column. Three hundred and ten people moving through the blue twilight, torches lit now against a darkness that arrived too early and lingered too long. Sefa walking at the rear with her chin up. Somewhere among them, his sister's soul waited below in a vault of salt and bone, and above them all the false winter tightened its grip like a fist closing around the throat of the mountain.

"I'm carrying a dead woman's grief," he said. "Fear would be a relief."

Ahead, the road bent east toward the old mine-heads above Greywater, and August felt the pouch against his chest pulse once, hard, like a heart startled — and felt, beneath his boots, through forty fathoms of stone, the Salt Court turn its attention upward and begin, patiently, to count them.
