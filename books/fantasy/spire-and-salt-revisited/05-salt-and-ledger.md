## 5. Salt and Ledger

The descent gate swallowed the last of the daylight behind us, and after that there was only salt.

Not the clean bite of sea air — this was older, heavier, a mineral silence that pressed against the ears like deep water. The tunnel walls glittered where Wren's lantern touched them, veins of white running through grey stone like frost grown inward. August had crossed the Thornfell passes for eleven years and had never once thought to wonder what lay beneath them. Now he knew. The mountain was hollow as a tooth, and something down here had been drinking.

"Stay close," he said. "And if you hear your own voice calling from the dark—"

"Don't answer it. I've read enough marginalia to know that one." Wren lifted the lantern higher. The light caught her face, hollowed by three days without proper sleep, and beneath it the book, strapped to her chest in oilcloth like a child she refused to set down. "Grandmother wrote a whole page on it. *The mountain speaks in borrowed voices.* She underlined it twice."

August grunted and kept walking. The floor sloped gently downward, worn smooth in places — smooth in ways water didn't explain. Something had traveled these corridors often, once. Or been dragged along them.

He felt the Court before he saw any sign of it. It came through his skin rather than his ears: a low, patient hum, the same frequency the ruined waystation had carried, but stronger now, closer, like standing beside a struck bell that never finished ringing. His storm-gift stirred in answer, gathering in his fingertips unbidden, and he flexed his hands until it settled. Eleven years of practice at holding lightning still, and it had taken the spires four days to undo all of it.

*They sold her,* he thought, for the hundredth time since the enforcers' ambush. *They sold my sister to cover my debt, and called it justice.*

Isolde's soul, held in payment beneath the mountain. Twenty years of it. He'd been seventeen when the bargain went wrong, when he'd traded a piece of his gift to save her from the fever, and the spires had needed someone to pay for what he'd spent. He'd always believed they'd chosen exile because it was merciful. Now he knew it was because it was tidy. A soul for a soul, an account balanced, a ledger closed with no loose threads left above ground.

Except the prophecy had left one thread loose. Him.

"The air's changing," Wren said.

She was right. The cold had shifted character — no longer the dry chill of stone but something damper, sharper, tasting faintly of brine and old iron. The salt in the walls thickened as they descended, first in veins, then in sheets, then in whole galleries of translucent crystal that the lantern lit up like frozen fire. And in that light, August saw the first of them.

"Wren." He put out an arm to stop her. "Don't look away from them. But don't look *at* them either."

"I know what wights are."

"You know what books say they are."

The figure stood perhaps thirty paces ahead, mid-stride, one foot raised as if caught between steps. It had been human once — the proportions were right, the posture — but salt had claimed it the way amber claims insects. Crystalline flesh, translucent and faintly luminous, every detail preserved: the tension in the shoulders, the turn of the head looking back over one shoulder toward the way it had come. Running. It had been running, and the mountain had simply stopped it, the way winter stops a river.

"They're not guards," August said quietly. "They're debtors. People who tried to leave without paying."

"And the Court let them become this instead?" Wren's voice was thin. "Why not just take the payment?"

"Because this is the payment." He looked at the wight's face, at the terror fixed forever in crystal, and understood. "Fear keeps. Fear is the only currency that doesn't spoil. Every one of these people is a coin the Court never has to spend."

There were more of them as they went deeper. Dozens. Hundreds. Whole processions of the crystallized, some alone, some clustered together — a family, maybe, their hands still reaching for each other across a gap of twenty years. A man in the rotted remains of a spire uniform, which meant even enforcers ran sometimes. A woman whose body had begun to dissolve back into raw salt, her edges slumping like a candle in warm air, as if even being a coin could be spent eventually.

Wren walked faster after that. August didn't blame her.

* * *

It was the humming that changed first.

The bell-tone in the bedrock swelled, softened, and resolved into something almost melodic — and then, impossibly, into a voice. Thin and sweet and carrying down the corridor from somewhere ahead, threading itself through the salt-light.

*"Augie."*

His whole body went rigid. No one had called him that in twenty years. No one alive had ever called him that except—

*"Augie, you came. I knew you'd come."*

She stepped out of a side gallery into the lantern light, and the world narrowed to a single point.

Isolde. Not as he'd left her at nineteen — she would be thirty-nine now, and the woman before him was thirty-nine, lines at her eyes, a scar on her chin from the fall off the mill roof that he'd teased her about for years afterward. Salt-pale, yes, translucent at the edges, yes, but *her*, down to the crooked way she stood with her weight on her left hip, down to the small defiant tilt of her head.

"Isolde." Her name came out of him like blood from a wound.

"You look old," she said, and laughed, and it was her laugh, the real one, the ugly-honest snorting one she'd never let anyone hear but family. "Eleven years and you finally come find me. I used to count the days. Then I stopped counting. Then I started again." She reached out her hand. "You're here. You're actually here. Take me home, Augie. Please. I'm so tired of being cold."

Every instinct in him screamed to take that hand. His feet were already moving. The storm-gift rose in his chest, eager, ready to burn through anything between him and her—

"*August.*"

Wren's hand closed on his arm. Hard. Her knuckles were white around the strap of the book.

"It's a wight," she said.

"Get off me."

"Look at her feet, August. *Look.*"

He looked. Isolde's feet did not quite touch the ground. There was no shadow under her, though the lantern threw both their shadows long and wavering against the crystal wall. And her eyes — he made himself really look at her eyes — were wrong. Not empty. Worse than empty. They were *attentive*, watching him with the careful hunger of something reading a page to learn how the story goes.

"She's real," he heard himself say. "Wren, she's — the scar, the laugh, things no wight could—"

"A wight wearing her face would know exactly those things. It would know everything the ledger knows. And the ledger knows what *you* remember." Wren didn't loosen her grip. "That's how it hunts. It doesn't show you a stranger. It shows you your own memory, wearing a face. Grandmother wrote it — *the false beloved offers the true hand; the true hand is the one that costs you.*"

The thing wearing Isolde's face tilted its head, and its expression flickered — hurt, wounded, perfectly calibrated. "You'd listen to her over me? Over your own sister?" Its voice cracked in the middle, and the crack was Isolde's crack, the one her voice did when she cried. "I waited twenty years, Augie. I counted every day. And you stand there and call me a *thing*?"

"Prove it," August said hoarsely. "Tell me something she wouldn't tell anyone. Tell me about the night Mother died."

The wight smiled. "You held her hand. You promised her you'd look after me. And you were so frightened you couldn't speak, so I spoke for you — I told her we'd be fine, and she believed me, and she died smiling." Tears slid down the crystal cheeks. "Is that proof enough? Does that hurt enough?"

It should have. It did. And that was exactly why August's stomach turned cold, because the memory it had just given back to him was *wrong*, and grief had nearly drowned the wrongness.

"That's not how it happened," he said slowly. "I did hold her hand. But I wasn't silent. I told her about the storm coming over the western hills, because she loved storms, and she said—" his voice broke, and he let it, "—she said *good, then part of me will get to see the world*. That was the last thing she said. Because I was there. Because it's mine."

The wight's smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing behind it moved.

"Check the book," August said.

Wren already had it open, oilcloth thrown back, pages turning in the lantern light. "Here. Grandmother's hand, bottom margin of the descent chapter." Her finger traced the faded ink. "*The false beloved knows your memories but cannot make new ones. Ask it what happened after. The dead past it can steal; the living present it cannot.*" She looked up. "Ask it what happened yesterday. Ask it anything that isn't yours to give it."

August turned back to the thing wearing his sister's face, and it cost him more than the ambush, more than the exile, more than anything in twenty years, to keep his voice level.

"What did I eat for breakfast this morning?"

The wight opened its mouth. Closed it. The smile remained, fixed now, painted on. "You had—"

"What did Wren say to me at the last campfire? What joke did she tell? What did the sky look like when we entered the gate?" He took a step forward, and the storm-gift rose in him not as fear now but as clarity, cold and bright. "Nothing. You have nothing. Everything I am that happened *after* her death, you can't touch — because I haven't finished remembering it yet. My sister is twenty years of memories I'm still making room for. You're just the shape of my wanting."

For a moment the wight held its form, beautiful and terrible, Isolde's face stretched over nothing at all.

Then it came apart.

Not violently — worse, gently, like frost surrendering to morning. The crystal face softened, ran, sloughed into white powder that drifted down and joined the salt of the floor, and for half a heartbeat August saw what was underneath: a thin, starved figure, features eroded past recognition, its mouth open in a soundless scream that had been screaming for decades. Then that too dissolved, and there was only salt settling in the lantern light, and the corridor, and the humming of the Court far below, patient as arithmetic.

August realized he was on his knees. He didn't remember falling.

Wren crouched beside him. She didn't offer comfort, which he was grateful for; she offered the truth instead, which was the only thing that would hold him together.

"It used your love as a hook," she said quietly. "That's all it had. That's all any of them have. The Court can't create, August. It can only spend what's already been paid in." She pressed the book into his hands, open to the margin, to their grandmother's ink. "Which means everything down here is secondhand. Remember that. Whatever waits below — whatever it shows us — none of it will be new."

He looked at the powder that had been a person. At the hundreds more waiting in the dark galleries, coins in a vault, each one somebody's sister, somebody's whole remembered life.

"No," he said, rising, wiping the salt dust from his palms. "Some of it will be mine."

The corridor bent downward, and the humming swelled to meet them, and far below, through a fissure in the crystal wall, August saw the first pale glow of the Court — vast and slow and shining like a heart of brine.

And carved into the wall beside the fissure, fresh enough that salt dust still clung to the edges of the letters, in a hand he had known since childhood:

*Augie — don't trust the book.*

Isolde's handwriting.
