## 6. The Court Beneath

The last gate was made of salt and would not open for force.

August learned this the hard way, driving his shoulder against it until the crystal sang a low, grudging note and Wren pulled him back by the sleeve. "It's not a door," she said. "It's a toll. Look."

She was right. The gate wasn't carved so much as grown — a single sheet of translucent mineral, veined with threads of darker salt that wound through it like writing. And when August held his palm against its surface, the veins stirred. They gathered toward his hand the way iron filings gather toward a lodestone, and beneath his skin his storm-gift woke without being asked, a low rolling pressure like weather deciding where to break.

"It knows me," he said.

"Of course it does." Wren's voice had gone thin. She'd been thinner all day, ever since the wight wearing Isolde's face had come apart into drifting grains at her proof from the margins. He'd watched her hands shake afterward and pretended not to. "You're in the book. You've been in the book since before you were born. This whole mountain has been waiting for you the way a snare waits for a foot."

He didn't answer that. He pressed harder instead, and let the storm rise.

It came up through him the way it always did now — unbidden, half-remembered, a gift that felt more like a debt. Lightning lived in the salt. He could feel it there, sleeping in the crystal lattice, and when he called, the gate drank it. The veins flared white. Somewhere far above them, thunder answered through a hundred fathoms of stone, and the sheet of salt parted down its center like a curtain drawn by careful hands.

Cold air rolled out. It smelled of brine and old incense and something sweeter underneath, something that made his mouth water and his eyes sting — the smell, he realized, of memory itself, if memory had a scent. Like rain on a road you'd walked as a child. Like his mother's kitchen. Like Isolde's hair after she'd swum in the cold rivers below the spires.

Wren made a small sound behind him. When he glanced back, tears were freezing on her cheeks, and she was smiling. "I wrote about this," she whispered. "Grandmother's hand, page two hundred and eleven: *the door remembers what you remember.* I never thought I'd smell it too."

They went in together, because they had agreed to do everything together now, and because neither of them said aloud that one of them might not walk out.

* * *

The Court Beneath was not what the smugglers' songs described.

There were no thrones of bone, no rivers of weeping brine, no chained souls rattling their cups against the bars of their ribs. There was only space — vast, vaulted, impossible space, a cathedral hollowed out of the mountain's heart and hung with chandeliers of salt that burned without flame, casting light the color of old pearls. The floor was polished to a mirror's depth, and when August looked down at himself walking across it, he saw not one reflection but many: himself at seven, himself at nineteen, himself the night he'd signed the bargain, himself old, himself dead, himself standing in a doorway holding a lantern while someone he loved walked away down a long dark road.

He stopped looking at the floor.

Figures moved in the vaults along the walls — alcoves of crystal, each one holding a shape. Some were clearly human. Some had been human once and had been worn smooth, like sea glass, like river stones, like things the water keeps. They didn't speak. But as August passed, they turned toward him, all of them, in perfect silence, in perfect unison, and he understood with a lurch that they knew his name. That they had known it for a hundred years.

"Don't read their faces," Wren murmured. "Grandmother's warning. *In the Court, every face is an invoice.*"

At the center of the cathedral stood a table.

That was all. A long table of black salt, scarred and mended a thousand times, set with scales of bone and ledgers bound in hide he refused to identify. Behind it stood the Salt Regent.

He had expected a monster. What stood there was worse: a person. Tall, gray-robed, with a face like a face you'd trust — a face assembled from every kind face August had ever known, his grandmother's patience laid over a caravan master's warmth laid over Isolde's crooked smile. It should have been horrifying. Instead, some traitorous part of August wanted to sit down across from it and tell it everything.

"You came," the Regent said. Its voice was the hum in the mountain, the frequency only storm-callers felt, given words at last. "A century of correspondence, and you finally came in person."

"I'm not here for correspondence." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I'm here for my sister. Isolde Dravenmoor. Sold to this Court eleven years ago by the Spire Conclave, against a debt that was mine. I've come to settle it."

The Regent tilted its borrowed head. Around the cathedral, the chandeliers dimmed by a fraction, attentive.

"And how," it asked gently, "do you propose to pay?"

* * *

He had rehearsed this on the descent. Through the caverns of crystallized debtors, past the wights frozen mid-flight with their faces full of longing, he had turned the words over until they were smooth as river stones. Now he laid them on the table like coins.

"Myself. My gift, my years, my name. Everything the spires cast me out for, everything I am — yours, in exchange for Isolde's soul, freed and whole and walking out of this mountain beside me."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the eardrums like deep water. Somewhere in the vaults, a crystal shape shifted — or he imagined it did.

Then the Regent smiled, and August's stomach dropped, because the smile came too fast. Too easily. There was no weighing of terms, no cold arithmetic of souls, none of the haggling every smuggler's song promised. The Regent simply reached for a ledger, opened it to a marked page, and dipped a pen.

"Agreed," it said.

And that was when August saw it.

The ledger lay open before him, and the page was old. Not days old, not years old — a century old, the ink browned, the handwriting faded but legible. And there, in the middle of the ancient columns, written in a hand he recognized because he had spent three weeks studying it by lamplight in a ruined waystation:

*August Dravenmoor offers himself for Isolde Dravenmoor. Accepted.*

Dated today. Dated a hundred years ago.

The cathedral seemed to tilt. He gripped the table's edge and the salt bit his palms, cold and real, while the truth assembled itself in his chest with terrible, patient clarity. Wren's grandmother hadn't decoded the prophecy. She'd transcribed it — copied it from somewhere, annotated it, believed it, died for it. And where had the text come from? Who writes a prophecy that names a man not yet born, that circles a date, that walks him step by step down a mountain to this exact table?

"The book," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "The prophecy. You wrote it."

"We drafted it," the Regent corrected, mild as a clerk. "Prophecy is merely contract with better marketing. Your spires have their storms; we have our paperwork. A soul freely offered is worth ten taken — everyone knows this. But a soul that believes it chose? That believes it *fulfilled*?" The Regent's pen hovered, patient. "That is the finest currency there is, August Dravenmoor. We have been cultivating you since before your birth. The exile. The caravan roads. Even the archivist — did you think it chance that she found you? That her grandmother's book survived a century of fires and floods to arrive at the precise waystation on the precise night?"

Behind him, Wren made a wounded sound. He didn't turn around. He couldn't. If he turned around and saw her face, he would have to consider that even their meeting had been inked a hundred years ago, and he wasn't sure what would be left of him.

"So it's done," he said instead. Flatly. "Whatever I choose, the ending is already written."

"Oh, the *ending* is written." The Regent set down the pen and folded its stolen, kindly hands. "Endings are easy. Endings are what we sell. But here is what your prophecies never mention, what no ledger records, what even we cannot price—" and it leaned forward, and for one flickering instant the assembled kindness fell away and August saw something vast and hungry and *old* looking out through the mask, something that had been trading in other people's hearts for so long it had forgotten what one felt like from the inside—

"—no one," the Regent said softly, "has ever read to the last page."

The chandeliers flared. In the vaults, a hundred crystal shapes turned their faces toward him at once, and this time he was certain — certain — that they were not watching him walk into the trap.

They were watching to see what he did next.

The Regent drew the ledger closer. "Your sister will be brought up. You may say your farewells before the transfer. And then, August Dravenmoor, we conclude a transaction one hundred years in the making—"

From the depths of Wren's satchel, muffled and sudden, came the sound of tearing paper.
