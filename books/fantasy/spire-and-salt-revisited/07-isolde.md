## 7. Isolde

The vaults of the Salt Court do not sleep, and neither, strictly speaking, does Isolde Dravenmoor.

She lies on a shelf of translucent salt in the third gallery down — the one the wights call the Quiet Row, though nothing here has a voice to call anything — and she counts. It is what she does instead of dreaming. Counting keeps the edges sharp.

Four hundred and eleven memories, give or take the ones she's still deciding whether to keep.

That is how she has survived six years in a place that eats people politely, one withdrawal at a time. The Court takes its debtors' memories as interest, and most souls let it happen the way rain lets itself fall — passively, gratefully, dissolving. Isolde had watched her first neighbor on the Quiet Row go that way: a ferryman named Aldous who began by surrendering his worst day and ended by forgetting he'd ever had hands. He smiled the whole time. That was the horror of it. The Court made surrender feel like rest.

Isolde had decided, somewhere in the first dark month, that she would rather be a thief than a saint.

So she stole herself back, piece by piece. The Court's ledger-keepers were meticulous about debts but careless about inventory — a soul shelved for payment was considered settled, inert, not worth guarding the way an active account was guarded. Isolde learned the vaults the way she'd once learned the tide-tables of their father's fishing village: by watching, by waiting, by noticing which doors breathed open when the Regent's processions passed. She slipped memories out of the counting-halls tucked against the inside of her cheek like hard candy. She hoarded them in the hollow of her sternum, where the salt couldn't reach. Some were hers. Most weren't.

She kept a lighthouse keeper's memory of his daughter learning to whistle. A bride's memory of laughing so hard at her own wedding she snorted wine. A child's memory of the exact color of the sky over Thornfell on the last warm day before the winter came wrong. She kept a smuggler's memory of a woman singing off-key in a waystation kitchen, flour on her knuckles —

*August*, she thought, and stopped counting, because the four hundred and twelfth memory was always him, and he was always worth more than arithmetic.

They had taken her soul as payment for his bargain. She knew that now, knew it with the terrible clarity the salt gave you once you stopped fighting it. Her brother had traded something to save her life when the fever took her at nineteen, and the spires — those beautiful, rotten towers — had sold the debt down-mountain rather than carry it themselves. August had been exiled for the crime of loving her expensively. And Isolde had spent six years crystallizing in the dark, furious on his behalf, hoarding other people's joy like a dragon of small warm things.

The salt around her had begun, lately, to sing.

Not music. A frequency. A hum in the deep stone that only the storm-touched could feel, and even here, even soul-shelved, some scrap of the old blood in her answered it. The wights grew restless. The Regent's processions came more often, its ledger-pages turning faster, and above — she could taste it through the mountain's roots — the winter was falling early, greedy, drawn upward like breath through a straw.

Something was coming down toward her. She could feel that too.

* * *

They came for her on a day that had no name, which was every day, which was why she counted.

Two wights escorted her up through the galleries, their crystal faces catching the brine-light, and Isolde walked between them with her chin up because whatever this was, she refused to be carried. The vaults opened at last into the Court itself — that cathedral of bone-white columns and hanging sheets of salt that chimed when the Regent moved — and there, standing small and stubborn before a throne of compressed centuries, was a figure she would have known blindfolded, deaf, dissolved to nothing.

He was thinner. His hair had gone grey at the temples, which was absurd, because August was only twenty-nine. He stood like a man holding a door open against a gale.

"Isolde." Her name in his voice cracked something the salt had spent six years sealing.

"Hello, August," she said, and was proud that it came out steady. "You look terrible."

He laughed — a broken, wet sound — and crossed the distance, and then stopped short, hands hovering, as if afraid she'd shatter. Which, to be fair, she might have. "I came for you. I found the prophecy, I found the gate, I — Wren decoded it, the whole thing was written a century ago, my name was already in it —"

"I know whose name was in it." She looked past him at the Regent, that vast slow shape of brine and patience coiled around its ledger, and lowered her voice. "August. Listen to me very carefully. Why is it so quiet in here? Why did they bring me up without a fight?"

His face did the thing it had always done when he was about to be noble — the set jaw, the storm-grey eyes going flat and determined. "Because we've made an exchange. My gift for your freedom. The Regent agreed. You walk out of here, and I stay whole, just — ordinary. It's done, Issy. You're free."

The endearment nearly undid her. She let it, for one breath. Then she took his face in her cold hands and made him look at her.

"It agreed too easily," she said. "Didn't it."

"...It's bound by its own laws of exchange—"

"It wrote the book, August." The words came out of her fast now, six years of hoarded certainty spilling like stolen coins. "You think a century-old prophecy just *happened* to name you? The Court doesn't predict the future. It *orders* it. Every debtor down here arrived believing they were the hero of their own rescue — I've watched hundreds of them come through those doors, certain, triumphant, walking straight into the trade the text promised them. The prophecy isn't a map. It's a hook. And you swallowed it all the way down."

Behind him, the Regent stirred, and the hanging salt-sheets chimed a low dissonant chord, and Isolde saw her brother hear her — really hear her — and refuse to.

"You're free," he repeated. "Whatever else is true, that part is true. Take it. Please. I've carried this for six years—"

"And I've *counted* for six years." She stepped back, arms crossing, and summoned the memory she'd been saving — the smuggler's memory, the kitchen, the song. She pressed it into his palm. "Do you know what this is? A woman named Maren, from a caravan you guided two winters ago. She lost this memory to a salt-wight on the high pass and never knew what she'd lost, only that she woke up sad. I stole it back. I've been stealing things back for years, August — not just mine. *Everyone's*. Four hundred and eleven of them. Because I found something down here in the margins of the ledger, in the parts nobody reads because nobody thinks a shelved soul can read."

"What?" His voice had gone careful.

"The ledger records debts. Transfers, payments, forfeitures. But it has no column for *refusal*." She let that land. "A debt can be transferred. That's the law the Regent hides behind — everything must balance, someone must always pay. But a transfer requires consent on the receiving end. And if one debtor stands surety — stands as collateral for *all* the others, voluntarily, wholly — then every individual debt collapses into a single account. One account. One holder." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a girl who had gutted fish at dawn and out-stubborned the sea. "And accounts can be *refused*, August. Refused en masse. The whole rotten book, voided in a single stroke — if someone is fool enough, and loved enough, to stand surety for everyone at once."

The silence rang like struck glass.

"That's not in the prophecy," he said finally.

"No," Isolde agreed. "Which means it's the one thing they didn't plan for."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued — they were Dravenmoors, argument was their native language, more fluent than grief. August raged that surety meant surrendering everything, not just his gift but his whole remembered life, every road and regret poured into the Court's maw until nothing was left to walk out with. Isolde countered that she had watched souls dissolve for six years and knew exactly how much of a person could be given before the person stopped being a person, and that the answer was: less than he feared, if the giving was chosen rather than taken.

"They take," she said, jabbing a finger at the Regent's slow-turning bulk. "We *give*. It matters. It's the entire difference. Their whole law runs on taking dressed up as exchange. Chosen sacrifice breaks the grammar."

"You'll be alone up there if this fails—"

"I've been alone for six years in a salt vault, brother mine. Alone is my *homeland*."

In the end — and she saw it happen, saw the moment land behind his eyes like weather — he stopped arguing. Not because she'd won. Because he'd understood. That was always the frightening thing about August: he never lost arguments, he simply absorbed them until they became his own idea.

"There's a page," he said quietly. "The last page of the prophecy. Wren says the margins warn of a false ending. I think — I think I'm supposed to walk the script first. Make the trade. Get you out. And then come back down."

"Then we do it in order," Isolde said. "Walk your false ending, August. Walk it all the way into the light. And when the light turns out to be another shelf—" she gripped his hands, her cold fingers around his warm ones, the first time they'd touched in six years and possibly the last, "—you come back down here, and you stand surety for all of us. Every debtor. Every wight. Even the ones who forgot their own names. Especially them."

"And if I forget mine?"

"Then I'll remember it for you." Her voice cracked at last, and she let it. "It's what sisters are for. We keep count."

Above them, far above, the winter pressed down on the mountain like a closing hand, and the Regent turned its ledger-page with a sound like a glacier calving, patient, certain, certain, certain.

Isolde held her brother's eyes and did not blink.

*Go up,* she mouthed. *Come back down. Break the book.*
