## 8. The False Ending

The Salt Regent's voice was the sound of tide withdrawing over gravel, patient and vast and utterly without malice. *It is done,* it said. *The exchange is sealed.*

August felt it leave him.

Not pain — he had braced for pain — but something worse: absence. The storm-gift had lived in him since he was nine years old, a second heartbeat lodged behind his sternum, a hum in his teeth when thunder gathered over Thornfell. He had hated it in the spires, resented it, blamed it for every door that closed against him. He had not understood until this moment that it was also the thing that made him *him* — the frequency he answered to, the thing the mountain sang to at night.

Now there was silence where it had been. A room in his chest with the furniture gone.

He staggered. Isolde caught his arm.

Her hand was warm. That was the first miracle — after eleven years of salt and vault-dark, his sister's hand was warm, and her eyes were her own, grey as slate rain, and she was looking at him with an expression he'd carried in memory since the night the spires took her: half fury, half unbearable tenderness.

"You absolute idiot," she said, and her voice cracked on the last word. "You came."

"Someone had to," August managed. "You were always terrible at escaping things. You got lost in our own cellar."

"I was *six*."

"And I never let you forget it." His legs nearly folded. She held him up, and they stood together in the cathedral of brine and bone while the Salt Regent watched from its throne of compressed centuries, and somewhere above them Wren Corvane was presumably reciting marginalia at the walls and calling it navigation.

Isolde laughed — wet, broken, real. "Come on," she said. "Before it changes its mind."

The Regent did not change its mind. It extended one limb of white crystal, and the way out opened like a wound of light — a stair cut through the salt, ascending toward air, toward winter, toward the grey smear of sky that was all Thornfell allowed itself these days. The ledger's chains fell away from Isolde's wrists as she passed beneath them, dissolving into grains that drifted down like snow falling upward in reverse.

They climbed. August counted the steps because counting was easier than feeling the hole inside himself. Two hundred and eleven steps. At step ninety, Isolde began humming the song their mother used to sing when the storms came off the coast, and August's throat closed so tightly he could not have spoken if the mountain demanded it.

At step two hundred, light.

Real light — thin, watery, snow-filtered, but *light*, pouring down the stairwell like water into a dry throat. August walked into it and felt it land on his face and thought, absurdly, *I should have done this years ago. I should have walked into the sun every day of my life just because I could.*

They emerged onto a ledge halfway down Thornfell's southern face, the world spread below them: the caravan road, the refugee camps strung along it like beads of grey wool, the villages in the valley wearing shrouds of early snow. The cold hit him and he welcomed it. Cold meant weather. Weather meant the sky still turned.

Wren was waiting at the ledge's mouth, prophecy book clutched to her chest, frost in her wild hair, her face doing three things at once — relief, terror, and the particular grim satisfaction of a woman whose footnotes had proven correct.

"It worked," she said. "You actually — the text said *the thief descends and ascends bearing what was stolen*, and I wrote in the margin, I wrote *surely not literally*, but you—"

"Wren." Isolde stepped past August and took the archivist's frozen hands. "You're the one who kept him alive down here. I saw your annotations in the vaults. They were passing your pages around like scripture."

"They were *not* scripture, they were *corrections*—" Wren stopped. Blinked. "You read my grandmother's book. Down there. In the Court."

"Bits of it. The wights traded pages for memories." Isolde smiled, and it was a smile August hadn't seen since childhood, crooked and sharp. "Your grandmother was right about almost everything."

"*Almost*," Wren repeated, and something flickered across her face — the shadow of a doubt she'd been carrying for thirty years, the doubt she'd confessed once by firelight: *the final page, August. The margins warn of a false ending. I've never been able to read it. The ink won't hold still.*

August remembered that firelight conversation now, standing in the snow, and felt the first cold thread of wrongness slide under his ribs.

Because the ink wouldn't hold still. And the final page was in Wren's book. And Wren's book was right there.

"Open it," he said.

Wren looked at him. "What?"

"The last page. Open it now. While we're standing in the light." His heart had begun to pound in the old rhythm, the storm-rhythm, except the storm was gone and the pounding had nothing to answer to. "Something's wrong. I can't feel the mountain anymore, Wren. I can't feel anything but cold. Open the page."

She opened it.

For a long moment nothing happened. The page lay blank and pale as the salt around them, and the wind moved, and Isolde's breath fogged, and August let himself believe — for exactly four heartbeats — that the false ending was a warning about something else entirely, some other ending, some other book.

Then the ink rose.

It came up through the paper like blood through linen, black lines spreading in patterns too deliberate to be accident, and the letters assembled themselves in a script August recognized from a hundred nights of Wren's lectures: the annotator's hand, his grandmother's hand, the hand that had circled his name beside today's date a century before he was born.

But the words were new. The words were being written *now*.

*And the thief ascends into the light, believing,* the page said. *And the Court shelves what it has taken, as it has always shelved. And the winter deepens, for the Court is hungry, and hunger does not honor bargains.*

"No," Wren whispered. "No, no — this isn't my grandmother's hand, this is — someone is writing this *now*—"

Below them, the valley flickered.

August saw it happen and would spend the rest of his life unable to describe it properly: the refugee camps on the road below simply *dimmed*, the way a candle dims when a hand cups around it. Not extinguished. Cupped. Held. Somewhere beneath their feet, in the cathedral of brine and bone, hundreds of souls that should have been walking free were being set back on shelves, and the Court was drinking the warmth of everyone who loved them, and the snow over Thornfell thickened from weather into appetite.

A village light went out in the valley. Then another. Then a whole string of them, east to west, like a seam being unpicked.

Isolde made a sound August had never heard from her — small, involuntary, the sound of someone watching their own hope get repossessed. "I felt them," she said. "In the vaults. There were others like me, August, dozens, we were going to walk out together, I *promised* them—"

The page kept writing.

*The bargain is honored,* it said, in letters that gleamed wet and fresh. *One soul for one soul. The Court keeps its ledgers perfectly. Ask instead who wrote the ledger. Ask who needed the thief to come believing.*

And then, beneath it, in smaller script, in a hand August finally recognized — not the grandmother's, not the annotator's, but the looping, elegant, tide-worn script he had heard speaking to him from a crystal throne:

*Thank you for your gift. It will keep us warm through the long dark.*

The Regent's handwriting. In a century-old book. On a page that had refused to be read for thirty years.

The prophecy hadn't been a map. It had been a lure, cast a hundred years upstream, and every annotation, every circled date, every desperate archivist who'd given her life to decoding it — they had all been the line, and August had been the hook, and he had swallowed it whole with his eyes open because wanting his sister back had made him *grateful* for the trap.

"We have to go back," Isolde said.

"No." August heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "You don't understand what just happened. I paid. The gift is gone. If I walk back down there, I'm nothing — no storm, no leverage, no currency. The Court trades in value, and I have none left."

"Then we find another kind of value." Isolde grabbed his sleeve. "There are people down there, August. People who hoarded memories like I did, who kept themselves alive coin by coin, waiting for—"

"For me to free them. And I didn't. I freed *you*. One soul for one soul, exactly as scripted, and the rest got re-shelved, and the Court got my gift as a bonus." He looked at the dead village lights. He thought of the dying woman on the mountain road whose memories he carried in a vial against his heart — grief that wasn't his, payment for passage he had accepted and not yet delivered. He thought of the wights crystallized mid-flight, faces full of longing, and how one of them had worn Isolde's face and he had almost believed it, because belief was the one thing the Court could always farm from him.

Despair came first. He let it. He stood in the snow and let it fill the empty room in his chest where the storm used to live, let it press against the walls, let it be as large as it wanted to be. Wren watched him do it and, to her credit, said nothing. Some grief you have to let finish its sentence.

But underneath the despair, something else was assembling itself. Slowly. The way pressure builds before lightning.

The Court kept perfect ledgers. That was its law, its nature, its prison as much as anyone's. It could not take what was not offered; it could not break a bargain; it could not refuse an exchange fairly tendered. Every trap it built was built *of its own rules* — which meant every rule was also a wall the Court itself could not pass.

The prophecy had scripted his ascent. Fine. Let the script end here, on a snowy ledge, with the ink still wet.

Nothing in the prophecy — not the original text, not the false ending, not the Regent's gloating postscript — had imagined what he might do next. Because next required nothing. Next required a man with no gift, no leverage, no value at all, walking back down two hundred and eleven steps into the dark.

That was the one move the Court couldn't price.

"August." Isolde's grip tightened. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking the ledger cuts both ways." He turned to Wren and held out his hand. "Give me the book."

She stared at him. "The book is the trap—"

"The book is the only thing down there older than the Regent. Its rules predate the Court's handwriting — your grandmother annotated it, the *Court* responded. Which means the book came first." He flexed his fingers. "I need to know what the true ending says, Wren. The one under the false one. There has to be one. Ink doesn't write over nothing."

Wren's jaw worked. Snow gathered on her eyelashes. Thirty years of solitary scholarship warred visibly with thirty seconds of watching a prophecy betray everything she'd believed — and then, slowly, she unslung the book from her shoulder and pressed it into his hands, and her expression was the same one she'd worn at the ruined waystation when she'd shown him his own name in a century-old margin: terrified, certain, and choosing anyway.

"If you go back down," she said, "and the Court takes whatever's left of you—"

"Then write it down." August opened the book. The false ending's ink was already fading, its purpose served, the page returning to blank. "You're the archivist. Make sure somebody reads it."

He kissed his sister's forehead — salt and warmth and eleven lost years — and stepped back toward the stair.

"August Dravenmoor," Isolde called after him, and her voice was their mother's voice, storm-voiced, unbending. "You come back up. You hear me? Whatever you find down there — you *come back up*."

He raised one hand without turning around, because if he turned around he would not go, and the lights in the valley were going out, and somewhere below him a cathedral of brine and bone was keeping perfect books.

Two hundred and eleven steps.

He started counting again, down into the dark.
