## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ink was the first thing Lena Vane had learned to fear.

Not the collectors with their iron-tipped staves, not the Provost's smile, not even the debt-mark burned into her left wrist when she turned fourteen — but the ink. Because ink remembered. Ink held every stroke exactly as it fell, and in the Saltspire scriptorium, where she had copied contracts for eleven years, a single wavering line could add a season to a man's servitude or strike a child's name from a family's obligation. The senior scribes said ink was honest. Lena had learned better. Ink was obedient. It recorded whatever hand commanded it, and so the only question that mattered, morning to night, was whose hand.

She sat now at the long desk by the eastern window, where the light came down gray and salt-white off the Spire itself, and wrote *Maren Cotter, weaver, owes the Temple of the Saltspire four crowns, eleven shillings, and service until repaid* for the ninth time that morning. Nine copies. One for the archive, one for the collector assigned to the Cotters' parish, one for the temple treasury, and six more because the bureaucracy of debt was a many-mouthed beast and each mouth required feeding. Maren Cotter would never see any of them. Maren Cotter would see only the collector at her door, and the stave, and the ledger opened against her like a wound.

Lena's pen did not waver. That was the discipline they had beaten into her — literally, in her first year, when a novice named Pell had let his attention drift and produced a contract with a smudged figure, and the Master of Scribes had caned him across both palms in front of the whole room while explaining, in a voice like dry paper, that the temple's authority lived in its documents and its documents lived in their precision. Pell had left the scriptorium that day and been reassigned to the tanneries. His debt had not lessened. It never did.

Around her, forty other scribes scratched in silence. The room smelled of tallow, oak gall, and the faint mineral tang that seeped up from the Saltspire's foundations — the mountain's breath, the novices called it, though Lena thought of it as something older and less friendly. Through the high windows she could see the Spire itself rising above the temple roofs: a needle of white stone crusted with ancient salt deposits, glittering even under cloud, from which the whole city took its name and its wealth. Salt was the kingdom's second god. The crown taxed it, the temple blessed it, and the poor of the lowtowns ate bread made bitter with the cheap grey grade and dreamed of the white.

*Maren Cotter.* Lena finished the ninth copy, blotted it, squared it with the others. Her own account was kept in a separate ledger, the personal register, and she knew its contents the way other people knew their prayers: *Lena Vane, orphan of the Thornfell flood-year, bound at age nine for board and schooling; principal long since consumed by interest; current balance, unpayable within a natural lifetime.* The temple had taught her letters, arithmetic, three scripts, and the fine art of writing herself deeper into bondage with every beautiful, precise page.

She was very good at her work. That was the joke no one laughed at.

* * *

The bell rang for midday meal, and the scribes rose together like a flock taking wing, chairs scraping, pens capped. Lena remained seated, hunched over her desk as though struggling with a difficult entry — a performance she had refined over years, shoulders curved inward, brow furrowed, the picture of a woman too conscientious to eat on time. Brother Aldous, the under-master who supervised the afternoon shift, paused beside her on his way out.

"Trouble with the Cotter accounts?"

"Only volume, Brother." She did not look up. "Nine copies, and the treasury wants a tenth."

"Devotion is its own reward," Aldous said, which was what he always said, and left.

The door boomed shut behind the last of them. Lena counted to sixty. Then another sixty. The scriptorium's silence settled around her like snowfall, and beneath it she could hear the building's old bones ticking — the temple had stood for six hundred years and groaned like an old man in cold weather.

She rose, crossed to the supply cabinet, and took down a fresh quill, a stick of sealing wax, and — this was the dangerous part, done without hesitation, without looking at the door, because hesitation was what got people caught — a blank travel writ from the stack reserved for pilgrims' passes. Pilgrim writs were the simplest documents the temple issued: name, parish, destination shrine, duration, seal. They were also, if altered correctly, indistinguishable from general travel permits, because the temple had grown lazy about its own forms two generations ago and no one had ever bothered to fix it. Lena had noticed the flaw at nineteen and had spent five years since perfecting the exploitation of it.

Back at her desk, she worked fast but never hurried. Hurrying showed in the hand. She trimmed the writ's margins to match the standard permit dimensions, adjusted the destination line — *shrine of Saint Verel* became, with two strokes and a ligature, *the free town of Verel's Crossing* — and set the date forward by a week, matching the ink tone by breathing on the fresh characters until they dulled. Last came the seal. The temple's signet sat locked in the Provost's office, but seals were wax and wax could be pressed, and Lena owned a dozen impressions taken over years from discarded documents, cast in soap and then in tin, hidden in a hollow beneath the loose flagstone by her cot. She melted a bead of dark wax, pressed her counterfeit, and held the finished writ up to the light.

*Tomas Bell, cooper, of the parish of Lowmarsh, is granted passage to Verel's Crossing, there to seek lawful work.*

Tomas Bell owed the temple seven crowns and would owe them for thirty years. In three days he would be gone — over the river and into the hill country, where the crown's collectors rarely bothered to follow and the temple's reach thinned to nothing. He would be a fugitive, yes. But he would be a fugitive with hands and a trade and a life, instead of a number in a ledger growing fat on compound interest.

Lena slid the writ between the pages of a psalter and began, calmly, copying the tenth Cotter contract.

This was the arithmetic no one taught in the scriptorium: that the temple's ledgers recorded debts, and debts were stories, and stories could be rewritten. Every forged writ was a sentence struck from someone's bondage. She had sent eleven people over the mountains in five years. Eleven names she kept nowhere, written in no ink, held only in the place behind her ribs where she kept everything that mattered. The collectors called the fleeing ones *leakage*. The temple called it theft of temple property — because that was what a debtor was, in the end. Property. Lena had read enough contracts to know the word appeared in the margins, in the legal glosses, small as a spider's leg: *chattel, until discharged.*

She had simply decided, one night at twenty-two, sitting in this same chair with her own account open before her, that she would rather be a thief than property. The decision had changed nothing about her days and everything about her nights.

* * *

The trouble came at the hour before dusk, as trouble always did — unannounced, wearing an official face.

She heard the scriptorium door open behind her and knew immediately that something was wrong, because the footsteps were wrong. Scribes shuffled; monks glided; the Master of Scribes strode. These steps were heavy, deliberate, and accompanied by the soft clink of metal fittings. Collector's gear. Lena did not turn. She finished the character she was writing, set down her pen, dusted the page, and then turned in her chair with the expression of mild, tired inquiry that was her armor.

Collector Dane stood in the aisle with a junior clerk behind him, and Dane was smiling, which was worse than if he hadn't been. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with the permanent flush of a drinker and the eyes of a man who enjoyed his work far more than his wages justified.

"Scribe Vane." He pronounced it like something he'd found on his boot. "Working late again. They say you never leave."

"The work doesn't leave, Collector." She gestured at the desks. "Contracts don't copy themselves."

"No. They don't." He came down the aisle slowly, trailing one finger along the desktops, and stopped at hers. His gaze moved over the neat stacks, the blotted pages, the psalter lying closed at her elbow — closed, thank every forgotten god, with the forged writ buried in its heart. "We've had a report."

"Have you."

"A discrepancy." He leaned in, and she caught the wine on his breath, sour under the incense. "Tomas Bell. Cooper. Lowmarsh parish. His collector went to serve papers yesterday and found the house empty. Wife gone too. And here's the thing—" he tapped the psalter once, lightly, and Lena's heartbeat did not change, because she had trained it not to, "—the parish register shows Bell requested a pilgrim's pass last month. Requested. Never issued. Yet a neighbor swears she saw Bell showing off a sealed writ, all proper-like, two days before he vanished."

"How strange." Lena's voice was flat, bored, faintly impatient — the voice of a woman interrupted over trivia. Inside, she was running the sequence backward with cold speed. *Bell had shown the writ. Fool. She had told them all, told them a hundred times: burn it after crossing, never show it, never—* "Perhaps he stole one. Pilgrim writs go missing; the shrine offices are careless. You'd have to take that up with the Provost's office."

"Maybe I will." Dane pulled out the chair opposite hers and sat, uninvited, making himself comfortable. "Or maybe I'll take it up right here. Because here's my thinking, Scribe. Writs don't walk out of this building on their own. Someone with access copied one. Someone clever. Someone who sits in this room all day with ink on her fingers." He smiled again, wider. "Someone with a debt of her own that never seems to shrink, no matter how many thousands of pages she writes. Doesn't that ever make you angry, Vane? Copying freedom for the temple all day, owning none of it yourself?"

"Every scribe in this room has a debt," Lena said. "If anger disqualified us, the scriptorium would be empty."

It was the right answer — dry, compliant, just enough edge to be believable. Dane's smile flickered. He wanted her frightened. Frightened people babbled, and babble could be spun into confession. She gave him nothing but the flat patience of the truly exhausted, and after a moment he grunted and stood.

"Open the psalter."

There it was. Lena felt the cold pass through her and out the other side, leaving clarity behind, the way a fever breaks. If she refused, he could summon the under-master and force the search, and the writ would be found, and she would be in the Provost's office by nightfall, and by spring she would be in the temple's penal workshops where debtors who committed document-crimes went to have their hands ruined past any further forging. If she complied — if she opened it herself, unhurried, innocent —

She picked up the psalter and opened it. To the wrong place. To the Song of the Deep Water, near the front, and let the pages fall naturally as she turned it toward him, and Dane leaned over her shoulder scanning for anything loose between leaves, and found nothing, because the writ was not there.

It was in her sleeve. It had been in her sleeve since the moment he tapped the cover — a small sleight accomplished while his eyes were on her face, her fingers working inside the book's shadow. Five years of practice lived in that motion. She set the psalter down, open, empty, and looked at him with raised brows.

"Satisfied? Or shall I fetch the under-master, so we can do this properly? I'm sure Brother Aldous will want a record of the accusation. The Provost does keep records of everything." She let that sit. "Everything, Collector. Including complaints against temple staff who disrupt the scriptorium without cause."

Dane stared at her. For a long moment the only sound was the junior clerk shifting his weight by the door. Then the collector snorted, straightened his belt, and stepped back.

"Cold fish," he said, almost admiringly. "You know, most of them shake."

"I shook once," Lena said. "Eleven years ago. I found it didn't help."

He laughed at that — a real laugh, ugly and surprised — and left, boots ringing on the stone, the clerk scurrying after. At the door he paused. "Bell will surface somewhere. They always do. And when he does, I'll remember your face, Scribe Vane."

"Everyone remembers my face," Lena said. "I write the contracts."

Then he was gone, and the door boomed, and she sat alone in the vast dimming room with a stolen writ against her forearm and her pulse finally, belatedly, beginning to hammer.

* * *

That night, in her cell — a monk's cell, narrow as a coffin, the temple's idea of lodging for its chattel-scribes — Lena fed the forged writ to the little clay stove piece by piece and watched Tomas Bell's freedom curl into ash. It was the ritual every time. The writ served its purpose at the moment of handoff, passed in a market crowd or slipped into a loaf; Bell already had his, given three days ago wrapped in oilcloth inside a barrel of nails. This copy existed only because she had needed a blank to practice the seal on, and now it needed to die, because ash testified to nothing.

She sat back on her cot afterward and pressed her thumb against the scar on her wrist — the debt-mark, a small brand shaped like a closed ledger, the temple's signature written on her skin. Somewhere below her, in the lowtowns, Tomas Bell was drinking a toast to his new life in some hill-country tavern, and Maren Cotter was weaving cloth she would never own, and the Saltspire glittered over all of it, white and holy and hungry.

Eleven people freed. Eleven, in five years, out of the thousands the ledgers held. A drop against an ocean. Some nights the smallness of it crushed her flat, and she lay staring at the ceiling wondering why she bothered, why she risked her hands — her hands, the only things she had ever owned outright — for arithmetic so small.

But then she thought of Bell's wife, who had wept in the market when Lena passed her the oilcloth bundle, silent, wordless, just pressing it into her hands and walking on. She thought of the others. Freedom wasn't an ocean. It was a drop, and drops were all anyone ever got, and you gave them where you could.

Tomorrow she would copy forty more contracts. Tomorrow the ink would obey whatever hand held it. Tomorrow—

A knock sounded at her door. Three slow strikes, deliberate, in the dead hours past midnight, when no monk had business walking the cells and no scribe had business being awake.

Lena went still, ash cooling in the stove behind her, and listened to the storm rising outside the walls — and to whoever stood on the other side of the thin oak, waiting to be answered.
