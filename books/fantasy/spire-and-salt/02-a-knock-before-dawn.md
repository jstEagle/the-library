## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The storm came off the Thornfells like something with a grudge, and by the third hour of it Lena Vane had given up on sleep entirely.

She sat at her narrow table in the attic room above the chandler's shop, a single candle burning down to its last finger's width, working by habit more than light. The forged travel writs had to be finished tonight — three of them, for the Ferrow family, who would slip out of the Saltspire district before the collectors made their morning rounds. She had copied the temple's seal so many times that her hand no longer needed her eyes. The wax, the pressure, the slight clockwise drag at the end of the spire-mark that the real clerks never noticed in themselves — all of it lived in her fingers now, the way prayers lived in other people's.

Outside, rain hammered the slate roof like a fist demanding entry. The gutters roared. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog had wedged itself under the stairs and was whining at the thunder, a thin continuous sound like a kettle about to sing.

Lena blew on the ink of the last writ and held it up. Good. The Provost's own clerks couldn't have done better, which was, she thought with a small cold satisfaction, because she *was* one of them — six years copying debt contracts until the letters had worn grooves into her soul, and every groove a road someone else could escape along.

She was sanding the page when she heard the knock.

Not on her door. On the street door below, three stories down, and so faint that she might have missed it under the storm if the storm hadn't chosen that exact moment to hold its breath. Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when they've been taught that loud knocking is rude even at the end of the world.

Lena went very still.

No one knocked on the chandler's door after midnight. Debtors didn't knock; they ran. Collectors didn't knock; they arrived with lanterns and witnesses and paper. And the smugglers' folk she served knew better than to come to her window twice in one season.

She took the candle and went down the stairs as quietly as she could, past the sleeping chandler's door, past the racks of tallow and the smell of old smoke, to the bolted door at the bottom. Lightning lit the frosted pane. For half a heartbeat the world outside went white, and in that white she saw nothing but rain — and then, as the dark rushed back, a shape resolved against the step like something the water had left behind.

A child. Small, maybe seven years, maybe younger — it was hard to tell, because the child was caked head to foot in grey mountain mud, hair plastered flat, clothes that might once have been fine reduced to rags weighted with silt. Bare feet. Bare feet, in this cold, on these stones, coming down out of the Thornfells where even the goats had sense enough to shelter.

Lena stood with her hand on the bolt and did not open the door.

Every instinct she had honed over six years of double living spoke clearly and reasonably: *You do not know this child. You do not know who sent it or who follows it. A child on your step is a hook, and hooks are baited.* Inquisitor Hale's men had been asking questions in the lower districts for a month now — courteous men, patient men, the kind who smiled while they wrote your name down. If the crown wanted leverage over the woman who forged writs, a foundling left weeping on her doorstep would be almost too elegant.

*Open the door and you may be opening it for the rest of your life,* said the voice of self-preservation, which sounded, Lena realized with distant disgust, exactly like the Provost.

The child raised one muddy hand and knocked again. Then she looked up — directly at the frosted pane, directly at Lena, though there was no way she could have seen her through it — and said something through the wood.

Lena heard it as sound first, as syllables shaped around breath, and only afterward understood that she did not know a single word of what had been said. It wasn't the trade-tongue. It wasn't high speech, the way priests mangled it at festival. It wasn't the border dialects she'd picked up from years of ferrying runaways, or the clipped cant of the smugglers, or anything else in the wide inventory of languages a scribe accumulates without meaning to.

It was older than any of those. That was the only way she could describe it, and she hated herself for the thought even as she had it. The words had sounded *old* — not archaic, not formal, but old the way stone is old, the way salt is old. As if the language had been here before the kingdom laid its first foundation stone, and would be here long after the last king was forgotten.

And the candle flame bent.

Lena looked down at the candle in her hand. The flame was leaning toward the door — not flickering, not guttering in the draft, but leaning, steadily, deliberately, the way a plant leans toward light. There was no draft. The door was oak and iron-banded and sealed against exactly this kind of weather. But the flame strained toward the child on the other side of it like a dog toward its master, and when the child spoke again, the flame bent further, and somewhere in the walls of the house another candle — the one she'd left burning upstairs — answered with a soft, wrong little sigh.

Lena's skin crawled from wrist to shoulder.

*Turn away,* said the reasonable voice. *Go back upstairs. Finish your writs. In the morning it will be someone else's problem, some parish wardens', some orphan-house's. You owe debts. You cannot afford this.*

The child knocked a third time, and this time the knocks were slower. Weaker. And then Lena heard the thing that undid her: the small, wet sound of the child sliding down against the door, settling onto the step, and — still speaking, still murmuring those impossible syllables, softer now, the way you'd speak to yourself to stay awake, to stay alive — the way you'd recite something you'd been told never to stop reciting.

Because that was what it was. Lena understood it suddenly, standing barefoot on her own cold floor with a bending candle in her hand. The child wasn't talking *to* her. The child was talking to keep something shut. You could hear it in the rhythm — the same phrases, over and over, circling like a rope around a wound. A prayer, or a lock, or both.

Nobody taught a seven-year-old to do that. Nobody taught a seven-year-old to keep saying words that made fire lean unless the alternative was too terrible to say aloud.

"Salt and ash," Lena whispered — the old oath, the one her grandmother had used, the one that meant *may I be ruined before I abandon what's mine to keep*. She hadn't spoken it since she was a girl. It came out of her now without permission.

She threw back the bolt.

The night fell into the hallway — wind and rain and the smell of wet stone and pine and, underneath it, something else, something mineral and strange, like the air inside a cave that has never seen a torch. The child looked up at her from the step, mud-streaked face tilted back, eyes very dark and very calm in a way that no freezing, starving child's eyes should be calm.

Up close, Lena could see what the child clutched against her chest with both hands, the way other children clutch dolls or blankets: a scrap of cloth, sodden and filthy, but even in the bad light Lena knew it instantly. Deep blue. Woven with gold thread. A fragment of royal seal cloth — the stuff they stitched onto banners and proclamations and the shoulders of the king's own guard. Not stolen, she thought distantly. *Torn.* The edges were ripped, violently, by someone in a hurry or someone fighting.

A child comes down out of the Thornfells in a killing storm, barefoot, carrying a piece of the king's own colors torn as if from a struggle, speaking a language older than the kingdom, and the candles bend toward her like flowers toward the sun.

*This is how it ends for people like me,* Lena thought. *Not with chains. With a knock at the door.*

But the child was shivering now — finally, blessedly shivering, the violent helpless shivering of an ordinary frozen body, and whatever ancient thing rode in her voice seemed to have gone quiet with exhaustion. Her lips were going blue. When Lena knelt and held out her hands, the child looked at them for a long moment, weighing something, deciding something — and then pitched forward into her arms, mud and all, and the scrap of seal cloth pressed between them like a secret neither of them had agreed to share.

"It's all right," Lena said, in the trade-tongue, uselessly, and then, because it felt important, in the border dialect, and then, feeling foolish, in the smugglers' cant. The child didn't answer in any of them. But at the sound of the cant her head lifted slightly, just slightly, the way a bird cocks toward a familiar song — and Lena filed that away, the way she filed everything, in the ledger-room of her mind where nothing was ever thrown out.

She got the door barred behind them. She got the child up the stairs, leaving grey tracks on the boards that she'd have to scrub before dawn or answer for. She heated water over the chandler's banked fire, heart hammering at every creak of the old man's floor above hers, and washed the mud from the child's hair in a basin while the child sat wrapped in Lena's own spare blanket, silent, watchful, drinking broth in small careful sips like someone who had learned that food could be taken away.

Under the filth, the child was a girl. Under the rags, there had been finer clothes once — the stitching was court-work, or near enough. And on the inside of her left wrist, when Lena peeled back the sleeve to wash it, there was a mark: a thin scar, deliberate, precise, in the shape of a circle broken by nine short lines radiating inward. Like a crown drawn by someone who had never seen one but been told about it. Like a brand. Like a signature.

Lena stared at it until the girl pulled her arm gently free and tucked it away, and then Lena said nothing, because there was nothing in six years of ledgers and contracts and forbidden archive-gossip that told her what to say.

She put the girl in her own bed and sat beside her with the candle, watching her drift toward sleep. And as the girl sank under, the murmuring started again — those old, old words, barely voiced now, a thread of sound holding something closed. The candle flame leaned toward the bed. Outside, the storm raged on, but the rain against the roof had begun to sound, to Lena's exhausted ear, like scratching. Like many small hands, patiently, patiently trying the latch.

*In the morning,* she told herself. *In the morning I'll think. I'll go to the archive, I'll find the fragments, I'll work out what she's saying. In the morning I'll decide whether I'm saving her or burying myself.*

The girl's eyes opened one last time, dark and calm, and fixed on Lena with an attention that felt less like a child looking at a stranger and more like a judge reading a ledger. Then she slept.

Lena sat up the rest of the night with the scrap of royal seal cloth drying on the table before her, gold thread glinting each time the bent candle flame swayed toward the bed and back, toward the bed and back, like something keeping count.

By the time the sky went grey over the Saltspire, she had already decided what she was going to tell the Provost if anyone asked: nothing. By the time the bells rang prime, she had hidden the scrap of cloth inside the false binding of a debt contract, sewn the girl's rags into the kindling basket, and memorized the mark on the wrist — nine lines and a broken circle — well enough to draw it blind.

And by the time the temple bell rang the hour after prime, and a runner in temple grey hammered on the chandler's door below with a summons in the Provost's own hand — *the Lady Scribe Vane will attend the archive at her earliest convenience; certain irregularities require her eye* — Lena was already awake, already dressed, already standing between the sleeping child and the stairs.

She looked down at the summons, and then at the girl, and understood that the morning she had promised herself had just been cancelled.

Whatever the archive held, she would need it sooner than that.
