## 4. What Wears the Crown

The summons came on wax the color of dried blood.

*By order of His Majesty King Aldric the Second of That Name, all guilds, temples, and chartered houses of the Saltspire district shall send two witnesses to the Autumn Audience, there to behold the justice and mercy of the crown.*

Lena read it twice in the scriptorium's gray morning light, her stylus hovering over a half-finished contract for a fishmonger who owed eleven marks and his daughter's labor besides. The temple was sending Provost Callen and Brother Ossian, which meant the third seat — the scribe's seat, the one that recorded what was said so the record could be corrected later — would fall to whoever's hand was fastest and least likely to be missed.

Her hand was fastest. She was never missed. That was rather the point of her.

"You'll attend," Callen said, not looking up from his correspondence. He had a way of issuing orders as though reading them off an inventory. "Wear the gray. Sit where you're told. Write what is said, not what you think."

"And if I think nothing?"

"Then you'll finally be suited to this work." He sealed a letter with a press of his thumb, unhurried, a man who had never once needed to hurry because the world arranged itself around men like him. "His Majesty has taken an interest in Saltspire accounts of late. It would go poorly for us if our records were found wanting."

*Our* records. *Their* debts. Lena bent back over her contract and said nothing, which was its own kind of fluency after nine years in the temple's service. Nine years since her father's shipping venture went down in the Strait of Gulls with someone else's cargo aboard, and the debt came ashore without him, patient as tide, and settled itself on her like a second skin.

She thought about the child then — she thought about the child constantly now, the way one worries a loose tooth — sleeping upstairs in the narrow room above her cell, fed on bread stolen from the refectory and honeyed water, silent except when she spoke that other language. The candle-bending language. The seal-language, if Lena's translation held, and every night it held better, every fragment clicking into place like bones knitting.

*It holds. It holds. It holds until the bearer forgets.*

Three days ago those words had been an academic curiosity. This morning they were a countdown.

* * *

The Hall of Audiences had been built to make people feel small, and it succeeded with the efficiency of all good architecture. The ceiling rose into vaults painted with the nine founders of the Aldric line, their faces gilded, their hands extended in eternal benediction or eternal demand — at this distance, and with this light, impossible to say which. Banners hung from iron rings, deep blue shot through with silver thread, the crowned mountain of the royal sigil repeated down the hall like a refrain.

Lena sat at the scribes' bench below the dais steps, between Brother Ossian's lavender-and-parchment smell and a crown clerk whose fingers were stained so deeply with ink they looked dipped to the knuckle. The hall filled with petitioners: farmers come about grain tithes, a merchant guild delegation in matching green, a woman holding a folded petition against her chest the way one holds a child. Guards lined the walls in polished scale. Somewhere above, in the clerestory, pigeons muttered.

"His Majesty the King."

The herald's voice struck the vaults and came down again doubled. Everyone stood. Lena stood too, stylus ready, eyes lowered as protocol demanded — and it was only because her eyes were lowered, tracking the king's approach across the pale marble floor, that she saw it.

The torches along the walls burned steady. No drafts; the hall was sealed and warm. And yet the king's shadow — thrown long and sharp by the great candelabrum behind the throne — arrived a half-beat after he did.

She told herself it was the flicker of flame. Light stutters; shadows breathe; the eye plays tricks in rooms designed to overwhelm it. She wrote the date, the occasion, the list of attending officials, her hand moving in its neat temple script while her mind ran backward through everything she'd seen, frame by frame, the way she proofed a difficult page.

King Aldric crossed to the throne and turned and sat. He was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulder, with the ruddy health of a man who hunted and feasted and slept soundly. His beard was oiled and curled. His robes were winter-blue velvet heavy with ermine. He looked exactly like the coins in Lena's purse, exactly like the portrait in the temple's gallery of benefactors, exactly like a king.

He looked *finished*, was the word that came to her, unbidden. Like a thing made rather than born. Like a mask carved by someone who had studied kingship very carefully from the outside.

The audience began. A farmer presented his grievance about the grain tithe; the king heard it with a slight, gracious tilt of the head and granted relief, and the hall murmured approval. A guild dispute over river tolls; resolved with a few measured sentences that pleased both parties and cost the crown nothing. The king's voice was beautiful — low, resonant, trained in the way voices are trained when they are instruments rather than expressions. Every pause landed where a pause should land. Every mercy sounded merciful.

And through all of it, Lena watched his shadow.

It lagged. Not always — when he was still, stillness hid the flaw — but each time he moved, lifted a hand, turned his head toward a petitioner, the dark shape on the marble behind him followed a fraction late, like a servant who had not quite mastered the choreography. Once, when the king raised his hand to still a murmuring section of the crowd, the shadow's fingers were still rising after his had settled.

Beside her, Brother Ossian yawned into his sleeve. Down the bench, the crown clerk transcribed without once looking up. Two hundred people in the hall, and none of them watching the floor.

Because no one watches the floor. People watch faces. Faces are where we keep the truth we've agreed to look for, and the face above the throne gave them everything they wanted: authority, warmth, the faint weary patience of a good ruler burdened by good rule.

Lena made herself look at the eyes.

From the scribes' bench she was nearly beneath the dais, close enough to see the king clearly when he leaned forward to receive a petition. His eyes were brown. Ordinary. Kindly creased at the corners, even.

They did not move the way eyes move.

When the farmer spoke, the king's gaze rested on him properly enough. But between petitions — in the small dead intervals when there was nothing to look at — his eyes did not drift or rest or blink in the idle way of a waiting man. They *fixed*. Each time, they fixed on something in the middle distance with an intensity that had nothing to do with the hall, nothing to do with the moment, an intensity like a starving man watching a table being set for someone else. And then, when the next petitioner stepped forward, the hunger would fold itself away, smooth as a ledger closing, and the kindly creases would return.

Patient, something in Lena whispered, in the voice she used for translating dead languages. *That is what it looks like. Not rage. Rage is quick. That is appetite that has learned to wait.*

She thought of the child's recitation, the fragments she'd pinned to the page of her hidden notebook three nights running:

*It wears what it takes. It wears it well. The bearer forgets first his griefs, then his joys, then his name.*

And beneath that, in the archive's oldest fragmentary hand, a line she had copied out and stared at until the letters swam:

*Seal it in flesh that cannot speak, for speech is the hinge, and a hinge may open from either side.*

A child who could not speak their tongue. A child speaking instead a language older than kingdoms, older than the crown, repeating a seal the way a prayer repeats — and the seal was failing, because the seal had been sent away, or had escaped, or had been *found*, and the thing under the crown had begun, at long last, to reach for the door.

The woman with the folded petition was called forward. She knelt on the third step and unrolled her paper with shaking hands — a widow's petition, Lena gathered, concerning a debt owed to a crown collector, concerning a house, concerning a son taken for labor in settlement. Her voice cracked twice. The hall was quiet. Even the pigeons stopped muttering.

And King Aldric listened, head tilted, eyes soft — and Lena watched his shadow on the marble lean toward the kneeling woman before he did. Lean, and hold, the way a dog leans at the end of a rope.

"I hear your grief," the king said, and his voice wrapped the words in velvet. "And the crown will examine it." He gestured, gracious, merciful. "Rise, goodmother. You shall have justice."

The woman wept thanks. The hall murmured. The clerk beside Lena wrote *justice granted, referred to assize,* and Lena wrote it too, because that was her work, writing what was said —

— and then, beneath it, small, in the personal shorthand she used for things that must never appear in any official record, she wrote: *the shadow moved first.*

The audience continued for another hour. Lena transcribed every word of it with perfect accuracy and remembered almost none of it. Her whole self had narrowed to a single point of attention: the figure on the throne, and the thing wearing it, and the terrible arithmetic assembling itself in her chest.

Because here was the arithmetic. If the seal was failing — if the Vessel, the name surfaced from the cant's deepest stratum, the *vessel that drinks* — if it was already wearing a king so well that two hundred subjects knelt contentedly beneath its gaze, then the child was not merely a refugee. The child was the lock walking free of the door. And things that wanted doors shut sent hunters after locks. Inquisitors. Hounds. Whatever the crown called them.

And here was the rest of the arithmetic, the part that made her stylus tremble against the page: the temple owned her debt. The temple had received a crown inquiry about Saltspire accounts. The temple would trade her in a heartbeat — traded everyone, eventually; that was what ledgers were *for*. If the crown learned a strange mute child had appeared in the Saltspire district, and learned that a temple scribe with debts and smugglers' roads memorized in her head had taken that child in—

She was already guilty. She had been guilty since the night she opened her door to the storm.

* * *

Afterward, in the antechamber, the officials milled and drank spiced wine while the king withdrew. Lena stood near the wall with her transcription board, invisible in her gray, doing what she did best: being furniture with excellent penmanship.

"An excellent audience," Provost Callen said to a crown chamberlain, close enough that Lena caught every word, because Callen never troubled to exclude her; excluding her would have acknowledged she was worth excluding. "His Majesty's interest in the provincial ledgers does the temples honor. We stand ready to render full account."

"Full account," the chamberlain agreed, "and full cooperation. There is a matter of some delicacy." He lowered his voice, and Lena, staring at the middle distance with the blank courtesy of the truly insignificant, did not lower hers. "An inquiry. A child, possibly traveling alone or with accomplices, last seen in the river districts. Unusual appearance. Unusual — condition. His Majesty's inquisitor will arrive within the fortnight to make enquiries. Hale is his name. The temples are expected to assist without reservation."

"Of course."

"The inquisitor asks specifically after anyone who keeps unusual records. Strange scripts. Old languages." The chamberlain smiled the smile of a man delivering a courtesy. "You understand. Nothing is suspected. Everything is verified."

Callen laughed the right amount. Lena counted the flagstones between her feet and the door and found there were thirty-one, and that she knew a route from the antechamber to the scriptorium that passed no one's office, and that the child had eaten that morning, and that the storm-swollen river road east of the city was closed to patrols after dark but not to people who had spent nine years copying the patrol schedules for forgery purposes.

"Inquisitor Hale," Callen repeated warmly. "We shall prepare quarters."

Lena looked up, once, toward the doors through which the king had withdrawn. A guard closed them. For half a heartbeat before they shut, she glimpsed the throne room beyond — empty now, banners still, candles guttering in their hundreds — and on the pale floor, thrown long across the marble where no body stood to cast it, a shadow that had not yet finished leaving.

She walked back to the temple through streets loud with ordinary life — fishwives crying the morning's catch, children racing hoops, a bell ringing the hour — and every ordinary sound felt like the surface of deep water, and she knew what swam beneath, and she knew, with the cold clarity that had carried her through every collector's inspection and every forged writ, that the water was rising.

Upstairs, the child was awake. She stood at the window with her palms flat against the glass, watching the street below, and when Lena came in she turned, and her eyes — dark, ancient, impossibly young — asked the question she had no common words to ask.

"They're coming," Lena said quietly. She set down her board. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady, the way they always were right before everything changed. "So we're going. Tonight."

The child looked at her for a long moment. Then she spoke, one soft syllable of the old cant, and every candle in the room bowed toward her like grass in wind — and Lena understood it this time without needing the notebook at all.

*Too late. It knows I'm awake.*
