## 6. The Price of Paper

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Lena would remember afterward as the only mercy in it. Tuesdays were tithe-days at the Saltspire; the Provost's attention would be divided between the grain-counts and the candle-inventory, and a man dividing his attention was a man who could be managed.

The note itself was a single line in the Provost's own hand — not his secretary's, not a clerk's: *Sister Vane. My study. After compline.* No reason given. No reason was ever given. In eleven years of service to the temple of Salt and Ledger, Lena had learned that the absence of a stated offense was itself information. It meant the offense was already known.

She read the note twice by the light of her cell's one permitted candle, then folded it into eighths and held the corner to the flame until it caught. Ash was safer than paper. Paper could be found, matched, read. She watched the last curl of it die against the stone floor and thought, with a strange detachment, *So this is how it ends — or this is how it begins, and I won't know which until I'm standing in that room.*

Behind the wall, muffled by plaster and old timber, the child slept. Lena had moved her from the undercroft three nights ago, after the second search of the temple grounds — inquisitors' men this time, gray-cloaked and quiet, moving through the cloisters like water finding cracks. The child had come without protest, had curled into the narrow space behind Lena's bed-frame among the bundles of archived contracts, and had slept the way soldiers sleep: lightly, completely, one hand always near something sharp. Where she'd gotten the shard of broken quill-knife, Lena never learned. She'd stopped asking questions the child couldn't answer and started answering the ones the child asked with her eyes.

*After compline.* That gave her six hours.

She spent them the way she spent every hour now: working. Not the copying — she'd finished her assigned contracts two days early, a habit she'd cultivated for years precisely because it bought hours like these — but the other work. The ledger beneath the ledger.

It lived inside the binding of a psalter, a genuine psalter, its spine re-sewn with linen thread that held thirty-two thin leaves of the temple's own contract-vellum. On those leaves, in an imitation of the Provost's secretary's hand so exact that Lena sometimes frightened herself, were travel writs. Pardon-papers. Debt-discharges stamped with the Saltspire seal, pressed from a mold she'd carved herself out of candle-wax and patience over four months of nights. Thirty-two writs. Thirty-two people walking free through gates that should have held them — the cooper's widow with her three sons, the lame ferryman, the girl who'd stolen bread for a dying mother and been bound for seven years' service for it. Thirty-two small treasons, each one worth, under crown law, the loss of her writing hand.

She checked the psalter's hiding place — loose brick behind the washstand, dry, undisturbed — and then did the thing she'd promised herself she wouldn't do: she took out the escape writ she'd made for herself. It was the only one written in her own hand, because if she ran, she wanted one honest thing in her pocket. Her name. Her freedom, forged but real, waiting.

*Vane, Lena. Scribe, third order. All debts discharged.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she put it back behind the brick, because carrying it tonight would be a confession, and went down to the Provost's study with her hands empty and her face arranged into the mild, tired obedience of a woman who has nothing to hide.

* * *

Provost Callowin received her in the room where all the temple's power lived, though you wouldn't have known it to look. His study was deliberately modest — a desk, two chairs, a window overlooking the tithe-yard where debtors stacked salt-blocks in the rain. He liked debtors to be visible from his window. He called it *keeping the accounts honest*. Lena had long understood that what he meant was *keeping them reminded*.

He was a small man, Callowin. Neat. Silver at the temples, ink permanently staining the first knuckle of his right hand, spectacles he polished when he wanted time to think. He had been kind to her once, in the early years — had praised her hand, had recommended her for the archive, had told her that a mind like hers deserved better than the salt-yards. She had believed, for almost two years, that he meant it. Then she'd seen him sell a deaf stable-boy's remaining term of service to a quarry-master to cover a shortfall in the candle-tithe, and she had understood that Callowin's kindness was simply another form of accounting. Everything was entered somewhere. Everything was owed.

"Sister Vane." He didn't rise. "Sit."

She sat. Folded her hands. Waited.

For nearly a minute he said nothing at all, which was its own technique, and she let him have it, watching the rain silver the window while her heart kept its own careful count. Then he opened the drawer at his right knee and set something on the desk between them.

A writ. One of hers.

Not one of the thirty-two. This one was blank — no, not blank. Half-finished. A travel pass begun in the secretary's hand and abandoned mid-line, the seal-mold impression half-pressed, the wax still soft enough that the flaw showed: a hairline crack through the spire-mark where her carved mold had chipped.

She knew it instantly. It was from the batch she'd burned. Three weeks ago, alarmed by the searches, she'd destroyed eleven unfinished writs in the scriptorium stove — and she had *watched* them burn. Had stirred the ash herself.

"You're wondering," Callowin said mildly, "how I came by it."

"I couldn't say, Provost."

"No," he agreed. "You couldn't." He turned the writ over with one finger, delicately, the way a man turns a dead bird to examine the wound. "Brother Emeric found it in the stove-ash. Most people, Sister Vane, would assume a half-burned forgery in a stove was simply carelessness. A novice practicing seals, perhaps. But I am not most people, and neither are you. So I asked myself: who in this house can write Brother Tam's hand well enough to fool the gate-wardens? Who has archive access? Who has, in the past eleven years, requested — and received — vellum allocations consistently eight percent above her documented need?"

The rain filled the silence. Lena kept her breathing even, kept her eyes on the middle distance just past his shoulder, and let the arithmetic hang there, because denying it was already useless and they both knew it. He hadn't brought her here to catch her. He'd brought her here because she was caught.

"Eight percent," she said at last. "You counted."

"I count everything." He removed his spectacles and polished them, and when he put them back on, something in his face had shifted — not softer, exactly. More careful. "Eleven years, Vane. You've copied more contracts than any scribe in the Spire's history. You know the debt-law better than the magistrates do. You could have bought your way free a dozen times over with what you know — sold copies of contracts, blackmailed creditors, any of it. Instead you used the finest forgery hand I have ever seen to discharge the debts of people whose combined value to this temple wouldn't cover the roof repairs." He tapped the ruined writ. "That is either sentiment or principle, and I have never known which, and frankly it no longer matters. What matters is this."

He rose, went to the cabinet behind his desk, and returned with a document sealed in gray wax — crown wax, the double-spire of the royal chancery. He did not open it. He laid it beside her forged writ like a physician laying two charts side by side.

"Three days ago, an inquisitor of the crown named Hale came to me privately. Not through channels — privately. He knows, Vane. Not everything, but enough. A child appeared in the Saltspire district during the storm, speaking a language no one knows, carrying a scrap of royal seal-cloth. Within a day, that child vanished. Within two days, a scribe of this house was seen buying lamp-oil in quantities inconsistent with her allotment, and asking after the pilgrim roads through Thornfell." He sat back down. "Hale believes the child is here. He is prepared to believe the temple sheltered her unknowing — a lapse, regrettable, forgivable. What he will *not* forgive, and what the crown will not survive, is complicity. And complicity is what I am holding in my hand, in your handwriting, in your wax."

Lena said nothing. Her mind was running the corridors of the building, counting doors, counting guards, counting the hours until dawn — and underneath all of it, colder and clearer than the rest, the image of the child asleep behind her bed-frame with her hand near the broken quill-knife, trusting the walls to hold.

"There is a bargain," Callowin said.

Of course there was. There was always a bargain. The temple was built on bargains; it was the whole architecture of the place, debt upon debt upon debt, and Callowin was its truest priest.

"Deliver the child to the crown," he said. "Tonight, before Hale's men widen their search. You will be the one who finds her — a loyal sister, vigilant, who discovered an intruder and did her duty. Your debt — all of it, the full eleven years, the principal, the interest, the bond your father signed — discharged. Not forgiven. *Discharged*, paid in full, clean paper, clean name. You walk out of the Saltspire tomorrow owing nothing to anyone." He folded his hands. "You have spent your life studying what freedom costs, Vane. Here is the price, itemized. One child."

The rain wrote its long gray sentences down the window. Somewhere below, in the tithe-yard, a debtor coughed.

"And if I refuse?" Lena asked. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Then Hale takes you both, and the temple disavows you, and you learn what the crown's justice does to forgers." Callowin's tone didn't change at all; that was the horror of him, that it never changed. "I don't want that. I have invested eleven years in your hand. But Vane—" and here he leaned forward, and for one moment the mask of the accountant slipped and she saw the thing beneath it, which was not cruelty but something worse, something like sincerity, "—you must understand what that child *is*. Hale showed me the translations his scholars have begun. Binding-cant. Pre-kingdom. Do you understand? The girl is not a refugee. She is an instrument. Something old and dangerous is wearing the king's crown, and that child is the lock on it, and locks do not get to choose their doors. You are not selling a person. You are returning a key to its custodians. There is a difference, and a woman of your intelligence knows there is a difference."

*A key,* Lena thought. *A child who flinches at raised voices. A child who drew a mountain in the dust and looked at it like a wound. A child who sleeps with a broken knife in her fist because the world taught her that walls open.*

"She has a name?" Lena said quietly. "Do we know it?"

Callowin blinked. "What?"

"The instrument. The key. Does it have a name?"

"That is not relevant—"

"It's relevant to me." She stood. Her legs were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. "I'll need until the third bell. She trusts me. If I go at her suddenly, she'll run, and your inquisitor's men will lose her in the Warrens, and then everyone's paper is worthless. Third bell. I'll bring her to the north postern myself."

Callowin studied her for a long moment, and Lena gave him what he needed: the slump of a woman beaten, the lowered eyes, the small mechanical nod of the owned accepting terms. Eleven years of practice. She could perform obedience the way she performed Brother Tam's hand — perfectly, and meaning none of it.

"Third bell," he said. "And Vane — I will know if you run. The gates, the roads, the ferries. The crown has eyes everywhere, and after tonight, so do I." He slid her forged writ across the desk toward her. "Burn this yourself. Consider it the last dishonest act of your old life."

She took it. Their fingers did not touch. She bowed — precisely, correctly, the bow of a third-order scribe to her provost — and walked out of the study with her heart hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears, and her face still smooth as fresh vellum.

* * *

The psalter came out from behind the brick. The thirty-two writs stayed where they were — those were done, those were spent, those belonged to the people they freed. But her own writ, *Vane, Lena, all debts discharged*, she held over the candle and did not burn.

Instead she turned it over, and on the back, in her own quick cipher — the one she'd invented at fourteen, bored and brilliant and already learning that some things must be hidden even from the pages that hid them — she wrote the route. Smuggler's roads, the ones the collectors never mapped because the collectors never paid the people who knew them. The shepherd's stair above Kettle Gap. The drowned ford at Nine Stones, passable only in the dark of the moon. Brannoc's barn, if Brannoc's price hadn't grown too high. And at the end of it, the only destination that mattered, the one the child's drawings pointed to like a finger of bone: *the forge. The mountain forge. Where it was made.*

Because Callowin had made one mistake, and it was the mistake of a man who had never been owned: he thought she would weigh a child against her freedom and find the arithmetic clear. He thought freedom was the thing she wanted most, because it was the thing he dangled. But Lena had spent eleven years learning what debt actually was — not the numbers, but the weight, the way it bent people, the way it made children of the strong and strangers of families — and she knew something Callowin's ledgers had no column for:

Some debts you chose were worth more than any freedom you were given.

The child was awake when she came back up. Of course she was. The child heard everything, felt everything, the way animals feel weather. She was sitting up in the dark behind the bed-frame, the quill-knife in her lap, her eyes catching the candlelight like a cat's.

"We're leaving," Lena said softly, in common tongue, and then — haltingly, badly, the accent surely atrocious — in the old cant, the words she'd pried out of forbidden fragments over three sleepless nights: *Road. Night. Together.*

The child stared at her. And then she did something she had never done: she reached out and took Lena's hand, and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, and held it there.

*Yes,* said the gesture, older than any kingdom. *Yes. Yes.*

They went out through the laundry court, where the rain covered sound, and past the tithe-yard wall, where a debtor stacking night-salt looked up — a hollow-faced woman Lena had once freed with writ number nine — and saw, and understood, and turned her back to stand guard over the gate-latch without being asked.

At the postern, Lena paused once, looking back at the Saltspire rising black against the storm-light, its windows like closed eyes, its ledgers full of sleeping names. Eleven years of her life were written in that building. Her father's signature was in that building. Her whole self, entered and filed and owed.

She pulled her hood up, tightened her grip on the small warm hand in hers, and stepped off temple ground into the rain — owing everything, owning nothing, and freer than she had ever been in her life.

Behind them, faint through the storm, the third bell began to ring.
