## 7. Smuggler's Roads

They left Saltspire before the fourth bell, when the fog off the harbor was thick enough to drink and the temple watchmen were still yawning over their cold tea. Lena had dressed the child in a novice's spare habit, cut down and pinned with ruthless efficiency, and stuffed their few belongings into a satchel that weighed less than a loaf of bread: a skin of water, hard cheese, dried plums, a flint, her mother's bone hairpin, and — wrapped in oilcloth against her heart — the forged writ that named them both pilgrims bound for the shrine at Vell's Rest.

The child had not spoken since they left the room behind the dye-works. She walked when Lena walked, stopped when Lena stopped, and watched everything with those old, old eyes that made Lena's skin prickle even now, even in the dark, even with the storm-wash still dripping from the eaves.

"Rules," Lena whispered as they slipped through the gap in the tannery wall where the mortar had crumbled years ago and no one had bothered to mend it, because mending it would mean admitting the gap existed. "First rule: we don't use the roads. Not once. Not for anything. Roads are where people get caught."

The child looked up at her. Rain-dark curls plastered to her forehead. She nodded once, slow and grave, like a magistrate accepting testimony.

"Second rule." Lena crouched so they were eye to eye, and the smell of wet wool and tannery brine rose around them. "If I say *run*, you run. You don't ask where. You don't wait for me. You run until you can't hear my voice anymore, and then you keep running. Do you understand?"

Something flickered across the child's face — not fear, Lena thought. Something older than fear. A kind of sorrowful recognition, as if she'd heard these words before, from someone else, in some other century, and knew how they ended.

She nodded again.

*Third rule*, Lena didn't say, because there wasn't one yet. Third rule was forming itself somewhere in her chest, taking shape out of the last three days of hiding a mute child beneath her floorboards while inquisitors knocked on doors down the street, and it felt less like a rule and more like a vow: *I will not be the reason this child is taken.*

She took the small hand. It was cold. It was always cold.

And they went up into the Thornfells by ways no honest person had ever walked.

* * *

The smugglers' paths were not marked on any map, which was rather the point of them. They existed in the negative space of the kingdom — in the places where surveyors' instruments spun uselessly, where tax collectors' horses refused to go, where the crown's authority thinned out like broth stretched too far. Goat tracks that became ledge-paths that became staircases cut into living rock by hands that predated the kingdom, predated the temple, maybe predated everything except the mountains themselves.

Lena knew them the way she knew her own handwriting. She'd memorized every route years ago, back when she was still telling herself she only studied them out of scholarly interest, before she admitted what she was really doing: tracing escape routes for strangers whose names she'd never know, whose debts she erased one forged line at a time.

Now she walked them for herself, and found the knowledge sat differently in her body. Every path was a sentence she'd copied a hundred times; now she was living inside the sentence.

By midmorning they were above the tree line, following a shelf of granite no wider than a coffin, with the world falling away to their left in gray-green miles. The child walked close behind her, one hand fisted in the back of Lena's cloak, and did not complain, did not stumble, did not make a sound. It was the silence that unnerved her most. Children cried. Children whined about their feet, about hunger, about the cold. This child moved through the mountains like something returning to them.

At noon they sheltered in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Lena broke out the cheese and counted out portions with a scribe's precision. The child ate slowly, watching the valley below them, where the road to Vell's Rest wound along the river like a pale scar.

"That's where our writ says we're going," Lena told her, pointing. "We're not going there. But if anyone asks, that's our answer. Pilgrims. Bound for the shrine. We got separated from our group in the storm." She tapped the child's chest. "You don't speak. You're simple — forgive me, but it's the truth that protects us. Simple pilgrim children don't answer questions."

The child turned and looked at her, and for a moment Lena could have sworn there was dry amusement in that ancient face. As if to say: *you would make me a fool to save my life. I have been made worse things.*

Then the child reached out, took a piece of cheese from Lena's own portion, and held it up to her mouth.

Lena blinked. "That's yours."

The child waited, arm extended, patient as stone.

It was such a small thing. Such an absurdly small thing. But Lena found, to her considerable embarrassment, that her eyes stung. Three days of terror and flight and lying awake listening for boots on stairs, and it was a piece of cheese offered by a small dirty hand that nearly undid her.

She ate it. The child nodded, satisfied, as though a bargain had been struck and sealed, and went back to studying the valley.

*We look out for each other now,* the gesture said. *I remember how this works.*

* * *

They made the high pass by late afternoon, and that was where the day went wrong.

Lena heard them first — the particular rhythm of hooves that meant soldiers moving at patrol pace, four abreast, coming up the switchback road below the pass. She pressed herself and the child flat into a fold of rock and watched through a seam of stone as six riders in crown blue crested the road and halted at the checkpoint she'd been hoping to bypass entirely.

The Thorn Gate. Of course. Of course they'd manned it today, of all days. A sergeant with a wax-sealed ledger stood checking papers, and behind him two soldiers had a man spread-eagled against the rocks, going through his pack with methodical cruelty. A peddler, by the look of his goods scattered on the stones — tinware, ribbons, cheap devotional prints of Saint Vell holding his lantern.

There was no way around. The pass narrowed here to the gate itself; the alternative was three days' detour through the Sere Valley, and Hale's hounds would run them down long before that. Lena ran the arithmetic of it with cold clarity and hated every number.

The writ would hold. Her forgery was good — it was very good, it had passed inspection twice already — but the child was the problem. A silent child with eyes like that, at a checkpoint, on the same week rumors of a strange child had come crawling up every road from Saltspire. One curious soldier asking one question the wrong way, and—

"There's another way," said a voice behind her, low and rough as scree.

Lena spun, heart slamming, one hand closing on the flint in her pocket. A man stood ten paces up the shelf-path where no man had been ten seconds ago: broad, gray-bearded, dressed in the patched leathers of a mountain guide, with a crossbow slung across his back and a face weathered into permanent skepticism. He had a coil of rope over one shoulder and the unbothered stance of someone who owned every rock in sight.

Brannoc. Of all the rotten luck in all the rotten mountains.

"Brannoc," she said carefully.

"Lena Vane." He said her name the way he'd say *avalanche*. "Scribe of the Saltspire. Copyist of contracts. Keeper of other people's sins." His eyes went to the child, then back to her, and narrowed. "Up past the treeline with no guide, no gear worth the name, and a bairn in a novice's habit. And below us, the crown's men pulling apart everyone who comes through." He shifted the rope on his shoulder. "Funny thing. I heard tell there's a woman wanted out of Saltspire. Scribe. Forger. Traveling with a child the inquisitors are calling—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. She softened it. "Don't finish that sentence. Not out loud. Not here."

Brannoc studied her for a long moment. Below them, the peddler shouted something angry; a soldier laughed. The wind carried the sound up the rocks like smoke.

"I knew your mother," Brannoc said at last, quieter. "Before the temple bought her debt and hers with it. She ran this path twice a year, carrying what needed carrying. She'd have cut my throat for charging you anything." He held out a broad palm. "So I won't charge you coin. I'll charge you answers. Three of them. True ones. That's my price, Lena Vane, and it's higher than gold, so mind how you pay."

Every instinct she owned screamed at her. Answers were debts; debts were chains; chains were the whole rotten architecture of her life. But the light was failing, the soldiers were forty feet below, and the child's hand had found hers again, cold fingers lacing tight.

"One," Lena said. "Why do you want to know?"

"Because I've been running cargo through these hills for thirty years," Brannoc said, "and in the last month, three of my best clients have vanished. Taken. Not arrested — *taken*, no trial, no paper. And the men who took them wear crown blue and carry warrants signed by a name that isn't a judge's name. So. Question one is mine to ask, and here it is: is the child the reason?"

Lena looked at him. At the weathered face and the steady eyes and the thirty years of mountain silence behind them. She thought about lies, and how many she had left, and how each one spent made the next one cheaper.

"Yes," she said.

Brannoc exhaled through his nose. "Question two. Are you running *from* the crown, or running *for* something?"

"Both."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have. Ask your third."

He chewed on it. Below, the soldiers let the peddler go with a shove and a scattering of his own tinware, and began waving forward the next travelers in line. Somewhere a hawk rode the wind off the peak, screaming its thin note.

"Last one," Brannoc said. "When this ends — however it ends — will knowing you have been a danger to me? Straight answer. My neck hangs on it."

Lena held his gaze. "Yes," she said. "Almost certainly."

To his credit, Brannoc laughed — a short, harsh bark with no humor in it, aimed at the sky. "Honest to the last. Your mother's blood, sure enough." He uncoiled the rope from his shoulder and jerked his chin toward a crack in the cliff face she'd have sworn was solid stone. "There's a chimney behind that lip. Comes up inside the old watchtower ruin above the gate. The crown's men don't watch the ruin because the ruin watches them — has done since before there was a crown. You go up quiet, you come down behind them on the far side, and you never saw old Brannoc at all."

"And your price?"

"Paid." He was already turning away, coiling his rope with quick, practiced motions. Then he paused, half-turned, and his voice dropped low. "One thing free, girl, since you paid honest. Those men down there — they're not hunting a fugitive. I watched them work. They're hunting like men under orders they don't understand, from a paper that scares *them*. Whatever you're carrying out of Saltspire—" his eyes flicked to the child, and something in the old smuggler's face went careful and grim, "—carry it fast."

Then he was gone, folded into the rocks like water into sand, and Lena was left with a cold hand in hers and a chimney of stone climbing into the dark.

* * *

The chimney was brutal. Narrow, black, slick with seep-water, the kind of climb that punished hesitation. Lena went first, testing each hold with a scribe's patience, talking the whole time in a low stream of words she wasn't sure the child understood — *left foot there, yes, good, now reach, I've got you, I've got you* — and the child climbed like the mountain had taught her personally, flowing up the stone without a wasted motion, and Lena tried very hard not to think about what that might mean.

They came out inside the ruined watchtower as the sun bled out behind the western peaks. The tower was older than the kingdom — its stones fitted without mortar, its walls carved in places with spirals and eye-shapes that matched nothing in any temple liturgy Lena had ever copied. The child stopped dead in the middle of the broken floor and stared at those carvings, and her grip on Lena's hand changed — tightened, trembled.

"What is it?" Lena whispered.

The child raised her free hand and touched the spiral, traced it once, twice. Then she looked up at Lena and made a sound — the first sound she'd made since the dye-works — a small, cracked syllable in that old language, soft as ash falling.

Lena didn't need the archive to translate this one. Some words carry their meaning in their shape, the way grief does.

*Mine,* the child had said. Or perhaps: *I was here first.*

Below them, through the tower's empty window-slits, they could see the Thorn Gate checkpoint, the soldiers banking their cookfire, the sergeant's ledger glowing orange in the firelight. In an hour the patrol would bed down for the night and the far side of the pass would open like a door.

Lena sank down against the ancient wall, pulled the child against her side, and shared the last of the water. Far below, the cookfire smoked. Above them, the first stars came out over the Thornfells, indifferent and vast.

"We'll go down at moonset," she murmured. "While they sleep. Then north, up the Pilgrim's Stair, and after that—" she closed her eyes, "—after that we go where the maps stop."

The child said nothing. But she leaned her weight into Lena's side, deliberately, completely, the way a creature lays down trust like a coin on a table, and together they watched the soldiers' fire burn lower.

It was full dark when Lena heard it: hoofbeats on the far road, many horses, moving fast and in formation. She pressed her eye to the window-slit and saw torchlight streaming up the valley from the south — a column of riders, twenty at least, with a banner she couldn't read at this distance but knew anyway, the way you know a scar.

Crown blue. And riding at the head of them, straight-backed beneath the torches, a figure in gray whose stillness marked him out from every soldier around him, the way a blade's stillness marks it out from the scabbard.

Inquisitor Hale had reached the mountains.
