## 8. The Child Who Remembers

The cave Brannoc had marked on the slate was less a cave than a wound in the mountain's flank, a hollow where some ancient flood had gnawed out a chamber and then thought better of it. Lena built the fire small, the way smugglers did — low flames, green wood banked to smoke thin, nothing that would glow through the screen of hawthorn at the mouth. The child sat cross-legged on her bedroll with her hands folded in her lap, watching the fire with an attention that made Lena's neck prickle.

It was not the way a child watched fire. Children wanted to poke it, to feed it twigs, to see what happened when things burned. This child watched the way a priest watches an altar. Or the way a prisoner watches a door.

"Are you hungry?" Lena asked, though she had asked it every night for four nights and received only that same level, dark-eyed stare. She held out a strip of dried mutton anyway. "Here."

The child took it. That was new. For three days she had refused food unless Lena ate first, and even then she had eaten in small, precise bites, like someone performing a rite rather than a meal. Tonight she bit into the meat and chewed, and after a moment her brow furrowed — an expression so ordinary, so *childish*, that Lena nearly laughed with relief.

"Salty," Lena said. "I know. Brannoc's stores are half salt by weight. He says it keeps. He says a lot of things."

The child swallowed. Then, without any warning at all, she spoke.

Not the cant. Not this time. It was one word, or something shaped like one, guttural and round, and she said it while looking directly at the fire, as if introducing them.

Lena went very still. "What was that?"

The child looked at her. Waited. Said it again, slower, and pointed one grimy finger at the flames.

"You're naming it." Lena felt the wonder arrive before the fear did, which was itself strange; for days the fear had come first, always first, a cold hand between her shoulder blades. "You're giving me its name. In the old tongue."

The child nodded once, gravely, and returned to her mutton.

* * *

They had fallen into a rhythm over the four days since the river crossing, since the border stones, since Brannoc had taken Lena's last silver coin and his own questions unanswered into the dark of the pass. By day they walked, and the child walked well — better than well, tireless, sure-footed on scree that would have broken Lena's ankle, though she tired strangely too, in long sudden collapses that came without warning and passed the same way, like tides. By night they hid, and Lena taught her words the way you'd drop crumbs for a wild thing: *fire, water, bread, sleep, no, yes, here.* The child hoarded each one visibly, turning it over, testing its weight. But she never gave up the other language. Every night, murmured into the dark or into her cupped hands, the seal-cant ran on, patient as dripping water, holding shut whatever it held shut.

Tonight, though, after the mutton, the child did something else.

She crawled to the flat stretch of stone near the fire where the flood-water had left a shelf of pale clay silt, hardened smooth as vellum. She knelt before it. And from somewhere inside her ragged coat — Lena had searched that coat twice during her sleep, for weapons, for tokens, for anything that might explain her — she produced a sliver of charcoal-black stone, sharp-edged, worn glossy at one end from long handling.

Lena's breath caught. "You've had that the whole time."

The child ignored her. She bent over the clay shelf and began to draw.

* * *

Lena had spent eleven years among documents. She knew what it looked like when a scribe worked: the hesitations, the corrections, the little vanity flourishes. What the child did was not that. There were no hesitations. Her hand moved across the stone shelf with the certainty of someone copying, not creating — as if the image already existed somewhere behind her eyes, complete, and she was merely transferring it, line by line, from a memory too large for her body.

First the mountain. A single stroke, curved, unmistakably Thornfell — not the Thornfell of maps, which showed the range as a jagged crown of peaks, but the mountain itself, whole, as it must have looked before anything human stood beneath it. The child drew it in one unbroken line and Lena felt something turn over in her chest, because she had grown up in these mountains' shadow and she knew that silhouette the way she knew her own hands.

Then the chain. It came down from the peak in linked loops, each link drawn twice around, and it descended the mountainside and ended at a shape the child rendered with terrible economy: a circle, and inside the circle, lines radiating outward like a wheel, or an eye, or a sun seen through iron bars.

A crown. Lena knew it without being told. She had seen it once, at distance, at Aldric's coronation fifteen years ago — everyone in Saltspire had crowded the walls to watch the procession, and the crown had caught the light like a captive star. Nine points. She counted the child's radiating lines. Nine.

The child kept drawing. Around the base of the mountain, figures — small, stick-limbed, but not crude; there was intention in their postures, their raised arms, the way some knelt and some stood apart. Nine of them again. They stood in a ring at the mountain's foot, and from each figure the child drew a line up to the crown above, connecting them, binding them to it — or it to them. Lena couldn't tell which direction the binding ran, and the not-knowing made her skin crawl.

And then the names.

Beneath each figure, the child scratched a sequence of characters — the cant-script, the same flowing, angular letters Lena had laboriously matched in the temple archive against fragments of pre-kingdom binding-cant older than any charter, older than Aldric's dynasty, older than the kingdom itself. Nine sequences. Nine names, presumably. And then the child did the thing that made Lena set down her tin cup very slowly and carefully, because her hands had begun to shake.

She struck the names out.

One by one. Deliberately. Each stroke of the charcoal stone crossed through a written name with the same unhurried pressure, the same finality, as a clerk closing a ledger entry. When she finished, all nine figures at the mountain's foot bore crossed-out names, and the child sat back on her heels and looked at the drawing, and her face —

Her face was the worst part. It wasn't grief. Grief would have been bearable. It was recognition, calm and total, the look of someone reading a list they have read ten thousand times, and Lena understood, sitting there in the smoke-thin firelight with her heart hammering against her ribs, that the child was not depicting history.

She was reciting it. From memory.

* * *

"You remember it." Lena's voice came out rough. She cleared her throat. "The forging. You were — " She stopped. Started again. "How old are you?"

The child looked at her. Held up both hands, palms out, fingers spread. Ten fingers. Then she folded them down, one by one, until only two remained upright. Then those folded too, and she opened her empty hands: *none.*

"Not twelve winters," Lena whispered. "Not any winters."

The child tilted her head — a gesture Lena had come to know, the closest thing she had to a shrug — and turned back to the drawing. She touched the first crossed-out name, traced the strike-through with one fingertip, and then, softly, in the cant, she said a word. Just one. And the candle-bending pressure Lena remembered from that first storm-night stirred the fire's flames, gently, the way breath moves a curtain.

A name. She had spoken the first name. The one that had been struck out.

"They were real people." Lena heard herself say it aloud, as if saying it would make it less true. "Nine forgers. Someone made that crown — nine someones — and it consumed them, didn't it? That's what the strike-throughs mean. That's what the archive fragments meant, the ones I couldn't translate — *the makers enter the making* — oh, saints." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "You watched them do it. You watched them *die* doing it."

The child considered this. Then she shook her head — slowly, side to side, and pointed at the drawing, at the ring of nine figures, and then at herself, and drew one more figure beside them. Small. Alone. Set slightly outside the ring, the way you'd draw yourself standing at the edge of a game other children played.

Lena stared at the tenth figure. At the smallness of it. At the space between it and the nine.

"You weren't one of them," she said. "You were there when they made it. You've been alive since before the kingdom — before *any* kingdom — and you've been carrying the seal ever since, and you're—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "You're a *child*. Whatever else you are, however old you are underneath, you're a child who's been alone longer than my whole nation has existed."

The child looked up at her then, and in the firelight her eyes were dark and depthless and utterly exhausted, and Lena saw — really saw, for the first time since the knock before dawn — not a puzzle, not a seal, not a living lock on a king's head. A person. A person who had been carrying something unbearable for so long that she had forgotten it could be put down, and who had walked through a storm to the door of a stranger because the stranger's window had been the only one still lit.

Lena did the only thing she could think of. She moved across the cave, sat down beside the child on the cold stone, and put her arm around those narrow, rag-coated shoulders.

The child went rigid. Lena almost withdrew. Then, by degrees, like ice deciding to be water, the child leaned — barely, a few ounces of weight, a trust measured in grains — and let her forehead rest against Lena's collarbone.

They stayed that way a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere far off, wind moved through the pass with a sound like slow breathing.

"I have a name," Lena said into the child's hair. "Lena. Lena Vane. It's not much of a name. I owe so much money on it that technically it belongs to the temple. But it's mine, and I'm giving it to you to use whenever you need it. If anyone asks whose child you are, you're mine. All right?"

The child didn't answer in words. But her hand crept out — small, blackened with charcoal, steady — and touched the drawing one last time. Not the mountain. Not the crown. Not the nine struck-out names.

The blank space beside the tenth figure. The empty place where a name should go.

She tapped it twice. Looked at Lena. Tapped it again.

And Lena, following the child's finger, felt the awe in her chest curdle slowly toward dread, because she understood what the gesture meant, or thought she did, and the meaning was a question:

*Whose name goes here?*

Before she could speak — before she could decide whether she wanted to know — the child's head snapped up, her whole body going taut as wire, her gaze fixing on the cave mouth and the hawthorn screen beyond it. Far down the valley, faint but unmistakable on the night wind, came a sound Lena had hoped never to hear again: the belling of hounds.
