## 10. Salt and Ash

The pass was gone before they reached it.

Lena felt it first as a wrongness in the mountain's voice — the Thornfells had a language of their own, a grammar of wind and stone she'd learned from smugglers and debt-runners over six years of quiet treason — and the mountain was saying something new this morning, something low and continuous, like a held breath that refused to release. Then they rounded the shoulder of Grey Crag and saw it: the pass at Wolf's Throat, the narrow throat of scree and switchback that every map in her head marked as the way through, buried under a hundred feet of fresh-fallen snow.

Not fallen. *Thrown.*

She stood at the treeline with the child's cold hand in hers and read the slope the way she read contracts — for what was hidden between the lines. The avalanche had come off the eastern face, which meant it had been set off from the eastern face, which meant someone had climbed up there in the dark with charges or crowbars and the patience to wait. The crown did not waste men on chance. Somewhere behind them, Inquisitor Hale had stopped following their trail and started erasing the roads ahead of it.

"They've closed the door," Lena said aloud, because the child needed to hear words even if she couldn't answer them, because silence in the mountains was a thing that could eat you.

The child looked up. Snow had gotten into the wool of her hood and melted into dark beads against her lashes. She studied the buried pass without fear — she never seemed afraid of anything except closed doors and locked boxes, and Lena had begun keeping a private tally of the things that frightened her, cataloguing them like entries in a ledger, because a person's fears were the truest record of who they were. The child feared small spaces. She feared the sound of chains. She feared, above all, being carried away by someone whose face she could not see.

Lena crouched beside her and drew in the snow with one gloved finger: the pass, blocked; then, lower down, a different line threading through the trees toward the river valley.

"The pilgrim road," she said. "Dead two hundred years. Which means no patrols, no tolls, no eyes." She hesitated. "And no shelter, no bridges, and a river crossing that killed people even when the bridges were standing."

The child put her small mittened hand over the drawn line. Over the river. She nodded once, the way she always nodded — not agreement exactly, but acknowledgment, the gesture of someone very old agreeing to terms she found acceptable.

"You're braver than me," Lena told her, and stood, and led them down into the trees.

* * *

The pilgrim road revealed itself slowly, the way old debts reveal themselves — a corner here, a fragment there, until suddenly you understood the whole shape of what you owed. It had been cut into the western flank of the Thornfells by a faith that predated the Saltspire, predated Aldric's line, maybe predated the kingdom itself. Lena found its bones in the stones: a shrine niche carved with a symbol worn to a smooth dimple; a flight of steps descending into nothing; a leaning post that might once have held a lantern. The child stopped at each one. At the third shrine she pressed her palm flat against the carving and made a sound — not speech, not quite, but a hum in her chest, low and resonant, and for a moment the snow around the shrine seemed to settle rather than fall.

"What are you?" Lena whispered, and immediately hated herself for it, because the child was not a *what*, and because the question had an answer Lena already knew and did not want: the child was a seal, a living lock, the last thread holding shut a hunger older than kingdoms. But knowing it in the archive among dead texts was different from knowing it here, walking beside her, watching her press her hand to shrines built by hands that had never known a king.

They walked all day. By late afternoon the light went the color of pewter and the temperature dropped so fast that Lena's breath froze in her scarf and the trees began to crack — sharp reports like distant gunfire as sap split in the cold. The child's pace slowed. Lena noticed it the way she noticed everything about her now: the drag in the small steps, the way the child had stopped looking around and started looking only down, conserving herself, spending herself carefully like a woman counting out coins she couldn't replace.

The seal is failing faster now. The thought arrived uninvited and stayed. Every mile they gained, the thing inside the crown gained too, pulling at the thread, and the child was the thread, and threads wear thin.

"We stop soon," Lena said. "There should be a pilgrim hospice past the ridge. Stone. Old. Probably a roof."

There was a roof. Half of one. The hospice huddled in a fold of the hillside like a creature trying not to be seen, its walls furred with frost, its doorway choked with two centuries of blown snow. Lena dug them in with the flat of her knife and a piece of broken shutter, and inside they found a floor of flagstones, a hearth black with ancient ash, and — miracle of miracles — half a rafter dry enough to burn. Lena got a fire going with numb fingers and the last of her tinder, fed it splinters until it caught, and sat back on her heels shaking.

The child sat close to the flames and held her hands out, and Lena watched the firelight move across her face and thought, not for the first time: *she looks like nobody.* Not unusual — children resemble everyone and no one — but the child resembled no lineage at all, no region, no people. Her features were like a word in a language you almost recognized. Like the cant itself.

They ate hard bread softened in melted snow, and a strip of salt pork Lena had been saving, and the child fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew, the way exhausted children do, and Lena eased her down onto the folded cloak and sat watching the fire and listening to the mountain crack and groan around them.

Somewhere out there, Hale was reading her trail. She imagined him doing it — that courteous, patient man with his neat gloves and his ledger of debts, kneeling in the snow where she'd knelt, seeing exactly what she'd done and understanding exactly what it meant. He wasn't stupid. That was the horror of him. Stupid hunters lost you. Hale would follow the logic of her choices better than she had, and he'd arrive at the pilgrim road eventually, because there was nowhere else the road could go.

Sleep took her in pieces after that, shallow and salted with dreams of ledgers unrolling to the horizon, every page bearing her name.

* * *

The river announced itself before dawn as a sound beneath the silence — a deep, muscular mutter under the ice, like something enormous talking in its sleep. They reached it an hour after first light, and Lena's stomach dropped through the frozen ground.

In summer, the maps said, the pilgrim road crossed at a ford below a bend, water to the knee over gravel. There was no summer now. The river ran fast and black between banks of blue ice, only partially frozen, its surface stitched with white seams of current where the water moved too violently to freeze. Ice shelves jutted out over the flow like the eaves of drowned houses. The air coming off it was wet and killing, cold enough to make breathing feel like swallowing glass.

"We cross here," Lena said, "or we don't cross at all."

She spent an hour finding the way. Downstream the banks steepened; upstream the river narrowed but ran faster, white and furious through a rock chute. The ford it was. She tested the near shelf with her whole weight, then the child's, then tied the rope from her pack around a pine trunk and around the child's waist under her arms, knotting it the way Brannoc had taught her — the smuggler's knot, quick-release, the kind that holds under load and lets go in a heartbeat. She talked the whole time, low and steady, nonsense mostly, the sound of her own voice a rope for both of them.

"Step where I step. Hold my hand, not my sleeve — sleeves tear. If you go in, don't fight the water, let the rope hold you. Can you nod? Good girl. Good."

The child nodded. And then, at the edge of the ice, she did something she had never done: she squeezed Lena's hand. Twice. Deliberate. A signal, a sentence, a whole vocabulary compressed into two small pressures of cold fingers.

*I trust you.*

Lena's throat closed. She had crossed borders with forged papers, lied to collectors' faces, burned six years of her life into other people's escapes, and nothing had ever hit her like those two squeezes. She squeezed back once — *I have you* — and stepped onto the ice.

It held. For eight steps it held, the river muttering beneath them like a debtor denied, and then the ninth step went through.

The world became noise and cold. Lena went in to the hip, the shock of it punching the air out of her lungs, and the current took her instantly — not gently, not like water at all, but like a hand closing on her leg and *pulling*. The rope snapped taut against the pine. She heard it creak. She heard, distantly, absurdly, the child screaming — the first sound resembling a scream she'd ever made, high and raw and human — and then Lena's free hand found the lip of the ice shelf and she clawed at it and the ice broke twice under her fingers before it held, and she hung there in the roaring black while the river tried to peel her off the world.

*Don't fight,* she told herself, hearing her own voice from minutes ago. *Let the rope hold.*

But the rope was holding the child too, or trying to — Lena could see her, sprawled on the shelf edge, small arms wrapped around the line, being dragged forward inch by inch toward the hole where Lena had gone through. The knot was slipping. Or the pine was giving. Or the child's grip was failing. All three, probably. Everything fails at once; that was the river's whole theology.

"Let GO of the rope!" Lena screamed, and the child didn't, of course she didn't, because letting go of the rope meant letting go of *her*, and this child had been let go of by everyone, kingdoms and temples and nine dead forgers, and she would not —

The pine cracked.

Later, Lena could never reconstruct the next ten seconds. She remembered the lurch as the tree gave, the shelf sliding, the child skidding toward the water on her belly with the rope hissing through her fists. She remembered hauling herself up onto the ice with strength she did not know she owned, the current still gripping her leg like a creditor. She remembered rolling, grabbing the rope, bracing her boots against a seam of blue ice, and taking the child's full weight through her shoulders, her back, her teeth. She remembered thinking, with strange clarity, through the white roar of pain and cold: *this is what I am for. This. Not the ledgers. This.*

The rope came taut. Held. Held. Held.

Then Lena was dragging, hand over hand, and the child's mittens were leaving the edge of the ice, and then they weren't, because Lena had her by the hood, by the coat, by the arm, and hauled her up out of the river's mouth onto the shelf, and the two of them lay there gasping on the frozen bank like things newly born, steaming, sobbing, alive.

* * *

Hypothermia does not announce itself. It arrives politely, like a collector, and offers you warmth, and you sign without reading.

Lena recognized the signature just in time. They were perhaps a quarter mile downstream of the crossing, having stumbled and crawled that far on legs that belonged to other people, when she caught herself thinking that the snow looked comfortable. That lying down for a moment would be fine. That the burning in her hands and feet had faded into something pleasant, and wouldn't it be nice to rest.

"No," she said aloud, to herself, to the child, to the polite voice. "No. Up. We walk."

She got a fire going — her second miracle of the day, fingers so clumsy she broke two matches and wept without noticing she was weeping — and stripped the child's soaked outer clothes and wrapped her in everything dry they owned, and then did the same for herself, and sat shuddering by the flames while the sun crossed the sky and neither of them spoke, because speaking cost breath and breath cost heat.

It was dusk when the child stirred against her side and looked up at her.

Her lips were still gray. Her eyes were clear, though — clearer than Lena had ever seen them, wide and dark and ancient and, underneath the antiquity, something young and stunned, the look of someone who has just learned a fact about the world that cannot be unlearned. Lena watched her form the word. Watched her mouth work around sounds that didn't fit it, the cant rising in her throat and being pushed back down, rejected, wrong, until finally — slowly, carefully, like a scribe copying a foreign script letter by letter, afraid of smudging it —

"Lena."

One syllable. Two. Her own name, spoken in a voice like a bell rung underwater, rough with disuse and wonder.

The child said it again, testing it, owning it. "*Lena.*"

And then she burrowed into Lena's side, under her arm, into the hollow of her shoulder as if the space had been measured for her, and closed her eyes, and slept — truly slept, deeply, trustingly, the sleep of a child who believes, for the first time in what might be centuries, that she will still be held when she wakes.

Lena sat perfectly still. The fire popped. The river muttered somewhere behind them in the dark, still talking, still hungry, cheated of its due. Her shoulders screamed. Her hands were ruined. Tomorrow they would have to find food, and warmth, and a path over the last ridge to the forge road, and somewhere behind them Hale would be standing at the broken pine, reading the story the rope had written, and he would understand it completely.

None of it mattered. None of it could touch this.

*She knows my name,* Lena thought, and the thought filled her entire chest like a lungful of warm air. *Out of everything she remembers — the forging, the chain, the nine names scratched out of the world — she chose to keep mine.*

She looked down at the sleeping child, at the small fist curled in her coat, and made the promise quietly, the way you initial a page you know will bind you:

"I'll get you there. Whatever it costs. I'll get you there."

Far to the south, down in the valleys where the roads were open and the fires were many, a bell began to ring — once, twice, a pattern, urgent and deliberate. Signal-codes. Lena knew them; she'd forged travel writs long enough to know how the crown talked to itself.

That was temple-bell rhythm. That was the Saltspire calling its own.

Someone had told them where she was going.
