## 12. Nine Names

The shrine found them the way old things find the desperate — by accident, half-buried, almost missed.

Lena had meant to push on. The light was failing, and every hour they lingered in the open hills of the Thornfells' lower skirts was an hour Hale's hounds could close. But the child had stopped dead in the middle of the track, mud to her knees, rain needling down, and pointed with a small, certain finger at a fold in the hillside where the bracken grew wrong — too thick, too green, fed by something beneath.

Lena had learned to trust that finger. It had found them water twice, a smuggler's cache once, and a hollow under a fallen oak when patrol lanterns swept the valley road below them like searchlights across a prison yard.

So she followed it up through the bracken, boots slipping on wet stone, until the hillside opened its mouth.

The shrine was older than the kingdom. That was Lena's first thought, and her second, and the thought she kept returning to as she stood dripping in the entrance while the child walked in ahead of her without hesitation, without fear, the way one walks into a house one has lived in before. The architecture predated everything Lena knew how to read. The stones were fitted without mortar, each one bearing marks that were not decoration but *writing* — the same cant the child spoke, carved deep and worn shallow by centuries of weather, so that the walls seemed to murmur even in silence.

A roof of overlapping slates had mostly held. In the center of the single round chamber, a fire pit still held ash from some traveler decades dead. Dry corners. A spring seeping through a crack in the far wall into a basin cut from a single block of stone, still running clear after however many hundreds of years. Whoever built this place had built it to last, and to be found, and Lena understood with a chill that crept up through her wet stockings that both of those intentions had been for the same purpose.

This was not a shrine to anything. It was a shrine *about* something. A warning, perhaps. Or a record.

"Here," Lena said aloud, uselessly, because the child had already begun unpacking the charcoal stubs they'd bought from Brannoc along with the bread and the rope, laying them out on the flat altar-stone at the chamber's heart with the gravity of a scribe preparing her desk.

Lena built a fire first. That was the order of things now: fire, food, water, then whatever the child needed. She'd stopped thinking of it as protecting her somewhere on the pilgrim road, in the dark of the river night. Somewhere between the freezing water and the small voice saying *Lena* — her name, the first word, given like a gift — the child had become hers in the way that mattered more than blood or contract: by choice, renewed daily, in every small act of care.

She got the fire going with the last of their dry tinder, hung their soaked cloaks on a fallen beam near the flames, and set out the hard bread and dried apples Brannoc's cousin had sold them at an outrageous markup two days ago. The child ate mechanically, eyes never leaving the walls, lips moving faintly around syllables that made the fire lean toward her.

Lena watched that happen three times before she let herself believe it. The flames bent. Not flickered — *bent*, all together, like grass under wind, tilting toward the child whenever her voice dropped into the low register of the cant, then straightening when she fell silent. The first time Lena had seen it, in her cottage during the storm, she'd told herself it was a draft. There were no drafts here. The shrine was sealed as tight as a tomb.

*She's reciting a seal,* Lena reminded herself. *Over and over. Like a prayer holding something shut.* And prayers, presumably, were allowed to bend fire. Prayers had been doing stranger things lately.

After the meal, the child went to the altar-stone and began to draw.

*

Lena had seen the drawings before. They'd been accumulating since the second night on the road — charcoal on the backs of the forged writs Lena no longer needed, on scraps of bark, on the waxed cloth of their food wrapping. At first they'd been fragments: a circle, a chain, a shape like a mountain with a hole in its heart. Lena had catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, in the neat mental ledgers that were the only inheritance her mother had left her, waiting for enough pieces to assemble a whole.

Tonight, apparently, the pieces were ready.

The child worked for over an hour, and she did not work like a child. She worked like a mason, like an engraver, checking her lines against the wall-carvings, sometimes standing to press her palm flat against a particular stone as though consulting it. The firelight threw her shadow huge against the carved walls, and twice Lena saw the shadow move a fraction out of step with the small body casting it — a lag, a hesitation, the way King Aldric's shadow had moved behind him in the throne room, half a heartbeat late.

Lena's hands went cold. She told herself the firelight lied. Firelight lied constantly; it was firelight's profession.

When the drawing was done, the child stepped back and looked at Lena, and pointed at the stone, and said something in the cant — a full sentence, low and deliberate, the fire bending hard toward her voice — and then sat down cross-legged on the floor to wait, the way a teacher waits for a student to finish reading.

Lena approached the altar-stone.

The drawing filled it edge to edge. At the top: the mountain, rendered with a precision no child could possess, every ridge correct, and in its heart a forge-fire drawn in careful strokes of white — the child had scraped the charcoal away to bare stone there, so the fire glowed pale against the black lines. Below the mountain, descending like roots: the chain. Nine links drawn large, each link distinct, and within each link a figure.

Nine figures. Nine people, kneeling or standing or reaching, each one rendered inside its own link of the chain, and each one connected by a thin line to the crown at the bottom of the drawing — the crown itself, drawn last, drawn heaviest, the charcoal laid on so thick it shone.

And above each figure, a name.

That was what stopped Lena's breath. Not the figures, not the chain, not even the crown. The names. Because they were written in the cant — she recognized maybe a third of the characters now, after weeks of the child's speech soaking into her scribe's memory like dye into cloth — and every single one of them had been struck through. Not crossed out carelessly. Each name had been scored through with a single deliberate line, and beside each struck name, in smaller characters, the same mark repeated nine times: a symbol Lena knew. The child used it constantly. It appeared in nearly every sentence she spoke.

Lena had assumed it was a common word. *The*, perhaps. Or *and*.

Standing over the altar-stone, matching the symbol to the context of the drawing — nine names, nine strikes, nine repetitions of the same sign — Lena understood what it meant, and understanding it felt like a door opening onto a long drop.

It meant *consumed*. Or possibly *paid*. The grammar of the cant didn't distinguish, and Lena suspected, with a scholar's distant horror, that the language's makers hadn't distinguished either.

"They paid," Lena whispered. "They paid with themselves."

The child watched her, unblinking, and nodded once.

Lena knelt at the stone and began, scribe-fashion, to work. This was the thing she knew how to do, the thing the temple had ground into her through ten years of copying contracts until her fingers cramped: read the document. Understand the terms. No matter what the terms cost anyone else, understand them first, because the person who understands the contract is the person who isn't destroyed by it.

She traced the structure with her eyes. Nine forgers. Nine names, struck through, consumed — the crown had eaten them, one by one, or they had fed themselves to it willingly; the drawing didn't say which, and Lena suspected the distinction didn't exist in the minds of whoever made it. Each consumed forger had gone into a link of the chain. The chain bound the crown. The crown held — held *what*? The Vessel, the hunger, the thing that wore Aldric now like a coat worn inside-out so the fur showed through the seams.

And the names. That was the part that made her scribe's soul sit up and scream. The names weren't memorial. They were structural. Nine names in the binding, nine links in the chain — the names *were* the links. Strike a name, lose a link. All nine struck, all nine consumed, and the chain was —

Was what? Broken? Complete?

The child answered her without being asked, because the child always seemed to know what Lena was asking before Lena finished asking it. She crawled forward on her knees, took Lena's hand, and guided it to the bottom of the drawing, below the crown, below the chain, to a single horizontal line drawn in charcoal.

Blank. Waiting. The only line in the entire composition without a name above it, without a strike through it, without a mark of any kind except the line itself — and the line was drawn differently from everything else. Everything else was carved into the drawing with pressure, dug in, final. The blank line was drawn lightly. Tentatively. Almost hopefully.

The tenth line.

"It's empty," Lena said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, the voice she used in the scriptorium when the Provost watched her work. "Why is it empty?"

The child took the charcoal from her hand. Drew, above the blank line, nothing — drew instead a pair of shapes Lena knew from the archive fragments, from the oldest stratum of the cant, the grammatical particles that marked volition. Willingness. Choice. The symbols for *freely* and *for another*.

Then she set the charcoal down, took Lena's face in her two small cold hands, and looked at her with eyes that were ancient and exhausted and desperately, urgently clear, and spoke one sentence of the cant, slowly, enunciating every character the way one speaks to someone learning.

Lena understood perhaps half the words. It was enough. It was more than enough.

*The tenth is not taken. The tenth must be given.*

*

She didn't sleep. She sat by the fire with the child asleep against her side — the child slept easily now, trust being a thing that once established ran on its own fuel — and stared into the flames and assembled the horror piece by piece, because assembling horrors into orderly rows was also something the temple had taught her, and she would rather do it than feel it.

The Vessel wore Aldric. Fine. Established. Seen with her own eyes: the lagging shadow, the patient hungry patience of those eyes. But wearing was not being. The seal — the child, the living seal, the last remnant of the binding woven into flesh — was failing, and a failing seal meant the Vessel strained against its chain, but strain was not escape. The drawing said otherwise. The drawing said the chain had nine links and nine names and all nine were spent, and the tenth line waited empty, and the tenth link could not be *taken*.

Must be *given*. Freely. For another.

Which meant the Vessel could not simply break out. It needed someone to open the door from the inside. It needed a volunteer. Someone who would walk to the crown, kneel, and offer up their own name to be struck through — freely, knowingly, for another — and in that moment of willing sacrifice the binding would invert, the seal would become a summons, and the hunger would come through fully loosed, wearing its new anchor, drinking through a straw dipped willingly into a willing throat.

And Aldric — the real Aldric, the man buried alive inside his own body, scratching coded messages into cell walls — Aldric was fighting it. Lena had seen the scratches. Whatever remained of the king spent his stolen moments sabotaging his captor, leaving warnings, holding doors shut from his side.

But the Vessel had time. The Vessel had a kingdom. The Vessel had inquisitors and collectors and hounds made of debt-bound men, and it had years — decades — to find its volunteer. To cultivate one. To make someone desperate enough, or devoted enough, or deceived enough to give their name freely.

Or — and the thought arrived like ice water — to make everyone believe such a giving was *necessary*. To arrange a crisis so dire that some hero would conclude, freely and sincerely, that offering themselves was the only way to save the ones they loved. The trap didn't need a traitor. It only needed love, aimed correctly.

Lena looked down at the sleeping child, at the small chest rising and falling, at the mouth slightly open around breath that carried the oldest language in the world, and felt the trap's teeth close around her own heart with a click she heard quite clearly.

Because she would do it. If it came to that — if the choice were offered, if the child's survival required a name on the tenth line — she would kneel at that altar and give hers without hesitation, and the Vessel knew it, and the Vessel had known it since the moment a mud-soaked child knocked on a scribe's door. Perhaps that was why the seal had been sent to *her*, of all people. Not to a soldier, not to a priest. To a woman whose whole life was other people's debts, who had spent ten years learning exactly what it cost to owe and exactly what it meant to pay.

*The tenth must be given.* And the Vessel had arranged the world so that giving it would look like salvation.

"No," Lena said softly, to the fire, to the shrine, to the ancient hungry thing wearing a king three hundred miles away. "No. I'll find another answer."

The fire bent toward the child, and straightened, and burned on.

*

Dawn came gray and clean through the shrine's broken roof-slate, and with it came the decision Lena had been circling all night.

They could not run forever. Running was Hale's game — he was a pursuer, bred and trained, and pursuit favored him. Every mile they fled bought him terrain, bought him informants, bought him the slow attrition of their food and luck. The child weakened as they traveled; Lena had watched it happen, day by day, the small body growing lighter, the cant coming faster, the fire bending harder — the seal spending itself, burning down like a candle lit at both ends. They had weeks. Maybe less.

Which meant the destination wasn't optional anymore. It had never been optional. The mountain forge where the crown was made — the child had drawn it a dozen times, always the same mountain, always the hole in its heart, and Lena finally admitted to herself that she'd known which mountain since the first drawing, because there was only one mountain in the Thornfells with a wound like that, only one peak the smugglers wouldn't go near, the one Brannoc had spat at and called *the Old Anvil* and refused, absolutely refused, to guide anyone toward.

Five days' hard travel. Through country where Hale's hounds ran thickest. Toward the one place the crown's power was rooted — and therefore, if the child's drawings and the shrine's carvings and Lena's own cold scribe logic could be trusted, the one place it could be unmade.

Not to escape. To end it.

Lena packed their few belongings while the child stood at the shrine entrance, looking out at the morning, and Lena noticed she was humming — the cant, but softened, rounded, almost a lullaby. The seal-song sung gently, for once, not as a lock but as a comfort. The sound settled over the ruined shrine like a blessing over a grave.

"We go to the mountain," Lena said. "Your mountain."

The child turned. Smiled — the first true smile Lena had seen from her, wide and unguarded and heartbreakingly young — and spoke a single word of the cant, pointing east toward the peaks where the Old Anvil stood with its wound full of sky.

Lena didn't know the word. But she knew the tone. Every debtor knew that tone; it was the tone of someone naming the place they intended, someday, to be free.

They went down through the bracken into the bright cold morning, two small figures on a road that every map of the kingdom left deliberately blank, walking toward the thing that had eaten nine names and was waiting, patiently, hungrily, for a tenth.

Behind them, in the ruined shrine, the fire pit's ancient ash stirred once in the wind — and settled, like a ledger closed.
