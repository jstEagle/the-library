## 13. Audience with Hunger

The palace of Aldric the Third had one hundred and fourteen rooms above ground. He had counted them as a boy, running through corridors with his brother's laughter chasing him, and he had counted them again in the first year of his captivity, because counting was the only thing left that was entirely his.

Below ground there were nine.

He knew this too, though he had never been told it. He had learned it the way a drowning man learns water — by what pressed against him, by where the dark gave way and where it did not. The Vessel kept its body in motion during the day: audiences, councils, hunts, the long theater of kingship performed with a precision that made the courtiers weep with pride at their king's renewed vigor. But at night — or what passed for night in the windowless deep — it brought him down here, into the ninth room, which was not a room at all but a throat, a place the mountain had hollowed out long before any kingdom laid claim to its stone.

There, when the hunger was sated enough to be careless, Aldric got his moments back.

* * *

They came like coins slipped between fingers. A breath of control, two, sometimes three if he was lucky — and luck, he had learned, was a currency the Vessel minted deliberately, spending small mercies to purchase larger complacence. It wanted him docile. A prisoner who struggled burned energy the thing needed; a prisoner who hoped, who bargained, who believed himself engaged in negotiation, stayed quiet.

So Aldric bargained. It was the only dignity remaining to him, and he clutched it the way a miser clutches coin — knowing full well the purse would be emptied either way.

"You're weakening," he said, into the dark of the ninth room. His voice came out of his own mouth, rough and strange, and he hated how much he had come to sound like a beggar even to himself. "The seal is failing. I can feel it in the hands." He raised them, turned them in the light of the brazier the Vessel permitted — iron braziers, always iron, never gold, never silver, nothing that could be worked into a binding. "You feel it too. Don't lie to me. Lying is the one thing you do badly."

The darkness answered. It did not speak as men speak; it spoke as cold speaks, as pressure speaks, a voice that arrived already inside the skull and left frost on the thoughts it touched.

***THE SEAL HOLDS.***

"It holds the way a dam holds. And dams break from the top down." Aldric let the hands fall. Somewhere in the walls, water moved — an old aqueduct, pre-kingdom work, the masons of the First Kingdom having built better than anything built since. He fixed on the sound. Anchors mattered. In six years he had learned that a man without anchors came apart like wet paper, and then there was nothing left for the Vessel to wear but meat and appetite, and even the thing wearing him seemed to prefer otherwise. "You need me coherent. You've said so. Written so, rather, in my own hand, on my own decrees, while I screamed behind my own eyes."

***COHERENCE IS PRESERVED.***

"Then preserve this." He stepped toward the brazier, and felt the Vessel's attention tighten around him like a fist closing — not stopping him, never quite stopping him, but weighing him, measuring whether the moment was worth the cost of suppression. It was the great flaw in his captor, the crack he had spent years learning the shape of: the Vessel could take everything, but taking everything meant holding everything, and holding was effort, and effort was something it hoarded like a starving man hoards bread. "Give me tonight. One night of my hands, my eyes, my tongue. In exchange I'll stop fighting the transition when you take the council session tomorrow. No resistance. No headaches for you. No... incidents."

***INCIDENTS ARE COSTLY TO YOU.***

"Yes," Aldric said quietly. "They are."

The last incident had cost him three days of existence as a passenger in his own skull while the Vessel flayed a treasury clerk's mind open in front of the full court, searching for a leak, and found only a man who had skimmed four crowns' worth of grain tariffs. The clerk had been executed for treason. Aldric had been made to sign the order. He remembered every stroke of the pen. He suspected he would remember every stroke of every pen until he died, and he had stopped believing, some time ago, that death would be permitted to arrive on schedule.

The silence stretched. Then, like a door unbarred:

***ONE NIGHT.***

* * *

Control, when it came, was agony and glory in equal measure. It was like surfacing after drowning — the first breath burning, the light almost unbearable, the body suddenly, shockingly *his*, heavy and real and aching in forty places the Vessel never bothered to notice. His knees nearly buckled. He caught himself on the brazier's edge and stood there, shaking, feeling his own heartbeat the way a blind man feels sunlight.

Then he moved, because he had perhaps four hours, and he had wasted enough of them over the years on weeping.

The palace at night was a country he ruled alone. He went up through the service stairs, past sleeping guards who saluted a king they did not know they were guarding, and into the scriptorium attached to the royal apartments — a small room, deliberately modest, because the Vessel had learned early that ostentatious privacy invited suspicion, and suspicion was the one predator it truly feared.

Aldric sat down at the desk, took a fresh sheet of the thin palace vellum, and began to write.

This was the game within the game. Everything he wrote would be read — he assumed as much, planned around it, had tested the assumption at terrible cost in the second year. So he wrote what a king under strain would write: memoranda, drafts of edicts, fragments of a speech for the spring court. And threaded through them, in the margins, in the spacing between lines, in the choice of one word over its twin, he wrote the other letter. The true one.

*Consider the matter of the northern tolls,* he wrote, and the *n-o-r* of *northern* sat a hair higher than the rest, and *tolls* was written with the old mercantile abbreviation for *thousand*, which no king used, which only a man who had spent his youth auditing his father's ledgers would use. *The assessors have grown lax.* *Lax* — and beneath it, invisible unless you knew to look, the ghost-strokes of letters he had practiced ten thousand times in the dark: *still alive. still me.*

It was a language of one speaker and, he prayed, eventually one reader. He had no idea if anyone would ever read it. That was the bargain he had made with himself in the third year, after the despair had come close to eating him whole: *write as if someone is coming. Live as if someone is coming. If no one comes, you will at least have lived as a man and not as a wound.*

Tonight he added new lines. The seal was failing — he could feel it in the tremor of his own hands, in the way the Vessel's control stuttered at odd hours now, in dreams that leaked through that were not his dreams: a mountain, a chain, a forge-fire seen from below. Something was pulling at the crown from outside. Someone.

He wrote: *If the child lives, find her. She is the door. I am the room behind it.*

And then, because hope was a dangerous animal and had to be kept muzzled, he crossed it out. Heavily. A king changing his mind. Nothing more.

* * *

Dawn, or the hour the palace called dawn, found him in the map room, standing before the great relief-map of the Thornfells that his grandfather had commissioned, and this was where the Vessel found him when it took the body back.

It did not announce itself. It never did. There was simply a moment when his hands stopped being his — a sensation he had never managed to describe, even in the privacy of his own mind, except as *the water closing over*. One instant he was tracing the passes with one finger, memorizing the route from Saltspire to the old forge road (why the forge road? why did his dreams keep dragging him there?), and the next his finger was moving without him, tapping twice against the map's edge, a gesture of impatience that belonged to something else entirely.

***YOU SOUGHT THE FORGE ROAD.***

"I sought sleep," Aldric said, from behind the glass of his own eyes. "I didn't find it."

***THE FORGE IS CLOSED. THE FORGE HAS ALWAYS BEEN CLOSED.***

"Then why do you dream about it?"

The silence that followed had texture. He had learned to read the Vessel's silences the way sailors read weather, and this one was the silence of a thing deciding how much truth a prisoner could be trusted with. When it answered, the words came slow, each one placed like a stone across a grave.

***THE DREAMS ARE NOT MINE.***

"No?"

***SOMETHING CALLS ALONG THE OLD CANT. FAINTLY. FROM WITHOUT THE SPIRE.*** A pause, and then, with something that in any living creature might have been unease: ***IT SHOULD NOT BE POSSIBLE.***

Aldric kept his face — the Vessel's face — perfectly still. Inside, his heart was a hammer. *Something calls along the old cant.* He knew the phrase from his grandfather's forbidden histories, the ones burned by royal decree in his father's reign: the binding-cant, the speech of the First Kingdom, the language in which the nine forgers had chained their hunger into gold. A language older than any living nation. A language that should have died with the last of the forgers.

Unless it hadn't.

Unless somewhere out there, beyond the Spire, beyond the reach of the crown and its inquisitors and its hounds, someone was speaking it. Reciting it. Over and over, the way one recites a prayer, or a lock.

"The child," he said aloud, before he could stop himself. "Is it the child?"

The reaction was instantaneous and terrible. The body lurched forward, his hand slamming flat against the map table hard enough to crack the lacquer, and for one blinding moment the Vessel forgot to be patient, forgot to be kingly, and Aldric saw it plain — saw it the way one sees the bottom of a river in flood, briefly, horribly. Hunger. Not appetite, not greed, none of the human vices it wore like borrowed clothes, but hunger itself, primordial and patient, the thing that had eaten nine names and worn a tenth.

***WHAT CHILD.***

"A figure of speech," Aldric said, and poured every ounce of six years' practice into mildness. "A story my nurse told me. The child in the mountain. Every kingdom has such stories."

***YES,*** said the Vessel, and the flood closed over the riverbed, and it was patient again, and kingly, and cold. ***EVERY KINGDOM HAS SUCH STORIES.***

But it carried the question away with it, down into the deep rooms, and Aldric felt it turning the question over in the dark like a man turning a suspicious coin, testing its weight, looking for the false metal. And he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like joy, that he had just learned something the Vessel had not wanted him to know.

It was afraid.

Not of him. Never of him. But of something outside — something small, perhaps, and far away, and speaking in a dead tongue. Something that called along the old cant.

*The child lives,* he thought, into the prison of himself. *Please. Let the child live.*

* * *

The audience came three days later, and it nearly destroyed him.

He was permitted — the Vessel was pleased to permit — the morning petitions, the ritual of justice performed before the assembled court. Aldric sat the throne and watched through his own eyes as the line of petitioners filed past: a widow seeking her confiscated mill, a merchant disputing a tariff, a temple provost in salt-white robes presenting the annual debt rolls with a bow so deep it was almost an insult in its perfection.

And then, near the end of the line, a name was announced that made the Vessel go very still inside him, and made Aldric go very still inside the Vessel, so that for a moment there were two statues wearing one skin.

"Inquisitor Hale," the herald called, "bearing report."

Hale was exactly what rumor painted and rumor had not done him justice: a lean man with a clerk's ink-stained fingers and a soldier's stillness, who bowed precisely as deeply as protocol required and not a hair further. He reported in a voice like dry paper — border irregularities, a smuggler network partially unraveled, certain irregularities in the Saltspire district. Certain rumors.

"Rumors of what?" the Vessel asked, through Aldric's mouth, with the bored cadence of a king humoring a functionary.

"Of a child, majesty. Mud-marked, mute-seeming, speaking an unknown tongue. Seen first near the temple district. The temple's records show no child of that description entered or housed." Hale's eyes lifted, just slightly, and Aldric felt the Vessel's attention sharpen to a needle's point. "The temple claims ignorance. The temple often does."

"And you believe the temple lies?"

"I believe, majesty, that ignorance is a garment, and the Saltspire wears it tailored." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "I have taken the liberty of opening an inquiry. Quietly. I will require access to the temple's archive. The restricted archive."

The Vessel considered. Aldric screamed inside the consideration — screamed *no, not the archive, she'll be found, whoever she is, whatever poor soul is hiding her will be found* — and the scream changed nothing, as screams never had, and the king's mouth opened and pronounced, in the king's voice, the words that signed whatever warrant Hale required.

"Order is the crown's first duty," the Vessel said. "See it done."

Hale bowed and withdrew, and the audience moved on to the widow and her mill, and the widow was denied, and the merchant was taxed, and the machinery of the kingdom ground forward, oiled and terrible.

That night, in the ninth room, in the dark that was not a room but a throat, Aldric lay on the cold stone that was the closest thing he had to a bed and did the arithmetic of despair.

*She'll be found.* If the child existed — if the calling thing was a child, and every instinct he had left said it was — then Hale would find her. Hale, who missed nothing, who believed utterly in the law he served, who would unravel the temple and the smugglers' roads and every kindness ever extended to a fugitive, with courtesy and patience and the full weight of the crown behind him. And the Vessel would take her, and the last door would be locked, and the seal would fail not because the child broke it but because the Vessel consumed her, and then—

Then the hunger would be loosed on a world that had forgotten, in six hundred comfortable years, what it was.

He lay in the dark and he bargained, because bargaining was all he had, and tonight he bargained with God, or with the mountain, or with the memory of his brother laughing in the sunlit corridors, or with whatever power watched over prisoners and fools.

*Let her run fast. Let her find the roads. Let someone help her — some scribe, some smuggler, some stranger with a kind heart and a fast horse. Let her reach the forge.*

And underneath the prayer, quieter, in the voice of a man who had stopped expecting rescue:

*And if she reaches the forge — if there is any mercy in the architecture of this world — let her know I'm in here. Let someone tell her I'm in here.*

*I am still in here.*

Far above, in the scriptorium, a sheet of vellum dried in the dark, its marginal ghosts waiting for eyes that might never come. Far below, the Vessel dreamed of a forge-fire seen from underneath, and counted, patiently, the days until the seal broke.

And in the Saltspire district, in a narrow house on a street of debtors, a candle bent sideways in a windless room, and a child spoke words no kingdom remembered, and a woman who copied contracts for a living woke from a dream of chains and could not say why her hands were shaking.

The message was already traveling. It simply did not know yet that it was a message at all.
