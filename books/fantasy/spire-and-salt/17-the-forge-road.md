## 17. The Forge Road

The mountain did not want them there.

Lena felt it in the way the path narrowed and then gave up pretending to be a path at all, crumbling into scree that shifted under each step like a floor of loose teeth. She felt it in the wind, which came down off the high snowfields in long, deliberate gusts, as though something vast above them were breathing out its displeasure. And she felt it most of all in the child, who had stopped speaking entirely — not the old cant, not Lena's name, not even the small wordless sounds she made when she was frightened — and had begun instead to flicker.

There was no better word for it. In the grey pre-dawn light, with frost silvering every stone, the girl's outline seemed to waver at the edges, like a candle flame seen through smoked glass. One moment her small shoulders were solid against Lena's arm; the next they were translucent, and Lena could see the dark rock of the slope through her sleeve.

"Stay with me," Lena said, for perhaps the hundredth time since they'd left Hale's signet burning in the crypt gate behind them. "Just stay with me a little longer."

The child's hand tightened in hers. That much remained solid. That much, at least, the mountain could not take.

They had been climbing for two days. Behind them, somewhere below the cloud line, the bound soldiers were climbing too.

* * *

Lena allowed herself no more than a hundred breaths of rest at each halt, counting them the way she'd once counted lines of contract script, because counting kept panic at bay and panic was a luxury the mountain did not sell. She built no fire now. Fire drew eyes, and eyes were what they could not afford. Instead she fed the child strips of dried mutton and hardtack softened in snowmelt, and wrapped them both in the single wool blanket from her satchel, and watched the north road below through gaps in the rocks.

On the second evening she saw them: torches strung along the valley floor like beads on a black cord, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who knew their quarry had nowhere to go but up. Thirty of them, maybe forty. Too many to fight. Too many to outrun on legs that hadn't slept properly in three days.

But not, she thought, watching the torch-line pause where the pilgrim road forked, too many to mislead.

She took out Brannoc's map — the scrap of oiled leather he'd pressed into her hands before Hale's hounds took him, may his debts be forgiven wherever he'd gone — and studied it by feel rather than light. The forge road proper wound up the mountain's southern face, wide enough for ore-carts in the old days, switchbacked and graded. It was also, if the map's faded annotations meant anything, watched. The Vessel would have sealed every approach it thought a person might use.

Which left the approaches no person would use.

"There's an old miners' stair," she told the child, who watched her face with those ancient, patient eyes that sat so strangely in a face so young. "Cut into the cliff on the east side, back when the forge still worked. The smugglers called it the Ladder. It goes straight up where the road goes around. It will be cold, and it will be narrow, and I am not going to lie to you — it will be terrifying."

The child reached up and touched Lena's cheek, one finger tracing the line of her jaw, the way she sometimes did when words failed her. *I trust you*, the gesture said. Lena closed her eyes briefly against it.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Don't trust me yet. Trust me when we're at the top."

* * *

The Ladder was worse than terrifying. It was a vertical gash in the mountain's flank, steps hacked by hands two thousand years dead, each tread barely wider than her boot, the inner wall slick with ice-melt and the outer edge open to a drop that the darkness made infinite. Lena climbed with the child strapped to her back in the blanket-and-rope harness she'd rigged at the base, the girl's arms locked around her throat, her breath hot and rapid against Lena's ear.

Halfway up, the child began to hum.

It was the cant — of course it was, the cant lived in the girl the way blood lived in her veins — but wrong. Slowed. Dragging, like a song played on a warped record, notes sliding flat and losing their places. Lena had spent weeks learning to hear that language, first as noise, then as pattern, finally as meaning, and what she heard now made her stomach clench. The seal wasn't just failing. It was *slurring*. A prayer said by someone falling asleep. A lock rusting open from the inside.

And with every slurred syllable, the child weighed more.

Lena noticed it gradually, the way one notices a fever — a heaviness here, a drag there — until, near the top of the Ladder, she paused on a ledge no wider than a coffin to haul herself over a fallen slab and nearly didn't make it. The girl on her back had become lead. Not flesh-and-blood weight; something denser, older, as though the child were slowly turning back into whatever she had been before the nine forgers gave her a body to hold the crown shut. Stone remembering it was stone.

"I can't—" Lena gasped, hanging by her fingers, boots scrabbling for purchase. "I can't carry you if you get heavier. Do you understand? You have to help me. You have to hold yourself together."

The humming stopped. For a moment there was only wind and the abyss breathing beneath them.

Then, deliberately, the weight lessened. Not all at once — the child couldn't manage that, Lena understood that much — but in increments, like a drowning swimmer choosing, stroke by stroke, to keep her head above water. Lena hauled herself over the slab and lay on the ledge above, chest heaving, tears freezing in the corners of her eyes, and felt the small arms around her neck tremble with effort that had nothing to do with muscle.

"You're doing it," Lena whispered. "You're doing it. We're almost there."

Above them, past another hundred feet of broken stair, the sky had begun to pale. Dawn found them crawling onto the eastern shoulder of the mountain, into a landscape that stole what little breath Lena had left.

The forge road ran below them now, a pale scar switchbacking up the southern face — and it was full of soldiers. Even from this height she could see the wrongness of them: they moved too evenly, ranks perfect, torches held at identical angles, and where the dawn light touched their faces it found nothing to warm. Bound men. Debt-soldiers, she realized, her scholar's mind cataloguing even as her body shook with cold. Men whose arrears had been purchased by the crown, whose contracts hung around their necks like collars, marching upward toward the forge with the patient inevitability of water finding its level.

The Vessel knew where they were going. Of course it did. There was only one place this road ended.

But the soldiers were on the road, and Lena and the child were above it, and between them and the summit lay a stretch of high ground the maps marked with a single word in Brannoc's blunt hand: *FORGE*.

"Almost," Lena said again, and discovered she no longer believed in almost. Only in next. Next step. Next hour. Next breath. A scribe's arithmetic, one line at a time, until the ledger was full.

* * *

The last day, the mountain stopped pretending to be stone.

It began at noon, when they crossed a field of black glass — fused sand, fused rock, a whole hillside melted and frozen mid-flow, vitrified by heat that must have come from the earth itself. The child went rigid on Lena's back and began to shake, and when Lena turned to look at her, she saw the girl staring at the glass with an expression Lena had never seen on her face before.

Recognition. And grief.

"This is where they made it," Lena said softly. "Isn't it. This is where they burned the mountain."

The child nodded once, a small, terrible motion, and pressed her face against Lena's shoulder.

They walked the glass field in silence. Underfoot, deep in the black, Lena could see shapes — half-melted tools, the skeleton of an ore-cart, and once, unmistakably, a hand. Nine forgers, the child had drawn in the ruined shrine. Nine names struck from every record. Nine people who had stood where Lena now stood and built the thing that ate them. She found herself walking carefully around the larger shapes, the way one walks around graves, though every step here was on a grave.

By late afternoon they reached the ruins of the forge town itself: a cluster of roofless houses clinging to a shelf of rock, walls built of the same black glass, windows empty and eyeless. Wind moaned through them in a dozen registers, and Lena — exhausted, hypothermic, her nerves worn to threads — heard voices in it before she caught herself and named the sound for what it was. Just wind. Just a dead town singing its one dead song.

Except.

Except that the child lifted her head, and listened, and answered.

One note. Clear and thin and utterly human, rising above the wind, and the wind *stopped*. All of it, everywhere, at once, as though the mountain had been holding its breath for two thousand years and had just been reminded how.

In the silence, far below and behind them, a horn sounded. Then another. Then a third, deeper, wronger, a sound like a bronze bell dragged across iron, and Lena knew without looking that the soldiers had found the Ladder's top, or the glass field, or some other thread of the trail she'd thought she'd cut. It didn't matter which. What mattered was the arithmetic: a few hours of lead, no more. Maybe less.

She looked at the child. The child looked back, and in the fading light her edges flickered again — solid, translucent, solid — a lantern running out of oil.

"How much longer can you hold?" Lena asked, and hated the question even as she asked it, because it was a debt-collector's question, a ledger's question, and this was a child.

The girl considered. Then she raised one small hand and held her thumb and forefinger a hair's width apart.

*This much.*

Lena nodded, and shouldered the satchel higher, and looked up at the last stretch of the climb: a final ridge, black against a bruised violet sky, and beyond it — she could feel it now, a warmth in the rock under her palms, a faint pulse like a sleeping heartbeat — the forge itself.

Behind them, the horns sounded again, closer.

"Then we don't stop," she said. "Whatever happens, we don't stop."

She took the first step. The child's arms tightened around her neck. And the mountain, breathing its slow cold breath, let them climb.

* * *

They summited the ridge as the last light died, and Lena stopped so abruptly that the child's chin bumped her shoulder.

Below them, cupped in the mountain's crown like a coal in a blackened hand, lay the forge.

It was vast — a crater, really, walled with slag and ancient masonry, and at its center squatted the great furnace itself, a dome of black iron bigger than the Saltspire's chapel, its throat open to the sky. Two thousand years cold, and yet as Lena descended the outer stair toward it she felt heat rising to meet her, faint and impossible, as though the fire had never fully died. As though it had been waiting. Around the furnace's rim, carved deep into the iron in letters taller than a man, ran a single line of text.

Lena knew it at a glance. She had spent weeks with fragments of this script, piecing it together from forbidden archives and a child's recitations, and here it was whole, monumental, the original from which everything else was a broken copy.

She read it aloud, her voice small in the enormous dark, translating as she went because translation was the only prayer she knew:

"*Let the debt be carried, and the carrier be consumed.*"

The child slid down from her back — lighter now, or perhaps simply past caring — and stood before the great iron door set into the furnace's flank. From her collar she drew out the thing she had carried since the night she arrived at Lena's door in the rain: the scrap of royal seal cloth, the fragment of the crown itself, crusted and ancient and humming faintly against the mountain's pulse.

She held it up. The cloth trembled. The door did not open.

Because doors, Lena thought suddenly, cold flooding through her colder than the wind — doors were not how it worked. She remembered the shrine, the ninth name struck through, the tenth line waiting blank. She remembered the cant's grammar, which she had puzzled over for nights: verbs of giving, of taking-on, of *assuming*. Not force. Never force. The crown had never been forced on anyone in all its hungry history.

"It has to be offered," she whispered. "Freely. Someone has to—"

The horn sounded again, and this time it came from above. From the ridge they had just crossed. Lena spun, and saw them silhouetted against the stars: the vanguard of the bound soldiers, thirty strong, beginning their descent into the crater with the smooth, tireless stride of things that did not need to sleep. And behind them, at the ridge's crest, something else — a shape in a king's cloak, moving with a grace no tired body should own, its shadow lagging half a second behind it even here, even now, even in starlight.

The Vessel had come to collect.

The child grabbed Lena's hand and pulled her toward the iron door, toward the heat, toward the blank tenth line waiting inside — and Lena, heart hammering the oldest rhythm in her body, went.
