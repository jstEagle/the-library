## 18. The Tenth Name

The forge had been dead longer than the kingdom had been alive.

Lena understood that the moment they crossed its threshold — not with her eyes, which saw only a cavern of blackened stone and rusted iron, but with something older in her, some scribe's instinct for what a place had been. This had been a heart once. She could feel the absence of it the way one felt the absence of a tooth: a constant, aching wrongness where heat should have been.

The great hearth dominated the eastern wall, wide enough to roast an ox whole, its chimney a throat of stone that climbed up through the mountain's body toward some distant vent. The workbenches were still there, their surfaces scarred by decades of hammers and tongs. Racks of tools hung on pegs, each tool fused to its hook by centuries of rust, so that the wall looked less like a workshop than like a reliquary — instruments of creation preserved in their own decay. Along the far wall ran nine iron chairs, simple and brutal in design, arranged in a crescent before the cold hearth like mourners at a funeral no one had attended.

And above the hearth, carved into the stone itself in letters deep enough to hold shadow even now, was the crown.

Not a drawing of it. The thing itself — or the idea of it, pressed into rock the way a seal presses into wax. Nine interlocking bands, each band etched with script Lena recognized from the child's recitations, from the archive fragments, from the ruined shrine where she had first seen the nine names struck through. The carving was vast, taller than two men standing, and though the forge was cold, the air around the carving seemed to shimmer faintly, as if the stone remembered being hot and could not quite forget.

The child walked to the center of the chamber and stopped, and her silence — which had been Lena's constant companion for weeks, more familiar now than speech — took on a different quality. It was not the silence of someone who cannot speak. It was the silence of someone arriving.

"We made it," Lena said. Her voice came out cracked, scraped raw by cold air and smoke and the things she had seen on the road. "Whatever this is. We're here."

The child did not turn. She stood facing the carved crown with her small hands open at her sides, and Lena watched her shoulders rise and fall with breaths that were too slow, too deliberate, the breathing of someone conserving something precious. Since the pass, since the river, since the night the child had spoken *Lena* in a voice like a bell cracking ice, the girl had been fading. Not ill, exactly. More like a lamp running low on oil — still burning, but the light thinner each day, the flame smaller, guttering when the wind of the world blew against it.

The seal is failing faster now. She had known it in her bones for three days. Every hour the child dimmed, and every hour, somewhere behind them down the mountain, the Vessel's bound soldiers climbed.

Lena shrugged off her pack and let it fall. Her knees protested; her back had been a single knot of pain since the pilgrim's road. None of it mattered. She crossed the chamber to the child and knelt beside her, following her gaze up to the carved crown, and only then noticed what she should have noticed at the threshold.

Beneath the carving, cut into a shelf of smooth stone at the height of a kneeling person's chest, were ten lines of text.

Nine names, each one struck through with a single deep chisel-blow — she knew them now, had learned them in the ruined shrine while the child traced them with a finger and her eyes went ancient and grief-stricken. And beneath the ninth, the tenth line: blank. A rectangle of untouched stone, smoothed and prepared, waiting as it had waited since before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather drew his first breath.

"You've brought me here to show me something," Lena said quietly. "Haven't you."

The child turned. In the grey light that filtered down through the mountain's veins, her face was hollowed, her eyes enormous, and there was something in them that Lena had never seen before — not the old weight, not the childlike trust that had grown between them over fires and frozen rivers and shared bread. This was apology. Deep and terrible and already given, the way one apologized for weather, for winter, for things set in motion long ago.

The child raised one hand and touched the blank line.

Then she began to speak.

---

She had never spoken this much at once. The cant poured out of her in a low, steady stream, and Lena — who had spent weeks piecing together fragments, matching sounds to archive scraps, building meaning the way a mason builds an arch one stone at a time — felt the language open beneath her like a door swinging wide. Whether it was because they stood in the place where the cant was born, or because the child was pouring everything she had left into these words, Lena couldn't say. It didn't matter. She understood.

*The crown was forged to bind a hunger,* the child said, in the old tongue that was older than hunger's name. *Nine made it. Nine fed it. Each forger gave what the binding required, and the binding required everything — name, years, self. When each was consumed, his name was struck from the record, for a consumed name must not be spoken or written, lest the hunger taste it and follow the thread back to the world.*

*But a binding made by force is imperfect. Force frays. So the ninth forger, wiser and more afraid than his brothers, carved a flaw into his own work: a tenth line, left empty. A door built into the lock.*

*The crown can be unbound — but only from outside, and only freely. No captive can break it. No slave can speak the words. The hunger holds all who are bound, all who owe, all who are owned; their debts are chains it wears as jewelry. Only a free hand can reach the lock.*

*And the price is the same price the nine paid. Whoever speaks the unbinding-cant and signs the tenth name assumes the bearer's debt. All of it. Every year the hunger has worn a king. Every debt it has devoured, every obligation it has twisted, every promise broken in its name — all of it transfers, entire, to the one who signs. The signer becomes owed-to and owing both, a ledger with a heartbeat, until the debt is paid in full.*

*No one knows how long that takes. No one has ever signed.*

The stream of cant slowed. Thinned. The child swayed, and Lena caught her, pulled her close, felt how light she had become — a bird's weight, a bundle of sticks wrapped in wool. The girl pressed her face into Lena's shoulder and spoke the last of it directly against her collarbone, barely a whisper:

*The tenth line waits for a free hand. It has always waited. I was made to keep it waiting. That is my whole purpose, and I have kept it, and kept it, and kept it—*

Her voice broke. Not into tears. Into silence — the true silence, the seal-silence, and Lena held her and felt the child trembling and understood, finally and completely, why the girl had come to her door.

Not to the temple. Not to the palace. To a debt-bound scribe in the Saltspire slums, because a debt-bound scribe was the last person in the world who might understand what freedom cost, and the first person who might recognize it when she saw it.

Except Lena wasn't free. That was the cruelty of it, the exquisite joke of it. She was owned body and ledger by the temple. Her debt was written in their books in her own copied hand. She had fled rather than be sold. By every law of crown and church, she was the least free woman in the nine kingdoms.

But the cant didn't say *free by law.* Lena read the old words again in her mind, turning them over, testing them the way she tested a suspect contract for forgery. *Only a free hand.* Free meant unowned. Unclaimed. Owing nothing to anyone.

And she had spent her whole life learning that debts were paper, and paper could burn.

---

She laid the child down on one of the iron chairs, wrapped in both their cloaks, and walked the length of the forge alone.

It was habit, this pacing, this reading of a room. She had done it in the scriptorium a thousand times — measuring exits, counting risks, weighing what she could afford to lose. Old habits. But the arithmetic was different here, and she knew it before she finished her first circuit.

Behind her, down the mountain, the Vessel was climbing. She had heard the horns at dawn, thin and wrong, carried up the switchbacks on the wind. Bound soldiers did not tire, did not eat, did not stop. They would arrive by nightfall. Perhaps sooner. There was no second road off this peak; Brannoc had told her that much truth before Hale's men took him. The forge was the end of every path, hers included.

So the choice was not whether to act. The choice was only what to be when the acting came.

She stopped before the hearth and looked up at the carved crown, and let herself feel the full shape of what the child had told her.

Her name. She thought of it the way one probes a bruise. *Lena Vane.* Written in the temple's ledgers in ink she had mixed herself. Written in forged travel writs scattered across half the mountains, the signature that had bought strangers their lives. Written — she realized with a start — nowhere else at all. No mother living to speak it. No father's grave bearing it. She had guarded that name jealously her whole life precisely because it was all she had, the one possession no collector could seize, and now she stood in a dead forge considering giving it away to a piece of stone.

Her freedom. What freedom? She laughed aloud, and the sound bounced strangely off the cold iron. The temple owned her. The crown wanted her head. Hale — kind, terrible Hale, who had opened the crypt gate and signed his own death warrant with a signet ring — had purchased her passage with his ruin. She had less freedom than any debtor she'd ever ferried to safety. On paper, she was the one person in the world disqualified from that blank line.

On paper.

But the cant was older than paper. Older than ledgers, older than temples, older than the lie that a person could be owned at all. And Lena had spent years forging documents, and she knew — she had always known, deep in the scribe's part of her soul where all the dangerous truths lived — that ownership was just a story everyone agreed to tell. The temple's claim on her was ink. The crown's claim was fear. Neither of them had ever held anything of hers except what she consented, out of exhaustion and terror, to surrender.

What did she actually owe? She turned the question over like a coin of doubtful minting. Her debt to the temple was a fiction compounded annually. Her debt to the refugees whose kindness had cost them — that was real, and it sat in her chest like swallowed glass, and no unbinding would erase it, and she would carry it regardless, gladly, because that was what carrying other people's wounds meant. Her debt to the child—

Ah. There it was. The true ledger, the one no provost had ever entered.

The child had come to her door with nothing, and Lena had taken her in. Everything since — the roads, the river, the ruined shrine, the cell beneath the temple, the long gray climb — everything had been payment on a debt Lena had incurred the moment she'd barred that door against the storm. And the final installment was waiting for her, carved in stone, patient as only very old things are patient.

A free person. Willingly.

She looked at her hands. Scribe's hands, ink-stained, the right middle finger calloused into a permanent ridge from twenty years of pens. Forger's hands. They had written lies that freed people; they could write one truth that would free everyone.

*I could refuse,* she thought. *I could take the child and run — where? There is no where. I could hide in the deep tunnels and wait for the soldiers to find us, and the seal fails, and the Vessel walks out of the king wearing the world like a coat. Or I could stand here and do the one thing no one else can do, and pay for it with everything I am.*

*Everything I am.* She tested that phrase too, honestly, because she had promised herself honesty in this cold place. It might mean death. The nine forgers had died — that much the shrine drawings had shown plainly, nine figures diminishing into nine graves. Or it might mean something worse and stranger: years, decades, a lifetime of service to a debt so vast it had eaten kings. It might mean becoming a creature of obligation, hollowed out, her self ground down grain by grain until nothing remained but the paying.

Or — and this was the thought that stopped her pacing, that turned her back to face the child sleeping in the iron chair — or it might mean exactly what her life had already been, only named at last. She had been spending herself for strangers since the first forged writ. She had never once regretted it. The temple had taught her that a person was a sum of what she owed. Fine. Then let her choose what she owed. Let her write her own contract, in her own hand, for the first time in her life — and sign it knowing, fully knowing, what the terms were.

That was the difference. That was everything. The nine forgers had been consumed by a thing they feared. She would not be consumed. She would *spend* — deliberately, knowingly, the way she had spent sleepless nights and stolen ink and every scrap of cunning she possessed. A debt assumed freely was not a chain. It was a promise. And a promise, kept, became part of you the way a scar did: proof of what you had survived choosing.

The child stirred. Opened her eyes. Found Lena looking at her across the cold length of the forge, and in the girl's ancient, exhausted gaze there was no plea. She had delivered her message. She would not beg. She had kept her seal for longer than kingdoms, and she would keep it to the end if the free hand never came.

That, more than anything, decided it. Not the stakes, not the soldiers, not even the shattering of crowns. The dignity of it. The child asking nothing, offering everything, trusting Lena to be who Lena had already chosen to be at a doorway in the rain.

"All right," Lena said softly. Her voice didn't shake. She was almost proud of that. "All right, little one. Show me how it's done."

The child sat up. And smiled — the first true smile Lena had ever seen on her face, small and blazing, like a coal remembering fire.

Then, beyond the walls of the forge, down the black throat of the mountain, the horns sounded again — close now, very close, and beneath them came a new sound: footsteps. Hundreds of them. Perfectly synchronized. Climbing.
