## 1. The Charm Seller's Quiet

The morning fog came off the Greywater in grey ribbons, and Ada Vane watched it crawl up Saltmarket Lane from behind her counter, counting the ways it could ruin her.

It dampened the chalk sigils she'd drawn on her doorstep — harmless ones, decorative ones, the kind any licensed charm-seller might use to advertise her trade without actually practicing anything worth arresting. It swelled the door of her shop so it stuck in its frame, which was good, because a sticking door meant an extra three seconds of warning if anyone official ever came through it. And it muffled sound, which was best of all. In Greyhollow, muffled sound was a currency more reliable than coin.

She had opened the shutters at dawn, as she did every morning, and arranged her wares with the precision of a woman defusing something: luck-charms braided from river-grass and gull feather, weather-knots that were honestly just knots, love-tokens cut from red glass that had never been anything but glass. On the shelf behind her, in a jar labeled *PICKLED EGGS*, she kept the real inventory — a dozen small charms worked with genuine intent, each one wrapped in wool so thick it couldn't whisper to itself, let alone to anyone else.

Fifteen years, and no one had ever looked inside the jar.

The bell over her door rang at half past eight, and Marta Cull came in smelling of herring and rain, the way she did every third morning.

"Vane." Marta set her basket on the counter with a thump that rattled the glass love-tokens. "The nets came up light again."

"The nets come up light every autumn."

"These came up *insulted*." Marta leaned across the counter, lowering her voice to the register Greyhollow citizens used for confessions and jokes about the Crown — the same register, really. "Old Toma says his grandfather used to buy a charm off a woman on this street, back before the ban, and his boat never once came home empty. I'm not saying I believe it. I'm saying my daughter's boots have holes in them and the fish aren't cooperating."

Ada reached under the counter and brought out the charm she'd finished at midnight: a flat disc of grey river-clay, fired hard, stamped with a coiled fish and threaded on a leather cord. There was nothing in it that any detection charm would flag. No sigil-work, no spoken binding, none of the things that got people dragged to the Spire and never seen again. What it had was three hours of Ada holding it in both hands and thinking, with the particular stubbornness she applied to everything, *full nets, full nets, safe water, full nets.*

Intent wasn't illegal. Intent couldn't be proven. That was the whole architecture of her life.

"River-clay," she said. "From the bend below the mill. Fish like that clay; ask any eel."

"Eels are liars," said Marta, but she took the charm and turned it over in her broad, cracked fingers. "How much?"

"For you? Four coppers."

"For me it should be two."

"For you it's four because your husband owes me three from the dice night he pretends didn't happen, and this way nobody has to be embarrassed."

Marta laughed — a great honking laugh that made the pickled-egg jar hum faintly against its shelf, and Ada's hand moved to still it before she'd even decided to move. Marta paid. Marta always paid. She hung the charm around her neck right there in the shop, tucked it into her shirt against her skin, and paused at the door with an expression that flickered between embarrassment and something softer.

"You're a good sort, Vane. For a woman who sells river mud."

"Get out of my shop, Cull."

The bell rang. The fog swallowed her. Ada stood a moment with her palm flat against the jar, feeling the small warm tickle of the charms settling, like birds rearranging themselves on a roost.

*Full nets,* she thought at the door Marta had gone through. *Safe water.* She didn't let herself think it too loudly. Even thinking had a shape, and shapes could be noticed by the wrong things.

* * *

The market inspector came at eleven, because Inspector Hobb believed in schedules the way other men believed in gods.

He was a wide man gone soft at the edges, with a waxed mustache and a ledger he carried like a weapon, and he entered Ada's shop the way he entered every shop on Saltmarket Lane: slowly, ostentatiously reading the license nailed beside the door, letting everyone on the street see him do it.

"Mistress Vane." He removed his hat, which was the most threatening thing about him. "Routine inspection."

"It's Tuesday, Inspector. Your routine inspections come Thursdays."

"My routine inspections come when the Crown's coin pays for them, and the Crown's coin has been generous lately." He drifted along her shelves, trailing one gloved finger an inch above the wares without touching, a performance of care that fooled no one. "New stock?"

"Same stock. Clay doesn't change much."

"Hm." He picked up a weather-knot, held it to the light, and put it down with the delicacy of a man handling evidence. "You know what I found in the Tannery Quarter last week? A woman selling *actual* workings. Little warming-stones. Sweet little things, kept your tea hot for an hour." His mustache twitched. "She's in the Spire cells now. Tea's probably cold."

"How tragic for her tea."

"There are those who'd say a charm-seller such as yourself — unlicensed gift or no, we can't prove anything, can we — might be wise to report neighbors who deal in the real article. Might find the inspectors remember such helpfulness."

Ada leaned on her counter and smiled the smile she'd spent fifteen years perfecting: pleasant, vacant, and utterly without purchase. "Inspector, if I knew anything worth reporting, I'd be too busy spending the reward money to chat with you. As it is, I sell river mud to fishermen and knots to grandmothers, and my most exciting secret is that I water down the vinegar."

Hobb studied her for a long moment. Behind the officiousness there was something tired — she'd seen it before, in men who enforced laws they hadn't written and couldn't quite explain — but tiredness wasn't mercy, and she wasn't fool enough to confuse them.

"Thursday, then," he said, putting his hat back on.

"I'll count the minutes."

He left. Through the window she watched him proceed down the lane at his ceremonial pace, and only when he'd turned the corner did she let out the breath she'd been holding since half past eight. Her hands, she noticed, were steady. Fifteen years of practice would do that. Her heart was another matter; it was doing something arrhythmic and undignified against her ribs, and she told it, silently, to get a grip.

This was the shape of safety in Greyhollow: insults traded with the instrument of your destruction, twice a week, forever. A life built like her shop — small, orderly, every dangerous thing wrapped in wool and hidden in a jar. It fit her exactly. That was the problem with it. It fit her exactly the way a coffin fits.

* * *

Home was three streets and a staircase away: a crooked room above a ropewalk, with a roof that leaked in philosophically interesting patterns and a window that looked out on nothing but chimney smoke and a slice of sky. Ada climbed to it as the light went amber, carrying a paper of fried fish and a bag of scraps, and before she'd gotten the key fully turned, the door was already open a paw's width.

"You're late," said a voice from the darkness, gravelly and imperious. "The big gray one sat on my spot. Again. I have *told* him about my spot."

"Good evening to you too, Soot."

She pushed into the room and the menagerie assembled itself around her with the practiced efficiency of a crew that had done this a thousand times: three cats, a limping pigeon, a hedgehog with opinions, and a raven named Quillfeather who lived on the windowsill and pretended he was nobody's. The big gray cat — a dock stray named Barnacle — removed himself from the cushion by the stove with tremendous dignity, radiating the grievance of the unjustly displaced.

Soot was the smallest of the cats and the largest of their personalities: black as spilled ink, missing his left ear to some old fight he refused to discuss, with a notch in the remaining one that gave him the look of a permanent skeptic. He wound once around Ada's ankles, accepted his share of fish, and then sat back on his haunches and fixed her with his yellow stare.

"You smell of fear," he said.

"I smell of fried fish."

"Under the fish. Under the inspector-smell, which is always on you after Tuesdays, like cheap pomade." His tail curled around his feet. "Fear. Old fear. You've been carrying it all day, and today it's heavier."

Ada sat down on the floor among them, because chairs were for company and these were family, and broke the fish into portions. For a while there was only the sound of eating and the tick of the cooling stove and the pigeon's idiotic contented cooing from the rafters.

"Fifteen years, Soot," she said finally. It wasn't the first time she'd said it. It was becoming a liturgy. "Fifteen years of being careful. Of wrapping everything in wool. Do you know what I did today? I sold a woman a lump of clay and lied to a fat man about vinegar. And that was a *good* day. That was a triumph of a day."

"You saved the fishmonger's boat," Soot said. "And the boat has a daughter with holes in her boots. And the fat man left with nothing. Sounds like a good day to me. I don't see the complaint."

"The complaint is that this is all there is." She scratched behind his one ear, and he permitted it, leaning into her hand with his eyes slitted. "I have — " she lowered her voice, though the walls here were hers, though the only ears were friendly ones — "I have a gift that could talk the rats out of the sewers and the gulls off the fishing grounds and maybe, maybe talk sense into things that have never heard sense. And I use it to tell pigeons not to eat the soap. I hide in a jar, Soot. I'm the safest thing in this city and the most wasted."

From the windowsill, a low croak. Quillfeather shuffled his wings and delivered his nightly contribution: "*Nevermore,*" he said, which was the only phrase he'd ever learned from anywhere, and deployed it, as always, with devastating timing.

"Yes, thank you, Quillfeather. Very comforting."

"He means you chose this," Soot translated, yawning. "Ravens are smug, but he's not wrong. You could run. You could go east, past the marshes, where they say the old magic still lives in the open air. Every year you could. Every year you stay."

Every year she stayed. Because the strays needed feeding, and the fishmonger needed her clay, and because Greyhollow was cruel but it was a cruelty she knew the shape of, and because — she admitted this only in the dark, only to herself — hiding had become a kind of skill, and she was proud of it, and pride is the most comfortable cage there is. She was good at being invisible. Being visible would mean being someone new, and she was thirty-four years old and tired, and the coffin fit.

The stove ticked. The fog pressed at the window. One by one the animals settled — Barnacle in the disputed spot, which Soot allowed tonight as a diplomatic concession; the hedgehog in his box; the pigeon under the rafters; Quillfeather with his head under his wing, muttering *nevermore* like a man rehearsing an argument he intended to win tomorrow.

Ada banked the fire and lay down on her narrow bed, and sleep was just reaching for her when Soot spoke again from the foot of the bed, softly, in the tone he saved for things that mattered.

"Something's coming."

She opened her eyes. The cat was sitting upright, both ears — ear, the one ear — swiveled toward the window, toward the stairs, toward the city below.

"The little ones have been chattering all evening. The mice in the walls, the rats in the drains. They've been running scared since sundown, all through the lanes, like a flood's coming." His yellow eyes caught the last ember-light. "But it isn't water, Ada. Water they'd say *water*. This they only say *running*, running, running, and one word underneath it they keep repeating, and I don't like the word."

"What word?"

Soot looked at her for a long moment.

"*Blood,*" he said.

Down in the lanes, far off, a dog began to bark — not the idle barking of dogs at shadows, but the flat, continuous alarm of a dog that has found something at a doorstep and will not stop. It was very close. It was getting closer. And beneath it, faint but rising, came another sound that Ada had heard only twice in fifteen years and had prayed never to hear again:

Whistles. Sigil-hunters' whistles, cutting through the fog like knives through wool.
