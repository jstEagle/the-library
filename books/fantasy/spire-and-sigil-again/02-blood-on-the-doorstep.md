## 2. Blood on the Doorstep

The knock came at midnight, and it was not a knock at all.

Ada Vane woke to the sound of a body sliding down her shop door — a long, wet scrape of cloth and something heavier beneath it, ending in a thud that she felt through the floorboards of her room above the Charm Seller's Quiet. She lay still in the dark, one hand already curled around the iron poker beside her bed, and listened.

Greyhollow at midnight had its own grammar. The fish-carts creaking home before dawn. The watch's boots on Cobbler's Row, unhurried, bored. Once or twice a month, the whistles of the sigil-hunters, sharp as broken glass, and after those, silence — the particular silence of a street holding its breath. Fifteen years in this city had taught her to read all of it the way other people read weather.

This was none of those sounds. This was a man dying against her door.

She should have stayed in bed. She knew that even then, even as her bare feet found the cold boards. The first law of hiding was simple: hear nothing, see nothing, owe nothing. Fifteen years she had kept that law. Fifteen years of luck-charms and love-knots and little brass bells blessed by no one, sold to fishmongers and grandmothers who never once looked too closely at the woman behind the counter. Fifteen years of being nobody, which is its own kind of magic, and the only kind Greyhollow permitted.

Her feet took the stairs anyway.

* * *

The shop was dark except for the moon through the front window, laying a pale stripe across the counter where she'd left a half-finished charm of river-stone and red thread. The door shuddered when she touched it — a weight on the other side, breathing.

"Who's there?" she whispered. Foolish question. Whoever it was couldn't answer, or wouldn't.

She unbarred the door an inch. It pushed against her, heavy, and then the night spilled in along with the smell — blood, mostly, and under it the mineral stink of the Spire district, that cold stone-and-ink scent that clung to anyone who climbed the palace hill. A man folded against her doorframe like wet laundry. He was young, or had been; the blood made him ageless. His courier's coat was grey wool gone black at the shoulder, and his left hand was pressed flat against his stomach as if he were holding himself together out of habit rather than hope.

His eyes found hers. They were very clear, which was worse than if they'd been glazed. Clear eyes meant he knew exactly where he was and what was happening to him.

"You're—" He coughed, and the cough cost him something he could not afford. "The charm-seller. Vane."

"I'm closed," Ada said, because terror always speaks in nonsense.

He laughed. It turned into another cough, and this time she saw the dark bloom of it between his fingers. "I know." His free hand came up — slow, trembling, deliberate, the way a man moves when he has rehearsed this moment and knows he may only get one attempt — and pressed something into her palm. Wax-sealed. Flat. Heavy for its size. "Take it."

Her fingers closed around it before her mind agreed to anything. That was the thing about the irrevocable moments, she would think later: they never announce themselves. They feel exactly like every other moment until they don't.

"What is it?"

"A map." His head tipped back against the doorframe. Above them, the moon sat fat and low over the rooftops, one night off from full. "Beneath the Spire. You have to— someone has to—"

"Someone has to what?" She glanced past him into the street. Empty. Cobbler's Row lay silver and still, shutters closed, chimneys breathing thin smoke into the cold. Too still. Even the strays were absent from their usual corners, and the strays never missed a meal or a spectacle. "You need a physicker. There's one on Tanner's Lane, I can—"

"No." The word came out with force that seemed impossible in him, and his hand shot out and caught her wrist. His grip was ice and iron. "Listen to me. There isn't time. There was never going to be time. *Thirteenth turn.*"

The words dropped into her like stones into a well. She waited for the splash and heard nothing.

"I don't know what that means."

"You will." His eyes held hers, and there was something in them she recognized from the animals she'd spoken to all her life without meaning to — that absolute, unembarrassed clarity creatures have about death. He knew. He wasn't asking her to save him. He was asking her to carry something. "The binding fails or holds on the thirteenth turn. Thirteen years, thirteen moons. If it holds—" His breath hitched. "Everything the king built dies with him. Everything. And no one left alive knows the way down but me, and I'm—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the ruin of him, almost apologetic, "—not going to make it to morning."

Whistles split the night.

Far off — two streets, maybe three — but unmistakable. The rising-falling cry of sigil-hunter signals, one whistle answering another, weaving a net of sound over the district. Ada's whole body went cold. In fifteen years she had heard that music perhaps six times, and each time it had passed her by like a storm passing a house whose owner had paid his debts.

They were not passing tonight. The whistles were coming closer.

"They're tracking me," the courier said. Not afraid. Resigned, the way one is resigned to weather. "Blood-scent. They'll have my trail from the bridge."

Ada stood in her doorway with a dead man's errand in her fist and a dying man bleeding on her step, and every instinct she had honed over fifteen years screamed the same single syllable: *no*. No, close the door, bar it, put the wax-sealed thing down on the step and walk away, let the hunters find him and let whatever he carried die with him, because that was the price of survival in Greyhollow and she had paid it gladly for fifteen years, she had paid it every single day—

"Inside," she heard herself say.

* * *

Getting him inside was ugly. He could stand, barely, if she bore most of his weight, and she walked him backward through the shop, past the counter, past the shelves of harmless trinkets, into the back room where she kept her stores and her cot and the small high window that faced the alley. Every footstep left a print. She saw them appearing behind them on the boards — dark, then darker — and some cold practical part of her mind began cataloguing: the rug by the hearth, the sand bucket, the lye under the sink.

"In here. Down. Gently — *gently*, you great fool, you'll open it again—"

He collapsed onto the cot more than lay upon it. She knelt, pulled his hand away from his stomach, and immediately wished she hadn't. The wound was bad in the way wounds are bad when they've been packed with moss and prayer and nothing else. Someone had tried to keep him alive long enough to travel. Someone had failed.

"Physicker," she said again. "Tanner's Lane. If I run—"

"If you run, they take you." He caught her sleeve. "They're not hunting me, charm-seller. They're hunting what I carry. Anyone near me is near it." He smiled, and it was a terrible, sweet, exhausted smile. "You should have left me on the step."

"Yes," Ada said. "I know."

Outside, the whistles had stopped. That was worse. Whistles meant searching. Silence meant they'd narrowed it down.

The courier's breathing had gone shallow and fast. She watched the color leave his face in real time, draining toward his center like water finding its level. His lips moved.

"What? What is it?"

"The map." His eyes were losing their terrible clarity, fogging now, and he fought it with visible effort. "Wax seal. Don't break it until— until you find the crew. Gullwing. On the *Kestrel's Debt*, moored at—"

The knock came again.

Three blows on the shop's front door, measured, patient, official. Then a voice, smooth as oiled leather: "Sigil-hunters. Open in the name of the Crown."

Ada did not move. The courier's hand found hers and squeezed once — *go* — and she rose, heart slamming, and did the only thing her fifteen years of practice allowed her to do well: she became nobody.

She lit the lamp. She smoothed her nightdress into something resembling a robe. She arranged her face into the mild, put-upon expression of a tradeswoman woken at an indecent hour, and she opened the front door on its chain.

Two hunters stood in the moonlight. The tall one wore the Crown's sigil worked in silver thread at his collar — a spire crossed by a key — and his eyes were the pale, patient gray of a fisherman watching a line. Behind them, at the corner of Cobbler's Row, a third figure held a leashed shape that was not quite a dog and not quite anything else, its nostrils working the air.

"Apologies for the hour, mistress." The tall one smiled without warmth. "A runner came this way. Wounded. Have you seen him?"

"Seen him?" Ada let irritation do the work fear would betray. "It's midnight. I was asleep. The only thing that's come down Cobbler's Row tonight is the rain."

"There was no rain."

"Then the wind and my imagination. Good night."

The hunter didn't move. His gaze drifted past her, into the shop's dim interior, over the counter, the shelves, the striped moonlight on the floorboards. Ada stood in the gap and let him look, because refusing the look would be worse than anything he might see. The back room door was shut. The rug in the back room was over the worst of it. The sand bucket was right there by the hearth, if it came to that, if it came to scrubbing and lying and burying a stranger in the marshes before dawn — and the thought arrived fully formed, uninvited, horrifying in its competence: *she was already planning how to hide a corpse.*

Fifteen years, and this was what she'd become. A woman with a plan for bodies.

"Mistress." The hunter's voice softened, which somehow made it worse. "Harboring a fugitive carries the same sentence as fugitive. You understand. The ban is the ban."

"I sell luck-charms," Ada said. "Brass bells and knotted thread. Whatever trouble you're chasing, it's bigger than my shop."

"Most things are." He studied her a moment longer — the pale eyes doing their patient arithmetic — and then stepped back. "Lock your door, mistress. There are dangerous people abroad."

The door closed. Through it, muffled, she heard the tall one speak to the third figure: "Lost it at the bridge. Sweep toward the water."

Boots receded. The not-dog whined once, a sound like a hinge, and was dragged away into the dark.

Ada stood with her forehead against the door until her legs remembered how to hold her.

* * *

He was dead by the time she got back to the cot.

Not dramatically. No last words, no final grasp. She had braced herself for a deathbed confession, instructions, a name to remember — and instead she found him simply finished, lying with his eyes half-open and his face eased into something almost peaceful, as though the whole dreadful business of carrying things had finally been set down. His hand was still curled loosely where hers had been.

Ada sat down hard on the floor beside the cot.

For a long while she did nothing at all. The lamp burned. The moon moved its stripe slowly across the floor. Somewhere in the walls, her house-mouse — a small opinionated creature named, in the privacy of Ada's own head, Minister — stirred and went still again, sensing the mood.

She had hidden a dying man from the Crown's hunters. She had lied to a sigil-hunter's face. She was sitting three feet from a corpse that would have to vanish before dawn, holding a wax-sealed map that some part of her desperately wanted to believe was a madman's fantasy — a smuggler's riddle, a debt-collection trick, anything but what he'd said it was.

*Beneath the Spire.*

*Thirteenth turn.*

Everyone in Greyhollow knew the story everyone told: thirteen years ago, King Aldric withdrew from public life. Grief, they said — the old queen's death had hollowed him, and the court spoke for him now, and the Spire's lights burned late, and the city learned to lower its voice. Thirteen years of a king seen only at a window, pale and distant, like a portrait that occasionally blinked. People whispered, early on. People stopped whispering. That was how Greyhollow survived: it swallowed its questions whole and called the swallowing peace.

And now a dying courier had bled on her doorstep whispering about bindings and turns of the moon, and the sigil-hunters had swept her street for the first time in years, and the arithmetic of it all pointed one direction whether she liked it or not.

"No," she said aloud, to the corpse, to the map, to the moon. "No, absolutely not. I'm a charm-seller. I sell *bells*."

The corpse declined to argue.

She got up because staying down was a luxury. Practicality took her hand by the wrist the way the courier had: the sand, the lye, the rug, the alley door. She worked in silence, methodically, hating herself a little more with each efficient motion, and somewhere in the middle of it — kneeling on the boards with her sleeves rolled, her hands ruined and reeking — she started to laugh. Quietly. The awful, airless laugh of a woman recognizing the exact shape of her life.

Because here was the truth underneath it, the one she'd been stepping around for fifteen years: she had never once used her gift to help anyone. She'd used it to talk cats off fences and calm frightened horses and learn which fishmonger was honest, small domestic magics, invisible ones, safe ones. Fifteen years of hiding, and the sum total of her courage was a well-fed menagerie.

And tonight, the first time the choice had ever truly mattered — a man bleeding out, hunters at the door, the Crown itself on the other side of her wall — she had chosen. Not the safe thing. Not the smart thing. She had opened the door.

Whatever happened next, that door did not close again. She understood that with the same certainty she understood the moon would rise tomorrow. The hunters had seen her face. The courier had died in her keeping. The map was warm in her pocket like a second heartbeat, and there was no version of the coming days in which she set it down and returned to selling bells to grandmothers.

She washed her hands. She wrapped the courier gently in the canvas she used for furniture deliveries, and she did not let herself think about how practiced the motions felt. At the alley door she paused, looking back at the little shop — the counter, the charms, the narrow stair to her narrow room, the whole small architecture of a life built precisely to fit inside the law.

"Thirteenth turn," she said softly, testing the words. They still meant nothing. But they weighed something. Everything heavy does.

Behind her, in the dark of the rafters, wings shifted — the raven that had haunted her eaves since autumn, silent as judgment, watching her with one black eye that caught the lamplight.

"Don't start," Ada told it.

The raven tilted its head, and said, in its cracked, perfect imitation of a dead man's voice:

"*Thirteenth turn.*"

Ada Vane stood in the doorway of everything she had spent fifteen years building, and felt it begin, very quietly, to fall.
