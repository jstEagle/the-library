## 3. What the Rats Know

The dead man had not grown lighter in the night. If anything, he seemed heavier, as though the dark itself had settled into his bones and made a home there.

Ada stood over him in her cellar at first light, a candle in one hand and no idea in her head worth having. She'd laid him out on the flagstones beneath the trapdoor to her shop because she could think of nowhere else, and now the cold of the stones was doing what cold does—slowly, patiently claiming him for its own. His blood had dried to a brown lace across the boards where she'd dragged him down the stairs. She would have to scrub that before the fishmonger's boy came for his second charm. Blood on the floor of a charm-seller's cellar was the kind of detail that got neighbors talking, and neighbors talking was the kind of thing that got sigil-hunters knocking.

"Thirteenth turn," she said aloud, testing the words. They meant nothing. They were the mutterings of a dying man, and dying men said all manner of things—names of wives they hadn't spoken to in decades, debts, prayers to gods who'd stopped listening. It didn't have to be a message. It didn't have to be hers.

But the map was still in her apron pocket, and it weighed like a stone from a riverbed, smooth and dense and patient.

She crouched beside the body and did the last kindness she could: closed his eyes with two fingers, straightened his collar, folded his hands over the hole in his chest so it looked less like violence and more like rest. His coat was good wool, city-made, but the boots were Spire-issue—she knew the make because everyone in Greyhollow knew the make, the way you knew the shape of the gallows without ever wanting to look directly at it. Black leather, double-stitched, stamped on the inner heel with a sigil she couldn't read and didn't need to. A courier's boots. A courier who ran messages for the palace, dead on the doorstep of a woman whose entire life was built on never being noticed by the palace.

"You picked a fine door," she told him quietly. "Of every door in Greyhollow."

He didn't answer. Dead men rarely did. But something else might.

* * *

She ate nothing. She filled the kettle and let it go cold. Twice she caught herself standing at the top of the cellar stairs with her hand on the latch, and twice she turned back and busied herself with the shop—the jars, the dried lavender, the little tin charms that were ninety percent superstition and ten percent genuine hedge-craft, diluted past the point of detection. That was the trick of her trade. Real enough to work, faint enough to pass under a hunter's charm-glass. Fifteen years of practice at being almost-nothing.

By midmorning she had run out of small tasks to hide behind, and the map was still in her pocket, and the dead man was still in her cellar, and the whistles she kept hearing in her memory were still whistling.

So she went down into the dark, knelt on the cold flags, and did the thing she had sworn, fifteen years ago, over her mother's grave, never to do again in Greyhollow.

She opened the gift.

It was like unclenching a fist she'd been holding shut so long the fingers had gone stiff. The world came flooding in—not sight, not sound, but *presence*, the low constant murmur of living things, which most people heard as silence and Ada heard as a crowded room. The mice in the walls went quiet when she reached. The spiders in the joists pulled their threads in tight. And below, down through the foundations, down through the old clay and brickwork of Greyhollow's buried veins, the rats stirred.

They always came when called. That was the trouble with rats—they had no pride, and no fear of anything but hunger and hawks, and a voice in their heads that smelled of bread and kindness was neither.

The first one came up through the gap in the foundation stone where the mortar had crumbled, a big brown male with a torn ear and the wary confidence of a creature that had survived four winters in a city that hated it. Two more followed, then five more, arranging themselves along the wall in a loose crescent, whiskers working, eyes like wet beads catching the candlelight.

"I need to know about a man," Ada said. Her voice came out rough. She hadn't used it for this in years, and it felt strange in her throat, like a language half-forgotten. "He came down my street last night, running. From the direction of the canal bridge. He was bleeding."

*Blood,* said the big rat, and the word arrived in her mind the way rats thought everything—flat, immediate, edged with appetite. *We smelled it. Old Tom smelled it first. Blood and boot-leather and fear.*

"Fear," Ada repeated. "Whose?"

*His.* A younger rat, quick and nervous, spoke up from the edge of the crescent. *Fear running downhill. Fear coming off him like heat off a forge. He ran past our wall and we pressed flat because fear that big pulls other things after it.*

Ada's mouth went dry. "Other things. What things?"

Silence among the rats. Not the empty silence of creatures who didn't know, but the held silence of creatures who did, and wished they didn't. The big one groomed its paw twice, three times, an obsessive little motion, then set it down.

*The hunters' dogs,* it said finally. *And behind the dogs, farther back, slower, something else. Something the dogs wouldn't go near.*

"What do you mean, wouldn't go near?"

*Dogs are simple,* the young one said. *Dogs smell a thing and chase a thing. But this thing—* it paused, whiskers trembling *—this thing smelled wrong. Smelled like nothing. Like a hole shaped like a smell. The dogs put their tails down and looked back at their handlers, and the handlers kicked them, and the dogs still wouldn't go.*

Ada sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something slow and heavy against her ribs, like a fist knocking on a door from inside.

"The man," she said, steering hard. "Where did he come from? Before the bridge. Before my street. Did any of you see?"

*We know the route,* the big rat said simply. *We know all the routes. He came down the Spire hill, through the servants' gate, along the drainage run under Tanner's Row. We were in the drainage run. We saw him come out of the pipe that comes down from inside.*

"From inside." The words tasted like iron. "Inside the Spire."

*Inside-inside,* the young rat confirmed. *Deep. Below the kitchens, below the cellars, below the places men go. There is a place down there where even we don't nest. Too cold. Not winter-cold. Another cold.*

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"Tell me about the cold," Ada said.

The rats shifted. The crescent tightened. For a moment none of them spoke, and Ada understood, with a lurch, that she was watching animals be afraid—and that animals were afraid of very few things, because fear was a luxury the small could rarely afford.

*There is a room,* the big rat said at last, slowly, choosing each thought like a creature crossing ice. *Below the throne floors. Our mothers' mothers' mothers knew it, and they taught us: do not den there, do not eat there, do not die there if you can help it. In that room there is a thing that wears a warm shape.*

"A warm shape," Ada echoed.

*A man-shape. A king-shape.* The rat's black eyes didn't blink; rats couldn't, quite. *It walks the halls sometimes, at night, when the palace sleeps. It has a face and hands and a voice, and everything about it is right except one thing. It wears a warm shape, but it smells of cold. Cold like deep water. Cold like the underside of a stone that hasn't seen sun since the city was built. And when it passes, we feel it here—* and the sensation it sent her was not a word at all but a feeling, a hollowing, a pulling-back, the way a burrow feels when a shadow crosses its entrance *—here, where our lives live.*

Ada became aware that her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees.

"The king," she said. "The man on the spire. You've smelled him too. Is it him?"

Another long pause. When the answer came, it came from the oldest rat present, a grizzled female who had not spoken until now, gray-muzzled, missing half a tail, sitting apart from the others with the stillness of great age.

*Once,* she said. *Years ago. Thirteen years ago, near enough. The true man smelled of ink and horses and honey-wine, and once—he was young then, and kind, or kind enough—he fed us crumbs from his own plate in the garden, and laughed when we took them from his fingers. I remember his smell. My daughters remember his smell. We taught it to our children, the smell of the kind one, in case he was ever lost and needed finding.*

"And now?" Ada whispered. "Do you smell him now?"

*Now,* the old rat said, *there is only the warm shape that smells of cold. And once, last winter, passing below the sealed door in the deep place, I smelled—faint, so faint, like a thread pulled through thirteen years of stone—I smelled ink and horses and honey-wine.*

The cellar was very quiet. The candle burned straight up, a thin gold needle stitching dark to dark.

"He's alive," Ada said. Her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she hated that, and didn't take it back. "Down there. Behind a sealed door. Alive."

*Alive,* the old rat agreed. *Or something near it. The cold thing keeps him the way a spider keeps a wrapped fly. Why, we don't know. What for, we don't know. But the moon is counting, charm-woman. Even we mark the moon. Thirteen turns, the deep ones say—the things older than us, the pale things in the deepest pipes. On the thirteenth turn, the bargain finishes. Whatever was traded stays traded. Forever is a long time to be a wrapped fly.*

*Thirteenth turn.* The dead man's whisper, and now the rats', and the word *bargain* hanging in the cold air between them like breath in winter.

Ada closed her eyes. She saw the map in her mind's eye, the wax seal cracked by her own thumb, the inked lines threading down and down beneath the city toward a small marked chamber beneath the spire's root. She saw the courier's folded hands. She heard the whistles.

When she opened them, the rats were waiting. All of them, still, patient, their small bodies arranged around her like a congregation.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You know what I am. You know what it costs me to ask. Why answer?"

The old rat groomed her ruined tail, once, unhurried.

*Because you asked,* she said. *No one asks. Men shout commands at dogs and beat horses and poison us, but no one asks. You asked what we know instead of telling us what to know. That is a rare thing, charm-woman. Rare things should be repaid with rare things.* She rose, arched her spine, and fixed Ada with those wet black eyes. *One more gift, then. The hunters came back at dawn. They walked your street. They stopped at your door.*

Ada's blood went to ice water.

*They stood there a long time,* the old rat continued. *The tall one touched your doorstep. He left a mark on the underside of the lintel—a sigil, small, hidden, the kind that wakes when magic passes near. It is sleeping now. But it is there. Waiting.*

"They know," Ada breathed. "Not who. Not yet. But they know something happened here."

*Something happened here,* the rats agreed, all together, a chorus of small voices. *Something happened here, and the moon is turning, and the cold thing below is counting.*

Then they poured away down through the crack in the foundation stone, all but the old one, who lingered a moment longer.

*The kind one fed us once,* she said. *Debts matter, even small ones. Especially small ones. Find him, charm-woman. Or don't. But decide quickly. The moon doesn't wait for the frightened.*

And she was gone, and the gift snapped shut behind her like a door, and Ada knelt alone in the cellar with a corpse and a candle and a heartbeat that would not slow down.

* * *

She climbed the stairs. She checked the lintel—there it was, a smudge of charcoal-gray no bigger than a thumbnail, tucked into the grain of the wood, invisible unless you knew to look. A tripwire made of law. One flare of undiluted magic under this roof and it would wake, and sing, and the hunters would come down her street like wolves on a scent.

She could burn the map. She could wrap the dead man in sacking, row him out to the deep channel at night, give him to the water, scrub the floor, scatter salt and ash, and go back to selling luck to fishmongers. Fifteen years of careful, suffocating, precious hiding, preserved. That was the sensible thing. That was the thing her mother, rest her stubborn soul, would have begged her to do.

Ada stood in her shop with the map in her hands and the morning light falling through the window in dusty bars, and she thought about a king who had once fed crumbs to rats because it cost him nothing and made him laugh. Thirteen years wrapped in the dark while something cold wore his warm shape and smiled at his daughter and signed his name. Thirteenth turn. Twelve moons left, maybe fewer—she didn't know when the count had started, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

If she did nothing, she would survive. She had gotten very good at surviving. It was the only thing she'd gotten good at, if she was honest, and the honesty of that thought was the worst part—that all her quiet comfort was just fear wearing an apron and keeping shop hours.

The raven shifted on its perch by the window, feathers rustling, one bright eye fixed on her with the terrible attention ravens reserved for moments of decision.

"Don't look at me like that," Ada said.

*Like what?* said the raven, which was another story entirely, and one she'd pay for someday.

"Like you already know what I'm going to do."

*I do know,* the raven said, preening. *I've known since you brought him downstairs. You have the face of a woman about to ruin her life. It's a good face. Very dramatic.*

Ada looked down at the map. Beneath the spire's root, the little marked chamber. Beneath the ink, whatever the courier had died carrying. Beneath everything, a man who smelled of ink and horses and honey-wine, alive because no one had bothered to finish him, kept because someone—or something—needed him kept.

Bramble Quill owed her. Bramble Quill hated owing her. Which meant Bramble Quill would listen, briefly, furiously, with a knife somewhere in reach—and Bramble Quill could read a map drawn in cipher, because half the smugglers in Greyhollow had learned their letters in the same stolen school, and because the seal on the courier's wax—

Ada turned the map over. Looked at the impression in the broken wax again, properly this time, for the first time. A gull, wings spread, over a crossed quill and key.

She knew that seal. Everyone who'd ever run cargo down the Siltway knew that seal.

"Bramble," she said aloud, and the word came out steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than the whistles had.

Outside, the market bells rang noon. Somewhere on her street, a sigil slept under a lintel, waiting for her to make a mistake. Somewhere beneath the city, the moon was counting down. And Ada Vane, charm-seller, beast-speaker, coward of fifteen years' standing, rolled the map into a tube, hid it in the false bottom of her herb basket, and reached for her coat.

The raven hopped onto her shoulder.

*About time,* it said.
