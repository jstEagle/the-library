## 4. The Gullwing Crew

The map had not left Ada's hands since midnight. She had washed the courier's blood from her palms twice, and still she felt it there, tacky and warm, a stain that lived in the skin rather than on it. By dawn she had wrapped the thing in oilcloth and buried it in the false bottom of her flour barrel, which was, like most of her hiding places, designed for things smaller than guilt.

By noon she could no longer stand the distance between herself and it. She took it out, unwrapped it, and looked at it again in the grey light that came through her shutters: the Spire rendered in careful ink, the cisterns beneath it drawn in a hand that had known them intimately, and at the corner, pressed into wax gone brown with age, a seal she did not recognize—a gull with a broken wing, wings spread over a crescent moon.

She recognized the *shape* of the seal's owner, though. Everyone in Greyhollow's underside knew the Gullwing mark. It meant one person, and that person owed Ada Vane a debt of eleven years' standing, and debts were the only currency in this city that never devalued.

She put on her plainest coat, told the cat to guard the shop—"guard" meaning sleep in the doorway looking inconvenienced—and went down to the Saltquay.

* * *

Bramble Quill kept her office above a chandlery that smelled of tallow and old rope, up a staircase so narrow that two people could only pass each other by becoming briefly intimate. The door at the top bore no name, only the gull-and-crescent burned into the wood, and beneath it, freshly chalked: **NO CROWN BUSINESS. NO PRIESTS. NO EXCEPTIONS.**

Ada knocked anyway.

"Read the sign," came a voice like gravel rolled in honey.

"I'm not crown business."

A pause. "Priests lie worse than captains. State your trade."

"Luck-charms. Mostly fraudulent."

Another pause, longer. Then the door opened on a chain, and one grey eye regarded Ada through the gap—the other side of the face lost to an old burn that pulled the skin into something between a sneer and a smile. "The charm-witch of Candlemaker's Row. You've got nerve, coming here the day after a man died on your step."

Ada's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "You've heard."

"The whole Saltquay's heard. Sigil-hunters walked the Row at first light asking after you." The eye narrowed. "They say you gave the dead man nothing. They say he forced himself on you. They also say you're lying, because everyone says that about everyone." The chain rattled off. "Get in before someone sees you standing there looking guilty. You look guilty standing still; it's a gift."

The office was small and dense as a ship's cabin—charts pinned three deep on every wall, a desk built from a hatch cover, and a window overlooking the grey slap of the harbor where a lean black sloop rode at anchor, her rails hung with fishing nets that had clearly never caught a fish. Bramble Quill was shorter than her reputation, broad through the shoulders, fifty-odd years old, with hands like knotted rope and hair cropped to grey stubble. She poured two cups of something dark without asking and pushed one across.

"Eleven years," she said. "That's what you're here to collect."

"You remember."

"I remember everything I owe. It's the only ledger I keep clean." She sat, and the chair groaned its familiar complaint. "You pulled my cook out of a plague-lock when the city would have burned him. Cost you your stock, your reputation with the Guild of Charms, and near your neck. I said: name your day, and I'll pay it. You said: not yet." Bramble drank. "So. Is it yet?"

Ada set the oilcloth bundle on the hatch-cover desk and unwrapped it.

Bramble looked at the map for a long moment. Then she set down her cup very carefully, the way people do when their hands have become unreliable and they'd rather die than show it.

"Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me. He came from the Spire."

"That's not what I asked." Her voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. "I asked where he got it. Because this seal—" her finger hovered over the wax, not quite touching, "—is my brother's. And my brother has been dead or worse for two years, and I have paid four separate liars to tell me otherwise, and every one of them showed me a different forgery. So before I answer you at all, charm-witch, you'll tell me true: is this his hand?"

Ada looked at the map again—at the confident strokes of the cistern channels, the little annotations in a cramped script along the margins. "I don't know his hand. But I know a dying man's grip, and whoever drew this held it while they bled."

Bramble stood. She crossed to the wall of charts, and from behind the third layer she took down a scrap of paper, yellowed, creased soft with handling. She laid it beside the map. Two drawings of the same gull, same broken wing, same crescent. Two hands, and Ada saw it before Bramble said anything: the same hand.

"Wren Quill," Bramble said. "My brother. Smarter than me, braver than me, and half my common sense, which is saying something, because mine fits in a thimble." She laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "Two years ago he took a contract. High pay, no questions, route through the drowned cisterns under the Spire. He kissed my forehead, called me an old barnacle, and went down the flood-stair with five crew. None of them came back. The Crown said pirates took them. The Crown lies the way rivers run downhill."

"And now his map turns up in a dying courier's hands."

"Now his map turns up," Bramble agreed, "the night before sigil-hunters start sniffing down your street. Which means either the gods love you, Ada Vane, or they're setting a hook." She turned, and both eyes—one grey, one ruined—fixed on Ada with the flat weight of a captain weighing cargo. "I'll decode this. I'll sail you wherever it points. But understand what you're buying with your eleven years: I'm not doing this for you. I'm doing it because if there's one chance in ten thousand my brother's bones are under that Spire, I mean to bring them home and bury them in salt water where he can hear the gulls. Whatever we find down there—king, monster, Crown secrets—you carry your own weight and keep your own counsel. Are we understood?"

"Understood," Ada said. And then, because the truth was a coin she was tired of clutching: "There's more. The man who died said *thirteenth turn*. The binding under the Spire—if that's what this is—completes on the thirteenth turn of the moon. Twelve nights from now."

Bramble was silent. Outside, a bell buoy clanked its idiot rhythm against the swell.

"Twelve nights," she repeated. "To get into the most guarded structure in Greyhollow, through drowned tunnels sealed by royal sigil-work, past whatever killed my brother's crew, and do—what, exactly? What does the map actually open?"

"I don't know. That's why I need you."

"No." Bramble shook her head slowly. "That's why you need *us*. One charm-witch and one old smuggler don't breach a royal prison. You need a crew." She was already moving, pulling charts, counting under her breath. "Fen for the locks—he's a coward but he's a genius coward. Marisol for the water-work; she swam the cisterns as a child, before the ban, before they flooded the lower ways. Doss for muscle and silence, in whichever order the moment demands." She stopped, hand flat on the map, and looked up. "And you'll want to know what you're sailing with. My crew are thieves and liars and the best people I've ever known. They'll die for each other and steal from each other in the same hour. If that's not family enough for you, say so now."

Ada thought of her shop, her strays, her fifteen years of small safe hiding. She thought of a dead man's blood soaking through her doorstep, and a cat with one ear who would be waiting, unimpressed, when she got home.

"It sounds exactly like family," she said.

Bramble snorted—the first almost-laugh of the meeting—and thrust out a rope-hard hand. "Then welcome aboard the *Gullwing*, charm-witch. Debt collected, alliance struck, and may every god we don't believe in have mercy on us both."

Ada shook it. The handshake hurt, which felt honest.

"One condition," Bramble added, as Ada turned for the narrow stair. "Tonight. Full crew, my ship, everything on the table—including why a luck-charm seller with no history and no guild ties knows words like *binding* and *thirteenth turn*. You show us your cards, we show ours. Fair warning: Fen will cry. He cries at everything. Don't let it unsettle you."

"And if I hold something back?"

Bramble smiled, and the burn scar made it terrible and warm at once. "Then my brother's ghost will haunt you instead of me, and trust me, he was far more annoying alive. Moonrise. Pier nine. Come alone-looking—that means leave your shadows at home, all of them."

Ada paused on the top stair. "How did you know I have shadows?"

"Charm-witch." Bramble was already turning back to her charts. "I run contraband past the Crown for a living. You think I can't spot a woman smuggling something bigger than her coat?"

* * *

Pier nine at moonrise smelled of tar, dead fish, and the particular desperation of a city that banned magic and then wondered why nothing worked anymore. The *Gullwing* sat low in the black water, nets draped over her rails like a disguise that fooled no one, and on her deck waited four figures and a lantern.

Bramble made introductions with the economy of a woman who hated wasting words on ceremony. "Fen. Marisol. Doss. The charm-witch. Charm-witch, the crew. Now sit, all of you, before the harbor watch decides our nets are suspicious."

Fen was young and wiry and already, as promised, damp-eyed. Marisol was older, weathered to leather, with the stillness of someone who had spent a life reading water. Doss was enormous and silent and knitting something with needles far too small for his hands.

Ada laid the map on the deck boards, weighted at the corners with four cups of tea Marisol produced from nowhere. She told them everything: the courier, the blood, the whispered words, the rats in her walls, the Spire, the countdown. She watched their faces take it—Fen's fear, Marisol's narrowing eyes, Doss's needles pausing mid-stitch—and when she finished, the silence lasted long enough for the tide to turn.

It was Marisol who spoke first. "The lower cisterns aren't just flooded. They're *occupied*. Ask me how I know."

"You've been down there," Ada said.

"I grew up in the river districts, before the ban, when the water-speakers kept the floods off. When they purged the gift-havers, the cisterns went untended. Something moved in. The old families won't speak of it. They leave offerings at the flood-grates and pull their children away from the water." Marisol looked at Ada directly. "If your rats say something lives below the Spire wearing a warm shape, believe them. Rats are honest. It's people who lie."

"Then it's settled," Bramble said, rolling the map and tapping it against her palm like a gavel. "Twelve nights. We go in through the flood-stair Wren marked, follow his route, and find out what the Crown is keeping under its own house." She looked around the circle of faces—her strange, loyal, impossible crew, plus one charm-witch who carried secrets like ballast. "Questions?"

"One," said Doss, in a voice like a landslide deciding to be polite. He held up his knitting: a small, lopsided woolen thing that might have been a duck. "Does anyone else think it's strange that the map surfaced *now*? Two years dead, and it walks into a charm shop three streets from the Spire, twelve nights before the deadline?" His small dark eyes found Ada's. "Someone wanted you to have it, miss. Question is whether they wanted you helped or baited."

No one had an answer. The lantern hissed. Out in the harbor, the moon laid a silver road across the black water, pointing at the Spire the way roads point at places you cannot come back from unchanged.

"Both, probably," Ada said quietly. "But it doesn't matter. Whoever sent it, the clock's running." She looked up at the moon, one night into its final count. "We have twelve turns to find out what's buried under the crown of Greyhollow."

Above them, unseen in the rigging, a raven that had followed Ada from the market shifted its weight, and spoke one word into the dark:

"Soon."
