## 5. Sigil-Hunters at the Market

The trouble came on a Thursday, which Ada would later think was fitting—Thursdays were when she did her best selling, and fate had always had a taste for irony.

She was halfway through haggling with a woman who wanted a luck-charm for her son's first voyage when the bells began. Not the market bells that marked the hours, but the deep, bronze-throated bells of the Spire itself, rung only for one purpose in all of Greyhollow: a sigil-hunt sweep.

The market changed around her like a tide reversing. Conversations died mid-word. A fruit-seller's hand froze over his scale. Somewhere down the row, a shutter banged closed, and then another, and another, the sound rippling outward like the market itself was trying to fold into its own pockets and disappear.

"Three coppers," Ada said quietly, to the woman still standing at her stall.

"Is it true they test everyone?" The woman's knuckles were white around her coin purse. "Even—"

"Everyone," Ada said. "Three coppers. Take the charm, take your boy's luck, go home by the fish lane."

The woman paid without counting, pressed the charm into her bodice like a secret against her heart, and hurried away. Ada watched her go, then began doing what fifteen years of Thursdays had taught her: nothing. She straightened her jars of dried lavender. She aligned her bundles of river-reed and crow-feather. She became exactly what Greyhollow expected her to be—a charm-seller of the licensed sort, the harmless kind, the kind whose wares were superstition dressed up as trade. Luck-charms were legal. Luck-charms were *nothing*. That was the whole beauty of them; half the city wore one, and none of them worked, because none of them were meant to.

Hers worked. But no one needed to know that.

They came up Fishmonger's Row at a walk, unhurried, which was somehow worse than running. Six of them in slate-grey coats, faces masked below the eyes, each carrying a detection charm on a silver chain—a sliver of moon-glass that glowed faint blue near any working of unlicensed magic. Behind them came the sergeant.

Ada knew his name before she saw his face. Everyone in the lower market knew Corvane. He was the hunter who knocked before he broke doors, who returned confiscated goods when the charges didn't stick, who once carried an old woman's stolen goat three miles back to her yard on his own shoulders. He was also the hunter whose sweeps had put four people on the exile barges last spring, and mercy in small things meant nothing if you were the thing being hunted.

He stopped at the mouth of her row and lifted one gloved hand. His hunters fanned out.

"Citizens of Greyhollow." His voice carried without effort, flat and clear as a struck coin. "By order of the Crown, this market will be tested for unlawful workings. Remain at your stalls. Touch nothing. The process is brief."

*Brief.* Ada arranged her hands on her counter, loose and idle, and thought about the charm in the fishmonger's wife's bodice three streets away, and the sleeping draught she'd brewed last night for old Marta's cough—not magic, just herbs, but brewed with a whispered word of intent that no moon-glass could ever catch—and most of all she thought about the cat.

Barnaby was under her stall. She felt him there the way she always felt him, a warm knot of attention curled against her ankle, his thoughts a drowsy loop of *warm, hers, safe, the smell of lavender, hers.* Fifteen years of listening to animals had taught her that their minds were not lesser minds, only simpler ones—honest minds, without the terrible furniture humans built inside themselves. Barnaby loved her the way weather loved the sea. It was the one truth in her life she'd never had to hide from anyone, because no one could hear it but her.

The hunters moved down the row. Moon-glass swung. At the pie stall it stayed dark. At the rope-seller's, dark. An old man selling secondhand boots flinched so hard when the glass passed over him that a hunter paused, and Corvane said, "Move along," and the pause ended, and Ada let out a breath she hadn't known she'd trapped.

Then the glass flickered.

It was nothing—a stutter of pale light at the edge of the lens, gone so fast that even the hunter holding it frowned and shook the chain. But Corvane had turned his head. He had the stillness of a man who had spent twenty years watching for exactly that kind of nothing.

"Again," he said.

The hunter swept the glass across the pie stall again. Dark. Across the boot-seller. Dark. Across—

Across the space in front of Ada's stall, where Barnaby had just slipped out from under the canvas into the open, low-bellied and silent, moving toward the fish waste pile behind the gutting tables.

No. No, no, *no.*

"Barnaby," Ada said, sweet and casual, "here, kitty." She held out a hand as if calling any market cat, and every eye on the row turned to her, and she smiled the smile of a woman mildly annoyed by a pest. "Sorry, sergeant. Stray. Been stealing my dried herring all week."

Corvane looked at her. His eyes were grey and patient and missed nothing, and Ada understood with cold clarity that he was filing her face away somewhere behind them, the way a careful man files everything.

The glass passed over Barnaby.

It flared.

Not a flicker this time—a bloom, blue-white, bright enough to throw shadows, and the hunter holding it shouted, "Contact!" and the whole machinery of the sweep pivoted like a compass needle finding north, and every hunter on the row was suddenly looking at a one-eared orange cat sitting by a pile of fish heads, washing his paw.

"It's an animal," someone said. "Glass catches beasts sometimes. Wild things carry a little of the old—"

"It caught something," Corvane said. "Net."

A hunter unslung a weighted net from his belt. Barnaby, who understood tone if not words, who had spent his whole life reading the weather of human voices, went still. Ada felt his mind turn toward her, a question shaped like a feeling: *the loud ones are angry, is it the game, do we run?*

*Run,* she thought at him, uselessly, the way she'd thought it a thousand times at a thousand creatures, knowing her gift carried only when she spoke aloud or touched skin or fur, and there were six hunters between her and her cat.

Barnaby ran anyway—but the wrong way. Whether it was panic or pride or some private calculus of a cat's heart, he bolted not for the alley behind her stall but straight through the gap between two hunters, a streak of orange fur, and a hunter grabbed for him and caught tail instead of body, and Barnaby yowled—the full, tearing yowl of a hurt animal—and twisted, and the knife-edge of the net's weight caught his hind leg as it dropped, and he went down screaming in the tangle.

"Hold it still," the hunter said, drawing a short blade. "We'll need it for questioning. Beast-speech leaves traces in the blood. We can find out who's been talking to it."

Ada did not decide. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that haunted the shape of every day after: there was no decision, no weighing, no voice in her head saying *fifteen years, Ada, fifteen years of hiding, are you going to spend them on a cat?* There was only Barnaby screaming, and her body already moving, dropping to her knees in the filth of the gutter beside him, her hands finding the torn net and the hot wet ruin of his leg, and her mouth opening, and the words coming out in the voice she used for no living person:

*"Easy. Easy, love. I've got you. I've got you."*

The market went silent the way a forest goes silent.

She felt it happen—felt the words leave her carrying what they always carried, that current beneath the sound, the thing that wasn't language but rode on language, the gift her mother had pressed into her hands at nine years old with the instruction *never, never, never.* She felt it land in Barnaby, felt his screaming ease into trembling, felt his mind open to her fully, pain and confusion and underneath it all, steady as bedrock: *you came. You always come.*

"I know," she said aloud, to the cat, in front of forty witnesses and six hunters and the sergeant with the patient grey eyes. "I'm here. Hold still, love. Let me see the leg."

Her hands worked the net away from the wound. A gash, deep but clean, bleeding freely. She tore a strip from her own sleeve, bound it tight, and Barnaby leaned into her chest and shuddered and began, horribly, to purr.

When she looked up, the point of a moon-glass pendant hung six inches from her face, glowing like a captive star.

* * *

They made her stand. They made her hold out her hands. One hunter bound her wrists with cord while another kept the glass trained on her, and it burned steadily now, blue-white, undeniable, drinking from whatever welled up in her that had never once been allowed to show itself in daylight.

"Name," said the hunter with the ledger.

"Ada Vane. I keep the charm stall. Licensed. My papers are—"

"We know your papers, Mistress Vane." Corvane had come around the stall and stood before her, close enough that she could see the stitching on his mask, the faint scar crossing his exposed brow. He studied her the way she'd seen him study suspects: not with cruelty, but with a thoroughness that felt worse. "Fifteen years at this market. Fifteen years, and my glass has never once sung at your stall. Do you understand how remarkable that is?"

"Maybe I'm just very good at being innocent."

"Maybe." He crouched slightly, bringing his eyes level with hers. "Or maybe you're very good at something else. Which is it?"

Behind him, the market watched. Ada saw their faces—Marta's grandson with his mouth open, the rope-seller looking anywhere else, the fishmonger's wife come back up the street and frozen mid-step, her hand pressed over the pocket where a luck-charm bought from Ada's stall sat against her heart. They looked at Ada the way people look at a fire in a neighbor's window: with pity, and relief that it isn't theirs, and the beginning of the calculation about how far away the sparks might land.

Fifteen years invisible. And now every eye on the row had found her, and would never unfind her.

"The animal," Corvane said. "You spoke to it. It answered. That's beast-speech, and beast-speech is a working, and a working outside license is outlawry. You know the law better than I do, I'd wager."

"I know the law," Ada said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Inside, something enormous was breaking loose and falling through her, floor after floor after floor, fifteen years of careful architecture collapsing inward, and she stood in the rubble of it and kept her chin level. "I broke it. Write it down."

"Why?" It wasn't accusation. It was genuine, and that frightened her more than the blade had. "Fifteen years clean. Why throw it away for a stray?"

Ada looked down at Barnaby, cradled against her bound wrists, his one ear swiveled toward her, his good eye fixed on her face with absolute, uncomplicated trust. *You came. You always come.*

"Because it was hurting," she said. "And I could help. That's the whole of it, sergeant. That's the entire crime."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. The glass between them glowed and glowed. Then he straightened, and turned to the hunter with the ledger, and said, in the same flat coin-strike voice:

"Mark it. Unlicensed working, class three, first offense, voluntary confession. Standard handling."

"Standard handling is the barge, sergeant."

"Standard handling," Corvane repeated, "is whatever I write in that book. Class three, first offense, voluntary confession." He took the ledger himself, wrote three lines in a hand surprisingly fine for such a large man, and signed it. When he looked up, his expression had closed like a door. "You'll be summoned to the Hall of Records within the week. Until then, you're remanded to your premises. Someone will be watching your shop."

"And the cat?"

Corvane glanced down at Barnaby. Something moved behind the grey eyes—there and gone, quick as the glass's first flicker. "Animals aren't citizens. There's no charge that sticks to a cat." He nodded to the hunter holding the cord. "Cut her loose. She walks home on her own feet. That's more than the law owes her."

The cord fell. Ada gathered Barnaby against her chest—he weighed almost nothing, all bone and stubbornness—and stood, legs unsteady, in the middle of a silence that had a hundred people in it.

"Mistress Vane." Corvane was already turning away, signaling the sweep onward, the machinery of the morning grinding back to life around the hole she'd left in it. He spoke without looking back. "In fifteen years, I've tested this market eleven times. Eleven times, and nothing. Either you learned your trick yesterday—" he paused, and the pause had weight, "—or you've been the best-hidden thing in Greyhollow for a very long time. I haven't decided which worries me more."

He walked on. The hunters walked with him. The moon-glass swung and stayed dark over stall after stall after stall, and the market exhaled, and the shutters began to reopen one by one like eyes deciding it was safe to blink.

Ada stood alone in the aisle with a bleeding cat in her arms and her whole hidden life lying in the gutter around her, glittering, unmistakable, picked clean of shadow by the morning light.

*You always come,* Barnaby thought at her, drowsy with pain and certainty, and licked her wrist where the cord had been.

"Yes," she said aloud, because there was no longer any reason not to, and heard her own voice break on it. "I always come."

She walked home through streets that parted around her. Doors closed softly as she passed. A child was pulled back from a windowsill by a mother's quick hand. By the time she reached her own door, the news had outrun her—she could feel it in the way the lane held its breath, in the curtain that twitched and stilled—and she understood that by nightfall every ear in Greyhollow would have the story, growing stranger with each telling: the charm-seller, the quiet one, the one nobody ever suspected.

She got Barnaby onto her worktable, cleaned and stitched his leg while he complained magnificently, and fed him the good dried herring, the whole packet. Outside, the afternoon went on. Somewhere across the city, a summons was being written with her name in it. Somewhere closer, a watcher was taking up a post across the lane from her shop, and she could feel his attention like weather.

She lit her lamp early. She sat with the cat purring against her ribs, and she looked at the door she'd kept locked for fifteen years—the real door, the one inside—and found it standing open, and could not for the life of her imagine how to close it again.

That was when the knock came. Three soft raps, unhurried, at the front of the shop—from a lane where no customer had dared approach her since noon.

Through the window, in the last grey light, Ada saw the silhouette waiting on her step: broad-shouldered, cloaked, and wearing no mask at all.

Sergeant Corvane had come alone.
