## 6. Princess in Disguise

The palace had a way of teaching you to lie with your whole body.

Sable had learned it young, the way other children learned to skip rope. Chin level, shoulders loose, hands quiet. Smile like the world was a gift someone had wrapped for you personally. Thirteen years of practice, and she could walk through a room full of courtiers and leave no more impression than smoke.

Tonight she wore the impression of a servant girl from the Salt Quarter: grey wool dress, patched at the hem, hair braided flat and hidden under a knitted cap, a laundry basket on her hip that smelled convincingly of lye and other people's sweat. The disguise was Master Ellery's idea, back when she'd started asking questions he didn't want asked in daylight. *If you must dig, Princess, dig in the dark, and wear a face no one looks twice at.*

She had gotten very good at being no one.

The curfew bell rang from the Spire as she slipped out through the scullery gate — nine tolls, deep and bronze, rolling over Greyhollow like a hand pressing down on the city. After the ninth toll, anyone abroad without a sigil-pass could be taken. Sable carried three forged passes in different names and trusted none of them. What she trusted was the fact that nobody stopped a girl carrying laundry. Servants were furniture. Furniture moved freely after dark, because palaces did not clean themselves.

She turned down Tanner's Row and let the city swallow her.

* * *

Thirteen years of silence. That was what she called it, privately, in the ledger she kept locked inside her own skull where no astrologer or chamberlain could read it. Thirteen years since her father had last truly spoken to her — not the warm public voice, not the *my daughter, my joy* voice he deployed at ceremonies like a coin flipped to a beggar, but his real voice, the one that used to sing off-key in the stables and argue passionately about the correct way to gut a fish.

One morning when she was eight years old, King Aldric had kissed her forehead before breakfast and said, *You have your mother's stubbornness, little storm. Guard it well.*

And by supper he was someone else.

Not visibly. That was the horror of it, the thing she could never say aloud to anyone because it sounded like madness. Her father still smiled. Still held audiences. Still signed decrees in his own careful hand. But something behind his eyes had been replaced with something older, something patient, the way a portrait of a man is patient. And the first edict signed by that new patience had been the Ban — magic outlawed, mages hunted, sigil-hunters raised up within the season.

Sable had spent thirteen years collecting small wrongnesses the way other girls collected ribbons. The way her father no longer hummed. The way he'd given away his favorite hound without a flicker of regret — the hound had howled for a week, and the king had watched it go with an expression Sable could only describe as *relief*. The way the royal astrologer, old Vess, had begun keeping a private count of lunar turns and gone grey as ash the night each turn completed, muttering about *the thirteenth* whenever he thought himself alone.

Twelve turns remained. She'd heard him whisper it through the observatory door last month, and the number had lodged in her chest like a fishhook.

Tonight she meant to pull the thread a little further. There was a woman in the Lamplight Market who sold charms — luck-knots, weather-bones, small illegal comforts — and according to the gossip Sable paid her maid to gather, that same woman had been seen talking to rats. Talking *with* them, the gossips said, the way you'd talk with a neighbor. If any soul in Greyhollow knew what moved beneath the Spire, if any soul could hear what the walls themselves had witnessed these thirteen years, it would be a beast-speaker.

And beast-speakers, since the Ban, did not come to princesses. Princesses went to them.

* * *

The Lamplight Market after curfew was a different country. By day it roared; by night it breathed. Lanterns guttered in their brackets, moths ticking against the glass. Sable moved through the shuttered stalls with her basket and her borrowed face, following the maid's directions — past the fishmonger's, past the fountain with the chipped mermaid, to a narrow shop wedged between a cobbler and a chandler, its sign reading THE CHARM SELLER'S QUIET in paint so faded it was nearly an apology.

A light burned behind the shutters.

Sable set down her basket, drew her hood lower, and knocked.

No answer. She tried the door. Unlocked.

Inside, the shop smelled of dried lavender and candlewax and something wilder underneath — river-mud, fur, rain. Shelves crowded every wall: bundles of herbs, jars of teeth and feathers, knots of colored thread that hummed faintly when Sable's sleeve brushed them. A back door stood open to the alley, swinging gently.

And on the counter, in a cage of woven willow, sat a raven.

It regarded her with one wet-black eye, head tilting with the slow deliberation of a judge. Then it opened its beak and spoke, in a voice like gravel poured over velvet:

"Too late, too late. She's gone to the water. Gone to ask the drowned things their business."

Sable froze. Speech from a bird was rare enough — trained ravens existed, though owning one was itself a gray crime under the Ban's broad language. But it wasn't the speech that stopped her heart. It was the cadence. The odd, formal tilt of it.

"Where did she go?" Sable heard herself ask. "The water? Which water?"

The raven ruffled its feathers, unimpressed by her authority. "Gone to the cistern-gate. Gone to ask the rats who walked the dead man's road." Its eye fixed on her, and something in its gaze made the hairs on her arms rise. "You smell of the Spire, crow-girl. You smell of cold stone and older lies."

"I don't know what you—"

"You do." The raven hopped along its perch. "But lying is a kind of clothing too, isn't it? You wear yours well."

Sable's hand went to the knife at her belt before she remembered she was supposed to be a laundry girl, and laundry girls did not carry palace steel. She forced her hand back down. "I'm looking for the charm seller. Ada Vane."

"Gone to the water," the raven repeated, and then, softer, almost sly: "But you could wait. Waiting is free. Everything else in this shop costs."

* * *

She waited. It was, as advertised, free.

Sable told herself she was conducting an investigation. She told herself she was gathering intelligence on a suspected mage, that this was duty, that when the woman returned she would place her under arrest and bring her to the Spire for questioning and thereby take one real step toward the truth instead of thirteen years of circling it.

She told herself all of this while she examined the shop like a woman reading a letter she wasn't supposed to see.

There was warmth here. That was the first thing. Fifteen years, the gossips said, the charm seller had kept this shop, and fifteen years of careful, hidden living showed in every corner — nothing valuable displayed, nothing incriminating kept, everything arranged to look smaller than it was. A life built like a hiding place. And yet: three mismatched bowls of water by the hearth for strays. A shelf of children's carved animals, worn smooth by small hands that couldn't possibly belong to the charm seller herself. A cot in the back room with a blanket that had been mended so many times it was more patch than cloth, and beside it, a single luxury — a jar of honeyed plums, half-eaten, kept like treasure.

Someone lived here who had decided long ago to want very little, and then wanted those little things fiercely.

The raven watched her catalog it all. "You're memorizing," it observed. "For the report?"

"For nothing," Sable said.

"Liar," the raven said comfortably, and began preening.

The bell above the door — which Sable had locked behind her, she was certain of it — chimed.

Sable spun, knife drawn now, consequences be damned, and found herself facing a woman in the doorway: mid-twenties perhaps, mud to the knees, hair escaping its braid in damp ropes, a smudge of something dark on her cheekbone. Her eyes went from the knife to Sable's face to the raven's cage, and something in them closed like a shutter.

"Well," the woman said quietly. "Either you're a thief, or you're worse."

"Ada Vane?" Sable kept the knife low, nonthreatening in geometry only. "You're under suspicion of practicing forbidden arts. I'm placing you under arrest. Come quietly and—"

"And what?" Ada Vane did not move toward her or away. She stood in her own doorway dripping river water, and there was no fear in her face at all — only a tired, sharp attention, like a fox that has heard hunters before. "You're a laundry girl with a nice knife and a guard's grammar. Where's your escort? Where's your writ? Curfew arrests come in pairs, everyone knows that. You came alone."

Sable's grip tightened. The woman was right, and they both knew it, and knowing it changed the shape of the room.

"Who are you?" Ada asked. Not *what are you doing here* — *who*. As if the second question mattered more.

"That's not your concern."

"It's everyone's concern, eventually." Ada stepped inside, closed the door, and leaned against it — casually, Sable noted, but with her weight on her back foot, ready. "You've got the Spire on you. Not the uniform — the stone. Marble dust in the creases of your collar. Court soap. And you walk like someone who's spent her life being watched, which means you either stole that walk from a noblewoman or you *are* one." Her gaze dropped to Sable's hands. "Calluses in the wrong places for laundry. Sword callus, near enough. So. Who are you?"

The silence stretched. Outside, somewhere in the maze of the market, a whistle sounded — thin, two notes, the sigil-hunters' night-call — and both of them flinched, and the flinch was its own strange kinship.

"I'm asking the questions," Sable said, but it came out thinner than intended.

"No," Ada said. "You're not. You came here alone, at night, in disguise, to a charm seller, because there's something you want to know that you can't ask anyone wearing a crown." Her eyes narrowed. "Or wearing anything at all."

Another whistle, closer. Boots on cobbles — more than one pair, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the night.

Ada crossed the room in three strides and snuffed the lamp. Darkness fell like a dropped curtain. Sable felt fingers close on her wrist — strong, work-roughened, startlingly gentle — and draw her behind the counter into a gap between shelving that smelled of dried nettle and old rain.

"Breathe slow," Ada whispered, barely audible. "They'll pass. They always pass."

"They're hunting *you*."

"They're hunting everyone. That's rather the point of them." A pause. In the dark, Sable could feel the woman's heartbeat through her grip, quick but steady. "Why did you really come here?"

And Sable — exhausted, pressed into darkness with a stranger's pulse against her palm, thirteen years of collected wrongnesses suddenly heavy as armor — heard herself answer honestly.

"My father stopped being my father thirteen years ago. I don't mean he died. I mean I've watched him every day since, and something behind his eyes is *counting*, and I need to know what the walls of the Spire have seen, and I'm told you can talk to—" 

The door burst open.

Lantern-light slashed across the shop. Two sigil-hunters filled the doorway, white cloaks bright as wounds, detection charms swinging from their fists — iron cages around captive motes of light that would flare red at the touch of active magic. The lead hunter's gaze swept the room.

"Charm seller's registered," he said to his partner. "Vane. Clean record, fifteen years. Check the back."

Boots. Drawers opening. Sable pressed deeper into the gap, Ada's body a shield before hers, and understood with surreal clarity that she — Princess Sable of Greyhollow, heir to the throne whose hunters these were — was about to be arrested in a smuggler's hiding spot by her own father's men, and that the scandal would solve nothing and save no one.

Then the raven screamed.

Not words — a sound like tearing metal, and it threw itself against the willow cage until the weave split, and burst into the air of the shop in a storm of black feathers. The hunters shouted. One swung his lantern; the bird banked off it, shrieking, and began to speak — loudly, obscenely, in a voice pitched to carry:

"MURDER! MURDER IN THE LAMPLIGHT MARKET! BLOOD ON THE COBBLES! COME AND SEE! COME AND SEE!"

"The bird's mad," the second hunter snapped. "Catch it before it wakes the quarter."

The raven swooped out the open door and into the night, still screaming murder, and the hunters — because a screaming raven crying blood in a market district was either nothing or something, and hunters were paid to care — plunged after it into the dark.

Silence rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

Ada exhaled. In the dark her voice was dry as paper. "He does that. Usually for bread."

"He saved us."

"He saved *himself*, probably. He hates cages." A pause. "We should go. They'll circle back."

"We?" Sable stepped out of the hiding gap. Her heart was slamming. "There is no *we*. I came to arrest you."

"You came to arrest me," Ada agreed, lighting the lamp again with hands that barely trembled, "alone, in disguise, after midnight, and you hid with me from your own hunters, and you told me about your father." She looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of river water over stone. "So here's my question, whoever-you-are. The raven. When he gets out, he repeats things. Things he's heard. He's been with me fifteen years, and most of what he carries is mine." She hesitated, and Sable saw — actually saw — the decision happen behind her eyes, a door opening onto a room the woman had never shown anyone. "But some of what he carries, he carried before me. From somewhere else. Say a phrase. Any phrase you'd know, if it belonged to the person you lost."

Sable stared at her. "That's nonsense. That's—"

"Is it?" Ada said softly. "You just told me your father is counting behind his eyes. Nonsense seems to be the currency tonight."

The whistles sounded again, farther off, circling as promised. There was no time, and Sable had no idea why she did it — except that she had spent thirteen years starving for one true sentence, and here was a stranger offering her a meal of pure chance.

"When I was eight," Sable said, "my father told me I had my mother's stubbornness. He called me *little storm*. No one else ever heard him say it. No one alive."

Ada looked at her for a long moment. Then she lifted two fingers to her lips and whistled — a low, rising note — and from the darkness outside the window, wings beat, and the raven flowed back into the shop and landed on the counter between them, feathers askew, one eye gleaming.

"Little storm," the raven said.

Sable's breath left her all at once.

"Guard it well," the raven continued, head cocking, voice sliding into a deeper register, a warmer one, a voice Sable had not heard in thirteen years and would have known in ten thousand — her father's voice, her *true* father's voice, singing off-key in the stables, arguing about fish, kissing her forehead before breakfast. "*You have your mother's stubbornness, little storm. Guard it well.*"

The shop tilted. Sable put a hand on the counter to keep the world steady. "Where," she whispered. "Where did he hear that. Where did you get that."

"From the Spire," Ada said. "From below the Spire. My raven flew the palace towers for years before he was mine — before that, he belonged to a courier who served the king directly. Whatever your father became thirteen years ago, whatever sits on the throne wearing his face—" she met Sable's eyes, and did not look away, and did not soften it, "—the man who said those words is still alive. Below. Bound. And there's a map that says how to reach him, and twelve turns of the moon left to do it."

Outside, the whistles sounded once more — closer now, deliberate, converging.

"So," Ada said, reaching for her cloak. "You can arrest me. Or you can tell me everything you know about what counts behind your father's eyes. Choose fast, little storm. They're coming back, and next time I won't have a bird to spend."
