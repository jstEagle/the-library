## 7. The Map Beneath the Floor

Bramble Quill had three rules aboard the *Gullwing*, and the first was that no one touched her charts.

So when Ada Vane laid the bloodstained map across her chart table without asking, Bramble's hand went to the knife at her hip out of pure reflex, and it stayed there while she looked down at what the woman had brought into her cabin.

The map was old. Not faded-old, not market-forgery-old — *old* in the way that meant someone had drawn it with care and meant it to last. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea, and the ink lines were fine as spider silk, rendered by a hand that understood both cartography and something else, something Bramble couldn't name until she tilted the map toward the lantern and saw that certain lines weren't ink at all. They were sigils, tiny ones, worked into the geography like stitches into a wound.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"A dying man put it in my hands at midnight." Ada said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. She had a face built for flatness — sharp cheekbones, a mouth that didn't waste itself on expression — but her eyes kept flicking to the door of the cabin, and her fingers drummed once against the table's edge before she caught herself and stilled them. "The sigil-hunters came for him. He was dead before I could do anything but hide the body."

"Hide the—" Bramble exhaled through her nose. "You hid a corpse."

"I hid a *courier*. The corpse part was already decided." Ada met her eyes then, and there was something underneath the flatness, something with teeth. "I need to know where this goes. You're the only one in Greyhollow who reads old water-marks, and you owe me for the Kessington run."

"I paid that debt."

"You paid half. Ask your own ledger if you don't believe me."

Bramble wanted to argue. Instead she looked back down at the map, because arguing was easier than admitting what she'd felt the moment the vellum hit her table — that cold drop behind the sternum, the one that arrived whenever a thing from the past came calling with its sleeves rolled up.

Because she knew the watermark. Of course she knew it. She'd spent two years learning every mark, every seal, every smug little flourish the Spire's chancery used, all in service of a search that had turned up nothing but her own exhaustion.

And there, in the lower corner, pressed into the wax that sealed the map's folded edge — cracked now, half-shattered, but unmistakable — was a signet ring's impression: a gull rising over three waves.

Her brother's seal.

* * *

She didn't tell Ada. Not yet. Not while the crew was still filtering into the cabin one by one, drawn by the smell of trouble the way gulls drew to offal.

Wick came first, as always, his small frame folding onto the bench beneath the porthole, his ever-present oil-lamp set on the floor between his boots like a pet. Then Marisol, who took one look at the map and started swearing in three languages, which was her version of saying good evening. Old Tam leaned in the doorway because his knees had opinions about benches, and last came Pim, sixteen and all elbows, who tried very hard to look like he belonged on a smuggling crew and mostly succeeded when no one looked directly at him.

"That's Spire work," Marisol said, jabbing a finger at the sigil-stitched lines. "That's *deep* Spire work. That's the kind of work that gets your name struck from the harbor rolls and your body never found."

"It's a route," Wick said quietly. He was always quiet; he saved his voice the way other men saved coin. His finger traced the main line of the map without touching it. "Starts at the old tannery sluice on Corvess Street. Goes down through the storm-drains here, then—"

"Then it stops being drains," Bramble finished.

Everyone looked at her.

She pulled the chart table's lamp closer and let herself see it properly, the way she hadn't let herself see anything properly since the courier's blood had come into her shop smelling of iron and panic. The map showed Greyhollow's underside — not the sewers the city admitted to, but the older network beneath them, the drowned cisterns of the pre-Imperial city, the ones the charter histories called *the King's Throat*. Waterways that had been sealed for two hundred years. Waterways that ran, if this map told true, directly beneath the royal Spire itself.

"There," she said, tapping a junction where three channels converged into a wide dark space the cartographer had left deliberately blank except for a single word in an archaic hand: *the Drowned Court.* "That's the First Cistern. And this line—" her finger followed a channel northward, climbing on the page though descending in truth, "—runs under the Spire foundations. There's a gate marked here. See the sigil-work around it?"

Marisol leaned in, and her swearing stopped, which was somehow worse.

"That's a binding," she said. "A living lock. Look at the structure — it's not a ward, wards keep things out. This is keyed. It opens for..." She frowned, tracing the small characters ringing the gate. "It opens for a mage. A living one. It has to be *fed*, in a way — the sigil drinks intention. You'd have to stand before it and mean your way through."

"Meaning how?" Pim asked.

"Meaning you have to want it with everything you've got, and the lock tastes whether you're lying." Marisol straightened, arms crossing. "Which is a problem, because there are maybe four living mages in Greyhollow who could push a sigil like that, and every one of them is either dead, fled, or wearing a hunter's badge."

The silence that followed had a shape, and everyone in the cabin knew its name. They all looked, very carefully, at nobody.

Ada broke it. "There's a fifth option."

"You," Bramble said.

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." Bramble studied her. Fifteen years in Greyhollow, running a charm shop that sold luck to fishmongers and nothing that would ever interest a sigil-hunter, and in two days she'd hidden a corpse, carried a forbidden map across half the city, and walked up the *Gullwing*'s gangplank asking after drowned cisterns. "You've been hiding something a long time, Vane. I don't know what it is yet. But you knew that courier was coming to you, or you knew what he carried, or you're the best liar I've ever shared air with, and I've sailed with professionals."

Ada's jaw tightened. For a moment Bramble thought she'd push too hard and the woman would walk — and then they'd all be standing around a map none of them could read the heart of, and somewhere below the Spire whatever this was would stay buried, and Tammas Quill would stay unburied too, and—

"I'll open the gate," Ada said quietly. "If it comes to that. But first I need to know why a smuggler captain keeps a chart of the King's Throat memorized well enough to finish another reader's sentence."

Bramble held her gaze. Around the cabin, the crew had gone very still, the way sailors go still when they sense the ship changing course beneath them.

"Because my brother drew it," she said.

* * *

She went to the floorboards then, because some things you couldn't say while looking at people.

The *Gullwing* had been her father's ship before it was hers, and her father had believed — in the way of stubborn harbor folk — that a vessel should carry its history in its bones. Under the chart table, bolted to the keel-timber, was a shallow iron box that three generations of Quills had used for the things too important to keep and too dangerous to throw away. Her mother's letters from the northern runs. Her father's pardon, bought at ruinous cost. And, for two years now, a bundle of papers tied with tarred cord that Bramble took out only on nights when sleep refused her.

She untied the cord and spread the papers beside the courier's map.

Tammas's handwriting was unmistakable — big, confident loops, the script of a man who wrote the way he laughed, too loud and taking up room. He'd been the better sailor of the two of them, everyone said so, and he'd been the braver one, which was the same thing in their family. Two years ago he'd taken a contract from a client who paid in unmarked silver and asked no questions aloud, and he'd gone down into the King's Throat with a copy of a route-map and a promise to be back before the tide turned twice.

He hadn't come back. No body. No boat. Just his satchel, found three weeks later wedged in the grate at the Corvess Street sluice, soaked through, containing these papers and nothing else worth naming.

"Bramble," Tam said softly, from the doorway. He'd known. Of course he'd known; he'd helped her pull the satchel from the water. "Lass, you don't have to—"

"I do, actually." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Because look."

She laid Tammas's route-copy beside the courier's map, and the cabin crowded in to see, and there it was: the same lines, the same junctions, the same blank space labeled *the Drowned Court*, the same gate ringed in living-lock sigils. Tammas's copy ended at the gate. The courier's map continued past it — up, into the roots of the Spire, through channels no smuggler had any business knowing, to a final chamber marked with a symbol Bramble had seen exactly once before and hoped never to see again.

A crown, drawn upside down.

"What's that mean?" Pim whispered.

"It means whoever made this map wasn't smuggling goods," Marisol said. Her brown face had gone gray around the mouth. "Goods don't need a crown. People do." She looked up. "This is a way *in* to the Spire's deep cells. Or a way out. And somebody died getting it to the surface."

"The courier," Wick said.

"The courier," Bramble agreed. "Who bled out on a charm-seller's doorstep whispering about—" she glanced at Ada.

"'Thirteenth turn,'" Ada supplied. "He said *thirteenth turn* and nothing else."

"Thirteenth turn of what?"

Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and the question sat in the lamplight like a stone dropped down a well whose bottom they couldn't hear.

Bramble gathered Tammas's papers slowly, squaring the edges against the table, giving her hands work so her voice wouldn't shake. Two years she'd told herself he was dead, because dead was a wound you could close over time, and missing was a wound that stayed open and wept. Two years she'd hated the not-knowing more than she'd have hated any body the river could have given her.

And now here was his route, in another man's blood, leading somewhere he'd never returned from — and leading *further* than his copy had gone. Which meant either the courier had come after him, or come before him, or come from the same hand that had sent him down.

Either way, the answer to what happened to Tammas Quill was under the Spire, behind a gate that drank intention, and Bramble Quill had spent her whole life collecting debts.

"We go," she said.

"Captain—" Marisol started.

"No. Hear me." She stood, and the cabin seemed to shrink to fit her, the way it did when she put her captain's voice on. "Two years ago my brother went down the King's Throat on a contract I told him was too clean, and I let him go alone because I was nursing a grudge about the split. That's the truth of it, and I've carried it every tide since. Now his road has washed up on my deck with fresh blood on it, and I am *not* letting it go alone again." She looked around the table, at each of them in turn — Wick with his lamp, Marisol with her curses, old Tam with his bad knees and older loyalty, Pim trying to look ready and nearly managing it. "I'm not ordering anyone. This isn't a run. This is the kind of job you don't come back from if it goes wrong, and the Crown will call it treason, and there's no cargo pay because there's no cargo. Anyone who wants off the *Gullwing* says so now, and I'll shake their hand and wish them fair winds, and there'll be no shame in it."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the harbor bells rang the second watch.

Wick reached down and turned his lamp's wick a hair higher, and the light in the cabin strengthened, and that was his answer — it was always his answer; the man had followed her into worse for less reason than light.

Marisol swore, comprehensively, in all three languages, and then said, "Someone has to keep you from drowning, and it may as well be me."

Tam pushed off the doorframe, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "Your father would skin me if I let you go without me. And I'd let him."

They all looked at Pim.

Pim swallowed. Looked at the map. Looked at the upside-down crown. "My mum always said I'd end up hanged," he said, and grinned, and it almost reached his eyes. "Might as well make it interesting."

Bramble nodded once, because if she'd tried to speak just then, the gratitude would have gotten loose and she'd have had to kill everyone present.

* * *

Later — much later, when the crew had dispersed to their bunks and the *Gullwing* rocked at her moorings with the particular creak of a ship holding secrets — Ada lingered in the cabin doorway.

"The gate," Bramble said, before the woman could speak. "The living lock. You said you'd open it if it came to that. I want to know what you meant."

Ada was quiet long enough that the lantern flame had time to gutter and steady. "You'll know when we're standing in front of it," she said finally. "Not before. Not because I don't trust you. Because the less you know, the safer you are, and I've buried enough people this week."

"One person."

"One is enough to change how you sleep."

Bramble almost laughed at that — a short, hard sound with nothing funny in it. "Vane. I haven't slept right in two years. Whatever you're hiding, it can't be worse than the not-knowing." She tapped the courier's map, on the blank space called the Drowned Court, and then, gently, on the corner where a cracked wax seal bore a gull rising over three waves. "But I'll grant you this much: you brought me the one map in Greyhollow I had a personal reason to read. So we'll call it even until the gate."

Ada studied her for a long moment, and Bramble watched the woman decide something — watched it happen behind those flat, careful eyes like weather building over open water.

"When we find the gate," Ada said, "there may be signs of who else passed that way. Two years ago. If there are—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "If there are, I'll be the one to look first. That's not kindness, Captain. It's just that I've already seen a man die this week, and you haven't."

Bramble held her gaze and didn't thank her, because thanking her would have meant agreeing that Tammas needed looking *for* rather than looking *after*, and she wasn't ready to make that trade.

"First light," she said instead. "We go down at slack tide, through the Corvess sluice. Tell your cat to say goodbye to the shop."

For the first time since the woman had boarded, Ada Vane smiled — a small, crooked, tired thing, gone almost before it arrived.

"He already knows," she said. "He heard the whole briefing through the hull. He has opinions about the cisterns."

And she was gone, down the gangway into the grey beginning of morning, leaving Bramble alone with the lamp, the map, and her brother's ghost sitting patient as ballast in the iron box beneath the floor.

She knelt, slid the box home, and rested her palm flat against the warm timber above it.

"Hold on, Tam," she murmured. "I'm coming down the throat after you."

Above the harbor, far to the east, the moon was setting — fat and white and one night fuller than it had been the night before, counting down toward something none of them yet had a name for.
