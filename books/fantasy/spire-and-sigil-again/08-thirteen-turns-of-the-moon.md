## 8. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

The astrologer's tower smelled of cold brass and burnt rosemary, and Sable had learned to hate both.

She climbed the spiral stair for the third time that week, her boots loud on stone worn smooth by a century of feet, and told herself—again—that there was nothing wrong with wanting to check a man's arithmetic. Master Ellery Voss was not a traitor. He was an old man who loved his charts the way some men loved their wine, and he had simply made a mistake in his calculations, or she had misread them, or—

Or the moon was counting down to something, and no one at court would say what.

"Princess." Voss did not look up from his table when she entered. He never looked up anymore. His instruments had multiplied since spring—astrolabes nested inside astrolabes, armillary spheres within spheres, until the whole room seemed to be orbiting around him like a slow metal storm. "I told your chamberlain I was not to be disturbed before the evening observation."

"You told my chamberlain a great many things," Sable said. "You also told him the thirteenth turn falls on the night of the Longmarket Feast. You said it as though it were a birthday."

Voss's hand paused over his chart. A single grain of chalk dust drifted down and settled on the parchment like snow.

"Did I," he said.

"You did. And then you went pale as whey and asked him to bring you more candles, which you have done every week this season, as though candlelight could hold back whatever it is you've written in those tables." She crossed the room and put her palm flat on the great lunar chart pinned beneath his elbow—the one he'd tried, clumsily, to cover with a star catalogue two days ago. Thirteen circles of moon, inked in silver, marching across the page. Twelve of them were crossed out.

Her father's reign was thirteen years old this winter.

Thirteen years since the famine ended. Thirteen years since the Spire's mirrors were curtained and the court astrologer's office tripled its staff overnight. Thirteen years since her father stopped laughing at anything that wasn't performed for an audience.

"Tell me what the count is for," Sable said quietly.

Voss finally looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and terribly tired, and for one naked moment he looked at her the way men look at a door they cannot decide whether to open or bar. Then the moment passed, and he smiled the small, careful smile of a servant who has survived four administrations by knowing exactly how much truth each of them could stomach.

"Astronomy, Highness. The moon does what the moon does. We merely write it down."

"You're lying to me in my father's house."

"I am *serving* in your father's house," Voss said, and there was something under the words, something almost like grief. "There is a difference, Princess, though I grant it grows harder to see every year." He gathered his charts against his chest like a shield. "Now if you'll excuse me. The evening observation waits for no one—not even royalty."

He shuffled past her toward the stair, and Sable let him go, because grabbing an old man's sleeve would gain her nothing but a scene. But at the top of the steps he paused, back still turned, and said, so softly she nearly missed it:

"When the moon is full on the feast night, stay away from the Spire. Whatever else you do that night—stay away from the Spire."

Then he was gone, and Sable stood alone in the whirling brass silence, her heart beating a slow, heavy rhythm against her ribs.

*Twelve turns remain.*

She hadn't needed him to say it. The chart had said it for him: twelve moons crossed out, one left bare. Something was ending. Something thirteen years in the making. And everyone in this palace—from the astrologer to the chamberlain to the servants who wouldn't meet her eyes in the corridors—knew what it was except her.

Except, perhaps, her father.

* * *

She went to find him because she could not bear not to.

This was the trouble with being the king's daughter: every other fear in her life could be investigated, interrogated, run to ground like a poacher in the royal woods. But this fear sat in the middle of her chest wearing her father's face, and she could not arrest it, could not question it, could not even name it aloud without committing a kind of treason against herself.

She found him in the small solar off the eastern gallery, where he took his supper alone most nights now. The guards outside the door bowed and let her pass—they always let her pass; she was the one person in Greyhollow whose presence near the king raised no alarm—and she stood a moment in the doorway just watching him.

Aldric Vane-who-was-not-a-Vane-by-blood but had taken the name anyway, King of Greyhollow and the Saltmarches, Warden of the Spire, Keeper of the Nine Seals. Fifty-one years old and grey at the temples now, broad through the shoulders still, reading dispatches by lamplight with his spectacles pushed up into his hair because he refused, on principle, to admit he needed them.

Something in Sable's chest unclenched at the sight of him. *See?* said the voice that sounded like her mother, dead nine years and still the sanest counsel in the kingdom. *See? It's a man eating cold mutton and arguing with a tax ledger. What monster is this?*

"Papa," she said. "You're working past supper again."

"Sable." He looked up, and his face did the thing it always did—warmed, brightened, arranged itself into delight—and she felt the familiar guilt of loving someone who was always pleased to see her and somehow never surprised. "Come in, come in. Sit. Tell me you haven't been haunting Voss's tower again; the man complains to my chamberlain like a scolded wife."

"He should complain to me directly. I'd tell him the truth: I'm not going to stop." She pulled a chair close to his, close enough that their knees nearly touched, the way they used to sit when she was small and he read her stories about the river-kings. "Papa. I need to ask you something, and I need you not to perform for me. Just answer me plainly, once, as yourself."

His quill stilled above the ledger.

"The astrologer's charts count thirteen turns of the moon," she said. "Twelve are struck through. The last falls on the feast night. Every servant in this palace crosses themselves when they think I'm not looking, the Spire's mirrors have been curtained for thirteen years, and you—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—you haven't laughed at anything real since the winter I turned eleven. So I'm asking you. Papa. Look at me and tell me what happens when the moon finishes turning."

For a heartbeat—a single heartbeat—he did look at her.

And his eyes were wrong.

It was not that they were cruel, or strange, or glowing with any sorcery out of a smuggler's tale. It was worse than that. They were *calm*. Perfectly, bottomlessly calm, the way deep water is calm, the way a tomb is calm. They were the eyes of something that had watched thirteen years pass the way a mountain watches weather—without impatience, without interest, without any of the warm human fret that made her father's face her father's face. For that one heartbeat, the man across from her was old in a way that had nothing to do with years. Old the way the Spire was old. Old the way stone under the city was old.

Then he blinked, and the warmth flooded back in like lamplight through a curtain, and Aldric was laughing, setting down his quill, rubbing his eyes behind the spectacles he pretended not to need.

"Forgive me. Tired. These tariffs will be the death of me before any moon gets the chance." He reached over and squeezed her hand, and his grip was warm, and right, and exactly as strong as she remembered from a thousand childhood crossings of the courtyard. "Sable. My girl. Nothing happens on the feast night except too much wine and your aunt's interminable toast. Whatever the servants are whispering—servants whisper, it's what they're for—you may put it out of your head."

"But the mirrors—"

"Curtained because cracked glass flatters no one at state functions. Ask the glazier's bill, if you doubt me." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and it was perfect, and that perfection was suddenly the most frightening thing in the room. Her father had never in his life had a *perfect* smile. His real smiles were crooked, lopsided, quick. This one was symmetrical as a coin.

"Papa," she said, very carefully, "what did you trade to end the famine?"

The lamplight flickered. Outside, far below, the bells of the Duskwatch rang the second hour, and somewhere in the palace a dog began to bark and was hushed.

"What a question," he said lightly. "Where do you get these things?"

"From listening. It's the one skill they couldn't school out of me." She held his gaze. *Look at me again,* she begged whatever was behind his eyes. *Do the wrong thing again. Be angry, be puzzled, be bored—be anything but kind.* "Thirteen years ago the granaries were empty and the river ran thin and the people were burning sigil-marked doors in the Lower City. And then, in a single winter, it all ended. No chronicle says how. No ballad sings it. There's a hole in the history exactly the shape of one winter, Papa, and you're sitting in the middle of it, and you won't—"

"*Enough.*"

The word came out flat and cold as a dropped blade, and for half a breath his face was the other face again—the calm one, the old one—and Sable's blood turned to ice water in her veins. Then he pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose and sighed, long and human and exhausted, and when he spoke again his voice was rough with something that might have been genuine feeling, or might have been the finest imitation of it ever crafted.

"You want the truth? Here is a truth, daughter, free of charge: kings pay for their miracles. Every one of them. I paid for ours, and the price is mine to carry, and I will thank you not to dig at the wound while I'm trying to keep this kingdom fed." He stood, and kissed the top of her head, and his lips lingered there a moment. "Go home, Sable. Go to your books and your investigations and your charmingly illegal visits to the Lower City—I know about those, by the way; I know everything that happens in my city—and let an old man finish his mutton."

He walked her to the door himself. At the threshold he stopped, one hand on the frame, and said without turning:

"And Sable. Stay away from the Spire on the feast night."

The same words. The same cadence. Exactly the warning Voss had given her, word for word, as though they had rehearsed it—or as though both of them served the same instruction.

* * *

She did not go home. She walked.

The palace corridors emptied around her as the night deepened, and Sable moved through them like a ghost in her own house, past tapestries woven before the ban, past the curtained mirrors that threw back nothing but dark cloth, past guards who saluted her and saw nothing. Her mind kept returning to that heartbeat. That single, terrible heartbeat of calm.

Here was the horror of it, she thought, standing at last on the eastern balcony where the wind came sharp off the sea: the monster, if there was a monster, had not roared at her. It had squeezed her hand. It had kissed her hair. It had worried about her safety on the feast night with what had felt, in the moment, indistinguishable from love.

If it could wear her father's tenderness that well—if it had worn it for thirteen years, tucking her in as a child, weeping at her mother's funeral, teaching her to ride—then what was she mourning, when she mourned the possibility that he was gone? A face? A set of gestures? Or was the love itself real, wherever it lived, whoever wore it?

*I don't know which is worse,* she thought. *That he is lost, or that I cannot prove it.*

Below her, Greyhollow sprawled in its thousand lamps, the Lower City a scatter of embers against the black of the harbor. Somewhere down there, in the crooked streets she'd begun slipping into disguised as a clerk, people whispered about a charm-seller who talked to animals. About sigil-hunters asking questions. About a courier who hadn't reported in.

About the thirteenth turn.

The moon rose over the harbor, three days shy of full, and Sable counted backward despite herself. Twelve more after this one. Twelve turns, and then whatever bargain had been struck in the famine winter came due, and the thing in her father's chair—whatever it was, whatever it wanted—would no longer need to pretend.

She gripped the balcony rail until her knuckles ached.

*Then I'll stop waiting to be told,* she resolved, and the resolution steadied her like a hand on her shoulder. *I'll find the charm-seller. I'll follow the whispers down into the Lower City where the truth keeps its lodgings. And on the feast night, I will not stay away from the Spire.*

Behind her, deep in the palace, faint as a memory, she heard the curtained mirrors chime—all of them at once, though no wind touched the halls.

They had never made a sound before.
