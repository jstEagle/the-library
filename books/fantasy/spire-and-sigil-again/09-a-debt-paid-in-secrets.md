## 9. A Debt Paid in Secrets

The rain came down in Greyhollow's usual fashion—not falling so much as seeping, a grey insistence that turned every cobbled street into a mirror and every lantern into a smeared halo. Ada Vane pulled her hood lower and told herself, for the third time since the Gullwing's crew had scattered to their separate errands, that she was not being followed.

She was wrong.

She felt it first as she always felt things from living creatures: a pressure at the edge of her awareness, like a hand hovering just above the nape of her neck. Not an animal. Animals were honest. This was something trained to dampen itself, to walk with its heartbeat banked low, and only one kind of person in Greyhollow moved like that.

Sigil-hunters.

Ada cut off Candlemaker's Row without breaking stride, ducked through the gap between the tallow-shop and the wall where the drainpipe had rotted away years ago—a gap she'd used since she was nine—and emerged into the tangle of alleys behind Tanner's Court. The pressure followed. Patient. Unhurried. Whoever it was knew exactly how this game went, and knew, too, that Ada knew.

Her heart was doing something complicated against her ribs. She thought of the map hidden in the false bottom of her charm-box back at the shop, of Bramble's rough handwriting now copied onto three separate scraps distributed among three separate pockets, of the raven—Mordant, insufferable, loyal Mordant—roosting above her door with instructions to scream if anyone in a grey coat came within twenty paces.

She thought of the cat, Patch, still limping slightly from the market raid, sleeping on her pillow as if he hadn't nearly died buying her those ten seconds of chaos.

*You owe them,* said the small clear voice inside her that sounded annoyingly like Mordant. *Every one of them. So don't be stupid.*

She stopped in the mouth of the alley behind the old dye-works, where the walls wept indigo stains and the rain collected in a shallow stone basin that had once been a baptismal font, back when Greyhollow had still pretended to have gods. She turned around.

"Come out, then," she said. "You've spent enough of the Crown's coin following me through puddles."

He stepped out of the shadow of the dye-works eave as though he'd simply been standing there all along waiting for an invitation, which, knowing Corvane, he probably had. Hunter-sergeant Corvane was not what the ballads promised sigil-hunters would be. He was neither tall nor scarred nor cruel-eyed. He was a broad, weathered man of perhaps fifty, with a grey travelling coat gone dark at the shoulders with rain, a face like a folded map—creased along lines that had been made long ago and never smoothed—and eyes the colour of river slate. He carried no drawn weapon. He carried, instead, a small oilcloth parcel, which he held in both hands the way a man holds bread he intends to share.

"Vane," he said. His voice was quiet, gravelled, the voice of a man who had learned that shouting accomplished nothing that patience could not do better.

"Sergeant." Ada kept her hands visible and loose. Fifteen years of practice. "Lovely evening for extortion."

"It's not extortion yet." He glanced up at the weeping sky, then at the indigo-stained walls, and something in his expression flickered—recognition, maybe, or memory. "I knew this place when it was a chapel. Before your time."

"Everything was something before my time. That's generally how time works."

Something that might have been amusement crossed his creased face and was gone. He took one step closer, and Ada forced herself not to step back. Retreat was confession. She'd learned that too.

"You were seen," Corvane said. "At the market. When the cat bolted the cart and the detection charms went sideways. Three witnesses say you spoke to it. One says the animals *answered* you." He let that hang in the wet air between them. "Witnesses lie, mostly. Fear makes people see what they've been warned about. But three witnesses, Vane, is a pattern. And patterns are my trade."

"I talked to a panicked animal because it was about to trample a child. Anyone would."

"Anyone would shout. You *spoke*, and it *listened*, and it changed its mind mid-gallop like a horse reconsidering a fence." He shook his head slowly. "I've watched beasts my whole career, Vane. Horses, hounds, the hunter-birds we fly out of the Spire mews. They don't change their minds for shouting. They change their minds for someone who asks them properly."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere above, a gull complained about the weather in the universal language of gulls, which was complaint.

"What do you want?" Ada asked.

Corvane looked down at the oilcloth parcel in his hands. Turned it over once, as if checking his own resolve. Then he held it out to her.

"Open it."

She didn't move. "I'm not touching anything from a sigil-hunter's pocket. For all I know it bites."

"It does," he said gravely. "That's rather the point. Open it anyway."

She took it. It was heavier than she expected, wrapped in three layers, and when she peeled the oilcloth back she found herself looking at a cage no bigger than two fists, wrought of black iron filigree, and inside the cage sat a beetle the size of her thumbnail, its shell polished obsidian, its legs folded neatly beneath it like a sleeping child's hands. A listening-beetle. She'd heard of them. Everyone had heard of them; almost no one had seen one outside the Spire. They recorded everything said within thirty feet and played it back under a truth-charm that no voice could evade.

"Why are you giving me this?"

"Because I'm about to offer you a bargain," Corvane said, "and I want there to be a record of it. Yours and mine. So that later, whichever way this goes, neither of us can pretend we said something we didn't." He met her eyes, and for the first time she saw how tired they were. Not lazy-tired. *Old*-tired. The tiredness of a man who has carried the same weight up the same stairs for decades. "Here is the bargain, Vane. Full immunity. Crown pardon, sealed by the Lord Justiciar's own signet. Your name struck from every watch-list in Greyhollow. Your shop left alone for the rest of your natural life. In exchange: the crew of the Gullwing. Names, routes, the map they're deciphering, and the location of Captain Quill's next rendezvous. You give me the smugglers, and the beast-speaker who doesn't exist never has to exist again."

The rain drummed on the oilcloth. Inside the little iron cage, the beetle stirred, legs whispering against filigree, and Ada understood suddenly that it was already listening. Had probably been listening since she opened the wrapping. Everything she said now was being pressed into that black shell like a flower into a book.

*Careful,* said the Mordant-voice in her head. *Everything. Careful.*

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Corvane sighed. It was a real sigh, she thought—the sigh of a man who had hoped, somewhere underneath the procedure, that she wouldn't make him do the next part. "Then I arrest you tonight. Beast-speech is a crown crime, Vane. Third clause of the Edict of Hollowing. You know the penalty as well as I do. The white room under the Sigil-House, and after the white room—" he didn't finish it. He didn't need to. Everyone in Greyhollow finished that sentence the same way, in the privacy of their own heads, usually late at night.

Ada looked at the beetle. At the creased, tired face above it. And she did the thing that fifteen years of hiding had trained her for, the thing that had kept her alive through raids and inspections and a hundred small suffocations: she weighed the exits.

There were none. The alley had one mouth and Corvane stood in it. Even if she ran, even if the rats and pigeons rose up around her in a chittering, feathered storm—and she had done it before, once, badly, and spent a week vomiting with the strain of it—he would simply follow, patient as rain, and next time he wouldn't offer a bargain first.

So instead of running, Ada Vane did something braver and more foolish.

She crouched down, set the open cage on the wet stones between them, and spoke—to the beetle.

"Little one," she said softly, in the wordless way that was not words at all but meaning poured directly across the thin membrane between minds, *you're tired, aren't you? You've been listening so long. Listening is heavy work.*

The beetle's antennae lifted. Its shell gleamed. And in the back of her mind she felt it answer—not in language, beetles didn't have language, but in sensation, in the simple pressing weight of its small existence: *heavy, yes, heavy, always listening, always carrying, want to sleep.*

"There," Ada said aloud, to Corvane, without taking her eyes off the insect. "It says listening is heavy. It wants to sleep. Do you know, Sergeant, that your own tools come to me more readily than your suspects do? Perhaps ask yourself why."

Corvane had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock—she'd seen shock, and this wasn't it. This was the stillness of confirmation. Of a man watching the last door close on a hope he'd been quietly keeping propped open.

"So it's true," he said quietly.

"You already knew it was true. You said so yourself—three witnesses is a pattern, and patterns are your trade." She straightened. Her heart was hammering but her voice, miraculously, held. "So here's my counter-offer, Sergeant, and your beetle can carry it home word for word. No. I won't sell the Gullwing's crew. Not Bramble, who fed half the Rookery through the famine winters while your Crown counted its taxes. Not Fen, who's barely nineteen and laughs like a kettle. Not any of them. They're mine. Do you understand what I'm saying? They're *mine*, the way your oath is yours, and I don't sell what's mine."

"They'll hang you with them, when this goes wrong. And it will go wrong. Whatever Quill is chasing with that map—it's older and colder than smuggling, Vane. I've read the requisition orders coming out of the Spire these last two months. Extra wardens for the lower levels. Chains ordered by the hundredweight. Something down there is waking up, and the Crown's answer is more chains." His jaw worked. "I am offering you the only lifeboat on a sinking ship."

"Then I'll swim." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real, sharp-edged, alive. "Fifteen years, Sergeant. Fifteen years I made myself small and quiet and harmless, and do you know what it bought me? A shop full of luck-charms that don't work, sold to people who need them to. A life shaped exactly like a hiding place. I'm done hiding. If the price of standing in the open is the white room, then at least I'll have stood."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. The rain fell. The beetle slept in its cage, dreaming whatever beetles dream, unburdened by thirty feet of treason.

Then he did something Ada had not expected in any of her weighings of exits.

He knelt, closed the cage's little door, and began rewrapping it in its oilcloth.

"The record will show," he said, in the flat tone of a man dictating to posterity, "that the suspect declined the Crown's offer, that no evidence of beast-speech was obtained, and that Sergeant Corvane found the witnesses' accounts inconsistent upon review." He tied the knot. Stood. "Three witnesses, Vane. Witnesses lie. I told you that myself, and the beetle heard it."

Ada stared at him. "Why?"

"Don't ask questions you'll have to live with the answers to."

"No." She stepped forward, into the rain, into his path, reckless in a way that would have horrified the woman she'd been a month ago. "I'm asking. You had me. You had the pattern, the witnesses, the tool, the alley with one exit. And you're letting me walk. Men like you don't do that for free. So what is it you actually serve, Sergeant? Because I don't think it's the man wearing the crown."

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off the creases of his face like water off old stone.

"When I was a young hunter," he said at last, "newer to the coat than you are to your gift, I swore my oath to Queen Isolde. Aldric's mother. You'll not remember her; she died before you were selling charms." His voice dropped, went somewhere private. "She made us swear a strange oath, for a queen. Not to protect the throne. To protect *the realm from the throne*, should the throne ever stop serving the realm. Every sigil-hunter swore it, in those days. Most of them have forgotten the wording." He paused. "I haven't. I recite it every morning. And every morning, for thirteen years, I have looked at the king's face and wondered why it never ages the way grief should."

The bottom dropped out of Ada's stomach. "You know."

"I know that something in the Spire smells of cold," Corvane said, "and that the requisitions for chains started eleven weeks ago, and that a good hunter follows patterns wherever they lead, even when the pattern leads somewhere the Crown would rather it didn't." He tucked the wrapped beetle inside his coat. "I can't help you, Vane. Understand that. If I'm seen helping you, everything I might do later becomes impossible. But I can fail to catch you. There's a difference, and it's the whole width of my honour, such as it is."

He turned to go, then stopped, half-turned back.

"The servant's door on the spire's north face, third landing below the bell gallery. The lock is old. Old locks remember kindnesses." He said it quickly, quietly, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far. "And Vane—when you get wherever you're going, and you will, because people like you always do—ask it what it wants. Whatever's down there. Nobody in thirteen years has asked it what it *wants*. They've only ever told it what it is."

Ada's skin prickled from scalp to sole. "How do you know that's what I'm—"

"Patterns," Corvane said, and almost smiled. "They're my trade."

He walked away down the alley, unhurried, a broad grey shape dissolving into the grey rain, and Ada stood alone in the ruins of the old chapel with her heart going like a trapped bird and the taste of the future sharp on her tongue.

* * *

She didn't go straight back to the shop. Fifteen years of habit died hard, and besides, she needed to move, to walk the fear out of her legs before it set like cement. She took the long way, along the harbour wall where the wind came off the water cold and clean and smelling of salt and weed, and she let the gulls shout at her and answered them absently, mindlessly, *yes, yes, the fish carts are late, the world is unjust*, while the rest of her turned Corvane over and over like a stone looking for what lived underneath.

A sigil-hunter who served the realm against the throne. An oath recited every morning for thirteen years. A servant's door with an old lock.

Or a trap. It could all be a trap. The kindest traps were the ones baited with exactly what you wanted to hear. She knew that. Bramble would say it first thing, scowling, arms crossed: *Hunters don't turn, girl. Hunters wait.* And Bramble would be right to say it, and Ada would have to hold both truths at once—the possibility of treachery and the possibility of grace—and choose her course with only her own judgement to steer by.

That was the thing about loyalty, she was beginning to understand. It wasn't a feeling. Feelings were cheap; she'd felt loyal to her own safety for fifteen years and it had never cost her a copper. Loyalty was a choice made in the dark, with incomplete information, and then another choice tomorrow, and the day after, each one spending a piece of you. Corvane had just spent something enormous—she'd seen the cost of it in his face, in the way he'd dictated his own false report to a beetle. Whatever happened next, that man had chosen his side tonight, and it wasn't the Crown's.

By the time she reached Charm Seller's Row the lamps were lit and the rain had thinned to mist. Mordant dropped from the eaves onto her shoulder before she'd even reached the door, talons gripping through her cloak, beak pressed briefly against her ear in the raven equivalent of a frantic interrogation.

*"Well? Well? You smell of fear and harbour. Which is it? Did he take you? Are you arrested? Am I a raven of interest to the authorities now? I've always wanted to be a raven of interest."*

"He offered me immunity," Ada said quietly, letting them both inside, bolting the door, lighting the lamp with hands that finally, finally allowed themselves to shake. "My freedom for the crew. And I refused. And then he let me go, and gave me a door into the Spire, and told me to ask the thing below it what it wants."

Silence from her shoulder. Then, very softly: *"He told you to ask it what it wants?"*

"Yes."

*"Ada."* Mordant's voice had gone strange—hushed, the way he got only when memory moved through him, the old memories from before her, from whoever had kept him before she'd found him with a broken wing and a vocabulary full of courtly phrases no street-raven should know. *"Those were the old king's words. Aldric's. He used to say it in the throne room, when petitioners came with grievances. 'Tell me what you want,' he'd say. 'Not what you think I want to hear. What you want.' I heard him say it a hundred times, back when I was a palace bird with pretensions."* The raven's feathers rippled. *"How does a sigil-hunter come to speak like Aldric?"*

Ada sank onto her stool beside the cold hearth, and Patch flowed off the pillow and into her lap, one-eared head butting her chin, purring like a small thunderstorm.

"I don't know," she said. "But I think we've just learned whose side the law is really on. And I think—" she scratched Patch's ears, and felt the map's weight in the box across the room, and thought of Bramble and Fen and the drowned cisterns waiting beneath the Spire, twelve turns of moon and counting— "I think we're out of time to be careful. Get some sleep, Mordant. Tomorrow we tell the crew everything. Tomorrow we go down."

From the shelf in the corner, the charm-box seemed almost to hum with what waited inside it, and outside, high above the rooftops, the moon rose thin and hungry over Greyhollow, one night closer to its thirteenth turn.
