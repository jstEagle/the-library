## 10. Into the Drowned Cisterns

*Ada*

The grate over the cistern mouth had not been moved in thirteen years, and it announced this fact with a shriek that Ada felt in her back teeth.

"Subtle," muttered Wick, the crew's lockwoman, kneeling in the mud with her picks still wedged in the rusted iron. "Very subtle. Why don't we just ring the chapel bells while we're at it."

"It's midnight," said Bramble Quill, standing at the edge of the drainage channel with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was pretending not to grieve. "Nobody rings bells at midnight except for funerals."

Ada crouched beside the grate and put her palm flat against the stone. The city above them was quiet in the way Greyhollow only got between the last tavern closing and the first fish-cart — a held-breath quiet, as if the whole sprawl of rooftops and chimneys were listening too.

The stone was cold, but beneath the cold there was water. She could feel it the way she felt everything living: a pressure against the inside of her skull, faint and patient. Water wasn't alive, exactly. But it remembered things. Rivers especially. And whatever fed these cisterns had once been a river, before the Spire drank it.

*There you are,* something murmured, far down in the dark. Not words, quite. More like the shape words leave behind.

Ada snatched her hand back.

"You all right?" Sable was beside her — Princess Sable, in a smuggler's oilskin two sizes too big, her signet ring hidden on a cord around her neck where it wouldn't catch the light. She still moved like royalty; Ada was beginning to suspect she always would. "You went white."

"The water talks," Ada said. It sounded mad even to her, and she'd spent fifteen years talking to cats. "I mean — it doesn't talk. But it's *aware*. And I think it knows we're here."

Bramble looked up from the map spread across her knee, its bloodstained wax seal long since cracked open, its inked route glowing faintly under Wick's shuttered lantern. "Then let's hope it's friendlier than the sigil-hunters. In. Quietly. Fen takes point, Wick on locks, Ada reads the marks. Sable—" a beat, the captain deciding, again, whether she could stomach giving orders to a princess, "—stays in the middle. Nobody wanders."

"I never wander," said Sable.

"You wandered into my ship at two in the morning wearing a disguise so bad my cook thought you were a tax collector."

"That was reconnaissance."

"Fen," Bramble said, ignoring her. "Go."

Fen grinned — he was always grinning, a lean, freckled young man who'd joined the Gullwing crew at fourteen and never learned that smuggling was supposed to be grim work — and dropped through the grate into the dark without so much as a rope. There was a splash, a pause, then his voice floating up, echoing strangely off wet stone:

"It's ankle-deep! And it smells like the underside of the world! Come on, it's *lovely!*"

Ada went down the ladder last. The moment her boots hit the water, the murmur rose around her calves like a tide coming in, and every hair on her arms stood up.

*Thirteen years,* the water said. *Thirteen years since anyone came down carrying an open hand.*

She didn't tell the others. Some translations took longer than a single night.

* * *

*Bramble*

Bramble Quill had sailed the Greywater delta for twenty years, and she knew drowned places. She knew how they swallowed sound, how they turned a lantern's circle into a small and temporary country, how they made even brave people walk closer together than they wanted to.

But she had never been in a place that felt *watched* the way this felt watched.

The cisterns beneath the Spire were older than the Spire itself — vaulted halls of black granite pillared like a petrified forest, built by some pre-royal masonry that hadn't believed in straight lines. Their boots echoed wrong. The echoes came back a half-beat late, or early, or from directions no echo should come from.

And everywhere, on every pillar, the sigils.

They'd been carved deep once, then filled with lead, then painted over, then carved again — layers upon layers of binding-marks, the kind the Crown used now to cage magic out of the world. Except these weren't the clean civic seals Corvane's hunters wore on their badges. These were old. Desperate. Some of them had been scratched out by human hands, and some of those had been scratched back in by hands that left no prints at all.

"Ada," Bramble called softly. "How far to the first junction?"

Ada ran her fingers along the map, then along the pillar beside her, matching the courier's inked route to the sigil-work. "Left at the broken seal. Then down. The map says the water gets deeper."

"The water says more than that," Fen piped up from the front. He'd stopped grinning somewhere in the last hundred yards, which frightened Bramble more than the sigils did. "Cap, you feel that? The current's moving against us. We're walking downstream and it's pushing *up*."

"Water does that in cisterns," said Wick. "Pressure. Pumps. Don't be a child."

"It's not pumps." Fen crouched, cupped a handful of black water, and let it run through his fingers. He stared at his palm afterward like it had told him something. "It's breathing."

Bramble opened her mouth to tell him to stop being poetic when the lantern light caught the next pillar, and every thought she had fell out of her head.

Someone had written on it. Freshly. The characters were dragged into the stone as if by fingernails, but they were legible, and they were in her brother Tam's handwriting — the looping, impatient script she'd spent two years finding in every corner of every port.

*TAM WAS HERE. THE WATER REMEMBERS. DO NOT TRUST THE WARM SHAPE.*

Her throat closed. Wick put a hand on her shoulder and, for once, said nothing at all.

"He came this far," Bramble managed. "Two years ago. He made it this far."

"Then we make it further," said Sable quietly. "For him."

Bramble nodded because nodding was easier than speaking, and gave the order to move, and tried very hard not to look at the word *warm*, because the air in the cisterns had been getting warmer for the last ten minutes, and she had told herself it was nothing, and she was running out of nothing to tell herself.

* * *

*Fen*

Fen had a theory about fear, which was that it lived in your stomach and you starved it by keeping your feet moving. It had worked on every job he'd ever run — the customs houses, the night crossings, the one memorable afternoon hiding in a barrel of pickled herring.

It was not working here.

Here, the fear wasn't in his stomach. It was in the water. It came up through his boot-soles and settled into his bones like silt, and the worst part — the part he couldn't have explained to Wick without sounding mad — was that the water wasn't threatening him. It was *warning* him. He'd grown up on the delta; he knew what rivers felt like when they were about to take someone. This wasn't that. This was a dog barking at a door before the fire comes.

*Below,* the current kept saying, in the wordless way currents say things. *Below, below, below.*

He raised a fist. Behind him, the column stopped. He heard Bramble's breath catch, Wick's picks go silent, the soft double-splash of Ada and the princess steadying each other.

"What is it?" Bramble whispered.

"Listen."

They listened. For a long moment there was nothing but their own dripping footsteps dying away into the dark, and the vast slow pulse of the cisterns, and then—

Singing.

Far below them, down where the map said the inner gate stood, something was singing. It wasn't a melody, exactly. It was the sound water makes when it goes over a lip of stone in the dark — but stretched, and layered, and *grieving*. A dozen voices' worth of grief folded into one note that never ended.

"That's our guardian," Ada said. Her voice had gone strange — soft and resonant, as if she were singing along without meaning to. "A river-spirit. Bound. It's been bound down there a long time."

"You can hear that?" Wick demanded. "From *words*?"

"I can hear that it hurts." Ada's eyes, in the lantern light, were the color of the water itself. "It's chained the same way the king is. Same sigil-work. Same hands."

Fen looked back down the flooded passage, at the black water rising past his knees now, warm as bathwater and rising still, and made the mistake of feeling sorry for it.

That was when the warm shape came.

* * *

*Sable*

Later — much later, in the years when she would tell this story and hate telling this story — Sable would remember it in fragments, the way drowning people remember weather.

The water rising. That she remembered clearly: how it climbed their bodies in silence, knee to waist to chest, warm as blood, warm as a fever, and how Ada shouted *it's not the spirit, it's something else, move, MOVE—*

The shape in the water. A man-shape, or near enough — tall, smooth-faced, glowing faintly from within like a lamp behind skin, its outline blurring wherever the black water touched it. It wore warmth the way her father's courtiers wore perfume: thickly, deliberately, to cover what was underneath. And underneath, when the thing turned its eyeless face toward them, Sable smelled winter. Cold iron. Thirteen years of empty rooms.

*Warm shape,* Tam Quill had written. *Smells of cold.* The rats had said it too, Ada had told her. Something below the palace that wears a warm shape but smells of cold.

It wasn't the usurper. It was smaller than that — a fragment, a finger of the thing, sent down to guard the door. But it moved like the usurper must move, and looking at it, Sable understood for the first time, in her marrow, what had been sitting at her father's table for thirteen years calling itself Papa.

"Bramble!" she screamed. "Behind you!"

Because the shape had gone past all of them — gliding, impossibly fast, ignoring Ada and Wick and Sable herself entirely — and reached for the captain. Reached for the one carrying the map. Its hand closed on Bramble's collar, and the water around them both began to steam.

Fen was closest.

Sable would replay this forever, she knew. The geometry of it. How many strides it would have taken any of them. Whether she could have been faster. Whether anyone could have. But Fen didn't calculate anything. Fen, who kept his feet moving because fear lived in the stomach, simply moved — threw himself between the shape and his captain, drove his shoulder into the thing's arm, and shouted the only spell any of the crew knew, the little luck-verse smugglers whispered over cargo, half prayer and half joke.

The verse did nothing. Of course it did nothing. But it bought a second — one second in which the shape's grip shifted from Bramble's collar to Fen's throat — and in that second Bramble drove her knife up under its arm, and the thing came apart like smoke in wind, and the water went cold all at once, everywhere, honest cold, and Fen went down under it and did not come up.

They found him against a pillar, in the shallows where the flood had begun to drain away through cracks in the floor, as if the cisterns themselves were ashamed. His eyes were open. His face was calm. Around his wrist, in water-swollen ink, someone had written a word in a language none of them read — and Ada, kneeling, went gray as bone and said, "It's Old Tongue. It says *answered*."

"No," Bramble said. She said it several times, in several tones, as if one of them might be true. She held him the way you hold cargo you're afraid of dropping, careful and fierce and far too late, and the cisterns rang with their own endless grieving note, and Sable stood in the freezing water with a dead boy's blood diluting in the current and understood that the map had a price printed on it after all, in ink that only showed once paid.

Wick closed his eyes. Bramble wouldn't let anyone else touch him yet. And Ada rose slowly to her feet, staring down the passage toward the singing, toward the inner gate, toward the bound guardian whose chains were the same chains, whose jailer was the same jailer, and her voice when it came was quiet enough that Sable had to lean close to catch it.

"It knows we're coming now. Both of them do." She turned, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "So we don't stop. We give this a meaning it can't take back. We go down, and we listen to what's below — and then we end it."

Above them, far above, through fathoms of stone, the moon kept climbing toward its twelfth turn.

And below, the guardian sang on — and this time, Ada thought she could hear, threaded through the grief, something almost like a question.

*Who has come? Who has come, after all this time, to ask?*
