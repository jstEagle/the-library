## 12. What Sable Saw

The rain had stopped an hour before moonrise, and the cobbles of the Tanner's Reach still shone like spilled mercury. Princess Sable Aldric stood in the shadow of a tannery's dripping eaves with her hood up, her guard's cloak buttoned to the throat, and the royal signet of House Aldric warm against her sternum where it hung on a leather cord beneath her shirt.

She had told herself she was only watching.

That was the lie she'd been telling herself for three days now, ever since she'd watched her father's eyes flicker wrong in the candlelight of his private study—too calm, too old, a heartbeat of something ancient looking out through the face she'd loved since before she could speak. Twelve turns of the moon remained. The court astrologer's ledger said so in careful, deniable notation, and Sable had copied the figures into her own hand and burned the original, because some knowledge was too dangerous to exist twice.

And then there was the other thing. The beast-speaker with the storm-grey eyes and the raven who spoke in her father's voice.

Sable had spent two nights tracking them—the charm-seller, the smuggler captain with the scarred knuckles, the exiled mage who walked like a man counting his steps, the quiet one called Fen whose hands were always moving, always mending something. They moved through Greyhollow's underbelly like water finding its level, and they were heading for the Spire. Of course they were. Everyone who carried that map was heading for the Spire.

She should have raised the alarm. She was, after all, still—technically, in the eyes of a court that no longer looked at her directly—a princess of the realm. She had sworn oaths at fourteen with her hand on her father's sword. Protect the crown. Protect the city. Protect the king.

*Which king?* whispered the voice that had been living rent-free behind her ribs since the night she saw those wrong eyes. *Protect which king?*

Below her, at the mouth of the old drainage arch where Tanner's Reach met the sunken road, lanterns kindled one by one behind shuttered slats. The crew was assembling. She counted them by their silhouettes: Bramble Quill broad as a door, checking knots on a coil of rope with the tenderness other people reserved for children. Fen with a satchel of glass vials that caught the moonlight. The mage, Wren, muttering to himself, his fingers tracing sigils in the condensation on a barrel-head and wiping them away again like a man ashamed of being caught praying.

And Ada Vane, standing slightly apart, the raven perched on her shoulder like a second conscience.

Sable's breath fogged in the cold. She watched Ada tilt her head, listening to something—listening to *someone*—and laugh softly at whatever the rats or the gulls or God knew what had said. The sound carried across the wet stones, small and unguarded, and it did something unfair to Sable's chest.

She had arrested people for less than what this woman was. Beast-speaking was a hanging crime; it was the first clause of the ban, written in the old queen's hand, *no voice shall be given to the voiceless*. Sable had sat through the trials. She had watched hunters lead weeping children away in wagons while the crowds looked anywhere but at them, because looking was how you became complicit, and everyone in Greyhollow had long ago perfected the art of not seeing.

But Ada Vane had spoken to her raven in the dead king's private words—*the moon keeps its own counsel*—a phrase from a letter Aldric had written to Sable when she was nine and afraid of the dark. No one else alive knew it. Sable had checked. She had spent a sleepless week checking, pulling the palace archives apart, and the phrase existed nowhere except in her father's hand, folded in a box under her bed, kept the way you keep a splinter: because removing it would hurt more than leaving it.

Which meant either the charm-seller had found a way to steal thirteen years of a king's most private language, or the king himself had once trusted animals with his secrets, and if the latter—

If the latter, then her father had been hiding things too. From the court. From the ban he enforced. From her.

*Everyone I love is a locked room,* Sable thought, and the thought was so precisely, miserably true that she almost laughed.

Below, the crew began to move. They filed into the drainage arch in single file, Bramble last, and the darkness took them the way water takes a dropped coin.

Sable gave them thirty seconds. Then she followed.

* * *

The sunken road descended in long switchbacks toward the river gate, and Sable kept to the higher wall where the shadows pooled deepest. She had done this kind of work before—in the early years after her father's silence began, when she'd still believed she could investigate her way back to him. She had learned to walk quietly, to dress plainly, to make her face forgettable. Thirteen years of practice at being nobody in particular.

The trouble with thirteen years of practice, she reflected grimly, was that it made treason feel like competence.

They caught her at the third switchback, of course. She'd known they would. A crew that survived the drowned cisterns did not miss a lone figure trailing them down a bottlenecked road, and sure enough, a rope snapped taut across the path at ankle height, and she went down hard on one knee, and then there were blades: Bramble's hatchet resting against her collarbone with terrible gentleness, Fen's knife at the small of her back, Wren holding a sigil that made the air around him shimmer like heat off summer stone.

"Well," said Bramble Quill, peering under Sable's hood. "Well, well, well. Look what the tide dragged in."

"Captain," Sable said, keeping her voice flat and low. "If you're going to kill me, do it quietly. There are watch patrols on the upper road every quarter hour."

"Funny girl." Bramble pressed the hatchet edge a hair closer. Not enough to cut. Enough to be a question. "You've been on us since the Ropewalk. Three days. You want to tell me why a palace spy follows us instead of just reporting us?"

"If I reported you," Sable said, "you'd already be in irons."

"She's right," said Wren uneasily. "Hunters don't tail. They swarm."

Ada stepped out of the dark then, and the raven lifted off her shoulder and settled on a rusted sign bracket above them all, its black eye catching the moon. Ada looked at Sable for a long moment, and Sable felt that look like a hand laid flat against her sternum, right over the signet.

"You," Ada said quietly. "The market. The day the hunters came." Her jaw tightened. "You stood ten feet from me while Corvane tested the whole square, and you did nothing."

"I did nothing," Sable agreed, "because doing something would have gotten your cat killed and you arrested, and neither of those outcomes served me."

"Served you." Ada's voice didn't rise, but it sharpened, honed to an edge. "You're a hunter."

"I'm nothing. I'm no one. That's rather the point of my life." Sable rose slowly, hands visible, palms out—an old habit from negotiating with armed dock gangs in her investigating years. "You're going into the Spire. Through the cisterns. And you're going to die, all of you, because the inner gate is sealed with the high royal sigil, and none of you can open it."

Silence. Even the raven went still.

Bramble's hatchet lowered an inch. "You know about the gate."

"I know about everything below the Spire that the official maps don't show. I grew up in that building. I used to sneak into the cistern galleries as a child because my nurse couldn't climb ladders." Sable reached into her collar, slowly, telegraphing every movement, and drew out the cord. The royal signet swung free, catching the moonlight—old gold, heavy, the direwolf-and-tower crest of House Aldric stamped deep in wax-colored metal. "This opens it. It's been in my family for six generations, and it has never once left my neck until tonight."

Fen made a small involuntary sound. Wren's sigil-hand dropped to his side.

"That's—" Bramble started.

"The king's seal," Sable said. "Yes. Which brings us to the awkward part of the evening, so let's have it out. You believe there's a man chained beneath the Spire wearing my father's face. I have seen my father's eyes go wrong, and I believe you too. I have believed you since the night your raven quoted a letter no one else has ever read." She looked at Ada, and made herself hold it, made herself say the rest without flinching. "I'm not here to stop you. I'm here because I need to see what's under that gate with my own eyes, and because if there's even a chance my father is alive down there, then every oath I ever swore points down that hole, not up at the throne."

"And if it's a trap?" Ada asked. Her voice was careful now, stripped of its edge, and underneath the care Sable heard something that sounded almost like hope trying very hard not to exist. "If you're Corvane's new trick?"

"Then I'll be standing in a flooded cistern with four criminals when the hunters arrive, which seems like a poor design for a trap." Sable allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Ask me the real question, Miss Vane."

Ada's chin lifted. For a moment she looked younger, and angrier, and much less armored. "Why?" she said. "Why help us? You have everything to lose. You're the princess. Whatever happens down there, you could walk away clean."

"Because I watched my father disappear thirteen years ago while sitting across the breakfast table from him," Sable said, "and I did nothing, because I didn't know what I was seeing. I am not doing nothing twice." She wound the cord around her fist. "Also," and here her voice betrayed her, just slightly, just enough, "because you talk to ravens and rats and probably fish, and you are the first person in thirteen years who has looked at this city and refused to pretend it isn't dying. I find I can't arrest that. I find I want to stand next to it."

The rain started again, fine as sifted flour. Nobody moved.

It was the raven who broke the standoff, dropping from its bracket onto Sable's shoulder with a weight like a verdict, and croaking, in a voice that was almost—but not quite—her father's:

*"The moon keeps its own counsel, little star."*

Sable closed her eyes. When she opened them, Ada was watching her with an expression Sable couldn't name—grief and recognition tangled together, the look of someone seeing their own loneliness in a stranger's face.

"Bramble," Ada said. "She comes."

"Oh, she comes," Bramble growled, retrieving her rope with a yank. "But hear me, Your Highness—if you turn on us down there, I won't kill you. I'll leave you. In the dark. With the things that live in the water." She leaned close. "My brother went down this road two years ago. I intend to find out what happened to him, and I will go through anyone, crowned or otherwise. Are we understood?"

"Perfectly," said Sable. "For what it's worth, Captain—I remember your brother. Tall. Red beard. He asked me once, at a border inspection, whether the king was well. I lied to him. I said yes." She met Bramble's eyes steadily. "I'd like the chance to stop lying."

Something shifted in the smuggler's weathered face—nothing softened, exactly, but something repositioned, the way ballast shifts in a hull. "Down the ladder, then, Highness. And mind the fourth rung. It's been rotten since before you were born."

* * *

They went down into the earth in single file, and the city closed over their heads like water.

The cistern galleries were older than the Spire itself—Aldric's grandfather's grandfather had built them, or so the plaques claimed—and they breathed cold and mineral, the air thick with the smell of wet stone and something underneath it, something sweeter and wronger, like flowers rotting in a locked room. Their lanterns made small trembling islands of light. Beyond them, the dark went on forever, and in the dark, the water moved.

Sable heard it before the others did—or perhaps she simply understood what she was hearing, because she'd grown up on these galleries listening to the pipes sing. The water wasn't flowing the way water flows. It was *hesitating*. Pooling at the edges of their lamplight and drawing back, drawing back, like a crowd making way.

"It knows we're here," Wren whispered. His sigils cast a faint blue wash over his terrified face. "The whole system's connected. Everything that touches the water—"

"It's not hostile," Ada said.

They all turned. She was walking with one bare hand trailing along the slick gallery wall, fingers in the runoff, her head tilted in that listening posture Sable was beginning to know. The raven rode her shoulder with its feathers slicked flat.

"How can you possibly know that?" Sable asked.

"Because it's frightened." Ada's voice was soft, absently gentle, the way she must have spoken to strays her whole hidden life. "It's been bound a long time. Bound things aren't angry, at first. They're lonely. The anger comes later." She paused at a junction where five tunnels branched into the dark, and stood there with her eyes half-closed, reading currents the rest of them couldn't perceive. "It says the guardian at the inner gate hasn't been asked anything in thirteen years. Only commanded." A muscle worked in her jaw. "It says the guardian cries at night, and the water carries the sound all through the city, and no one has ever wondered why the cisterns moan in winter."

Sable felt the words land somewhere beneath her armor. *No one has ever wondered.* How many voices had this city drowned in its long obedience to the ban? How many bound things cried in the dark while a kingdom perfected the art of not hearing?

"That's why they fear you," she said quietly. "Not the magic. The listening."

Ada opened her eyes and looked at her, and in the lantern light her storm-grey gaze was steady and unguarded for the first time since the market. "That's why *I* fear me," she said. "Everything else is just other people's version of it."

Bramble cleared her throat with the delicacy of a landslide. "Touching. Truly. Now—Highness, you claim to know these galleries. The map shows a descent at the Weeping Stair, then a long flooded run to the gate chamber. Is that the way you climbed as a child?"

Sable studied the junction, dredging thirteen-year-old memories up through the silt. "There. Second tunnel. But the stair's been gated since the Regency reforms—they installed iron grates after the flood of my twelfth year." She frowned. "Unless..."

"Unless?" Fen prompted.

"Unless someone removed them recently. From below." Sable crouched, touching the mud at the tunnel mouth, and her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. Boot prints. Fresh ones, pressed deep by someone climbing *up* from the flooded levels—and beside them, stranger marks: long parallel grooves, as if something had been dragged that left no footprints at all. "Someone came up this way. Days ago, maybe less. And they weren't alone."

Wren went grey. "The usurper knows the route."

"The usurper *is* the route," Bramble muttered. "It built half these seals."

Ada knelt beside Sable in the mud, close enough that Sable could smell woodsmoke and river-water on her, and studied the grooves. The raven hopped down and stalked along them, head cocked, then looked up at Ada and made a low sound in its chest—not a word, just a note, a minor key.

"What is it?" Sable asked.

Ada was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone carefully, deliberately calm, which told Sable more than any tremor would have.

"It's the courier," she said. "The man who gave me the map. He died on my doorstep with these same drag-marks near him—I saw them in the mud outside my shop and didn't understand them." She touched the groove lightly, reverently, the way you touch a grave. "Whatever pulled him, it came up from below. It let him escape far enough to deliver this. Or—" her hand closed into a fist, "—it *sent* him. Either way, the thing below the Spire wanted this map in our hands. It wanted us down here."

The water at the tunnel mouths went utterly, perfectly still, five black mirrors reflecting five lantern flames and five faces.

"Then it's a trap," Fen whispered.

"Probably," said Ada. She rose, mud on her knees, and held out her hand to Sable—not for help standing, Sable realized, but palm-up, open, an offer. Above them, twelve days' short of the thirteenth turn, the moon would be rising over a city that had forgotten how to hope. "Are you still with us, Highness?"

Sable looked at that open hand, at the woman who talked to frightened water, at the long dark descending toward her father or the thing wearing him. Treason, she thought. This is what it feels like. It feels like a hand in the dark.

She took it.

"Past the point of no return, Miss Vane," she said, and stepped with them into the tunnel, where the water waited, and the gate, and whatever else the thirteenth turn would bring to light.
