## 13. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The mirrors of the Spire were famous before the war of oaths—before the silence, before the false calm that had settled over Greyhollow like frost on a window. Ada remembered them from her mother's stories: the Hall of Reflections, where ambassadors from nine kingdoms once came to see themselves rendered in silvered glass so clear it was said to show not your face but your intentions.

Her mother had told those stories in a whisper, the way she told all stories about the Spire. *Careful what you show the glass,* she'd say. *Glass remembers.*

Ada crouched now behind a pillar of black marble at the hall's eastern edge, her breath shallow, the raven Bramble's—a smuggler's bird named Soot with opinions like knives—pressed flat against her shoulder. Below, through the colonnade, the Hall of Reflections stretched away in a long corridor of standing mirrors, each one taller than two men, each one dark.

Not reflecting. That was the wrongness that raised the hair on her arms. A hundred mirrors in a torchlit hall, and none of them gave back the light.

"They're covered," Sable breathed beside her. The princess had shed her disguise an hour ago, somewhere between the servant stair and this corridor, trading the gray cloak of a laundress for the fitted leather of someone who had clearly done this kind of trespassing before. It should have surprised Ada. It didn't, quite. There had always been something in Sable's walk—the way she moved through crowds like they were water she'd learned to swim in—that spoke of practice rather than privilege. "Every mirror in the palace is draped. I thought it was mourning."

"For whom?"

"That's what I never understood." Sable's jaw tightened. "Thirteen years of official mourning for a queen who died before my father went quiet. The drapes never came down. When I asked, the chamberlain said the king wished it." She paused. "My father hated mourning. He said grief was a room you visited, not a house you lived in."

*Was,* Ada thought. The word sat heavy between them. They were here to find out whether the man behind the throne-room doors was still a father at all, or only the shape of one—and every step deeper into the Spire made the question worse, because the Spire itself was wrong in small ways. Servants who walked too evenly. Guards who blinked too rarely. A hush in the stones, as if the whole building were holding its breath.

Soot shifted on her shoulder and murmured, barely audible, into her ear: *"Something down there smells like the end of a promise."*

"I know," Ada whispered back.

"You understand it talks to you." Sable wasn't asking. She'd stopped asking somewhere around the cisterns, when Ada had stood hip-deep in black water and asked the river-spirit's bound remnant for passage, and the water had *answered*, and no one on the crew had looked at her the same way since—not with fear, exactly. With recalibration.

"It talks to everything," Ada said. "Most things just don't expect anyone to answer back."

"And the mirrors?"

Ada looked down the colonnade again. Her gift was a quiet thing, usually—a murmur at the edge of hearing, the language of cats and rats and river-water. But the hall below made a sound she'd never heard before. Not silence. *Absence.* Like pressing your ear to a door and hearing not quiet but a deliberate nothing, a held note, a throat closed around a word.

"The mirrors aren't empty," she said slowly. "They're *full*. Something's in them. Something's been poured into them—or poured itself." She flexed her fingers against her knee. "I want to see."

"Ada." Sable caught her wrist. Her grip was warm and certain and had been the single most disorienting fact of Ada's last six days: that the princess's hands were calloused, that she knew the servant tunnels better than the smugglers did, that when Fen died in the drowning dark of the cisterns it had been Sable who pulled Bramble up by the collar, swearing in three languages, none of them courtly. "If there's something in those mirrors, looking might be how it sees us."

"Everything we've done since the courier died has been how it sees us," Ada said. "We're past choosing whether to be seen. We're only choosing whether to look back."

Sable held her gaze for a long moment. Torchlight moved across her face, catching the stubborn line of her jaw—the king's jaw, Ada realized, the same architecture she'd seen in the faded portraits in Bramble's map-room, the face that had launched a thousand loyalist ballads before the silence began.

"All right," Sable said. "But together. And if anything moves in that glass, we run, and we don't stop running until we're back in the cisterns being miserable."

"That's the most royal thing you've ever said."

"I'm full of surprises." But her hand slid down Ada's wrist to her palm, brief and deliberate, and squeezed once before letting go, and Ada's heart did something complicated and inconvenient that she filed away firmly for later, assuming there was a later.

They went down together.

* * *

The Hall of Reflections was colder than the rest of the Spire, cold in a specific way—cold like the far side of a window in winter, cold like standing outside a lit house. Their footsteps rang on marble. The draped mirrors rose around them like shrouded mourners, and up close Ada could see that the cloth covering them was not gray but white, white gone ivory with age, stitched at every edge with sigils she recognized from the binding chains they'd found in the deep rooms below: oath-script, the written language of promises.

Someone had sealed these mirrors the way the true king had been sealed. Someone had bound them shut.

"The night my father changed," Sable said quietly, walking the aisle between them, her voice flat with the effort of steadiness, "I was eleven. There was a storm. I remember lightning through these windows, and the mirrors uncovered, and—" She stopped. Her breath caught audibly. "And I remember my father standing right there, in front of that one, talking to his reflection. Laughing. I'd never seen him laugh like that. And then the lightning came again and he was gone from the hall, and the servants were draping the mirrors, and no one would tell me why, and the next morning he wouldn't look at me. He hasn't looked at me in thirteen years. Not properly. Not the way—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Not the way fathers look."

"Sable—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't be kind to me yet. If you're kind to me yet I'll fall apart in this hallway and I refuse to fall apart until we know." She turned to the nearest draped mirror. "Help me with the cloth."

The sigil-stitching resisted at first—Ada felt it push back against her fingers like a sleeping animal flinching from touch—but her gift had been growing louder all week, fed by the cisterns and the guardian-spirit's freedom and the strange new openness of her own ears, and when she laid her palm flat against the seal and *listened*, really listened, she heard what the stitching was made of.

"These are promises," she breathed. "Written ones. Vows. This thread—it's a wedding vow. This one's a coronation oath. This one—" she touched a line of script gone brown with age, "—this is a child's promise. *I'll come home before the snow.* Someone broke that one."

"People break promises every day," Sable said.

"Yes," Ada said. "And apparently it adds up."

Together they pulled the cloth free.

The mirror did not show them the hall.

It showed a country.

That was the only word for it. The glass was full of moving images, hundreds of them layered like sediment: a farmer's hands planting wheat; a soldier's oath-swollen knuckles; a woman setting a lamp in a window; a fisherman casting a net into grey water; a mother's mouth shaping words to a child; a builder laying a cornerstone; hands clasped over a bargain; lips pressed to a ring; a thousand small ceremonies of faith, of labor, of love—all of it dimmed, all of it *fading*, colors leaching out frame by frame like a fire going down.

And threaded through it all, woven through the images the way a spider's silk threads through a hedge, ran strands of darkness. Not shadow. *Thread.* Black filament, fine as hair, stitching image to image, pulling them taut, drawing them inward toward—

Toward the center of the glass, where something sat.

Ada's mind refused it at first. Her eyes kept trying to make it a person, because its outline was a person's outline, seated, robed, crowned. But the robe was made of the same black thread, and the crown was made of broken rings—wedding bands, signets, oath-coins, all snapped and fused—and the face beneath it was a smooth blank oval like an egg of smoke, featureless except when the light of some dying memory passed across it, and then, for a heartbeat, it wore a borrowed expression. A farmer's squint. A bride's smile. A child's frown.

"Oh," Sable whispered. Her hand had found Ada's without either of them deciding it. "Oh, that's what's wearing my father."

*"It's wearing more than your father,"* Soot rasped from Ada's shoulder, feathers flattened, voice shaking in a way she'd never heard from the vain, sharp-tongued bird. *"Look at the thread. Look where it comes from."*

Ada looked. Followed the filaments outward, past the edges of the seated figure, out of the mirror entirely—they passed through the glass like it was water, thinning to invisibility, spreading through the air of the hall, through the walls, through the city beyond. She couldn't see where they ended. She suspected they didn't end. She suspected that if she followed one far enough it would lead to a farmstead in the eastern valleys where a man had stopped believing his crops would come up, or to a tenement in Lowbridge where a widow had finally stopped setting the lamp in the window, or to a guard post where a young soldier had quietly decided the crown meant nothing, or—

"To everyone," she said aloud. Her voice sounded very small in the cold hall. "It's made of everyone. Every promise this kingdom made and didn't keep. Every vow that got discarded. It's not wearing the king. It's wearing *us*. Thirteen years of us giving up on things, and it wove all of that into a shape, and it sat down on the throne because nobody else would sit there and mean it."

"That's not possible," Sable said, but her voice said she already believed it. "Magic's been banned for fifteen years. The purge took every mage, every charm, every—"

"Promises aren't magic," Ada said. "Or they're the oldest magic. The kind nobody can ban, because everybody does it. You don't need a sigil to swear an oath. You just need to mean it—and then to fail." She stared at the seated figure, at the crown of broken rings, and felt something turn over in her chest, horror and pity tangled so tightly she couldn't tell which was which. "Fifteen years ago they outlawed magic. Thirteen years ago the famine came. And a kingdom full of frightened people started breaking their promises—to each other, to the land, to their dead, to their future. All that broken meaning had to go somewhere."

The figure in the glass turned its head.

It was a slow movement, terrible in its patience, the blank oval face rotating toward them across whatever distance existed inside the mirror—which was no distance at all, which was every distance, which was the space between a promise made and a promise broken.

Ada's whole body screamed at her to run. She didn't run. Later she would try to explain why and fail; in the moment there was simply no running in her, the way there is no running in someone standing at a sickbed. The creature's face had no eyes, and it looked directly at her anyway, and the pressure of that attention was physical, a hand laid flat against her sternum.

Then, impossibly, horribly, the blank face began to change.

Features surfaced in the smoke like drowned things rising: a brow, cheekbones, a jaw she knew—knew from portraits, from the stubborn line of the woman beside her, from thirteen years of coins and statues and official portraits in every government office in Greyhollow. King Aldric's face assembled itself in the glass, gentle and kingly and *wrong*, the smile a half-beat too slow, the warmth arriving like lamplight behind frosted panes.

"There you are," it said. Its voice was every voice. It was the herald crying the market news; it was a mother calling a child home; it was the courthouse clerk reading sentences; it was a thousand small civic sounds braided into one warm, reasonable tone. "The little listener. And the daughter." The Aldric-face tilted, and something moved behind it, in it, the black thread shifting under the skin like veins of ink. "Thirteen years I have worn this hall's silence. Do you know how long I listened to no one? Even the king stopped speaking to me, once the bargain was struck. Even him."

"What bargain?" Sable's voice came out steady, which cost Ada everything to witness, because she could feel the tremor in the hand gripping hers. "What did you do to my father?"

"Your father," the thing said, with something that in any other mouth might have been fondness, "made me. As much as anyone did. Ask him, when you find him. Ask him what he promised. Ask him what he was willing to give." The face rippled. For one flickering instant it was not Aldric at all but a hundred faces at once, a crowd, a kingdom, mouths open. "You came all this way through my drowned halls, past my sleeping river, past my faithful Corvane. You are not thieves. You are not assassins. So tell me, little listener—"

The mirrors down the entire hall began, one by one, to shed their cloths. White drapes slithered to the marble like molting skins. A hundred panes of glass woke, and every one of them filled with the same sedimentary country, the same fading memories, the same black thread—and in every mirror, in every frame, the seated figure turned to face them, crowned in broken rings, wearing the king's almost-smile.

"—what is it you want?"

The question landed in Ada like a hook. Because it was *her* question, the one she'd promised herself she'd ask when she finally stood before the usurper, the weapon she'd been carrying since the cisterns since the rats since the bloodstained map—and it had taken it from her mouth before she could speak, or it had read it off her heart, or it had simply gotten there first because it had been waiting thirteen years for someone, anyone, to ask *it* the same thing.

"I—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "I want the truth."

"Ah." The Aldric-face closed its eyes, and the expression on it was so precisely, so devastatingly *tired* that Ada's pity surged up and drowned her fear for one full second. "Truth. Yes. That was the first promise ever made to me, do you know? Before I had a shape. *We will always tell you the truth.* A king to his people, people to their king. First vow, oldest thread." The eyes opened. They were not eyes. They were two small mirrors, and in them Ada saw herself, tiny and pale and reflected infinitely. "It was also the first one broken."

The torches guttered, all along the hall, all at once.

In the sudden dark, the creature's voice came from everywhere—from the glass, from the walls, from the black thread she now realized ran through the mortar of the Spire itself, through the whole city, through the ground:

"But you asked. After thirteen years, someone finally asked me something real. So I will give you a gift, little listener, daughter-of-the-oath. I will let you keep your truth. Go down. Go to the binding room. Ask the king what he traded me, and ask him why he begged me—*begged* me—to take it." A pause, vast and amused and grieving all at once. "And then come back and ask me your question. I find I am curious what you'll want, once you know what he wanted."

The mirrors went dark. Not covered—*dark*, every pane becoming a slab of night, and in the deepest center of the nearest one, two small reflections watched them go: a beast-speaker and a princess, hand in hand, small as figures on a coin.

Sable pulled her toward the servant stair. They ran. Neither of them spoke until they were three levels down, lungs burning, torchlight ordinary again, the world reassuringly made of stone instead of thread.

"He wants us to go down," Sable said finally, bent double, hands on knees. "He's letting us through. Why would he—"

"Because he thinks what we find down there will break us," Ada said. Her heart was still hammering, but underneath the fear, cold and clear as well-water, a thought had formed that she couldn't shake. "Or because he wants witnesses. Sable—he said *ask him what he begged*. Kings don't beg. Unless—"

Unless the rescue target doesn't want rescuing. Unless the man in the chains had chained himself.

Sable straightened slowly. In the dim light her face had gone the color of the drained mirrors, and when she spoke, her voice was the voice of an eleven-year-old in a lightning-lit hall, watching her father laugh at his own reflection.

"Then let's go ask him," she said, "what my father threw away."

And far above them, faint as a memory of a memory, the mirrors laughed.
