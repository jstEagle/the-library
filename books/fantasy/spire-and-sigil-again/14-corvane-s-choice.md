## 14. Corvane's Choice

The order came at the turn of the third bell, written in the king's own hand and delivered by a page who would not meet his eyes.

*Seal the spire. All gates, all doors, all ways in or out. Let no one pass until the matter within is resolved.*

Corvane read it twice by lamplight in the guard barracks, and both times his eye snagged on the same word. *Resolved.* Not *apprehended*. Not *captured*. Resolved, the way one resolves a debt, or a fever, or a rat problem. The way one resolves a thing one does not intend to look at afterward.

The page shifted from foot to foot. "Sergeant? His Majesty requires confirmation of—"

"I'll confirm nothing," Corvane said. "Tell him the order is received."

The boy fled. Corvane sat a while longer with the paper in his hands, feeling its weight wrong for what it was. A single sheet. It should have weighed more. Orders like this ought to arrive on stone tablets, heavy enough to break the hands that carried them, so that everyone involved understood what they were holding.

Around him the barracks slept, or pretended to. Somewhere above, the spire rose against the moonless dark, its crown lit with that pale, sourceless glow that had appeared three nights ago and that no one in the palace would speak of aloud. The astrologers had locked themselves in their tower. The chambermaids had started leaving offerings of bread and salt outside the throne room doors, an old country superstition, done furtively, like children leaving milk for something under the stairs.

And somewhere beneath all of it, in the drowned dark of the cisterns, five intruders were climbing toward the heart of the kingdom with a map drawn in blood.

Corvane knew their names now. He'd had two days and the full apparatus of the Crown's intelligence to learn them, and learn them he had, because that was what he did, because thoroughness was the only virtue he had never once failed to practice. Bramble Quill, smuggler captain, wanted in four ports for crimes he privately considered civic services. Fen Marsh, river-rat turned lockbreaker, no family, no record beyond petty theft. Two exiled mages whose files were thin as winter ice. And Ada Vane.

He spread her file open on the table again, though he had long since memorized it. Charm-seller. Quiet. Fifteen years in Greyhollow without so much as a jaywalking fine. And then, eleven days ago, in front of forty witnesses and a detection charm, she had spoken to a cat in a voice that made grown men weep and made the cat sit down and listen like a scholar at lecture.

He had been there. He had watched her kneel in the wreckage of the market square with blood on her hands—the cat's blood, though the beast had lived—and speak to it, and he had felt something move through the crowd that he had not felt since he was a boy. Not fear. The crowd had been afraid, yes; fear was cheap and always available. But underneath the fear, in a dozen faces, he had seen the other thing. Recognition. The look of people remembering that the world was larger than the laws written about it.

He should have arrested her then. He had had her wrist in reach. Instead he had stood in the trampled cabbage leaves and watched her go, and told himself it was tactical, that a sergeant alone did not take a mage, that he needed backup, that there would be time.

There had been time. He had spent it following her instead of catching her.

Corvane closed the file. On the cover, stamped in red wax, was the sigil of the Crown: the spire and the star, the mark he had sworn beneath at nineteen years old, kneeling on cold flagstones before a woman in a grey dress with flour still on her sleeves.

That was the part no one remembered now, the part the histories had already begun to sand smooth: Queen Maren had not worn ceremony when she administered oaths. She had come down from the dais herself, because she said a vow given to a symbol was given to nothing, and she had taken each new guard's hands in hers—flour and all, she baked her own bread every morning of her life until the famine years—and she had said, *You do not swear to me. I am temporary. You swear to what this crown is supposed to mean. Protect the small against the large. Tell the truth even to power, especially to power. And if ever the wearer of this crown and the meaning of it come apart, you choose the meaning. That is the whole oath. Everything else is decoration.*

She had died in the second year of the famine. Or that was the story. Corvane had carried her coffin, or a coffin, and he had never been permitted to see the body, and for thirteen years he had kept that fact folded away in the same drawer as a hundred others—the astrologer's midnight meetings, the king's sudden left-handedness, the way the royal signature changed its slant the same season the ban on magic went up—all the small wrongnesses a loyal man files under *not my concern* until the drawer swells and groans and will not shut.

The order on his table would not shut the drawer. The order had kicked it open.

*Let the intruders die inside.*

Not capture. Not trial. The word *die* was not actually written anywhere in the document—it was written in the space around the words, in the seal-the-doors-and-wait architecture of it, in the fact that the cisterns' floodgates had already been opened by someone with authority over waterworks that answered only to the Crown. Corvane had checked. Of course he had checked. The sluices at the Grayfall intake had been cranked wide two hours before the order reached him. Whatever was happening below the spire right now was happening underwater.

He thought about the rats. That was the detail that kept returning to him, gnawing at him like—well. Like a rat. In fifteen years of policing Greyhollow's markets he had learned things the academy didn't teach, and one of them was that you could read a city by its vermin. Rats fled where poison was laid. Rats fled fire, flood, collapse. Three nights ago, the rats of the Spire district had come up out of the ground in their hundreds and run west, away from the palace, in a river of small brown bodies that had emptied half the taverns in the quarter with screaming. He had filed a report. The report had been returned to him unfiled, with a note in a clerk's neat hand: *Vermin migration is not a security matter.*

Something below the spire frightened the rats more than daylight did. Something down there wore a warm shape and smelled of cold—that phrase had come back to him too, from an informant, a sewer-keeper who talked to his charges the way some men talked to horses, and who had gone white as tallow repeating it before Corvane paid him to forget he'd said it.

And into that darkness, five people had gone. One of them a charm-seller who spoke to beasts. One of them—he was nearly certain, though the evidence was circumstantial and he hated himself for how carefully he'd gathered it—the princess.

Sable. Thirteen years old when her father stopped being her father, whatever had happened in that season; twenty-six now, and moving through the palace like a ghost auditing its own haunting. Corvane had watched her for years the way he watched everything, professionally, dispassionately, and had catalogued the details: the princess who never used the word *father* anymore, only *the king*, with a small flat pause before it; the princess who interviewed servants about the famine years with the gentle relentlessness of an inquisitor; the princess who had vanished from her rooms last night, leaving behind a decoy of pillows and a signet ring missing from its case.

The signet. That was the piece that made his stomach drop. The final gate below the spire, according to the architect's plans he had pulled from the restricted archive—a violation, another one, the list was getting long—required royal blood-seal. The intruders had no royal blood. Unless they had acquired some.

She had joined them. The fool girl had climbed down into a flooding dark with smugglers and a beast-speaker, carrying the one key that mattered, and the Crown's response, drafted in the king's own hand, was to seal the doors and let the water rise.

*Resolved.*

Corvane got up. He put on his coat. He took his sword because a sergeant without a sword was just a man with opinions, and he took the file on Ada Vane because he found he could not leave it lying in a barracks where the next purge might find it, and he took one other thing from the bottom of his footlocker, wrapped in oilcloth, which he had not unwrapped in thirteen years.

Then he went out into the dark, toward the spire, to commit treason.

* * *

The servant's door was in the north face of the spire's foundation, behind the old kitchens, and it was guarded by two men under his command, which was either luck or design, and Corvane had stopped believing in luck around the same time he'd stopped believing in the king.

Private Hollis and Private Wren. Hollis was nineteen and worshipped him. Wren was fifty and had served the old queen, and had never once said so in thirteen years, but Corvane had seen the way the man stood at attention when anyone mentioned Maren's name—as if the name itself required a straighter spine.

"Status," Corvane said.

"Quiet, Sergeant," Hollis reported. "Water's rising in the lower galleries, we can hear it through the stone. No movement since the last sweep."

"You've had no further orders?"

"Seal holds till dawn," Wren said. His eyes flicked to the paper in Corvane's hand. "That them?"

"This is them."

Wren looked at the order for a long moment. Then he looked at Corvane, and something passed between the two old soldiers that had no words in it, or rather had all the words in it, compressed to silence, the way a lifetime of shared service compresses.

"The kitchens flood first," Wren said, conversationally, to no one in particular. "Always did. Queen couldn't abide waste, so she had the drains built deep. There's a service stair behind the flour store runs all the way down to the cistern gallery. Dry, mostly. She had it built during the famine, so's food could be moved without going past the throne room." He scratched his jaw. "Man might wonder why a door like that gets bricked over by a king who never eats."

Hollis was looking between them with the expression of a young man watching weather change. "Sergeant?"

"Private," Corvane said, "in approximately one minute, I am going to walk around to the flour store. While I am walking, this door will be unattended. I have no idea how long I'll be. Could be an hour. Could be till morning." He met Hollis's eyes. "Do you understand me?"

Hollis's throat worked. "That's dereliction, Sergeant."

"It is."

"And worse, if they catch—" The boy stopped. Corvane watched him do the arithmetic, watched him arrive at the sum that Corvane himself had spent all night arriving at: that the order was sealed doors and drowning, that the princess was below, that the word *resolved* meant what it meant, and that a soldier's choices in the coming hour were going to be counted by someone, someday, in a ledger that mattered more than any court-martial.

"If they catch us," Hollis said slowly, "we were checking a noise. We heard a noise at the door."

"There it is," Wren said. "A noise. Terrible thing, noises." He spat, philosophically. "Queen always said the worst day of a soldier's life is the day the crown asks him to be small."

Corvane walked around to the flour store.

* * *

The brickwork over the old stair had been done hastily and recently—he could tell by the mortar, too pale, too quick-set—and it came away under his crowbar in slabs rather than courses, as if whoever sealed it had known in their bones that it was a shameful act and had wanted it deniable. Behind the brick, dust. Behind the dust, air moving faintly, cold and mineral, tasting of deep water. And under the taste of water, something else. Something that made the hair on his arms lift and his hindbrain whisper *run*, the way it whispered near open graves.

He ignored it. He was getting good at ignoring things; he'd had thirteen years of practice, and tonight he intended to stop.

Before he descended, he unwrapped the oilcloth bundle he had carried from the barracks.

It was a sword belt. Old leather, cracked at the folds, the buckle tarnished green. It had belonged to no soldier; it had hung in the queen's private armory, on a peg, beside a short ceremonial blade she'd been gifted at her coronation and never worn. When the palace purged her effects in the second year—when her portrait came down from the great hall and her gardens were plowed under for the grain stores and her name became a thing said only in whispers—Corvane had been the guard assigned to inventory the removals. He had signed for everything. He had signed for one item twice.

He had told himself for thirteen years that he'd taken it for safekeeping. Evidence, perhaps. Historical preservation. Any of the respectable words. Tonight, standing at the top of a hidden stair with water thundering somewhere far below, he finally admitted what it was.

It was a promise he'd been keeping in escrow. Held in trust. Until such time as the crown and its meaning came apart, and a man had to choose which one he'd actually sworn to.

He buckled the old belt over his uniform, where no one would see it, where it didn't need to be seen. Some oaths were load-bearing whether or not they were visible.

Then he took the lamp and went down.

* * *

The stair was sound. That surprised him; he had expected rot, collapse, the general decay of anything the present kingdom neglected. But Maren's builders had built for famine and siege, and the steps rang solid under his boots, and the ventilation shafts still drew breath, and twice he passed niches in the walls where emergency stores had once lain—empty now, but swept clean, maintained, as if someone had kept faith with this place even after bricking it shut.

Someone had known it was here. Someone had kept it ready. He filed that away with everything else.

The sound of water grew as he descended, from thunder to roar to a vast grinding rush that he felt in his teeth before he heard it, and the temperature fell, and the lamp flame leaned steadily toward the depths as if it too were being pulled down. At the last turning, where the stair met a gallery overlooking the cistern level, he stopped and raised the light.

Below him, the drowned city under the city. Arches marching away into blackness, water churning through them waist-high and rising, white-capped, furious, funneled from the opened sluices into channels never meant to carry this much. And along the one high causeway that remained above the flood, small and distant and unmistakable, figures moving. Five of them. Four upright, one supported between two others, limping. Heading for the inner gate, where the pale glow of the spire's heart bled down through the stone like light through a bruise.

He could not see faces at this distance. He did not need to. The tall one giving orders in a smuggler's cadence was Quill. The slight figure beside her, hood back, hair dark as spilled ink, moved with a bearing that no disguise survived—twenty-six years of being trained to walk like the floor owed her deference.

Princess Sable Vane-Aldric, wading through treason toward her father's prison, and the whole weight of the Crown arrayed to drown her for it.

Corvane stood in the dark with the lamp in one hand and his sword hilt under the other, and he did the last of his arithmetic.

If he went back up, sealed the stair, reported the breach, he would be the most loyal officer in the palace by tomorrow's light. Promotion. Commendation. Perhaps a place close to the throne, close enough to learn at last what wore it. And the water would keep rising, and the causeway would go under, and the matter would be resolved, and he would spend the rest of his life—which might be long; traitors to their own conscience often lived long—knowing exactly which side of the queen's oath he had chosen when it counted.

If he went down—

Down was not a plan. Down was a lamp, a sword, and a choice already made.

He was reaching for the rail to descend when the water spoke.

He would deny it to his dying day, deny it the way he'd denied every strange thing he'd ever half-seen in fifteen years of Greyhollow nights—but the flood below stilled, all at once, across the whole vast gallery, flat and silent as hammered tin, and out of that silence came a voice. Not through the air. Through the stone, through the soles of his boots, up his shins and spine, a voice like current over gravel, and it said:

*Small warm one on the stair. She said you would come. The listener said you would come. She is busy with the gate. I am telling you: hurry.*

Corvane stood very still. Then, because he was a man who followed orders all his life and saw no reason to stop with the orders had gotten strange, he hurried.

Behind him, far above, the moon began its twelfth turn.
