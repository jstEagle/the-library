## 15. The Binding Room

The river-spirit's passage opened like a throat.

One moment they were wading through the last flooded gallery of the cisterns, water to their thighs and cold as a grave-digger's promise; the next, the current parted around them in a slow spiral, drawing back from a stair of black stone that rose out of the flood like the spine of some drowned animal. The spirit had kept its word. Ada had felt it keeping its word—a pressure against her mind like a hand on her shoulder, vast and patient and terribly sad, and then a voice that was not a voice at all but a sensation of being *moved*, gently, toward the dry dark above.

*The gate remembers your promise,* it had said, or something like said. *I remember mine. Go.*

Now the stair climbed through rock that sweated moisture, and Bramble went first with a lantern held low, and Sable went second with the royal signet clutched so tightly in her fist that Ada could hear the metal creaking against her rings, and Fen's absence walked behind all of them like a fourth traveler no one would name.

They had buried what they could carry of him. The rest stayed in the water, which Ada privately thought was where he'd have wanted to be—Fen, who had complained about every wet sock and every cold swim and who had gone into the flood without hesitation when the ceiling came down over Bramble's head. Ada kept hearing his voice in the echo of their footsteps. *Mind the third step, it's rotten. Mind the third step.* He'd said that on the first day, joking. She heard it now on every landing.

"Stairs end ahead," Bramble murmured. "Feel that?"

Ada felt it. A wrongness in the air, subtle as a changed key in a familiar song. The dampness thinned. The stone warmed under her palm by a single degree—not comfortable warmth, not living warmth, but the warmth of something that had been told to simulate it and was doing its best. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, a sound she couldn't name until they reached the top of the stair and she understood she'd been hearing it for an hour: scratching. Endless, patient, rhythmic scratching, like a pen moving across paper, like ten thousand pens, like a scriptorium buried alive.

The door at the top of the stairs was iron, and it was covered in writing.

Not carved—*written*, in ink that had soaked into the metal itself, lines and lines of small precise script flowing across every surface, around hinges, through keyholes, down onto the floor and up the walls beyond, so that the door seemed less like an object than like a page torn from an enormous book and hung here as a barrier. Ada's skin prickled. She knew sigil-work when she saw it; she'd spent fifteen years learning to recognize it so she could avoid it. But this was nothing like the crude detection charms the hunters carried or the ward-marks stamped on city gates. This was old. This was careful. And every line of it, when she let her gift brush against it the way she'd touch a strange dog before petting—

Every line of it was a promise.

"I can read some of it," Sable whispered. She had gone pale in the lantern light. Her fingers hovered over the door without touching. "*I swear to hold the harvest against the winter. I swear to keep the bridges standing. I swear to answer every petition brought before me, even unto my dying breath.* These are coronation oaths. Father's oaths." Her voice caught on the word. "There are thousands of them."

"There are thirteen years of them," Bramble said grimly. She set her lantern down and drew a knife—not to cut the door, Ada realized, but to pry at the hinge, testing. "My brother wrote me about oath-chains once. Before he vanished. Said the old mages used them for things too dangerous to cage and too valuable to kill. You don't bind the body. You bind the *word*. Everything a thing has ever promised, made solid, made heavy, wrapped around it until it can't move for the weight of its own given word." She looked back at them, and in the lantern light her face was older than Ada had ever seen it. "You can't cut these chains. You can only take them back. And whoever holds the other end of the promises—" she tapped the signet in Sable's fist "—has to be the one to do the taking."

Sable swallowed. Nodded. Pressed the signet to the center of the door.

For a moment, nothing. Then the ink began to move.

It flowed away from the signet like water from a lifted stone, gathering into rivulets, then streams, then rivers of written light that poured off the door and spiraled upward into the darkness beyond—and the door swung inward without a sound, and the scratching stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the scratching. It was the silence of a room holding its breath.

Bramble took up her lantern. "Together," she said, which was captain-speak for *I'm frightened too*, and they went in.

* * *

The Binding Room was round, and it was full of light, and none of it came from any lamp.

Oath-script crawled the walls in slow golden rivers, circling, always circling, converging at last upon the center of the chamber—upon the figure bound there, suspended a hand's breadth above the floor in chains that were not chains. They were ribbons of light and ink, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a sentence, each one anchored somewhere in the walls, and they wrapped the man at the center so completely that at first Ada could see only fragments: a hand, gray and still. A shoulder. A length of hair, white where portraits in the market squares showed gold. A face, half-turned toward them, eyes closed.

King Aldric. Thirteen years missing from his kingdom, and here he was, hanging in the heart of the spire like a fly in amber made of words.

"He's alive," Sable breathed. She was already moving forward, signet raised, tears cutting clean tracks down her dust-streaked face. "Father. *Father.* It's Sable. We've come to—"

"Don't."

The voice was ash and rust. It came from the bound king, whose eyes had opened—gray eyes, faded as old coin, and in them Ada saw something that stopped her own feet mid-stride. Not gratitude. Not hope.

Terror.

"Sable." His lips cracked around the name. "Sweet girl. My girl. You should not be here. None of you should be here." His gaze found Ada, and whatever he saw in her face—the beast-speaker's mark of attention, perhaps, the way she listened with more than her ears—made him close his eyes again as if in pain. "You've come to unbind me. I can see it. Don't. Please. Leave the chains. Leave me. Go home while the doors are still open behind you."

"We didn't lose four people getting this far to walk away because you're feeling modest," Bramble snapped, but her voice shook.

"It is not modesty." Aldric's laugh was a terrible sound, dry as pages turning. "Child of the gull-wing, look at me. *Look* at me." He turned his face fully into the light, and Ada looked, and understood, and wished she hadn't. There was nothing behind his eyes. Not emptiness exactly—worse. Patience. The infinite, exhausted patience of a man who had been listening to his own promises recited back to him for thirteen years, every vow he'd ever spoken, every oath, every casual assurance—I'll be home for supper, I'll protect you, I swear it, I swear it, I swear it—each one a ribbon, each one a weight, each one true, and that was the horror of it. Oath-chains could only bind with truth. Every link was something he had actually meant.

"You think you're rescuing a prisoner," he said softly. "I am not a prisoner. I am a *payment*. Now untie your tongues and listen, because I will say this once, and then I will spend what strength I have left pretending I never said it at all."

* * *

He told them about the famine.

He told it plainly, the way men tell stories they have told themselves ten thousand times in the dark, and the golden light of the oath-ribbons moved on the walls around him like a congregation nodding along.

"The winter of the gray blight. You were children; you won't remember how bad it was. Three harvests rotted in the ground. The granaries empty. The wells tasting of ash. I watched a mother in the lower city trade her daughter's coat for half a loaf, and the bread-seller wept while he took it, because his own children were hungry too, and there was simply *nothing*. Nothing anywhere. I rode out to every village. I prayed in every shrine. I spent the treasury on grain from kingdoms that had none to spare, and I swore oaths—gods, the oaths I swore. To feed them. To save them. To do *anything*." His voice frayed. "And then it came to me. Or I came to it. I honestly no longer know which way that meeting ran.

"The thing below. The one that wears my crown now. It was already here—it had been here since before the spire, sleeping in the foundations, made of... of leftovers. Of every promise a kingdom ever made and broke. Every soldier's widow cheated of her pension. Every treaty signed and shredded. Every time this land said *we will care for our people* and did not. It is made of broken words, and it is *hungry* the way only a debt is hungry, and it offered me a bargain." The ribbons tightened almost imperceptibly as he spoke, as if the room itself were leaning in. "It could end the famine. It had that power—power enough to make three dead harvests live, power enough to fill the wells with sweet water. In exchange..."

"In exchange you gave it the crown," Sable whispered.

"I gave it everything I had promised and could not deliver." His gray eyes found hers, and for one moment something lived in them again—something broken and loving and unbearably tired. "That is what an oath-chain is, my darling girl. It doesn't hold me *here*. It holds me *together*. Every ribbon is a promise I made to someone—to the farmers, to the soldiers, to your mother, to *you*—and if you unbind them, if you take those promises back..." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ada had ever seen. "Then I stop being anything at all. The chains aren't my prison, Sable. They're the last pieces of me. And the famine ends, and the kingdom lives, and the thing wearing my face rules gently, because I made it promise to. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain. I just never expected anyone to come looking."

The silence rang.

Ada became aware, distantly, that she was crying—that they all were, even Bramble, who had turned her face to the wall and stood with her jaw set like a woman holding a door shut against a storm. Thirteen years. Thirteen years of a king choosing, daily, hourly, to hang in the dark rather than take back his word. And the city above them, the city that had outlawed magic and hunted gifted children into cellars and alleys—Ada thought of fifteen years of hiding, of whispering to cats behind locked doors, of the slow suffocation of a life built on fear—and all of it, all of it, had grown like moss on top of this one man's sacrifice, this one bargain struck in desperation and honored past all reason.

"No," Ada said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak. Her voice came out rough and low, the voice she used for frightened animals.

"No, that's not what happened. That's not the whole of it." She stepped forward, into the golden light, close enough to see the individual sentences winding around his wrists. *I will protect them. I will not fail them. I will be worthy.* "You made a bargain with a thing made of broken promises, and you paid it with kept ones. But you didn't ask it what it wanted. Did you? All those years ago—you bargained, you paid, you chained yourself up. But you never once asked it *why*. What it was. What it needed. Because you were afraid of the answer."

Aldric stared at her. "Girl, I don't know what you—"

"I talk to animals." It came out flat, unhidden, for the first time in fifteen years said aloud in front of strangers without flinching. "Rats and ravens and cats and rivers. And I'll tell you what fifteen years of listening taught me that ruling never did: everything that hurts is hurting *for a reason*. Even the thing that bites. Especially the thing that bites." She looked up at the web of light, at the king hanging in it like a saint in a burning window. "You ended the famine. Fine. Noble. But you left the wound open underneath, and it's been bleeding for thirteen years, and it's going to take the whole city down with it when the moon finishes turning unless somebody finally does what you were too afraid to do."

"And what is that?" he asked, and his voice had gone very quiet.

"*Ask it what it wants.*"

The oath-light flickered. Somewhere far above them, through miles of stone, Ada felt—or imagined she felt—an enormous attention turn toward them, slow as a whale surfacing, curious and cold and desperately, achingly lonely.

Aldric's face crumpled. For a heartbeat he was not a king at all, only an old man in chains of his own making, and when he spoke again his voice was barely air.

"If you unbind me, I unravel. If you leave me bound, the usurper completes its thirteenth turn and the bargain seals forever, and everything I gave becomes permanent—including its rule. Those are your choices. I have had thirteen years to weigh them." He closed his eyes. "Choose to go home. Please. Let an old man keep the only thing he has left."

Bramble's knife-hand flexed. Sable's grip on the signet whitened. And Ada stood in the golden light with the weight of two impossible choices pressing down on her—and felt, rising through the soles of her boots, through the ancient stone, faint and unmistakable:

Scratching. Pen on paper. Writing.

Something above them was adding new lines to the story.

And it had just written their names.
