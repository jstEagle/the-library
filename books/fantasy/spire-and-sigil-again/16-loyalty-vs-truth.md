## 16. Loyalty vs. Truth

Sable had imagined this moment a hundred times in the dark of her rooms, and in none of those imaginings did her father look like this.

The binding room sat beneath the Spire's foundations, older than the palace above it, its walls carved with script that predated the kingdom itself. Chains of written oaths hung from the ceiling like a curtain of dead moths—thousands of them, each strip of vellum inked in a hand she recognized as her father's, each one glowing faintly with the light of a promise kept too well. And in the center of it all, King Aldric Vane-Adair sat on a stone bench with his wrists crossed in his lap, watching them come with eyes that were clear and tired and terribly, horribly sane.

"You shouldn't have come," he said. His voice was rust. "The twelfth turn is four days away. If the binding breaks before the creature has fully released its claim, everything it swallowed comes back at once. The famine. The plague winter. Every broken oath the city ever fed it." He shook his head slowly. "I traded my crown to keep those things buried. I would trade it again."

Sable stood very still. Behind her, she heard Ada's breath catch—the small, involuntary sound of someone whose whole plan had just been taken apart bolt by bolt.

"You *chose* this," Sable said. It wasn't a question. She made it not a question because if it became one, she would have to hear the answer twice.

"I chose to stop being king so that the kingdom would survive me." Her father smiled, and it was her father's smile, the one from before, the one that used to mean *come here, little storm, let me show you the stars*. "You were nine years old. You'd stopped eating. The whole city had. Do you remember the winter the river ran thin? I remember it. I remember every hour of it, because I was the one who went down into the dark and asked what could be done, and something answered, and the price was—" he lifted his bound wrists slightly, an absurd little shrug, "—this."

"You could have told someone. You could have told *me*."

"And you would have done exactly what you're doing now," Aldric said gently. "You would have come for me. You would have broken it open and let thirteen years of grief and hunger pour back into Greyhollow like a burst dam. I lied to you, Sable, because the truth was a door I couldn't let you open." His gaze moved past her, to Ada, to Bramble's grim face, to the raven hunched on Ada's shoulder with its feathers slicked flat. "All of you. Everything you've risked—I'm grateful, and I'm sorry, and I need you to walk away."

"No," said Ada.

It was the first thing she'd said since they entered, and it came out flat and certain, the way she spoke to frightened animals. Sable turned to look at her—at the charm-seller with the ink-stained fingers and the impossible green eyes, who had walked into treason for a man she'd never met.

"You made a bargain out of fear," Ada said. "Fear makes bad contracts. Ask any rat."

* * *

They retreated up the service stairs to argue where the chains couldn't hear, and that was where the fracture came—not between Sable and her father, but between Sable and everyone else.

"The binding holds until the thirteenth turn," Bramble was saying, pacing the narrow landing, her boots ringing on old stone. "That's what the map says, that's what the rats say, that's what your astrologer's countdown says. Four days. We came here to unbind him, and now he's asking us not to, and you want to just—*leave*?"

"I want to think!" Sable's voice cracked against the walls. She pressed her back to the cold brick. "Do you understand what he told me down there? Thirteen years. Thirteen years I lit candles for him. I wrote him letters he'll never read. I learned statecraft from a *thing wearing his face*, and defended its edicts in council, and—" Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake as though they belonged to a stranger. "Every memory I have of my father since I was sixteen is a lie. And now the true memories are lies too, because he *chose* this, he chose it over telling me, over trusting me—"

"Sable." Ada's voice, quiet.

"Don't." Sable held up a hand. "Don't be gentle with me right now. Not you."

"Why not me?"

"Because you knew!" It tore out of her before she could dress it in anything kinder. "Bramble told me. On the cistern stairs, while Fen's body was still cold behind us—she told me you've had your gift since you were a child. Fifteen years, Ada. Fifteen years of sleeping beside us, fighting beside us, letting Corvane nearly kill that cat, letting *me* put myself on the block for you—and never once saying *I am the most illegal thing in this room*."

The landing went silent. Somewhere far above, water moved through the Spire's pipes like a slow heartbeat.

Ada didn't flinch. That was the worst part, Sable thought later. She just stood there in the lamplight and took it, the way she took everything, with that stillness that wasn't emptiness but endurance.

"That's fair," Ada said.

"It's *fair*?"

"Yes. It's fair." Ada folded her arms, unfolded them. "But before you decide what my silence means, you should know what it cost. Because you've been lied to by a king who loves you, and I've been lying to people I love, and I promise you they feel nothing alike from the inside."

"Then tell me how they feel."

Ada looked at her for a long moment. Then she slid down the wall and sat, right there on the dirty landing, skirts in the dust, like a woman setting down a load she'd carried so long her spine had grown around it.

"When I was eight years old," she said, "my mother sold charms at a stall two streets from mine. Real ones. And one morning the sigil-hunters came, and she pushed me under the table and told me to hold my tongue no matter what I heard, and I heard her die. They didn't even use magic to do it. They used a hook and rope, because magic is the crime but cruelty is just procedure." The raven made a low sound and pressed itself against her neck. "Afterward, an old woman who owed my mother a debt taught me the rule that kept me alive: *never speak where ears can find you*. Fifteen years, Sable. Do you know what fifteen years of silence does? It doesn't stay quiet. It grows teeth. Every kindness I accepted from the crew, every meal Bramble cooked, every time Fen called me 'little sister'—I was stealing it. I was taking love under false names. And every night I told myself: tomorrow I'll tell them. Tomorrow. Because tomorrow they can't hate me yet."

Her voice didn't break. It just got very small.

"And then a dying man bled on my doorstep and handed me a map, and suddenly the hiding was useful, and I told myself the quest excused it. But that's a lie too. The truth is I hid because hiding was the only shape my life had left. Your father hid inside a bargain. I hid inside a shop. We're both cowards who dressed it up as protection." She looked up, and her green eyes were wet and furious and steady all at once. "So yes. Be angry. You're right to be. But don't you dare decide my silence meant I loved you less. It meant I loved you more than I knew how to survive."

* * *

Sable walked away from her.

She told herself it was to stand watch at the stairhead, which was even true, mostly. She stood with her back to the landing and listened to the drip of water and tried to assemble herself out of the pieces.

*A king who chose his chains. A charm-seller who chose her silence. A crew that followed a map into drowning water and lost Fen to it.* Everyone she had trusted had been keeping a private darkness, and the terrible, humiliating discovery underneath all her anger was this: so had she. She had known something was wrong with her father for years—the flicker of wrongness in his eyes, the calm that was too calm—and she had buried it, because the alternative was unthinkable. She had hidden her doubt the way Ada hid her gift. The way her father hid his crown.

We are all of us vaults, she thought. And we resent most in others the locks we built ourselves.

Footsteps behind her. Soft ones. Ada came and stood at the rail a arm's length away, not touching, looking out at the black throat of the stairwell.

"For what it's worth," Ada said, "the cat knew. About my gift. Knew for years and never told anyone. Animals don't consider it a betrayal—they consider it manners." A pause. "He also thinks you're too hard on yourself. He says you smell like someone who apologizes in her sleep."

Despite everything—a laugh escaped Sable, ragged and unwilling. "Your cat has opinions about me."

"My cat has opinions about everyone. Mostly complaints. Yours are unusually gentle."

Sable turned. In the lamplight Ada's face was tired and open and waiting, braced for the blow or the absolution, whichever came. And Sable understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she could end this right now—could keep the anger like armor, safe and familiar—or she could do the harder thing her father never had.

She could open the lock.

"I'm not finished being angry," she said.

"I know."

"But I'd rather be angry *with* you than safe without you." She reached out and took Ada's ink-stained hand, the one that had signed nothing and spoken everything. "Fifteen years, Ada. What did you see, all those nights in the market, when you looked at me buying thread and pretending not to know your name?"

Ada's breath went uneven. "A girl I couldn't have. So I settled for watching."

"Well," Sable said, and squeezed her fingers, "stop settling."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the whole drowned dark of the Spire seemed to hold its breath around them—the chains below, the creature above, the moon climbing toward its twelfth turn. Then the raven croaked, disgusted: "*Finally.* Honestly. I've had to listen to a year of pining and I'm a bird. I have a lifespan of forty years and I will spend all of them narrating this."

And that was when Bramble's voice came down the stairwell, stripped of everything but urgency:

"You need to see this. Now. The water's rising—and there's writing in it. Someone's sent us a message, and it knows our names."
