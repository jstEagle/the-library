## 18. An Audience with the Usurper

The water was rising and Ada Vane walked toward it.

Behind her, the flooded cisterns groaned against their ancient walls; ahead, the servant stair climbed out of the dark toward the spire's upper halls, where the throne room waited like a held breath. Bramble had shouted her name—*Ada, the other way, the other way*—and Fen's ghost was still wet on all of them, and every sane instinct she owned said *run, hide, survive*, the same instinct that had kept her alive for fifteen years of small charms and smaller lies.

She climbed the stairs instead.

Her boots left wet prints on stone that hadn't felt a servant's foot in years. The raven rode her shoulder, feathers slicked flat against his neck, and he did not tell her she was a fool, which from him was a kind of blessing.

"You could say something," she murmured.

"I'm deciding whether to abandon you," the raven said. "Give me a moment."

"Take your time."

They passed through a door that stood open for no reason, into a corridor lit by sigil-lamps that burned without flame—the crown's magic, hoarded for itself while the city below outlawed a child's luck-charm. The lamps hummed. Everything here hummed, a low note beneath hearing, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Ada felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the place behind her ribs where her gift lived, the place she'd kept locked for fifteen years and had finally, somewhere between the rats and the river-spirit and Sable's hand in hers in the dark, stopped locking.

The corridor ended at doors twice her height, black oak banded in silver, carved with the sigil of Aldric's line: a spire crowned by a star. Thirteen years of dust lay on the threshold except for one clean path down the center, worn by feet that came and went and never spoke of what they saw.

Ada put her palm against the oak.

Through the wood, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, she felt something vast and tired shifting its weight. Not malice. She knew malice—she'd heard it in a hunter-sergeant's voice once, in a slaver's whistle on the docks years ago. This was different. This was the feeling of her menagerie on a storm night: every animal pressed to the far wall, watching the lightning, waiting for it to be over.

"It knows you're there," the raven said quietly.

"I know."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"Frightened isn't the right word." She searched for it. "It's like standing outside a door where someone's been crying for thirteen years, and everyone in the house has agreed to pretend they can't hear it."

"And your gift is opening doors."

"My gift is listening." She straightened. "There's a difference."

She pushed.

The doors swung inward without sound, which was somehow worse than thunder, and the throne room opened before her like the inside of a bell.

* * *

It was not what the stories said. The stories said gold, said banners, said a king tall and terrible upon his seat. What there was, was mirrors.

Hundreds of them. They lined the walls floor to ceiling, pane after pane after pane, and each one reflected the room wrong—not the mirrors opposite them, not Ada standing small and dripping in the doorway, but something else. In some, the room burned. In some, it drowned. In some it stood empty but for a figure on the throne that was shaped like a man the way a glove is shaped like a hand—with nothing inside it, holding the form because the form was expected.

And on the actual throne, in the actual light of the actual sigil-lamps, sat the thing that wore King Aldric's face.

It wore it well. That was the horror of it. It had worn it so long the face had settled into it like silt into a riverbed—the gray at the temples, the scar over the left brow, the deep lines around a mouth that had smiled at coronations and funerals alike. It wore the crown loosely, tilted, as if it had never quite gotten used to the weight. Its hands rested on the arms of the throne, and its eyes—

Its eyes were open, and they were looking at her, and they were the color of a winter sky at dusk, and they were *empty* the way a house is empty when everyone who loved it is gone.

"You are not supposed to be here," the creature said.

Its voice was Aldric's voice. Of course it was. It had been speaking with that voice for thirteen years, signing decrees with those hands, kissing his daughter's forehead with that mouth. Ada's stomach turned, and she made herself stand in it.

"No," she agreed. "I'm not."

"The cisterns are flooding. Your companions are drowning or fleeing or dead. You should have run." The head tilted, birdlike, studying her. "You are the beast-speaker. The courier reached you. I have been trying to understand why he chose you."

"He didn't choose me," Ada said. "He chose the first door that wasn't locked. He was dying. People don't strategize when they're dying. They just reach for warmth."

"Warmth." The creature turned the word over like a coin it didn't recognize. "Yes. He smelled of that. They all do, at the end. Fear smells of copper. Warmth smells of—" It paused, and for a moment the empty eyes flickered, searching. "I don't have the word. Isn't that strange. Thirteen years of words, and I don't have that one."

Ada's heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her throat. Every plan she'd carried up the stairs—stall it, study it, find the seam in it where Sable's signet and the counter-sigil might bite—crumbled, because the thing on the throne sounded less like a monster than like a scholar who had lost a book.

"What do I call you?" she asked.

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, water moved through pipes; the whole spire was a body with a flood in its veins.

"I have been called many things," it said at last. "Majesty. Sire. The Usurper, by the ones who whisper. The False King. The Cold One, by the rats in your sewers—they talk about me, did you know? They think I don't listen." Something moved across the borrowed face—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "But my name. My own name. No one has spoken it since before the binding. I find I have... misplaced it."

"Then we'll start without it." Ada took one step forward. The raven's claws tightened on her shoulder—a warning, or a wish of luck, or both. "I've come a long way through cold water to ask you a question, and everyone told me not to. Bramble said fight. Sable said seal the doors. Corvane, if you can believe it, said *listen to the girl*. But none of them asked me the only question that matters, so I'll ask it for all of us, and I need you to answer honestly, because I'll know if you lie—I speak to beasts, and beasts cannot lie, and whatever else you are, you were made of oaths, and oaths can't either."

The creature's hands curled slowly on the throne's arms. The mirrors shivered, all of them at once, like the surface of a pond disturbed by a single dropped stone.

"Ask," it said.

Ada drew a breath that tasted of dust and lamp-oil and thirteen years of a kingdom pretending.

"What do you want?"

* * *

The word that came out of it was not a word.

It was a sound—a low, cracked, aching sound, like ice on a lake in springtime, like a hinge that hasn't moved in years, like the noise a person makes when they have been crying alone so long they've forgotten how crying works. The sigil-lamps guttered. In the mirrors, the burning rooms and drowning rooms flickered and went dark, all of them, until the reflections showed only the throne room, true and plain, and the creature on the throne with its face coming apart at the seams.

"To be *answered*," it said, when it could speak again. "That is what I want. That is all I have ever wanted, and no one—"

It stopped. Started again. The great hollow chest rose and fell.

"Do you know what I am? Truly? Your smuggler found her brother's bones and his last letter, so perhaps you half-know. I am the discarded things. Every oath sworn carelessly and broken. Every promise made to a dying mother and forgotten by morning. Every vow this kingdom gave itself—*we will protect you, we will feed you, we will remember*—and then did not. I am what's left when a people spend their words like coins and never count what they owe." The empty eyes fixed on her. "I was small once. A scrap. A rumor in the walls of the old palace, feeding on crumbs. And then came the famine year, and the king—your true king, the one you came to rescue—came down into the dark to bargain."

"Aldric," Ada whispered.

"He offered me his crown. His name. His *face*." The creature laughed, and it was the worst sound yet, because it was a kind laugh, bitter and tired and human. "Not to save himself. To save the city. *Take the throne,* he said, *take everything, only make the grain come, only stop the dying.* And I said: I cannot make grain grow. I am made of broken promises. I can only hold what is given me. And he said—"

The voice broke.

"He said, *then hold it well.*"

Ada stood very still. Water dripped from her hem onto the marble, each drop loud as a bell. Behind her eyes she saw the binding room below, the chains of written oaths, the hollowed man who had begged them *not* to unbind him—and now she understood, finally, wholly, that the chains ran both directions. The king had bound himself to the bargain. The bargain had bound itself to the king. Two prisoners sharing one cell, and neither able to say so aloud, because a kingdom needs its monster and its martyr more than it needs the truth.

"Thirteen years," the creature said. "I have ruled as well as I could. I am not built for ruling. I am built of endings, and I have tried to govern beginnings. I signed the ban on magic because I thought—if I am the discarded thing, let there be no more discarding. Let no child be cast out for what it is. I thought I was protecting. I think I may have been..." It looked down at the hands that had signed the decree. "...breaking more promises. Making more of me. Do you see the shape of it? Every law I pass in fear feeds me. Every oath this city swears and fails feeds me. On the thirteenth turn I will be strong enough that no one will ever unmake me—and I do not *want* to be strong enough. I want it to stop. I have wanted it to stop since the second year, and there was no one to tell, because kings are not asked what they want."

The raven made a small sound against Ada's ear—almost a word, almost a prayer.

"They aren't," Ada said softly. "Kings least of all. I think that might be the cruelest spell in this whole building."

She walked forward. Ten steps. Twenty. The guards' alcoves stood empty; the creature had dismissed them, or they had learned not to look too closely at what they served. Up close, the borrowed face was worse and better—worse because she could see the seams now, faint as watermark, the places where grief had been woven into skin; better because the eyes weren't empty at all. They were full. Full to overflowing, and holding it back the way a dam holds back a river, and Ada had spent her whole life around creatures holding back too much.

"You asked why the courier chose me," she said. "I'll tell you what I think. He was carrying the map, and the truth, and he was dying, and he wanted one person who would *hear* him before the end. Not arrest him. Not use him. Hear him." She stopped at the foot of the dais, close enough to see the crown's reflection trembling in those winter-sky eyes. "That's my gift. Everyone calls it beast-speech, as if it's about talking. It isn't. It's about listening to the things everyone agrees have nothing to say. Rats. Rivers. Cats. Kings. Monsters." She paused. "Grief-made things."

The creature's breath caught—an audible, human, broken catch.

"Thirteen years," it whispered. "Thirteen years, and one charm-seller walks in off a flooded stair and asks me what I want." The shoulders shook, once. "What I want is to put it down. All of it. The crown, the face, the weight. I want to give back every stolen oath and go back to being small. I want someone to tell the city it isn't their fault—that they made me without meaning to, the way you make a shadow without meaning to, just by standing between a child and the sun. I want—" The voice failed entirely, and what came through was barely audible. "I want to stop being the thing everyone needs me to be, so that they can learn to need each other instead."

"Then help us put it down," Ada said. "The counter-sigil. Sable has the signet. The river-spirit is free and owes me a passage. We planned to break the binding and pull Aldric out whether he liked it or not—but that was when we thought you were a thief. You're not a thief. You're a vault someone filled and forgot to empty." She held out her hand—her bare, ordinary, rain-washed hand. "Help us unmake the bargain. Both halves of it. Him from the chains, you from the crown. On the twelfth turn, together."

The creature stared at her hand as though it had never seen one extended in anything but fear or fealty.

"If you fail," it said, "the binding becomes permanent at the thirteenth turn, and I will be beyond even pity."

"Then we won't fail."

"You say that like it's simple."

"Everything's simple," Ada said. "It's just rarely easy. Come down from there."

For a long moment the throne room held its breath—all those mirrors, all those possible rooms, waiting. Then the creature rose, and the crown slid from its brow, and it caught the crown in both hands and looked at it the way a drowning man looks at the anchor that dragged him under.

"It will hurt," it warned. "Unmaking always does. For all of us."

"I know," said Ada, who had spent fifteen years unmaking herself piece by piece just to stay safe, and who had finally learned that the pain of becoming whole was worth every hour of it. "We're rather good at hurting, my family and I. We practice."

The creature stepped down from the dais. The mirrors did not shatter, but every reflection changed at once—in each one now, the throne stood empty, and two figures walked side by side toward the doors: a woman with river-water in her hair, and a shape that grew less kingly and more *itself* with every step, smaller and stranger and lighter, like a shadow learning to be a person.

At the doors, it paused.

"The twelfth turn rises tomorrow night," it said. "There is something you must see first—the room beneath the binding chamber, where the original bargain was written. Your princess will need to read it with her own eyes. And Ada Vane—" The borrowed face turned to her, and for the first time the smile that crossed it belonged to no one but itself. "When this is done, when the oaths go home and the crown comes down—there will be a moment, in the middle of the unmaking, when everything I am tries to pour back into the world at once. Thirteen years of every broken promise, all at once. Someone will have to hold the door open from the inside."

Ada met its eyes. "Someone will."

"I hoped you'd say that." It pushed open the doors onto the lamplit corridor, onto the rising water-song of the spire, onto the last night of the false reign. "Come, then, beast-speaker. Let us go wake a king who would rather sleep—and afterward, if we live, you must teach me the word for what the dying smell of. I have been trying to remember it for thirteen years."

"Warmth," Ada said, and followed it into the dark. "*It's called warmth.*"
