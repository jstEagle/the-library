## 19. The Unbinding

The twelfth moon rose over Greyhollow like a coin held up to a dying man's eye.

Ada felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her ribs, the same pressure she'd felt every night since the courier died on her doorstep, tightening now toward something final. Below her feet, in the drowned dark of the cisterns, the river-spirit sang against its chains, and above her head, in the throne room of the Spire, something made of grief was listening to the same moon.

*It's time,* said the raven, perched on her shoulder with his feathers slicked flat by cistern damp. *In case the bleeding sky hadn't told you.*

"It's time," Ada agreed.

They stood in the antechamber beneath the binding room, six of them where there had been eight. Fen's absence was a shape in the room, the way an empty chair is a shape. Bramble stood at the edge of the lantern light with her brother's bones wrapped in oilcloth across her back and his final message folded against her heart: *it cannot be killed, only answered.* She had read it aloud twice, voice steady as a keel, and then she had gone very quiet in the way of people who have decided not to drown.

Sable knelt by the inner gate, turning her father's signet ring over in her fingers. The royal seal of Aldric Vane caught the lantern light and threw it back wrong—warped, as if the metal itself remembered being part of a different crown. "The counter-sigil needs four anchors," she said. "Blood of the hidden gift." She looked at Ada. "Blood of the crown." Herself. "Blood of the oath-broken." A pause, and Bramble lifted her chin. "And water that remembers being free."

The river-spirit coiled up through the grate then—not the bound, guttering thing they had found guarding the gate three days ago, but something taller, brighter, its body a rope of moving light threaded with silt and old rain. It had no face, but it managed, somehow, to look at all of them at once.

*I remember,* it said, and its voice was the sound of a thaw. *I remember everything. That is what they never understood about us.*

"Us?" Sable asked.

The spirit's light dimmed, thoughtful. *Everything you bind remembers. The river. The king. The thing upstairs. Binding is not forgetting. It is forcing someone to remember forever.* It flowed toward the gate. *Come. Let us go remind your kingdom what it promised.*

* * *

The binding room had been a chapel once. Ada could see it in the bones of the place—the arched windows, long bricked over; the faded circle of saints or kings or whatever this city worshipped before it learned to fear itself—scoured from the floor but leaving ghosts of gilt. Now the room belonged to chains.

Not iron. Chains of written oaths, thousands upon thousands of them, inked onto strips of vellum and lead and skin, link forged to link, running from the walls to the figure at the center. King Aldric hung among them like a moth in a web, barefoot, grey-bearded, his eyes open and fixed on nothing. Thirteen years of oaths, each one a promise someone in Greyhollow had made and meant and broken, each one feeding the thing upstairs and anchoring the man below. He did not look like a king. He looked like a word that had been said too many times.

"Papa." Sable's voice cracked on the second syllable. She crossed the floor fast, hands out—and stopped three steps away, because his eyes moved to her and there was nothing in them. Not hatred. Not love. Just the terrible patience of stone.

"He's still in there," Ada said quietly. "Look at his hands."

His hands were moving. Fingers twitching, one after another, in a rhythm too regular to be random—and Ada's gift caught it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards. He was signing. Not court-sign, not smuggler-hand-talk. Beast-speech. Old beast-speech, the kind the river used, the kind her grandmother had used.

*He's talking to the chains,* she breathed. *He's been talking to the chains for thirteen years.*

"What's he saying?" Bramble asked.

Ada listened harder, letting her gift open the way it had opened for rats and gulls and rivers, and what came through made her stomach drop. *Counting. He's counting the moons. And he's saying—" She swallowed. "He's saying he's sorry. Over and over. To the chains. As if the chains can hear him."

"The chains are made of promises," Sable whispered. "Of course they can hear him."

Aldric's lips parted. His voice came out rusted, a hinge in a house no one lived in. "You shouldn't have come." Each word cost him visibly, like pulling nails. "The bargain holds while I hold. If you break it—"

"The bargain starved half the city to save the other half," Sable said. "I know. I've read the ledgers. I've done the arithmetic you must have done, thirteen years ago, alone, with a famine coming down the mountains." Her voice shook. "I even understand it. But you didn't tell anyone. You let us mourn a father who was alive ten floors above our heads. You let me grow up talking to a stranger wearing your face."

"I let you grow up," Aldric rasped, and something flickered behind his eyes—old, tired, human. "That was the only thing left I could give."

Then the mirrors lit.

Every polished surface in the room—the great mirror behind the throne far above, the little hand-glass in Sable's pack, the still black water pooling through the broken grate—all of them filled at once with the same image: the throne room, and the thing upon it. It had stopped pretending. The warm shape of King Aldric sloughed away like wax off a candle, and what remained was vast and woven, a body made of ribboned parchment and frayed braid and wedding rings and soldiers' buttons and children's letters never sent—every discarded oath in thirteen years, braided into a shape that sat where a king should sit and watched them watching it.

It did not speak. It didn't need to. Ada felt its attention settle over the binding room like snowfall, and underneath the dread she felt the other thing, the thing she had heard in the throne room when she asked it what it wanted, the thing that had unmade a piece of it right there on the dais:

*No one ever asks.*

"It knows we're here," Bramble said, drawing her knife out of habit and then, with a grimace, putting it away again. Useless. They'd all known it was useless since the cisterns. "Course it knows. Question is whether we've got until moonset or until it decides to come down those stairs."

"We have until moonset," Ada said. "It's holding itself together up there. Every hour it spends whole costs it more than it can spare. Being heard is killing it, and it keeps choosing to listen anyway." She looked around the circle of faces—Bramble's grief-honed resolve, Sable's white-knuckled courage, Corvane standing awkward guard by the door in his hunter's blacks, having chosen his side with a servant's key and no going back. Even the raven, feathers puffed, muttering, *I want it noted that I objected to all of this from the beginning.*

"Four anchors," Ada said. "Now."

* * *

They had rehearsed it in the cisterns by lantern light, drawing the counter-sigil in spilled flour because none of them dared chalk it early. It was not a weapon. Corvane had kept asking for the weapon in it, some sigil to break, some chain to snap, and Ada had kept telling him no. *Unbinding isn't breaking. Breaking is just another kind of binding—you force the thing into pieces instead of forcing it into place. Unbinding is asking everything to let go at once. Including yourself.*

The four anchors took their places at the compass points of the old saint-circle. Sable in the north, signet pressed to the floor, blood of the crown. Bramble in the east, her palm cut and laid flat beside her brother's unwrapped bones—blood of the oath-broken, and the bones themselves, because the dead keep their promises better than the living. The river-spirit in the west, pouring itself through the grate until the whole western arc of the circle ran with living water. And Ada in the south, knife open in her hand, her own blood welling into the flour-lines, fifteen years of hiding ending in a single red signature.

"Now the words," she said. "Everyone. The ones we agreed. Mean them, or don't say them."

They spoke together, four voices ragged and human:

*We remember what was promised.*
*We do not ask it to be kept.*
*We only ask that it be set down.*

The sigil woke.

Light climbed the flour-lines like fire climbing a fuse, but cold, silver, moon-colored, and where it passed, the chains of written oaths began to shiver. Not snap—shiver, the way a dog shivers off water. Vellum links fluttered. Lead links sweated ink. And Aldric cried out—a raw, cracking sound, nothing kingly in it—as thirteen years of other people's promises began to lift off him like smoke off a doused fire.

Above them, through every mirror and every pool of standing water, they saw the creature rise from the throne.

It came down the stairwell the way fog comes down a hill, pouring through the mirrors first and condensing into the binding room in a flood of ribbon and button and bone-dry vellum, filling the doorway, filling the windows' brick, towering over the lit circle. Its face—if it had one—was a knot of wedding vows tied tight. When it moved, Ada heard whispers: *I promise to protect them. I promise to return. I promise, I promise, I promise.* Thousands of voices, all of them broken, all of them still trying.

Corvane put himself between it and Sable with his sword drawn after all, because some men are built that way, and the creature looked at him and the whispers swelled—*I swear to serve the crown, I swear by the old queen, I swear*—and he went pale, because it was wearing his own promises, every one he'd ever made and failed.

"Hold the circle!" Ada shouted. "Don't fight it! Don't feed it!"

Because that was the truth Bramble's brother had died to carry back: fighting it fed it. Fear fed it. Broken oaths were its flesh, and every person in Greyhollow broke a dozen a day without knowing, and the creature had grown fat and enormous on a city's worth of good intentions gone sour. For thirteen years it had eaten. Tonight, for the first time, someone was offering it something else.

Ada stepped to the edge of the circle, blood on her palm, and looked up into the knot of vows that served it for a face.

"You're not a monster," she said, and the whispers faltered. "You're what's left over. When everyone stops keeping their word, the words don't die. They just wait somewhere. And nobody ever asked you what you wanted."

The creature loomed. The whispers rose to a roar—*promise, promise, promise*—and the chains around Aldric shrieked taut, and the moon pressed down through the ceiling like a thumb on a wound, and Ada planted her feet and did the unthinkable again, the thing she had done on the throne room dais two nights ago, the thing that had started all of this:

She listened.

Underneath the roar she heard it. Small. Older than the famine, older than the binding, older than the false king. A single voice, worn threadbare, saying the same thing it had been saying for thirteen years to a kingdom that never once heard it:

*Keep me. Please. Someone keep me. I was promised and no one kept me and I am so tired of waiting.*

"I know," Ada said, and her voice broke, and she let it. "I know exactly what that's like. I spent fifteen years making sure no one could hear me either." She held out her bloody hand. "So here's my promise, and I'll keep it if it's the last oath I ever make: you can be finished. Whatever you were owed—it's paid. Whatever you were waiting for—it's over. You can set it down."

For a moment, nothing. The moon hung. The chains hung. The whole world seemed to hang on the creature's answer.

Then it began to let go.

It came apart the way a knot comes apart when someone finally finds the right end—not torn, not cut, but eased, loop sliding from loop, ribbon drifting loose. Oaths rose off it like birds off a winter field: a soldier's vow to protect, dissolving into light; a merchant's broken contract, sighing into nothing; a mother's letter, finally delivered to no address at all; a king's bargain, the oldest and heaviest of them all, rising last, glowing like a coal, and hanging in the air above the circle where everyone could see it.

*I traded my crown to save them,* the old oath said, in Aldric's younger voice. *Let them never know the cost.*

"Well," said Bramble, wet-eyed and dry-voiced, "that one's broken now, innit."

And the oath laughed—actually laughed, a young man's laugh, startled out of it—and came apart.

The chains fell. All of them, everywhere, at once—vellum and lead and ink raining down as harmless paper and dull metal, and King Aldric dropped with them, and Sable caught him, on her knees in the ruins of the sigil, holding a father who weighed almost nothing at all.

* * *

He was smaller than his portraits. That was the first thing anyone noticed, later, trying to describe it. Thirteen years of being an anchor had drawn him down to wire and sinew, and when the last chain touched the floor, what rose from Sable's arms was not the king who had vanished but a hollowed, humbled, diminished man with frost-white hair and eyes like a cleared sky after a storm—emptier, and cleaner.

He looked at his daughter for a long time.

"Sable," he said. Testing the word. Finding it still true.

"Papa." She was crying without any dignity whatsoever, and Corvane turned his back to give them privacy and pretended very hard to inspect a wall.

"I'm sorry," Aldric said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry." The same words he'd signed to the chains for thirteen years, finally aimed at a face that could hear them.

"I know," Sable said. "I heard. The whole city heard, eventually. That's rather the problem." And she laughed, wrecked and relieved, and held on to him anyway.

Ada stood at the circle's southern point, swaying, her blood drying tacky in the flour, and felt the moon begin its last descent. Around her the binding room was carpeted in drifted oaths, settling like snow, going soft and grey and ordinary. The raven hopped down and picked up a strip of vellum, considered it, and dropped it.

*Well,* he said. *Nobody died. Well. Almost nobody.* He ruffled his feathers toward Bramble, who stood with her hand resting on the oilcloth bundle of her brother's bones, and did not say anything else, because even ravens know when not to.

The river-spirit gathered itself by the broken grate, thinner now, its work spent, its light gone gentle. *The water will run clean by morning,* it told Ada. *And the ban?*

Ada looked at the drifts of fallen oaths, at the king diminished and his daughter holding him up, at the door where a sigil-hunter stood guard over the people he'd been ordered to kill. Somewhere above them, Greyhollow was waking to a morning with no false king on the throne and no true one able to sit it, and every councilman and guildmaster in the city was already reaching for the old law, the safe law, the ban—

"They'll try to bring it back," she said. "The ban. Tomorrow, probably. By noon."

*Then you had best be there to stop them,* said the river, and poured itself away down into the dark, singing.

Ada looked down at her open palm, at the cut already closing, at fifteen years of hiding ended in one night of blood and moonlight. Above, through the shattered mirrors, the twelfth moon touched the horizon and began, at last, to set.

One turn remained.

She picked up the raven, shouldered her pack, and went to help Sable lift the king—for whatever came next, they would carry him together.
