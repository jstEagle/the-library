## 20. Crowns and Consequences

The throne room still smelled of rain and river-water three days after the unbinding.

Sable stood at the base of the dais and watched the servants scrub the last of the silt from between the flagstones, their brushes rasping in slow rhythms, and she thought: *this is what a kingdom looks like when it has been wrung out.* The great mirrors along the eastern wall had been covered in mourning-cloth by order of the council—no one wanted to look into them now, no one wanted to risk catching some fragment of the thing that had worn her father's face for thirteen years—but even shrouded they seemed to lean over the room like patient relatives at a deathbed.

Her father sat on the throne for the first time since his rescue, and he looked wrong on it.

Not usurped-wrong. Not the too-calm, too-old flicker she had seen that terrible evening when doubt first took root behind her ribs. This was a different wrongness, quieter and more human: the wrongness of a man sitting in a chair built for someone else. Aldric had come back from the binding room diminished—not smaller in body, but *thinner*, as though thirteen years of oaths had been woven through him and pulling them out had left gaps where a king should be solid. His hands rested on the arms of the throne the way a guest's hands rest on anything: lightly, apologetically, ready to lift away.

"Sable." His voice was better than it had been. The healers said the hollow places would fill, given years. He did not have years. She could see that already, in the way he looked at the hall—at the banners, the sigil-worked columns, the whole gilded apparatus of rule—as a man looks at a house he once loved and no longer recognizes. "You've read the council's petition."

"I've read it." She held the folded parchment up between two fingers, as if it were something dead. "They want the ban restored. All of it. Registration of the gifted, the hunter companies re-sworn, the cisterns sealed with new wards before the river-spirit changes its mind." She let the parchment drop to her side. "They want everything back the way it was before Tuesday."

"Before the truth," her father said gently.

"Before the truth," she agreed. "Which is worse."

Below them, the last servant finished and withdrew, and the doors boomed shut, and then it was only the two of them and the shrouded mirrors and the long light falling amber through the high windows. Sable climbed the dais steps and sat on the arm of the throne—not the seat itself; something in her flinched from the seat itself—and her father leaned his head briefly against her shoulder, the way he had when she was small and afraid of storms.

"They're not villains," Aldric said. "Councillor Vess lost her son to a mage-fire thirty years ago. Halloway watched the plague-wards fail in the outer quarters when he was a boy. Fear is the only inheritance some people ever receive, and they guard it jealously."

"You made a bargain with a creature made of fear," Sable said. "To end a famine. I know. Ada told me. Bramble told me. Half the city tells me, now, in taverns, in whispers, in songs that are getting less polite by the day." She exhaled. "I'm not angry at you, Father. I keep waiting to be, and it doesn't come. You traded yourself to save them. They're repaying you by demanding we pretend none of it happened—that the thing wasn't *made of us*, that the magic didn't save us, that Ada didn't—" Her throat closed. She hadn't meant to say the name yet. It came anyway, the way true things do. "That Ada didn't walk into that throne room armed with nothing but a question and unmake what an army couldn't touch."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful weight of a man setting down something fragile. "What will you do?"

"The council convenes at noon. They'll call for the vote publicly—they want witnesses, they want the appearance of unanimity." She slid off the throne's arm and stood, smoothing her coat, the plain grey coat she'd worn sneaking through curfew streets, because she found she could not bear the ceremonial white anymore. It felt like a costume for someone else's daughter. "And I'm going to give them witnesses."

* * *

The council chamber had been built to intimidate. Sable had known that since childhood—the fan of seats rising above the single speaker's circle, the windows set high so that petitioners stood always in shadow while the councillors sat in light. She had been intimidated by it at nine, at fifteen, at twenty. Standing in the speaker's circle now, with the noon light deliberately angled down onto her face by her own instruction, she found the architecture merely quaint. She had been in drowned cisterns. She had seen what intimidation looked like when it grew teeth.

Eleven faces regarded her from the arc of seats. Councillor Vess sat at the center, silver-haired and iron-spined, the restoration petition laid on the table before her like a verdict awaiting signature.

"Princess Sable," Vess began, "the crown thanks you for your service in the recent... crisis. Your discretion during the infiltration of the spire is noted with gratitude, if also with concern."

"My treason," Sable said pleasantly. "Say the word. Everyone else has."

Murmurs moved along the arc like wind through wheat. Vess did not blink. "The kingdom owes a debt to those who ended the usurpation. But debts are paid once, Princess, and then settled. What the council proposes today is not vengeance. It is *stability*. Magic was outlawed for reasons written in ash and graves. One woman's gift did not erase those reasons. It demonstrated, rather, how dangerous a single ungoverned talent can be. We move that the Edict of Greyhollow be restored in full, effective immediately, and that the gifted present themselves for registration within thirty days."

"Seconded," said Halloway, and four voices after him, the words falling in the practiced rhythm of a thing rehearsed.

Sable let the silence sit until it became uncomfortable for them instead of her. Then: "May I ask the council a question? A single one, before the vote."

Vess inclined her head, wary.

"When the creature wore my father's face for thirteen years—when it signed the edicts, appointed the hunters, tightened the ban year after year—which of you suspected?" She turned slowly, letting the question land on each face in turn. "Which of you looked at the king and thought: *that calmness is wrong, that patience is too old, something beneath that smile smells of cold?* Because I lived in that palace. I ate supper across from it. And I never knew. None of us ever knew. So forgive me, Councillor, if I decline to entrust the governance of magic to the same discernment that governed everything else."

"Your father," Vess said, with terrible gentleness, "was deceived by a creature beyond mortal kenning. We do not fault him."

"No," Sable agreed. "You fault the woman who saw through it. Who asked it a question no one had asked in thirteen years. Who heard it—truly heard it—and discovered your monster was made of discarded oaths and forgotten promises. *Yours.* Oaths this council swore and abandoned. Promises this city made and broke. Ada Vane didn't defeat it with power. She defeated it with attention. And your response is to outlaw attention." She shook her head. "You are voting to blind yourselves again, and you're calling it healing."

Halloway leaned forward, jowls tight. "Sentiment. The girl speaks sentiment. The law exists precisely so that kingdoms need not depend on the goodwill of gifted individuals—however heroic. Today's heroine is tomorrow's tyrant. That is not blindness, Princess. That is memory."

"Then let me offer you a different memory." Sable reached into her coat and drew out two things: her father's signet ring, heavy gold, warm from her skin; and a folded writ, sealed in black wax, which she had written at dawn with a hand that only shook a little. "The king abdicates. As of this morning. He will address the city himself this evening, but the instrument is signed and witnessed, and it names me regent until such time as the succession is lawfully resolved." She set the ring on the table in front of Vess, where it gleamed like a small captive sun. "And this—" she lifted the writ, "—is my first act as regent. Read it aloud, Councillor. I'd rather they hear it from your voice than mine. It will carry further."

Vess unfolded the writ. Sable watched her eyes move down the page and stop, and widen, and the whole arc of councillors leaned forward as one body, hungry and horrified, as Vess read in a voice that started steady and did not stay that way:

*"By order of Sable, daughter of Aldric, Regent of Greyhollow: the Edict of Greyhollow is repealed. Magic is neither crime nor curse in this city henceforth. Further: I declare before the court and the city my own alliance—I name Ada Vane, beast-speaker, my counselor and my companion, and I will hear no word against her gift or her person that I would not answer with my office."*

The chamber detonated. Voices overlapped—outrage, disbelief, one councillor actually rising from her seat—and through it all Sable stood in the circle of light and let it break against her like weather. Somewhere in the gallery above, where the public had been admitted despite Halloway's objection, a cheer went up, ragged and young, and then another, and the sound of it changed the room's chemistry entirely: the councillors' fury suddenly had an audience, and audiences cut both ways.

"The vote," Vess said at last, over the din, her knuckles white on the petition, "is moot. The regent holds the seal."

"The vote was always moot," Sable said. "You simply needed to see it to believe it."

* * *

She found Ada on the spire's western stair, halfway up, standing at the window where the whole of Greyhollow spilled out below—rooftops and smoke and the glittering scar of the river, the market already rebuilding its burned stalls, gulls wheeling over the fish-quarter like scraps of thrown paper.

Ada didn't turn when Sable's footsteps stopped beside her. "I can hear your heart from here," she said. "It's going like a war drum. Whatever you did down there, it cost you."

"It cost me nothing I wasn't already spending." Sable looked out at the city. From this height the smoke of chimneys rose straight in the windless afternoon, hundreds of thin grey threads stitching the city to the sky. "I named you. In front of the full council. In front of the gallery. Counselor and companion. I used the word *companion*, Ada, which in a royal writ has exactly one meaning, and every lawyer in Greyhollow will spend a fortnight confirming it in footnotes."

Now Ada turned. Up close her eyes were tired in the way of someone who had spent three days being celebrated by strangers and interrogated by everyone else. The raven was absent—roosting somewhere, sulking about the lack of pastries in the palace kitchens, presumably. "You didn't ask me," Ada said quietly.

"No. I didn't." Sable had rehearsed apologies on the climb and found, arriving, that she didn't want any of them. "Because I knew you'd tell me to wait. To be sensible. To let the repeal settle first, to protect the school, to protect *you*. And you'd have been right, the way you're always right about hiding. Fifteen years of practice." She took Ada's hand—chapped, ink-stained, faintly smelling of the charm-shop's dried lavender—and held it where the window-light could see it. "I'm done letting right-and-sensible make my decisions. I spent twenty-three years watching my father trade pieces of himself for the kingdom's comfort, and it bought thirteen years of a lie wearing his smile. Love that hides isn't love, Ada. It's just a longer way of losing."

Ada's breath caught—a small audible thing, quickly mastered, but Sable had learned to listen for the unmastered sounds in people, these last weeks. Below them a bell rang the half hour, and neither of them moved.

"They'll try to unmake you," Ada said at last. "The council. The guilds. Some of the crowd, even—the ones who cheered the repeal and will choke on the rest. They'll call you bewitched. It's the oldest accusation there is, and it writes itself."

"Let them write it. I'll publish it with illustrations." Sable smiled, and felt the smile crack open into something truer. "Bramble says the *Gullwing's* hold is nearly empty and the crew is bored, and bored smugglers are a political force. Corvane has offered to resign twice and I've refused twice—he guards the school now whether he likes it or not. The river-spirit has flooded the lower cisterns exactly once, which the engineers inform me is practically a love letter. We are not without allies." She squeezed Ada's hand. "And you haven't answered me."

"I'm answering." Ada turned back to the window, to the city, and her voice came low and steady, the voice Sable had first heard in a drowned dark asking a bound spirit *what do you want*: "Yes. Publicly. Loudly. In whatever shape they can't look away from." A pause, and then, with the ghost of her old dry humor surfacing like a fish: "Though I warn you, my cat will expect a title."

* * *

Evening came, and with it her father's address.

Aldric stood on the balcony above the Reconciliation Square—not the throne room, he had insisted, never that room again—and spoke to a crowd that filled the square and spilled down every street feeding into it. His voice carried by charm-work, borrowed openly now, and it was thinner than the voice Sable remembered from her childhood, but it reached.

He told them the truth. All of it: the famine, the bargain, the willing crown, the thirteen years of complicity dressed as captivity. He told them the creature had been made of their own broken promises, and that it had died—or changed, or gone home, no one could say—because one woman asked it what it wanted. He told them he could not rule what he had abandoned, and that he had named his daughter regent, and that he would spend what strength remained teaching in the school on Charm Street, if the school would have him.

The square was silent for three full breaths. Then it made a sound Sable had never heard a crowd make—not a cheer, not a roar, but a long exhale, thousands of people releasing thirteen years of held air at once. Somewhere in it, a child laughed, bright and unafraid, and the laugh spread the way fire spreads through dry grass.

Sable stood at the balcony's edge with Ada's shoulder warm against hers, and below them the city began, haltingly, to sing.

It was Bramble who brought the news, shouldering up onto the balcony with the total disregard for protocol that Sable was learning to find restful. The captain's face was grim beneath its salt-weather, and she held a scrap of paper sealed with wax the color of old blood.

"Courier just came up the river road," Bramble said. "Fast rider. This is addressed to you, Highness, but it's marked with my brother's seal." Her jaw worked. "Tobin's seal. The one he wore when he walked into the cisterns two years ago and never walked out."

Sable took the letter. The wax was fresh. The seal was unmistakable.

And the paper, when she broke it open, held only five words, in a hand she had never seen but recognized instantly, because it smelled—even through ink and distance—of cold:

*We remember what was promised.*

Down in the square, the singing swelled. On the balcony, three women stood very still, watching the moon rise thin and new over the spire—the first turn of thirteen.

"We need to talk to the rats again," Ada said quietly.
