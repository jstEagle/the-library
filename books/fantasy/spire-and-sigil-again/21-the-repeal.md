## 21. The Repeal

The proclamation went up on a Tuesday, nailed to the market post where the sigil-hunters had once tested children for the taint of magic with cold iron charms.

Ada Vane stood in the crowd and read it three times, because the words refused to stay still in her head. *By decree of Her Regent's Council, the Edict of Greyhollow is hereby repealed. The practice of magic is no longer a crime within city walls.* Below it, in smaller script: *All persons imprisoned under the old edict are to be released forthwith.*

Around her, the market did what markets do. A woman haggled over eels. Two boys chased each other between the stalls. Someone dropped a crate of turnips and swore about it. The world did not stop turning because the law had changed; it just kept turning, lighter somehow, like a cart that had finally been unloaded.

But not everyone kept moving. An old man near the fish stalls had gone very still, his hands pressed flat against his chest as though holding something in—or letting something out. Ada recognized him. Master Ellery, who sold candles and had never once met her eyes in fifteen years of neighboring stalls. He was mouthing something, over and over, and when she leaned close she caught it: *I can put it down now. I can put it down now.*

Fifteen years of carrying a gift like a stone in the belly, and here was the day he could set it on the ground.

Ada's own eyes were burning, which was ridiculous, because she'd known this was coming for weeks. She'd sat in the council chamber—*in* it, at the table, invited—while Sable argued the repeal through eleven hours of procedural hell. She'd watched Corvane stand before the assembled hunters and say, in that flat gravel voice of his, "I have arrested forty-one people for the crime of existing wrong. I will not arrest a forty-second." She'd thought she was prepared.

She was not prepared for Master Ellery's hands on his chest, or for the way the whole square seemed to breathe out at once, or for the small girl beside her—seven, maybe eight—who tugged her mother's sleeve and said, too loudly, "Mama, look! Look what I can do!" And then made a dandelion bloom in December, right there in the frozen mud, golden and impossible and entirely unhidden.

The mother looked around wildly—for hunters, for witnesses, for the old fear—and found none of it waiting. Then she knelt in the mud and laughed until she cried.

Ada walked home through all of it, past the candle stall where Master Ellery was now openly whittling a charm-shape into a taper, past the dandelion girl, past the fishmonger who bought luck-charms from her and had never once asked why they worked, and she thought: *this is what it sounds like when a city exhales.*

Her shop door was open when she got there. It was always open now. That had taken some getting used to.

* * *

The school had started by accident, the way most true things do.

In the first week after the repeal, people simply began arriving. A widow whose knitting needles moved on their own. A dockhand who could calm any animal with a word. Three siblings from the Tanner's Row who could all, inexplicably, speak to smoke. They came to the charm shop because Ada Vane was the only name anyone knew—the beast-speaker who'd walked into the throne room, who'd unmade the thing in the crown, whose face was on half the broadsheets in the city (rendered, in every version, far more heroic-looking than she felt).

"I'm not a teacher," she told the first dozen of them, standing in her crowded shop with a raven on her shoulder and a cat judging everyone from the counter.

"No," agreed the raven, whose name was Soot and whose opinion of humanity had improved only marginally since the fall of the usurper. "You're worse. You're famous."

"They'll listen to you anyway," said the widow, wringing her self-knitting scarf in her hands. "Please. My granddaughter's been hiding what she can do since she was four. She flinches when she sneezes. I don't—" The widow's voice cracked. "I don't want her to flinch anymore."

Ada looked at the room full of frightened, hopeful faces. She looked at the cat, One-Ear, who gave her the slow blink that meant, in cat, *you're going to say yes, we both know it, spare us both the speech.*

She said yes.

That had been four months ago. The charm shop still sold charms—she refused to give up the counter, the little brass bell, the ritual of wrapping luck in paper—but the back room had knocked-through walls now, and the cellar was a classroom, and the roof was a training yard, and every morning between eight bells and noon, the narrow lane outside her door filled with children carrying satchels instead of secrets.

Today's lesson was water-craft, which meant the cellar was flooded ankle-deep, which meant the river-spirit was teaching, which meant everything was going to be soaked and no one would mind.

* * *

The spirit had come back in spring, rising up through the cistern grate in a column of silver-green water that resolved, slowly, into a shape it seemed to be practicing: a tall figure with hair like flowing current and eyes the color of deep pools. It had chosen the name Wren, after much deliberation, because—as it explained—"it is small and quick and lives near water, and also I enjoy that it annoys the raven."

"It does not annoy me," Soot had said. "Nothing annoys me. I am a creature of perfect equanimity."

Wren had flicked one droplet of water precisely into the raven's ear, and the resulting outrage had lasted the better part of an afternoon.

Now Wren stood in the flooded cellar in its practiced shape, barefoot on the surface of the water as though it were glass, while nine children stood knee-deep and tried to make the water listen.

"Water is not obedient," Wren was saying, in that voice like a stream over stones. "It is not loyal, and it does not love you, and if you approach it as a servant to be commanded, it will drown you without malice, the way a mountain avalanches without malice. Water listens to those who ask properly. Show me asking."

A boy named Tam—dockworker's son, all elbows and impatience—thrust out his hand and barked a command. The water ignored him so thoroughly that it might have been insulted. Beside him, a small girl with her grandmother's flinch—Maren, seven years old, who had spent her whole life making things bloom and hiding them—crouched down, put both hands in the water, and whispered something too quiet for anyone to hear.

The water rose around her wrists like a greeting. Like a dog recognizing a friend.

"Again," said Wren, but gently, to Tam. "You are commanding a flood, child. Try courting a stream."

From the stairs, Ada watched, arms crossed, leaning against the frame. This was the part she liked best—not teaching herself, though she taught beast-craft twice a week and loved it fiercely, but watching the others teach. Watching Wren, who had been bound beneath the Spire for longer than any of these children had been alive, patiently explaining freedom to people who'd never lost theirs. Watching Maren's shoulders come down from around her ears, inch by inch, week by week.

One-Ear picked her way down the stairs with the dignity of a duchess inspecting premises, stepped delicately into the floodwater, immediately regretted it, and settled onto the one dry shelf with an expression that assigned blame to all present.

"You're supposed to supervise," Ada told her.

"I am supervising," said the cat, in the way cats speak—a thing less heard than understood, a meaning settling behind the eyes. "I am supervising your tendency to hover. Go upstairs. You have a visitor who has been standing outside for ten minutes working up the courage to knock, and if he stands there much longer I am going to go out and trip him."

Ada glanced toward the front of the shop. Through the fogged window she could make out a broad silhouette, hat in hand, shifting weight from foot to foot.

She knew that silhouette. She'd know it anywhere, these days.

"Let him sweat another minute," she said. "It builds character."

"He has character," One-Ear said, curling her tail over her paws. "He has an entire regiment of it. Let him in before he wears a groove in my flagstones."

* * *

Sergeant—no, Captain—Corvane stood in the middle of her shop looking like a man who had wandered into the wrong life and couldn't find the exit.

He was out of uniform. That alone would have caused talk in the city. Corvane owned exactly one set of civilian clothes, purchased apparently under protest, and wore them the way other men wore penance: grey coat, plain boots, the sigil-hunter's badge nowhere visible except, she noticed, worn anyway on a cord under his shirt, where he probably thought no one could see it.

"Captain," she said.

"Miss Vane." He cleared his throat. "Vane. Ada." He said her name like a man testing ice. "Bramble."

"Bramble," she agreed, delighted. "Progress. What brings you off the wall? Don't tell me there's trouble."

"There is always trouble. Today's trouble is administrative." He produced a folded paper from his coat and held it out stiffly, like a man surrendering a weapon. "The guard rotation for the school. You requested—"

"Sable requested. You agreed. I witnessed."

"—a permanent watch presence," he continued, ignoring her, "given the... incidents."

The incidents. There had been three: a brick through the window with *witch-lover* chalked on it, back in the first month; a petition to the council signed by forty merchants demanding the school relocate "somewhere appropriate"; and one genuinely alarming evening when a drunk ex-hunter had stood across the street shouting scripture-adjacent nonsense until Bramble had walked out, planted herself in front of him, and stared at him with the flat patience of a woman who had smuggled contraband past worse men than this, until he'd gone home.

Corvane had taken the brick personally. Ada suspected he'd taken the petition personally too, though he'd never admit it.

"The rotation is attached," he said. "Six guards, staggered shifts. I've selected them myself. Every one of them served under me during the edict years, and every one of them has—" he paused, jaw working, "—regrets. They'll do the work properly."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"You drew up a rotation for six guards, Captain. Rotations have seven names or none. Whose shift is the empty one?"

Corvane looked at the ceiling. At the shelves of charms. At One-Ear, who was watching him from the doorway with the smug attention of a cat who already knew the answer.

"Mine," he said finally. "Evenings. After the wall shift." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "The children should learn what a hunter looks like when he isn't hunting. They should learn that we—" he stopped. Started again. "I spent fifteen years teaching this city to be afraid of people like you. I can spend the next fifteen teaching it not to be. It won't balance the ledger. Nothing balances that ledger. But the accounts don't close just because the numbers are ugly."

Ada let the silence sit for a moment, because Corvane deserved the discomfort—he'd earned the discomfort, in a strange way, the same way he'd earned the second chance.

Then she stuck out her hand. "Evenings. But fair warning: Maren will make flowers grow in your boot pockets. She thinks it's hilarious. There is no defense."

"I've been told." He shook her hand, grip careful, like she was something breakable, which she hated and secretly didn't mind. "And Vane—Bramble." He hesitated at the door, hat twisting in his hands. "The girl who bloomed the dandelion in the market square last week. In front of everyone."

"Maren. Yes."

"That was her?" Something crossed his weathered face—grief, maybe, or its cousin, relief. "When I was a boy, a girl in my village did that. Made a flower bloom in frost. They took her away on a cart, and I cheered, because I was twelve and stupid and the cart had pretty ribbons on it." He put his hat on. "Tell her the flower was well done. Tell her—" His voice did something complicated. "Tell her the whole city saw, and nobody came with a cart."

He left. Ada stood in her shop, in the smell of dried lavender and candle wax, and found she needed a moment before she could go back downstairs.

* * *

Sable arrived at dusk, as she did most evenings now, coming through the back gate rather than the front because regents apparently learned to move quietly or they drowned in ceremony.

She came down into the classroom while the last lesson was ending, and Ada watched the children react to her the way they always did: a ripple of awe, quickly mastered, because Sable had insisted from the first visit that she be treated as a person and not a procession. She wore plain clothes. She'd cut her hair shorter since taking the regency. She still moved like a princess when she forgot herself—all those years of court training living in the spine—but more and more often lately, she moved like someone who'd learned to laugh in a flooded cellar with wet boots.

"How many today?" she asked, nodding at the children filing out, chattering about streams and asking-properly.

"Nineteen. Two new ones—a pair of twins from the Weavers' Quarter who can hear colors. Their father cried in my shop for twenty minutes."

"Good crying?"

"The best kind."

Sable nodded and stood there a moment, working up to something. Ada had learned to recognize the signs: the regent squared her shoulders before hard council sessions, but Princess Sable twisted the signet ring on her finger before saying anything that mattered.

"Father's leaving," Sable said.

Ada went still. "Leaving Greyhollow?"

"Leaving the throne. Officially, formally, with documents and witnesses and a ceremony he refuses to let them make grand." Sable turned the ring. "He's going north. To the old queen's holdings, the ones nobody's touched in thirteen years. He says—" her voice caught, steadied, "—he says a king who gave away his crown shouldn't keep pretending to hold it. He says he wants to spend whatever years he has left planting orchards. Actual orchards, Ada. He asked me if I thought the land would forgive him. I didn't know what to say."

"What did you say?"

"I said trees don't keep ledgers." A ghost of a smile. "He liked that. He cried a little. Kings aren't supposed to cry, but he's not really a king anymore, so I suppose it's allowed." She looked around the cellar—at the waterline marks on the walls, the children's chalk drawings of Wren's true shape (all wrong, all beloved), the shelf where One-Ear supervised. "The council wants me to take the crown outright. Full coronation. They're drafting the proclamation."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd wear the circlet. The regent's circlet. Not the crown." She met Ada's eyes. "My father traded a crown to save this city and it nearly destroyed everything. I'm not going to pretend the crown is the point. The city is the point. The school is the point. The—" she gestured vaguely at everything, at the repealed world, "—the dandelion in December is the point."

Ada reached out and took her hand, right there in the emptying classroom, water sloshing around their ankles, because fifteen years of hiding had taught her exactly how precious it was to touch someone in the open, in the light, where anyone could see.

"The council will grumble," she said.

"The council can learn to swim. Wren offers lessons."

From the deepest corner of the flooded cellar, where the shadows pooled thickest, the water chuckled—a low, rolling sound like stones in a current. Wren's voice rose up through the floor itself, amused and ancient:

"The regent jokes about me. I am becoming respected."

"You are becoming *quoted*," Ada said. "There's a difference."

"There is not," said Wren, "and one day I will explain why at length, and you will all suffer."

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the school was dark and the shop was dark and even One-Ear had conceded the day—Ada climbed to the roof with a cup of tea and sat on the warm chimney bricks where she could see the Spire.

It looked different now. Everyone said so. The mirrors had been removed from the throne room and melted down, and without them the Spire seemed almost ordinary—a tall old building with lit windows, home to clerks and councils and one regent who preferred plain clothes. The usurper was gone, unmade, answered; the oaths it had stolen had flowed back into the city like a tide returning, and people woke some mornings remembering promises they'd forgotten they'd made, and wept, and kept them.

Below the Spire, deep in the stone, the binding room stood empty. Aldric himself had unlocked the last chain. *Some doors,* he'd said, *you have to open from the inside.*

Ada drank her tea. Somewhere below her, in the cellar, the water murmured in its sleep, dreaming in the pipes. Somewhere across the rooftops, Soot was out doing whatever ravens did at midnight—Ada had stopped asking. On the wall opposite, a stray dog lifted its head and regarded her with bright, intelligent eyes, and she sent it a lazy pulse of greeting, *evening, friend*, and the dog wagged its tail once and trotted on, and neither of them thought anything of it at all.

That was the miracle, she thought. Not the repeal nailed to the market post. Not the school, or the spirit in the cellar, or the captain guarding its door. The miracle was this: a beast-speaker on a rooftop, speaking to a dog, hiding nothing, flinching at nothing, and the night swallowing it all without complaint.

Fifteen years she had built her life like a wall, stone by careful stone, and called the wall safety.

She'd torn it down and found a family on the other side.

Ada finished her tea, and was just turning to go inside—to sleep, tomorrow being a school day, with two new twins to welcome and a captain to embarrass and a regent expecting her at dinner—when Soot came out of the dark like a thrown knife and landed on the chimney beside her, feathers ruffled, eyes bright with something between alarm and delight.

"Vane," the raven said. "You're going to want to hear this. There's a ship coming up the coast flying no colors at all, and the gulls say it's carrying a passenger who keeps asking for you by name—and Vane, the gulls say she has your mother's face."

Ada set down her cup very carefully, and did not spill a drop.

"My mother," she said, "has been dead for sixteen years."

"Well," said Soot, preening one wing, "then either the gulls have developed a sense of humor—which, given their general character, seems unlikely—or someone aboard that ship is about to make your quiet new life considerably less quiet. Dawn tide. I'd start worrying now, if I were you. It saves time in the morning."
