## 22. Spire and Sigil

The charm shop smelled of beeswax and river-water, which was not a combination any charm seller in Greyhollow had ever offered before, but then no charm seller in Greyhollow had ever kept a river-spirit in the rain barrel out back.

Ada Vane stood in the doorway of what had been her hiding place for fifteen years and was now something else entirely—a school, though the sign above the door still read VANE'S CHARMS & CURIOSITIES because Bramble Quill refused to pay for a new one until Ada admitted she'd been wrong about something, anything, just so Bramble could win an argument for once. The morning light came through windows that no longer had to be shuttered against detection charms. Children's chalk marks covered the floorboards where the sigil-work used to be hidden under a rug: wobbly attempts at water-craft, a lopsided wind-knot, one ambitious attempt at fire that had singed the eyebrows off young Tam Wicker and taught the whole class a valuable lesson about respect.

"You're brooding," said the raven from his perch by the door. His name was still Messenger, because he refused to answer to anything else, and he considered himself the school's headmaster in all but salary. "You have your brooding face on. The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic about grief."

"I'm not brooding."

"You've been standing in that doorway for four minutes."

"It's a nice doorway."

"It's a *doorway*, Ada." He ruffled his feathers, which was how he shrugged. "Go do whatever it is people do on anniversaries. Bring bread. The gulls at the docks expect you, and if you disappoint them again they'll follow you around screaming, and I refuse to be associated with that."

Ada smiled and touched the doorframe—the same doorframe where a dying courier had collapsed one year ago tonight, pressing a bloodstained map into her hands with the last strength he had. She had scrubbed the blood away within the hour, back when magic was a crime and evidence was a death sentence. Now there was a small brass plaque there instead, unmarked except for a single word in Old Script that only three people in the city could still read: *Remembered.*

She locked up, whistled for the cat, and went to collect the rest of her strange, impossible family.

* * *

Princess Sable—Regent Sable now, though she still flinched at the title like it was a thrown stone—was waiting at the foot of the Spire stairs in plain clothes, which fooled absolutely no one, because regents did not usually wear boots scuffed from climbing cistern walls and carry their own bread.

"You're late," Sable said.

"The gulls detained me."

"The gulls *detained* you."

"They had grievances. Something about the fishing quotas." Ada handed her half a loaf. "I negotiated. There will be reforms."

Sable laughed—that real laugh, the rare one, the one Ada had spent a year learning to earn—and tore the bread in two. Above them the Spire rose into the dusk like a needle threaded with lamplight, its mirrors long since shattered or repurposed, its throne room converted to a council hall where, rumor held, the regent argued with fishermen's wives and gutter-mages on equal footing and usually lost.

"Your father sends regrets," Sable said, quieter now. "He says climbing stairs is for people who haven't spent thirteen years chained to a wall."

"He's not wrong."

"He's never wrong. It's insufferable. I finally understand why the usurper wore him." She said it lightly, but Ada caught the flicker underneath—the old wound, still healing, still tender. A year ago Sable had learned her father had chosen his own binding, traded his crown to end a famine, and had let his daughter grow up orphaned in a palace full of lies rather than tell her the truth of what grief had made of him. They had fought about it. They had cried about it. Last month, for the first time, they had simply talked about it, over fish stew, while the cat supervised from the table like a small furry magistrate.

"He'll come next year," Ada said. "He's walking again. Give him time."

"Time." Sable looked up at the darkening sky, where the moon hung fat and nearly full. "We've all learned a lot about time."

They climbed.

* * *

The others were already at the top.

Bramble Quill had claimed the best vantage point on the western parapet, naturally, and had brought a flask of something that was technically tea and functionally courage. She stood with her back to the city, arms crossed, watching the stair with the expression of a woman pretending she hadn't been counting heads as they arrived. Her brother's seal hung on a cord around her neck now, polished smooth from handling. Fen's name was carved into the parapet stone beside it, along with eleven others lost in the cisterns over thirteen years whose bones the freed waters had finally given back.

"You're late," Bramble said.

"So I'm told."

"Corvane beat you here. *Corvane.* The man has the emotional range of a filing cabinet and even he managed punctuality."

Sergeant-turned-Captain Corvane stood at the far end of the parapet in his grey hunter's coat, re-cut and re-dyed to the school's colors, looking profoundly uncomfortable in the way he always did when confronted with sentiment. He nodded at Ada. It was, from Corvane, practically an embrace.

"Captain," Ada said.

"Vane." He hesitated, then produced, from inside his coat, a small paper bag of sugared almonds and set it on the parapet with the rigid dignity of a man defusing a bomb. "For the children. At the school. They asked after—" He stopped. Started again. "The little one. With the water-gift. She asked if I would attend the demonstration on Thursday."

"And you said?"

"I said I would inspect the premises for security vulnerabilities." He stared out at the harbor. "It is the same thing. Do not make me say it is the same thing."

"We would never," said the raven, landing on his shoulder. Corvane did not flinch anymore. Progress.

The last to arrive were the ones who didn't use stairs. The river rose.

It came up through the old cistern mouths and the rain channels and the forgotten fountains, gathering along the Spire's base and then climbing, impossibly, gracefully, in ropes and ribbons of silver water that coiled up the ancient stonework and pooled on the parapet into the shape of a woman wearing a crown of spray. The guardian. The bound spirit who had guarded the inner gate for thirteen years and now guarded nothing but a school's rain barrel and, occasionally, the harbor's fishing rights.

"You came," Ada said.

The water laughed, a sound like pebbles rolling in a streambed. "*You called. That is enough. That has always been enough.*"

One year ago tonight, Ada had stood in a flooded gatehouse beneath this very spire with a dying king's chains around her wrists and a creature made of broken promises filling the throne room above, and she had done the thing no one in thirteen years had thought to do: she had asked it what it wanted. Not fought it. Not banished it. *Asked.*

And it had answered. Thirteen years of stolen oaths and swallowed grief, and all it had ever wanted was for someone to hear it—to know that the kingdom's discarded promises, the vows made in famine and forgotten in plenty, had not simply vanished. They had *pooled*. They had grown cold and hungry and enormous. And when Ada listened—truly listened, the way she listened to cats and rats and rivers—it had wept itself apart, releasing everything it had taken, and the false crown had fallen from a shape that had never wanted to wear it at all.

Magic was legal now. The ban had fallen with the usurper. And every full moon since, the whole city had watched the sky together, waiting for the thirteenth turn to pass and prove the binding truly broken.

Tonight was that night.

* * *

They gathered at the parapet's edge—Ada and Sable shoulder to shoulder, Bramble and Corvane flanking them like bookends carved from very different stone, the raven on Ada's arm, the cat in Sable's, the river-spirit rising beside them in a column of moonlit water. Below, the city had turned out. Every rooftop, every dock, every hillside beyond the walls held lanterns and faces turned upward. Greyhollow, the grey city, the afraid city, glowed like a coal banked with light.

"There it is," Bramble said softly.

The moon cleared the eastern hills, swollen and gold-rimmed, one sliver shy of full. The thirteenth turn. The night the binding would have become permanent, had it not already been undone. The night a courier had died on a charm seller's doorstep so that a map could find the one person in Greyhollow who spoke to beasts, and rats, and rivers, and—though she hadn't known it yet—grief itself.

Ada felt Sable's hand find hers. Warm. Certain. A year ago, love had been a treason Sable committed in the dark, joining smugglers and mages in the cisterns with her father's signet clenched in her fist. Tonight she held Ada's hand on a public parapet before half the city, and nobody gasped, and if anyone disapproved, they had the good sense to do it silently.

"Do you remember," Sable murmured, "when I caught you breaking curfew? You looked like a startled deer. Your raven kept shouting about *thirteenth turns* and I thought you were a spy."

"I *was* a spy. Badly."

"You quoted my father. The real one. 'The moon keeps honest books.'" She shook her head. "Thirteen years I searched that palace for proof he was still in there somewhere, and a smuggler's raven found him before I did."

"To be fair," said the raven, "I'm extremely talented."

Below them, the crowd began to sing—not an anthem, nothing official, just the old harvest round that everyone knew and no one had dared sing in public during the bad years, when singing felt too much like hoping. The sound rose up the Spire's flank and broke over the parapet like weather.

The moon climbed. The thirteenth turn began.

And nothing happened.

That was the miracle of it, Ada thought. No sigil flared. No chain snapped shut across the kingdom's heart. No cold shape stirred beneath the crown. The moon simply crossed the sky, honest and indifferent, keeping its books, and the world went on being free. Somewhere below, King Aldric was probably watching from his garden cottage, drinking tea he no longer had to trade a kingdom for, diminished and changed and—for the first time in thirteen years—merely a man.

Bramble raised her flask to the horizon. "To Fen," she said. "Who found the answer first."

"To the courier," said Corvane, whose name for the dead man, Ada had learned, was Wren, and who had sworn the old queen's oath to protect people exactly like him.

"To the rats," said the raven, "without whom none of this—"

"To everyone," Ada said, laughing, and the river-spirit raised a ribbon of water that caught the moonlight and scattered it over the parapet like thrown coins.

The moon reached its height. The thirteenth turn passed over a free city, and the city sang it home.

Later—much later, when Bramble had won the argument about the sign by declaring herself right about the bread, and Corvane had fallen asleep sitting upright with the dignity of a monument, and the river had poured itself gently back down the Spire to sleep in its barrel—they stayed. Just the two of them, and the raven, and the cat, leaning on the parapet while Greyhollow's lanterns burned themselves down to embers below.

"One year," Sable said. "Sometimes I can't believe it's only been one year."

"Sometimes I can't believe it's been a whole year and I still haven't told you about the eels."

"*The eels?*"

"In the cisterns. During the flood. They had opinions about us, Sable. Strong opinions. I promised them harbor rights and I intend to honor that promise, which means Thursday's council session is going to be very strange for you."

Sable groaned, then laughed, then pulled Ada close against the cold wind, and above them the moon—the thirteenth turn behind it now, forever—moved through a sky with nothing left to fear in it.

Ada watched it go and thought of a doorstep, and a map, and a whisper: *thirteenth turn.* She had spent fifteen years building a life out of hiding, and it had taken one dying stranger and a talking cat to teach her that she had never actually been hiding at all. She had been waiting. For the question no one had asked. For the voice no one had lent. For this—moonrise, family, wonder, the whole loud living chorus of a city that had learned, at last and at cost, to listen back.

"Come on," she said, taking Sable's hand. "The gulls will want a report."

And they went down together, into the glowing streets, toward home.
