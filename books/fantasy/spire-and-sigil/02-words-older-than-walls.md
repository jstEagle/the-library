## 2. Words Older Than Walls

Silas Kavanagh had spent eleven years copying other people's prayers, and he had learned exactly one thing from the exercise: the dead were far more polite than the living.

The folios in the temple archive said things like *O light that remembers us, receive this servant gently.* The debt collectors who came to his door on the first of every month said things like *Kavanagh, your family's interest has compounded again, and Master Corvane is not a patient man.*

He was thinking about this particular injustice at dawn, three days after the child had appeared at his door, because the child was currently sitting on his kitchen floor eating the last of his bread with the solemn concentration of a scholar defusing a bomb.

"Slowly," Silas advised. "It's not going anywhere. Neither am I, financially speaking."

Wren — he had decided to call her Wren, because she was small and brown-eyed and had looked at him that first night like a bird deciding whether a windowsill was safe — paused mid-chew and regarded him with an expression of profound skepticism. She did not speak Vhaleni. Or rather, she did not speak it *well*. She had picked up perhaps thirty words in three days, deployed them with terrifying precision, and otherwise communicated through gestures so expressive that Silas suspected she could negotiate a trade agreement without a single syllable.

But when she slept, or when something startled her, or sometimes for no reason at all except that the morning light hit the window a certain way — then the other language came out of her. Low, liquid, full of sounds that seemed to bend around corners. Words no one in Vhalen spoke. Words, as far as Silas knew, that no one *had* spoken since before the kingdom had a name.

And here was the problem. Here was the problem sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal.

He knew those words.

Not well. Not fluently. But he knew them, because for six years he had been committing small crimes against the Temple of the Veiled Flame, and those crimes lived in a locked box under his bed.

* * *

The box contained forty-one folios, copied by hand in Silas's smallest, most deniable script: pages from the sealed archive, the restricted stacks behind the iron gate that only Inquisitors and senior clerics could open. He had begun copying them out of desperation — the temple paid a bonus for scribes willing to restore damaged texts, and damaged texts were often old texts, and old texts were often forbidden texts, and nobody counted the pages of a book they'd ordered destroyed if the scribe seemed suitably bored.

Silas had gotten very good at seeming suitably bored.

The oldest folios were written in glyphs that predated the Sundering War, predated the ban, predated everything. The archivists called the script *First-Tongue* and treated it the way sensible people treat a nest of vipers: from a distance, with gloves, and while saying something pious. There was no key to it. No Rosetta stone, no bilingual tablet, nothing. Scholars had broken their careers against those glyphs for two centuries and produced, collectively, a handful of disputed translations and several nervous breakdowns.

And now a barefoot child was eating his bread and murmuring it in her sleep.

Silas sat down across from her, slowly, the way you sit down across from a lit fuse. "Say that again," he said. "The thing you just said."

Wren cocked her head. Then, obligingly, in that low liquid voice: *"Vesseth-ka. Vesseth ilun."*

His hands went cold.

He stood up too fast, knocked his chair over, apologized to the chair out of habit, righted it, and went to the box under his bed. His fingers shook so badly getting the lock open that he dropped the key twice. Wren watched him with the air of someone cataloguing symptoms.

Folio nineteen. The funerary cycle, recovered from a collapsed shrine under the Salt Quarter, ordered burned in the year of his apprenticeship. He had copied every glyph, and beside each one, in his own cramped hand, he had written his best guesses — guesses built from context, repetition, and the desperate logic of a man trying to squeeze meaning from stone.

*Vesseth.* He had guessed *sleep*, or possibly *rest*, from its position in the burial formulas.

*"Vesseth-ka,"* Wren repeated, watching him. Then she pointed at herself. Then, after a moment's thought, she pointed at him too.

*Sleep-us? Rest-we?* Or — and his mouth went dry — *we sleep together*, in the sense the old shrines had meant it. *We rest in the same ground. We belong to one another.*

"That's not possible," Silas told her conversationally. "You're maybe eight years old. That language died before my grandfather's grandfather learned to walk. Whatever you think you're doing—"

She reached out and touched folio nineteen, her finger landing on a glyph he had never managed to translate at all — a spiral crossed by three descending strokes, appearing seventeen times across the corpus, always near words for *water* or *depth* or *below*.

*"Sulenneth,"* she said.

And then she frowned, as though listening to something far away, and added a second word he had never seen written anywhere. A word that made the candle flames in his little room lean sideways, all of them, all at once, like grass under wind.

Silas blew out the candles himself, quickly, before they could do anything worse than lean.

"Right," he said faintly. "New rule. We don't say that word indoors."

* * *

He should have gone to the Inquisitors. He knew that. It was, in fact, the law — discovery of unlicensed speech, report within a day, penalty for concealment being roughly the same as the penalty for the original crime, which struck Silas as the kind of legal design that discouraged honesty. If he walked into the Inquisitorial precinct with the child, he would be questioned, searched, his folios found, and his family's debt would become the least of anyone's concerns, including his own, briefly, until the pyre.

If he did *not* go to the Inquisitors, he was harboring illegal magic in a city where the Inquisition had informants in every bakery line.

He spent the afternoon doing sums, which was what he did instead of praying. The sums went like this: Option one, betray the child, lose whatever remained of his soul, keep his ledgers balanced. Option two, protect the child, lose everything else. Option three, which his heart kept proposing despite having no legal standing whatsoever — find out what she actually was first, and *then* decide.

He was on option three, elbow-deep in folio twenty-two, when the knocking started.

Not at his door. At the building's front door. Heavy, rhythmic, official. And beneath it, the sound Silas had dreaded his whole careful life: the flat voice of an Inquisitor announcing a search warrant to his landlady, followed by his landlady's voice going up half an octave in the particular way people's voices do when soldiers ask questions.

Silas moved without deciding to move. Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and find only a gap, the way there's a gap in memory between slipping on ice and hitting the ground. He swept the folios into their box, the box into a grain sack, the sack over his shoulder. He took Wren's hand. She was already standing, already alert, as though she'd heard the knocking minutes before it started — and maybe she had; he was past arguing with the evidence about her.

"Back window," he whispered. "Don't step on the cabbages. Mrs. Hallow counts her cabbages."

They went out over the herb boxes and into the alley, where the rain had turned the cobblestones slick as oiled glass. Behind them, boots on stairs. Ahead, the maze of tenements and drying lines and drainage channels that the poor of Vhalen called home and the Inquisition called, dismissively, *the sprawl* — as if you could search a place by giving it a smaller name.

Silas ran the way he had run as a boy, when the debt collectors first came and his father taught him the back paths: left at the tanner's, down the steps that didn't look like steps, along the channel wall where the watch never bothered because the smell discouraged piety. Wren matched him stride for stride, silent as spilled ink, and once — when a patrol cut across their path below — she pulled him backward into a doorway a full second before he'd have done it himself, her grip surprisingly strong, her eyes fixed on the street with an attention that felt less like fear and more like *listening*.

The patrol passed. The rain kept falling. Somewhere above, a temple bell rang the hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

"They'll check the scriptorium next," Silas breathed. "My desk. My copies. They'll know it's me by tomorrow noon." He laughed, a small unhinged sound he was not proud of. "Eleven years of being boring. Eleven years of being the man nobody remembers. All it takes is one child."

Wren tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed — down, past the channel grate, into the dark under the streets. The undercity. The warrens beneath the warrens, where the smugglers traded and the drains sang and the Temple's writ ran thin as gutter water.

"No," Silas said. "Absolutely not. Down there is—" He gestured vaguely, encompassing drowning, robbery, worse. "I sell charms, Wren. Paper ones. I am not equipped for the undercity."

She looked at him. Rain dripped off her chin. And she said, in careful, accented, heartbreakingly precise Vhaleni:

"You. Come." A pause while she hunted for the next word, found it, and aimed it at him like a small determined arrow: "*Together.*"

*Vesseth-ka,* she had said that morning. *We belong to one another.* Apparently she meant it.

Silas Kavanagh stood in the rain with a stolen archive on his back, a fugitive child at his side, and his entire lawful life burning quietly behind him like a lamp left on in an empty house. He thought of his mother's face when the collectors came. He thought of forty-one folios of dead men's prayers. He thought, absurdly, of Mrs. Hallow's cabbages, untrampled, at least.

Then he knelt, wrenched the grate loose, and lowered himself into the dark after her.

Whatever the sigil was — whatever slept in the girl's skin and leaned on candle flames — the Inquisitor-General would not be the one to hold it. Silas didn't yet know how he knew that, or what it would cost. He only knew that some debts are paid in coin, and some are paid in courage, and he had finally, after eleven years, chosen which kind he owed.
