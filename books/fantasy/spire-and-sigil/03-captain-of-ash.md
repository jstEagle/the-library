## 3. Captain of Ash

The summons came at dawn, as the Inquisitor-General's summons always did — delivered by a boy in gray who never met her eyes and never waited for a reply.

Isolde Vey dressed in the dark, as she had for eleven years. Breeches, boots, the ash-gray coat of the Vigil with its collar buttoned to the throat. She braided her hair back tight enough to hurt a little, which she found helpful. Pain was a fine thing to carry into a meeting with Aldous Renne.

Her quarters above the garrison stable were small and smelled of leather and lamp oil, and she liked them that way. A woman with too many rooms began arranging them, and a woman who arranged things began wanting things, and wanting was how officers ended up kneeling in the General's garden, so to speak. She kept one luxury: a tin cup that had belonged to her brother, dented on one side where he'd dropped it off the aqueduct wall at nine years old and sworn it was the wind's fault. She drank her morning tea from it now, standing at the window, watching Vhalen wake up wet.

Rain again. The city wore rain like an old coat it refused to take off.

The boy in gray was still waiting at the bottom of the stairs, which meant the General wanted her promptly. She took the warrant from him without a word and read it on the walk over, boots striking cobbles in time with her heartbeat, which was steady, because she had trained it to be.

*By order of the Office of the Inquisition-General, Captain Isolde Vey is granted full authority to locate and apprehend one Silas Kavanagh, scribe, third scriptorium, Temple Ward, and one unidentified child, designation ANOMALY, believed in his company. All resources of the Vigil are placed at her disposal.*

All resources. For a scribe and a child. Isolde had hunted smugglers with less paper than this, and poisoners, and once a man who had burned down half the Tannery Quarter with what he swore was a lamp and bad luck. The paperwork told you everything if you knew how to listen. This much paper meant someone upstairs was frightened.

She was shown into the General's study at the top of the Spire Annex, where the windows were tall and the light came through glass tinted faintly amber, so that even the rain looked like something holy happening to somebody else.

Aldous Renne did not look up from his desk at first. He never did. It was a technique, she supposed — make the visitor stand in the amber light and feel like a specimen pinned under glass. He was a narrow man, dry as a ledger line, with hands folded neatly atop a folio as though the folio might bolt.

"Captain Vey." He looked up then, and smiled his small smile, the one that used exactly none of his eyes. "You've read the warrant."

"I have, sir."

"Then you know the shape of it. A scribe named Kavanagh has been selling charms out of the third scriptorium. Trinkets, mostly — love knots, fever wards, that sort of harmless superstition we ordinarily let the temples handle." His fingers tapped the folio once. "Until three nights ago, when a child appeared at his door speaking the Old Tongue. Fluently. A language dead four hundred years, Captain, coming out of a girl of perhaps eight years, barefoot, in the rain."

"Perhaps she learned it somewhere," Isolde said. "There are collectors. Mad scholars—"

"There are no mad scholars who could teach fluency in a night." He rose, finally, and went to the window, hands clasped behind him, a man addressing the weather rather than his officer. "You will find them. You will bring the child to me unharmed — *unharmed*, Captain, I want that understood — and Kavanagh as well. What he knows about how she came to speak, where she came from, who sent her. There are parties in this city who would very much like to get their hands on that child before we do. I trust I don't need to describe what they'd do with her."

"No, sir."

He turned. "And your brother."

Isolde did not move. Eleven years of training, and still the name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

"Tomas's case comes before the tribunal next month," Renne said pleasantly. "Forgery of temple writs. Smuggling of restricted texts. The penalty is ten years in the Saltcells, and the Saltcells are not kind to men who can read." He let that sit. "Bring me the scribe and the anomaly within the fortnight, and I will see the charges dissolved. Pardon, clean record. He could take a post in the archives, even. Quiet work. He'd like that, wouldn't he? The quiet ones always do."

"He would," Isolde said, and hated how easily the words came, how much of herself they gave away.

"Good." Renne returned to his desk, already dismissing her. "You're the best hunter I have, Captain. It isn't flattery; it's arithmetic. Bring them in."

She saluted. She walked out. Her tea was cold in her stomach and her brother's name was a stone behind her ribs, and she told herself, all the way down the seven flights of stairs, that this was simple. A scribe. A lost child. A lawful task, fairly rewarded.

It was simple. It was simple. She made herself believe it for almost two days.

* * *

They found Kavanagh's rooms empty, of course — the man had run the same night the raid hit the scriptorium, which was itself damning. Innocent men stayed and answered questions. But he had run *badly*, or rather he had run like a man who had never run before in his life, pausing to lock his door, pausing to leave rent under the mat, pausing — this was the detail Isolde kept turning over — pausing to take a child's damp stockings off the hearth-rail and fold them into his bag.

You didn't fold the stockings of a hostage.

His confiscated ledgers came up from the evidence room in two crates, and she sat with them long past her watch's end, in the garrison office with one lamp lit, telling herself she was looking for leads.

The first crate was arithmetic. Debts, mostly — the crushing, patient debt of a family bound to the temple, itemized in a small, careful hand. *Wax, 2 bits. Vellum scrap, 4 bits. Fee for father's physic, 14 silvers.* Page after page of a man counting his life out in copper, and beside each entry, occasionally, a note in the margin. Beside the physic fee: *worth it. he laughed at supper.* Beside a charge for mended shoes: *she'll outgrow them by spring anyway. buy the big ones.*

Isolde set that page down and looked at the lamp for a while.

The second crate was worse. Charms inventory — hundreds of entries, each one dutifully logged, each one, by the letter of the law, illegal. And here was the thing she had not expected: he had graded himself. Next to each charm, in the same small hand, a verdict. *Harmless. Harmless. Harmless — probably. Took this one back.* An entire criminal enterprise, conducted with the conscience of a parish clerk. One entry near the end read simply: *Sold a "luck knot" to a widow on Candle Lane. It does nothing. Nothing at all. But she stood straighter when she left. Is that fraud? I have decided it is not, and I am prepared to defend this position to anyone, including God, including the Inquisition.*

Isolde laughed. Alone, at midnight, in a garrison office — a short, startled laugh, quickly strangled. She hadn't laughed in this room in years. She wasn't sure she'd laughed since Tomas.

Because here was the trouble, and she circled it slowly, the way you circle a wound to see how deep it goes: the law said magic was outlawed, all of it, root and branch, since the Sundering. And the law was the only thing standing between Vhalen and the fires that had eaten half the world once already. She believed that. She had built her whole life on that belief, brick by brick, the way you build anything worth standing in.

But the law also said that Silas Kavanagh — a man who sold widows nothing at all and worried about whether hope counted as fraud — deserved the same cell as the blood-mages of the old histories. The law said a child who spoke strange words in the rain was an *anomaly*, a designation, a file number. The law had a great many ways of saying *thing* instead of *person*, and Isolde had repeated every one of them for eleven years without ever once hearing what they sounded like.

She heard them now. They sounded like Aldous Renne smiling with none of his eyes.

* * *

On the fourth day, she caught the trail.

A ferryman at the Gray Stair remembered a tall scribe with a girl on his shoulders, heading toward the tannery district, paying with exact change and apologizing for it. A rag-picker remembered the girl waving at her. *Waving,* the woman said, puzzled, as though children waved at rag-pickers often enough to be remarkable. Isolde wrote it all down in her field book in her neat, square hand, and beneath it, without quite deciding to, she wrote: *He carries her. Carries her himself. Bad knee, per garrison records. Does it anyway.*

She looked at that sentence for a long time.

That evening she stood on the aqueduct road above the Tannery Quarter, watching lamplight come on below in the warrens where the smugglers' tunnels opened like roots under the city, and she did the arithmetic Renne would have approved of. Two days left on the fortnight. Trail warm. Child in reach. Brother pardoned.

And underneath the arithmetic, quiet as water under ice, another sum running: *a man who folds stockings. a child who waves at rag-pickers. a general who wants the anomaly brought to him unharmed, and why, and why, and why.*

"Where are you, scribe?" she said aloud, to the rain.

The city didn't answer, but far below, at the mouth of the old culvert by the tannery wall, a lantern flickered — there and gone, there and gone — like something being signaled, or something being followed.

Isolde Vey buttoned her collar against the wet, settled her hat, and started down.

She told herself it was duty. She was almost sure the words still fit.
