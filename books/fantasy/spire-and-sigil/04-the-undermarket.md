## 4. The Undermarket

The way down began behind a fishmonger's stall that sold, as far as Silas could tell, no fish.

He had learned this from a man named Doss, a fence with a wheezing laugh and a ledger of debts longer than the temple's own, who had taken one look at Wren and said, "You want Old Words? You want the Undermarket. Third arch past the stink-stall, knock twice, wait for the cough." Then he'd charged Silas three coppers for the information, which Silas considered fair, since Doss had also given Wren a boiled sweet and taught her to cheat at knucklebones in under four minutes.

"Third arch past the stink-stall," Silas murmured now, guiding Wren by the shoulder through the warrens beneath Vhalen. The tunnels here had been sewers once, then cellars, then something older that no one liked to name. Lamplight leaked from cracks in the walls like the city itself was glowing faintly rotten. The air smelled of tallow, wet stone, frying onions — an unholy trinity, but after two days of hiding in drainage culverts, it smelled almost like dinner.

Wren walked close beside him, barefoot still — they'd found her boots in a refuse heap and she'd looked at them with such profound offense that Silas hadn't dared raise the subject again. She was humming. It was the same melody she always hummed, low and strange, and he had stopped asking where it came from because the answer, whenever she tried to give one, arrived in syllables that made his teeth ache pleasantly, like biting foil.

*Words older than walls.* He'd written that in his hidden folio, back when the archive thefts were an intellectual crime and not a hanging one. Now the words lived in a child who hummed them, and every Inquisitor in Vhalen wanted them both in irons.

"Almost there," he said, mostly to convince himself.

At the third arch, a door of riveted iron sat flush in the stone. Silas knocked twice. Somewhere beyond, someone coughed — elaborately, theatrically, the sort of cough that took a full ten seconds and included a flourish at the end.

The door opened on a woman with a shaved head, a leather apron, and forearms like mooring ropes. She looked at Silas. She looked at Wren. Her expression performed surprise the way a bad actor performs grief: thoroughly and not at all convincingly.

"Well," she said. "Visitors."

"We're looking for a translator," Silas said. "Someone who reads pre-kingdom script. I can pay—"

"You'll come in first," the woman said, stepping aside. "Everyone comes in first. House rules. Also you look half-starved and I've got stew on."

Silas hesitated. Free stew, in the Undermarket, was roughly as trustworthy as free gold. But Wren was already walking through the doorway, drawn by the smell of onions, and Silas had learned over the past days that his choices were largely ceremonial.

* * *

The room beyond was long and low, lit by oil lamps whose flames burned — Silas noticed this only after several seconds, and then could not stop noticing — slightly *wrong*. They bent toward the center of the room, all of them, regardless of draft, like flowers toward sun.

People sat at trestle tables: dockhands, a woman mending nets, an old man teaching cards to a boy who was clearly robbing him blind. A pot of stew burbled on a iron stove. And everywhere, worked into everything — carved into table edges, stitched into cushions, branded discreetly into the beams overhead — ran the same symbol: a flame inside a circle that did not quite close.

An unquenched flame. A circle left open, so the fire could never be contained.

Silas's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. He knew that mark. Every scribe knew it, the way every scribe knew the shape of the gallows rope. The Order of the Unquenched Flame — heretics, arsonists, martyrs, depending on which temple pamphlet you'd swallowed — supposedly exterminated to the last believer during the purges. Supposedly.

"You recognize it," said a voice behind him, warm as fresh bread.

Silas turned. A man stood there, silver-haired, round-faced, dressed in merchant's gray of very good quality worn very carelessly. He had the eyes of someone who had spent decades practicing kindness in a mirror until it looked natural.

"I recognize trouble," Silas said.

"Ha! Honest. Good." The man pressed a bowl of stew into Silas's hands and another into Wren's, crouching to her level with the smoothness of long practice. "I am Brother Aldous. This is the hearth of the Unquenched Flame, little one. You're safe here. We've waited a long time for you."

There it was. *Waited.* Not *welcome* — *waited*, the way one waits for a delivery.

Wren looked up at Aldous with her flat gray gaze, spoon halfway to her mouth, and said nothing, which Silas had come to understand was her most eloquent register.

"She doesn't speak much," Silas said carefully. "And when she does, it's—"

"The old tongue. Yes." Aldous rose, beaming. "We know. Oh, we know, Master Kavanagh."

The stew went leaden in Silas's hands. "I didn't give my name."

"You didn't have to." Aldous gestured graciously toward a bench, and Silas sat because refusing would have required a plan, and he had none. "Word travels. A scribe from the temple scriptorium flees with a child who speaks the dead language — well. There are those among us who read the signs. Who have read them for years." His eyes glittered with something that was not quite warmth. "She is the vessel, Master Kavanagh. The living sigil. The key to the Wellspring itself."

Around the room, Silas noticed, conversation had died. Not entirely — the card game continued, the net-mender kept mending — but heads were tilted their way. Listening. The lamplight leaned inward, all of it, hungry.

"That's a lovely story," Silas said. "Here's mine: I'm looking for a translator. Someone who can tell me what the child is saying before the Inquisition finds us. I pay in coin or copied folios, whichever you prefer, and then we leave."

Aldous laughed again, and this time the warmth slipped, just for a heartbeat, showing the machinery underneath. "You misunderstand your role in this, scribe."

"Do I? Tell me what it is, then. I'm fond of accuracy. Occupational hazard."

"You are the *keeper*," Aldous said, spreading his hands as though unveiling a monument. "Chosen by circumstance, guided by fate. You brought her to us. That is no small thing. No small grace." He turned to Wren, and his voice softened into something syrupy. "And you, little flame. You must be so tired. So lonely. Wouldn't you like to rest? To be among people who understand what sings inside you?"

Something flickered across Wren's face. Something Silas had never seen there before: a kind of reaching. Her spoon lowered. Her humming, faint and constant these past days, swelled — just barely — and the lamps around the room all flared at once, a soft golden gasp, and thirty people drew breath as one.

Then Wren blinked, looked down at her stew, and ate.

Silas set down his own bowl, appetite gone. *Their kindness feels rehearsed,* he thought, *because it is.* And underneath the gratitude he owed them for the stew and the shelter and the door that locked against Inquisitors, something small and sharp and sensible unfolded its wings.

Suspicion. Old friend. He'd been raised on it; debt taught you that every gift had a price listed somewhere in fine print.

"Aldous," he said lightly, "you mentioned rest. Where exactly do your guests sleep?"

"In the cloister below. Warm beds. Quiet." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The child will be quite safe. We guard our treasures jealously."

*Mm,* thought Silas. *Yes. That's precisely the problem.*

* * *

The cloister below was warm, quiet, and full of beds, exactly as promised. It was also, Silas noted while pretending to admire the stonework, accessible by exactly one stair, guarded by two large men who smiled at him with the fixed serenity of temple statues.

"They lock it at night," Aldous said cheerfully. "For safety. Yours, of course."

"Of course."

They gave Wren a small cell with a real bed and a blanket that smelled of lavender and something else underneath — ash, maybe, or old smoke. She sat on the edge of it, feet swinging, watching Silas with those gray eyes.

"I'll be right there," Silas said, pointing to a pallet across the corridor. "Door open. If anything feels wrong, you—" He patted his own chest, then hers. Their private shorthand. *Feel wrong, tell me.*

Wren nodded. Then, softly, in the old tongue, she said something — three syllables, lilting, and for once Silas thought he caught the shape of it, having stared at the glyphs for years: *not yet* or perhaps *not here.*

"Not yet," he agreed, having no idea what he was agreeing to. "Get some sleep."

He lay on his pallet listening to the Order breathe around him. Through the wall came murmured voices — Aldous and someone else, low and urgent. He caught fragments. *...the General's men are already asking questions in the Warrens... ...mustn't rush the vessel, the sigil ripens at its own pace... ...the gate opens at the dark of the moon, whether she wills it or—*

Whether she wills it or not.

Silas closed his eyes and did arithmetic. Three days to the dark of the moon. One stair, two guards, thirty believers, and himself with a letter-knife in his boot and a child who trusted him. The math was terrible. The math had been terrible since midnight outside his door, and he had run the numbers anyway, every time, and every time the answer came out the same.

*You chose her over your own neck, Kavanagh. Keep choosing.*

He must have slept, because he woke to silence — the wrong kind, the held-breath kind. The corridor lamp had guttered low. Across the way, Wren's door stood open, her bed empty, blanket folded neatly as if for inspection.

His heart dropped through the floor and kept going.

He found her at the end of the lower passage, in a round chamber he hadn't seen before, where the open-circle flame was carved vast into the floor and ringed with candles — hundreds of them, all burning with that wrong, inward lean. The Order stood gathered in a crescent, hooded now, silent, and at their center Aldous raised his arms, and his voice poured out honeyed and triumphant:

"—come home, little flame. Come to the altar. You'll never be lonely again—"

And Wren walked toward him, barefoot and silent, eyes open and utterly absent, humming the melody that made teeth ache — except now the candles were humming it too, and the stones, and something far below, deep under Vhalen, answering.

"Wren!" Silas's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip.

Her foot paused at the altar's first step.

He shoved through the hooded figures — they let him, too astonished, too certain of their numbers — and caught her hand, and her fingers were cold as well-water, and somewhere beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the whole city shivered.

Aldous's smile did not waver. That was the worst part. It did not waver at all.

"Master Kavanagh," he said gently. "You're interrupting a homecoming."
