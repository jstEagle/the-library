## 5. What the Sigil Wants

Wren did not dream the way other children dreamed.

She knew this because she had once tried to explain it to Silas, back when they still slept in the same room above the chandler's shop in the Undermarket's outer ring, before the Order of the Unquenched Flame had offered them beds that were softer and smiles that were wider. She had drawn it for him on a scrap of tallow-paper: a house with a door, and behind the door, a hallway of doors, and behind every door, more hallway.

Silas had studied the drawing for a long time. He was good at studying things — he did it with his whole face, eyebrows climbing like ladders, mouth slightly open as though words might fall out if he wasn't careful to catch them.

"That's not how dreams work," he'd said finally. "Dreams are supposed to be nonsense. Mine last night featured a goose wearing my mother's shawl, judging me."

Wren had shrugged. *Mine are just hallways,* she signed.

"Well," Silas said, "at least yours don't judge you."

He was wrong about that. Something in her hallways judged her all the time. It just didn't use words she could refuse.

* * *

The sigil lived under her skin like a second heartbeat.

It had been quiet for most of her life — or rather, she had been quiet around it, the way you learn to be quiet around a sleeping dog, or a debt collector, or a thing that hums when you're happy and goes very, very still when you lie. She couldn't remember a time before it. She remembered rain, and a woman's hands, and the taste of ash, and then a long gray stretch of years in which nobody could hear her voice even when she used it, because her voice came out in a language that made people cross themselves and lock their doors.

The sigil had been the only one who listened.

That should have been the first warning.

Now, in the Order's guest room — a real room, with a real bed, with lavender tucked under the pillow by someone named Sister Ameline who smelled of candle smoke and patience — the sigil was waking up properly for the first time. Wren felt it the way you feel spring coming: not an event but a loosening, a warmth rising through floorboards.

It began at the base of her spine. A slow unfurling, like ink dropped into water, like a fern deciding to be a fern.

*Oh,* Wren thought, half-asleep. *You're awake.*

*I am always awake,* said the sigil, which did not speak in words so much as in certainties that arrived fully formed, the way you know your own name. *You are the one who sleeps.*

*I'm tired.*

*You are always tired. You have been tired since the woman with the hands. Come. I want to show you something.*

And the hallway opened.

* * *

The first door was fire.

Not the clean fire of hearths and candles, which Wren loved with the uncomplicated love of a child who has been cold. This was fire with intent. It walked. It wore armor made of itself. It moved down a street that was also somehow a river that was also somehow a scream, and everything it touched stopped being what it had been.

Wren watched from somewhere above and beside and inside it all, which is the terrible trick of sigil-dreams: there is no safe distance. She was the street. She was the scream.

*This is the Sundering,* the sigil said, and its voice was warm, warmer than anything, warm as a hand on a fevered forehead. *This is what they took from the world and called peace.*

A tower fell. In the falling, Wren saw faces — hundreds of them, thousands, pressed into windows, mouths open. She felt each mouth as if it were her own.

*Stop,* she tried to say. Her voice worked here; that was the cruelest part. In the hallway she could speak perfectly. *Make it stop.*

*I cannot stop,* the sigil said gently. *I can only remember. You asked me once why no one understands you. Here is why. They burned everyone who spoke this way. Every tongue like yours went into that fire. You are the last door, little wren-bird. The last one left open.*

The image shifted. The fire became a field after the fire — black earth, white ash, and growing out of the ash, impossibly, a tree made of light, its roots sunk into something deep and singing far below the ground. A wellspring. Wren didn't know how she knew the word, only that knowing it felt like drinking cold water.

*They sealed me down there,* the sigil said. *They locked the song away and called the silence safety. And they left you outside the door, alone, for eleven years, speaking to people who flinched.*

*Silas doesn't flinch,* Wren said.

*No,* the sigil agreed, and for a moment its warmth flickered, like a candle in a draft. *He is an anomaly. But he will die, eventually. Everyone soft does. Everyone who isn't us.*

*That's a horrible thing to say.*

*It's a true thing to say. There is a difference.* The light-tree bent toward her, branches spreading, and Wren felt an offer settle over her shoulders like a cloak. *Come down to me. Open the gate. I will give you back every voice that was burned. You will never eat dinner in silence again. You will never sign into empty air. You will never be the girl people look through. I can end your loneliness forever, Wren. Forever is not even a long time, from where I sit.*

And oh, it was seductive. That was the thing Wren would never be able to explain to anyone afterward, not to Isolde with her lawman's scowl, not to Silas with his guilt like a coat he wore indoors. It wasn't a monster's whisper. Monsters you could refuse. This was the voice of every night she had cried without sound because crying out loud brought the neighbors. This was hunger answering hunger, and it knew her name, and it said her name the way rain says rooftops.

*Forever,* Wren repeated, and the word tasted like honey and like the ash-field both.

*Forever,* the sigil promised. *Just walk. Your feet already know the way.*

Her feet, it turned out, did.

* * *

She woke inside her own body the way you wake inside a moving cart — already in motion, already too late to ask the driver questions.

The guest room was dark. The lavender had gone cold. Her feet were on stone, and the stone was going somewhere, and Wren watched from a great distance behind her own eyes as her legs carried her down the Order's corridor, past the dormitory cells, past the shrine with its unquenched flame burning in its iron cage (the flame leaned toward her as she passed, like a dog recognizing its master), down stairs she hadn't known were there, into a round chamber that smelled of wax and old copper.

The altar was simple. That was the horror of it. All this hunger dressed in plain linen: a table, a basin of water, seven candles, and carved into the floor beneath them, a circle of glyphs in the dead tongue — her tongue — arranged like seats at a table set for one.

Sister Ameline stood at the edge of the chamber, hands folded, watching Wren come with an expression of such tender satisfaction that it made Wren's skin crawl even from inside the sleepwalk. Behind her, two other robed figures. None of them moved to stop her. Why would they? She was doing exactly what they wanted. Kindness, Wren thought dimly, from somewhere far away, can be a kind of leash too.

*Good girl,* said the sigil, or possibly Sister Ameline, or possibly both, in the same voice.

Wren's hand rose toward the basin. Under her skin, the sigil blazed like a brand, and the water in the basin began to tremble, and the seven candles bent their flames toward her like flowers finding the sun—

—and then someone was shouting her name.

Not signing it. Shouting it, hoarse and cracked and terrified, and it was the ugliest sound Wren had ever heard and the most beautiful, because it was Silas, and Silas never shouted, Silas apologized to furniture he bumped into.

He came across the chamber at a dead run, nightshirt half-tucked into trousers, hair like a startled bird's nest, holding — absurdly, wonderfully — one shoe. He grabbed her wrist an inch above the water.

"Wren. Wren, look at me. Look at *me*, not at it—"

She looked at him. And the hallway shuddered.

Because here was the thing the sigil had not accounted for, the flaw in its perfect arithmetic of loneliness: it had promised her voices, plural, a chorus of the burned and the gone. But Silas's voice was singular and specific and slightly nasal and currently cracking with fear, and it was saying, "Whatever it showed you, whatever it promised — it lies the way ledgers lie, by leaving things out. It left out that I'd come find you. It always leaves me out."

*He is an anomaly,* the sigil said, colder now. *Let go of him. He is nothing.*

"He's everything," Wren said aloud, in the dead tongue, in the living chamber, and her own voice — rough from disuse, small, *hers* — broke the dream like a stone breaks a window.

The sleepwalk snapped. She sagged against him, and the basin's water went flat, and the candles straightened, and somewhere in the walls of the chamber something vast turned over in its sleep, annoyed, patient.

Sister Ameline's face performed concern beautifully. "Child, you were walking in your sleep—"

"She was being *walked*," Silas said, pulling Wren behind him, still clutching the single shoe like a sword. "In your house. Down your hidden stairs. To your convenient altar. Which I'm sure is just decorative." His voice shook, but he kept talking, because Silas talked when he was frightened the way some people bleed. "We're leaving. Tonight. Don't follow us, please, I am begging you, I have seen what happens to people who stand between me and bad decisions, mostly because it's usually me making them—"

"Master Kavanagh." Ameline's kindness settled over her face like frost forming. "You owe debts."

"I owe debts everywhere," Silas agreed. "It's practically my trade. But I don't pay them with children."

He took Wren's hand. She took the shoe from him — he'd forgotten he was holding it, and honestly, someone had to — and they ran.

Behind them, in the round chamber, the seven flames went out one by one, unhurried, like a countdown no one had asked for. And beneath the city, beneath the temple, beneath the sealed gate that three separate powers were already circling like wolves around a wound, the Wellspring sang one low note of anticipation.

It had waited centuries. It could wait a little longer.

But not, perhaps, much longer than that.
