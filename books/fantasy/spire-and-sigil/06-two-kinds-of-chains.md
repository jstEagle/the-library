## 6. Two Kinds of Chains

The aqueduct had been built by kings who believed their city would last forever, and so far the city was doing its best to prove them wrong.

Isolde Vey crouched at the base of the third arch, where the old stone channel crossed the Rookery district like a stone river suspended in air. Below her, ten stories down, the evening markets were lighting their lamps one by one — little orange eyes blinking open in the dusk. Above her, the channel itself ran dry these days; whatever water had once flowed from the hills now moved through pipes of newer, uglier iron. The aqueduct was a spine with nothing left to carry.

Except, according to her informants, two fugitives and a stolen child.

She checked her crossbow for the fourth time. It was a habit, not a necessity. She'd maintained weapons since she was nine years old, when her brother Tam had first put one in her hands and said, *Respect it or put it down.* The memory arrived uninvited, as it always did lately, and she pushed it away with more force than usual.

*You're doing your job,* she told herself. *That's all this is.*

Her job. Her warrant sat folded against her heart, and she could feel it there like a coal that wouldn't cool. *Captain Isolde Vey is authorized to apprehend the scribe Silas Kavanagh and recover the anomaly known as Wren, by any means necessary.* By any means necessary. Four words that gave her permission to stop thinking, if she wanted to. Some days she wanted to very badly.

She'd read his ledgers instead. That was the problem. Three nights in the evidence room with Silas Kavanagh's confiscated accounts, and what she'd found wasn't the ledger of a criminal. It was the ledger of a drowning man. Columns of small sums paid toward a temple debt that grew faster than he could pay it. Notes in a cramped, careful hand: *Sold six luck-knots. All harmless. All blessed twice, just to be sure.* A margin note beside an entry for a fever-charm: *Mrs. Ollivan's boy slept through the night. Worth every lash of guilt.*

She'd laughed. Alone in the evidence room at midnight, she had actually laughed, and then felt sick about it, because none of it was funny. This man sold knotted string to anxious grandmothers and apologized to his own conscience about it, and somewhere above him the Inquisitor-General had signed a warrant with the word *anomaly* on it as though the child were a leak in a roof.

Footsteps. Soft ones, on the western stair.

Isolde went still and let the arch's shadow take her. Through the gap between stones she watched them come: a tall man moving with the careful exhaustion of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks, leading a small girl by the hand. The girl walked barefoot on ancient stone as though it were grass. That was wrong. Everything about them was slightly wrong — the way the man kept glancing upward as if expecting the sky to file a complaint, the way the girl looked not at the path but at the spaces between things.

They reached the middle of the span. There was nowhere else to go. Isolde stepped out of the shadow, crossbow leveled, and said the words she'd rehearsed:

"Silas Kavanagh. In the name of the Inquisition, you'll come with me."

She had expected him to run. She had prepared for him to run — the western stair was blocked, the eastern end was hers, and the drop below would discourage even a desperate man. What she had not prepared for was the way he simply stopped, sighed like a bellows collapsing, and turned around with the expression of a shopkeeper greeting a customer he'd been dreading all day.

"Captain Vey," he said. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my name."

"It's on the warrant."

"Of course it is." He glanced at the crossbow without much alarm. "Is that loaded? Only I ask because if you're going to shoot me, I'd rather you did it before Wren gets frightened. She's had a long week."

"Silas." The girl tugged his hand. She was looking directly at Isolde now, and her eyes were the color of deep water, and Isolde felt something in the air change pressure, the way it does before a storm decides.

"Stay behind me, little one," Silas murmured.

But the girl didn't stay behind him. She took one step forward, bare feet silent on the stone, tilted her head as though listening to something very far away — and spoke.

"*Tamuel Vey ash-kaelen.*"

The words came out in a language Isolde had never heard and yet understood instantly, the way you understand a voice calling from another room. Old. Older than walls. And inside the strange syllables, unmistakable, impossible: her brother's name.

The crossbow dipped. She let it dip.

"Say that again," Isolde heard herself say. Her voice came out scraped raw.

Wren cocked her head. "*Tamuel Vey ash-kaelen. Sera-vosh. Sera-vosh, im-mira.*" And though Isolde could not have translated a single word under torture, the meaning settled into her chest like cold rain: *He waits. He waits, and he is not angry.*

"That's not possible," Isolde said. "That's — those are tricks. The Order taught you—"

"The Order hasn't had her a day," Silas said quietly. "And Captain — I've spent years copying forbidden folios, and I can tell you the old tongue doesn't do tricks. It doesn't lie, either. It's too old for lying. Lying was invented later, along with taxes." He tried a smile; it didn't quite hold. "I don't know how she knows your brother's name. But I know she isn't making it up, because I've watched her say things no one taught her, and every time, it costs her something. Look at her hands."

Isolde looked. The girl's small fingers were trembling, and faint lines of light moved beneath the skin of her wrists like fish under ice. The sigil. The anomaly. The thing the General wanted back in a vault.

"She hears them," Silas said. "The dead. Or something older than the dead. I don't fully understand it, and I've stopped pretending I do. But I understand this much—" He took a breath, and here it came, the moment Isolde had been braced for since the rooftop in the Rookery: the plea, the bargain, the offer of gold or freedom or some smuggler's lie.

Instead, Silas Kavanagh simply told her the truth.

"The Inquisitor-General isn't trying to contain the Wellspring," he said. "He's trying to drain it. All of it. Every last drop of what's sealed beneath this city, siphoned into his private vaults, held in his hand alone. Your warrant isn't about protecting Vhalen from magic, Captain. It's about making sure only one man in Vhalen has any."

"You're lying," Isolde said automatically. "That's treason."

"Yes," Silas agreed pleasantly. "Most true things are, these days. Ask yourself — why send one captain, alone, quietly? Why not a company? If the child were merely dangerous, you'd have soldiers. If she were merely lost, you'd have clerks. You got neither. You got a pardon dangled in front of you — your brother's pardon, yes? I saw it in your face just now, when she said his name. You're not hunting us because you believe we're guilty. You're hunting us because they're holding something you love hostage."

The wind moved along the empty channel, carrying lamp-smoke and the smell of rain coming. Isolde stood very still, and hated him, and knew with terrible clarity that he was right.

She thought of the General's office, the way he'd never once looked at her while promising Tam's pardon — as though she were furniture that happened to hold a rank. She thought of the evidence room, and Mrs. Ollivan's sleeping boy. She thought of Tam himself, three years gone, arrested for a charm he'd bought to ease their mother's coughing, sentenced by men who called it justice and meant arithmetic.

"Sera-vosh," Wren said softly, watching her. "Im-mira."

*Not angry. Waiting.*

"I should arrest you," Isolde said. "Right now. Both of you. It's the law."

"It is," Silas said. "I've read most of it, out of boredom and then out of necessity. It's a fine law, mostly. Written by people who meant well and were tired." He spread his empty hands. "Here's my offer, Captain, and it's the only one I have: not gold, not escape. The truth. Come with us far enough to see it for yourself. One night. If I'm lying, arrest us both at dawn and sleep well after. If I'm not—" His voice gentled. "Then you get to decide whose chain you've been wearing. Because there are two kinds, Captain Vey. The kind they lock on you. And the kind you clasp yourself and call duty."

Below them, the market lamps burned on, indifferent. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Isolde looked at the crossbow in her hands — Tam's crossbow, technically, inherited along with his debts and his name — and then at the girl who had spoken a dead man's name in a dead tongue and made the dead sound patient.

"If this is a trap," Isolde said slowly, "I will put you through the evidence room wall."

"A fair and reasonable threat. I accept."

"And if you're lying about the General—"

"Then I'm the finest liar in Vhalen," Silas said, "and you may tell everyone you were deceived by a debt-ridden charm-seller, which will be humiliating for both of us."

Wren slipped her small hand into Isolde's free one. Isolde looked down at it in alarm — at the faint light swimming beneath the skin, warm as a banked fire — and found, to her considerable irritation, that she didn't want to let go.

"This way," Silas said, turning toward the dark mouth of the maintenance stair at the aqueduct's broken end. "It's a long way down, and most of it is on fire, metaphorically speaking. Mostly metaphorically."

Behind them, far across the rooftops, a bell began to ring — not the market bell, not the temple bell. The sharp triple-stroke of the Inquisition's summons, echoing off the wet stone of a hundred buildings.

Someone had found the empty safehouse. Someone was already moving.

Isolde tightened her grip on the child's hand, slung the crossbow across her back — *respect it or put it down*, Tam had said, and she was doing neither, she was simply carrying it, which felt suddenly like the truest description of her whole life — and followed the scribe into the dark.

*One night,* she promised herself. *One night, and then I decide.*

Above them, the bell rang on, and beneath their feet, deeper than any of them knew, something old and patient turned over in its sleep.
