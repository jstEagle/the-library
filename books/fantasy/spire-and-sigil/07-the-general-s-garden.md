## 7. The General's Garden

The Inquisitor-General's headquarters had always smelled of lye and candle smoke to Isolde Vey, the particular scent of a building scrubbed too often by people who were afraid of what might otherwise accumulate. Tonight it smelled of something else. Something green.

She noticed it the moment she passed the third-floor landing — a faint sweetness threading through the austerity, like crushed mint, like rain on turned earth. She stopped on the stairs with her hand on the banister and listened to her own heartbeat do something unprofessional.

*Gardens don't grow here,* she told herself. *Nothing grows here.*

She had come back to deliver her report on the aqueduct. That was what she told herself, anyway. The truth was uglier and simpler: she had let them go. She had stood on that rain-slick span of stone with her crossbow leveled at a tired scribe and a barefoot child, and when Wren had opened her mouth and spoken a dead man's name in a language three hundred years buried — *Tomas*, her brother's name, Tomas's name, said gently, the way you'd say it to someone grieving — Isolde had lowered the crossbow and watched them walk away into the dark.

She could have lied in her report. She had drafted three versions of the lie on the walk back, each one more plausible than the last. But every version felt like a stone dropped into a well she couldn't see the bottom of, so instead she wrote the truth in her neat, dutiful hand: *Contact made. Subject released pending further investigation.* A coward's phrasing. An honest coward's.

The General would read between the lines. The General always did.

So when she smelled the garden, she did not go straight to his office as protocol demanded. She went up.

* * *

The fourth floor was supposed to be archives. It was supposed to be dust and dead paper and clerks with ink-stained cuffs. Instead Isolde found a door she had never once noticed in eleven years of service, iron-banded and unmarked, standing slightly ajar at the end of a corridor that smelled increasingly of springtime.

She should have reported it. She noted this, distantly, the way one notes the obligations of a former life.

Inside was warmth. That was the first shock — actual warmth, humid and living, after years of stone and lye. The second shock was the light: no lamps, no candles, but a soft gold radiance rising from rows of glass vessels arranged along shelves that climbed toward a ceiling lost in leafy shadow. And growing out of the vessels — out of the *light* itself — were plants. Flowers she had no names for. Vines with silver undersides. A small tree heavy with fruit the color of bruises.

A garden. In the heart of the Inquisition. Tended by no gardener she had ever seen.

Isolde walked the aisles slowly, her boots loud on flagstones that should not exist, and it took her longer than it should have to understand what she was looking at. Because the vessels weren't empty. Each one held something beneath the soil-line, something pale and curled, and when she bent close to the nearest one her breath went out of her all at once.

Fingers. A hand, small and fine-boned, rooted in the vessel like a bulb.

On the shelf above it, a brass plaque, engraved in the General's precise hand: *MAREN DUSK, convicted 4th of Thaw. Source: hearth-charms, minor healing. Yield: exceptional.*

Isolde knew the name. Every officer in Vhalen knew the names they were required to know. Maren Dusk had been burned in the courtyard below this very building when Isolde was nineteen, a herb-witch from the river districts whose crimes, as read aloud, had amounted to curing a fever the temple physicians couldn't. Isolde had stood in formation for that execution. She had held her posture correctly. She had thought, at the time, that the law was the law, and grief was not an argument against it.

Now Maren Dusk grew flowers in a jar four floors above the courtyard where she'd died.

Isolde moved down the rows faster, reading plaques, and the names kept coming, and some of them she didn't know and some of them she did, and then —

*VEY, CORWIN. Convicted 11th of Frost. Source: weather-sense, storm-calling. Yield: dormant but viable.*

Her knees found the flagstones before she decided to kneel.

Corwin Vey. Her father. Executed when she was nine, in a year she had spent her whole adult life refusing to think about, in a purge the histories called *necessary*. She had been raised afterward by a temple aunt who spoke of him only in the past tense and never warmly. She had joined the Inquisition's civil arm — not the priesthood, never the priesthood — partly, if she was honest in the privacy of her own skull, because she wanted to believe the thing that had taken her father was at least orderly. Principled. *Fair*, in its terrible way.

He was here. What remained of him. Rooted in glass, feeding a General's private orchard, labeled like inventory.

Isolde knelt on the floor of the General's garden for a long time. She did not weep, or rather she wept the way soldiers weep — silently, efficiently, without changing expression — and when she finished she discovered her hands had gone cold and steady, which was worse than shaking and also more useful.

Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid: the General had promised her Tomas's pardon. Her brother, rotting in a debt-prison for smuggling he'd done to feed her family after their father's confiscation. *Bring me the scribe and the anomaly child,* the General had said, laying his hand on her shoulder like a benediction, *and your brother walks free.* She had believed it was justice being offered. A trade, even — one fugitive for one prisoner. The law's cold fairness.

But men who kept gardens like this did not pardon anyone. Men who kept gardens like this *harvested*. If she delivered Wren — a child carrying a living sigil, a wellspring of power beyond anything in these jars — the General would not release Tomas. He would plant him. Or he would simply keep him, forever, as the surest leash on the captain who had delivered the prize.

And the Wellspring itself. *Drain it for himself.* Silas Kavanagh had said it on the aqueduct, and she had wanted to dismiss it as a fugitive's desperation, and now she stood in the proof. A man who grew a garden from the dead did not intend to leave magic free in the world. He intended to own every drop of it, sealed behind iron doors, watered by whatever hands he chose to root.

Isolde stood. She took the brass plaque bearing her father's name off its shelf, and she put it in her coat pocket, because no one — not the General, not history, not God — was going to keep Corwin Vey filed as *inventory*.

Then she walked out of the garden, closed the door softly behind her, and went to find her report.

* * *

In the undercity, Silas Kavanagh was losing an argument with a kettle.

"It's not that I distrust your hospitality," he said to Bramble, the Order's round, cheerful quartermaster, "it's that I've met your hospitality before, and it asked me a great deal of questions about the child."

"She's sleeping," Bramble said, pouring tea with the serenity of a man who had never once been struck by a thrown kettle. "Safe. Fed. Loved."

"You keep saying *loved* like it's a security measure."

Wren slept in the next alcove, curled under a good blanket, and Silas hated how good the blanket was. Everything here was good — the food, the beds, the gentle voices. That was precisely the problem. He had spent ten years among the poor of Vhalen, and the poor were many things, but they were never this *smooth*. Kindness with rough edges was real. Kindness this polished had been sanded on purpose.

He was still turning the problem over when the pigeon arrived.

It came down the smugglers' shaft on whirring wings, a plain gray bird with a message tube, and it landed on Silas's knee with the confidence of an animal that had been trained by someone extremely competent. He worked the cap off with numb fingers and unfolded the slip inside.

No signature. No greeting. Just a string of characters in a cipher he recognized with a jolt — temple scriptorium shorthand, the kind used for inventory ledgers, the kind a lawwoman would have learned auditing confiscated goods:

*GARDEN ON FOURTH FLOOR. THEY GROW THE DEAD IN GLASS. MY FATHER IS ONE OF THEM. BURNING MY PAPERS NOW. GATE OPENS AT MOONSET. TRUST THE CHILD, NOT THE ORDER. — V*

Silas read it twice. Then a third time, slower, and by the end of the third reading his hands had stopped trembling and started doing something else entirely, something that felt dangerously like hope.

"Bramble," he said pleasantly, folding the message into his sleeve, "how soon can the Order move on the Wellspring gate?"

The quartermaster's smile stayed perfectly fixed. "Why, whenever the vessel is ready, scribe. Why do you ask?"

"No reason," said Silas, and began, very quietly, to plan his second escape in a week.

* * *

Back at headquarters, in the small stone room that served her as both office and cell, Captain Isolde Vey fed her warrant page by page into the stove.

It burned beautifully. Eleven years of oath and duty, reduced to orange lace in under a minute. She watched it go with the strange lightness of a woman stepping off a ledge and discovering, mid-fall, that she had always known how to fly.

There would be consequences. There already were — she could hear boots in the corridor, the General's private guard making their rounds ahead of schedule, the whole building tightening like a fist. Moonset was hours away. The Order would move on the gate tonight; the General's garden needed watering, and there was only one vessel in Vhalen rich enough for what he meant to plant.

Isolde took out a fresh sheet of paper, wrote her coded warning, and sent it by pigeon through the archive window she'd bribed a clerk to leave unlatched. Then she buckled on her sword, pocketed her father's name, and went looking for two fugitives in a city full of hunters.

Above her, unseen, the first clouds of the night gathered over Vhalen — and somewhere far below, old and patient, the Wellspring waited to learn who would hold the key.

Or whether anyone would dare to break it.
