## 8. Nightfall Over Vhalen

The bells began at dusk.

Not the temple bells — those had been silent since the Sundering, their throats filled with lead by men who feared what they might call down. These were the alarm bells of the wardens' towers, and they rang wrong: ragged, overlapping, each tower starting before the last had finished, as if the whole city were stammering.

Silas stood at the window of the safehouse above the tannery lane and watched Vhalen come apart in the rain.

Fires bloomed in the merchant quarter. Not the warm orange of hearths but the white-blue of something burning that had no business burning — alchemical stores, he guessed, or worse. A column of the General's private guard marched along the Processional with lanterns hooded, moving not toward the fires but away from them, toward the temple hill. And behind them, at a distance calculated to look like coincidence, came the gray cloaks of the Order of the Unquenched Flame, walking with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where everyone else was going.

"Everyone's invited," Silas said to no one. "Nobody brought a gift."

Behind him, Wren sat cross-legged on the floor with Isolde's spare cloak pooled around her like a small gray mountain, turning one of Silas's harmless charms over in her hands — a luck-knot, worth perhaps two coppers, the kind of thing he'd sold a thousand times to market wives who wanted magic the way they wanted salt: a pinch of it, safely diluted. The sigil under her skin had gone quiet since the aqueduct, but quiet, Silas had learned, was not the same as asleep. It was the quiet of a held breath.

She looked up at him and signed something quick and fluid. *They're coming for me.*

"They're coming for all of us," Silas said. "It's very flattering, actually. I've never been popular before. It took me thirty-one years and a citywide emergency."

Wren's mouth twitched. Even now — especially now — she gave him that look, the one that said *you are ridiculous and I have decided to keep you anyway.* He'd grown strangely dependent on it. It was, he suspected, the only honest currency left in Vhalen tonight.

Isolde came up the stairs two at a time, already armored, her captain's sash torn off and stuffed in her belt like a confession she hadn't finished making. She'd spent the afternoon doing something she refused to describe, and she had ink on her fingers and a singed smell about her person, and when Silas raised an eyebrow she said, "I burned some paperwork," in a tone that closed the subject forever.

"The tunnels," she said instead, unrolling a map on the table — an old one, pre-Sundering, stolen from her own headquarters in a theft she was clearly still proud of. "The General will take the Processional stairs. The Order knows the pilgrim passages. That leaves us—" her finger traced a line through the tannery district, down past the old cisterns, "—the drain."

Silas looked at the line. Then at her. "The drain."

"The storm drain."

"Isolde. My love for you is well documented and entirely platonic, but you are asking me to enter the sewer."

"I'm asking you to enter *history*," she said, "through the sewer. Everyone great has done it. There's probably a plaque."

"There is not a plaque."

"There could be. If we win."

Wren was already pulling on her boots.

* * *

The drain smelled exactly as promised, which is to say it smelled like Vhalen's whole history of bad decisions, concentrated and gently warmed. Silas walked with his sleeve over his nose and his lantern held high, cataloguing the indignities aloud because if he stopped talking he would start thinking, and thinking was a luxury they couldn't afford.

"—and I want it noted that my family's debt ledger did not include a line item for sewage. The temple was very thorough. They charged me for candle wax. Candle wax, Isolde. And yet somehow 'wading' went unbilled—"

"Silas." Isolde's voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when she was listening to something he couldn't hear. "Stop walking."

He stopped. The water around his ankles kept moving, which felt suddenly obscene.

Ahead, the tunnel bent toward the cistern junction, and from somewhere beyond that bend came a sound like a giant clearing its throat — a deep, grinding groan of stone against stone. Dust sifted down through the lantern light. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of earth and the whole panicking weight of the city, another bell began to ring.

"The Inquisition's engineers," Isolde breathed. "They're collapsing the old passages. Sealing the approaches so only their own route stays open." Her jaw tightened. "The General isn't leaving anything to chance. Or anyone else a door."

"Then we go around," Silas said. "There's always an around. I've built my entire life on arounds."

"There's one around." She pointed up, to where a rusted maintenance ladder climbed the tunnel wall into a black throat of older brickwork. "The cistern gallery. Pre-kingdom construction. Which means—"

"Which means Wren might know it." Silas turned. The girl was staring at the ladder with an expression he'd learned to read the way sailors read weather: the sigil stirring, memory rising in her that wasn't hers. Her hands moved slowly, uncertainly. *I have been here. Before. When it was not dark.*

"Well," Silas said lightly, because someone had to be light, and apparently it was going to be him until the day he died. "Lead on, then. Mind the gap. All the gaps. There appear to be nothing but gaps."

She climbed. Isolde followed. Silas took one last look at the reasonable, modern, honest-to-the-gods sewer behind him, sighed the sigh of a man whose life had gone entirely off the ledger, and climbed after them.

* * *

The cistern gallery was a cathedral that had drowned.

Their lantern found arches upon arches marching off into darkness, pillars furred with mineral bloom, the air cold and tasting of deep stone. Water lay everywhere — shallow sheets of it mirroring the vaults overhead, so that they seemed to walk across the sky of some buried heaven. And carved into every pillar, every arch, every lintel: glyphs. Thousands of them. The dead tongue, written in stone by hands that had trusted the future to keep reading.

Silas had copied forbidden folios for nine years in secret, hunched over his desk with the shutters barred, and none of it — none of it — had prepared him for the sheer *volume* of it. This wasn't hidden knowledge. This had been a wall of the world once. People had walked beneath these words daily and thought nothing of it, the way he thought nothing of rain.

Wren walked ahead of them with her hand trailing along the pillars, and where she touched, the glyphs woke.

It was faint — a shimmer, a warmth, letters filling with pale gold light like veins remembering blood — but it spread from her fingertips in slow ripples, pillar to pillar, arch to arch, until the whole gallery glimmered around them and their shadows danced on water. Wren laughed. Actually laughed, a small startled sound, the first Silas had ever heard from her, and pressed both palms flat against a pillar so that a whole column of script blazed up around her like a tree made of morning.

"It likes her," Silas whispered.

"It's *her*," Isolde said quietly. "Or she's it. I don't think there's a difference anymore." She was looking at the light with an expression Silas hadn't seen on her before — not the hard watchfulness of the Captain of Ash, but something younger and more frightened and more hopeful, the face of a woman watching a door open that she'd been told led nowhere. "My brother used to say the ban didn't kill magic. It just made it hold its breath." Her voice caught slightly. "I told him he was being poetic."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was being accurate, and I'd give a great deal to tell him so."

Silas reached over and squeezed her gauntleted hand, briefly, awkwardly, the way one pats a large and dangerous dog. She let him. Progress.

Then the gallery shuddered.

Dust rained golden through the glyph-light. Far behind them, back toward the drain, came a boom like a struck bell, and then another, closer, and the mirror-still water shivered into rings. Somewhere in the dark ahead, stone cracked — a long, splintering sigh, the sound of an arch deciding whether to remain an arch.

"They're bringing the whole gallery down," Isolde snapped. "Cutting our path and theirs behind them. Move! Wren, which way?"

But Wren had gone rigid, her head cocked, her eyes wide and lit from within. Her hands flew, fast and urgent. *Down. It wants us down. The gate is waking and it is calling and I can hear it through the floor—*

"No," Silas said, before he could stop himself. "No, we don't answer calls from floors. Floors calling you is how every horror story in every market tavern begins—"

Another crack, much nearer. A pillar to their left leaned, groaned, and shed its capital in a spray of ancient masonry. The gallery was failing around them, and behind them the way back was already gone, swallowed in rolling dust, and ahead—

Ahead, the floor opened.

Not collapsed — *opened*, seams parting along lines too straight and too deliberate to be accident, revealing a stairway spiraling down into warmth and gold-veined dark. Air rose out of it carrying a scent Silas knew from nowhere and recognized instantly: rain on hot stone, green things growing, the smell of the world before anyone decided to be afraid of it.

The Wellspring, breathing.

Isolde drew her sword — not at anything, just drew it, the way a man grips a rail in a storm. "That stair wasn't there a moment ago."

"No," Silas agreed faintly. "It very much was not."

Wren took his hand. Her small fingers were trembling, but her grip was iron, and when she looked up at him her eyes were wet and fierce and utterly certain. She signed with her free hand, slow enough that even his terrified brain could follow.

*It opened for us. Not for them. We go first or we lose it.*

"She's right," Isolde said grimly. "If the General reaches the gate before the sigil does—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. They had all seen the General's garden, in one way or another. They knew what waited in a world where he got there first.

Above them, muffled and distant, the alarm bells faltered — and then, impossibly, began ringing in unison. One voice. One rhythm. As if the towers themselves had remembered what they were for.

Silas looked at the stair. He looked at Wren's hand in his. He thought about debt ledgers and candle wax and thirty-one years of keeping his head down, and he heard himself laugh — a thin, giddy, entirely unhinged sound.

"You know," he said, stepping onto the first stair, "I always assumed I'd die owing money."

The gallery groaned its last above them. Gold light poured up out of the deep like dawn arriving from the wrong direction. And far below, at the bottom of everything Vhalen had buried, something vast and patient turned its attention toward the three small figures descending through the dark — and toward the fourth figure who had arrived before them, standing at the sealed gate with his hands folded and his smile ready, waiting to collect.

The Inquisitor-General turned. Even at this distance, even across all that shining dark, Silas could feel the warmth of his welcome.

"Right on time," the General called pleasantly, as the stair crumbled away behind them. "I do hate to begin without the guests of honor."
