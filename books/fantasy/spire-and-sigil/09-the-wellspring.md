## 9. The Wellspring

### Silas

The last stair gave out beneath them with a sound like a snapped bone, and Silas caught Wren by the collar as the step where she'd been standing crumbled into the dark. Below them, the air changed. It was hard to describe to anyone who hadn't spent a decade copying forbidden glyphs by candlelight — but the air at the bottom of that stair was *thick*, the way honey is thick, the way a held breath is thick.

"Careful," he whispered, though whispering seemed absurd. The whole city above them was screaming. He could feel it through the stone: boots on marble, bells rung ragged, the Inquisition's horns sounding three notes over and over, which meant *heresy in motion*.

Wren tugged his sleeve and pointed.

The gate was not what he had imagined. He had imagined doors — great bronze things with chains, maybe, or a wall of fused glass like something melted in a fire. Instead there was simply a place where the tunnel stopped pretending to be a tunnel. The stone ahead of them was smooth and pale as the inside of a shell, and across its surface moved lines of light, slow and patient, like veins under skin. Like writing.

"It's been waiting," Silas said, and his voice cracked on the second word. "All this time. It's just been *waiting*."

Wren looked up at him. In the light of the sigil-veins her face was very old and very young at once, the way it always went when the thing inside her stirred. She raised one small hand and pressed it flat against the pale stone, and every line of light in the wall leaned toward her fingers like grass toward sun.

"Don't," said Isolde behind them, quietly. "Not yet. Not until we know who's coming down those stairs after us."

Silas turned. Far above, torchlight bobbed and multiplied. Voices echoed down the shaft — the Order's soft, rehearsed cadences; the General's guards barking in formation; somewhere, horribly, an Inquisitor hymn, sung in unison, the one they sang at executions.

"Everyone we've ever met," Silas said, "is coming to this room."

"Then let's be early," said Isolde, and drew her sword.

* * *

### Isolde

Isolde had arrested forty-one people in her career for crimes of magic. She remembered every name. She had told herself, for years, that remembering them was a form of respect. Standing now in the heart of the world with her warrant burned to ash in her breast pocket, she understood it had been a form of cowardice instead.

The first of the Order reached the chamber at the count of two hundred. They came without torches, which should have frightened her more than it did — twelve figures in undyed wool, moving with the calm of people who have already decided how everything will go. Their leader was a woman with kind eyes and ink-stained fingertips, the same woman who had pressed bread into Wren's hands in the Undermarket and called her *little vessel*.

"Captain Vey." The woman inclined her head, gracious as a hostess. "And the scribe. You've brought her beautifully. We're grateful."

"You're welcome to be grateful from over there," Isolde said.

The woman's smile did not waver. "You misunderstand. There's no faction here. There's only the work. The child opens the gate; the Flame is unquenched; the Wellspring flows where it *should* flow — into hands that understand restraint." Her gaze slid past Isolde's sword as if the sword were furniture. "Better us than the General. Surely even you can see that."

"I can see," Isolde said, "that everyone who wants this power has a speech prepared about why they deserve it."

Behind the Order, the stairs filled with red-and-black. The Inquisitor-General himself came last, unhurried, his white gloves immaculate despite the collapsing tunnels, and Isolde felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world, because she knew those gloves. She had seen them sign her brother's sentence. She had seen them sign pardons too — including, folded in her pocket until three nights ago, her own.

"Captain Vey," the General said warmly. "I confess I hoped you'd die in the chase. It would have been tidier. But there's a certain poetry in your being here to watch."

"There's going to be poetry," Isolde said, "in a lot of things today."

He gestured, almost gently, and six guards leveled crossbows at Silas and Wren.

"Now then," said the General, to the child, in the tone of a man asking after someone's grandmother. "Let's open it, shall we?"

* * *

### Wren

*(She could not say the words, so the words said themselves.)*

Cold. Cold like the well-water cold, cold like the word *mother* before she lost it. The men in red want her hand on the door. The door wants her hand on the door. Everyone wants her hand on the door and nobody, nobody in the whole grey city, has once asked what *she* wants.

Except.

Except the scribe, who reads aloud to her badly, mispronouncing everything, making the dead tongue stumble like a drunk uncle, and laughs at himself when he does. Except the captain, whose name means *island* in the old language, who stood between her and the arrows without being asked.

The sigil uncoils along her arm, warm now, so warm, whispering in the voice of everyone she has ever lost: *open me and never be alone again.*

Wren looks at Silas Kavanagh, who is holding a scribe's knife the way a man holds a spoon against a bear, and she thinks: *alone is a thing I already fixed.*

She steps back from the door.

The General's face goes through several interesting changes.

* * *

### Silas

It happened fast after that. It always happens fast, Silas thought later, at the exact moments you most need it to happen slowly.

The General signaled; the crossbows fired; Isolde moved like weather and put herself and her shield in the way, and two bolts shrieked off iron while a third buried itself in Silas's shoulder, which hurt considerably more than he had budgeted for. The Order surged forward chanting, and their chant *worked* — flame leapt up along their arms, real flame, hoarded flame, decades of stolen embers spent all at once — and the General's guards broke like a dropped plate.

And through all of it, the gate drank. Every spark, every scream, every death in the room fed back into the pale stone, and the veins of light brightened, and Silas understood with sick clarity that the Sundering War had started exactly like this. Not with an army. With a room full of people reaching for the same thing.

The sigil spoke to him then. Not through Wren — through him, finding the archive folios copied in secret, the years of longing, the debt-ledger arithmetic of a life too small. It offered, the way a flood offers water.

*I can save them,* it said, in his own handwriting. *Take me and no one here dies. Take me and the debt is nothing. Take me and you will never count coins again.*

And oh, it was beautiful. That was the trap of it. He saw himself remade — Vhalen healed, the ban undone, Wren safe behind walls of light he alone could raise —

"No," Silas said aloud, to a thing made of light, in a room full of people trying to kill each other. "That's how *he* starts. That's how all of them start."

He looked at Isolde, bleeding, braced against the shield, holding the line because holding lines was the only prayer she knew.

"Isolde! It talks to everyone — it's offering everyone — we break it *together* or it takes one of us!"

She understood in half a heartbeat, because she was the sort of person who understood things in half heartbeats and regretted them for years. "Together," she said. "On three."

"One," said Silas, and took Wren's hand, because if the sigil lived in her then she would feel it break, and she should not feel it alone.

"Two," said Isolde, and dropped her shield, and stepped out from behind it into the open, which was the bravest thing Silas had ever watched anyone do.

"*Three*," they said together — scribe and lawwoman, ink and iron — and drove the scribe's knife and the captain's broken sword-point into the brightest vein of light at the same instant, the way you crack an egg, the way you break a seal, the way two people who trust each other completely do the one thing neither could survive doing alone.

* * *

### Isolde

The world went white, then quiet, then *green*.

Not destruction. That was the miracle she would spend the rest of her life failing to explain properly. The sigil didn't shatter like glass; it shattered like a seed pod. Light burst outward through the stone, through the floor, through the roots of the city itself, and everywhere it touched, things grew. Moss raced up the walls. Water rose singing through cracks that had been dry since the Sundering. The General's stolen flames guttered out — not extinguished, *released*, streaming off his soldiers' arms and up into the dark like sparks returning to a fire.

The Inquisitor-General screamed. Not in pain — in outrage, which Isolde found deeply satisfying. He lunged for the fading gate as if he could gather the light back into his gloves, and the pale stone, suddenly only stone, simply let him beat his fists against it.

The Order's leader stood very still amid the green, watching moss climb her sleeves, and Isolde saw the exact moment the woman's faith died and something else — grief, maybe, or finally, *rest* — took its place.

Wren was laughing. Isolde had never heard her laugh. It was a small rusty sound, like a gate opening after years, and it was the best thing Isolde had ever heard, better than her brother's pardon would ever have been, better than any promotion, any medal, any clean record.

Silas sat down hard on the wet ground, clutching his shoulder, grinning like an idiot.

"We're going to be arrested," he said.

"By whom?" Isolde asked.

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the bells of Vhalen began to ring — and kept ringing, wild and unscheduled and joyful, because somewhere far above, ordinary people were waking to find small impossible things happening in their kitchens, and the law that forbade it had just become a law against the sunrise.

Isolde laughed too. Then she picked up her broken sword, pointed it at the stairs, and said:

"All right. Now comes the hard part."

* * *

### Silas

They climbed out of the earth at dawn, the three of them, filthy and bleeding and lighter than they had any right to be. The city spread below them in the early light, and everywhere Silas looked, Vhalen was *changing* — fountains running clear where they'd been dry, old wardstones glowing softly in doorways, a baker standing in the street with flour-white hands raised while a stray thread of light wound around his fingers like a cat greeting him after a long absence.

Wren tugged his sleeve. Pointed at the sky, where the rain had stopped for the first time in weeks.

Then she opened her mouth, and in a voice rusty and small and entirely her own — not the dead tongue, not the sigil's whisper, but plain, living, nine-year-old speech — she said:

"What happens now?"

Silas looked at Isolde. Isolde looked at Silas. Somewhere below, the temple bells were still ringing, and somewhere above them, the laws of an empire were quietly becoming kindling.

"Honestly?" Silas said. "No idea. But I know a market that opens at eight, and I'm done counting coins."

Hand in hand in hand, they walked down into the city that remembered.
