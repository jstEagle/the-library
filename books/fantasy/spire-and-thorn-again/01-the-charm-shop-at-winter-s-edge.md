## 1. The Charm Shop at Winter's Edge

The snow came down on Vell like ash that had forgotten what it was burning.

Tamsin Sterling watched it from behind his shop window, breath fogging the glass in slow, patient clouds, and counted the bells of the garrison church as they rang the ninth hour. Nine bells meant the market was lawful. Nine bells meant he could turn his sign, unlatch the door, and begin the day's small theater of being nobody at all.

He had gotten very good at being nobody. Three years of practice will do that.

The shop itself was narrow and deep as a coffin, wedged between a chandler's and a cobbler's on Cordwainer's Row, where the buildings leaned so close overhead that the sky showed only as a gray ribbon. Shelves ran up both walls to the ceiling beams, crowded with the inventory of an honest man: luck-knots braided from red thread and river reed; mended-bone charms, chicken bones knitted back together with copper wire and sold to farmers whose children had taken falls from haylofts; dried lavender against bad dreams; iron nails bent into horseshoe shapes for door lintels; little clay whistles shaped like larks, which made no sound at all when blown, because Tamsin had never quite trusted himself to make them sing.

Everything in the shop was exactly what it appeared to be. That was the whole art of it. A charm-seller in occupied Vell could survive only if every single thing he sold was useless.

Magic had been outlawed for eleven years now, since the purges began — since the Inquisition came north with its ledgers and its firewood and its terrible, patient courtesy. The edicts were posted at every gate in letters tall enough to read from horseback: *No practitioners. No covens. No commerce in the uncanny.* The penalty was written smaller, which somehow made it worse, the way a whisper is worse than a shout. *By flame.*

And so Tamsin sold knots and bones and lavender, and he smiled his shopkeeper's smile, and three years had passed without a single customer looking at him twice.

Except for the wren.

* * *

She lived in a cage above the counter, a wren no bigger than a fistful of feathers, brown as a winter twig, with one pale fleck at her throat like a drop of milk. He had bought her two autumns ago from a fowler at the Southgate market because she had looked at him — really looked, the way nothing in Vell was permitted to look at anything — and because he had been lonely in a way he could not name even to himself.

He should have left her there. He told himself that most mornings.

"Wind's turning," she said, in her voice like a dry leaf dragged over stone. Wrens did not have pretty voices. Hers sounded like something old wearing a small body as a disguise. "Turning hard out of the north. You'll want to bank the fire before dark."

"Mm," said Tamsin, arranging luck-knots on their pegs, not looking at her.

"And there's a dog been circling the block since dawn. Big one. Gray muzzle, but shoulders like a barrel. It isn't lost."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"You never do." She hopped along her perch, claws ticking. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

He turned his back on her and opened the shutters, and the cold poured in smelling of coal smoke and snow, and he let it, because the cold was honest and he was not, and some small stubborn part of him found that comforting.

This was the arrangement they had, the wren and he. She spoke. He did not hear. She warned. He did not listen. And if ever the Inquisition kicked his door open and found a talking bird in his shop, well — birds chattered. Birds mimicked. Everyone knew that a wren could be taught a dozen phrases by a patient hand, and Tamsin Sterling was a patient man, wasn't he? Wasn't that what patience was for?

He had never taught her a thing. She had simply begun speaking to him the second week, in the flat accent of the Thornwalk far to the north, and he had sat down on the floor of his own shop with his back against the counter and shaken until his teeth clicked, because he had not heard another voice answer his gift since his mother died, and he had sworn he never would again.

Then he had stood up, wiped his face, and put a cloth over her cage.

The cloth stayed on at night. In the day he took it off, because a covered bird drew questions and an uncovered one did not, and because — though he would have bitten off his own tongue before admitting it — he liked to see her. He liked the small green light of her eye tracking him across the room. He liked that somewhere in the world, one creature knew precisely what he was and said so, daily, mercilessly, and was too small to hang for it.

"Good morning, mistress," he said to the first customer through the door, a cobbler's wife buying lavender against nightmares. "Two coppers the bundle, or three for the fine cut."

His voice was pleasant and empty as a swept room. He had practiced that too.

* * *

The morning passed in the ordinary way of mornings, which is to say it passed slowly and then all at once, the way water gathers at the lip of a gutter before letting go.

A farmer's boy bought a mended-bone charm for his sister's wrist, and Tamsin wrapped it in brown paper and tied it with plain string and said, "There's nothing in it but bone and wire, mind, but folk say the mending itself is lucky." The boy nodded solemnly, paid his copper, and left believing exactly the right amount of nothing.

A widow bought three luck-knots, one for each of her sons gone south to the garrison levies. Tamsin did not tell her that luck-knots kept no luck. He told her they were handsome, which was true, and that red thread wore well, which was also true, and she went out into the snow clutching them like talismans, and he watched her go and felt the familiar sour taste rise at the back of his throat.

It was not guilt, exactly. Or it was guilt, but a strange domesticated breed of it, housebroken, fed on scraps. He had made his peace in the only way peace was available: he sold hope honestly labeled as trinkets, and if the trinkets worked anyway — if the widow's sons came home, if the farmer's daughter's bones knit straight and strong — then that was between the world and whatever old covenant still leaked through the cracks, and not between him and the fire.

That was the story he told himself. He told it every day. Stories repeated often enough grow calluses.

The trouble was the animals.

They found him. They always found him. There was a cat who slept under his eaves and narrated her dreams to him at night in a voice like wet gravel. Pigeons on the roof argued politics. Once, memorably, a carthorse outside the chandler's had fixed him with one rolling eye and said, *You smell like my grandmother's field,* and Tamsin had walked away mid-transaction and been sick behind the woodpile.

Because here was the truth under the floorboards of his life, the truth he kept wrapped in more layers than the wren's cage: Tamsin Sterling was a beast-speaker. Had been since before he could walk, born the last son of a line that had spoken with the wild things since before Vell had walls, since before the Spire stood watch over the thorn-realm, since the covenant — but no. He did not think about the covenant. Thinking about the covenant was a luxury of dead people.

He thought about it constantly.

Every animal in the city spoke to him, all day, unbidden, a tide of small voices he could not shut off any more than he could shut off his ears. So he had learned the other discipline, the harder one: he answered nothing. He gave them no sign. A beast-speaker who answers is a beacon; a beast-speaker who walks silent through the noise of the world is just a man with a headache. Three years of headache. He had gotten so good at silence that sometimes he dreamed in it, dreamed of standing in a meadow while every living thing called his name and his mouth was full of stones.

The wren was the one crack in the dam, and he hated her for it, and he fed her fat mealworms every morning from his own coin.

"There's that boy again," she said, around noon.

"What boy?" He was grinding dried lavender in a mortar, and the smell made the whole shop drowsy.

"The one who stands across the street. Third time this week. Thin coat, no hat. He's watching your door, not your window. Watching the door like he's counting who goes in and out." A pause, feathers rustling. "I don't care for him."

"He's waiting for the cobbler's, probably. Or courting someone."

"At noon, in falling snow, without a coat."

"People are fools about love."

"People are fools," she agreed. "But this one smells wrong. Not magic-wrong. Fear-wrong. He's afraid of your door, and he keeps coming back to it anyway. That's not courting. That's being made to do something."

Tamsin set down the mortar. Through the window, past the falling snow, he could see the boy: perhaps fourteen, hollow-cheeked, standing too still beneath the leaning eaves of the chandler's, hands jammed in armpits, eyes fixed on the shop door with the flat persistence of a creature guarding something.

Tamsin looked away first. He always looked away first. Looking too long at anyone in Vell was a mistake; looking too long at someone who might be watching for watchers was a catastrophe.

"He's gone," the wren reported later. "Walked off toward Tanner's Lane. Quick, once he'd decided. Like a lad late for supper."

"Good," said Tamsin, and hated how much the word cost him.

* * *

At dusk he closed early, as the law required — shops shuttered by the sixth bell in winter, streets empty by the seventh, curfew enforced by patrols carrying lanterns and short swords and lists. He banked the fire, as the wren had instructed and he had pretended not to hear, and cooked his supper of barley and salt pork in the little room behind the shop, eating standing up, as he always did, because sitting down to a table laid for one had become more than he could manage some nights, and this was one of those nights, the snow thickening past the window, the city going quiet in that particular strangled way Vell had, quiet not of peace but of held breath.

Three years. He had come to Vell after the purge with nothing but his mother's ring sewn into his boot heel and a name borrowed from a dead cousin, and he had built this: four walls, a legal trade, a reputation for harmlessness so thorough that even the tax assessor yawned in his presence. It was a good life. It was a life. Some days he could almost feel the difference.

He uncovered the wren's cage and set a dish of mealworms on the counter beside it.

"Eat," he said. "Before I change my mind."

She ate, daintily, head cocking. Then: "Storm tonight. Bad one. Off the mountains." She said it casually, the way she said everything, but Tamsin had learned to hear the second layer under her words, the way you learn to hear a second heartbeat in a house.

"You said the wind was turning north this morning."

"I said a great many things this morning."

"About the dog?"

"Still circling. It's gone now. Went north." She stopped eating. Her eye, black and bright as a peppercorn, fixed on him. "North is where the Spire is, little merchant."

"Don't call me that."

"Little merchant," she repeated sweetly, "who talks to birds."

"I don't talk to birds. I talk to myself, badly, and you eavesdrop."

"One of us is lying," she said, "and one of us is dinner-sized, and yet here we are, three winters together, and no one has burned."

He turned away and busied his hands with the fire, feeding it split oak, watching the flames take. The heat pressed against his shins. Outside, the snow fell thicker, erasing the street, the roofs, the distant walls, the whole grudging city, until Vell seemed less a place than an idea someone was having trouble remembering.

"Something's coming," the wren said quietly, behind him. No mockery in it now. "I've felt it for six days. Under my breastbone, like a second pulse. The rats have gone strange — they're leaving the cellars, all of them, moving west in lines, and rats don't march, Tamsin. Rats scatter. When they march, it's because something older than fear is telling them where to go."

"Rats leave cellars when ships unload grain. It's winter trade—"

"When did a grain ship last dock? The river's ice-bound. You know that. You buy your flour from the cart-train like everyone else." Her voice dropped further, until it was barely a stir of air. "Something is walking toward this city. Something that makes rats hold formation. And today a boy stood across your street and was afraid of your door, and came back anyway, three times, because something is making him."

Tamsin stood with his back to her, firelight crawling up his arms, and felt the old ache open in his chest — the ache he lived with the way a man lives with a healed-badly break, a weather in the bone. His gift stirred toward her words like a plant toward a window. Every instinct he had spent three years strangling leaned forward at once, wanting to ask, wanting to listen, wanting to send his senses out into the frozen dark and touch the minds of every rat and crow and cur within a mile and *know.*

He strangled it again. He was good at it. He had had practice.

"It's a storm," he said. "Storms frighten everything. Go to sleep."

"Tamsin."

"*Go to sleep.*"

Silence. Then the soft sound of her settling onto her perch, feathers fluffing against the cold, and one last murmur, almost tender, almost pitying:

"The wind's turning, little merchant. It's turning, and it's bringing something with it, and when it knocks, you'll want to remember that you once knew how to open doors."

He blew out the lamp. He lay down on his narrow bed in his narrow room behind his narrow shop, in the city that had swallowed his name, and listened to the storm build against the mountains of the north like a throat clearing before speech.

Sleep came late and thin. And in the gray hour before dawn, half-dreaming, he heard — or dreamed he heard, which amounted to the same thing — every animal in the street below fall silent at once. The cat under the eaves. The pigeons. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

As if something were passing through the snow that all of them, in their different tongues, recognized.

As if it had stopped outside his door.
