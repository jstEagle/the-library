## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The silence woke him.

Not the absence of sound — Vell was never silent, even in the dead of curfew, even under snow. There was always the creak of timber settling, the groan of the harbor ice, the small industrious noises of rats in walls and mice in rafters, the city's animal population going about its secret business beneath the human one. Tamsin had spent three years learning to sleep inside that second city's murmur, the way a man learns to sleep beside a river. It had become, without his ever admitting it, a comfort. Proof that the world still spoke, even if he could not answer.

Now it had stopped.

He lay still in the dark and listened with his whole body, the way his mother had taught him to listen — not with the ears alone but with the skin, the sternum, the roots of the teeth. *Listen with everything you are,* she used to say, kneeling in the garden behind their old house in Aldermoor with her palms flat against the turned earth, *because everything you are is what they speak to.* He had been seven. The garden had been full of worms saying slow green things and sparrows quarreling over the fat of the world, and he had understood all of it at once, effortlessly, the way other children understood their mother's voice.

He understood nothing now. That was what frightened him. Not silence — silence could be weather, could be a hawk's shadow passing over the street, could be any number of innocent things. But this silence had a shape. It had edges. It sat against his door like a weight set down carefully by careful hands.

The storm had arrived in the night, riding down out of the mountains the way the wren had said it would, and it threw itself against the shutters in long exhausted lunges. Snow came through every gap the carpenters had missed and gathered in fine white seams along the window frame. Curfew had been called at dusk — the criers' bells, hoarse with cold, had gone street to street: *All souls within doors by dark bell. All fires banked. The Inquisitor's watch is abroad.* Which meant that whoever stood outside his door in the gray hour before dawn, standing so quietly that even the rats held their breath, was either breaking the law or enforcing it.

Tamsin did not move for a long time. He counted his own heartbeats and found them too fast, and made himself slower, the way you gentled a frightened animal. *You are a charm-seller,* he told himself, in the flat voice he used for such lies. *You sell luck-knots and mended-bone charms and pretty glass beads from the southern trade. You have no gift. You have never had a gift. You are a shopkeeper lying awake because of a storm.*

But the silence outside his door did not believe him either.

Then came the knock.

It was not a knock like a fist or a knocker or a boot. It was three soft sounds, spaced like words, like a sentence spoken very carefully by someone who had learned knocking only recently and from a description. And underneath it — woven into it, or perhaps carried on it the way scent is carried on wind — came syllables.

Tamsin sat up so quickly that the blanket slid to the floor and he did not notice.

The language had no name that humans used. His mother had called it the Old Tongue, and sometimes, when she thought he was asleep, she spoke it to the hedge and the well and the fox that came down from the ridge in hard winters, and the hedge and the well and the fox had answered her. It was not a language of the throat. It lived somewhere deeper than the throat, in the chest and the blood and the long memory of the body, and no human mouth could make its sounds — only shape them, the way a reed shapes wind into music without being the wind. He had not heard it since Aldermoor burned. Three years. Three years of pressing it down and down and down until it was a stone at the bottom of him, and now it rose through his whole chest in one convulsive motion, recognition so violent it was closer to grief.

*Open,* said the door, in the Old Tongue. Or something near enough to *open.* Or something older than *open*, some word that meant *open* the way a seed means a tree.

"No," Tamsin whispered, to the door, to the boy or the thing or whatever stood behind it, to himself most of all. "No, no, go away."

The knock came again. Softer. Almost apologetic. And then, unmistakably, a child's voice — thin, cracked with cold, speaking the trade-tongue of Vell in an accent from nowhere, from before places:

"Please. I knocked everywhere else and the doors wouldn't hear me."

*Wouldn't hear me.* Not *wouldn't open.* As if doors heard. As if he knew they did.

Tamsin found his boots in the dark. He did not remember deciding to put them on. His hands were shaking and he hated them for it, hated himself, hated the stone at the bottom of him that had begun, after three years of perfect stillness, to roll.

* * *

The shop was black and cold and smelled of dried lavender and beeswax and the faint mineral tang of river stones waiting in their baskets to be sold as *worry-soothers, two coppers each, guaranteed to hold a worry so you needn't carry it yourself.* He knew every board that would creak and stepped over all of them, a thief in his own life, and paused at the shuttered window with his eye to the slat.

Snow fell thick as wool. The street was a white trench between leaning houses, the lamps drowned in their own halos, and the drifts had already erased the day's cart-ruts. Nothing moved. Nothing at all — and that was the wrongness of it, pressed home again. In three years Tamsin had never once looked out at this street and seen it empty of animals. Sparrows huddled under every eave in weather like this, fat and indignant. The innkeeper's cat made her rounds regardless of snow, sovereign and unbothered. Rats crossed open ground in bold little dashes, tails writing quick signatures in the white.

The street was bare. The eaves were bare. Somewhere under the snow, he was certain, the rats were pressed flat and still in their runs, holding their small breaths, and the pigeons stood rigid in the belfry of the chandlers' hall like carved birds on a church, and every living thing with fur or feathers was watching one spot on the street the way a field watches lightning.

The spot was his doorstep.

The boy stood in it, up to his ankles in snow, and did not seem to feel it.

He was small — eight years old, perhaps, though there was something about his proportions that kept sliding out of true whenever Tamsin tried to fix them, as if the eye kept miscounting. Mud caked him from hair to hem, old mud, dried and cracked and layered, mud of many weathers, and beneath the mud his clothes were not clothes at all in any fashion Tamsin recognized: a smock of some grey-green fiber, hand-woven, undyed, stitched with thread that caught the lamplight like spider silk. His hair was pale as birch bark. His eyes, when he lifted his face to the window — directly to the window, to the sliver of Tamsin's eye behind the slat, as if he had known exactly where it would be — were the brown of fresh-turned earth, and they did not blink in the driving snow.

And around him, where he stood, the snow was melting. Just there. A circle of wet dark cobbles, steaming faintly, precisely the width of his shoulders, while the storm piled white on every side of it.

"Oh," Tamsin said aloud, to the empty shop, in a voice he didn't recognize. "Oh, no."

Because he knew. That was the unbearable thing. Some part of him that had spent three years pretending to be a shopkeeper looked at the melting circle and the unblinking eyes and the mud of many weathers and said, calmly, *of course*, the way you say *of course* when a debt comes due. He had known since the animals went silent. He had perhaps known since the storm began building against the mountains, since the wren's warning yesterday, since — if he was honest, and honesty was a country he visited rarely and never stayed in — since the day he first hung a luck-knot in his window and felt the knot *want* to mean what it meant, and took it down and tied it again, badly, deliberately, until it was only string.

He opened the door.

Cold poured in. The boy tilted his head back to look up at him, snowflakes landing in his lashes and refusing to melt there, which should have been impossible, and said, in the trade-tongue, with the grave courtesy of a child repeating phrases learned whole:

"You're the one who still listens. They said there was one left in the low city who still listens. Are you?"

Every word Tamsin owned abandoned him at once. He looked down the street — empty, white, blind — and up at the sky going grey-grey-grey toward a dawn that would bring the watch, and the market, and the fishwives, and eyes. He looked at the boy. The boy waited, patient as winter, patient as a stone, patient as something that had been waiting far longer than children wait.

"In," Tamsin said finally, hoarsely. "Quickly. Before — just in."

The boy stepped over the threshold, and as he crossed it, every animal in Vell began to breathe again. Tamsin felt it happen — felt the second city wake beneath the first, the mice resuming their small economies, the cat resuming her rounds, the pigeons bursting upward off the chandlers' roof in one sudden clatter of wings that sounded, in the hush after silence, almost like applause. Or alarm. With beasts it was often both.

The boy stopped in the middle of the shop floor and stood dripping mud onto boards Tamsin scrubbed every restday, and looked around at the hanging charms with an expression Tamsin could not read — something between sorrow and recognition, the way you might look at a chapel converted to a granary.

"They don't mean anything anymore," the boy said, nodding at the luck-knots. "Did you know? The knots. They used to hold. Now they're only string." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. Then his gaze snagged on the cage by the counter, and changed entirely.

* * *

The wren had been silent since before the knock. She sat on her perch with her feathers puffed to a sphere, her black eyes fixed on the boy with an intensity Tamsin had never seen in her — and the wren's intensities were legend, at least in the private taxonomy of his own loneliness. She had been with him two winters, bought ostensibly as inventory (*songbird, trained, five silvers*) and kept ostensibly as company, and she had never once sung where anyone could hear. What she did instead was talk to him, in the sharp syntax of wrens, usually to complain: about the cage, about the draft, about the quality of the mealworms available in occupied Vell, about his cowardice, which she considered, in her professional opinion, excessive.

She talked to him now.

*Little brother of the thorn,* she said, and her voice in his skull was dry and precise and shaking, *little brother of the thorn, little brother of the thorn.* Wrens repeat things; it is how they think. But this repetition had the cadence of prayer, or of a name being tested for weight.

"Do you know him?" Tamsin asked aloud, then winced — aloud, in front of the boy, in front of the listening dark, his tongue already forgetting its discipline.

*I know what he is,* the wren said. *I knew what he was the moment the street went quiet. I hoped I was wrong. I am very old, shopkeeper, and I am rarely wrong, and I have never been sorry to be right until tonight.*

"What is he?" And then, because the boy was watching him with those earth-brown eyes, watching his lips move as he spoke to a bird in a cage, seeing everything and understanding all of it: "No. Don't tell me here. Boy — sit. By the banked stove. There's last night's porridge, I'll warm it."

"I'm not hungry," the boy said, and then, with the sudden ferocious practicality of childhood: "I'm very hungry."

Tamsin warmed the porridge. His hands had mostly stopped shaking, which surprised him; there was a strange steadiness that came over the body, he was discovering, once the catastrophe had actually arrived, a kind of clean terror that was easier to work inside than dread had ever been. The boy ate like a bird himself, quick precise swallows, watching the room between bites, and the melted-circle strangeness followed him indoors — the snow on his boots turning to water and the water, Tamsin noticed with a lurch, turning faintly green where it pooled, tiny bright threads of something sprouting and dying in the space of a breath, unable to live yet unable to stop trying.

When the bowl was empty, the boy set it down, folded his muddy hands, and waited. The wren hopped once along her perch. Outside, the storm threw itself against the shutters and slid away, threw itself and slid away, like something testing a door it could not yet open.

"Tell me your name," Tamsin said.

"I had a name," the boy said. "It was given to me by the ones who sent me, and it isn't a name a mouth can hold. The closest words are—" and here he made a sound.

Later, Tamsin could not have said whether the boy made it with his voice or with something behind his voice, whether the air in the shop actually moved or only seemed to. It was two syllables, or the ghosts of two syllables, and they were shaped like roots — like the sound a root must make, forcing itself through clay, patient and irresistible and utterly indifferent to what it splits. The lamp flame bent. The water in the basin shivered into rings. Something deep under the floor, under the foundations, under the city itself, seemed to lean toward the sound the way a plant leans toward light.

And the wren said, softly, in the speech she shared with him and no one else alive:

*Hollowroot.*

"That's what they call me," the boy agreed. "Where I'm going, you can say it properly."

"Where are you going?" Tamsin asked, though the wren had already begun, in the back of his mind, in her dry terrible certainty, to tell him — *the Spire, shopkeeper, he has come from the Spire and he is going back to the Spire, and oh, you fool, oh, poor fool, look at me, look at my eyes, do you see what I am wearing? Do you think I was always a bird?* — and Tamsin pressed that voice down, down, down with the stone, with all his practiced strength, because he wanted one more hour of being a shopkeeper, one more hour of the narrow safe lie, and he knew even as he reached for it that the hour was already spent.

Hollowroot looked up at him. Snowmelt dripped from his hem, greening. Around his small muddy shoulders the air had a faint shimmer, like heat above a summer road, in the middle of winter, indoors.

"The doors wouldn't hear me anywhere else," he said simply. "But you listened. So it has to be you."

Above the shop, thunder rolled down out of the northern mountains — too early in the year for thunder, too cold, too wrong — and beneath the thunder, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the Inquisitors' garrison by the river gate, Tamsin heard dogs begin to howl.

Not the way dogs howl at storms.

The way they howl at blood.
