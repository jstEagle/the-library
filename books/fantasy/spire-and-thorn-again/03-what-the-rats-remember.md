## 3. What the Rats Remember

The boy slept like something felled.

Tamsin had laid him on the cot behind the counter, beneath the patchwork quilt his mother had stitched in another life, and the child had not stirred since. His breathing was slow and even and wrong — too deep, too patient, the way a tree breathes rather than a boy. Mud still crusted the hollows of his ears. When Tamsin had tried to wash it away, it came off smelling of loam and lightning, of soil turned after a long frost.

The wren sat on the curtain rod above the cot and did not sleep at all.

"You will ask them," she said. It was not a question. Her voice was a dry leaf scraped along stone, a voice Tamsin heard with the part of him he kept folded small and hidden, the part that had no name in any lawful tongue.

"I don't know what you mean," Tamsin said, because saying *I know exactly what you mean* was how men ended up ash.

"You will go down into the dark and ask the rats what they saw." The wren preened one wing with terrible calm. "Because the boy fell out of the sky of your life three nights past, and you have not slept either, and your hands shake when you think he is not watching. You are going to do the thing you swore never to do again."

Tamsin looked at her. She was a small brown bird with a torn tail feather and eyes like wet garnets, and she had lived in his shop for two years eating suet and telling him truths he paid for in crumbs. He had told himself she was only a wren. He had told himself a great many things.

"The rats don't talk to me anymore," he said. "Not since—"

"Not since you stopped listening. There is a difference, shopkeeper, and it is the whole difference in the world."

* * *

He went at the grey hour before dawn, when even the Inquisitor's patrols grew drowsy and the lamps along Tanner's Row burned low and orange as dying coals. He left the boy sleeping and the wren standing guard — *if anyone knocks, wake me by screaming*, he'd said, and she had replied, *I always scream; it is my finest quality* — and he walked out into streets still slicked from the curfew storm.

Vell in the rain's aftermath smelled of wet stone and tannin and old smoke. Occupation had settled over the city the way moss settles over a fallen log: slowly, thoroughly, pretending to be natural. Banners of white and iron-grey hung from the customs house, stiff with damp. A proclamation board near the well bore fresh paste and fresh law: *MAGIC IS THEFT. THEFT IS DEATH.* Beneath it, someone had chalked a small crooked flower, already half washed away. Tamsin looked at it too long. Hope was a dangerous thing to be seen looking at.

The sewers opened behind the chandlery, through an iron grate he had known since childhood — back when knowing such things was innocence instead of evidence. He crouched beside it, set his palm flat against the cold iron, and let down the shutters he had spent three years building.

It was like opening a door onto a loud room after years of silence. Not sound, exactly. Pressure. Attention. The city below was *aware* — rats in their hundreds threading the dark, cats on their rounds, pigeons shifting on their roosts, the great sluggish mind of fish in the canal. All of it had been there all along, murmuring at the edge of his discipline, and he had spent three years refusing to hear it the way a man refuses to hear his own hunger.

*Here,* he said into that pressure, not in words but in the older grammar, the one made of intention and scent and shape. *Small cousins. Grey runners. I bring bread and no harm. I ask a thing.*

The answer took its time. It always had. Beasts were not servants and never had been — that was the first lie the old covens told themselves, and look where it had gotten everyone burned. What came up through the grate at last was a single rat, big as a kitten, scarred across the haunch, with the wary dignity of a creature that has survived every trap ever set.

She sat on the wet cobbles and regarded him with black bead eyes.

*Bread,* she said. Her speech was not speech; it was a smell-shape, a memory of grain, a picture of crumbs. But Tamsin's gift turned it into meaning the way the ear turns air into music.

"Bread," he agreed, and broke the heel of yesterday's loaf between them. "And questions."

*Questions cost more than bread.*

"Then I'll pay in bread and patience both."

The rat ate with quick darting bites, always angled so she could flee, and Tamsin waited, kneeling in the wet, letting his knees soak through. Patience was the toll. It had always been the toll.

*Three nights ago,* the rat said at last, *the ground under the north hills screamed.*

Tamsin's breath stopped. "Screamed."

*Not loud. Deep. Like a bone breaking inside a body, felt but not heard. Every tunnel-rat from here to the Ashfields felt it. The worms came up and died in the moonlight. The bats flew until their wings tore. And then—* she paused, and her whiskers trembled, and Tamsin understood he was watching a rat be afraid, which was rarer and worse than watching a man be afraid, because rats did not waste fear on things that were not real. *—then the water ran sweet. For one hour, the filth ran sweet as spring melt. And a smell came down the pipes we had no name for. Green. Old green. Green from before stone.*

The Spire. Tamsin thought of the stories his mother had told in whispers, of the thorn-realm beyond the northern hills, the wild country that predated Vell, predated the kingdoms, predated law. The covenant realm. Sealed. Sleeping. Everyone said sealed and sleeping the way they said *magic is theft* — because repetition made a thing safe to say.

"And the boy?" he asked. "Did anything come down the pipes? Small. Human-shaped. Walking out of the green smell?"

The rat's whiskers stilled.

*A cub came. Walking on two legs, which is wrong for a cub, but he smelled right — he smelled of the green, of the sweet hour. He was crying without sound. He went up the grate on Chandler's Row and into the world of men.* She fixed him with those bead eyes. *Something followed him, speaker. Something that walks the tunnels now. We do not go below the third level anymore. The things it leaves behind us do not rot. They stand in the dark, dripping, and they listen with their whole skins.*

Cold walked up Tamsin's spine and put its cold hands on the back of his neck. "What does it smell like?"

*Like nothing,* the rat said. *That is the horror of it. Everything smells. Stone smells. Water smells. Death smells. This thing has been scrubbed of scent, scrubbed of self. It moves like a hole moving. And sometimes, far off, it makes a sound like a hound breathing through a horn.*

Hounds. Vale's hounds. Tamsin had never seen them — no one in Vell spoke of them above a whisper, and most didn't speak of them at all — but every charm-seller knew the shape of the fear. They say the Inquisitor General keeps dogs that can smell magic the way other dogs smell blood. They say once they catch your scent, they do not lose it. They say she feeds them on—

He stood too fast. The rat flinched back toward the grate.

"Thank you," he said. "More bread comes tonight. Tell the others: if the hole-thing comes up this street, come to the chandlery grate and scratch three times."

*Why would we warn you?* the rat asked, and there was no malice in it, only the honest arithmetic of the wild. *What are you to us?*

Tamsin opened his mouth to give the safe answer — *nothing, I'm nothing, just a shopkeeper* — and found, to his quiet astonishment, that the words would not come. Three years of saying them and now, with a sleeping impossible child under his roof and the sewers full of scentless death, the lie had finally gone stale in his mouth.

"I'm the last one who asks politely," he said.

The rat considered this. Then she flowed down through the grate and was gone, and the loud dark room of the city closed over her absence like water.

* * *

He bought salt pork and a second loaf on the way home, because the boy would wake hungry and because buying things was normal, buying things was a man walking through his ordinary lawful life, and Tamsin needed the ritual of it badly. The porkmonger joked about the weather. Tamsin laughed in the right places. Inside his skull, the rat's words turned over and over like a stone in a stream: *the ground screamed, the water ran sweet, the cub came crying without sound.*

At the mouth of Copper Lane, a cat sat on a wall and stared at him with an intensity that raised the fur along his own arms. Cats rarely bothered speaking to him — they considered his kind tedious — but this one sent a single image flicking across the gap between them: a long grey shape at the end of the street, four-legged, wrong-gaited, nose down, tasting the cobbles.

Then it was gone, leaping to the rooftops, and Tamsin stood very still with the pork wrapped in brown paper against his chest.

He did not see the hound. That was somehow worse. The lane was empty in both directions, rain-pooled, lamplit, innocent. But the cat had seen, and cats did not lie about hunters; lying was a human invention.

He walked home fast without running. Running drew eyes.

* * *

The boy was awake when Tamsin came in, sitting up on the cot with the quilt pooled around his waist, watching the door with those too-old eyes. The wren had lit the lamp. On the table between them lay the contents of the boy's pockets, arranged with strange precision: a smooth grey pebble veined with green; a curl of bark from some tree Tamsin did not recognize; and a dried seed pod shaped almost exactly like a tiny closed fist.

"Eat," Tamsin said, setting down the bread and pork, and the boy ate — quickly, neatly, with a gratitude so complete it hurt to witness. He watched Tamsin the whole time, and when he finished he touched his own chest and said a word in that language no human throat should know, and pointed at Tamsin with a question in it.

"Tamsin," he said. "Tam-sin." He tapped his own chest. "You're Hollowroot, the bird says. Don't ask me why. She names everything."

The boy's face did something complicated at the word *bird*. He looked at the wren with an expression Tamsin could only call recognition — deep, formal, grief-stricken recognition, the way a man looks at the uniform of a dead friend.

"He says," the wren translated from the curtain rod, in her dry-leaf voice, "that he knows my order. He says the wardens are few now. He says—" and here she paused, and for the first time since Tamsin had known her, the wren's composure cracked like river ice, "—he says he thought he was the last."

Silence filled the shop. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog began to bark — then cut off abruptly, mid-note, as though a hand had closed around its throat.

Tamsin's blood went thin.

"Hollowroot." He crouched to the boy's level, keeping his voice low and even, the voice he used for frightened animals. "Three nights ago. Did you come from the north? From the hills? From—" he almost said *the Spire* and couldn't make his mouth form it — "from a place of thorns?"

The boy nodded slowly. Then he held up three fingers. Then he pressed both hands flat against his own chest, over his heart, and his face crumpled with an effort of communication that wrung Tamsin's gut — and he made a sound, the first human sound Tamsin had heard from him, a low keening note that was somehow also the sound of splitting wood, of ice giving way, of the ground screaming under the north hills.

*He fled it falling,* the wren said softly. *He carried what he could. He has been running for three nights with the sweet hour in his pockets and the dark behind him.*

"And the dark has a shape," Tamsin said. "A hound's shape. It's in the tunnels. Maybe it's in the streets." He looked at the wren. "How long do we have?"

"How long does any of us have?" the wren asked, which was her way of saying *less than you hope.*

The barking started again, closer now — two streets over, then one — and this time it did not stop. Other dogs joined it, a rising ragged chorus sweeping along the riverfront, and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Tamsin had no name for and would remember all his life: a long, slow inhalation, like a bellows drawing breath, like something tasting the air through a horn of bone.

The boy went rigid. He grabbed Tamsin's sleeve with both hands, and through the contact — skin to skin, the first time he had touched him — something poured across the gap that was not words at all. An image: a corridor of dark water. A shape moving through it, low and grey and glistening, wearing a hound's outline the way a puppet wears strings. And threaded through the image, a smell that was not a smell: the absence of everything, scrubbed clean, a hole in the world walking.

Then, beneath the terror, steady as bedrock, a second thing passed from the boy's hands into Tamsin's skin. Warmth. Green and patient. An old green smell, green from before stone. Trust, offered without condition, by a creature who had nowhere else to put it.

Tamsin looked down at the small mud-stained hand gripping his sleeve. He thought of his mother's hands, and of ash, and of three years of small lies stacked like coins in a jar. He thought: *they will burn him. Whatever he is, whatever this costs me, they will burn him in the square and call it law.*

The chorus of dogs had reached the corner of Copper Lane.

"Back room," Tamsin said quietly. "Now. Both of you. There's a cellar hatch behind the flour shelf — wren, you know the drill, take him down and not one feather or footstep more than needed." He was already moving, pulling the lamp toward the stove, his mind doing arithmetic it had never wanted to do: one door, two exits, a boy who smelled of miracles, and hounds outside that smelled magic like blood.

The knocking, when it came, was polite. Three measured raps, courteous as a summons.

Through the window glass, at the edge of the lamplight, Tamsin saw the grey shape sit down in the middle of Copper Lane, patient as winter, and lift its head, and wait.

Behind it, out of the dark, came the soft tread of boots, and a woman's voice, pleasant as a market day:

"Master Sterling? Inquisitor's office. Might we have a word about your stock?"
