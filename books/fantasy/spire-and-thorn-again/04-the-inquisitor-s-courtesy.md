## 4. The Inquisitor's Courtesy

The rain had stopped by the time Maren Vale reached Copperlane, but the street still wept. Water ran in bright threads along the gutters, carrying soot and cabbage leaves toward the drains, and the shopfronts had that hunched, apologetic look of a quarter that knew it was being watched. Vale watched it back.

She did not travel with soldiers when she worked a street. Soldiers were for sieges and executions; her work required something quieter. She came instead with two clerks in grey — Brother Ellery, who carried the ledger, and young Sister Anthe, who carried the instruments — and behind them, at a distance calculated to seem accidental, four of the hounds on long braided leads. The hounds were not dogs, whatever the city called them. Dogs had been bred from wolves; these had been bred from something older and more obedient, and they smelled magic the way other animals smelled blood: eagerly, helplessly, with their whole bodies.

Vale herself wore plain wool the color of wet slate, cut well but not richly, and her only ornament was the signet of the Office of Honest Law, which she wore on her right hand where a suspicious person would be sure to see it. Honesty, in her experience, flourished best under good lighting. She intended to provide some.

"Number eleven," said Brother Ellery, consulting the ledger against the rain-darkened street. "Marrow Lane. The charm-seller."

"Marrow Lane," Vale agreed pleasantly. "And after him?"

Ellery turned the page with a clerk's dry delicacy. "After him, General, the list continues."

It always continued. The list was the truest thing Vale owned — truer than her name, which she had buried with her sister; truer than her face, which changed as she aged while the list stayed exactly what it was. Forty-one names in Copperlane alone. Some were fools who sold knotted string to gullible lovers. Some were worse. All of them would be spoken to, politely, one at a time, until Vell understood that magic was not a force to be suppressed but a lie to be corrected, and that lies could be corrected permanently.

She paused at the corner of Marrow Lane and let the hounds settle. They sat in a row on the wet cobbles like carved things, steam rising faintly off their shoulders, and their nostrils worked the air in unison — six nostrils, twelve, however many they had this season; they bred strangely — reading the street the way a priest reads scripture.

"Well?" she asked them softly.

The lead hound turned its head. Not down the lane, where the charm-seller's shop squatted between a dyer's and a shuttered chandlery. Down the *other* way — toward the wider street, the better-lit one, where a small shop with a green door sold luck-knots and mended-bone charms under a sign painted with a wren.

Vale felt the particular stillness come over her that her clerks had learned to fear more than any shouting. It was the stillness of a scale finding its balance.

"Noted," she said. "But we go in order. Order is the whole of the law. If I let a hound's nose write my itinerary, I may as well let the crows write my sermons."

She smiled at Ellery to show the joke had been a joke, and walked down Marrow Lane to number eleven, and knocked with two courteous knuckles.

* * *

The charm-seller's name was Oskit Brall, and he was sweating before he finished opening the door.

They always sweated. Vale had long ago stopped taking it as evidence. Fear was not guilt; fear was simply the honest body's response to power, and she respected honesty wherever she found it. She stepped into his shop — small, dim, smelling of tallow and dried lavender — and remarked on the weather, and asked after his health, and accepted a chair that wobbled, all with the unhurried grace of a woman visiting an aunt.

Sister Anthe set the instrument case on the counter without being asked. Brother Ellery stood by the door with the ledger open. The hounds remained outside, which was itself a courtesy; their presence indoors made men faint, and fainting men were difficult to question.

"Master Brall," Vale said, folding her hands. "You sell charms."

"I sell *trinkets*, General." His voice cracked on the second word and he pushed through it. "Luck-knots. Keepsakes. There's no — there's nothing in them. Anyone can tie a knot."

"Anyone can tie a knot," Vale agreed. "I have tied knots. I have untied them. I have never once felt lucky afterward." She let the silence do a little work, then added, gently, "Your neighbor's child has a fever that has lasted nine days. You sold the mother a mended-bone charm last month. She tells me the fever broke the next morning."

Brall's hands twisted together. "Children recover. Fevers break. That's — that's just what bodies do, General, please—"

"It is indeed what bodies do," Vale said warmly. "Most of the time. And when a body does something remarkable, people look for a reason, and when they cannot find one, they invent one, and the invention is called magic, and magic is a lie, and lies are my profession to correct. Do you follow me, Master Brall? I am not here because I believe you guilty. I am here because I believe you *lucky*, and luck makes liars of everyone around it."

He blinked at her. For a moment the terror in his face loosened into confusion, which was the desired effect. Confusion opened doors that fear only rattled.

"You keep a ledger," Vale continued. "All shopkeepers do. May I?"

He slid it across the counter as if it might bite him. Ellery took it, and the three of them read it together in the lamplight — names, dates, prices, the small honest arithmetic of a small dishonest trade. Vale read quickly. She had read ten thousand such ledgers, and they sang to her now, the way a musician hears the false note in a chord.

Here. Third page. A purchase recorded twice in one week by a customer whose name appeared in another ledger, in another quarter, beside a notation in Vale's own hand: *known associate of the Corrow boy; watch.*

And here, further down — this was interesting — a sale of *nine* mended-bone charms in a single afternoon to a buyer who paid in old coin, pre-Occupation coin, the kind that circulated mostly among people who had reasons not to visit banks.

"Master Brall," Vale said, closing the ledger with a soft clap, "who buys nine healing charms in one day?"

"No one! I mean — a man. A stranger. Passing through." Sweat again, fresh, at the hairline. "Road-worn. Said he was going north."

"North," Vale repeated, tasting the word. North lay the ashfields, and beyond the ashfields the Thornwalk, and beyond the Thornwalk the Spire — or the ruin of it, the wound in the world where the old covenant had rotted through. Three nights ago, something had happened out there. Her reports used words like *seismic disturbance* and *unexplained luminescence*, which were clerk's words for *the sky over the Spire caught fire and every animal within thirty miles went silent as the grave.*

"Describe him."

"Tall. Grey coat. Nervous." Brall swallowed. "General, I swear on my mother's grave I only sold him what he asked for—"

"Of course you did. Selling is your trade; I have no quarrel with trade." She rose, smoothing her skirt, and gave him the smile she saved for the almost-innocent. "You will keep your ledger current. You will send word to the Office if the nervous man returns, or if anyone asks you about the road north, or if anything at all happens that strikes you as *remarkable*. Remarkable things are rarely innocent, Master Brall. That is the first lesson of Honest Law."

"And — and the second, General?" He seemed to genuinely want to know. They often did, near the end. People wanted rules when the ground shook.

"The second lesson," Vale said, "is that I always know more than I say." She tapped the ledger once. "For instance, I know that the fever-child's mother paid you with a silver button, and that you keep the button in the drawer under your scales, and that you touch it when you're frightened, the way a man touches a talisman. Which suggests that somewhere under all this very sensible commerce, Master Brall, you believe too. Everyone believes. Belief is the enemy. Good day."

She left him standing in his shop with his mouth open, and stepped out into the washed light of Marrow Lane, and permitted herself one slow breath of the cold air before turning to Ellery.

"The list," she said. "Read me the top of it."

Ellery cleared his throat. Behind him, the hounds had risen to their feet without any visible signal, ears forward, all of them facing the same direction at once — toward the wide street, the green door, the painted wren.

"'Copperlane register, suspected practitioners, by priority,'" Ellery read. "'First: unnamed subject, referred to in captured correspondence only as the beast-speaker of Copperlane; identity unconfirmed; believed to communicate with animals; connection to the Sterling coven presumed.' Second—" 

"Second," Vale said, because she had the list by heart and liked hearing it confirmed, the way a miser likes hearing his fortune counted aloud.

"Second: Tamsin Sterling. Charm-seller. Green door. Surviving son of Elowen Sterling, executed eight years past. Subject was questioned at the time of the purges and released for lack of evidence." Ellery looked up. "Released by your predecessor, General. With an apology, the record notes."

"Yes. He apologized." Vale remembered the man — soft, frightened of his own office, dead now of a fever that had been neither mysterious nor magical. "Apologies are how cowards sign their work. Note for the record: the release was an error. The boy was questioned too kindly."

She began walking toward the wide street, and the hounds fell in around her, and the clerks hurried to keep pace.

"Understand the shape of it," she said, conversational, as though discussing drainage. "A coven burns. The mother dies singing — they always sing, it's meant to unsettle us. Two children survive: the daughter fled beyond reach, and the son — the son opens a *charm shop*. In the same quarter. Under our noses. For years." She shook her head with something close to admiration. "That is either the boldest hideout in Vell or the most patient trap ever baited, and I confess I have not yet decided which. But the hounds have been pulling toward that green door for three years, Ellery. Every patrol, every pass through Copperlane, they lean toward it like compass needles. And today, on the same morning that something wakes at the Spire, the lead hound turns toward it *first*."

"You think the disturbance and Sterling are connected."

"I think," Vale said, "that coincidences are what chaos calls its alibis." She stopped at the corner opposite the green door and looked at it — really looked, the way she looked at everything, with the patience of a woman who had learned that most secrets were simply truths wearing bad disguises. The shop was shut up tight against the curfew hour. Rain dripped from the painted wren's beak. In the upper window, a candle had been lit and hastily shuttered, and the light leaked out in a thin gold line like a held breath.

Someone was awake in there. Someone was awake in there at an hour when honest shopkeepers slept.

"Tomorrow," Vale said. "We'll call on him tomorrow, in daylight, with full courtesy. No hounds at the door — I want him relaxed. Anthe will carry the case."

Ellery went pale in the way clerks did. "The case, General? For a first interview?"

"For a *second* interview, Brother, if the first goes as interviews go." She watched the gold line of candlelight. "But I find it focuses the mind wonderfully, showing a man the evidence before asking him to become it."

* * *

Back at the Office of Honest Law, in the room at the top of the tower that the clerks called the Quiet Room because sound behaved strangely there — arrived late, left early — Maren Vale sat alone with the glass case.

It was a simple thing: a box of thick clear glass on a stand of black oak, locked with three keys she kept in three different pockets. Inside, on a bed of grey ash, lay a thorn splinter the length of her forefinger.

It had been taken from the burning at Harrow Fen, eleven years ago, from the wreckage of the coven circle her sister had died inside. The inquisitors of that day had catalogued it as debris. Vale had known better the moment she touched it, because it had been warm — warm as a living thing, days after the fire — and because when she closed her hand around it, she had felt, distinctly, unmistakably, a pulse.

Not her pulse. Slower. Older. Patient in a way nothing human managed to be.

She unlocked the case now, as she did every night, and looked at it. The splinter lay still. It always lay still when observed directly, which was either cunning or shyness, and Vale had spent eleven years deciding which, without success.

"Three nights ago," she told it, conversationally, "something woke at the Spire. My hounds can smell it on the wind even from here — they've hardly eaten since. And today they pulled toward a charm-shop in Copperlane as if the door itself were bleeding." She folded her hands. "You're a piece of the old covenant. Perhaps the last piece in captivity. So I'll ask you plainly, as one professional to another: is it waking? Is that what this is?"

The splinter moved.

It was a small motion — a slow roll, a quarter-turn, the way a sleeping man turns toward a voice — and it should not have been possible, because nothing in the case had touched it, and the case had not been jostled, and the ash beneath it bore no print. Vale watched it settle into its new position and felt the familiar cold climb her spine, the cold that was not fear exactly but its aristocratic cousin: the recognition of something greater than oneself going about its business nearby.

"There you are," she whispered.

Then she saw what the turn had revealed. The splinter's tip, which had pointed south toward the harbor for eleven years — toward the sea, toward away — now pointed north. Toward the ashfields. Toward the Spire.

Toward, if one extended the line carefully through the city map in her mind, across the rooftops and the lanes and the market square, a small shop with a green door.

Vale sat very still for a long moment. Outside, the curfew bell rang the third hour, and somewhere below, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it, and the whole city held its breath the way it did every night, pretending to sleep.

"Thank you," she said at last, quietly, to the splinter, to the night, to whatever patient old thing was listening. "That will be all."

She locked the case with its three keys. Tomorrow, daylight, full courtesy: Master Tamsin Sterling, charm-seller, second from the top of the list, would receive a visit from the Office of Honest Law, and he would smile his shopkeeper's smile, and she would smile back, and between them they would determine which of them was lying.

In the glass case behind her, in the dark, the thorn splinter pointed north, and waited.
