## 5. Three Years of Small Lies

The boy slept in the back room, curled on the floor among the packing straw like something that had grown there rather than been laid down. Tamsin had given him the blanket off his own cot and a heel of yesterday's bread, and Hollowroot — if that was truly what the wren called him, and Tamsin no longer doubted her — had eaten it in small, precise bites, watching Tamsin the whole time with eyes the color of rain through leaves.

Now the shop was dark, shuttered against the curfew storm that rattled the tiles overhead, and Tamsin sat behind his counter with a luck-knot half-plaited in his hands because his fingers needed work or they would shake.

*You are not sleeping,* said the wren from her cage by the window. Her voice was dry as seed husks, pitched so low only he could hear it. *Neither am I. Neither is he. Listen.*

He listened. Beneath the wind, beneath the groan of the sign outside, there was the boy's breathing — slow, deep, and underneath *that*, faint as water under stone, a sound like roots growing. Like green things pushing patiently through soil. It came from the back room. It came from the boy.

Tamsin set down the knot. "Three years," he said quietly, to the wren, to himself, to the dark. "Three years I kept my head down and my mouth shut and nothing found me. Three days, and the whole world is at my door."

*It was always coming,* the wren said. *You knew that. You simply chose not to know it loudly.*

He almost laughed. That was the trouble with beasts; they had no talent for lying kindly.

He banked the fire, climbed to the loft above the shop where his cot sat under a sloped ceiling, and lay staring at the beams. And because sleep would not come, memory came instead, the way it always did on nights when the wind sounded like shouting.

* * *

He was fourteen the night the purges came to Thornhollow, and he remembered it first as a smell: pitch and burning feathers, the sickly sweetness of a charm-shop's stock going up in flames. His mother's shop. Their shop.

They had lived above it, the way he lived above his own now, and he had often thought since that he had built his life in Vell as an exact copy of the one that had been taken from him, right down to the creak of the third stair, as though he could rebuild the burned house so carefully that the fire would never find it again.

His mother had been a speaker of the old kind — not just beasts but the green speech too, the language of root and briar, the covenant-tongue. She had served the village as midwife and mender, and every soul in Thornhollow had known what she was and loved her for it, right up until the Inquisition's riders came down the valley with their warrants and their iron cages, and every soul in Thornhollow discovered that love was lighter than fear and easier to carry.

Tamsin remembered the knock. Not on the door — on the *walls*, fists against the timbers, many fists, and voices calling his mother's name in the flat friendly tones of neighbors. He remembered his mother waking him without panic, dressing him in his sister's old coat because it was plain and brown and would not be remembered, and pressing something into his hand: a thorn, black and curved, wrapped in wool.

"Take the sheep path," she whispered. "Down to the river. There will be a barge. Tell the bargeman *the hedge remembers him*. He'll know."

"Mama—"

"Don't speak." Her hands were steady on his shoulders. Her eyes were not. "Not a word, do you hear me? Not to anyone, not ever, not even to the animals. A gift unused looks like no gift at all. Be dull, Tam. Be forgettable. Be *safe*." She kissed his forehead, and her lips tasted of the bitter tea she drank to keep her own gift quiet, and then she turned and went down to open the door to her neighbors and their torches, smiling, so that they would look at her and not up the stairs.

He went out the back window. He went down the sheep path. At the bend where the path dropped toward the river he stopped — he had never let himself remember why he stopped, all these years, and now, lying in the dark above his own little shop, he finally let it come — and looked back.

Thornhollow burned orange against the black hills. The pyre they built for the witches was in the square, and it was already lit, and it was very tall, and he could see figures around it, small with distance, and he told himself he could not tell which one was his mother, and he believed himself, because believing it was the only way to keep walking.

He was fourteen. He had done exactly what she asked. He had been dull and forgettable and safe.

And every night since, some patient, honest part of him had asked: *which figure was she? Did you look away, or did you look and choose not to see?*

* * *

The bargeman had known the words. The hedge remembers him — his mother had bought that man's silence years before with a mended-bone charm for his lame daughter, and this was the debt called in. Tamsin rode the barge downriver through the grey dawn, huddled under sacking among the eel traps, and the bargeman never once looked at him, which was its own kindness.

In Vell he had learned the second lesson fast: the city was not Thornhollow. No one here had known his mother. No one here cared what he was, so long as he sold them charms that worked by coincidence and priced them fairly. So he became a shopkeeper. He learned to smile with his whole face while keeping his gift folded up inside him like a wing broken on purpose. He learned which customers wanted flattery and which wanted silence. He learned to buy grain for the street cats openly, laughing at himself — *soft-hearted fool, me* — so that no one would wonder why the cats always seemed to be telling him things.

And once a year, on the anniversary, he walked out past the walls to the ashfields and spoke one sentence aloud to the wind, in the old tongue, where no human ear could reach: *I'm alive, Mama. Forgive me how.*

Then he walked back and locked the sentence away again.

There had been one person, in those first raw months, who had known the truth. One friend.

Dace Corrow.

* * *

Dace had come to Vell two winters after the burning, half-starved, knocking on the shop door with snow in his hair, and Tamsin had known him before he raised his head — by the way he stood, weight on his heels, hands jammed in armpits, the exact posture of a boy waiting outside a schoolroom to be caned. They had fished the same river as children. They had dared each other to touch the Thornwalk's edge. Dace had been there the day Tamsin first spoke to a fox and the fox answered, and Dace had sworn on his mother's grave he'd tell no one, and he never had, not even when the other boys mocked Tamsin for being strange and quiet, not even when mocking turned to stones.

So when Dace stood dripping in his doorway, Tamsin did the dangerous thing. He let him in.

"I heard you were alive," Dace said, hunched over the soup Tamsin made him, eating like a man afraid the bowl would be taken away. "I heard there was a charm-seller in Vell, young, dark-haired, came down from the north. I had to see."

"You shouldn't have." But he was smiling. God help him, he had been *glad*.

"They're still hunting speakers," Dace said, staring into the soup. "Even here. Even after three years. There's a woman — Vale. Maren Vale. Inquisitor General now. She keeps lists." He looked up, and his eyes were the same warm brown they'd always been, and there was something behind them moving like a fish under ice. "Tam. Do you still — I mean. You don't. Do you?"

And here was the moment. Here, looking back across three years of small lies, Tamsin could see the moment standing in the room like a third man, and he could see the two roads out of it, and he could see which one he took and why, and none of the seeing helped.

"No," Tamsin had said. "I'm just a shopkeeper, Dace. You know me. Dull and forgettable."

Dace had laughed with relief, and finished his soup, and stayed three days, and gone away again. And Tamsin had lain awake each of those nights knowing he had lied to the one person alive who deserved the truth, and telling himself it was kindness. Protection. If Dace didn't know, Dace couldn't be made to tell.

That was the story he told himself for a year. Then the stories changed, because the facts changed.

* * *

The facts were these: eight months ago, a hedge-witch named Sorrel Fenn had been taken in the Tanner's Quarter. Tamsin had known her slightly — she bought beeswax from him, and once, over the counter, in the beast-speech so low it was barely breath, she had said *you smell of the north country, brother*, and he had frozen, and she had smiled and never mentioned it again. She had known. She had protected him with her silence, the way his mother had, the way women of the craft always seemed to do, spending themselves to buy strangers time.

Sorrel Fenn had burned on a Tuesday. And the witness list read out at her trial — Tamsin had been in the crowd, because not going would have been worse — had included a name that stopped his heart and restarted it wrong:

*Dace Corrow, of Vell, who deposes that the accused did traffic in unlawful arts, and did receive goods in furtherance thereof.*

Dace had testified. Not under torture — the record said *willing deposition*. Dace had stood in the courthouse in his clerk's grey coat — a clerk's coat, an Inquisition clerk's coat — and given evidence about beeswax purchases and dried herbs and the comings and goings of a woman who had once, out of pure kindness, pretended not to know what Tamsin was.

Tamsin had gone home and been sick, and then he had done the arithmetic that guilt always does, the cruel kind, the thorough kind. *When did Dace join them? Was he already wearing the grey coat when he knocked on my door with snow in his hair? Did he come to see me, or to weigh me? Did I pass?*

He had never asked. He had never confronted him. Because the worst possibility, the one that squatted under all the others like a toad under a stone, was this: that Dace had come as a friend, honestly, and left as a friend, honestly, and reported nothing — and that his testimony against Sorrel Fenn had cost him something, had been extracted somehow, had been the price of keeping *one name off a list*. Tamsin's name.

He had no proof of that. He had only the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a thing in the dark. And the shape fit too well. *A charm-seller in Vell, young, dark-haired, came down from the north. I had to see.* Had to see. Or had been sent to confirm.

Either way, the conclusion was the same, and Tamsin had built his walls higher after that. Fewer conversations. Shorter hours. The wren had come to him that winter — flown in the window during a storm, half-dead, and he had nursed her because he could not help it, and she had refused to leave, and he had told himself a caged bird was safer than a free one, and maybe that was even true. But mostly he had kept her because her small dry voice was the only voice in Vell that knew him whole, and a man can live on very little bread if someone knows him whole.

*You are doing it again,* the wren said now, from below, through the floorboards, as if she had followed his thoughts down through the wood. *The remembering. The counting of your sins like coins.*

"It's not sin-counting," he murmured. "It's inventory."

*It is the same thing, in a shop.*

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Below him, the growing sound from the back room had softened into true sleep, and the storm was easing, and for a moment — one treacherous, ordinary moment — he thought: *perhaps it will be fine. Perhaps the boy wandered in from nowhere. Perhaps tomorrow I'll find his family, and—*

The wren cut through the thought like a knife through wet cloth. *Come down. Now. He is dreaming, and his dream is leaking.*

Tamsin took the stairs too fast. In the back room, the boy lay tangled in the blanket, and around him the packing straw had *changed* — every stalk within arm's reach had put out a pale green shoot, tiny leaves unfurling in the cold air, and where the boy's fingers touched the floorboards, moss was creeping across the wood in delicate fern-patterns, growing as Tamsin watched. Frost on the window had bloomed into shapes like fronds, like wings.

"Hollowroot," Tamsin said softly, and then, because the boy didn't wake, he tried the other tongue — the one his mother had used over cradles and garden beds, the covenant-speech, syllables like water over stones. *Little seed. Wake. You're dreaming the world awake.*

The boy's eyes opened. Rain-through-leaves, fixed on Tamsin with terrible calm.

"The tall ones are angry," Hollowroot said, in the old tongue, in a voice like wind in wheat. "They say the pact broke. They say the last warden died. They say—" his brow furrowed, searching for human words, finding them the way a root finds a crack in pavement, "—they say a *Sterling* owes a debt."

The name went through Tamsin like the thorn his mother had pressed into his hand, cold and curved and true. He had never spoken his family name in Vell. Not once in three years. Sterling was a dead name from a dead village, buried with the pyre smoke.

"How do you know that word?" he whispered.

But the boy's eyes had already drifted shut again, and the moss crept on, and from her cage by the window the wren said, in the driest, oldest voice Tamsin had ever heard her use:

"Sit down, Tamsin Sterling. It is long past time you and I talked about your mother — and about what she sent you down that mountain carrying besides a coat and a lie."
