## 6. The Wren's Bargain

The boy slept in the back room with his hands folded on his chest like a small effigy on a tomb, and Tamsin sat across from him in the dark and did not sleep at all.

Three nights now. Three nights of the same arrangement: Hollowroot — for that was what the wren called him, and no other name would stick to him, sliding off like rain off oiled leather — curled beneath the good wool blanket, breathing slow and even as a tide, while Tamsin kept watch from the chair by the cold hearth and tried to convince himself that any of this was survivable.

The storm had passed, but its memory lingered in the walls. Water dripped somewhere behind the plaster with the patience of a thing counting down. The shop below smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and, faintly, of char — because Tamsin had burned his luck-knot stock two nights ago, all forty of them, in a panic he could not entirely explain even to himself. Something in the knots had begun to *work*. A customer's knot had unraveled itself into a perfect circle on her counter, and she had laughed and said *oh, clever*, and Tamsin had smiled his shopkeeper smile and taken her coin and then gone home and fed every charm he owned to the stove.

Magic was leaking out of the boy the way heat leaked out of an unbanked fire. Tamsin could feel it in his teeth.

"You might as well come out," he said quietly. "I know you've been listening."

From the shadowed shelf above the hearth, where a wicker cage hung with its door long since unlatched, the wren hopped once to the edge and regarded him with one black eye. She did not speak immediately. She never wasted words; it was one of the many things about her that had stopped feeling like a bird's habit and started feeling like something much older wearing feathers.

*You burned your work,* she said at last. Her voice inside his skull was dry and precise, like a page turning. *That was wise. It will not be enough.*

"Comforting. Thank you."

*Truth is not required to comfort you.*

Tamsin leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at her properly. He had owned her — or been owned by her; the distinction had grown murky over three years — since the winter he first opened the shop, when she had landed on his windowsill during a snowfall and simply refused to leave. He had told himself she was an ordinary wren who happened to have learned human speech, the way some magpies did, the way parrots did. He had told himself a great many things in three years. He was beginning to suspect that lying to himself was the one magic he had never actually given up.

"Say what you've been circling," he said. "You've been working up to something since the boy arrived. I can hear it in how you don't talk."

The wren ruffled her feathers once, a gesture that might have been a sigh if birds sighed, and hopped down to the mantel, closer to the sleeping child. In the grey light seeping under the shutters, her brown plumage showed threads of something else — a green-gold shimmer along the wing edges that Tamsin had always attributed to oil-sheen, to lamplight, to anything other than what it plainly was.

*Do you know what a covenant-warden is?*

"A story. My mother used to—" He stopped. His mother's voice lived in his memory mostly as ash now, mostly as smoke and screaming and the specific sound of a crowd that has decided something. But before that, before the purges, there had been stories at the kitchen table: wardens bound to old families, beasts who kept faith with bloodlines, the Spire standing at the world's root like a nail holding two floors together. Children's stories. He had buried them in the same grave as everything else.

*Your mother did not tell them as stories,* the wren said, reading him with terrible ease. *She told them as history. Because they were history. I am a warden, Tamsin Sterling. I was bound to your bloodline eleven generations before your grandmother drew breath. My name — my true name, not this feather-shape — is Serevanne of the Third Rooting, and I have kept faith with the Sterlings since before Vell had walls.*

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a floor does when a ship remembers it is on water. Tamsin gripped the arms of his chair.

"That's not possible. The wardens died with the covenant. Everyone says—"

*Everyone is wrong, because everyone alive learned their history from the people who burned yours.* She hopped closer, head cocked, that black eye fixed on him without mercy. *A warden does not die when the covenant breaks, child. A warden endures. That is the entire purpose of us. We are the knot tied so tight that even betrayal cannot untie it — only delay it. I have spent three years in your shop pretending to be a clever bird, and you have spent three years pretending to be a man with no gift, and between us we have maintained a fiction so complete that even I nearly believed it.*

"Why?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Why stay hidden? Why let me smother it all this time?"

*Because a gift untrained draws hunters,* she said simply, *and a gift denied draws nothing but grief. Your mother asked it of me with her last living thought. Hide him. Let him be small. Let him live.* The wren's feathers settled flat, smooth, still. *I have regretted the bargain every day since. And I have honored it every day since. Both things are true.*

Tamsin found he could not look at her. He looked instead at the boy — at Hollowroot — whose breathing had not changed, whose face in sleep held the serene blankness of something that had never needed to lie about anything.

"The rats told me he came from the Spire," Tamsin said. "That it fell. That something walked out of it."

*Something did not walk out of it,* the wren said. *Something crawled. And then it stood up, and shaped itself, and wrapped its need in the most persuasive form it could find — because the last seed of the Spire knows that no one carries a thorn to safety, but everyone carries a child.*

The words took a moment to land. When they did, they landed all at once, like a cartload of stone tipping.

"He's not a boy."

*He is a seed. The last one. The Spire's heart made flesh and sent into the world the only way it could travel — wearing the shape that humans cannot bring themselves to harm.* The wren hopped onto the bedframe, light as a dropped leaf, and the boy did not stir. *Look at him closely, Tamsin. Have you noticed he does not shiver? Have you noticed the mud dries off him faster than it should? Have you noticed that he has not eaten in three days, though you have offered food at every meal, and he takes a bite only to please you?*

He had noticed. He had noticed and explained each strangeness away, one by one, the way he explained away everything: the boy was in shock, the boy was feral, the boy had eaten before arriving. Tamsin felt the whole architecture of his denial groan and begin, joint by joint, to collapse.

"What happens to him? To it? If the covenant stays broken."

The silence stretched long enough that Tamsin heard the drip behind the plaster count four more seconds.

*Then the wild magic comes home uninvited,* Serevanne said. *Understand what the covenant was. It was not a wall. It was a door with rules. On one side, humanity: fields, fences, laws, fires lit on purpose. On the other side, the thorn-realm: everything green and hungry and older than naming. The door let each side borrow from the other in measured amounts. Rain for reverence. Growth for gratitude. Your ancestors' beast-speakers were the hinge-keepers — the ones who could stand in the doorway and speak both tongues, keeping the traffic honest.*

"And my family helped break it." The guilt was an old companion; it had teeth marks in it from years of chewing. "I know. The Sterlings signed the Severance."

*Some of them. Under threat. Which is a distinction the thorn-realm has never cared for, and I cannot blame it.* Her voice went quieter, which for a voice inside the skull was a strange sensation — a hush applied directly to the mind. *When the door broke, the wild magic did not vanish. It was penned. Compressed. The Spire was the pen — the last knot of the old binding, holding the feral deeps of the thorn-realm back from spilling into the world. For three hundred years it held. Now it has fallen, and the pen is open, and the seed is the last living piece of the lock.*

Tamsin stood. He didn't remember deciding to stand. His legs simply informed him.

"So we take it back. Carry it to the Spire, plant it, done. Right?" He heard his own voice trying to make the shape of a plan out of terror. "How far is the Spire?"

*Nine days' ride. Fourteen on foot, with a child who does not tire but also does not hurry.*

"Then we leave tonight. Before Vale's hounds—"

*Tamsin.* Just his name, and it stopped him the way a hand on the chest stops a man walking into a blade. *There is a second half to it. There is always a second half. Sit down.*

He did not sit. But he stopped.

*The seed cannot simply be planted,* the wren said. *Soil alone will not take it. The rite of reforging requires a speaker — a true beast-speaker, of the old blood, willing to be bound. Voice, name, and future, Tamsin. Not a metaphor. You would give your voice into the covenant's keeping, so that the door has a tongue again. You would give your name into its roots, so that the door has an anchor in the human side. And you would give your future — your years, your road, whatever shape your life was going to take — woven into the binding, so that the door has a reason to hold. The covenant failed last time because the people who made it treated it as ink and parchment. This time it must be made of someone. Of you.*

The drip behind the plaster counted five seconds. Six. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past through the wet street, and a horse blew out a breath, and Tamsin heard it — really heard it, the way he had spent three years teaching himself not to — a low equine grumble of complaint about the weather, distinct as speech.

"You're asking me to die," he said.

*I am asking you to be bound,* the wren corrected. *They are not always the same thing. They are sometimes the same thing. I will not lie to you about which, because I do not know. No warden has performed this rite in living memory. The old texts say the speaker becomes the door's keeper. They do not say whether the keeper remains a man.*

Tamsin laughed. It came out wrong — too short, too sharp, a bark with nothing behind it. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and saw, in the dark there, the shop below: the counter worn smooth by his own elbow, the shelf of mended-bone charms, the little brass bell over the door that he had polished every morning for three years because it was the first thing he'd ever owned outright. The small safe life. The life built out of small lies, stacked careful as coins.

"I hid," he said. "For three years I hid. I watched them burn Mistress Ellery the hedge-charmer and I stayed behind my shutters. I watched them take the tanner's son for talking to his own dogs and I sold charms to the men who dragged him. I made myself so small, so *carefully* small, and you're telling me the only way out is to become the largest thing in the realm."

*Yes.*

"One moon. You said within one moon."

*The seed fades,* Serevanne said. *It is already fading. Look at his hands.*

Tamsin crossed the room and knelt by the bed and took the boy's hand — gently, the way you handle something you already know is more fragile than it looks. The fingers were cool. Not dead-cool, not ill-cool, but the cool of earth turned up in January, the cool of something patient and vegetal waiting out a winter. Along the backs of the small hands, faint as watermark, ran lines like the veins of a leaf. Three days ago they hadn't been there. Or they had, and Tamsin had explained them away too.

Hollowroot opened his eyes. They were the color of new beech leaves, and they regarded Tamsin without fear, without pleading, with a kind of ancient courtesy that was worse than either.

The boy spoke then — not in the Old Tongue, not this time. In trade-speech, halting, each word placed like a stone across a stream.

"You carry me," he said. "Or the green comes through. All of it. All at once." A pause, and then, with the mildness of a child reciting a lesson: "The wolves are already asking the fences' permission. The fences are tired."

Tamsin's skin prickled from nape to wrist.

"He talks in his sleep," the wren murmured. "And lately, near waking. The land speaks to him and he answers honestly, because he does not know how to lie. That honesty is drawing things, Tamsin. Good things and bad. The wolves gather north of the city. And yesterday, on the roof across the lane, a dog sat watching this house for six hours. Not a stray. A hound with a cropped ear and a collar stamped with the Inquisitor's seal. It was not watching the house. It was watching the *green* coming off the house."

Tamsin rose slowly. Through the gap in the shutters he could see the lane below, silver-wet with storm runoff, empty in the way streets are empty just before dawn — and empty in another way too, the way a field goes quiet when something with teeth is standing downwind.

"Maren Vale," he said. "She's close."

*She has always been close. She keeps a list, and your name sits second from the top, and the boy has moved you to first.* The wren flew from the bedframe to his shoulder — a liberty she had never taken in three years, her talons light through his shirt, her warmth startlingly alive. *So. The bargain, plainly, one last time. Carry the seed to the Spire's heart within the moon. Speak the reforging. Be bound — voice, name, and future — to both realms, forever, with no promise that forever resembles survival. Refuse, and the covenant dies with the seed, and everything the old binding penned up comes home to the world unfenced. Those are the doors. There is no third one, however hard you look for it.*

Tamsin stood in the middle of his back room, in the middle of his small life, and felt the scale of the ask settle onto him like snow onto a bough — not breaking him, not yet, but bending him, bending him further with every silent second.

Below, in the shop, the brass bell over the door gave the smallest possible chime.

Neither of them had touched it. Nothing had come in. But it rang anyway, one thin bright note, like a warning passed hand to hand — and out in the lane, a dog began to bark, deep and rhythmic and disciplined, the bark of an animal reporting to a handler rather than speaking for itself.

Serevanne's talons tightened on his shoulder.

*Dawn is early today,* she said. *Decide while you climb.*
