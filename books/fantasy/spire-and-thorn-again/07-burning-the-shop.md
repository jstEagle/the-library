## 7. Burning the Shop

The hounds came at dusk, and Tamsin knew it before he heard it — knew it in the way the wren went still in her cage, feathers pressed flat as if the whole world had become a hawk's shadow.

"They're on Copperlane," she said. Her voice was a dry rustle, the voice of dead leaves and old fences. "Three of them. Grey coats, grey eyes, no tails docked because the Inquisitor likes them natural. They've stopped at Ferren's door."

Ferren was the charm-seller two streets over, the one who sold love-knots dyed with onion skin and called it magic to gull the lonely. Harmless. Carefully harmless, the way Tamsin had been harmless for three years.

"Will they arrest him?" Hollowroot asked from the corner where he sat among the luck-knots, cross-legged, his strange eyes catching the lamplight like water catching moon. The boy spoke Common now more often than not, though his accent was wrong in a way Tamsin couldn't name — every word placed too precisely, like stones set by someone who had never built a wall but had watched walls being built for a thousand years.

"No," the wren said. "They'll question him. Politely. And then they'll burn whatever's left of him that questions back."

Tamsin stood in the middle of his shop — his shop, his small warm kingdom of ribbon and twine and honest nothing — and felt the shape of his life the way you feel the shape of an egg just before it cracks. Three years of careful living. Three years of selling charms that were only knots and bone and blessed-if-you-believed-it, three years of smiling at customers while a wren whispered warnings in his ear and he pretended, always pretended, that he couldn't hear her.

He heard her now. He heard everything now. That was the trouble with letting the gift out even one finger's width — it came flooding through the gap like winter through a broken window.

The rats under the floorboards were already fleeing west. He could feel their small panicked hearts like rain on a roof. The cat that slept in Ferren's window was running too. Every animal within a quarter mile was moving away from Copperlane, away from the smell those hounds carried — magic smelled like blood to them, the wren had said, blood and honey both, and once they caught the thread they did not let go.

"Tamsin." The wren hopped twice along her perch. "Tamsin, listen to me. You have perhaps half an hour. Maybe less. Vale doesn't knock politely when she's certain."

"I'm listening." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I'm thinking."

"Think faster."

He looked around the shop and tried to see it as the hounds would see it — as Vale would see it. The counter where he'd carved a tiny wren into the wood one slow afternoon, an indulgence he'd told himself was decoration. The back room where the wren's cage hung beside the window, beside the shelf of mended-bone charms that were *not* only bone, that hummed faintly if you held them to your ear, that he'd never been able to stop making because the making was the breathing of him, the living of him. The cellar where he'd hidden during the purge of 'nineteen, a boy of seventeen curled among turnips while his mother burned four streets away.

Evidence everywhere. Evidence in every joint and corner. A life leaves fingerprints the way a bird leaves wingbeats — invisible until something comes along that can read the air.

"We can't run clean," he said slowly. "If I take everything, they'll know I ran knowing. If I leave anything—"

"You leave *everything*," the wren said. "You leave ash. Ash tells no stories."

Hollowroot rose from among the luck-knots. For a moment the lamplight bent strangely around him, and Tamsin saw — as he sometimes saw, in unguarded instants — what moved beneath the child's shape: something vast and green and patient, a seed the size of a cathedral folded impossibly small. The Spire's last seed. Wearing mud and a borrowed face.

"The shop is your nest," the boy said. "Animals grieve nests."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "They do."

He took down the lamp.

* * *

Fire is honest work. That was the terrible thing about it, standing there in his own doorway with the flame climbing the curtain he'd sewn himself from market cloth — fire did not pretend, did not smile, did not sell luck-knots to women who wanted their sons home from the war. Fire simply ate.

Tamsin fed it carefully. Oil from the lamp-tray across the counter first, so the heart of the shop went up quick and hot. Then the back room, the shelves of humming charms — he watched the little bones blacken and split and felt each one go like a tooth pulled, like syllables torn out of his own mouth. The carved wren in the counter wood curled and darkened and vanished. He made himself watch that especially. If he was going to do this, he would do all of it awake.

Smoke rolled thick and sweet with burning tallow and charred oak, pouring out into the street where the curfew storm still spat sleet. Somewhere a neighbor began to shout. Good. Let them shout. Let the whole street come running with buckets and gossip; a fire was the most ordinary catastrophe in Vell, the kind of story that drowned every other story in its telling. By morning everyone on Tanner's Row would have a version, and none of them would be true, and all of them would be loud.

"It hurts," Hollowroot said quietly, at his shoulder. Not a question. The boy had a way of stating things that made them heavier.

"Yes."

"You are allowed to grieve it. Grief is a root. It goes down deep and holds."

Tamsin laughed — a short, cracked sound, smoke-roughened. "That's very wise for an eight-year-old."

"I am older than eight." Hollowroot tilted his head, watching the flames reflect in his eyes. "I am also eight. Both things are true. The Thornwalk taught me that truth can hold more than one shape without breaking." He paused. "Your mother held two shapes. Shopkeeper and speaker. It nearly broke her, near the end. She told the trees so."

Tamsin went very still. "She talked to the trees?"

"She talked to everything. She was tired of hiding. Hiding was the heaviest charm she ever carried." The boy looked up at him, and there was something unbearably old and unbearably young in his face at once. "You carry it lighter. But you carry it."

The roof beams groaned. Heat pressed against Tamsin's face like a hand. He thought of the mornings here — the kettle on the iron plate, the wren scolding him about the price of millet, the first customer of the day shaking sleet off her cloak and asking, *anything lucky today, Master Sterling?* Small. Safe. A life the size of a cupped palm, and he had guarded it like a fortress, and now he stood watching it burn by his own hand because the alternative was the Inquisitor General reading his life like a letter.

Better to burn a letter than let your enemy read it.

"Come away," the wren said from inside his coat, where her cage had been replaced by a basket lined with his own scarf. "Come away now, or the fire will introduce us to the hounds itself."

* * *

The fish cart waited in the alley behind the tannery, exactly where the rat-queen had promised it would be. Tamsin had spent the last two days buying favors in the only currency beasts understood — food, shelter, attention, honesty — and the rats of Vell, who saw everything and judged everyone, had arranged this for the price of a wheel of hard cheese and a promise: *no cats in the tannery yard until spring.* The tanner would curse him come thaw. Let him. Tamsin was done paying debts in small coin.

The cart stank magnificently. Its driver, a broad silent woman named Odile with forearms like ship rope, had been paid in silver and asked no questions beyond one: "What's in the barrels?"

"Fish," Tamsin said.

Odile looked at him for a long moment with eyes the color of harbor water. "Fish," she agreed, in the tone of a woman deciding that whatever else was in the barrels, it wasn't her problem until it became her problem, and silver could delay that becoming considerably.

Hollowroot climbed into the second barrel through the cut hatch, folding his impossible limbs down among the packed ice and salted herring, and pulled the lid over himself. Before it closed, his face appeared in the gap one last time.

"If I wilt," he said seriously, "open the lid."

"You won't wilt."

"Everything wilts away from its soil. Open the lid anyway."

The lid closed. Tamsin stood in the freezing alley with his heart hammering against his ribs like something trying to escape, and realized he was laughing — silently, helplessly, tears freezing at the corners of his eyes. Eight days ago he had been a man who sold ribbons. Now he smuggled the soul of a dying realm in a herring barrel and took advice from it about wilting.

"Move," the wren murmured from his coat. "Grief later. Motion now."

They went north through the tannery yard, past the rendering sheds where the stink covered all other smells — even, he prayed, the blood-and-honey smell of a beast-speaker and a seed of the old world. The city wall rose ahead, black against the bruised sky, torches guttering along its walk. The Northgate was closed for curfew; the postern by the fish-market was not, because the fish-market never slept entirely, and Odile's cart had a pass stamped with the guild seal that Odile had not explained and Tamsin had not asked about.

At the gate, a guard lifted his lantern and peered in at the barrels. Tamsin held his breath and made his face into the face he'd worn for three years — pleasant, forgettable, a shopkeeper's nothing-face.

"Herring?" the guard said.

"Best catch of the season," Odile said. "Smells like it, don't it?"

The guard laughed, waved them through, and Tamsin breathed again only when the wall was fifty yards behind them and the open country lay ahead, white with old snow under a tearing wind.

Then, at the bend where the North Road met the river path, he saw him.

Dace Corrow stood beneath the bare elm at the roadside, alone, no lantern, no escort, his Inquisitor's badge glinting dully at his collar. Snow gathered on his shoulders. He had clearly been waiting some time. Clearly been watching the gate. Watching *him*.

Tamsin's whole body went to stone. Beside his heart, the wren whispered one word: "*Run.*"

But Dace didn't raise an alarm. Didn't move. Didn't call out. He just stood there in the falling snow, and across forty feet of frozen ground their eyes met — and Tamsin saw something in his old friend's face that he had spent three years refusing to look for. Not triumph. Not duty. Something rawer and more ashamed than either.

Dace Corrow opened his mouth. Closed it. And stepped back, deliberately, into the elm's shadow, letting the cart pass.

Tamsin did not understand it. He turned it over the whole length of the river road, past the last farms, past the first black line of the ashfields, while Odile drove and the ice shifted softly around the sleeping seed in the barrel. Why wait in the cold to say nothing? Why watch and let go? A warning? A weakness? Or the worst answer of all — that Dace wanted to know where Tamsin was going, so that when the time came, he could trade that knowledge for whatever the Inquisitor General paid her friends these days.

"He saw us," Tamsin said finally, when the city was a smudge of orange glow against the southern sky — his shop still burning, a scar of light on the horizon he could not stop looking at. "He'll tell her."

"Perhaps," the wren said.

"Perhaps? He testified against my mother. He put fourteen names in Vale's hands to save his own. He won't hesitate now that there's a pardon in it."

"Perhaps," the wren said again, softer. "And perhaps a man who has sold one friend learns what the coin costs. Birds remember which hands fed them and which hands netted them, Tamsin. Men are slower, but they learn the same lessons." She ruffled her feathers against the wind. "Either way, fly faster."

Ahead, the ashfields spread under the stars — miles of grey ruin where the old purge-fires had eaten the farmland down to bone, and nothing grew, and nothing had grown for nineteen years. Behind him, Vell burned in one small bright place. Ahead lay the dead land, and beyond it, somewhere past the horizon, the Thornwalk, and the Spire, and a covenant waiting to be reforged or lost forever.

Tamsin settled deeper into the cart bed, one hand resting on the cold barrel lid.

"Hold on, Hollowroot," he whispered. "We're going home."

From inside the barrel, faint as a heartbeat, came the smell of green things growing — impossible, out of season, rising up through salt and herring and packed ice like a promise the world had almost forgotten how to keep.

Behind them, far to the south, a horn sounded from the walls of Vell. Once. Twice. A third time.

The hunt had begun.
