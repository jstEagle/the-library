## 8. Road Through the Ashfields

The fish cart smelled of three days of herring and one day of freedom, and Tamsin could not stop breathing it in like perfume.

He rode on the bench beside old Master Ollin, who had asked no questions beyond *where to* and *how much*, and behind them, beneath a tarpaulin stiff with salt, Hollowroot slept among the empty barrels. The boy had not stirred since they passed the last of Vell's watchtowers. Behind them, a smudge of smoke still rose where his shop had been — his home, his livelihood, his carefully constructed tomb of small safe lies — and Tamsin found he could not look at it for long without something in his chest pulling loose.

So he looked north instead.

The road out of Vell ran through country that had once been farmland before the purges, before the Inquisition's first General had ordered every hedgerow burned for fear of what might nest in it. What remained was the Ashfields: mile upon mile of grey stubble and blackened fence posts, soil so scalded by the old fires that nothing grew but a bitter moss the farmers' grandchildren called widow's hair. The wind came off it carrying dust and the memory of smoke. Even the crows kept their distance, wheeling high and silent, as though the ground below offended them.

Tamsin knew why it was bare. He had always known, in the way you know a scar is there without looking at it. This land had been green when the covenant held. His mother had told him — sung him, rather, in the old tongue she'd thought he was too young to remember — how the fields around Vell had ripened gold under the Spire's long protection, how the wild things had walked the field-margins like neighbors, how no blight had touched a crop in four hundred years.

Then the covenant broke. Then the burning began. And the earth itself, insulted, had simply stopped trying.

"Cheerful country," Master Ollin said, spitting over the cart's side. "You'd think after thirty years even dirt would learn to forgive."

"Dirt has a long memory," Tamsin said.

Ollin grunted, which might have been agreement or might have been the whole of his conversational budget for the morning, and clicked his tongue at the mule.

Behind them, under the tarp, a small voice said, "Dirt remembers us too."

Tamsin went very still.

Hollowroot had spoken perhaps twenty words since Vell — mostly in that language older than kingdoms, syllables that made Tamsin's back teeth ache and the wren flutter in her travel basket. The human tongue sat strangely in the boy's mouth, as if he were wearing someone else's coat. But he was speaking more each day. The wren had warned him of this, in her dry way: *the seed wakes as it nears its soil. Every mile north, he will be more himself and less the child you see.*

"Hollowroot," Tamsin said quietly, not turning. "You should stay under the canvas. If anyone passes—"

"No one passes," the boy said, with the simple certainty of weather. "The road is empty because the land told everything living to leave it. They're waiting to see what I do."

A cold finger traced Tamsin's spine. He glanced at Ollin, who was humming tunelessly, deaf to all of it — or mercifully unable to hear; the gift did not extend to men, only to beasts, and whatever else Hollowroot was, he was not a beast.

"What are you going to do?" Tamsin asked.

"I don't know yet." A pause, and then, wonderingly, almost childlike despite everything: "There's so much dead here. It's like walking through a house where everyone died mid-sentence."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a collapsed barn, its beams charred to the color of old bone. Ollin turned his cart toward Vell at dusk with a nod and no questions — Tamsin had paid him triple, and the man had the good sense of the long-employed not to wonder about passengers who paid triple. After that it was just the two of them, and the wren, and a fire Tamsin built small and shielded with stones.

Hollowroot crouched at the edge of the firelight, hands pressed flat against the ash-grey soil, and Tamsin watched him the way you watch a fever patient, waiting for the crisis.

"What are you doing?"

"Listening." The boy's eyes were closed. "It's very quiet down there. Like people holding their breath. But—" his brow furrowed— "not angry. That's the strange part. My roots expected anger. This is just... tired."

"The Ashfields were burned," Tamsin said. "Thirty years ago. On purpose. To starve anything wild of shelter."

"I know. I can taste it." Hollowroot opened his eyes, and in the firelight they were the deep green of the underside of leaves. "I can taste the smoke still. It's in the soil like blood in cloth." He considered. "You burned your own garden because you were afraid of what lived in it."

*Your own garden.* Tamsin opened his mouth to say *we didn't know*, and heard his mother's voice in the memory, singing, and closed it again. "Yes," he said finally. "We did."

Hollowroot nodded, unsurprised, unsurprisable, and put his hand back on the ground.

Tamsin woke once in the night to a sound he couldn't name — a soft, vast sighing, like wind through a forest where no forest stood. He sat up. Hollowroot was asleep, curled small among the barrels they'd kept from the cart, and the sound was gone. But in the firelight's last ember-glow, Tamsin saw it: a ring of pale green shoots around the boy's sleeping body, no taller than a fingernail, pushing up through thirty years of ash.

By dawn they were knee-high.

* * *

The wren made him stop at midmorning.

"*Look,*" she said from her basket, and her voice had lost its usual dryness; there was something underneath it he had never heard from her before, and it took him a moment to recognize as awe. "*Look behind you.*"

He turned. The road they had walked since dawn — a grey scar through grey land — was no longer grey. Grass ran along their backtrail in a ribbon perhaps ten paces wide, vivid, impossible, dotted with flowers Tamsin had no names for because no one alive had seen them bloom this far south. Butterflies moved above it. Actual butterflies, in the Ashfields, in early spring, where the last recorded butterfly had been pressed into an Inquisitor's ledger as evidence forty years ago.

And it wasn't just their trail. Off to the east, across a fallow field that had been stubble yesterday, a haze of green hung in the air like heat-shimmer in reverse. As he watched, a blackened fence post — dead wood, burnt to charcoal — put out a single white blossom.

"That's not possible," Tamsin said.

"Everything is possible," the wren said. "Very little of it is wise. He's leaking, Tamsin. The seed is waking faster than we're traveling, and it doesn't know how to be subtle. It never learned. Subtlety is a mortal skill."

"He's a child."

"He's a child the way a river is a drink of water." She ruffled her feathers. "Keep him moving. Keep him fed. And keep him away from open ground, because—" She stopped. Her head cocked sharply eastward.

"What?" Tamsin said. "What is it?"

"Wolves," she said. "Coming fast. Don't run. Whatever happens, don't run, and don't let him run."

They came out of the eastern scrub within minutes — six of them, lean and grey and moving with the low-headed purpose of animals that have decided something is food. Tamsin's heart slammed against his ribs. He put himself between the pack and Hollowroot and reached, desperately, into the old place inside him where the listening lived, the place he had kept boarded shut for three years, and threw open every door at once.

*We are not prey,* he pushed at them, the words raw from disuse. *We are passing. We mean no harm. Go around.*

The lead wolf stopped ten feet away. Its nostrils flared. And Tamsin felt it feel him — felt the animal mind brush against his like a hand testing a door — and then the wolf's head swung past him entirely, to the boy.

Hollowroot stepped out from behind Tamsin's leg.

What happened next, Tamsin would spend the rest of his life failing to describe properly. The wolves did not attack. They did not merely refrain from attacking. They *lowered themselves* — bellies to the new grass, ears flat, tails still — in a gesture so ancient and so deliberate that Tamsin's skin crawled. Not submission to a predator. Something older. The way you kneel in a temple.

The lead wolf whined once, softly, and touched its nose to the boy's bare foot.

"They're sad," Hollowroot said, looking down at them. His voice wavered, and for a moment he sounded exactly, heartbreakingly, like an eight-year-old. "They've been so hungry for so long, and they don't know why, and they thought maybe—" He knelt and put both hands into the fur of the lead wolf's neck. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it's been so long."

The pack stayed with them for most of that day. They did not follow closely; they ran the ridgelines parallel to the road, visible against the sky like escorts, like an honor guard. Once, at dusk, they sang — a long rising chorus that made the hairs on Tamsin's arms stand up, and the wren said quietly, "That's not a hunting song. I haven't heard that song since the covenant broke. That's a *greeting* song. They think the old days are coming back."

"And if they're wrong?" Tamsin asked.

"Then they'll have greeted a corpse," the wren said. "Get him to the Spire, shopkeeper. Before the whole world starts believing in him."

* * *

They walked the next two days through a country being born.

Tamsin tried to keep count of the miracles and gave up by noon of the first day. A stream that the maps marked as poisoned ran clear over clean gravel, and fish stood in it, finning, as though they'd been waiting underground for permission. An orchard of thirty-year-dead apple trees came into blossom over the course of a single afternoon, petals drifting across the road like snow falling upward. Wherever Hollowroot slept, the ground greened in a circle around him, and the circles were getting larger. On the second night the grass reached the road's edge on both sides, and by morning the Ashfields behind them were a rumor and the Ashfields ahead were retreating like a tide going out.

Hollowroot talked now constantly — to the soil, to the birds, to the mule they no longer had, to worms Tamsin couldn't hear at all. He named plants Tamsin had never seen and described their uses. He asked questions a child would ask — *why do humans burn things, do you sleep in the winter, what is a birthday* — and questions no child should ask — *when the last warden died, who held the border between the dreaming and the waking, does the feral magic have a shape yet or is it still formless.*

To that last one, Tamsin had said, "I don't know," and Hollowroot had nodded gravely and said, "Neither does it. That's what frightens me."

But it was on the third evening, camped in the ruin of a village whose name had been scraped off the signpost by some careful official hand, that the wonder curdled.

Tamsin had climbed the village well's broken parapet to look back the way they'd come, a habit he couldn't break — the hunted animal's habit, checking the treeline, checking the horizon. The land behind them glowed green in the sunset, a swath of impossible spring miles wide, visible from any height for any distance.

Visible from a watchtower. Visible from the air, if the Inquisitor General kept any winged spies. Visible, unmistakable, undeniable: *something passed this way, and it was holy, and it was heading north.*

He thought of Maren Vale — he had never met her, but everyone in Vell had a picture of her built from other people's frightened whispers — and her list, and his name second from the top, and the hounds that smelled magic like blood. He thought of what a column of soldiers would make of a green road through thirty years of ash. He thought of the word the wren used, *leaking*, and understood that they were not fleeing anymore.

They were leaving a trail a blind man could follow.

He climbed down and found Hollowroot sitting among the ruins, holding a robin in his cupped hands, whispering to it. Around the boy, in the ruined village square, the weeds had come up waist-high in a single day, and something that might have been a rosebush was forcing its way through the flagstones beside the well.

"Hollowroot." He kept his voice gentle. "We need to talk about what you're doing to the land."

The boy looked up. "I'm not doing it. It's doing itself. It's like—" he searched— "like when you've been holding your breath underwater for a really long time. The land is breathing out. I can't tell it not to breathe."

"Can you try? Smaller breaths?" Tamsin crouched to his level. "Because right now, everything growing marks our road as clearly as if we painted it. And there are people following us who burn things. That's the first thing they do. It's the only thing they do."

Something moved through the boy's face — and Tamsin realized, with a lurch, that Hollowroot had known. Of course he had known. He'd probably known before Tamsin did.

"I can try," the boy said. "But it will hurt the land. It's been starving for thirty years, and I'm the first food it's tasted, and you want me to walk through it pretending to be empty." His chin trembled, and for one moment he was purely, simply a tired eight-year-old in a dead village at dusk. Then he steadied, and the steadiness was ancient again, and somehow that was worse. "The warden before you — my mother's warden, the last one — she would have understood. You understand too. You just wish you didn't."

Tamsin's throat tightened. "Understanding doesn't keep the fires away."

"No," Hollowroot agreed. "But it's what the fires are *for*. They burn to keep people from understanding." He released the robin; it flew up into the new weeds. "I'll try to be quiet. Small breaths. But Tamsin—" and here his voice dropped, and the shadows under the broken houses seemed to lean in— "there's something else. The wolves told me tonight. There are dogs on our road. Not wolves. Dogs. Big ones, pale ones, and they carry a smell ahead of them like a wound. The animals are all hiding from it. Even the worms have gone deep."

Tamsin's stomach dropped through the fresh green earth. "How far?"

"A day," Hollowroot said. "Maybe less. They don't sleep. The woman who runs them doesn't believe in sleep. She thinks it's a kind of lying."

The wren, from the rosebush taking shape beside the well, said one word: "*Ride.*"

There was nothing left to ride. So they walked — through that night and the next day, eating as they marched, Hollowroot doing his best, Tamsin could see, to hold his breath, to be small, to be empty. The greening slowed. The miracles thinned to a stubborn grass along the roadside and one defiant line of daisies. It hurt the boy; Tamsin could see it in the grey creeping back into his cheeks, the way his steps dragged, a seed being asked to unfurl and fold shut, unfurl and fold shut, over and over.

"I'm sorry," Tamsin kept saying.

"Don't be sorry," Hollowroot panted, near midnight of the second day, at the top of a long hill. "Be *fast*."

From the crest of the hill, Tamsin looked north — and stopped.

Below them, the Ashfields ended. Not faded, not thinned: *ended*, along a line as clean as a drawn blade, and beyond it lay darkness — a vast, unbroken dark that no moon explained, rolling to the horizon like a black sea. Trees. Old trees. A forest so dense and so deep-green it looked solid, a country-sized shadow that predated every kingdom whose coins Tamsin had ever handled.

The Thornwalk. The forest that remembered names. The last approach to the Spire.

And behind them, far to the south, at the very edge of seeing, a thread of orange light flickered against the stars. Small. Distant. Moving.

Torches. Many torches, strung out along the green road they had made, coming on at a pace no exhausted man and a wilting child could match.

Hollowroot gripped Tamsin's hand. His palm was cold, and damp, and Tamsin tried not to notice that it felt, faintly, like the bark of a tree beginning to die.

"The forest will hide us," the boy whispered.

"The forest tests everyone who enters it," Tamsin said. "My mother told me. It shows you what you are."

"Good," said Hollowroot, with a weariness far too old for his face. "Then it will show her too. Come on. Before the light catches us."

Hand in hand, they went down the hill toward the dark, while behind them the torches of honest law came on through the miracle they had sown.
