## 10. Hounds of Honest Law

The rain had followed them out of Vell like a debt collector, patient and inevitable, and Maren Vale rode at the head of her column with her hood down because she had never once in her life hidden from weather, or from anything else.

Twelve hounds ran in the loose crescent before her horse — lean, grey-flanked beasts bred in the kennels beneath the Inquisitorial Hall, their bloodlines pruned for three generations toward a single purpose: the scent of magic. Not blood, though they knew that too. Magic. The old kind, the buried kind, the kind that clung to skin and hearthstone and the inside of a liar's mouth. When a hound caught it, it did not bark. It went silent, and its hackles rose in a ridge from skull to tail, and it pointed like an arrow of living law.

Vale trusted them more than she trusted any person alive.

That was not a confession she would have made aloud. It was simply arithmetic. People lied — beautifully, desperately, with tears and scripture and their children held up as shields. She had watched a grandmother swear on the bones of her own dead husband while a splinter of thornwood burned in her apron pocket. She had watched a boy of eleven denounce his mother to save his own skin, and she had signed both warrants with the same steady hand, because mercy was a door left open and doors were how fires spread.

Hounds did not lie. Hounds smelled what was true.

"Report," she said, and Sergeant Ilvane drew alongside, water streaming off the brim of her helm.

"The ashfields end two miles north, General. After that, hedgerows, then the river country. The fish-cart tracks turn off the high road at the standing stone — someone's tried to sweep them, but the mud's too soft." A pause. "And the hounds are... restless."

"Restless."

Ilvane's jaw worked. She was a good soldier, Ilvane, which meant she hated delivering anything that sounded like superstition. "They keep stopping, General. At nothing. They lift their heads like they're listening to something we can't hear, and then they go on. Ash has been doing it since dawn."

Ash. The lead hound. Twelve years old, grey-muzzled now, sired on the last of the true witch-hound lines. Vale had raised her from a pup that bit everything, including Vale, until the day Vale had held her starving sister's body in front of her and said, *This is what magic does. Smell it. Remember.* And Ash had remembered. Ash had never forgotten anything in her life.

"Show me."

They found the hounds at the edge of the ashfields where the burnt ground gave way to green, and Vale reined in and looked at what the green was doing, and felt the first cold thread of something she refused to name.

The fields should have been mud and stubble this time of year. Instead, in a rough circle perhaps forty paces across, the earth had broken open into new growth — not weeds, not the ordinary stubborn grass of early spring, but clover in flower, vetch, wild barley already headed out, all of it lush and wrong, all of it growing as if the season itself had been persuaded to lie. Frost still rimed the fence posts at the circle's edge. Inside the circle, bees moved among the blossoms.

Bees. In the frost.

"It was half this size yesterday," Ilvane said quietly. "I measured it against the boundary stone."

Vale did not answer. She dismounted, boots sinking into soil soft as a fresh-turned grave, and walked the circle's rim while the column waited in the rain behind her. The green crunched faintly under her heels where frost and growth fought each other for the same inch of ground. She crouched and pressed her gloved palm flat against a flowering stem and thought: *this is what unchecked looks like. This is what my sister's killers wanted the whole world to be — a place where power grew wild and no one asked it for a license or a reason.*

She stood. She had trained herself, over twenty years, to convert every wonder into evidence. It was the discipline of her office. Wonder made people kneel; evidence made them think; thinking kept them alive.

"Ash," she called. "Seek."

The hound came forward through the rain, low-shouldered, and put her nose to the cart-tracks, and Vale watched the great body go rigid with certainty. North. Toward the river country. Toward the toll bridge over the Greywater, if the fugitive had any sense, and by all accounts the charm-seller Sterling had sense enough to fill a warehouse.

They rode hard through the afternoon. The green patches came more often now — a hedgerow budding blackthorn blossom out of season, a dead elm pushing up suckers around its scorched trunk, a flock of starlings wheeling in patterns too deliberate to be accident. Vale catalogued each one in her ledger with date and distance and noted the trend line the way she'd note the spread of plague: the source was moving north, and the source was getting stronger.

*Hollowroot.*

The name had come to her on the third night after the shop fire, delivered by a sewer-rat catcher who'd been paid too well to lie: a child, or something shaped like a child, that the beasts of Vell had gone strange and quiet around. The rat-catcher had crossed himself twice telling it. Vale had written the word down in her precise, upright hand and felt, beneath the ink, the old cold anger stir and stretch like a dog waking.

She knew what the Spire was. Everyone in her office knew, whatever the official histories said about *superstition* and *the old cults*. The Spire was the wound the world had never been allowed to close — the place where the thorn-realm bled into theirs, where the covenant-folk had once traded with things that wore the shapes of animals and spoke with the voices of rivers. Her order existed because of what came out of such places. Because of what had come out of one such place, on a spring night nineteen years ago, into a farm kitchen where a girl of nine had been singing to herself while she churned butter.

Vale had been fourteen. She had been the one who found the kitchen.

She did not think about the kitchen. She thought about the work. The work was clean. The work had a list, and names on the list, and names could be crossed off, and when the last name was crossed off the world would be safe for girls churning butter, and no one would ever again have to stand in a doorway looking at what she had looked at.

"Toll bridge ahead," Ilvane called back as dusk came down blue and iron-grey. "Lights burning."

---

The bridge-keeper's name was Brannoc, and he was a bad liar, which Vale noted with professional approval even as she despised him for trying. His hands wouldn't stay still. His eyes went to the north road twice in the first minute, and once — fatally — to the west, toward the old drover's path that climbed along the river gorge.

"You've seen a fish cart," Vale said. It was not a question. "Today. One man driving, heavy load, going north."

"No cart, lady. Been quiet all day." He wiped his palms on his apron. Behind him, in the toll-house window, a girl's dress hung drying — small, a child's, though he'd told the census-takers he lived alone. Vale filed that away without deciding to use it. Filing things away was most of command.

"Quiet," she repeated. She let the silence do its work. Silence always did. Around her, the twelve hounds sat in the rain in perfect discipline, and she saw the bridge-keeper's gaze snag on them and flinch, because everyone flinched at the hounds eventually. They were the honest face of honest law. They could not be bribed or charmed or argued with. They simply knew.

Then Ash stood up.

It was a small motion — a shift of weight, head lifting, nostrils working the wet air off the western gorge — but Vale had spent twelve years reading this animal the way priests read scripture, and she read it instantly. *Scent. Fresh. Close.*

"How far along the drover's path is shelter?" Vale asked softly, not looking at Brannoc anymore. Looking at the west. "A barn? A shrine? Anything with a roof?"

"There's the old shepherd's hut," Brannoc said, and heard himself, and went grey. "I mean — I couldn't say—"

"You've said enough. Thank you for your honesty." She swung back into the saddle. "Sergeant, six hounds, take the path. Quietly. If it's the Sterling man, I want him alive — the General Writ specifies alive. The child—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—the specimen comes to me untouched. No blade touches it. No rope around its neck. Am I understood?"

Understood, they said, and melted into the dark along the gorge path, and Vale sat her horse at the bridge foot and listened to the rain and felt, for the first time in nineteen years, something she had no ledger-column for.

Doubt? No. She rejected the word before it finished forming. Doubt was for people who hadn't seen the kitchen.

Apprehension, then. The soldier's animal sense that the plan and the world had briefly stopped matching. Because the green circles were getting bigger. Because the bees were flying in the frost. Because somewhere north of here, a thing wearing a child's body was rewriting the seasons as it walked, and her hounds — her honest, unlying hounds — had begun, in the last two days, to do something she had never once seen them do.

At night, in camp, they faced north. All twelve. In their sleep. Like compass needles.

---

They took the shepherd's hut an hour past midnight, and it was empty, but it had been warm within the hour, and there were signs Vale read like a confession: two cups, one small; a scatter of barley dropped from a pocket; and on the dirt floor, pressed deep and recent, the print of a bare child's foot — a print that was wrong, that was *deeply* wrong, because around each toe-mark the dirt had sprouted. Fine white roots, hair-thin, already browning at the tips where the cold found them.

Vale crouched over the print for a long time. Her lantern threw her shadow huge against the hut's far wall, and she was aware, distantly, that her own breath had gone shallow.

*Roots,* she thought. *It puts down roots when it stands still. What happens when it reaches the Spire? What takes root there?*

"General." Ilvane at the door, voice carefully level. "You'll want to see this."

Outside, in the trampled yard, the six hounds had formed a ring around something, and were not attacking it, and were making sounds Vale had never heard from them — low, wavering, almost plaintive. She pushed through her soldiers and looked down.

A bird lay in the mud. A wren, brown and small and unmistakably dead, its wings folded wrong, one spot of dark blood at its breast. Shielding-wounds, some distant part of her noted — a killing blow taken from above, from a hawk or an owl, the body placed — no, *left* — here deliberately, in the middle of the yard, in their path.

The hounds circled it and whined.

"Move back," Vale said. "All of you. Now."

They didn't move.

Six hounds, six years of training each, the finest animals in the realm, and they stood over the corpse of a songbird and *grieved*, and when Vale stepped forward herself, Ash broke from the ring and planted herself between Vale and the wren's body — not snarling, not baring teeth, Ash had never bared teeth at her — but standing, head lowered, ears flat, in the oldest posture a dog knows.

The posture of pleading. *Don't. Please don't.*

"Ash. Heel."

The hound trembled. Rain ran off her grey muzzle. And then, slowly, deliberately, in full view of the entire column, Ash lowered her forequarters to the mud and knelt — there was no other word for it, no other word in any language Vale knew — knelt to the dead bird, or to whatever the dead bird meant, or to whatever was north of here that the dead bird had died protecting, and she looked up at Vale with brown eyes that held, impossibly, unmistakably, an expression Vale had spent her whole career teaching the world to distrust.

Understanding.

"General," someone whispered behind her. "General, what's it doing?"

What was it doing. What was *she* doing, standing in the rain at midnight over a dead wren, feeling the ground tilt under twenty years of certainty. Because here was the thing she had built her life on: the hounds did not lie. The hounds smelled truth. And her hound — her best, her truest, the animal she had raised on grief and purpose — was kneeling to the enemy and begging her to stop.

Two explanations, then. Only two.

Either the hounds had been compromised — beguiled, ensorcelled, turned — in which case everything she trusted was rotten, and the world was worse than she'd believed.

Or the hounds were telling the truth.

Vale stood very still in the rain and let both possibilities cut her, because she was not a woman who flinched from a blade just because it was honest. She thought of the kitchen. She thought of the splinter in its glass case in her baggage train, the thorn-splinter taken from a hedge-witch's heart years ago, which turned, very slowly, of its own accord, to point north — and which she had never reported, because reports invited questions, and questions invited doubt, and doubt was a door.

*A door,* she thought again. *Everything is doors.*

"Heel," she said to Ash, one final time, and her voice did not shake, because she had practiced it not shaking for nineteen years.

The hound rose. Came to her side. Pressed her wet flank against Vale's boot — the old greeting, the pup-greeting, *I am yours, I am yours* — and Vale looked down at her and felt the terrible arithmetic complete itself.

If the hound was lying, the hound was ruined, and everything built on hounds was ruined, and she had nothing.

If the hound was telling the truth, then the truth was that the thing ahead deserved kneeling to, and everything built on *her* was ruined, and she had nothing.

There was a third option. There was always a third option, the one that cost the most and bought the most: that truth was larger than her ledger, that the covenant and the kitchen could both be real, that the world contained a magic that sang to children and a magic that ate them and that her office had spent nineteen years refusing to learn the difference because learning the difference would mean admitting the purges had burned innocents alongside monsters, hundreds of them, thousands, names on lists crossed off in her own steady hand—

No.

She shut the door on it. She was good at shutting doors.

"Ilvane," she said. "Bring me the lead."

The sergeant brought the lead — a short length of chain and leather, standard issue, used for controlling hounds in combat — and Vale took it in her right hand, and Ash looked up at her, and the whole column held its breath in the rain.

"She's disobeyed a direct order," Vale said, loudly, so they all heard. "She knelt to the enemy. Whatever beguilement is working on these animals, it has reached my lead hound, and I will not carry a corrupted instrument into the field. This is how the old covens worked — not with armies, but with *affection*. With loyalty turned sideways. You have all seen what love of the wrong thing costs." She thought of Dace Corrow, waiting at the ford with his warrant and his ruined face, and did not know why she thought of him. "We are the law of honesty. We do not get to keep dishonest instruments. Even beloved ones. *Especially* beloved ones."

"General," Ilvane started, "she's twelve years old, she's the finest—"

"I know what she is." Vale's voice was quiet now, and quieter was worse; her soldiers knew that. "I have known what she is since she was eight weeks old. That is precisely why it must be me."

She knelt in the mud beside her hound. Ash licked her wrist, once, the way she had as a puppy, and Vale took the animal's muzzle gently in her left hand, feeling the warm breath, the trust, the terrible unlying trust, and she said — and only the hound heard it, and perhaps that was a mercy, and perhaps it was the opposite —

"If you're right about what's out there, then I'm sorry for what I'm about to do to the world. But I can't build safety on maybe. Forgive me. I never taught you any language but obedience, and now it's the only prayer either of us knows."

Then she did it quickly, the way she did everything, because hesitation was a door, and when it was done she stood and washed her hands in the rain-water pooling in the trough, thoroughly, the way the old rites prescribed, though she had never believed in rites.

"Mount up," she said. "We ride for the Spire road. Double pace. Nothing stops us now."

And the column moved out into the dark, eleven hounds running silent in the crescent, all of them facing north, and behind them in the drowned yard the wren lay in the mud with her wings folded wrong, and the rain came down on the shepherd's hut and the blooming fields and the whole green, listening land, and far to the north, at the edge of a forest older than kingdoms, the trees had begun, very softly, to remember her name.
