## 11. Dace at the Ford

The river called the Liss ran shallow where it bent around the old standing stone, and Dace Corrow had been waiting in the reeds since before dawn.

He had chosen the ford because it was the only crossing for thirty miles that a man with a child and a fish cart could use without swimming. He had chosen the reeds because he did not want to be seen waiting. And he had chosen to come alone — no hounds, no riders, no Inquisitor's white-and-ash livery — because what he meant to do this morning was not, strictly speaking, his duty.

It was the opposite of his duty. He had been telling himself for three days that it wasn't, and he had stopped believing himself somewhere outside Ashfield Hollow, watching a field of barley bloom out of season through the slats of a burning barn.

The mist came off the water in slow ropes. Somewhere upstream a heron shifted its weight, and Dace heard it — actually heard it, the creak of its legs like an old door — because three years in Vale's service sharpened every sense except the one that mattered. He crouched lower among the reeds, felt the cold of the water seep through his boot seams, and waited for Tamsin Sterling the way you wait at the bedside of someone dying: knowing exactly how it ends, unable to leave.

*You'll arrest him,* Vale had said. *Or you'll bring me back his head, and I'll know which man you are.*

She hadn't said it as a threat. That was the worst of her. She said it the way a physician says *we'll know more after the bleeding.* She had given him two riders and four hounds and then, at the last moment, taken them back. "Go alone," she'd said, studying him with those pale eyes that never blinked when they ought to. "A man runs from soldiers. He listens to a friend."

And that was the thing about Maren Vale. She was not cruel, or not only cruel. She was *correct*. She had looked at Dace Corrow — testified against his own coven, sworn away his own mother's memory, worn the gray sash of a converted practitioner for six years — and she had seen precisely what he was good for.

*A friend,* she'd said, and smiled, and it had gone through him like a splinter working toward the heart.

* * *

He heard the cart before he saw it. The axle needed grease; Tamsin had always been careless with axles, with buckles, with everything except the small bright things he made with his hands. Dace remembered being eleven years old and watching Tamsin knot a luck-charm out of river grass, fingers moving like they were threading music, and Tamsin saying, *Don't tell anyone, Dace. Promise me.* And Dace promising, and meaning it, right up until the day meaning it would have cost him his neck.

The cart came down through the birches with the light behind it, and there he was. Tamsin Sterling, charm-seller of Copperlane, wanted by the Inquisition, driving a borrowed fish cart north with a boy beside him who was not a boy. He looked thinner than Dace remembered. He looked tired in the way that doesn't wash off. He had a wren perched on his shoulder — Dace saw it and his stomach dropped, because everyone knew what a beast-speaker's familiar meant, and if Vale's people ever got a description—

Tamsin saw him at the same instant. The cart stopped so abruptly the horse complained.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The river talked to itself over the stones. Mist unwound between them like something being said in a language neither spoke anymore.

"Dace." Tamsin's voice came out flat. Not surprised. That hurt worse than surprise would have. "Of course it's you."

"Hello, Tam."

"You're alone."

"I'm alone."

"The hounds?"

"A mile back, on a long lead. They'll come when I whistle." He stood up out of the reeds slowly, hands open and empty, showing the sword at his belt but not touching it. "I'd rather they stayed bored."

Tamsin laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry and short, with nothing in it. "Would you. Gods. You used to be a bad liar, Dace. Now you're a good one. I don't know which version I hate more."

"I'm not lying."

"No," Tamsin agreed softly. "That's the horror of it. You never were."

* * *

They ended up sitting on the standing stone, which was absurd, which was eleven years old again, which was the whole problem. The stone was older than Vell, older than the kingdom, one of the ones the Inquisition hadn't gotten around to toppling because it was too far from any road that mattered. Moss covered whatever carving might once have been on it. Dace sat with his sword across his knees where Tamsin could see it, because pretending it wasn't there would have been a kindness, and kindness was the one currency he'd lost the right to spend.

The boy watched them from the cart. He didn't blink enough. Dace kept catching himself looking at the child and having to look away, the way you look away from a wound.

"He doesn't talk?" Dace asked.

"He talks plenty," Tamsin said. "Just not to you."

Fair enough. Dace turned his cupped hands over in his lap. There was mud under his nails from the reeds. Six years ago he'd been a clerk's apprentice with soft hands. Before that he'd been a hedge-coven brat who knew which mushrooms killed and which cured, whose mother had taught him the words that made bees settle and milk sour and fevers break. His mother. Who had burned on the same day as Tamsin's, in the same square, while Dace stood in the crowd in his new gray sash and did not look away, because looking away would have been the last dishonest thing left available to him.

"Tamsin." He made himself say it plainly. "Vale has the Thornwalk mapped. She has outriders on every track north of the Liss. She has forty men, four hounds that can smell magic the way sharks smell blood, and a warrant with your name on it that isn't going to expire. You cannot win this. Nobody wins against her. People survive her, occasionally, by doing exactly what she says."

"And what does she say?"

"She says—" Dace stopped. Started again. "Surrender quietly. Come in before the hounds. Don't make her burn anything else to get to you." He heard his own voice go low and urgent and hated how much it sounded like begging, because it was begging. "Tam, listen to me. If you come in quiet — if the boy comes in quiet — she's promised me mercy. Formal mercy. Pardon papers. She keeps her word; that's the one thing about her that's iron. You'll do time, maybe the penitent's year, maybe service. But you'll live. The boy will live. And I will have done my job without—"

"Without what?"

"Without anyone else getting hurt." His voice cracked on *hurt*, and he pressed his lips together until it passed.

Tamsin was looking at him with an expression Dace had never seen on him before. It took him a moment to name it, and when he named it his chest went cold: pity. Tamsin Sterling was looking at him with pity.

"Do you believe her?" Tamsin asked gently. "About the mercy?"

"Yes." And he did. That was the trap of Maren Vale — she was honest. She told you exactly what she intended, and then she did it, and the mercy was real, it simply wasn't the mercy you imagined. Mercy, in Vale's vocabulary, meant you died quickly instead of slowly. It meant your name went into the record as *penitent* rather than *heretic*. It meant your ashes weren't displayed.

"Then here's what I believe," Tamsin said. He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and for a second Dace saw the boy he'd grown up with underneath the shopkeeper's weathered face — the same stubborn set of the jaw, the same eyes that had once held a captured sunbeam to show Dace how light bent through a charm-glass. "I believe you think you're saving me. I believe you'd take the hounds' teeth yourself if it would keep them off my throat. And I believe none of it matters, Dace, because what you're asking me to do is walk into a square and let them burn the last living piece of something older than their law, and call it mercy, and sleep afterward."

"It's not—"

"What is the boy, Dace? What did Vale tell you he is?"

"A practitioner. A wild-touched child. Dangerous."

Tamsin smiled, and it was the saddest thing Dace had ever seen. "He's a seed," he said. "He's the last seed of the Spire, wearing a shape we can bear to look at. And the covenant that kept the old magic leashed — the magic that ate whole villages before our grandmothers' grandmothers bound it — that covenant is dying, and the only thing that can save both realms is carrying him home. If he dies in a cell, Dace, if he dies on a pyre, the leash dies with him. Everything your Inquisition is afraid of comes off its chain at once. Vale isn't hunting a criminal. She's cutting the rope that's holding the wolf."

Dace stared at him. The river talked. The heron cried somewhere upstream, one raw note.

"That's what the beasts tell you," he said finally. "That's wren-talk and rat-talk and you expect me to—"

"I expect you to remember your mother," Tamsin said.

The words landed like a slap. Dace was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, and the sword was half-drawn, and Tamsin hadn't flinched, hadn't even stood, just sat there on the old stone looking up at him with those eyes.

"Don't," Dace said. "Don't you dare say her name."

"Somebody has to. You won't. You traded her testimony for a sash and now you can't say her name because saying it means remembering what you did, and what you did wasn't evil, Dace, it was worse, it was *small*. You were scared and they gave you a way to be scared and safe at the same time, and you took it, and you've been taking it every day for six years, and now you've ridden ahead of the hounds to offer me the same bargain because misery loves—"

"*Stop.*"

"—company."

The word hung between them. Downstream, a fish jumped, a small silver stitch in the gray morning. Dace became aware that his hand was shaking on the sword grip, and that the boy in the cart had risen to his feet, and that every bird within earshot had gone silent at once — that sudden, total, wrong silence that followed the child everywhere, that Dace had read about in interrogation reports and never believed.

Slowly, hand by hand, he sheathed the sword.

* * *

They stood apart from each other after that, two men on opposite banks of something wider than the Liss. Dace found he couldn't look at Tamsin directly anymore, so he looked at the water instead, at the light coming up through the mist, gold beginning to bleed into the gray.

"I testified," he said, when he could trust his voice. "Yes. I did. I was seventeen years old and they had me in a room with a man whose job was breaking people, and he showed me the list, Tam. The list of names. Mine was on it. My mother's was on it. And he said, very kindly, that I could be on the list or I could be under it, and I chose, and I have woken up every morning since choosing it again." He swallowed. "You want me to say it was a wound. Fine. It was a wound. I cut us both with the same knife. But don't stand there and tell me you wouldn't have done the same, because I watched you hide for three years, Tamsin. Three years of selling mended-bone charms and smiling at customers and letting that wren whisper warnings you pretended not to hear. You hid your gift so deep you nearly drowned it. We're the same. We were always the same. The only difference is that Vale caught me first."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time.

"You're right," he said at last, and Dace flinched, because he'd expected a fight and gotten agreement, and agreement was worse. "I hid. Every day I hid, and it cost me things I can't count, and I'm not better than you. But here's the difference, Dace, and it's the only one." He stood, finally, and came closer, close enough that Dace could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes, the burrs in his cloak, the wren's talons dimpling his shoulder through the cloth. "I stopped. Three nights ago a child knocked on my door speaking the old tongue, and I stopped hiding. Whatever happens next — the pyre, the river, the Spire, all of it — I'm done lying. You should try it sometime. You'd be amazed what's still alive under all that ash."

"The pardon—"

"Bury it."

"The mercy—"

"Is a knife with a ribbon on it, and you know it, and some part of you rode ahead of those hounds hoping I'd refuse, because if I refuse, then you don't have to find out what you'd have done if I'd said yes." Tamsin's voice gentled. "Go back to Vale. Tell her you found the ford empty. Buy me a day, Dace. One day. That's all I'm asking, and it's the last thing I'll ever ask you."

Dace looked at him. Six years of gray sash and correct reports and waking up choosing it again, all of it balanced on the point of one sentence: *the ford was empty.*

He could lie. He was good at it now. That was the whole tragedy; he was so very good at it.

But Tamsin had said *done lying*, and the words had gone into Dace like the splinter Vale kept in her glass case, the thorn that moved on its own, working toward the heart.

"If I let you cross," Dace said hoarsely, "she'll know. She always knows. And then it's not a pyre for you, Tam. It's two."

"Then don't let me cross," Tamsin said. "Arrest me. Draw steel. Do your duty, Corrow." He spread his arms slightly, the wren mantling on his shoulder, the boy watching from the cart with those unblinking eyes. "Or step aside. Either way, choose it honestly. For once. Choose it the way I'm choosing this — knowing what it costs."

The mist was burning off. The light came down the river valley in a long slow flood, and it lit Tamsin's face, and Dace thought: *he's already grieved for me. He said goodbye somewhere on the road, and this — this whole conversation — is just the burial.*

That was the unbearable part. Not the accusation. The grief. Being mourned by a living man while you still stood breathing in front of him.

Dace stepped aside.

He stepped aside, and it was the largest movement of his life, and it took less than a pace. Tamsin looked at him for a long moment — and nodded, once, the nod you give at a graveside — and climbed onto the cart, and clicked his tongue, and the horse leaned into the traces, and the wheels went down into the shallows of the ford with a sound like tearing cloth.

Dace stood on the bank and watched him cross. The water broke white around the wheels. The boy in the cart turned and looked back at him, and for one instant Dace felt the child's gaze go through him like cold water finding a crack — felt, absurdly, impossibly, that the boy was *sorry* for him — and then the far bank took them, and the birches closed, and the ford was empty.

* * *

He stood there until the mist finished burning away and the sun lay hard on the water. Then he whistled, once, high and thin, and heard the hounds answer from a mile off, voices braiding together in the cold air.

He had perhaps twenty minutes to decide what kind of liar he was going to be.

He walked up the bank to meet them, and the lead hound — a huge brindled bitch named Verity, Vale's favorite, a beast that could smell a hedge-charm through a foot of river mud — came bounding ahead of the riders and stopped dead at the waterline. Her nose worked the shingle. The cart tracks led away north, plain as scripture, Tamsin's scent all over them, and the other scent beneath it, the green growing smell that made the hounds whine and hackles rise.

Verity looked at the tracks. Then she looked up at Dace, head cocked, ears forward, with an expression he had never seen on a hound's face before and had no business seeing on any face but a person's.

It was disappointment.

"Lost them at the water," Dace said aloud, to the hounds, to the riders coming up through the birches, to the sky. "Crossed and scattered. Could be either bank. We'll need the full pack."

His voice didn't shake. He was, after all, a very good liar.

But as the riders splashed down into the ford and the horns went up and the hunt swung north along the wrong bank, Dace Corrow stood in the shallows a moment longer, and put his hand in his pocket, and touched the thing he'd carried for six years without ever once looking at it: a luck-knot of river grass, frayed now, gone brown, knotted by an eleven-year-old's quick clever fingers.

*Don't tell anyone, Dace. Promise me.*

He'd broken that promise in every way there was. But the knot had held. Little crooked thing, tied by a child who didn't yet know what the world did to gifts — it had held through six years of ash and gray sashes and waking up choosing it again.

Dace closed his fist around it, turned, and rode north after the hunt — riding, as he had ridden for six years, toward the sound of the horns, carrying, for the first time, the knowledge of exactly which side of the river he was really on.
