## 12. Thornwalk

The forest announced itself long before we reached it.

We had walked three days north from the ford where Dace and I had parted as enemies, and for three days the land had been growing strange. Grass came up between the cobbles of the old king's road in green whorls. A fox trotted beside us for an entire afternoon, unafraid, watching Hollowroot with the fixed devotion of a hound watching its master's door. And the birds — the birds had been singing our names. I did not know how I knew that, but the wren confirmed it on the morning of the fourth day, when the treeline rose out of the mist like the wall of some drowned and sunken city.

*They smell him coming,* she said, from her perch on my pack strap. *The Thornwalk has been waiting a hundred years for something worth remembering.*

"It doesn't look like much," I said, because my mouth has always run ahead of my courage.

It looked like much. It looked like the end of the world as Vell understood it. The trees at the forest's edge were blackthorn and elder grown vast beyond reason, their trunks wider than carts, their branches interlaced overhead so thickly that no light fell through so much as seeped, green-gold and slow as honey. The air beneath them was warm despite the season, and it smelled of loam and crushed leaves and something sweeter underneath — the scent of a room you loved as a child and could never find again.

Hollowroot stopped at the treeline and put his small hand flat against the nearest trunk.

The tree *shivered*. I saw it — saw the whole crown of it tremble though there was no wind — and then, slowly, a seam opened in the bark at the height of the boy's chest. Not a wound. An eye. It was the color of deep water, and it regarded us with a patience that made my skin crawl, because it was not the patience of a beast or even of a man. It was the patience of stone. Of soil. Of things that measure time in centuries and consider a human life a passing weather.

*Do not apologize to it,* the wren said sharply, because I had already drawn breath to do exactly that. *The old ones hate apology. It smells of guilt, and guilt is what broke the covenant. Speak your name and your purpose, and nothing else.*

I looked down at Hollowroot. He was still touching the tree, his mud-brown eyes lifted to mine, and he spoke then — one of his rare sentences, in his voice like wind through winter reeds:

"She wants to know if you're afraid."

Not *the tree*. *She.* Of course. Every tree in this forest would be a she, or a he, or something older than either word. I knelt in the wet leaf-mold until my eyes were level with the boy's, and I told the truth, because I had promised myself at the ford that I would never again survive by lying.

"Yes," I said. "But I'm coming anyway."

The eye closed. The seam in the bark widened into a gap, and through it I saw not darkness but a path, winding away under the green-gold light, and I understood that we had been admitted the way a guest is admitted through a door — and that we could just as easily have been refused.

"Charming hospitality," I muttered, and shouldered my pack, and stepped into the Thornwalk.

* * *

Inside, the forest did not behave like a forest.

There were no birds, and yet the silence was full — layered, listening. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, filtering down through a canopy so high I could not see its top. The path was soft underfoot, moss over root over stone, and it bent when it wished; twice I was certain we were walking in a circle, and twice the wren told me to stop counting turns, because *the Thornwalk does not go anywhere, Tamsin Sterling. It goes through you.*

"What does that mean?" I asked.

*You'll see,* she said, and there was something in her voice I had not heard before. Pity, maybe. Or apology — the thing she'd just told me the old ones hated.

Hollowroot walked ahead of me, barefoot on the cold moss, and everything he passed leaned toward him. Ferns unfurled. Dead limbs budded. Once, a fallen trunk across our path simply stood up out of the leaf-litter, shook itself like a dog, and walked away on roots I had taken for dead wood, leaving us a road. The boy glanced back at me after each small miracle, checking, the way a child checks whether the adult has seen the trick too. Each time I nodded. Each time my heart knocked harder against my ribs.

Because I knew what was coming. My mother had told me stories of the Thornwalk when I was small enough to be told such things — before the purges, before the ashfields, before I learned to make my gift small and my smile smaller. *The trees remember names,* she used to say, turning her hands palm-up as if holding something weightless. *And they give them back to you. All of them. Even the ones you buried.*

We had been walking perhaps two hours when the light changed.

It did not dim. It *narrowed* — drew in around us like a hand closing — and the green-gold warmth went out of it, replaced by a gray that I recognized with a sick lurch of the stomach. Torchlight. The gray of torchlight on wet cobblestones, on a crowd's damp wool coats, on a scaffold built in the market square of a town whose name I had not spoken aloud in fifteen years.

"No," I said. To whom, I don't know. "No, we haven't time for this."

*Time is the one thing the Thornwalk has endless amounts of,* the wren said quietly. *It will spend as much of yours as it needs.*

The path opened into a clearing that was not a clearing. It was the square. Ashenfell's market square, rendered in moss and shadow and living wood — the well was a ring of pale stones furred with lichen, the inn's signboard a crooked elder branch hung with ghost-white berries, and the crowd, gods help me, the crowd was there: dozens of figures made of woven twigs and packed earth, standing shoulder to shoulder in the drizzle that was not falling but *remembered*, each twig-face turned toward the center of the square with the terrible blank attention of people who have come to watch something they will later pretend they did not enjoy.

In the center of the square stood a pyre. And on the pyre stood my mother.

She was not made of twigs. She was made of memory, which is worse, because memory gets every detail right. Her gray shawl pinned with the thorn-blossom brooch. Her hair coming loose from its braid the way it always did when she worked. Her chin lifted — that was the detail that undid me, that has always undone me — her chin lifted, and her eyes moving through the crowd, not searching for rescue but *counting faces*, because even at the end she was a warden of the covenant and wardens count what remains.

"Tamsin." The voice came from everywhere. From the trees, from the woven crowd, from inside my own skull. "Tamsin, love, don't watch."

"I have to," I heard myself say, in the voice of a nine-year-old boy, because apparently I was nine years old again, pressed against my father's side in the crowd, my father's hand clamped over my eyes and doing nothing to stop me seeing. "Mam, they've got it wrong, tell them, tell them what you are—"

"They know what I am." Her gaze found me through fifteen years and forty feet of remembered rain, and she smiled, and it was the smile she gave me when I'd skinned a knee badly enough to need more than a kiss. "That's why they're frightened."

The torch came down. I knew every beat of what followed the way you know a song you heard at a funeral — the catch of the kindling, the first black breath of smoke, the way the crowd's woven faces tilted all together like wheat in wind. I knew that she did not scream. I knew that the last thing she did was turn her head, deliberately, away from the crowd and toward the dark line of the northern hills, toward the Spire, toward the covenant she had died rather than name her fellow wardens to break — and I knew that I had watched her do it and done *nothing*, had been nine years old and silent and alive, and that I had spent every day since building a shop and a smile and a small safe life on top of that silence like a house built on a grave.

My legs gave. I sat down hard in the moss, in the middle of a forest that had become a market square, and the smoke of a fire fifteen years cold filled my throat, and I said the thing I had never said aloud, not to the wren, not to Hollowroot, not to the dark of my own locked room at night:

"I could have shouted. I knew the words. She taught me the words that would have called every beast in that town, every rat and crow and cart-horse, and they'd have stampeded the square, they'd have — I was *right there*, and I held still, because Da squeezed my hand, because I was afraid, and she burned while I held still."

The square did not answer. That was the cruelty of it. The Thornwalk was not judging me; it was only showing me myself, the way a still pool shows you your face whether you want to see it or not. My mother on the pyre turned her head, and smiled, and burned, and would burn forever, because the trees remember names and mine was written deepest of all in that square: *the boy who held still.*

"I can't," I said. My voice cracked on it. "Wren. I can't walk past this. Take him on. You know the way now, he doesn't need—"

A small hand closed around mine.

Hollowroot had come to stand beside me without my hearing, the way he did everything, quiet as growing things. His fingers were cool and slightly damp, like a stone from a streambed, and they did not squeeze or comfort or any of the things a human child would have done. They simply held on, the way a root holds on — patient, absolute, with no intention whatsoever of letting go.

He looked up at the burning figure on the pyre. Then at me. Then he spoke, and his voice was very soft, and it was not entirely his own — there was a depth in it, an echo, the Spire speaking through its seed the way wind speaks through a reed.

"The tree says: you were nine."

"I was nine," I agreed, and my eyes were streaming, and I didn't care anymore who saw.

"The tree says: it also remembers what you did after." He tilted his head, listening to something far below speech. "Every charm you sold with a true blessing hidden in it. Every mended bird. Three years of small kindnesses, smuggled past the law like coins sewn into a coat. The tree says—" and here the boy frowned, struggling with the translation, "—the tree says a seed does not blame itself for being underground."

The pyre was going out.

Not quickly — memory does not release its grip quickly — but flame by flame, the way dusk lets go of a field. My mother's figure softened, blurred, became shadow, became suggestion, became at the last a single gray shawl's shape woven out of drifting mist. Before it went entirely, she turned her head once more, and this time there was no crowd, no rain, no rope-smoke. There was only the clearing, and the green-gold light returning like a tide, and her voice — hers alone now, or the Thornwalk's best imitation of a dead woman's love:

"You held still, my darling. Then you learned to speak. Both things are true. Bring both to the Spire."

Then it was moss and shadow and enormous patient trees, and I was kneeling in the leaf-mold of a forest older than kingdoms, holding the hand of a boy who was not a boy, crying the tears I had rationed out over fifteen years all at once, and finding — this was the miracle, this was the thing I would carry the rest of my life — finding that on the far side of them there was *room*. Space in my chest where the grief had been packed solid as clay. Air where there had only ever been smoke.

*Now you can go on,* the wren said, landing on my knee. Her voice was gentler than I had ever heard it. *That is what the Thornwalk wanted. Not your suffering. Your carrying-capacity. It cannot let you deeper while you drag that behind you.*

"And if I'd turned back?" I asked. My voice was wrecked. I found I didn't mind.

*Then the path would have led you out,* she said simply, *and the forest would have grieved, and the covenant would have died with your mother's name still waiting to be honored. The test was never pass or fail, Tamsin. It was only: will you look?*

I got up. My knees complained. Hollowroot kept hold of my hand, and I let him, and we walked on.

* * *

The Thornwalk changed after that. Or I changed, and so it seemed to. The path grew steeper, climbing through trees so vast that their roots formed walls on either side, and the light thickened from gold to a deep emerald twilight. The air grew warmer, and sweeter, and older. I began to hear things at the edge of hearing — a slow pulse, vast and regular, like a heartbeat heard through miles of earth. Hollowroot's steps quickened. He was pulling me now rather than being led, his small face lit from within, and I understood that the seed knew the way home the way water knows downhill.

Once, near dusk-that-was-not-dusk, we passed a tree unlike the others. It was silver-barked and leafless, and set into its trunk, at the height of a man's heart, was a name. Grown into the wood, raised like a scar in reverse — letters of living grain, old script, the covenant-tongue. Below it were others, dozens, hundreds, names upon names climbing the trunk like ivy, and I understood with a chill and a lift of the heart together that these were the wardens. Every speaker bound to the covenant since its making, written into the forest's memory. Somewhere on that silver column, in letters I could not yet read, was my mother's name. And somewhere below it, waiting, mine.

Hollowroot touched the lowest name gently, the way you touch a sleeping person's shoulder, and walked on.

We camped that night in the hollow of a root-vault the size of a chapel, and the Thornwalk fed us — berries that tasted of honey and bread both, and clear water rising sweetly from a spring that appeared where Hollowroot sat. The wren preened on my boot and pretended not to be pleased when I shared. Above us, through the root-lattice, the first stars showed as green points through the canopy, and the great slow heartbeat of the forest pulsed on beneath everything, patient as forgiveness.

I was nearly asleep when Hollowroot spoke.

"He's coming too."

I opened my eyes. The boy was sitting upright in the dark, looking north, through the trees, through the hills, through everything, and his voice had that doubled quality again — the reed and the wind both.

"Who's coming?"

"The friend who hurt you." He said it without malice, the way he said everything, a fact reported by the roots. "The one who sold the names. He is following. He is sorry and he is not sorry and he is afraid, and the trees can taste him on the wind like blood in water." The boy's head turned, and his eyes caught the starlight and held it, green as the canopy. "He will reach the gate before we do."

I lay back down and stared up through the lattice of roots at the green stars, and felt the old wound in me — the Dace-shaped wound, the testimony-shaped wound — shift and ache, newly cleaned, newly open, thanks to a forest that did not believe in letting things stay buried.

"Let him come," I said at last, and was surprised to find I half meant it.

The heartbeat beneath us pulsed on. And far to the north, faint as a struck bell, something answered it — a second pulse, faster, brighter, calling its seed home through the dark.
