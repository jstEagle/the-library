## 13. The Herd That Waits

They came at moonrise, and the forest went quiet to receive them.

Tamsin felt it first in his feet — a tremor through the root-woven ground of the Thornwalk, like the pulse of something vast turning over in its sleep. Then the wren lifted from his shoulder with a cry that was half warning, half reverence, and every bird in the canopy rose at once, a black net of wings against the stars, before settling into a silence deeper than any he had ever heard.

"They're coming," the wren said. Her voice was thin. "The Old Herd. The ones who remember."

"Remember what?"

"Everything." She ruffled her feathers, and for once she did not sound ancient or knowing. She sounded small. "Boy — whatever they ask you, do not lie. They will taste a lie before it leaves your teeth. And do not beg. Begging is worse than lying to them. Begging says you believe words are all you have."

"And if they are?"

Hollowroot's small hand found Tamsin's. The boy had not spoken since dusk, but now he said, in that voice like wind through hollow reeds: "They are not."

* * *

The stags came out of the dark between the trees the way fog comes off a river — without sound, without hurry, as though the night had simply decided to grow antlers.

There were nine of them. No — Tamsin counted again, and lost count, because their shadows did not match their bodies; where one stood, three shadows branched away, and each shadow moved independently, grazing on light. Their coats were the grey of old bark, streaked with lichen-green, and their antlers were not bone but living wood, hung with moss and small white flowers that opened as Tamsin watched and closed when he looked away. Their eyes were amber and old and utterly without mercy.

The largest stood at the center. His antlers spread wider than Tamsin was tall, and in their branches nested things Tamsin could not name — a slow-turning constellation of moths, a single drop of water that never fell, a curl of mist shaped, briefly, like a burning woman.

Tamsin's chest seized. He knew that shape. He had seen it every night for three years behind his closed eyes.

*Do not look,* the wren murmured. *They show you your own grief first. It is how they weigh you.*

The great stag stepped forward, and the ground rang beneath his hoof like a struck bell.

"You are the speaker." His voice was not sound so much as pressure — Tamsin felt it in his ribs, in his back teeth, in the fillings of old scars. It was the voice of a tree falling in a forest no one heard, finally heard. "The last of the Sterling line. The line that swore. The line that broke."

"I am Tamsin Sterling."

"We know your name. We knew it before your mother bore you. We spoke it over her cradle, as we spoke it over hers." The stag's breath washed over him — cold and green-smelling, loam and rain and something underneath like smoke. "And then your kind put her to the torch, and did not speak it again."

Behind him, Hollowroot made a small sound. Not fear. Grief. The boy's hand tightened on Tamsin's until the bones ached.

* * *

They arranged themselves in a circle around a clearing Tamsin had not noticed before — or perhaps the clearing had not existed until they needed it. Moonlight pooled in its center like standing water. The trees at its edge leaned inward, and Tamsin understood, with a crawling certainty, that they were audience. That the whole Thornwalk had come to watch.

"The covenant is dying," the great stag said. "You know this. You carry its last seed in a child's skin, toward a heart that may already be too cold to wake it. We have watched you walk. We have smelled the smoke of the burners on your coat. We have heard the hounds behind you, and we have done nothing, because we wished to see what you would do when the road grew hard."

"It has grown hard," Tamsin said.

"It has grown hard," the stag agreed, and there was something almost like approval in the pressure of his voice. "So now we ask the question we asked your grandmother's grandmother, when she came to us with oaths in her mouth and iron in her heart. Why should the seed live?"

The silence that followed was total. Even the moths in the stag's antlers stopped turning.

"Why should it live," the stag repeated, "when the people who made the promise burned it? When every warden is dead or caged or hiding behind a shopkeeper's smile? When the last speaker spent three years selling luck-knots to the grandchildren of the men who built the pyres?" His head lowered, antlers leveling like a drawn bow. "We say: let it die with dignity. Let the seed return to us, to the deep roots, and sleep out the age of men. In a thousand years, when your cities are moss, perhaps something will grow again. But if you carry him to the Spire — if you wake the old magic while the burners still hold torches — there will be war such as neither realm has seen. Feral magic, unbound, against fire and iron. We will be caught between. Our young will die in it. We have buried enough of our young in your wars."

Tamsin's mouth was dry. He was aware of Hollowroot beside him, small and still, watching the stag with those too-old eyes. He was aware of the wren on his shoulder, trembling.

Every answer he had prepared — the speeches, the appeals, the history of his family's guilt — turned to ash in his throat. Because the stag was right. All of it was right. His family had broken the covenant. His city burned witches. He himself had hidden for three years while wardens were dragged screaming to the squares.

He could apologize. He wanted to apologize. The apology rose in him like bile, hot and easy and worthless.

*Do not beg,* the wren had said. *Begging says you believe words are all you have.*

Tamsin closed his mouth. And instead of speaking, he did the thing that had kept him alive in Vell for three years, the thing no one in that city had ever once done to him:

He listened.

Not the way men listen — waiting for the pause, loading their reply. He listened the way he listened to rats in walls and horses in rain and the caged wren whose warnings he'd pretended not to hear. He let the stag's grief pour into him and did not flinch from it. He let the circle's anger settle on his shoulders like snow, and he stood under its weight, and he did not shrug it off, and he did not drown in it either.

The clearing waited. A minute. Five. The moonlight in its center shivered.

At last the great stag tilted his head, and one ear swiveled forward, and something flickered in the amber depths of his eye — surprise, maybe, or its older cousin.

"You do not answer."

"Not yet," Tamsin said. "You haven't finished."

* * *

They finished. That was the thing about the Old Herd — they had been owed a hearing for two hundred years, and by the gods, they took it.

A hind with a broken tine spoke of the Ashfields, which had been birch-wood when her grandmother grazed there, and of the year the burners came with salt and lime so that nothing would grow again. A young stag, his antlers barely budded, spoke of his sister, taken by a snare set in the Thornwalk's edge — not for meat, the burners hadn't eaten her, but for her hooves and her hide and the alchemical virtues of her marrow. An ancient doe, grey as ash herself, spoke in a voice so quiet Tamsin had to lean forward to catch it, and what she spoke of was the Spire itself: how it had once sung, day and night, a song that held the feral magic in its cradle, and how the song had grown fainter every year since the last warden died, and how lately it had begun to skip, like a heart missing beats.

"When it stops," she said, "everything the covenant bound comes loose at once. Not slowly. All together. Like a dam breaking, like a hive breaking, like—" and here her gentle old face did something terrible, "—like a cage full of starving things opening."

Tamsin listened to all of it. Hours of it. The moon crossed the clearing and began to descend. Hollowroot fell asleep sitting up, twice, and twice woke himself with a start and gripped Tamsin's sleeve, refusing to lie down, refusing to miss anything. The wren tucked her head under her wing but Tamsin knew she wasn't sleeping either.

And somewhere in the middle hours, Tamsin understood what was actually happening. This was not a trial. Trials ended in verdicts. This was a grief that had never been allowed to speak, speaking at last — and the test was not whether he could defend humanity. The test was whether he could bear witness. Whether he would stand in this circle and hold all of it, the whole weight of what his kind had done, without collapsing into excuse or fleeing into shame.

His legs ached. His eyes burned. He stayed.

When the last of them had spoken — the great stag again, closing the circle as he had opened it — the silence returned, and this time it had a different texture. Not hostile. Waiting.

"Now," the stag said. "Answer."

* * *

Tamsin knelt. Not to grovel — he was careful of that, careful to keep his spine straight, his head level — but so that his eyes were level with the great stag's own. So that they met as near-equals meet, one kneeling by choice rather than force.

"You asked why the seed should live," he said. "I'm not going to answer that. I'm going to answer what you actually asked, which is whether I heard you."

The stag's ears came forward.

"I heard you. The Ashfields. The snare. Your sister." He looked at the young stag as he said it, and held the look, and did not look away from the animal pain in those amber eyes. "I heard the song skipping. I've heard it myself — the boy dreams it, and when he dreams it, the birds near us go silent. I know the dam is breaking. I'm not carrying him to the Spire despite that. I'm carrying him to the Spire because of it."

"He will wake the old magic," the hind with the broken tine said. "Waking is war."

"Waking is risk," Tamsin said. "Letting it die is certain ruin. Those aren't the same thing, and I think you know they aren't, or you wouldn't have waited three days to judge me. You'd have judged me the moment I crossed your border." He drew a breath. "But here is what I will not do. I will not stand here and apologize for my family as though an apology settles anything. Apologies are currency among men, and among men they buy forgiveness cheap. You don't want my sorrow. Sorrow doesn't regrow the Ashfields. Sorrow doesn't unsnare your sister."

"Then what do you offer?"

"Proof," Tamsin said. "That's all I have. Proof that listening isn't dead in my blood." He raised his voice slightly, letting it carry to the ring of leaning trees. "For three years I sat in a shop in Vell and heard everything — the rats in the granaries telling each other where the grain rotted, the carthorses describing their drivers' cruelty, the gulls fighting over the harbor filth — and I said nothing, because saying it would have killed me. I told myself survival was enough. It wasn't. It was just a slower way of being part of the breaking." His voice roughened. "Your covenant didn't die the day the last warden burned. It died a little every time a speaker heard and stayed silent. I stayed silent for three years. I won't stay silent again. Whatever happens at the Spire — whatever it costs — I will hear, and I will speak, and I will stand between the realms if standing is what's needed. Not because I inherited the right. Because I'm asking to earn it. Now. Tonight. Starting here."

Silence.

Then the great stag did something that stopped Tamsin's heart. He lowered his enormous head, and the living antlers — moss, moths, the drop of unfalling water, the curl of mist shaped like a burning woman — came to rest gently above Tamsin's bowed shoulders. Not touching. Canopying. The way a father stands over a sleeping child.

"You knelt," the stag said, "and you did not lower your eyes. You confessed your silence, and you did not dress it as virtue. You offered proof, and the only proof you had was yourself." A long breath, green and cold. "The last warden who stood in this circle — your grandmother's mother — came with a speech her elders had written for her. It was beautiful. It was perfect. We ate her words and gave her nothing, because she had memorized them and none of them were hers." The amber eyes glittered. "Yours were yours. Every stumbling one."

The stag stepped back. The circle stirred.

"But words are still only words, speaker. We are the Herd That Waits. Waiting is what we do. So we give you this: passage through the deep Thornwalk, the paths the burners cannot find. And we give you the night. If by dawn you can tell us what the seed needs — not what your books say, not what the wren says, but what *he* needs, having truly heard him — we will call you speaker indeed, and the herd will walk you to the Spire's edge itself."

* * *

They left him alone in the clearing with the sleeping boy and the descending moon.

Tamsin sat with Hollowroot's head in his lap, the child's hair cool as river stones, and did not pretend he knew the answer. The wren hopped down onto the boy's chest and regarded Tamsin with one bright eye.

"Well," she said. "You didn't disgrace the bloodline. High praise, from me."

"That wasn't a compliment."

"It was the closest thing I have." She preened once, briskly. "You should sleep."

"I need to listen to him first. Really listen. Before dawn."

"He's asleep."

"He's dreaming," Tamsin said quietly. "That's better."

He laid his palm flat on the boy's sternum, closed his eyes, and opened the door in his mind he had kept barred for three years — the door behind which lived every voice he'd ever smothered. And out of the sleeping seed of the Spire, faint and far and growing clearer with every heartbeat, came the sound of a song skipping.

Missing beats. Running down.

And beneath the song, so soft Tamsin might have imagined it, another sound: a child's voice, dreaming, saying the same word over and over in the old tongue.

The wren stiffened. "*What is he saying?*"

Tamsin's eyes opened. His blood had gone cold.

"He isn't asking for soil," he said. "He isn't asking for the Spire at all." He looked north, through the leaning trees, toward the distant dark where the Inquisitor General's fires would be burning the Thornwalk's edge even now. "He's asking for *her*. He keeps saying her name. Vale. He's dreaming about Maren Vale."
