## 14. Halfway to the Heart

The Thornwalk did not burn the way other forests burned.

Inquisitor General Maren Vale stood at the column's head on the first morning of the firing and watched the smoke rise wrong — green-tinged at its heart, curling in spirals no wind had asked for, as though the trees were exhaling rather than dying. The soldiers behind her murmured. She let them. Fear was a kind of discipline, and discipline was the only scripture she trusted.

"Torches forward," she said. "Sap lines. We burn a corridor a hundred paces wide, no more. I want the beast-speaker flushed north, not cooked."

Her lieutenant, a broad man named Osk with ash already in his beard, hesitated. "General — the trees. They're not catching proper."

"They're catching," Vale said. "Look closer."

He looked. And she watched him see it: the way the flame moved through the undergrowth like something wading, deliberate, tasting each root before it took it. The way some trees blackened instantly while their neighbors stood untouched, green and smug, as if the fire had been given a list of names.

Vale had burned suspected practitioners out of three cities and eleven villages. Fire was honest. Fire did not distinguish; that was its virtue and its lesson. But this fire distinguished, and every distinction it made reminded her that the forest was not merely fuel. It was a party to what happened here. Possibly a judge.

She did not believe in judges. She believed in law, and law was made by people, and people could be made to kneel.

At her saddle hung the glass case, wrapped in wool against the jostle of the ride. Inside it, the thorn splinter lay on its velvet bed, and all morning it had been turning. Slowly. Patiently. Pointing north, toward the Spire, like a needle that had finally found its pole.

"Hold," Vale said suddenly, and raised her hand.

The column stopped. Ahead, at the scorched edge where the burning met the living wood, something stood in the smoke. A deer — no. Too tall for a deer, too still for anything alive. It regarded the flames without fear, regarded the soldiers without hatred, and then it simply walked backward into the unburned dark and was gone, taking the light with it.

Osk crossed himself with two fingers, an old gesture from a country none of them came from anymore.

"It's watching us," he said.

"Everything here is watching us." Vale turned her horse along the fire line. "That is how you know we are winning. The guilty always watch."

She said it clearly, so the men would hear it and carry it. She said it because saying it made it true, or true enough, and true enough had carried her twenty years. But that night, when the camp fires were lit inside the ring of ash and the sentries doubled, Vale unwrapped the glass case and held it up to the light of her own small flame.

The splinter had stopped pointing north.

It pointed at her.

* * *

Dace Corrow had set fires before. He told himself that on the second day, and the third, and by the fourth he had stopped telling himself anything at all, because the telling wasn't working.

They burned the Thornwalk's edge in shifts, and Dace walked his shift with a torch he kept forgetting to lower, and the forest made him remember things he had spent years burying under duty the way you bury seeds and hope they never come up.

He remembered being nine, and Tamsin Sterling showing him a beetle in the churchyard grass, and Tamsin bending close and listening, actually listening, and then laughing and saying, *he says the bell scares him, he says it rings in his feet.* Dace had laughed too. He had thought it was a game. He had been nine, and it had been a game, right up until the night it became testimony.

Now the trees were dying around him and they died loudly. That was the worst of it. He had expected silence — forests burned silent in his imagination, dignified, like executed men. But the Thornwalk screamed. Not with animal voices; the animals had fled days ago, streaming north past the column in rivers of fur and feather that even Osk refused to shoot at. The trees themselves screamed, a creaking groan that rose through the roots and out through the crowns, and sometimes, when the wind came off the fire, it arranged itself into something almost like words.

*Names,* one of the local guides had said, before deserting in the night. *The old ones say these trees keep names. Every soul the forest ever knew.*

Dace lowered his torch and stood in the smoke and let a young oak die beside him, and he thought: *Tamsin Sterling is in there somewhere. Tamsin, who listened to beetles. Tamsin, whose mother burned while I signed my name.* And the thought did not feel like duty. It felt like the tree sounded.

"Corrow." Osk's hand landed heavy on his shoulder. "Your torch's going out."

Dace looked down. The flame had shrunk to a blue nub, starved. Around him, in a rough circle, the fires of his whole shift had guttered low, while the rest of the line roared on. As if the ground beneath his boots had drunk them.

"Bad pitch," he said.

"Aye." Osk looked at the circle a moment longer than he needed to. "Bad pitch," he agreed, and moved on, and Dace understood that Osk had seen it too, and chosen, for reasons of his own, to call it nothing.

That was the thing about complicity, Dace thought, relighting his torch with hands that would not quite steady. It was never one great sin. It was a thousand small agreements to call things nothing.

* * *

Brannoc found his daughter on the fifth day, inside the fire line, alive.

He had told himself he wouldn't go in. He had sold the travelers' road to the Inquisitor General for a promise — *your daughter may yet live, toll-keeper, and I will find her* — and the promise had curdled in him ever since, sitting in his stomach like river stones. When Vale's column marched past his bridge, Brannoc shouldered his axe and followed the smoke instead of the soldiers, telling himself it was curiosity, telling himself anything at all.

The burned edge of the Thornwalk was not dead. That was the first wrongness. Ash lay over everything, thick as snowfall, but up through the ash came shoots — green, urgent, weeks' worth of growth in five days — and among the shoots moved small creatures he had no names for: pale moths the size of his palm, beetles armored in what looked like living leaf. The fire had passed through here like a flood through a valley, and the valley was already forgetting it.

He found Sefia sitting in the hollow of a half-burned oak, cross-legged, calm, her hair grown long and silvered at the tips as though frost lived in it now.

"Papa," she said, and her voice was her voice, but underneath it ran another sound, a rustle, as if leaves spoke just behind her teeth.

Brannoc dropped his axe. He crossed the ash on his knees and took her face in his big cracked hands and she let him, smiling, patient, the way you are patient with someone much younger than you.

"They said the old magic took you," he managed. "Six years ago. Off the bridge. I watched the water—"

"The water gave me to the thorns." She said it simply, without blame, which was somehow worse than blame. "They kept me. They taught me. Papa, the trees have your name. All of them. They've known it since before the bridge had a bridge. They say you used to leave bread on the far post in winter, for the foxes, and the foxes told the crows, and the crows told the oaks, and the oaks wrote it down."

"I—" His throat closed. Six years of grief, of the empty bed, of the bread he'd stopped leaving because leaving it hurt too much. "You remember that?"

"I remember everything they remember." She took his hand, and her fingers were cool, and faintly ridged, like bark gone soft. "Papa. You have to stop them. The soldiers. The woman with the case. The forest isn't fighting back yet, but it will, and when it does it won't know our names from theirs. It only knows that men with fire came."

"They promised me you were alive," Brannoc whispered. "I sold two travelers' lives for that promise."

Sefia looked at him for a long moment, and in her eyes he saw both his daughter and something older looking out, weighing him, the way he imagined kings weighed coins of doubtful metal.

"Then spend the rest of the payment well," she said.

* * *

On the ridge above the burning, where the ash ended and the living green began, Tamsin Sterling knelt in the bracken with Hollowroot's small hand in his and watched the smoke climb into a sky gone the color of dirty wool.

"She's burning the edge," he said. "A corridor. She wants to funnel us north." He heard his own voice and was surprised by how level it was. Three years of shopkeeper's calm, and it finally had a use. "She's herding us, Wren. Straight up the Spire's throat."

*Yes,* said the wren from the alder above them, her voice thin with exhaustion. *And it is working, which is the humiliating part.*

Below them, the fire line glowed like a wound across the world's skin. Tamsin could feel it — that was the thing no one warned you about, once you stopped pretending. He felt the trees dying the way you feel a tooth ache, distantly, in a part of the body you don't control. And threaded through the pain, fainter, something else: the beasts fleeing north, hundreds of them, carrying fear like a contagion, and carrying something else too. A direction. A pull. All of them, wolf and wren and stag, drawn toward the Spire the way water is drawn downhill.

Hollowroot tugged his hand.

"They're sorry," the boy said. His Old Tongue had begun, in the last days, to braid itself with common speech, so that he spoke both at once and Tamsin understood both, which felt like standing in a doorway between rooms. "The trees. They're sorry they can't fight."

"Tell them not to be sorry," Tamsin said. "Tell them to grow. Everything they can. Behind us, ahead of us. Roots deep, trunks thick. If she wants a corridor, let the corridor close behind her."

Hollowroot closed his eyes. Far below, along miles of smoking edge, the green shoots coming up through the ash shivered, straightened, and began — slowly, steadily, horribly — to grow.

The wren ruffled her feathers. *You've just declared war, boy.*

"No," Tamsin said quietly, watching the smoke, watching the road north where a column of soldiers crawled like ants along a burning seam. "She declared it. I'm just done pretending I didn't hear."

He gathered Hollowroot up and turned north, toward the Spire, toward the heart, with the fire at his back and the whole waking wild pulling him forward — and behind him, unseen, at the edge of the ash, a toll-keeper and his changed daughter stood watching them go, and Brannoc raised one hand, not in greeting, but in warning:

because down the soldiers' road, moving fast, came riders, and at their head rode Dace Corrow, and Dace was looking directly at the ridge.
