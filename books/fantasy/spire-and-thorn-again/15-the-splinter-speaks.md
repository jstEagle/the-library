## 15. The Splinter Speaks

The glass case sat on Maren Vale's campaign desk like a small cathedral built for one prisoner.

She had commissioned it herself, three years ago, from a glazier in Vell who asked no questions and was paid too well to remember the order. Black iron frame, seven panes of true-clear glass, sealed with wax blessed by three different clerics because one blessing had never seemed sufficient. Inside, resting on a bed of grey ash, lay a splinter of thorn no longer than her smallest finger.

It had been taken from the hand of a hedge-witch in the Ashfields, pried from flesh that had grown around it like bark around a nail. The witch had screamed when they took it — not in pain, Vale had noted at the time, but in something closer to grief — and had gone quiet ever after, sitting in her cell with her open palm turned upward as if waiting for rain.

Vale had kept the splinter because it moved.

Not often. Not obviously. Once or twice a month, when the lamplight fell across the case at a certain angle, the thing would turn on its bed of ash — slowly, deliberately, the way a sleeper turns toward a window — until its sharpest point faced the glass. Faced her. She had measured the movement once, marking its position in ink on the case's base, and found that over six hours it had rotated eleven degrees. Eleven degrees, toward her, in a sealed box, with no wind, no hand, no reason.

Magic was outlawed because magic was lawless. That was the first principle of the Honest Law, the axiom upon which every purge, every pyre, every list of names had been built: the old power answered to nothing, honored nothing, took what it pleased and gave back rot. Her whole life was an argument for that axiom. She had built it stone by stone, execution by execution, into a fortress no doubt could breach.

And every month or so, in a box on her desk, a dead witch's thorn turned to look at her.

Tonight it had spoken.

* * *

The column had made camp at the edge of the Thornwalk proper, where the road north narrowed between fields gone strangely green. That green troubled her more than she admitted. The Ashfields should have been ash — the name was earned, the soil poisoned by the purge-fires of two generations past, good for nothing but sorrel and crows. But since Vell, since the charm-seller's shop burned and the fish cart slipped through the west gate, the land along their route had been blooming out of season. Wheat stood ripe in fields sown weeks ago. Hedgerows flowered white. Her quartermaster reported wells running sweeter, horses fattening on grass that ought to be sparse, and her soldiers — she had heard them, laughing around fires they were too superstitious to notice themselves huddling close to — said the country felt *awake*.

The country felt awake. As if it had been sleeping for three hundred years and something small and running had begun to wake it.

Hollowroot. The boy-shaped thing Sterling was carrying north like a coal cupped in bare hands.

Vale sat alone in her command tent with the map spread before her, the lamp trimmed low, and the glass case at her elbow. Two days' hard ride to the Spire's outer gate, less if she pushed. Sterling would be slower — a man on foot with a child, avoiding roads, trusting beasts and hedgerows and whatever other outlawry the wild still offered him. She had time. She permitted herself one hour of stillness per night, a discipline older than her rank, and she spent it now as she always did: with the case, and with the letter she never sent.

The letter lived in the case's iron lid, folded into a pocket sewn beneath the padding. It was written in her own hand, in ink gone brown with age, addressed to a girl who had been dead for twenty-six years.

*Sera. If you can read this somehow, wherever you are — the apples came in well this year. I have kept the orchard. I have kept everything.*

Her sister had been nine years old. Vale had been thirteen. They had been in the orchard behind their father's house in the hill-country south of Vell, on an evening in late summer so golden that even now she could not think of it without her chest tightening, and Sera had gone into the deep grass beyond the wall to chase a fox she swore had spoken to her — *spoken, Mare, it said my name* — and the old magic had taken her. Not killed her. Taken her. What came back out of the grass an hour later wore Sera's face and Sera's dress and did not know their mother's lullaby, and it smiled wrong, and it spoke in a voice like wind through a bone flute, and their father had put it down with a hay-knife while Vale held the lantern and watched her sister die twice in one night.

That was the truth under the Honest Law, under all her honest laws: not philosophy. An orchard. A lantern. A hay-knife. A fox that spoke a child's name in the deep grass.

The purge had come to the hill-country within the year, and Vale had given testimony at fourteen that helped send three practitioners to the pyre, and she had watched them burn and felt — for the first and only time in her life — that the world had been set right. The feeling lasted four days. Then it faded, the way all feelings fade, and she discovered that purpose could be rebuilt each morning like a fire laid fresh, and she had been laying it fresh for thirty years.

She reached for the case to perform the nightly ritual — check the seal, note the splinter's position, add a line to the log she kept in a ledger no one else was permitted to open —

— and the splinter was standing upright in its bed of ash.

It had never done that. In three years it had turned, tilted, trembled; it had never *risen*. It stood now like a blade planted point-down, quivering faintly, and the lamp flame bent toward it though there was no draft, and the tent's canvas walls pressed inward as if the night outside had leaned against them to listen.

Vale did not step back. She had not stepped back from anything in thirty years, and she would not begin with a stick in a box.

"Captain," she said, to the empty air, testing whether her voice still worked. It did. She did not call for him. Some things must be witnessed alone or not at all.

The splinter shivered. And then, in a voice that came from nowhere inside the sealed glass — a voice thin as thread and sweet as rot, a voice that slid under the door of her ears the way water slides under a fence — it said:

*"Mare."*

Only that. Only the name no living person used. Her father had died calling it. Her mother had died whispering it. No soldier, no clerk, no cleric in three decades had dared shorten Maren to anything, and certainly not to the soft childish syllable reserved for an orchard and a lantern and a girl chasing a fox.

The lamp flame steadied. The canvas relaxed. The night went back to being merely night.

Vale stood very still for the length of ten heartbeats, and then she did the thing her discipline demanded and her hands resisted: she sat down, opened the ledger, and wrote, in her precise clerk's hand: *Subject vocalized. One word. Analysis pending.* Her pen left a small blot on the word *pending*, the first blot in eleven volumes of records, and she looked at it for a long moment before turning the page.

*Analysis pending.* As if she could analyze her way out of hearing her sister say her name.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself it was strategy — the push to the Spire required early departure, the men needed organizing, the scouts' reports needed reading — and she performed all of it, efficiently, by lamplight, while the glass case sat at the edge of her vision like a lit window in a dark house.

At some point past midnight, Captain Ossler ducked through the tent flap with the evening's reports and stopped short, because his General was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

"News from the rear, General. The toll-keeper at Marrow Bridge confirmed the route. Sterling crossed with the boy two nights ago, heading north along the river road." He hesitated. "There's... something else. The bridge keeper's daughter. The one taken by the wilds, years back. Our outriders found her."

Vale set down the knife. "Dead?"

"No, General. Alive. Inside the fire line we cut yesterday, sitting under a hawthorn that shouldn't be standing. Unharmed. Changed, they say — eyes like a deer's, wouldn't speak at first. Then she told the corporal—" Ossler's throat worked. "She told him she'd been happy. All these years. Said the Spire folk kept her like a daughter, and she'd been *waiting* for us to come."

The knife lay on the map, its edge catching lamplight. Vale looked at it and thought: *a lie. Of course it is a lie. That is what they do; that is what they did to Sera; they wear our loved ones and speak in our loved ones' voices and the only mercy is the blade.* She thought it firmly, the way one presses a thumb into a wound to feel the clean pain of it rather than the festering doubt.

But underneath the firmness, something had shifted its weight. Something that remembered a fox saying a child's name in deep grass, and wondered — for the first time in twenty-six years, in the privacy of her own skull where no Honest Law could reach — *what if the fox was only a fox, and the thing in the grass was something else entirely, something we never stayed long enough to understand?*

"Double the pickets," she said. "We ride at first light. Full speed to the gate."

"Yes, General." Ossler paused at the flap. "And the... evidence, General? The men are asking. There's talk. The green coming up along the road, the wolves that watch us and don't hunt — they're saying the country itself has taken sides."

"The country," Vale said, "has no sides. It has appetites. We are the law that stands between it and everything it wants to eat." She heard how the words rang, hollow as a struck pot, and hated him a little for making her say them aloud. "Dismissed."

He went. The lamp burned. And in the silence after, the splinter spoke again.

* * *

This time she was looking directly at the case when it happened, and still she could not see the mechanism of it — the voice simply arrived, inside her head rather than her ears, the way a memory arrives, the way grief arrives.

*"You kept me,"* it said, in Sera's voice, in the exact bright pitch of a nine-year-old girl who had once sung off-key on purpose to make her stern elder sister laugh. *"All these years, in your little glass church. You kept me like I kept you."*

"You are not my sister," Vale said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "My sister is dead. Whatever you are, you are wearing her the way a crow wears a stolen ring."

*"Am I?"* The splinter swayed in its ash, gentle as a reed. *"Then why do you write to her? Why is there a letter in the lid of my prison, Mare, in your hand, that begins 'if you can read this somehow'? You wrote to a dead girl for twenty-six years. Doesn't that make you the one who believes I might answer?"*

The tent was very cold. Or she was. She became aware that she had risen from her chair without deciding to, that she was standing over the case with her fists at her sides, and that her breath was fogging in air that had no business being cold in high summer.

"State your purpose," she said. "If you have one beyond torment, state it. I have burned better voices than yours."

A pause. When the voice came again it had aged — still Sera, but Sera grown, Sera as she might have sounded at forty, at fifty, at the age Vale herself was now, all the birthdays the grave had stolen stacked up in a single sentence. It was the cruelest thing the splinter had done yet, and it knew it, and it did it anyway.

*"I could give her back."*

Vale's knees went loose. She gripped the desk's edge.

*"Not a copy. Not a crow in a stolen ring. The covenant between the Spire and your kind was a covenant of keeping, Mare — of return. Everything the wild takes, the wild keeps safe. Your sister is not dust. She is held, the way seed is held in winter ground. Let the pact die — let Hollowroot fail, let the old magic run free as it did before your grandmothers chained it — and the keeping ends, and everything comes home. Every taken child. Every lost harvest. Every soul your purges sent screaming into the dark. The Spire opens its hand and gives back all of it."* The voice softened, went intimate, went into the orchard itself, into the golden evening. *"Apples came in well this year, you wrote. I could tell you which tree she liked best. I could stand beside you under it. I could sing the lullaby, Mare. I remember it. I've kept it safe all this time, the way we keep everything."*

The silence afterward had weight and texture, like wet wool.

Vale found she was crying. She had not cried since her father's funeral, and even then only once, briefly, angrily, and here she was at fifty with tears running silently down her face in her own command tent, and she could not stop them, and worse — she could not find the argument against them. Thirty years of fortress, and the enemy had not breached the walls. It had walked up to the gate in her sister's voice and the gate had opened from inside.

"That is a bargain," she said at last, and her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "Everything given freely is a bargain. And bargains with the wild are how children end up in the deep grass."

*"Yes,"* the splinter agreed, gently, horribly. *"Sometimes. And sometimes the thing in the deep grass is lonely, and old, and dying, and only wanted to hold a warm small life before the dark closed in. You never stayed to learn which. None of you ever stayed. You burned the grass instead."* A pause, and then, quieter, almost tender: *"I am not asking you to trust me, Mare. I am asking you to look. You have spent your whole life refusing to look. Look once. Come to the Spire. See what your law has been burning. And if you still choose the fire afterward — then burn, little warden. Burn us all. At least you will have chosen with your eyes open, and I will not hate you, because I will be free either way."*

The voice withdrew like a tide. The splinter sank slowly back into its bed of ash and lay still, spent, its work done — and Vale understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that it had been saving its strength for three years. Turning toward her, degree by degree, month by month. Waiting for the night she would be alone with a letter and a lamp and a road leading exactly where it wanted her to go.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She sat. She opened the ledger and wrote: *Subject silent. Position unchanged.* Then, after a moment, in smaller letters, in the margin, where no auditor would ever look:

*It knows about the letter.*

* * *

Dawn came up grey-gold over a countryside too green for the season, and Maren Vale mounted her black mare before the camp was half-stirred, and gave the order that made Captain Ossler blink.

"We ride ahead of the column," she said. "You, me, and six picked men. The rest follow at best speed. We'll reach the gate tonight."

"Tonight, General? The men are tired, the horses—"

"The horses are fat," she said, "on grass that should be barren. The men are rested in camps the land has made soft. Everything on this road is being *helped*, Captain, and I want to know by whom, and I want to know before it finishes helping." She gathered the reins. Beneath her arm, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to the saddle like a relic — because she could not leave it behind, because leaving it behind had become unthinkable somewhere in the night without her noticing — rode the glass case, and the ash, and the small standing splinter that had lain down only when it was ready to.

Ossler studied her a moment longer than a captain should study his General. "With respect — you look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't." She turned the mare north, toward the line of ancient trees visible now on the horizon, dark as a wound in the bright morning, and heard herself add, quietly, in a voice she did not recognize as her own: "Someone spoke to me. Someone I buried a long time ago."

Ossler's face did the careful blank thing soldiers learn. "The dead don't speak, General."

"No," Vale said, and put her heels to the horse. "They wait. There's a difference. Move."

They rode. Behind them the column woke in stages, horns and harness and the smell of cookfires drowned by dew, and ahead of them the Thornwalk stood waiting at the world's edge, older than kingdoms, patient as roots. And in the case against her ribs, in ash that smelled faintly — she was almost sure, though she would never record it — of apples in late summer, the splinter lay still and listened to her heartbeat quicken, and counted the miles home.
