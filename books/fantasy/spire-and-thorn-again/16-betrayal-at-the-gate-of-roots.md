## 16. Betrayal at the Gate of Roots

The Gate of Roots did not look like a gate. That was the first thing Tamsin understood, standing before it with mud to his knees and the boy's hand small and warm in his own.

It looked like a wound in the world that had been healing for a thousand years.

Two trees — no, not trees; the word *tree* was too small for them — rose on either side of a gap in the hills, their trunks broad as watchtowers, their bark silvered and scarred with knotwork older than any script Tamsin had ever sold a charm against. Their branches leaned toward each other overhead but did not touch, leaving a corridor of grey morning light between them, and through that corridor he could see the Spire itself for the first time: a black needle of stone rising from a bowl of valley green, wrapped in thorn to half its height, its crown lost in cloud.

The wren rode his shoulder, feathers puffed against the cold, and said nothing. She had said very little since the ford. Since Dace.

*He will come again,* she had told him that night, as they'd huddled under an overhang of living rock while the river ran loud enough to drown a whisper. *Men like him always come again. A man who sells a friend once has learned the price of friends.*

Tamsin had wanted to argue. He had wanted to say, *You didn't see his face. You didn't hear him say* Vale will be merciful *like it was a prayer he needed answered.* But he had seen Dace's eyes in the torchlight at the water's edge, and they had not been cruel. They had been worse than cruel. They had been certain.

Now the boy tugged his hand. "It's singing," Hollowroot said, in that voice like wind through wheat, and pointed at the gate-trees with his free arm. "Can you hear it? It knows we're coming."

"I hear it," Tamsin said, and found that it was true. The gift he had smothered for three years behind a shopkeeper's counter had been waking steadily since the Thornwalk, since the stags' judgment, since the night he'd stood before beings older than kingdoms and argued for humanity without apology or flattery — and now the gate's slow song came to him plainly, a bass note felt in the teeth more than heard, patient as geology. *Come. Come. Come home.*

"Almost there," he said aloud, and was surprised by the roughness of his own voice. Three weeks on the road. Three weeks of ashfields and toll-bridges and a forest that tested him with his mother's burning. Three weeks of a child who was not a child growing heavier in his arms each night, wilting a little more, needing the Spire's soil the way a drowning man needs air. "Almost home."

Hollowroot smiled up at him, and for a moment — just a moment — Tamsin let himself believe the worst was behind them.

That was when the wren went rigid on his shoulder.

*Down,* she said. Not loudly. Wrens do not shout. But her voice went through him like a nail through pine. *Down, down, get him down, they're IN THE TREES—*

He was already moving, hauling Hollowroot behind the left-hand gate-tree's vast root buttress, when the horn sounded.

It was not a hunting horn. Hunting horns were for sport. This was a long, flat, deliberate note, blown three times, and Tamsin knew it from childhood — from the purge, from the night the coven burned — as the sound the Inquisitor General's column used to announce itself. *We are here,* it said. *We are law. Run if you like. We have all morning.*

They came out of the treeline to the east in a line of grey and iron, maybe forty riders, banners hanging limp in the still air, and at their head — small in the distance but unmistakable, straight-backed as a lance — rode Maren Vale.

And beside her, on a borrowed horse with a soldier's blanket for a saddle, rode Dace Corrow.

* * *

Time went strange after that. Tamsin crouched behind the root with Hollowroot pressed against his chest, the boy's heartbeat fluttering against his ribs like a bird in a sack, and watched the column fan out across the meadow with terrible unhurried discipline. They were not charging. They did not need to charge. There was nowhere to go. Behind them lay the Thornwalk, days of travel; ahead lay the Spire, sealed behind a gate that would not open for a fleeing man; and around them spread open ground where forty riders could run down anything on two legs.

*Options,* said the shopkeeper in him, the part that had survived three years in Vell by thinking three moves ahead. *Run for the gate and hope it opens. Hide in the roots. Give up the boy and bargain for—*

No. He shut that last door so hard it rang.

*Tamsin.* The wren's voice, gentle now, which frightened him more than her alarm had. *Listen to me. There is something you must know, and there is no kind way to say it.*

"Then say it unkindly," he breathed.

*Dace did not follow us. Dace did not track us. Dace knew where we would be, because I told him.*

The world tilted. He gripped the root-bark until splinters bit his palm. *What?*

*At the ford. When you slept, I flew back along the road. I found his camp. I spoke to him — mind to mind, as wardens may. I told him the gate, the day, the hour. I thought—* For the first time since Tamsin had known her, the wren's voice faltered. *I thought a man who begs a friend to surrender is a man who still loves him. I thought if Dace came alone, if he stood between you and Vale's justice one more time, he might find his spine. I gambled a friend's life on a friend's heart. It is the oldest wager wardens make, and I have made it before, and I should have known better.*

"You brought them here." The words came out flat, dead, unbelievable. "You gave him the map."

*I gave him a chance,* she said. *He spent it. That is not my sin alone, Tamsin Sterling, and you know whose testimony bought his pardon thirty years of mornings ago. Do not spend your grief on me yet. Spend it where it's owed — later, if there is a later.*

Hoofbeats. Close now. Tamsin pressed himself flat and peered around the root's edge, and saw the riders splitting into a crescent that would close around the gate-trees, and Vale riding at its center with Dace a half-length behind, and understood that hiding was finished. Whatever happened next would happen in the open, in front of witnesses, because Maren Vale did nothing without witnesses. Law required an audience.

He stood up. He took Hollowroot's hand.

"It's all right," he told the boy, who was looking up at him with those too-old eyes gone wide and dark. "Whatever happens, you keep walking toward the gate. Do you understand? You don't stop. You don't look back. The gate knows you. The gate will open for you even if it won't open for me."

"But you'll come too."

"Yes," Tamsin lied, and hated how easily it came, and stepped out into the open meadow with the boy's hand in his and the whole grey weight of the Inquisition riding down upon them.

* * *

Vale reined in ten paces away. Up close, without the distance of rumor, she was smaller than Tamsin expected and somehow more frightening for it — a narrow woman in plain steel, grey-eyed, weathered, with the patience of someone who had waited years for this morning and could wait years more if the morning disappointed her.

"Tamsin Sterling," she said. Her voice carried across the meadow effortlessly, pitched for the soldiers, for the record, for history. "Charm-seller. Practitioner. Second on the list I have carried for six years." She glanced at the boy, and something flickered behind her eyes — something almost like reluctance — before the law closed over it. "And the abomination. The seed. You've led us quite a chase."

"He's a child," Tamsin said.

"He is a weapon wearing a child, Mr. Sterling. I have read the old accounts. I know what walked out of the Spire before the covenant caged it." She dismounted, unhurried, boots sinking slightly in the wet grass. "You could make this easy. Kneel. Hand the creature to my men. Testify about the others — the hedge-witches, the beast-speakers, everyone who helped you along this road. Cooperate, and you'll see trial instead of pyre. I am, despite my reputation, a reasonable woman."

Behind her, Dace sat his horse and would not meet Tamsin's eyes.

That was the thing that broke something loose in Tamsin's chest — not the swords, not the odds, not Vale's terrible courtesy. Dace. His oldest friend. The boy who had shared stolen apples with him on the temple steps, who had held his hair back the first time Tamsin drank rotgut, who had testified against Tamsin's mother and called it survival and then spent three years unable to look at him. Who had begged him at the ford to surrender quietly, *because Vale will be merciful*, with tears standing in his eyes.

And now he sat mute while forty soldiers closed a crescent around a child.

"Dace," Tamsin said, and his voice cracked on the name, and he hated himself for it. "Look at me. Just — look at me. One time. Look at me and tell me what you see."

Dace looked. For a long moment, across the wet grass and the drawn steel, their eyes met, and Tamsin saw everything he'd feared and everything he'd hoped: the guilt, the love, the cowardice, the desperate arithmetic of a man who had traded his only remaining friend for a pardon and was already learning what the coin purchased.

"I'm sorry," Dace said hoarsely. "Tamsin. Gods. I'm sorry. But she'd have found you anyway. At least this way there's a trial, there's a record, there's—"

"There's a pyre," Tamsin said. "There's always been a pyre. You just finally stopped pretending otherwise."

"Take them," Vale said quietly.

* * *

What happened next, Tamsin would remember for the rest of his life in fragments, the way a man remembers a shipwreck: in pieces, out of order, each one too bright.

A soldier reaching for Hollowroot, and the boy not screaming, only going terribly still, the way a tree goes still before winter.

The meadow grass browning in a ring around the boy's feet, fast as spilled ink.

Himself lunging forward, shouting words he couldn't later recall, and two soldiers wrestling him down into the mud with a knee between his shoulders, his cheek pressed to the cold earth, close enough to smell the crushed grass going grey.

Vale watching, arms folded, saying something about chains of cold-iron and proper procedure.

And above it all — above all of it — a small brown blur dropping from the sky like a thrown stone.

The wren hit the lead soldier's face in a fury of wings and claws, going for his eyes, and the man staggered back with a shriek, swatting at her, and another soldier raised a sword — not even meaning to, Tamsin would think afterward, just reflex, just panic, just a blade swinging at a blur —

"No!" Tamsin's voice tore out of him, raw, wordless, and every animal within a mile heard it. Somewhere in the treeline wolves began to howl. Crows lifted from the Thornwalk in a black ragged sheet. The horses screamed and plunged, four of them breaking free of their riders entirely, stampeding for the treeline.

But the sword had already fallen.

She managed to reach him. That was the mercy of it, if it was mercy. She dragged herself across ten feet of trampled grass with one wing bent wrong and blood matting her breast feathers, and she climbed onto his chest where it pressed against the mud, and she put her small hot face close to his ear.

*Tamsin.* Faint now. Fainter. *Listen. Listen to me, because I have little breath left for speeches. The gate will open for him. Only for him. Get up. Get up and finish this, because I did not bind myself to your grandmother's grandmother's bloodline to watch it end face-down in a cow field.*

"Wren—" His voice was wrecked. "Wren, I can't—"

*You can.* A pause. When she spoke again, her voice had softened into something he had never heard from her: tenderness, unarmored, ancient. *Three years I watched you smother yourself behind that counter, selling luck-knots to fools, and never once — not once — did you use the gift for spite. Do you know how rare that is? How rare kindness is, in a gift that could so easily be cruelty? I chose your family a thousand years ago, little speaker. I would choose you again. Now choose to stand up.*

Her heartbeat slowed against his collarbone. Slowed. Stopped.

Around him, the meadow had gone silent except for the wind. The soldiers stood frozen, staring at the small brown body on the prisoner's chest, at the ring of dead grass spreading outward from it like a stain. Even Vale had not moved. Even Vale's certainty, perhaps, had found something in that scene it could not immediately file.

Tamsin lay in the mud with his dead warden on his heart, and grief went through him so vast and so quiet that it passed beyond weeping, beyond sound, into a place like the bottom of a well. He thought of her on the shop windowsill, scolding him about grain prices. He thought of her naming the boy *Hollowroot* in the storm-dark. He thought of a thousand years of wardens, and a covenant broken, and a family that had helped break it, and a debt that had just been paid on his behalf by four ounces of feather and bone.

Then he thought of the gate. *The gate will open for him. Only for him.*

And Tamsin Sterling, who had spent three years being careful, who had spent a lifetime being small, did the least careful thing he had ever done.

He rolled his shoulders, twisted hard, and threw off the soldier pinning him — caught off-guard, unsettled by the horses, by the birds, by the silence — and came up out of the mud with his hands free and his voice free, and he shouted it across the whole grey meadow, to Vale, to Dace, to the soldiers, to the listening land:

"Hollowroot! RUN!"

The boy ran.

He ran the way water runs downhill, the way spring runs north — inevitable, unarguable — and the gate-trees woke. Tamsin saw it happen: the silver bark flushing green from the roots upward, the great limbs groaning apart, the corridor of light widening, widening, becoming a doorway, becoming an invitation. Roots burst from the earth behind the running boy like a wake behind a boat, hurling two soldiers aside, and the Spire beyond the gate seemed to lean forward, the whole valley leaning, the whole old world holding its breath—

Vale drew her sword. "Stop him!"

Dace moved first.

Later, Tamsin would never be sure what Dace had intended — whether to cut the boy down, or to cut down the men ordered to do it, or simply to throw himself bodily into the gap between law and love one final time. What Tamsin saw, face-down in the grass with a boot on his spine, was this: Dace spurring his horse forward, drawing steel, and Vale's voice cracking across the meadow like a whip — "*Corrow, stand down*" — and Dace not standing down, and the collision of bodies and horses at the gate's threshold, and blood, bright and shocking on the new-green grass.

And past all of it, small and swift and burning like a coal, Hollowroot crossed the threshold of the Gate of Roots.

The trees closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

The silence that followed was total. Forty soldiers, one Inquisitor General, one bleeding traitor, one pinned prisoner — and the gate sealed, and the Spire silent, and somewhere beyond that silver-and-thorn doorway a child-shaped seed walking home through country no human eye would see again soon.

Tamsin began, very quietly, to laugh. It was not a sane sound. He could hear that himself, distantly, and could not stop it. Because they had failed — Vale had failed, the whole apparatus of honest law had failed — and because the wren was dead on his chest, and because Dace was down in the grass and not moving, and because the boy was through, *through*, and none of it, none of it, changed the fact that Tamsin himself was face-down in the mud with the entire Inquisition arranged around him like a funeral.

Vale walked over. She stood above him for a long moment, sword still bare, grey eyes unreadable.

"Get the prisoner up," she said at last, to no one in particular. "Carefully. And someone see to Corrow." She turned away, then paused, and her voice dropped low enough that only Tamsin could hear it — and there was something in it he had not heard before. Something almost like uncertainty.

"The gate opened for it," she said. "It wasn't supposed to be able to do that."

She walked away toward her horse, and the soldiers hauled Tamsin upright, and the last thing he saw before they bound his wrists was the wren's small body lying in the trampled grass, and the first green shoots — impossibly, absurdly, tenderly — already pushing up through the dead ring around her.
