## 17. The Seed and the Sword

They kept him in a tent that smelled of tallow and cold iron.

It was not a cruel captivity — that was the worst of it. Maren Vale did not need cruelty; she had the law, and the law was patient as frost. A canvas wall-tent at the edge of her camp, a cot with one blanket, a guard outside who shifted his weight every quarter hour like a clock made of flesh. Two iron rings bolted to the center pole, and a chain long enough to let Tamsin reach the water jug, the slop pail, and the boy.

Only the boy.

Hollowroot lay on the second cot, and Tamsin had not left his side since they'd dragged them both from the Gate of Roots three days ago. Three days, and the boy had spoken perhaps a dozen words, each one thinner than the last, as though language itself were draining out of him along with everything else.

The wren was dead. He could not think of it for longer than a breath at a time or something behind his ribs would crack further, and he needed what remained of himself whole. She had gone up off his shoulder in a rush of grey feathers and thorn-dark blood, and she had not come down, and the Spire's gate had closed behind them like an eye shutting.

So he watched the boy instead. Watching was a kind of prayer, if you did it long enough.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Tamsin understood what he was looking at, and wished he didn't.

Hollowroot's hands had changed. They had always been strange — bark-knuckled, sap-veined, a child's shape grown over older wood — but now the strangeness was failing. The brown of his skin was going grey at the edges, the way a leaf goes when frost finds it. The fine green filaments beneath his fingernails, which had glowed faintly in the dark of the Thornwalk like foxfire, were dim. When Tamsin took the boy's wrist to feel for a pulse, the skin came away cool and dry, and a flake of something like bark sloughed off under his thumb.

"Hey," Tamsin said softly. "Hey. Look at me."

One eye opened. It had been the color of new leaves once, deep and wet. Now it was the color of leaves in their last week, yellowing at the rim, and Tamsin felt the sight of it go through him like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

"You're wilting," Tamsin said. There was no gentler word. He had spent three years selling charms to farmers and fishwives; he knew every soft lie in the trade, and none of them fit this. "You're wilting, and I know why."

The boy's lips moved. What came out was barely a whisper, and it was not Vellish. It was the old tongue, the one no human throat should know, the one that had silenced every animal in a street on a storm-night half a lifetime ago. But Tamsin had been listening to it for weeks now, learning it the way you learn rain — not word by word but all at once, in the body.

*Far,* the boy said. *Too far from the root.*

"I know." Tamsin smoothed the blanket over those greying hands. "I know. We're close. Two days, maybe less, if the road holds."

*Roads don't hold.* The ghost of something that might have been humor crossed the small face, there and gone. *Roots hold. I am not holding.*

Tamsin looked away, because the boy did not need to see what was happening to his face. Outside the tent, the camp of the Inquisitor General went about its morning: boots on frozen mud, the jingle of harness, someone singing a marching song badly. Somewhere beyond the picket lines, the Thornwalk stood black against the pale sky where Vale's men had burned its edge, and past it, hidden in folds of hill and haze, the Spire waited with its heart full of dying soil.

Two days. The boy might have two days. And between here and there lay an army, an executioner's warrant, and a man who had once been Tamsin's whole world and was now the reason a wren was dead.

He sat back down on the cot and took Hollowroot's hand again, carefully, the way you hold something already broken.

"Then we don't wait for roads," he said.

* * *

Maren Vale came at midday, because of course she did. She was a woman who did everything at the hour it was most inconvenient to be done.

She entered without ceremony — two guards, then herself, plain grey wool over mail, iron-grey hair pinned back with what Tamsin recognized, with a lurch, as a charm. Not a working. Just a knot of rowan wood, old-fashioned, the kind his mother used to tie for travelers. Someone had given it to her once, long ago, and she had never thrown it away. He filed that away with the other small contradictions he'd been collecting since the ford: the woman who executed her own hound for kneeling, and whose voice had shaken, just slightly, when she'd said *the law is not cruelty, Sterling, it is mercy with a spine.*

She stopped three paces inside the tent and looked at the boy for a long moment. Whatever she saw in that wilting face, she did not let it reach her own.

"He's dying," Tamsin said.

"Inquisitors are not physicians."

"No. But you've seen death before. You know what it looks like walking around." He rose, and the chain rattled, and he hated the sound and made himself stand straight through it anyway. "Look at his hands, General. Look at his eyes. You've read your heresy manuals — tell me which chapter describes *this.*"

Something flickered in her expression. Not doubt. Interest. That was worse, or better; he hadn't decided.

"The seed is failing," Tamsin said. "That's what he is — you must have guessed by now, whatever your reports call him. The last living piece of the covenant, wearing a shape it borrowed so it could survive long enough to be planted. And it can't survive here. It needs the Spire's soil, the heart-soil, the ground your grandmother's grandmother swore on before anyone decided oaths were heresy. Without it—" He gestured at the cot, at the grey creeping up the boy's wrists like rising water. "Without it, days. Maybe fewer. Your fire at the Thornwalk's edge sped it up, if you want the credit."

Vale's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "The burn was a lawful clearing operation."

"It was a bonfire lit by frightened people," Tamsin said, "and it killed things older than your office. But we're not here to argue forestry." He drew a breath. This was the moment. Everything he had left — the shop, the name, the mother, the wren, all of it spent or burning — came down to the next sixty seconds, and he found, strangely, that his hands were steady. Desperation, it turned out, felt a great deal like calm once you got all the way into it.

"I'm going to make you an offer, General. And I want you to hear all of it before you decide I'm lying, because you're going to want to decide that quickly. Lying is easier to dismiss."

"Then speak."

"Witness the truth of the covenant before you destroy it."

Her stillness was total. Even the guards behind her seemed to stop breathing.

"You have a splinter," Tamsin went on, quietly. "In a glass case, in your baggage train. Thorn-wood, taken from the first purge, thirty years ago. It moves on its own. It's been moving toward the Spire this entire march — you've checked, haven't you? Every morning, a little closer to the east side of the case. Don't look surprised. The wren told me what it was before she told me anything else. It's a piece of the covenant, General. A splinter of the same oath your office exists to deny, and it has been sitting on your shelf for thirty years, alive, pointing home." He let that settle. "You keep it because you can't bring yourself to burn it. Ask yourself why."

"That is speculation—"

"It's observation. I'm a beast-speaker, Maren Vale. Listening is the only thing I've ever been good at, and I have listened to you since the ford. You don't hate the old magic. You fear it, and you have excellent reasons, and I'm not going to take those from you." His voice caught, and he mastered it. "But there is a difference between fearing a thing and refusing to look at it, and you have built an empire on not looking. So look. Once. Come inside the Spire's threshold with me — not to the heart, not to the rite, just to the threshold, where the old magic shows everyone who enters exactly what it is. No tricks. The covenant doesn't trick; that was always the trouble with it, it's too honest for its own good. Let it show you what it showed me. Let it show you what happens if it dies unbound. And then, if you still want to destroy it—" he spread his hands, chains clinking, offering her the whole ruinous arithmetic of himself— "then destroy it with me standing in front of it, and I will not say one word in my defense. You'll have your purge. You'll have your peace of mind. You'll have earned both honestly, which is more than any Inquisitor has managed in a hundred years."

Silence. The camp noise went on outside, absurdly ordinary — a horse blowing, the bad singer starting another verse.

"And if I refuse?" Vale said at last.

"Then in two days the last seed of the covenant dies in a military tent, alone, far from its soil, and the feral magic it was holding back comes loose into a world that has forgotten how to bind it. You've seen the edge of that already. The thing that took your sister—" he saw her flinch, a single millimeter, and pressed on gently, hating himself and unable to stop, "—that was a leak, General. One leak, from one weakening seam. The whole vessel is about to fail. You are not preventing that catastrophe. You are standing on it."

"Do not," Vale said, and her voice had gone very quiet and very level, the way steel goes quiet, "speak of my sister."

"Then don't make me the only one who talks about the dead," Tamsin said. "Come talk to the Spire. It remembers everyone. It even remembers the ones we'd rather it forgot." He held her gaze. "That's the offer. Witness, then judge. Truth first, sword after. Isn't that the order your own charter claims? *Verum prius.* I read it carved over the Inquisition hall in Vell, every day, for three years, while I sold luck-knots two streets from where my mother burned."

For a long moment she simply looked at him, and he let himself be looked at — the shopkeeper's smile gone, the mask burned with the shop, nothing left but a tired man in chains who had run out of everything except the truth.

"You are either the most sincere heretic I have ever arrested," Vale said finally, "or the most dangerous liar."

"My mother used to say those were usually the same person."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not a smile, nothing so generous, but the place where a smile had once lived and been evicted. She turned toward the entrance, then paused.

"The boy," she said, without turning fully around. "If he dies in your custody—"

"He won't die in my custody," Tamsin said. "He'll die in yours, in this camp, unless you move. That's the other half of the offer, General, and it isn't negotiable, and it isn't a trick. Every hour you spend deciding, he spends dying. I've given you the only honest bargain I have. Decide fast."

She stood there a moment longer, and Tamsin watched her look at the boy — really look, the way she'd looked at the kneeling hound before the sword fell, the look of a woman measuring a truth against a law and feeling the measurement come out wrong. The rowan knot in her hair caught the grey light through the canvas.

Then she was gone, and the guards with her, and the tent-flap settled, and Tamsin sank down onto the cot beside Hollowroot and put his forehead against the boy's cooling hand.

"Well," he said to no one. "Either that worked, or I've just volunteered us both for a faster execution."

From the cot came a whisper, thin as a thread of sap: *She will come.*

"How do you know?"

A pause. Then, in Vellish, in the boy's small rusted voice, one of the few sentences of the trade tongue he had ever bothered with:

*"Because the splinter told her so."*

Outside, across the camp, a bell began to ring — the assembly bell, quick and urgent, the sound of an army changing its mind. Tamsin lifted his head. Through the gap in the canvas he could see officers striding toward the command tent, and beyond them, at the baggage line, soldiers gathered around a wagon, and above their heads, held up in a gloved hand for all to see, a glass case in which something small and dark and thorned pressed against the eastern wall, straining eastward, toward the Spire, like a compass needle that had finally been believed.
