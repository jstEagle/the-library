## 18. What Vale Buried

The Spire's threshold was not a door. Maren Vale had spent her life believing doors were things that could be barred, and this was the first thing she had ever met that refused to be one.

It stood at the mountain's root where the Thornwalk thinned into bare black stone: an arch of living wood, grown, not built, its grain spiraling inward like water finding a drain. No guards could have held it. No ram could have broken it. It simply stood there, older than the word *gate*, and when her column of forty picked men and nine hounds drew up before it in the grey hour before dawn, every hound sat down at once, all nine, in perfect unison, like a congregation.

Vale dismounted. Her breath smoked. Behind her, the prisoner Tamsin Sterling knelt between two soldiers with his wrists bound in iron-threaded cord, and beside him — also bound, though no one had suggested it was necessary; the soldiers had simply done it, the way one ties down cargo in a storm — the child called Hollowroot swayed on his feet like a cut stem.

The boy was wilting. Vale had seen it first as she might notice a flaw in a coin: the skin gone from cream to parchment, the eyes sunk back, the hair losing its color the way leaves do in reverse. He had asked for nothing. That was the worst of it. Children begged. This thing wearing a child's shape only looked at the gate with an expression she had no honest name for, and so she had named it *longing*, and hated herself for the naming.

"General," said Lieutenant Corrow at her shoulder. Dace Corrow's voice had been wrong for two days now, fraying at the edges like rope over a pulley. "We can burn it. We brought the oil."

She did not answer immediately. In the case at her hip, wrapped in wool, the thorn splinter lay still. It had been still since the ford, since it spoke to her in Sera's voice, and its silence was worse than its speaking. A thing that wanted something went quiet only when it was about to be given it.

"No burning," she said. "Not yet."

"General—"

"I said not yet, Lieutenant." She heard the edge in her own voice and let it stand. Corrow had been her best officer for three years precisely because he never flinched from what the law required. Lately he flinched at everything. She would deal with that later. There was a list of things she would deal with later, and it was growing, and she did not look at it directly, the way one does not look at the sun.

She walked forward alone.

* * *

The threshold took her without ceremony.

One step she was in the cold; the next, the cold was gone and she stood in a green light that came from everywhere and nowhere, light the color of the underside of leaves, and the air tasted of rain on hot stone and something sweeter beneath it, honey and loam and old smoke. The arch was behind her — she turned and saw it, impossibly small now, a distant ring of wood with her army a scatter of ants beyond — and ahead of her the mountain opened inward into halls of root and stone, pillars grown rather than carved, their surfaces moving very slowly, like sleeping animals breathing.

"You are the one who keeps the list."

The voice came from the walls themselves, or the floor, or the light. It was not a voice so much as a pressure that arranged itself into words. It was not Sera's voice. Vale noticed that first, with relief that curdled instantly into suspicion — because it knew about the list. Of course it knew. Things like this knew everything; that was what made them abominations.

"I keep the law," she said aloud, and her voice fell flat in the green air, small as a child's.

"You keep a list." The light shifted, deepened. "Forty-one names. You have crossed off nine. You tell yourself each crossing is justice. Come. Let us read the list together, Maren Vale. Let us see what is written beneath the names."

"I don't make bargains with—"

"This is not a bargain. Bargains require two wills. I am only going to show you what you already carry. You have been showing it to yourself for twenty years. You have simply been very disciplined about not watching."

And the green light closed over her head like water.

* * *

She was eleven years old again, and it was summer, and the orchard behind the house at Harrow's Bend smelled of crushed grass and early apples.

Sera was fourteen that summer, all elbows and laughter, braiding daisies into the mane of the miller's old mare and talking to her the whole time — *hold still, you great lump, you've a burr behind your ear* — and the mare, notoriously vicious, stood like a saint for her. Everyone said Sera had a way with animals. Everyone said it fondly then. Later they would say it in the other tone, the tone that came after the edicts, the tone that meant *report her*.

Vale watched her sister from across the fence, and the memory was so complete that her adult hands, her scarred, sword-calloused hands, reached for the rail and found the sun-warmed wood of twenty years ago, splinters and all.

*I know this,* she told the light. *I know how it ends.*

"Yes," said the Spire, gently, which was somehow more terrible than cruelty. "You know how it ends. But you do not know why. You were sent away to your aunt's the week before. You were told it was illness in the village. You have spent twenty years building your life on the foundation of that lie. Watch."

The orchard dissolved like sugar in rain.

* * *

Harrow's Bend, three years later. Autumn. Smoke.

Not hearth-smoke. The other kind. Vale stood in the lane she had grown up in and watched the feral magic come out of the western woods, and it was not the tidy hedge-craft of charms and luck-knots, nothing any charm-seller in Vell could have made. It was the covenant dying. She understood that now, standing inside the thing itself, seeing with eyes that were both eleven and forty-one: the old pact between the Spire and the settlements had been breaking for a generation, starved by human expansion, by burned practitioners, by the slow arrogant forgetting of terms no one remembered agreeing to. And what a covenant contains does not vanish when the covenant breaks. It only stops being held.

The wild magic moved through Harrow's Bend like weather. Trees walked. Not swayed — *walked*, roots tearing free of the earth in black fists. The river ran backward. And in the orchard, in the place where Sera had once braided daisies, the mare screamed, and the scream was wrong, layered, many-throated, and the mare was no longer entirely a mare. Something old and starving had poured into the beasts nearest the breach, and Sera — Sera who loved animals, who had never feared one in her life — went to soothe it, because she was fourteen and kind and had no idea that the world had changed shape while she slept.

Vale tried to run to her. Her legs would not move. The memory held her where memory holds witnesses: exactly where she had not been, seeing exactly what she had not seen, which was everything.

She saw her sister reach out her hand. She saw the creature that had been the mare look at that hand with too many reflections in its eye. She saw Sera smile — even then, even at the end, she smiled, some last instinct of trust — and she saw the wildness take her, not quickly, not cleanly, folding her into itself like a wave taking a sandcastle, and the light went out of the orchard, and the walking trees turned toward the village, and—

The scene cut. Cleanly. Mercilessly. Like a surgeon.

"She died of the covenant's collapse," the Spire said, in the dark between scenes. "Not of witchcraft. The witches of Harrow's Bend spent that night doing what your edicts have made nearly impossible: they fought the breach. Three of them died holding the line so the village could evacuate. Your records call them the cause. Your records were written by frightened men who needed a cause, and then by ambitious men who found the fear useful. You inherited their arithmetic, Maren Vale. Fear plus grief equals purpose. You have been solving that equation for twenty years."

"Stop." Her voice cracked on it. "Stop, I—"

"One more. Then you may stop."

* * *

The future, this time. Or a possible future — she knew the difference, and knew the thing showing her knew she knew, and showed her anyway.

Vell, but not Vell. Vell unheld. The same streets she had walked for fifteen years with her list and her courteous voice, and running through them now was the green fire of the uncontained — the feral old magic, the thing the covenant had bound for a thousand years, loose at last because the pact had failed, because the seed had died unwanted in an iron-bound cart, because a woman with a dead sister and a glass case had ridden faster than mercy.

She saw the hounds change. She saw her own soldiers run. She saw the river run backward through the fish market and the fish swim through the air among the screaming gulls, beautiful and appalling, beauty with teeth, the world reverting to something magnificent and utterly indifferent to human throats. She saw children — she saw *a* child, mud-caked, eight years old, wilting — and the vision held there, on the boy's face, on the exact expression he wore looking at the gate, and the word she had refused finally arrived and she could not refuse it anymore.

Grief. It was grief. The land grieving itself.

"It ends either way," the Spire said quietly. "That is what you must understand, Inquisitor General. You cannot prevent the ending. The covenant is broken; the seed is dying; the wild magic comes home regardless. The only question left in the world is whether it comes home *bound* — reforged, held, shared, with a speaker willing to be tied to both realms — or whether it comes home the way it came to Harrow's Bend. Unheld. Starving. Yours was never a choice between the old law and the new. It was a choice between a door and a flood."

"And if I refuse?" Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake and could not stop them. "If I burn the seed and salt the heart? If I end magic altogether, root and branch?"

The light around her dimmed, and in the dimming she felt — not saw, *felt* — the size of the thing she was threatening, the vast slow patient presence of the Spire's heart, older than kingdoms, holding back a weight she had spent her whole life pretending did not exist. It did not threaten her back. That was the unbearable part. It simply let her feel the scale, the way one lets a child feel the depth of a well.

"Then you will spend the rest of your life fighting a war you have already lost," it said, "and your soldiers' grandchildren will live in the trees' world, if they live at all. You are not powerful enough to destroy me, Maren Vale. You are only powerful enough to decide whether anyone survives my falling."

* * *

She came out of the threshold on her knees in the frost, though she had no memory of kneeling. The green light was gone. The arch stood silent. Behind her, her column waited in the grey dawn, forty men and nine hounds, and none of them had moved, and the frost on the ground around the gate — she saw it now, and her stomach turned — the frost ended in a perfect line, as if the world beyond the arch were a different season entirely.

Tamsin Sterling was watching her. Bound, exhausted, condemned, he watched her with the weary attention of a man reading a document he has already signed. Beside him the boy swayed, and his skin had gone the grey-white of birch bark, and when a gust came off the mountain his whole body leaned into it like a plant.

"General," Corrow started, "the oil is ready, we can—"

"Lieutenant." Her voice came out level. She was grateful for that, at least; whatever else the Spire had taken from her, it had left the discipline. "How long has the boy got?"

Corrow blinked. "I— General, I don't—"

"How long, Corrow. You've been looking at him for two days. You've been looking at him the way I used to look at the splinter. Answer the question."

Dace Corrow looked at the child, and something in his face gave way — not broke, *gave way*, like ice under a boot, all at once and irreversibly. "Days," he said hoarsely. "Maybe less."

Vale stood in the frost at the border of two seasons, twenty years of arithmetic dissolving behind her eyes — *fear plus grief equals purpose* — and understood that the equation had always been wrong, that it had been wrong the day they wrote it, that Sera had died not of witchcraft but of a door left unheld, and that she, Maren Vale, keeper of lists, crosser-off of names, had spent two decades guarding the lock while picking it herself.

The splinter stirred in its case at her hip. Warm. Waiting.

She thought of Sera's hand, reaching out in trust. She thought of the boy leaning into the wind.

"Cut the prisoner's cords," she said.

The order landed in the silence like a dropped bell. Forty men stared. Corrow stared. Somewhere behind the ranks, a hound whined — not in threat, Vale realized. In hope.

"General—" Corrow began.

"That was not a request, Lieutenant." She drew a breath that hurt all the way down, the breath of a woman stepping off a cliff she had been standing on for twenty years. "Sterling. Get up. You have until moonset to show me this rite of yours — and if it fails, if this is trickery, if anything in that mountain lies to me—"

"It won't need to," Tamsin Sterling said quietly, rubbing feeling back into his wrists. His eyes, when they met hers, held no triumph at all. Only a tired, terrible kindness. "Nothing in there lies, General. That's rather the problem."

He turned toward the gate. The boy reached up and took his hand. And Maren Vale, Inquisitor General of Vell, breaker of her own list, followed them into the green.
