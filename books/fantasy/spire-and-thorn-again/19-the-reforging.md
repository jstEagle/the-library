## 19. The Reforging

The Spire's heart was not what I had imagined in three years of not daring to imagine it.

I had pictured a tower — the old stories all said *tower*, white and crooked as a heron's leg, crowned with thorns that scraped the belly of the sky. What we found instead, past the Gate of Roots and down through corridors where the walls breathed like sleeping animals, was a garden. A garden gone to ruin.

Hollowroot stood between me and the center of it, swaying on his small feet, and I could see now how much of him had already gone. The green had drained from beneath his skin until he looked like paper left out in rain. His eyes were still bright — they would be bright until the very last hour, I think; hope was simply what he was made of — but when he lifted his hand to point at the soil ahead, the fingers trembled like new leaves in wind.

"There," the wren had told me, before Vale's hounds took her. Before Dace took everything else. *The last living soil. My bloodline kept it, and yours forgot it, and now you must remember for both of us.*

I remembered now. That was the terrible gift of this place: it pressed memory into you like a seal into wax. Every step deeper into the Spire's heart, I felt my mother's hands again, felt the kitchen of my childhood with its hanging bundles of yarrow, felt the words she used to murmur over the stewpot — words I had thought were nonsense, or prayer, and which were neither. They were bookkeeping. The covenant was an account, kept balanced by speakers on both sides, and my family had been the human ledger for nine hundred years.

And we had let it fall into arrears.

* * *

The soil lay in a shallow bowl of white stone at the garden's center, no bigger than a grave plot, and it was the only living thing in all that vast hollow dark. Everything else had gone to gray — trees like smoke, vines like wire, flowers reduced to their own ghosts. But the bowl of earth was black and rich and faintly warm, and when I knelt beside it and pressed my palm flat against its surface, something pressed back.

Not a hand. A pulse. Slow, enormous, patient as geology.

"It knows you," Hollowroot whispered. His voice had been failing all day, going thin and rusted at the edges, but he managed a small, crooked smile. "It remembers your grandmother's grandmother. It says she sang badly."

"She did," I said, and my throat closed around it. "She sang terribly."

He laughed, and the laugh cost him. He folded down onto the stone rim of the bowl like a coat slipping off a hook, and I caught him, and he weighed almost nothing. A child of eight should have weighed more than grief. He weighed exactly grief.

"Tell me what to do," I said.

"You know what to do." He looked up at me with those too-bright eyes. "You've known since the wren told you. You just haven't said it out loud yet, because saying things out loud is the hardest magic there is."

He was right. Of course he was right; he was the Spire's last seed wearing a stolen shape, and he knew the shape of every truth I'd spent three years burying under shopkeeper's smiles and harmless luck-knots.

The rite was simple. That was the cruelty of it. Simple as a knife.

Plant the seed in the last living soil. And then a speaker must bind himself — voice, name, and future — to both realms at once. Not a bargain. Bargains could be broken; bargains were what my family had broken. A binding. My voice would speak for thorn and stone as readily as for flesh and feather, forever. My name would be written into the roots of the world, and the roots do not forget and do not release. My future — whatever future remained after the Inquisitor General's column burned its way toward us — belonged equally to the wild and the human, and neither side would ever let me wholly go.

And the binding blow. Because nothing this old was given freely. The rite demanded a price paid in the moment of joining, and the price was death — the speaker's death, taken by the land itself, quick and root-deep, so that the covenant would begin as all covenants should: with someone giving everything and keeping nothing back.

My mother had known this. I understood that suddenly, kneeling in the cold ghost-garden with a dying god-child in my arms. She hadn't fled Vell because she feared the pyre. She'd fled because she was the last speaker, and if she died on the Inquisitor's fire, unbound, the covenant would collapse with no one left to reforge it. She had run not from death but *toward* a different one, saving hers for a purpose, and the hounds had caught her anyway, and her death had bought me three years of hiding and shame.

I would not waste it. I set Hollowroot down gently at the rim of the bowl.

* * *

Digging was easy. The soil opened for my hands like a mouth.

Lifting him was harder. He weighed less than a bundle of kindling now, and when I settled him into the shallow grave-shaped bed of earth, he sighed the way children sigh when they're finally allowed to sleep.

"Not asleep," he murmured, reading my face. "Planted. There's a difference. Asleep is forgetting. Planted is remembering *harder*."

"I know." I drew the black soil up over his legs, his hips, his chest. He didn't flinch. "I'll stay until—"

"Until the end. Yes. That's the whole point of you, Tamsin Sterling." He smiled up at me through the dirt. "You always stay. Even when staying is stupid. Especially then."

I smoothed soil over his shoulders, his throat, careful around his chin, and he became a boy-shaped mound with a face, watching me with the patience of forests. Above us, far away, I could hear it beginning — the groan of old wood, the crackle of fire at the Thornwalk's edge where Vale's column burned its way inward. We had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less.

"Hollowroot," I said. "If this doesn't work—"

"It will work." No doubt in him at all. "It has to work. Those are nearly the same thing, at this depth."

I laughed despite everything, and the laugh echoed strangely off the gray trees, and some of them — I swear it — leaned a little closer to listen.

Then I knelt in the soil beside him, put both hands flat on the warm black earth, and began to speak.

* * *

I spoke in the old tongue first, because the rite was older than any kingdom and deserved its birth-language. Then I spoke in common, because I was a human speaker and my truth needed my own tongue. I gave the rite my voice — felt it leave me, actually leave, a warmth drawn up out of my chest like a splinter pulled from a wound — and promised it to thorn and stone and feather and scale, to every throat that was not mine.

I gave it my name. *Tamsin.* My mother chose it; her mother before her; a name that meant, in the old tongue, *the hinge*. I felt the roots take hold of it, felt it sink into me like a taproot, anchoring me to both worlds at once. From that moment I would never again be able to hear a sparrow without hearing the stone beneath the sparrow, nor walk a city street without feeling the wild patience of the ground under the cobbles. Both realms, forever, in one pair of ears.

The future came last, and it came hard, because I had to mean it. I offered the years I might have had — the shop rebuilt, the quiet life, the small safe lies — all of it, poured into the soil like water. And the soil drank.

And then the land rose up to take its due.

I felt it come the way you feel a wave before it breaks: a gathering in the ground, a tightening in the roots, the whole buried patience of the Spire drawing itself together into a single strike. Root and stone and thorn, rising through the black earth toward my heart, swift and clean and merciful as falling asleep in snow.

I closed my eyes. I thought of my mother. I hoped she could see.

The blow never landed.

* * *

What landed instead was a body.

I heard him coming — of course I heard him; I heard everything now, every footfall in the Spire, every rat in the walls, every worm turning in the dead flowerbeds — and I had half a heartbeat to recognize the stride, that familiar loping stride I'd chased through the orchards of my childhood, and then Dace Corrow hit me full in the chest and knocked me sideways out of the bowl of soil, and the binding blow struck home into *him*.

He made no sound. That was the worst of it. Dace, who had never in his life been quiet, who talked through silences the way other men walked through doors — he made no sound at all as the roots took him. I saw it happen: the thorn-light rising through the black soil, finding him, knowing him, entering through his back between the shoulder blades where the blow was aimed. His eyes went wide. His mouth opened on a word that never came.

I caught him as he fell.

"Dace. Dace, no — you idiot, you *idiot*, it was mine, it was supposed to be mine—"

"I know." His voice came out sanded down to almost nothing, and still, even dying, even now, he tried to make a joke of it. "That's why. Had to be quick. You'd have argued."

"I'm arguing *now*—"

"You argue beautiful." He coughed, and there was green in it — actual green, leaf-light at the corner of his mouth, the covenant sealing itself through him cell by cell. "Listen. Listen, Tam. I don't get long, and I've wasted eleven years, so shut up and let me spend these right."

I shut up. I held him. The gray garden seemed to lean in around us, every dead tree straining to witness.

"I testified," he said. "Against the coven. Against your mother. I told myself it was to save my own skin, and it was, but that wasn't all of it. I hated her a little. I hated that she had the gift and I had nothing, and you had her, and I had a father who beat the magic out of anything soft in our house. So when Vale's people asked me to name names, I named them *gladly*, and I have lived inside that gladly every single day since, and it is a room with no windows, Tam. It is a room with no windows and I built it myself, brick by brick, out of your mother's name."

"Dace—"

"I'm not done." Green tears, real ones, tracking down his face. "When I saw you leave the city with the boy — I said nothing. When Vale sent me to the ford, I begged you to surrender because I couldn't bear to watch you burn, and when you refused I went to her gate and traded your road north for a pardon, and I told myself it was mercy, getting you arrested instead of killed. Lies. All of it lies, stacked like cordwood. And then she killed the wren." His breath hitched. "She killed the wren, and I watched, and I understood that I had spent eleven years serving a woman who burns the living language out of the world, and that every brick in my windowless room was a lie about love."

His hand found my collar and gripped it with the last of his strength.

"So here's my truth, spoken aloud, in front of the roots and the stones and every gray tree in this dead garden, so it can't ever be unsaid: I betrayed you, and I loved you, and those were always the same act, because I betrayed you *out of* love, coward's love, love that saves itself. And this—" he pressed my hand flat against his chest, where the green light was spreading, gentle as dawn, "—this is the only payment I have. Take it. Take all of it. Let me be the last lie you ever forgive."

The roots were taking him softly now. I could feel it through my palms — the covenant flowing into him, filling him, and I understood with a speaker's new-born certainty that the land was doing what the land does with everything it loves: keeping him. Not erasing. Keeping.

"Dace," I said, and my voice broke and I let it break, because I was done with hidden things, "you were my brother before you were my betrayer, and you are my brother now, and I forgive you — not the last lie, all of them, every brick — and I will carry your name in the covenant same as hers. Same as my mother's. You'll be in the roots, you stubborn fool. You'll finally be somewhere with no walls at all."

He smiled. It was the orchard smile, the twelve-year-old smile, the one from before.

"Told you," he whispered. "You argue beautiful."

And then the green took all of him, and he was gone — not dead, I knew, not in any way that mattered to roots — and I knelt alone in the last living soil of the Spire, holding the empty shape of my oldest friend, while somewhere above us the fires of honest law came crashing down through the gray trees.

* * *

Behind me, the soil moved.

I turned, and my heart stopped and started and stopped again, because Hollowroot was sitting up out of the earth — sitting up, *growing*, the black soil streaming off him like water off a swimmer — and he was taller. Not a boy anymore. A youth of fifteen, sixteen, bark-textured skin, eyes like two green dawns, hair full of small white blossoms that had no business existing in a place with no sun. The seed had drunk deep: my voice, my name, my future, and Dace's death, freely given, the richest soil of all.

"The rite is sealed," he said, and his voice was no longer a child's voice. It was a voice like wind through a whole forest at once. "Voice, name, and future, bound and witnessed. One speaker gave his death. Another gave his forgiveness. The covenant takes root in both." He reached down and took my hand, and his grip was strong as a young oak, and he pulled me to my feet. "Get up, Tamsin Sterling, heir of the hinge. Your work isn't finished. It's only just begun."

Above us, through the vault of the Spire, I heard Vale's column breaking through the outer groves — steel and fire and the baying of hounds, and one voice calling orders in a tone like a drawn blade.

And beneath my feet, in the roots, in the stones, in the gray trees leaning close — I heard something answer. Something vast and old and waking, rising through the last living soil with a patience nine hundred years deep, asking me, in the tongue older than kingdoms, one simple question:

*Shall we show them what the wild remembers?*

Hollowroot smiled, blossoms trembling in his hair.

"They're here," he said. "All of them. And so is everything else."
