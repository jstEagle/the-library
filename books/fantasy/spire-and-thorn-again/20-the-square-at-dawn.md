## 20. The Square at Dawn

I did not sleep on the road back to Vell.

The covenant was reforged — I felt it in my blood the way a man feels a bone knit wrong, an ache that was also a mending — and still I walked through the dark with Hollowroot's soil under my fingernails and Dace's name caught in my throat like a fishbone. Every mile south, the land changed around me. Frost that had gripped the ditches for a week loosened its hold. A fox trotted alongside me for half a league, unafraid, and when it left it dipped its head as if to a magistrate. The wild things knew what had been done at the Spire's heart. Only men did not yet know, and men were the ones who burned.

Vell rose out of the pre-dawn mist the way it always had: grey walls, grey roofs, the bell tower of the Assize Court stabbing up like a splinter from a wound. Smoke stood over the tanneries. Somewhere inside those walls, three years of my life sat in a burnt shop on Cooper's Row, and somewhere inside those walls, Maren Vale's writ of purge was still law.

"You don't have to do this," I said aloud, to no one, and then corrected myself, because there was never again going to be a *no one*. "You don't have to do this," I told the crow perched on the milestone ahead of me.

"I know," the crow said. Crows are blunt creatures. They waste nothing on comfort. "But you're going to anyway. You have the look of a man walking into a fire he's already decided about."

"Is that how I look?"

"That's how all your kind looks, at the end." It ruffled its feathers against the cold. "The wardens used to say it. *They always choose the square.*"

*Your kind.* The words should have stung. Instead they settled into me like a key into a lock I hadn't known was mine. Three years I had called my gift a sickness, a secret, a small dishonesty practiced in a shop that smelled of beeswax and fear. But the beasts had never once called it anything but what it was. I was one of theirs. And they had been waiting a generation for one of us to stand up where everyone could see.

The gates of Vell opened at first light for the market carts, and I walked in among them, mud to my knees, smelling of woodsmoke and thorn-sap, carrying nothing but a dead friend's knife and a living promise.

* * *

The market square of Vell is a long cobbled bowl between the Assize Court and the old wool exchange, and by midmorning it fills with the honest noise of a city pretending the world is simple — fishwives crying herring, a knife-grinder's wheel singing, children weaving between cart wheels with stolen apples. I had stood in that square a thousand times. I had sold luck-knots at its edge every Thawday for three years, smiling my shopkeeper's smile, listening to the sparrows gossip above the awnings and saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing until silence became the shape of my soul.

Today I climbed onto the fountain at the square's center. The fountain had run dry since the occupation began — the Inquisitorate had ruled public fountains wasteful — and its dry basin made a poor pulpit, cold stone stained with pigeon filth and older, darker stains I chose not to examine. I stood on the rim. My legs shook. I let them.

For a long moment nobody noticed. That was the mercy of markets: they are deaf with their own commerce. Then a fishwife looked up, frowned, elbowed her neighbor. A knot of quiet spread outward from the fountain like ice forming on a pond, and within the space of twenty breaths the whole square had turned its face to me, and the only sound was the knife-grinder's wheel spinning down, down, into silence.

I looked out at them. Bakers and beggars, a sergeant of the watch with his hand drifting toward his baton, a woman holding a child on her hip, an old man whose face I knew from Cooper's Row, who had bought a mended-bone charm from me for his hip and never once asked why it worked. All of them had passed my stall. All of them had smiled at me. Not one of them would have lifted a finger if the Inquisitors had taken me, because not one of them had known there was anything to lift a finger for. That was the arithmetic of fear, and I had done the same sums myself, so I could not hate them for it. I had hidden too. Hiding was the city's native tongue.

"My name is Tamsin Sterling," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, because I had not spoken my own name aloud in truth since my mother died. "I kept a charm shop on Cooper's Row. I told you the charms were tricks. Knots and bones and superstition. I told you that because telling you the truth would have gotten me burned."

A murmur went through the crowd. Somewhere near the Assize Court steps, two grey-cloaked Inquisitors stopped and turned. I saw them see me. I saw one of them start forward, and I saw the other catch his sleeve, because even then — before any of them understood what was coming — the square had begun to fill with wings.

* * *

It started with the pigeons.

Every pigeon in Vell came off the rooftops at once, a grey clatter of them, and instead of scattering they settled — on the fountain's rim around my feet, on the awnings, on the shoulders of statues, on the hat of the astonished knife-grinder. Then the sparrows came down in a soft brown snowfall. Then the gulls off the river, screaming once and going silent. Dogs slipped their chains or simply lay down in the middle of streets and stared toward the square. Cats appeared on windowsills in rows, tails wrapped, watching. From the drains and gutters came the rats — hundreds of them, thousands, pouring out into the daylight without fear, without hunger, sitting up on their haunches in the gutters with their whiskers trembling, and the people of Vell pressed back against the walls and made the sounds people make when the world tilts.

And above it all, silent, the birds. The whole sky over the square was feathered. The light came down through wings.

"They can hear me," I said, and the square went quieter than a tomb, because my voice was not the only voice anymore. When I spoke now, the beasts answered, and every human ear in that square could hear both sides of the conversation, and there was no room left in the world for pretending otherwise.

The old raven — she had flown down from the Thornwalk behind me, one of the wren's kin in spirit if not in blood, a warden of the new covenant's outer marches — dropped from the eave of the wool exchange and landed on the fountain beside my boot.

"He speaks true," the raven said, in a voice like a hinge that had never been oiled, and the square heard it, heard every syllable, and a woman near the front sat down hard on the cobbles. "We have known his blood since before these walls were walls. He is the last speaker of the Sterling line, and the covenant is reforged, and we are come to witness him say it."

"Tamsin Sterling." The voice came from the Assize Court steps, and I knew it before I turned, the way you know a blade by the sound it makes leaving a sheath. Maren Vale walked down into the square with six Inquisitors at her back and the morning light making her grey cloak look almost silver. She had ridden hard from the Spire — there was ash still on her boots, and something else in her face, something I had seen there in the threshold of the old magic and never expected to see on a street: doubt, worn smooth by effort, like a stone a river has been working on for years. "By the authority of the Assize, you will come down from there."

"No," I said.

The word fell into the silence and rang. Around us, ten thousand animal eyes did not blink.

"Inquisitor General." I stayed on the fountain's rim. My heart was slamming so hard I was sure she could hear it, and I let it slam, because hiding it would have been one more lie and I had come here to be finished with lies forever. "Three days ago you stood inside the Spire. You saw what I saw. You heard what the old magic keeps — what it kept *contained*, all those centuries, while the covenant held. You know what comes through the cracks now that it's dying. You watched it try to give you back your sister."

"Don't." One word, flat as a slammed door. Behind her, the Inquisitors shifted.

"You know the purges didn't stop the feral magic," I went on, louder, turning so the whole square could hear, giving my voice away like bread. "They fed it. Every practitioner burned fed it. Every name struck from a ledger starved the covenant that bound it. My mother burned for it. My friend died for it, three days ago, taking a blow meant for me. And you—" I looked at her straight. "You know I'm not lying. That's the worst of it, isn't it, Maren? You've built thirty years of honest law on a lie you personally know is a lie, because you saw the truth with your own eyes and it didn't kill you. It just asked you to change."

The square held its breath. The pigeons on the fountain's rim held theirs too — I could feel their small hearts hammering against my awareness like rain on a roof.

Vale stood very still. I watched her hand at her side open and close, open and close, around nothing — around, I thought, the shape of a glass case, and a splinter of thorn that moved on its own, and a voice that had spoken to her in the dark in her dead sister's cadence, offering her everything back.

"You could order your men to take me," I said gently. "Some of them would. And the birds would remember, and the dogs would remember, and the rats in your own courthouse walls would remember, and every child in this city would grow up knowing that the day the animals testified, the Inquisitor General drew steel on a man standing in a fountain. Or—" I spread my hands. "You could say what you saw."

For a long moment — long enough that a gull cried twice over the wool exchange — Maren Vale said nothing at all.

Then she reached into her cloak and drew out the glass case. Small, iron-bound, the size of a prayer book. Inside it, green and alive after all these months in captivity, a single thorn splinter turned slowly, like a finger pointing at each of us in turn.

"This moved," she said, and her voice carried the way voices carry when a woman has decided to spend everything on one sentence. "In my keeping, sealed, for eleven months, it moved. I told myself it was trickery. I told myself a great many things." She looked out across the square — at the rats, at the birds, at the fishwife with the child, at me. "Three nights past I stood in the root-halls beneath the Spire, and the old magic showed me the night my sister died. All of it. What killed her, and what would have saved her, and which of those things my Inquisitorate has spent thirty years doing." Her jaw tightened. "I am not a woman who enjoys being wrong. But I am less a woman who enjoys serving the thing that killed her family. So hear me."

She raised her voice then, full and clear, the parade-ground voice that had commanded purges across three provinces, and gave it to the other side:

"By my authority as Inquisitor General of Vell and the Marches — which authority I hereby surrender even as I use it — the writs of purge are void. Every warrant. Every list. Every burning ordered in the name of honest law. There is no crime in speaking with beasts. There never was. There is only a debt, and it is ours to pay." She let the glass case fall from her hand. It cracked on the cobbles, and the thorn splinter rolled free, took root in a seam between the stones before our eyes, and put out one small, defiant leaf.

The square did not cheer. Cities are too old and too tired to cheer; cheering is for villages. What happened instead was worse and better: the silence broke all at once into a hundred private weathers — sobbing, laughter, furious argument, a baker dropping to his knees, the old man from Cooper's Row pushing through the crowd to grip my hand and say, over and over, *it worked, your charms, they actually worked, you crooked wonderful liar.* The sergeant of the watch sat down on a bollard and put his head in his hands. Two of Vale's Inquisitors walked away without being dismissed. The rest stayed, looking at their commander as if seeing her for the first time, which, in fairness, they were.

And the birds rose. All of them, together, a sound like the sky tearing open and mending, wheeling once over the square and streaming north and east and west along the rooftops — carrying it, I understood, carrying the news the way they had once carried warnings, wing to wing, to every hedge-witch in hiding, every beast-speaker in a cellar, every creature that had waited a generation in the thorn-shadow for one human voice to say the true thing out loud.

I climbed down from the fountain on legs that barely belonged to me, and the crowd parted, and Maren Vale stood alone in the middle of the square with her authority lying shattered at her feet, and I did the only thing left that frightened me more than anything that had come before.

I offered her my hand.

She looked at it for a long time. "I burned your mother's coven," she said quietly. "I signed the order. I was younger then. It seemed clean."

"It never was," I said. "But it can end clean. That's more than most wounds get."

She took my hand. Her grip was iron and her eyes were wet and furious, and neither of us said anything else, because some treaties are signed in silence, and this — this uneasy, honest, imperfect peace — was the covenant's second half, the half written in ink instead of sap, and it needed two signatures even if one of them trembled.

* * *

Dusk found me on Cooper's Row, standing in the ashes of my own shop.

The Inquisitorate had burned it thoroughly, professionally, the way they burned everything — but the walls still stood, black as charcoal sketches of themselves, and the little counter where I had smiled for three years was a slab of char with a luck-knot's brass ring fused to its edge. I crouched and picked up the ring. It was warm. Everything was warm. Under the rubble, in the seams, green shoots had already come up — thorn-shoots, covenant-quick, curling through the ash like handwriting. The new pact was already reading my ruin back to me.

"You'll rebuild," said a voice above me, small and sharp and achingly familiar, and my heart broke and healed in the same instant.

She was a wren — of course she was a wren — but a young one, this year's brood, with the same cocked head and the same imperious black eye as the one who had died on the Spire's steps with her wings spread over me. Her granddaughter, the ravens told me. Warden-blood ran in the line now, bound to mine, bound to the reforged pact.

"The shop?" I said. "Or me?"

"Both. Neither. You'll build something new and it won't be a shop exactly and you won't be exactly you, and that's the whole point, Sterling, honestly, must everything be explained to you?" She hopped down onto the charred counter, right onto the fused brass ring, and looked at me with the old wren's exact expression of tolerant exasperation. "The Thornwalk's edge is moving. Did you know that? A mile closer to Vell since the reforging. The stags say it will reach the walls by spring. Some of the stags think that's a threat." She fluffed her feathers. "I told them it's a handshake. We'll see who's right."

I looked west, where the sun was going down red behind the city, and beyond the city, where the dark line of the forest had crept visibly nearer across the fallow fields. Green over broken stone. Roots under walls. A city full of people who had heard animals speak that morning and would spend the rest of their lives deciding what to believe about it.

"There's a toll-keeper on the north road," I said slowly. "Brannoc. He sold our route to Vale for a promise about his daughter. The girl the old magic took, years ago." I turned the brass ring over in my fingers. "The searchers found her alive inside the fire line, changed. If anyone in this realm knows what the Thornwalk does with the people it takes — whether they can come home, and what home means to them after — it's him. And he owes an answer to more people than just himself."

The young wren tilted her head. "Going to go collect?"

"I'm going to go listen," I said. "That's the job now. That's the whole job."

Above Cooper's Row, the first stars came out, and in the eaves of the ruined shop — already, impossibly, that same evening — a wren began arranging twigs.
