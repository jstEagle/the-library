## 22. Spire and Thorn Again

The frost came early that year, silvering the thorn hedges along the road from Vell, and on the morning I opened the shop for the first time in its new life, the whole Thornwalk edge glittered like a thing made of glass and patience.

I had built it myself — or rather, I had begun it, and then the forest had finished it. That was the way of things now. I set the first stones of the foundation in spring, and by midsummer the walls had grown a skin of living wood where my mortar was thin, as if the Thornwalk had leaned over the boundary line to help, the way an old woman leans over a garden fence to correct a neighbor's pruning. The roof was slate I'd carried up from the ruined quarry, but between the slates, small green shoots insisted on emerging, and I had stopped pulling them out. In winter they slept. In summer the shop breathed.

I called it what it had always been: Sterling's Charms. But the sign over the door was honest now, carved in both tongues — the common script of Vell above, and beneath it, in letters of pale sapwood that darkened and brightened with the seasons, the old language of the Spire. *Sterling's Charms,* said the top. *And True,* said the bottom. Hollowroot had chosen the second line himself, laughing in that rustling voice of his, because he said that honesty was the only charm worth selling anymore.

He stood beside the door now, taller than me — which is to say, taller than a boy of nine has any right to be, though he was not a boy, whatever shape he wore. A year ago he had been a seed no bigger than my fist, planted in the last living soil at the Spire's heart while Dace's blood soaked into the ground beside him and Vale watched with her sister's name finally spoken aloud. Now he was a sapling given hands, bark-smooth skin gone warm and human-brown, hair like a crown of new leaves that never quite fell even in the cold. He had grown a foot since harvest. He would grow another before spring. He measured himself against the doorframe every morning and marked it with a thumbnail, and every morning he laughed at how much room there still was above his head.

"Your sign is crooked," he said, in the common tongue. His accent in it was still strange — each word arrived slightly green, slightly damp, like something pulled fresh from the earth. "The left side droops."

"The left side *grows*," I corrected. "There's a difference."

"It droops with intent."

"Everything here does." I stepped out into the blue morning and squinted up at the eaves. "Speaking of intent — your cousins are awake."

* * *

The wrens came back in the third week of March, three of them, and I knew before they sang whose children they were. There was a cadence in their morning chatter that I had heard for years through the bars of a cage in a shop that no longer existed — a quick, interrogative trill, like a question asked twice because the asker already knew the answer. The wren who had named a mud-caked child *Hollowroot* in the dark of a curfew storm had died at the Gate of Roots with a crossbow bolt through her small body, shielding me with the only thing she had left to give, which was her position between me and the arrow. She had been a covenant-warden bound to my bloodline, older than kingdoms, wearing feathers. And she had died in the space of one heartbeat, and I had carried her body in my cupped hands for two days because I could not make myself bury her, and when I finally did, at the roots of the first oak inside the Thornwalk, the whole forest had gone silent from edge to edge, the way a street goes silent when a beloved shopkeeper dies.

Her descendants nested in my eaves now. They were not wardens — the covenant had changed, and the old bindings had loosened into something more like kinship — but they kept the habits. They scolded me when I mispriced things. They screamed warnings when customers lied. And every dawn they sat on the ridgepole in a row and sang the old tongue down at the valley, syllables no human throat should know, taught to them by a mother who had learned them from a tree that remembered everything.

"They're arguing about the nest site again," Hollowroot said, tilting his head. His ears moved when he listened — not visibly, not the way a beast's do, but I could feel it, a faint lean in the air around him, the whole of him turning toward sound like a plant toward light. "The eldest wants the north corner. The young one wants to build over the door so they can watch the customers."

"The young one has better commercial instincts than I ever did."

"She says you undercharge."

"She says I undercharge everyone except people who deserve it, which is most people, which is why we eat." I smiled and let the thought settle into the place where it lived now, warm and permanent: *we.* For three years in Vell I had eaten alone behind a counter, listening to a caged wren whisper warnings I pretended not to hear, and the loneliness of that life had been so ordinary I hadn't known it was loneliness until it ended. Now there was a sapling-child marking his height on my doorframe and wrens in my eaves and wolves who trotted down from the high passes in winter just to sit in my yard and watch the firelight through the window, and I had stopped being surprised by any of it, which was perhaps the strangest gift of all.

* * *

The first customer of the day came up the road from Vell just as the sun cleared the thorn hedges: a broad-shouldered woman with a fish cart and a face I knew, because her cart had once carried a hidden boy north out of a burning city while she stared straight ahead and whistled.

"Marta," I said. "You're early."

"You're open early." She tied the cart to the post — the post groaned; the Thornwalk had opinions about being tied to — and stomped snow off her boots. "Everyone's early these days. Half of Vell can't sleep. They say the hedge-witches are lighting candles in windows now. Openly. Green candles." She said it the way she said everything, flat and practical, but her eyes went past me to Hollowroot, and softened the way they always did. "Morning, sapling."

"Morning, Marta. Your daughter's cough is better?"

"Aye, thanks to your mended-lung charm, which I notice you charged me half of nothing for." She dug in her coat and produced a wrapped bundle, thrusting it at me with the aggression of the genuinely grateful. "Smoked trout. Don't argue. And I'll have two luck-knots, real ones, none of your old rope-tricks, and—" she lowered her voice, glancing at the road behind her, empty and white and singing with frost, "—one of the listening charms. For my sister. Her man's been... quiet lately. Wrong kind of quiet."

I took the order without flinching, though the word *listening* still landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. Once, *listening* had been the word that killed. My mother had listened to bees, and they had burned her for it in Vell's great square while I hid in a cellar and memorized the sound of the crowd so I could spend the rest of my life never making it. Three years I had sold knots and bones and called them luck, magicless, harmless, a lie so small I could hold it in my mouth all day without biting down.

Then I had stood in that same square, one year and some months ago, with the covenant newly reforged and the Spire humming at my back, and I had opened my mouth and spoken to every animal in Vell at once — the horses, the dogs, the rats in the walls, the gulls screaming over the fish market — and told them, in front of the whole city, that I heard them too. And Inquisitor General Maren Vale, bound by what she had witnessed inside the Spire's threshold, bound by truth the way other officers were bound by law, had formally ended the purges rather than deny what she had seen. Not forgiven. Not gentle. Vale did not do gentle. But she had stood in the square with her scarred hands folded and declared, in a voice that carried to the walls, that the age of burning was over, and that anyone who lit a pyre now would answer to her.

She still sent me a letter, once a season. Short ones. The last one had said only: *The splinter in the glass case has gone still. I am told this means peace. I find I do not know what to do with peace. — M.V.* I had written back: *Neither did I. It gets easier. Sometimes it doesn't. Plant something.* Last week a courier brought word that the Inquisitor General's office in Vell now had a window box of thorn roses, grown from a cutting I had sent, tended by the most feared woman in the realm, who watered them herself and told no one.

I made Marta's charms at the back bench while Hollowroot waited on the front step, because customers liked being greeted by him and he liked greeting them, and because — this was the part that still caught in my throat, every time — the charms I made now were true. Luck-knots woven with a thread of intention, mended-bone charms sung over until the bone remembered its wholeness, listening charms that were exactly what they sounded like. No more rope-tricks. No more small lies. My mother's craft, practiced in daylight, priced fairly, sold to farmers and fishermen and one former toll-keeper who came every month and never quite met my eye.

* * *

Brannoc arrived at noon, as he always did, walking the long road from his bridge with his collar up and his guilt worn smooth as river stone.

We had never spoken of it directly, what he had done — selling our route to Vale for a promise about his daughter. There had been no need. He had found her after the fires, alive inside the Thornwalk's edge, changed, remembering the Spire fondly, speaking sometimes in her sleep in the old tongue. And I had come to understand, somewhere on the long road between betrayal and forgiveness, that everyone pays for survival, and that Brannoc's payment had simply been made in a coin I hadn't expected. He paid still. Every visit, he paid a little more.

"Toll-keeper," I said, using his old title, because he liked that.

"Charm-seller." He set a small sack of bridge-minted copper on the counter — full price, always full price, his penance measured in exact change — and then, instead of ordering, he stood there working his jaw like a man chewing a stone.

Hollowroot appeared silently beside him, the way he did, and put a hand briefly on the old man's arm. "She's well," the sapling said. "Sela. She sends word through the birches. She wants you to know the dreams have stopped hurting."

Brannoc's breath went out of him all at once, and he gripped the counter and looked at the floorboards — living boards, faintly green-veined — and nodded, and nodded, and could not speak. I busied myself with the luck-knots and gave him the privacy of my turned back, and when he finally raised his head his eyes were wet and furious about it, and he bought three charms he didn't need and left one on the counter deliberately, and walked home lighter than he'd come, which was the only interest I ever charged him.

"He'll be all right," Hollowroot said quietly.

"He'll be all right slowly."

"That's the only way anyone is." The sapling looked out the window at the white road, at the distant smoke of Vell rising thin and gray against the winter sky, and something moved across his young-old face — a shadow of the seed's memory, maybe, of the dying pact and the feral magic straining at its seams, of the world we had nearly lost. "Tamsin. The Thornwalk sent me a message this morning. Before you woke."

I set down the knot I was weaving. Messages from the Thornwalk before dawn were rarely idle. "What kind of message?"

"The trees at the far edge — the western march, past the ashfields — say the ground is moving." He turned, and his leaf-crown stirred though there was no wind indoors. "Not breaking. Not feral. *Moving.* Roots uncoiling after a long sleep. Something old is waking under the ashfields, and it isn't angry, but it is *large*, and the stags think someone should go and listen before it decides to stop waiting to be heard."

Outside, the three wrens on the ridgepole went silent all at once, heads cocked toward the west, asking their question twice.

I looked at my shop — my honest shop, my breathing walls, my life rebuilt from the ashes of the one I'd burned — and felt the old dread rise in me and meet, for the first time, no resistance at all. Just a man reaching for his coat.

"Well," I said, and smiled at the last heir of the Spire, standing in the doorway of the house we'd made together. "Get your boots, Hollowroot. Let's go listen."
