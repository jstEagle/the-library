## 1. The Quiet Valley

The frost came to Thornvale the way gossip came: early, quietly, and settling into everything before anyone thought to object.

Quentin Halloran woke before dawn, as she always did, to the sound of the valley breathing. That was how she thought of it — the long exhale of wind down from the Grayfell crags, moving through the pines and over the rooftops of the town below, carrying woodsmoke and cold stone and the particular silence that Thornvale wore like a badge of honor. No magic here. No charms humming in doorframes, no wards ticking like clockwork, none of the strange bright wrongness that travelers sometimes brought with them from beyond the pass. Thornvale had been scoured clean three hundred years ago, or so the story went, and its people had spent every generation since polishing the emptiness until it shone.

*Clean hands,* her grandmother used to say. *That's what a Thornvale man brags about. Clean hands and an empty house.*

Her grandmother had also taught her to carve bone.

Quentin rose, dressed in the dark, and built up the fire in the small hearth of the cottage she'd inherited along with the carving tools and the silence. Outside, the first gray light was finding the edges of things: the fence posts, the well cover, the row of winter squash still sitting on her windowsill because she kept forgetting to bring them in. Frost had furred them white overnight. They looked like small pale skulls, and she looked at them a moment too long, the way she always did, before turning away.

The workshop was attached to the back of the cottage, a low room her grandfather had built for tanning and her grandmother had claimed for finer work. It smelled of linseed oil and bone dust and, faintly, of the tallow candles Quentin preferred to the newfangled lamps the trader caravans sold. She lit two candles, set out her tools on the leather apron spread across the bench — the fine knife with the antler handle, the rasps, the burnishing stone worn smooth as a river pebble — and took from the shelf above the bench the day's material.

A knucklebone of ox, cured and cleaned, waiting to become a name-marker for old Tam Wetherby's cow.

Not *that* Tam. A different Tam. Thornvale had perhaps forty souls named some variation of Tam, which Quentin privately considered proof that the valley's founders had run short of imagination somewhere around the second generation.

She sat, and she breathed, and she did what she did every morning of her working life: she listened to whether anything spoke.

Most days, nothing did. The dead of Thornvale were quiet dead, settled dead, the kind that dissolved into the ground they were buried in without complaint. The valley's emptiness extended, apparently, even past the grave. Whatever gift — sickness, inheritance, affliction, call it what you liked — lived in Quentin's hands needed something to hold onto. Bone was best. Wood would do in a pinch. Antler, horn, teeth. Once, desperately, a piece of chalk, when old Widow Sorrel's son died in the rockslide and there was nothing else to hand and the widow stood in Quentin's doorway wringing her apron and saying *he never told me where he put the deed, he never told me where he put it.*

Quentin had carved the deed's hiding place into a piece of chalk while the widow watched, and the widow had gone home and dug up her floorboards and found it, and then the widow had gone to Maren the healer and said *the Halloran girl speaks through stones now,* and Maren had come and looked at Quentin for a long time with those flat gray eyes and said nothing at all.

That had been four years ago. Quentin had been more careful since.

She picked up the knucklebone and turned it in her fingers. Warm it first, her grandmother had always said. Bone remembers being alive; give it a reason to remember kindly. So she rolled it between her palms until it lost the chill of the shelf, and she set the tip of her finest knife against it, and she began to carve the cow's name — *Bramble*, because farmers in Thornvale named their cows after hedgerow plants and had done since time out of mind — in the looping script her grandmother had taught her, the one that wasn't quite any alphabet the valley used.

And because it was morning, and she was tired, and the fire was warm, and no one was watching —

— she let herself listen.

Nothing came. Just the ordinary sounds of the workshop: the tick of cooling ash in the hearth, wind worrying the eaves, somewhere far off the first rooster making its case for dawn. Quentin carved *Bramble* into the bone and felt the familiar small ache behind her sternum that was either disappointment or relief, depending on the hour, and decided it was relief, this morning, definitely relief, because today was market day and market days required a steady hand and an untroubled face.

She finished the marker, burnished it, strung it on the leather cord Tam Wetherby had left with a copper coin as payment, and hung it by the door with the other completed commissions: a charm-bone for the miller's new horse, a set of buttons for the cooper's wife who liked the way bone caught candlelight, a memorial bead for a grave nobody visited anymore, carved years ago and never collected, because the family had moved over the pass and Quentin had never had the heart to melt it down for stock.

She looked at that bead a moment longer than necessary. It hummed, very faintly, under her fingertips. Not words. Just presence. The way a room feels different after someone has just left it.

*You're still in there,* she murmured to it. *I know.*

Then she banked the fire, wrapped up, and went out into the waking valley.

Thornvale in autumn was a place that seemed designed to prove a point. Every field was tidy. Every lane was swept. The wardstones that ringed the valley — twelve of them, squat gray pillars older than any record — stood at their intervals like sentries, and every child in the valley learned to touch one before they could walk, learned the catechism before they could read: *No magic within, no magic without, the vale is clean, the vale is whole.* Quentin had recited it a thousand times as a girl, standing on a stool beside her grandmother, and had never once believed it applied to her, because surely if it applied to her, someone would have noticed. Surely the stones themselves would have hummed, or cracked, or turned her away at the ford.

They hadn't. Which meant either the stones didn't care, or — and this was the thought she kept in a locked room at the back of her mind and visited only at night — the stones couldn't tell the difference between a gift and a sickness, and neither could the valley.

Market day filled the square by midmorning. Quentin set up her stall between the wheelwright and the honey-seller, laid out her wares on clean cloth, and prepared to spend six hours being unremarkable. This was a skill, being unremarkable. She'd practiced it the way other people practiced fiddle or archery. She knew exactly how much to smile, exactly how long to hold eye contact, exactly how to laugh at jokes about the weather so that no one ever looked at her hands twice.

"Morning, Quentin." Tam Wetherby, collecting his cow-charm, all beard and suspicion. "Carved it proper? Last one you did for Joss Fenwick's goat went crooked inside a month."

"The goat chewed it," Quentin said. "Bone isn't for chewing."

"Hmph." He turned the marker over in his thick fingers. "Does it work?"

"It works."

"What I mean is—" He lowered his voice, glancing around, though there was nothing to glance at but turnips. "It's not one of them *charmed* things, is it. Not like what they sell over the pass. You carve plain, don't you. Plain bone, plain marks."

"Plain as bread," Quentin said, and smiled her market-day smile, and hated herself a little, and watched him pay and go.

This was the arrangement. This was the whole architecture of her life: she made beautiful things from bone, and the valley bought them because they were beautiful and because the Hallorans had always made them, and no one asked why a name-marker carved by Quentin Halloran seemed to keep livestock healthier than anyone else's, or why her memorial beads were said to sit easier on grieving hearts, or why the horses she carved little protection-teeth for never went lame. People didn't ask, in Thornvale. Asking questions was how you found answers, and answers about magic were how valleys got frightened.

Maren the healer passed the stall around noon, basket on her arm, gray eyes doing their slow inventory of the square. She stopped. Of course she stopped.

"Quentin."

"Maren."

"You look tired."

"I'm always tired in autumn."

"Mm." Maren's gaze moved over the stall, the wares, the neat rows of bone. It lingered, briefly, on the memorial bead Quentin had decided at the last minute not to bring. "My grandmother used to say the Halloran women had listening hands. Old wives' talk. Bone-whispering, she called it." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Superstition, of course."

"Of course," Quentin said.

"But superstition grows from somewhere, doesn't it. Like mushrooms. In the dark, on old rot." Maren smiled, and it reached her eyes the way frost reaches a window — all over the surface, none of the way in. "Take care of your hands, Quentin. They're clever hands. The valley values clever hands." She touched the edge of the stall, once, lightly, the way you'd touch a doorframe to see if it was solid. "As long as they stay *clean* hands."

Then she moved on, greeting the honey-seller, and Quentin stood in the cold sun and discovered that her heart was going like a rabbit's and her smile hadn't moved at all.

*Mushrooms in the dark, on old rot.* Four years of carefulness, and Maren had never stopped watching. Had maybe never once stopped.

That night, back in the workshop, Quentin sat a long time without lighting the candles. The dark was total except for the frost-light coming through the window, blue-white, the color of held breath. Winter was close. She could feel it the way the whole valley felt it, in the shortened days and the smoke lying flat under the eaves, and beneath that, underneath everything, she felt something else — something she had been feeling for weeks now and telling herself was nothing.

The dead were restless lately. Not speaking, exactly. Not yet. But pressing. Like sleepers turning over in a bed too small for them. She'd felt it in every piece of bone she'd touched for a month, a low murmur just below the threshold of meaning, dozens of voices or one voice, she couldn't tell, saying something she couldn't hear. Even the settled dead of Thornvale, the quiet ones, the ones who had never given her trouble in thirty years of listening — even they had begun to stir, like dogs lifting their heads before a storm arrives.

And there was something else. Something new. For three nights running she had dreamed of snow falling upward, and of a country she had no name for, green and gold and burning, and of a woman's voice saying a word over and over that was not a word in any language Quentin knew, and waking with her own lips shaped around syllables that meant nothing and would not stop meaning.

She lit the candles at last. Took a scrap of antler from the offcut bin — a broken piece, good for nothing, safe for nothing, which was why she chose it — and held it in both hands.

"Who's asking?" she whispered. It was the question her grandmother had taught her first, before any technique, before any craft. *When they press at you, girl, don't ask what they want. Ask who's asking. Names are doors.*

The antler warmed. The murmur rose. And for the first time in her life, the answer came clear enough to almost catch:

"—not here, we are not *here*, no one carries us, no one carries us *home*—"

Then it was gone, like a wave drawing back, leaving the workshop silent and Quentin sitting very still with the antler in her shaking hands and frost flowering up the window glass outside, earlier this year than any winter in living memory.

She should have burned the antler. She knew that, even then. Her grandmother's rules were iron: what presses, release it; what asks, refuse it; the valley must never hear you listening.

But the voice had said *home*. And Quentin Halloran, who belonged to a valley that would exile her if it ever heard the truth in her hands, sat alone in the blue frost-light holding a piece of broken antler, and realized she had never once in her life been asked for anything by anything — and that some starved, stubborn part of her had already answered *yes*.

Outside, far to the north, over the pass, the sky had begun, very faintly, to glow.
