## 4. The Healer's Verdict

The trouble began, as most troubles in Thornvale did, with a knock at the door.

Quentin heard it through the floorboards — three raps, measured and unhurried, the knock of someone who had never once been made to wait. She knew that knock the way she knew the ache in her hands on damp mornings. Maren Solvane, healer of Thornvale, keeper of the valley's ledger of small mercies and smaller suspicions.

Quentin set down her knife. On the workbench before her lay half a name-marker, birchwood this time, because the stranger had deserved better than pine and Quentin had nothing else of her but a name pulled from frost-cracked lips: *Ilsa Vey*. The letters were rough still, unfinished, and the wood was quiet — which was its own kind of wrong. Wood never spoke to her. Only bone did, bone and antler and the grey river-stones people sometimes mistook for skulls. But since the night at the ford, everything she touched had gone faintly murmurous, like a house where someone has just stopped talking when you enter the room.

She covered the marker with a cloth. She covered the map case with her shawl. Then she opened the door and put on the face she wore to market, the one that said *nothing here but bread and weather*.

"Quentin." Maren stood on the step with snowmelt dripping off the eaves behind her, though no snow had fallen yet. Her grey braid was pinned up tight, her satchel hung square across her body, and her eyes went past Quentin's shoulder into the house the way water goes into cracks — patiently, finding every low place. "You didn't come to the well yesterday."

"I was working."

"You're always working." Maren stepped inside without being asked, because in Thornvale nobody asked; doors there were considered polite suggestions rather than boundaries. She sniffed the air — literally sniffed it, the way she did over sickbeds — and her gaze snagged on the cloth-covered shape on the workbench. "Someone died?"

"A traveler. At the ford." Quentin kept her voice level, kept her hands loose at her sides. "Frostbite took her before I could do anything. I'm making her a marker."

Maren crossed the room and lifted the cloth before Quentin could stop her, and there it was: *ILSA VEY*, half-carved, the V still raw-edged. Maren read it the way she read symptoms. Slowly. Twice.

"Ilsa Vey," she said. "Not from here."

"No."

"Where from?"

"I don't know." That much was true, or true enough. What Quentin knew — what the bones had begun telling her in the three nights since — sat in her chest like swallowed river ice. *Cindral*, the dead woman whispered. *The map. It chose you.* A country with no name in any book Quentin had ever seen, a country the whispers spoke of the way drowning people speak of shore.

Maren set the marker down with exaggerated care, the care one gives something possibly contagious. "And her things? Travelers carry things. Papers. Coin. The council will want them recorded."

"They'll be recorded." Quentin heard the flatness creep into her own voice and could not quite pull it back. "When I've finished the marker. The dead deserve their names before the living deserve their inventories."

Something flickered across Maren's face — not anger. Something older and wearier, the look of a woman who had been waiting years for a door to open so she could walk through it. "Your grandmother used to say things like that," she said softly. "Right before the end."

* * *

Maren Solvane had been healer of Thornvale for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had kept two ledgers. The first was public: births, fevers, broken arms, the ordinary arithmetic of a valley that prided itself on needing nothing it couldn't grow, mend, or reason out. The second lived only in her head, and it was longer.

In the second ledger were the small wrongnesses. The year old Piet Hallow's cow birthed a two-headed calf and Piet swore he'd dreamed it first, three nights running, and Maren had noted the dream and not the calf. The winter the millpond froze in perfect rings, seven of them, concentric as fingerprints. The way Quentin Halloran's grandmother had gone into the burial ground alone every quarter-day and come out with sawdust on her skirts and an expression like a woman leaving confession. And after her — the way Quentin, barely grown, had taken up the same solitary pilgrimages, the same sawdust, the same face.

Maren did not believe in magic. That was the valley's whole creed, carved into the wardstones at every pass: *Thornvale keeps clean.* No hexes, no hedge-craft, no whispering dead. What Maren believed in was patterns. And Quentin Halloran was a pattern twenty years long, and patterns, left unexamined, became traditions, and traditions became rot.

Three days ago, a stranger had frozen to death at the south ford clutching a leather map case, and Tam Oster's boy had run to fetch the healer, and by the time Maren arrived the body had already been tended — eyes closed, hands folded, a blanket tucked with a tenderness that told her exactly who had gotten there first. Quentin Halloran, kneeling in the shallows, soaked to the waist, holding the dead woman's hand and saying nothing.

Maren had watched her for a long moment from the alder bank. Watched Quentin's lips move. Watched the dead woman's fingers — and Maren would swear this on any ledger you liked — twitch, just once, like a sleeper disturbed by a voice.

Now she stood in Quentin's kitchen, and the pattern was nearly complete, and all that remained was to see the last piece with her own eyes.

"The council will want to know about the traveler," she said. "Cause of death. Where she came from. Whether she carried sickness."

"She carried cold. That's all."

"And the map?" Maren let the word fall into the room like a stone into a well, and listened for the splash.

Quentin's face did something very small and very controlled. "What map?"

"The one she was clutching hard enough to crack the leather. Tam's boy saw it. He tells everyone everything; you know how children are." Maren moved toward the chair by the hearth where Quentin had draped her shawl, and Quentin moved too — too fast, a half-step, her hand rising — and that was answer enough, but Maren lifted the shawl anyway, because a healer learns early that the body always confesses before the mouth does.

The map case was warm.

That was the thing Maren would remember afterward, in the long bad hours: not the maps themselves, though they were strange enough — roads drawn in ink that shifted like oil on water, borders marked in a language she almost recognized and then couldn't, a country's shape that seemed to rearrange itself when looked at directly, the way a fish does underwater. Not even the name stamped into the leather, worn nearly smooth: a word in no alphabet she knew, though some traitor part of her mind kept trying to pronounce it. *Cindral.*

It was the warmth. Leather left in a cold house should be cold. This was warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a hand, and when Maren's fingers closed around it she felt — distinctly, undeniably — a pulse. Faint. Patient. Like something under the skin of the world, waiting to be noticed.

She dropped it as if it had bitten her.

The case hit the floorboards and fell open, and the map spilled out, and in the firelight the ink moved — flowed, gathered, reformed — and for one naked instant Maren Solvane, who did not believe in magic, saw a road draw itself across blank vellum like a vein filling with blood, and heard, rising out of the walls and the floor and the half-carved wooden marker on the bench, a sound like many voices breathing one breath.

Then Quentin snatched the map up and the room was only a room again, cold and ordinary, and both women stood trembling in the wreckage of twenty years of careful not-knowing.

* * *

"It's sickness," Maren said at last, because the alternative was unthinkable, and Maren had built her whole life on thinking the thinkable. "Whatever it is. It's in the house. It's in you."

"Maren—"

"Don't." The healer's voice cracked like ice underfoot. She pressed her back against the door, putting distance between herself and the workbench, between herself and Quentin, and Quentin saw — really saw, for the first time — fear in the face of a woman who had lanced abscesses and set hips and held the dying through the worst nights of their lives without flinching. "Twenty years, Quentin. Twenty years I've watched you and said nothing, because your grandmother vouched for you, because you mended fences and sat with the dying and never once made trouble. I told myself it was grief. A girl who talks to her dead grandmother's memory — that's sorrow, that's human. But this—" she gestured at the map, now rolled tight in Quentin's white-knuckled fists, "—this is not sorrow. This is the thing the wardstones are for."

"The wardstones keep out magic. This is a dead woman asking to go home."

"Dead women don't ask!" Maren's voice rose, and somewhere outside a dog began to bark. "Dead women lie quiet, and we grieve them, and we go on. That is the order of things. That is why our valley stands when the world outside rots with hexes and hauntings and every other filth the wars left behind. You of all people know what's out there. Your grandmother crossed the passes. She never spoke of it, but she woke screaming for ten years, and whatever she brought home with her, she buried it in that burial ground, and now you're digging it up!"

"She didn't bring anything home," Quentin said quietly. "She was already carrying it. It's called inheritance, Maren. It runs in blood, not in borders. Your wardstones never kept me out. I was born inside them."

The words landed, and Quentin watched them land, watched Maren's face go through disbelief and denial and arrive, horribly fast, at acceptance — because Maren was a pattern-keeper too, and the pattern had just closed.

"That's not possible," Maren said, but she said it the way one reads a terminal symptom aloud. "The valley is clean. The valley has always been clean."

"The valley is clean because I have spent my whole life being quiet." Quentin's voice shook now, and she let it. There was no more use for steadiness. "Every marker I ever carved, every grave I tended, every fevered night I sat with your patients while you slept — I did it quietly, so you could keep believing in your clean valley. I buried your dead with their names in their mouths so they wouldn't haunt you. That was my gift, Maren. That was my whole life. And you're standing in my kitchen calling it filth."

For a moment neither of them moved. The fire ticked. Outside, the dog gave up barking, and the silence that followed was worse than any sound.

Maren straightened her satchel. Squared her shoulders. Became, visibly, the healer of Thornvale again, the officer of a small proud nation, and Quentin understood that whatever happened next would happen not between two women who had known each other twenty years, but between the valley and its sickness.

"There will be a hearing," Maren said. "The council will convene. Elder Brannock will preside." She paused at the threshold, and for one instant the old weariness surfaced again, the look of a woman carrying two ledgers, and she said, not unkindly, "First snow comes within the fortnight. The rite requires snow. Clean snow, falling clean."

"The cleansing rite hasn't been performed in sixty years."

"No," Maren agreed. "It hasn't." And then, softer, almost beneath hearing, almost — Quentin would torture herself with this later, wondering — almost a warning: "You were at the last one. In your grandmother's stories. Ask her ghost, if you can, what was done to the Fenwick child."

The door closed. Quentin stood alone in her kitchen with a warm map against her chest and a hundred voices pressing at the backs of her teeth, and above the village, over the black line of the fells, the sky was gathering itself into the color of a decision.

* * *

They came at dusk the next day — not the mob Quentin had half-feared, which would almost have been easier, but a delegation. Elder Brannock walking first with his white stick and his council chain, then Maren with her ledger open, then six others whose faces Quentin had known since childhood: the cooper's wife who had once paid her in honeycomb for carving her mother's marker; the ferryman's widow whose son's grave Quentin tended still; old Piet Hallow, who owed her three seasons of fence-mending and met her eyes exactly once and then not again.

They stood in her yard in the blue hour, breath steaming, and Elder Brannock read from a paper Quentin had helped him draft sentences for, years ago, in another life.

"...whereas the keeping of unclean craft is contrary to the covenant of Thornvale, and whereas evidence has been presented..." His voice droned on. Quentin listened to the wind instead. Under the wind, faint and growing, she could feel the dead of the valley stirring in their graves like sleepers turning toward a window, listening with her.

"...and whereas mercy is also our covenant, the council offers this: depart before first snow, freely and without return, and no rite need be performed. Or remain, and be cleansed, as the covenant provides."

He lowered the paper. For a moment his official face slipped, and underneath was just an old man who remembered her grandmother bringing him soup through a fever that nearly killed him. "Quentin," he said quietly. "Take the road. Please. Nobody wants the other thing."

Nobody wants the other thing. As if the other thing were weather. As if it weren't six strong men and a basin of salt water and prayers to scrub a person out of herself like a stain from wood.

Quentin looked at them all — the honeycomb woman, the widow, the old man with his stick — and felt something break in her, cleanly, almost painlessly, the way ice breaks when spring finally commits. It wasn't hatred she felt. That was the terrible discovery. It was grief, plain and familiar, the same grief she'd carried her whole life, and she understood at last that she had always been grieving this: the knowledge that home was a place you could live in for twenty years without ever once being *in* it.

"I'll need until tomorrow," she said. "To finish her marker. She gets her name before I go. That's not negotiable."

Brannock nodded, relieved, and the delegation turned to leave, and it was Maren who lingered, Maren who hung back in the darkening yard until the others' lanterns had swung away down the lane.

"The map," Maren said. "Burn it. Before you go. Whatever it is, wherever it leads — a country that erased itself doesn't want finding, and things that don't want finding take a price." Her voice was low, urgent, and Quentin realized with a start that this was the second ledger speaking, the private one, the healer under the officer. "I've read the old accounts, Quentin. Of the war. Of what the Hollow King did to the places that resisted him. Cindral isn't on any map because he *unmade* it, and the last people who went looking for it came back wrong, if they came back at all."

"Then why warn me? If I'm sickness, why not let me walk into it?"

Maren was silent a long moment. Snow-smell rode the wind now, unmistakable, the first breath of it coming down off the fells like a messenger arriving ahead of an army.

"Because your grandmother saved my life once," she said finally, "and I never repaid it, and I am too old to owe debts." She turned away, then stopped. "First snow, Quentin. The council counts it from the moment it touches the wardstone at the south pass. Don't be here when it falls."

Quentin watched her go. Then she went inside, lit her lamp, uncovered the birchwood marker, and picked up her knife.

Under her hands, the wood hummed — not bone, never bone, and yet tonight it murmured anyway, and through the murmur came Ilsa Vey's voice, clearer than it had ever been, patient as falling snow:

*Two days, carver. The pass closes with the snow, and the Door opens only for the map-bearer. We have waited a hundred years. Do not make us wait through another winter.*

Outside, high on the black fells, the first flakes came down — fat, slow, deliberate, counting themselves onto the stone.

Quentin packed her tools.
