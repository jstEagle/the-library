## 5. Leaving by Night

The last night in Thornvale, Quentin did not sleep.

She sat at her workbench with a single candle burning and packed the way her grandmother had taught her to pack for a journey she'd never believed they'd take: everything that mattered first, everything that could be replaced not at all. A spare shirt rolled tight. The good knife. Dried apples wrapped in cloth because Maren had once said Quentin's stomach was too stubborn to remember food on its own.

And the tools.

She laid them out one by one on the bench, each blade worn to the shape of her grip, each handle darkened by years of her palms. The small gouge Grandmother Ashgrove had pressed into her hands when Quentin was nine years old, saying *these are older than you, older than me, older than the valley's pride — treat them like kin.* The bone saw with its teeth like a patient smile. The burnisher of river stone, smooth as a closed eye.

Quentin wrapped them in leather, one by one, the way you'd wrap an infant's fingers against the cold.

*You're really going,* said the voice that lived in the marrow she kept in the jar by the window — old Harl, dead eleven years, who had spent his life complaining about the price of nails and now spent his death doing much the same. *Past the wardstones. Into the wild. You'll freeze before you clear the ridge.*

"Probably," Quentin said aloud, to the empty room, as she had learned to do. Talking to air was safer than talking to bones where neighbors might see. "But I'll freeze carrying something worth the freezing."

*Stubborn girl,* Harl said, without heat. He'd called her that since she was six. It was, she suspected, his way of loving her.

On the table, the map case sat where it had sat for three days, since the frostbitten woman collapsed at the ford and died with Quentin's name — *her* name, a stranger's name — in her mouth. Since she'd pressed the leather into Quentin's hands and whispered *Cindral* like a prayer and a wound at once, and then said: *It chose you.*

Since then the whispers in Quentin's hands had changed key. For twenty-six years the dead of Thornvale had murmured through her carvings — complaints and comforts, gossip and grief, the small weather of small lives. Now there was a new voice beneath them, or perhaps above them, a woman's voice with an accent like frost forming, speaking words Quentin didn't know in a language that nonetheless understood her.

Ilsa. The cartographer. The woman who had drawn a country that no longer existed, and whose ghost now stood — Quentin was almost sure — in the corner of her workshop, watching her pack with an expression Quentin couldn't see but somehow felt: patient, sad, and waiting.

Almost sure. In Thornvale, behind the wardstones, the dead were only voices. Whatever else they were got thinned out crossing the boundary, like light through dirty glass.

Tonight, past the stones, Quentin would find out what she'd been hearing all along.

* * *

She left by the goat path, because the goat path was hers. She'd walked it since childhood, up behind the ash grove where the ground fell away toward the valley wall, and no one questioned a carver going up to gather ash wood for handles. Her bag was small enough to be a woodcutter's satchel. Her coat was plain. Her face, she hoped, was nothing at all.

The valley at night was a held breath. Thornvale lay below her in its bowl of darkness, a scatter of embers and one lit window — Maren's, the healer's, burning late. Quentin looked at that window longer than she meant to.

Three days ago Maren had found the map. Three days ago Maren — who had set Quentin's broken arm at eight, who had brought willow tea through the fever-winter when half the town coughed blood, who had held Quentin's grandmother's hand at the end and wept — had stood in this very workshop holding the leather case like something venomous, and had said, in the flat voice Quentin had never heard her use:

*First snow, Quentin. Leave, or the rite.*

The rite. The cleansing. Quentin had seen it once, when she was small — a man from the far farms who'd been caught whispering to his dead wife's shawl. They'd walked him between the wardstones at dawn and the wardens had burned cedar and sung the old clean songs, and afterward the man had smiled and smiled and hadn't known his own children's names. They said it was mercy. They said it took the sickness out.

They said, sometimes, that those it failed came back hollow, and were quietly drowned in the mere.

Quentin turned from the window. Below, the embers of Thornvale glowed on, warm and certain and blind, and she loved them, and that was the worst of it. You could not grieve properly for a place unless you loved it, and she loved it the way you love a parent who has decided, with the best intentions, to break the thing most essential in you.

*I'm sorry,* she told the valley silently. Not for leaving. For ever having hidden. Or maybe both. The two sorrows had grown together like roots under a stone, impossible to tell apart anymore.

Then she climbed.

* * *

The wardstones stood along the ridgeline like a jaw of old teeth — twelve of them, granite, taller than a man, each carved with the clean geometric lines the Founders had cut four hundred years ago when they built Thornvale to be *safe*. Safe from magic. Safe from the wild things beyond. Safe from the whispering rot of the unquiet dead.

Safe from her.

Quentin had touched a wardstone exactly once, at fourteen, on a dare from Tam Vetch's older brother, and had felt nothing — which everyone said proved she was clean, and which Quentin had never told anyone was a lie. She had felt something. A pressure, like a hand pressing down on the top of her head. Like being told to be smaller.

Now she stood before the nearest stone with her breath smoking in the starlight and her heart going like a bird trapped in a shuttered room.

Beyond the stones, the world waited. She could see it in the way the night sky seemed wider out there, stars crowding down to the horizon without the valley's rim to hold them back. Beyond lay the high moor, and the Greywater beyond that, and somewhere out there — days away, weeks, she didn't know — a country erased from every record, drawn only on the leather now pressed against her spine.

*Cindral,* whispered the new voice, soft as snowfall. *Cindral. Cindral.*

"You could have chosen anyone," Quentin muttered. "A warden. Someone brave."

*It doesn't choose the brave,* said Ilsa's voice, from everywhere and nowhere. *It chooses the ones who listen.*

"That's what frightens me."

She put her hand on the wardstone.

For a moment, nothing. Just cold granite and her own pulse and the absurd urge to laugh at herself — a grown woman, standing in the dark, afraid of a rock.

Then she stepped past it.

The change hit her like diving into a winter river.

Sound. That was the only word for it — sound after deafness, color after grayness, breath after drowning. Magic flooded back into the world all at once, everywhere, and Quentin staggered and dropped to one knee on the frozen heather because the sheer *weight* of it buckled something in her chest.

All her life she had thought the valley was quiet. She had thought the whispers came thin and faint because the dead were far away, or shy, or tired. She had thought her gift was a candle flame guttering in a drafty room.

It was none of those things. The valley was a fist around her ears. And now the fist had opened, and the world was *loud*.

Magic moved in the heather like current under ice. It hummed in the stones themselves, in the old bones of the hills, in the slow green dreams of trees sleeping through winter. It tasted of iron and rain and something sweeter underneath, something like honey left too long in the sun. And threaded through all of it, dense as fish in a stream, were the dead.

Not just Thornvale's dead. *Everyone's.* Dead of every age and every war and every unmarked grave across the whole dark moor, drifting and murmuring and turning toward her like flowers toward light, because she was past the wards now, and past the wards she was a lighthouse, and they were all at sea.

"Oh," Quentin breathed. Her eyes were wet and she didn't know when that had happened. "Oh, it was never — I was never sick."

*No,* said Ilsa. *You were never sick.*

And then Ilsa stepped out of the dark and became visible.

Later, Quentin would try to describe it and fail. It wasn't like seeing a person; it was like remembering one so hard that the memory stood up and walked. A tall woman in a long coat stiff with frost that never melted, hair bound back in a cartographer's practical knot, face sharp-featured and wind-burned even in death, eyes the gray of a winter horizon. Frost glittered in the air around her like the dust of crushed glass. Where her boots should have touched the ground, the heather bent but did not break.

She looked at Quentin with an expression of such careful, aching kindness that Quentin had to look away.

"There you are," Quentin managed. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Twenty-six years of voices, and the first time I see one of you, it's —"

*A stranger,* Ilsa said. Her lips moved now, Quentin realized, and the sound came less through the ears than through the bones behind them, resonant and close. *Yes. I'm sorry. I'd have shown myself sooner if the valley had let me. It kept us both thin, that place. You more than me — you were living inside a lie about your own nature. No wonder you're so tired.*

"I'm not tired."

*You've been tired since you were nine years old,* Ilsa said gently, *and hiding it so well that you forgot it was tiredness and started calling it personality.*

Quentin laughed — a wet, startled, ugly laugh — and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. The moor wind came off the high fells and cut through her coat, and it was bitterly cold, and it was the freest air she had ever breathed.

Above them, the stars had come out in their thousands, thick as spilled salt, and among them moved faint lights that Quentin knew were not stars at all. The dead, going about whatever the dead go about. One drifted closer, curious — an old shepherd by his crook — circled her twice, and moved on, satisfied.

"They can see me," she said. "Out here, they can all see me."

*Every one,* Ilsa said. *Which is why we walk fast tonight. Word travels quickly among the dead, and not all of them are kind, and some of them have been waiting a very long time for someone like you — and waiting does things to people, living or otherwise.* She turned, and the frost of her stirred, and she nodded toward the north, where the moor rose black against the blacker sky. *The Greywater is three days' walk if the weather holds. It won't. Winter is coming early this year, and wrong, and you need to be across the river before it settles in earnest.*

"Why? What happens when it settles?"

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone strange — layered, as though several women were speaking through one throat, all of them grieving.

*The winter isn't weather, Quentin. Not entirely. It's a country's grief, falling as snow. And grief that falls long enough becomes a burial.*

Quentin looked north. Somewhere out there, past the moor and the river and the ranges she had no names for, lay a land that had been unwritten from the world — and its dead were whispering through her hands, asking, always asking, to be carried home.

"All right," she said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. She tightened the strap of her bag. She checked the tools against her hip, felt the map's warmth through the leather against her spine. "Three days to the Greywater. Then we find whoever ferries fools across it."

*One ferryman in particular,* Ilsa said, and there was something almost like dry humor in her voice now, the first Quentin had heard from her. *He owes my people a debt. He will hate that you know that.*

"Good," said Quentin. "Someone should hate something other than me for a change."

She took one last look south, down into the valley. Thornvale's embers still glowed in their bowl, small and warm and certain of themselves. Maren's window had gone dark. Somewhere down there, in a few weeks, the first snow would fall, and the town would mark it as it always did — a festival, cedar smoke, the clean songs — never knowing that the girl who carved their name-markers had walked out rather than be cleansed of the only true thing about her.

*They'll tell stories,* Ilsa said softly, following her gaze. *About the carver who vanished. Some of them will be kind.*

"Some of them will say the sickness finally took me."

*Let them,* Ilsa said. *Sickness took nothing. You walked out whole.*

Quentin stood a moment longer, letting the wind have her coat and her hair and the tears she'd stopped bothering to hide. Then she turned her back on the valley — on the whole shape of her old life, small and lit and finished — and faced north, where the moor ran black and endless under the crowded stars, and the cold came down off the high fells smelling of iron and snow and something older than either.

The dead of the moor turned toward her as she walked, hundreds of pale attentions, murmuring her name in a dozen generations of accents. Beside her, solid as memory, Ilsa matched her stride, frost scattering from her shoulders like flour from a baker's hands.

*Tell me about the Greywater,* Quentin said. *If I'm going to drown in it, I'd like to know what's in there.*

*Everything the river ever took,* Ilsa said. *And lately, Quentin — it has been taking a great deal.*

Ahead, far off across the dark, lightning flickered without thunder — silent, vertical, wrong — lighting for one heartbeat a line of white on the northern horizon that might have been cloud, or might have been snow already falling over a country that did not exist.

Quentin walked toward it.
