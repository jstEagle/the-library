## 6. The Road That Isn't There

The wardstones were a day behind her now, and Quentin still flinched at the sound of her own heartbeat.

It was not that Thornvale had been silent — she had never thought of it as silent until she left it. But its silence had been a held breath, a hush pressed down over everything like snow over buried things, and she had lived inside that hush so long she had mistaken it for quiet. Out here, past the stones, the world rang. Grass sang against grass in frequencies she felt in her teeth. Water spoke in the streams with a hundred small voices, and wind moved through the pines carrying conversations she could almost but not quite parse, like speech heard through a wall. Magic did not flood back into her so much as it poured in, wave after wave after wave, and every wave reminded her of what she had been living without.

Ilsa walked beside her, visible now as morning mist is visible — there when you looked straight at her, thinner when you didn't. The cartographer's ghost wore the shape of her last day: frost-burned cheeks, cracked lips, the grey travel coat stiff with ice that no longer melted because ice does not care about being dead. Her eyes were kind. That was the strangest part. Quentin had spent her whole life believing the dead came back angry, and here was one who looked at her like a grandmother looking at a child asleep over her sewing.

"You keep touching your ears," Ilsa observed. Her voice arrived the way voices arrive in dreams — from everywhere and nowhere, from just beside the ear and from very far away at once.

"Everything's loud."

"It was always this loud." Ilsa smiled with half her mouth. "You were born deaf to it and never knew. Most people are. Your valley made a virtue of it." She nodded ahead, where the road bent around a shoulder of hill. "The world has been talking about you since before you could walk, Quentin Halloran. You're only now hearing it say your name."

Quentin did not answer. She had learned, in one day of walking, that grief sat in her throat like a swallowed stone whenever she tried to speak of Thornvale, and so she kept her mouth shut and let the stone sit. Behind them lay a town that would hold a cleansing rite for her if she ever returned. Ahead lay a country that did not exist. Between them stretched a road she could not see.

Because that was the trouble. The map said there was a road. Quentin had unrolled it on the flat top of a boundary marker an hour past dawn, weighting the corners with four smooth stones while Ilsa hovered at her elbow giving instructions in the tone of a woman who had given instructions to apprentices for thirty years and missed it terribly. And the map had shown her — clearly, confidently, in ink the color of old honey — a road running west by northwest from the ford, through the hills called the Marrowbacks, toward a river whose name on the page made Quentin's fingers ache to pronounce: *the Greywater*.

But her eyes, lifted from the page to the land, found nothing. No track, no rut, no sheep-path. Only bracken, boulder, bog, and the indifferent green slope of the hills going up and up into cloud.

"Roads don't vanish," Quentin had said.

"These do," Ilsa had answered, "and they don't. Look again. Not with your eyes."

So now they walked where no road was, and Quentin felt like a woman wading into a lake blindfolded, trusting the bottom to rise to meet her feet. The bracken closed over her boots. Wet seed-heads slapped her wrists. Twice she stumbled on stone that seemed to have arranged itself out of pure malice, and twice she caught herself with a word her grandmother would have boxed her ears for.

"Stop," Ilsa said, on the third stumble. "Stop, child. Sit."

"I can't sit. We've lost half the morning—"

"We have lost nothing. Time walks differently on Cindral's roads. Sit *down*, Quentin."

Quentin sat, on a boulder furred with moss the color of verdigris, and pulled her bag into her lap and hugged it like a shield. The morning fog was burning off the valleys below in long banners. Somewhere a bird she couldn't name made a sound like a hinge. She became aware, distantly, through the bone charm at her throat — a small carved sparrow, the first thing she'd shaped since leaving, made from a knuckle-bone Ilsa had asked her to keep — that other presences were near. Not speaking. Just *near*, the way the dead were always near her, patient as furniture.

"Why can't I see the road?" she asked, and hated how young her voice sounded.

Ilsa settled across from her on the air as though it were a chair. Ghosts, Quentin was learning, did not bother with the courtesies of physics. "Because you're holding it wrong."

"Holding—" Quentin touched the map case at her belt. "I haven't even got it out."

"You're holding it with your hands. That's the wrong end entirely." Ilsa leaned forward, and the frost on her eyelashes caught the light. "The map is not a picture of a road, girl. It is a promise of one. Cindral's cartographers did not draw what *was*. They drew what *could be walked*, and then they taught the land to keep its word. A road on my map exists exactly as long as someone walks it in trust. Trust in the map. Trust in the makers. Trust in—" she paused, and something flickered across her ruined face, some old private grief, "—trust in whoever walks beside you. Doubt is heavier than you know. Put enough of it down on the path and the path cannot bear the weight. It goes out under you like a candle."

"And if it goes out while I'm standing on it?"

"Then you fall," Ilsa said simply, "into the hollow dark, which is the space between what was erased and what remembers it. And nothing that falls into the hollow dark comes back, because there is no *back* for it to come to. The hollow dark is not a place. It is an absence wearing the shape of one." She tilted her head. "You felt it already. Don't pretend you didn't. An hour ago, on the slope. Your boot found no ground and for half a heartbeat you knew."

Quentin's stomach turned over slowly, remembering. There had been a step — one step among hundreds — where the world had simply declined to be there. Where her foot had swung down into a coldness that was not cold air but *unbeing*, a nothing with texture, and in that half-heartbeat she had understood viscerally, the way you understand falling in a dream, that the nothing went down forever. Then her heel had struck stone and she had laughed shakily and blamed the bracken, and Ilsa had said nothing, and had been watching her with those kind, terrible eyes ever since.

"That was doubt," Quentin said quietly.

"A crack in the plaster. The whole wall will come down the same way." Ilsa rose, drifting a hand's width above the ground, and beckoned. "Get up. Walk with me. And while you walk, tell me why you doubt."

"I don't doubt the map. I saw it. It drew itself warmer when I touched it—"

"Not the map." Ilsa began to move, unhurried, along the invisible line of the road, and Quentin scrambled off the boulder to follow. "Tell me why you doubt yourself."

The question landed between her shoulder blades like a thrown stone.

They walked. The bracken parted for Ilsa and did not part for Quentin, and yet Quentin's feet, when she stopped thinking about them, found firmness beneath them — packed earth, the faint twin ruts of old wheels, a milestone lying face-down in the heather that she was almost certain had not been there when they passed this way before. The road was there. It was there the way a voice is there under noise: audible only when you believed in it enough to listen.

"I doubt myself," Quentin said at last, because Ilsa waited the way the dead wait, which is to say without any hurry whatsoever, "because everyone who ever loved me told me I was sick."

The words came out easier than she expected. They had been packed a long time.

"Maren says it. Said it. The healer. She calls it the wasting, the soul-sickness, she has a whole shelf of books about people like me and none of them agree except that we shouldn't exist. My mother wouldn't touch anything I carved. When I was nine I made her a little bone bird for her name-day and she burned it in the stove and told me never again, and she wasn't cruel about it, that's the worst part, she was *kind*, she was kind the way you're kind to something rabid." Quentin watched her own boots press into earth that existed because she believed in it. "My grandmother was the only one who said otherwise. She said it was inheritance. She said the women in our line carried the dead the way other women carry water. But she died when I was fourteen, and kindness doesn't carry weight against a whole town, and after a while I started saying it to myself too. Sick. Wrong. A thing to hide." She laughed, short and bitter. "Twenty-two years of hiding it. And now I'm supposed to walk on a road made of belief, and every step I take, there's a voice in me saying *you are the thing that gets cleansed, you are the sickness, why would the road want you*."

She stopped. So did the road.

She felt it go. There was no drama to it — no lurch, no roar. One moment the packed earth was under her right foot; the next, her left foot came down on bracken and bog-moss and a darkness underneath that had no bottom, and the world tipped, and she was falling, actually falling, arms wheeling, the sky swinging sideways, and the cold rising up around her was the cold of the space between stars—

A hand closed on her wrist.

It should not have been able to. Ilsa had no more substance than breath. But the grip was iron, was winter, was the strength of a woman who had died holding on to something and had never learned how to stop, and it hauled Quentin up and back and onto solidity with a jerk that nearly dislocated her shoulder, and then they were both kneeling on the road — the road, ruts and milestones and all — and Quentin was shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and Ilsa was holding her wrist still, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

Below them, where the road had broken, the hillside showed a seam of darkness like a wound in cloth. It was already closing. It closed the way water closes over a drowned thing: smoothly, indifferently, erasing all evidence that anything had ever fallen.

"The hollow dark," Ilsa said at last. Her voice had lost its gentleness and gained something older. "That is what the Hollow King leaves behind everywhere he unmakes. Every road he cut, every name he burned, every door he sealed — the holes go somewhere, Quentin. They go *there*. And it is hungry, because an absence is always hungry. It would have taken you in three breaths and the world would not have noticed you were gone, because you come from a valley that has already agreed to forget you." She turned Quentin's wrist over, examining it, though there was no mark. "Do you understand now? This is not a game of maps. This is the war itself, still running, sixty years after the last battle. The Hollow King does not need to send soldiers after you. He only needs you to doubt."

Quentin pulled her wrist free — gently — and sat back on her heels, and made herself breathe. In through the nose. Out through the mouth. The way she breathed when the dead spoke too many at once and she needed to find herself among them. The bird-charm at her throat hummed faintly, warm as a held chick.

"How," she said, when her voice would obey her. "How do I walk on it, then? If doubt breaks it. I can't just — decide not to doubt. I've been deciding to believe I'm sick for twenty years. Belief doesn't obey."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It doesn't obey. It obeys nothing. That is rather the point." She settled beside Quentin on the road, close enough that the cold of her was almost companionable, the cold of a cellar, of a grave tended with flowers. "So stop trying to believe, child. You cannot bully your heart into faith; I watched kings try, and their roads fell out from under their armies mid-march, and it served them right. The map does not read your courage. It reads your heart. And your heart—" she tapped Quentin's sternum with one frost-white finger, and the tap rang through her ribs like a bell through bronze, "—your heart has been telling the truth your whole life, hasn't it. Even when your mouth lied. Even when the whole valley lied. Your heart never once believed you were sick."

Quentin stared at her.

"You carve names for the dead," Ilsa said softly. "Nobody pays you for most of them. Nobody sees you do them. You sit up nights shaping little markers for strangers' ghosts because leaving them unnamed is unbearable to you — I know, I watched you do mine, I watched you argue with yourself over whether the R should curl. A sick soul hoards. A whole one gives. You have been giving since you were a child, into a valley that gave you nothing back but silence and shame, and you *still* gave." Her eyes were bright, and ghosts' tears, Quentin discovered, look like frost forming. "That is what the road wants. Not your confidence. Your heart. Walk the way you carve. Open-handed. Expecting nothing. Willing to be wrong. The road will hold you precisely as long as you hold *it* — not with your fists, but the way you hold a sparrow. Loose enough that it lives."

Quentin looked down the road, west by northwest, into the hills and the weather coming down out of them. The Greywater lay somewhere beyond, two days' walk if the map kept its promises. Beyond that, a country that had been deleted. Beyond that, whatever it meant to be a door-keeper, a word Ilsa used carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat.

Her heart was pounding. She let it pound. She noticed, deliberately, the fear in her — the cold residue of the fall, the shame of it, the voice that whispered *sick, wrong, cleansed* — and instead of fighting it she made room for it, the way she made room in a carving for the grain of the bone, working with the flaw instead of against it. Fear could ride along. Fear could sit in the cart. It simply could not hold the reins.

"All right," she said aloud, to the road, to the map, to the dead woman beside her and the chorus of patient presences humming at the edge of her senses. "I'm not sure. I'm scared. I miss a home that tried to erase me, and I don't know what I'm walking toward, and I'll probably cry about it before nightfall." She stood up. Her knees were steady. That surprised her. "And I'm going anyway. Take it or leave it."

The road — and she would have sworn this to her dying day, would have carved it in stone if anyone had asked — the road *warmed* under her boots. Faintly, unmistakably, like a hearthstone hours after the fire. Like something glad.

Ilsa laughed. It was a real laugh, rusty with disuse, sixty years deep, and it scattered crows out of the heather a quarter-mile downslope. "Oh, well done. Well done, Quentin Halloran. That's the first honest prayer I've heard in decades, and the land heard it too." She drifted up, gesturing grandly westward like a guide presenting a cathedral. "Walk on, keeper. Mind the milestone at the bend — it lies about distances. And when we make the ridge by dusk, you'll want to see what the map shows next, because—"

She stopped. All the laughter went out of her at once, the way light goes out of a window.

Quentin followed her gaze to the ridge line. Against the pale sky, thin as scratches on glass, stood figures. Four of them. Five. Still as fence posts, watching the road — watching *her* — from a distance no human eyes should have been able to bridge.

"They're not mine," Ilsa said quietly. "They're not Cindral's dead. Quentin — someone else knows you're carrying the map."

* * *

The figures did not move as Quentin and Ilsa climbed, and that was worse than pursuit. Pursuit would have meant they could be outrun. These simply *waited*, patient as the hollow dark, and with every switchback of the road Quentin's newly-learned trust thinned and frayed, and twice the ground under her went soft as old bread before she caught herself, breathing, opening her hands, giving the road her heart again like a woman feeding a fire one stick at a time.

By the last rise she could see them plainly, and wished she couldn't. They wore the shapes of soldiers, or things that had studied soldiers: grey cloaks, helms with the faces hammered blank, and where eyes should have been, a frost that did not melt. They stood in a rough crescent across the crest of the ridge, directly astride the road, and the heather within arm's reach of each of them was dead. Not brown. Dead — grey, brittle, *absent*, as though the life had been not killed but subtracted.

"Hollow King's wardens," Ilsa murmured. "Scouts. He cannot cross the old borders himself, not yet, not while the Door holds — but his leash reaches far. They hunt the map. They hunt anyone who carries it." Her hand found Quentin's shoulder, cold and certain. "There is a shepherd's hollow fifty paces east of the road, behind the standing stone. I mapped it myself, sixty years ago, when it was a shelter and not a memory. Can you trust me one more time today?"

Quentin's heart was slamming. Below the ridge, the blank-faced things had begun, very slowly, to descend.

"Yes," she said — and meant it, wholly, easily, the easiest word she'd spoken in years — and the road flared warm beneath her feet like a lit fuse running west.
