## 7. The Ferryman's Debt

The Greywater announced itself long before we saw it.

First there was the smell — cold iron and river mud, the mineral breath of water that had traveled far and forgotten most of what it carried. Then the ground changed under our boots, softening from frost-hard clay to something darker, richer, threaded with pale roots that surfaced like the backs of sleeping fish. And then, faintly, a sound I had no name for: not rushing, not lapping, but a low continuous murmur, like a crowd heard through a wall.

"Is that the river?" I asked. "Or is that—"

"That's both," Ilsa said. She walked beside me now as easily as breathing, her outline sharp in the grey morning light, her boots leaving no prints in the mud. Three days of travel had taught me to stop flinching when she spoke. It had not taught me to stop being astonished by it. In Thornvale, the dead came through my hands or not at all — voices without faces, warmth without shape. Ilsa walked. Ilsa pointed at hawks. Ilsa complained about the state of the roads with the particular grievance of someone who had once drawn them for a living.

"The Greywater is old," she said. "Older than the war. Older than most borders. The dead like it because it doesn't ask where they're from."

I said nothing. My hands were in my coat pockets, wrapped around the map case, which had grown warm again as we drew near the water — a living warmth, like a bird's breast against my palm. The map had been quiet for two days, letting us walk its straight invisible road through empty country, but now it stirred against my fingers the way a dog stirs when it smells home.

We crested a last low rise, and there it was.

The Greywater was wider than any river I'd ever seen, so wide the far bank was a rumor, a grey suggestion beneath a grey sky. It moved slowly, massively, with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. Mist lay along its surface in long unbroken sheets, and where the mist thinned I could see the water itself — dark, almost black, shot through with veins of silver where the current turned over stones.

And moored at a rickety jetty that jutted out into the mist like a splinter, there was a boat.

It was flat-bottomed and broad, built for cargo rather than grace, with a small cabin aft and a pole rack along either gunwale. A lantern hung from the cabin's eave, unlit. Painted on the hull, in letters that had been white once and were now the color of old teeth, was a name: *Vetch's Ferry*.

Standing at the jetty's end, coiling rope with the economy of a man who had done nothing else for thirty years, was the ferryman.

He was shorter than me, broad through the shoulders and chest in the way of men who wrestle rivers for their bread, with a face like a knot in hardwood — weathered, dense, impossible to read. His hair was iron-grey and cut close. He wore oilskins gone soft with age, and he did not look up when we came down the slope toward him, though I would have sworn he'd marked us from the moment we crested the rise.

"Closed," he said, before we were even within shouting distance.

"We haven't asked anything yet," I called back.

"You were going to ask for a crossing." He finished his coil, hung it on a peg, and turned to look at us at last. His eyes went over me — the road-worn coat, the satchel of carving tools, the map case clutched to my chest like a child — and then past me, and stopped.

I watched him see Ilsa.

I watched him go very still, the way animals go still, and I watched his gaze track her — because he *could* see her, that was plain, could see the dead woman standing in the morning light with river mist drifting through the place where her ribs should have cast a shadow — and I watched something move behind his face that was not fear.

It was guilt. Old, worn-smooth guilt, the kind that has been handled so many times it has lost all its edges except the deepest one.

"No," he said, quieter now. "Whatever you're selling, no."

"We're not selling anything," I said. "We need to cross."

"Then you need a different river." He turned back to his ropes with the finality of a door closing.

Ilsa drifted forward — drifted, she only drifted when she forgot herself, when feeling pulled her off the ground the way wind pulls smoke — and stood at the jetty's edge, looking down at him. "Hello, Tam," she said gently.

His hands stopped moving.

"I don't know you," he said to the air, which was almost true and entirely a lie, and we all three of us knew it.

"You knew my country," Ilsa said. "You carried Cindrali grain down this river for six years before the war. You took payment in Cindrali coin. And in the last winter, when the roads closed and the boats stopped coming—" she paused, and her voice did something complicated, "—you took one more load anyway. Refugees. Forty-one of them, crammed into your hold like salt fish, because a woman with a baby begged you at the dock and you couldn't say no even though the wardens had forbidden it."

The ferryman's jaw worked. "That was a long time ago."

"It was," Ilsa agreed. "And the wardens caught you at the mouth of the river. And they took the forty-one. And they let you live, because a man who ferries is useful, and a man who ferries and is afraid is *very* useful." She tilted her head. "You've been afraid ever since. But underneath the fear, Tam Vetch, you've been carrying them. Every day. Every crossing. You named your boat after the youngest one."

"*Vetch,*" I said stupidly. "I thought—"

"It's my name too," the ferryman said roughly. "Same name. Doesn't mean anything."

"It means everything," Ilsa said. "You gave her your name because she died nameless, and you couldn't bear it. I was there, Tam. I'm one of the forty-one."

His name was Tam Vetch, and he made us tea because he didn't know what else to do with us.

The cabin was small and smelled of woodsmoke, tar, and decades of river damp. A kettle hung on an iron hook over a stove whose firebox glowed dull orange. There was one chair, one bunk, one shelf of chipped crockery, and one wall covered — completely covered, floor to ceiling, every inch — in paper.

Maps. Dozens of them. Hand-drawn, hand-inked, some faded nearly to ghosts themselves, pinned and glued and layered over one another until the wall was less a collection than a palimpsest. Rivers, coastlines, mountain passes, trade roads. And among them, over and over, appearing and reappearing like a face in a crowd, a country I now recognized from Ilsa's memories and the map's warm insistence: Cindral. Its valleys. Its spires. Its long southern shore.

Tam Vetch saw me looking and his ears went red.

"A man forgets things," he said, pouring tea into three cups — three cups, though he must have known, must have understood on some level, that one of his guests couldn't lift a cup. "Paper remembers. That's all that is. Paper remembering."

"That's not all it is," I said quietly.

He shoved a cup into my hands hard enough to slosh it. "Drink your tea."

We drank. Ilsa sat on the bunk — sat, deliberately, choosing the weight of it, and the straw tick didn't even sigh beneath her — and looked around the little cabin with an expression I couldn't parse until I realized what it was: she was *memorizing* it. The way a cartographer memorizes. Filing away the shape of a kindness she hadn't expected to find.

"Why did the wardens stop you?" I asked. "All those years ago. Why does anyone care about refugees from a country that doesn't exist?"

Tam set his cup down. For a long moment he studied the stove, and I thought he wouldn't answer. Then:

"Because a country that doesn't exist can't testify," he said. "You can do anything to people who come from nowhere. Nobody's counting. Nobody's keeping the books." He glanced at the map wall, and his voice dropped. "Except the ones who can't stop remembering."

Outside, the light was changing. I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in music — not consciously at first, just a prickle at the neck. Through the cabin's salt-fogged window, the grey morning had begun to take on a different quality. Harder. Whiter. The mist over the river was thickening instead of burning off, and the air that pressed against the glass carried a new weight, a fine dry cold that had nothing to do with the season.

It was barely autumn. The trees on the near bank still held most of their leaves, gold and stubborn.

But snow was starting to fall.

Not falling — *arriving*. The flakes came down slow and enormous, each one distinct, unhurried, like ash from a fire very far away. They landed on the jetty boards and did not melt.

Tam followed my stare to the window and his face closed like a fist.

"There it is," he said. "Right on time. Early, same as last year. Earlier than the year before." He stood, joints cracking, and went to the window himself. "First winter I ran this ferry, the Greywater froze solid on the twelfth of Deepmonth. Now look at it. Snow before harvest's done. Ice on the oar-locks in the mornings. The river's getting colder year by year, and the old-timers say the fish are going strange, swimming in circles, gathering in the deep holes like they're hiding from something." He spat, with feeling. "Folk blame the weather. Folk blame the king across the mountains. Folk'll blame anything except the truth."

"Which is?" I asked, though I already felt it, the way I felt everything now — through my palms, through the bones of my wrists, a low ache like a tooth that knows a storm is coming.

Tam turned from the window. He looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face — the valley girl who talked to dead women, the carver of bone, the keeper of a map that warmed like a heartbeat — seemed to decide him.

"Cindral's leaking," he said simply. "Grief runs downhill, same as water. That country didn't die clean. It died screaming, and nobody listened, and all that grief had nowhere to go. So it's been seeping out along the old roads, down the old rivers, into the world's weather. Every winter comes a little earlier and bites a little deeper because the grief is growing. And someday—" he tapped the glass, where the enormous flakes drifted down without melting, "—someday soon it won't be winter arriving early. It'll be *the* winter. One long fall that doesn't lift. Snow that buries the memory of the place along with everyone carrying it." He shrugged, huge and hopeless. "That's what the drowned tell me, anyway."

I went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the window. "The drowned tell you."

"They talk." He said it flatly, daring me to make something of it. "In the deep water, mid-crossing, when the mist comes up over the gunwales. They talk, and I listen, because listening is the least I owe them." His eyes flicked to Ilsa, then away. "It's how I pay the debt. Crossing after crossing. I carry whoever needs carrying, and I listen to the ones nobody else will."

For a moment the cabin was very quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere out on the black water, the murmur rose and fell like breathing.

"I hear them too," I said.

Tam's head came around sharply.

"Not in the water. Through my hands. When I carve." I set down my cup and pulled off my right glove, and held up my bare palm between us — the calluses, the fine white scars from knife slips, the hand that Thornvale's healer had spent twenty years trying to cure. "Bone, mostly. Wood, if bone's short. Whatever I shape, they speak through it. I've been doing it since I was a child. Where I come from, they call it sickness."

"And what do you call it?" Tam asked. His voice had changed — gruffness thinning over something rawer.

I looked at Ilsa. She was watching me with an expression I was beginning to know well: hope, held carefully, the way you hold something that has broken on you before.

"Inheritance," I said. "My grandmother called it inheritance. I'm trying to learn to agree with her."

Tam Vetch looked at my hand for a long time. Then he looked at the map case on my hip, and at Ilsa, and at the snow piling soundlessly on his jetty outside, and something in his knotted face shifted — not softening exactly; more like a knot working itself a quarter-turn looser.

"The map brought you here," he said. Not a question.

"It chose her," Ilsa said.

"Then the map's got better sense than most folk I've met." He crossed to the stove, refilled my cup unasked, and stood with his back to me, staring at the map wall — at the dozens of careful renderings of a country erased from every atlas in the world. When he spoke again, his voice was rough as rope.

"I'll take you across. Tomorrow, first light, before the ice gets ideas." He held up a finger before I could thank him. "Conditions. One: you sit where I put you and touch nothing. Two: if the mist comes up over the sides, you keep your hands inside the boat no matter what you hear. Three—" he paused, and the pause had weight, "—if they call your name, you don't answer. Not the first time. Not the second. The dead on the Greywater aren't cruel, girl, but they're *hungry*, and hunger makes honest things dishonest. You answer on the third call, you'd best be sure it's family."

"What happens on the third call?" I asked.

"Depends who's calling," Tam said. He drained his tea and set the cup upside down on the shelf, a ferryman's superstition I didn't yet understand. "My sister calls for me every crossing. Hasn't missed one in nineteen years. One of these days I'm going to answer her." He said it the way other men say *one of these days I'm going to die*, and I understood that for him the two sentences meant the same thing.

I should have been afraid. Some part of me noted, distantly, that a sensible person would have been afraid — of the hungry dead, the freezing river, the gruff stranger with a wall full of forbidden maps and a debt written in forty-one lives. But the feeling that actually moved through me, sitting in that smoky little cabin while the wrong snow fell on the wrong season, was something I hadn't felt since my grandmother died.

I felt *expected*. The tea had been poured for me. The third cup had been set out for a ghost, and no one had remarked on it. A man who talked to drowned strangers had decided, in the space of an hour, that I was worth a crossing — not because I'd earned it, but because the map had chosen me and he had learned, bitterly and at great cost, not to argue with the dead about who they wanted.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me yet." Tam jerked his chin at the window, at the vast slow flakes settling on the river without melting, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from him was somehow worse than anger. "Sleep tonight while you can, carver. Because tomorrow we cross the Greywater, and the Greywater's been waiting a long time to meet the likes of you." He nodded at my bare hand, still resting open on my knee. "They already know your name. I heard them saying it this morning, out in the mist. All of them. At once."

Outside, the snow fell thicker, and beneath its silence the river murmured on — and this time, unmistakably, I heard my name in it.

*Quentin. Quentin. Quentin.*

Like forty-one voices learning one word.

Like a tide coming in.
