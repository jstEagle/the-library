## 8. Ghosts of the Greywater

The Greywater did not look like a river. It looked like a held breath.

Quentin stood at the bank in the grey hour before dawn, her bag heavy on her shoulder, and watched the water move without moving — a slow, muscular slide of current beneath a skin of mist, the color of iron left out in rain. No birds crossed it. Even the reeds along the margin grew bent, leaning away from the water as if from a fire.

"You'll want to hold on to something," Tam Vetch said behind her. He was already at the ferry, a flat-bellied thing of blackened planks with a pole taller than he was, checking the mooring knots with the absent competence of a man who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. "The Greywater doesn't like passengers. It tolerates them."

"Charming," Ilsa murmured. Only Quentin could hear her — or rather, only Quentin could see her, standing at the water's edge in her soldier's coat, frost still ghosting her shoulders though she had been dead for sixty years. She was looking out over the river with an expression Quentin hadn't seen on her before. Not fear. Recognition.

"You know it," Quentin said quietly.

"I drowned near here." Ilsa's voice was very even. "The retreat from the border. We came through the marshes and the river took eleven of us before the wardens ever caught up." She turned her head, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "I'm sorry. I should have told you before we walked two days to get here."

"It would have changed what? I'd have taken the long way around?"

"There is no long way around," Tam said, overhearing nothing but somehow answering anyway. "Not anymore. The roads south are gone — not closed, gone. The map knows it too." He nodded at the leather case against Quentin's hip, where the map of Cindral lay warm as a sleeping animal. "That's why you came to me. That's why anyone comes to me. Last crossing for forty miles, and the only one that doesn't ask where you're going."

He shoved off without ceremony, and Quentin stepped aboard into the smell of tar and wet rope, and the Greywater took them.

* * *

The first voice came when they were perhaps a third of the way across, where the mist thickened until Tam's lantern made a small brown coin of light that seemed to be swallowed rather than shed.

It began in her hands.

She'd been holding them loosely in her lap, and suddenly both palms prickled, the way they did when she shaped bone — that deep, resonant hum, like pressing her ear to a door with voices behind it. She looked down. Her fingers were flexing without her permission, curling and uncurling, and the mist around the boat had begun to move wrong. It gathered. It leaned in.

*Cold,* said a voice, and it wasn't sound, it was pressure, arriving through her skin, her wrists, the small bones of her ears. *So cold, and the water so heavy, and we have been here so long.*

"Oh no," Quentin whispered.

"What?" Tam didn't look back. His pole worked steadily, his eyes fixed ahead. "If you're going to be sick, do it over the side, not in my boat."

"They're here." Her teeth were chattering already, and not from cold. "The dead. The whole river is full of them, Tam, can't you feel—"

"No." But his pole had faltered, just once, a half-beat hitch in its rhythm, and Quentin saw the tendons stand in his neck. "There's nothing in my river but water. There has never been anything in my river but water."

*Ilsa,* said another voice, closer now, wondering. *Ilsa Vetch, is that you? We thought — we thought the marsh took you whole.*

And Ilsa went rigid beside her, staring out into the mist with her dead eyes wide, and Quentin understood that whatever this was, it wasn't coming through her hands alone. The Greywater was thin. The Greywater had been thin for sixty years, a place where the drowned had pooled like rain in a cellar, and now something was calling them up.

"Ilsa," Quentin breathed. "Who is that?"

"My sergeant." Ilsa's voice cracked on the word. "Corvin. He — he pushed me onto a raft. I saw him go under." Her hand rose toward the mist and stopped, hovering, the way you reach toward a fire. "Corvin, I'm here. I'm here."

*You're cold,* Corvin said, and there was wonder in it, grief in it, sixty years of it compressed into three words. *Why are you still cold, Ilsa? You were supposed to get out.*

The mist was thickening around the boat now, and shapes moved in it — not figures, not quite, but suggestions of figures, the way faces form in smoke. And Quentin's hands were burning, every bone in them ringing like struck glass, because the dead of the Greywater had found the one living thing on the river that could carry their voices, and they were all trying to speak at once.

*We waited.* *The water is so heavy.* *Tell someone we're here.* *Is the war over? Did we win? Is Cindral—*

*Is Cindral free?*

That last voice cut through all the others, young and sharp and desperate, and the mist went quiet around it, the way a room goes quiet when someone important speaks.

Tam's pole stopped.

He stood motionless in the stern, the pole dripping, his face gone the color of the river. And Quentin, drowning in voices, heard him say — barely aloud, mostly to himself — a name.

"Nessa."

* * *

*Nessa,* the voice said again, and it was everywhere now, in the mist and the water and the small bones of Quentin's hands. *Nessa Vetch. Ferryman's daughter. Drowned age nineteen, spring flood, carrying the morning milk across like Papa asked. Tell him the milk got spilled but I'm sorry, tell him I didn't mean to let go of the rope, tell him—*

"Stop." Tam's voice came out sanded flat. "Stop it. Whatever trick this is—"

"It's not a trick." Quentin's own voice sounded far away to her. Her hands had risen without her willing it, palms out, and the mist was streaming toward them like breath toward a window. "They're not lying, Tam. They can't lie. They're just — asking. They've been asking for sixty years and nobody could hear."

"I don't have a sister."

*Tam,* Nessa said, gentle as snowfall. *You used to say it wrong on purpose. You used to call me Nessarina because I hated it. You'd sing it off-key at the well and Mama would throw wash-water at you both.*

The pole slipped from Tam's fingers.

Quentin heard it clatter against the gunwale and then he was on his knees in the bottom of the boat, this big gruff man who'd spent two days pretending she was cargo, and his face had come apart entirely.

"She was fourteen," he said, to no one, to the water. "When I left. I took the ferry route north because there was money in it and I told her I'd come back at harvest and she stood on the bank and waved and—" His breath hitched. "That was the year the winter started early. That was the year everything started going wrong. I kept meaning to come back. I kept meaning to."

*You did come back,* Nessa said. *Twice. You polled right past our landing because you couldn't look at the house. I called your name and you polled faster.*

A sound came out of Tam that Quentin would remember for the rest of her life — half laugh, half wound, the noise a person makes when grief arrives too large for the body holding it.

"I know," he said. "Ness. I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

*It's all right.* And the voice was warm now, warmer than anything had a right to be in the middle of that freezing mist. *It's all right, Tammy. Listen. Listen, because I don't have long — the water wants us back, and there's worse than water under here now. The winter isn't weather. You have to understand that. It's not weather.*

"What is it, then?" Quentin managed. Her hands were shaking; the mist pressed close, dozens of voices layered beneath Nessa's like sediment.

*It's waiting,* Nessa said. *Cindral's grief, spilled out past the borders where nobody could see it fall. And it's been falling and falling and it will finish falling by solstice. When the last snow lands, the Door closes forever. Everything inside stays inside. Everyone carrying it—"*

She paused, and Quentin felt the pause like a hand around her throat.

*Everyone carrying it gets buried with the memory. Every map, every song, every name-marker carved by clever girls with gifted hands. Gone. The Hollow King wins by forgetting, and the forgetting finishes at solstice.*

"How long?" Ilsa asked, and her soldier's voice was steady but her face was not. "How long until solstice?"

*Twenty-six days,* said Corvin, from deeper in the mist. *Give or take a day the sun can't count properly anymore.*

Twenty-six days. Quentin did the arithmetic without wanting to: the mountains ahead, the city of salvage, the Door itself, wherever and whatever it was. Twenty-six days, in a world where the roads themselves existed only while you trusted them.

"The crossing," Tam said hoarsely. He hauled himself up by the gunwale, found his pole, gripped it like a man gripping a railing over a drop. "How much farther?"

*Half,* said the drowned, all together, a chorus like wind through reeds. *But the middle is theirs. The middle belongs to the ones who went under fighting. Hold your oarsman's heart steady, Tam Vetch. Your sister will hold yours.*

* * *

The middle of the Greywater was a different country.

The mist turned white — not grey, not silver, but a dense, blind white, and the temperature dropped so hard the spray on the gunwales froze mid-drip. And the voices changed. The ones who spoke now didn't ask. They accused.

*Deserters.* *Turncoats.* *You rowed past us.* *Every day for years you rowed past us, ferryman, and you never once put your hand in the water.*

Tam poled. His jaw was set hard enough to crack stone, and tears froze in the creases of his cheeks, and he poled. Quentin knelt in the bilge with her palms pressed flat to the wet planks, because if she didn't give the voices somewhere to go they would tear the boat apart looking for one.

"Talk to me," she gasped. "All of you — talk through me, I'll carry it, I'll carry all of it—"

And they did.

It poured through her like the river itself: names, regiments, last words, the specific weight of specific bodies going under. A boy of sixteen who'd lied about his age. A woman who'd held her comrade's hair as the water filled her lungs, so she wouldn't die alone. A pair of brothers who drowned holding on to each other because neither would let go first. Quentin carried every one of them, named them silently as they passed, gave each voice one moment of being heard, and it was the hardest thing she had ever done and the most necessary, and somewhere in the middle of it she understood — fully, finally — what the map had chosen her for.

Then, gradually, the white thinned. The accusations softened into something else. *Thank you,* the dead said, over and over, in a dozen old accents. *Thank you. Thank you. Someone heard.*

The far bank emerged like a promise.

Tam brought the boat in slow, grounding it on the shingle, and for a long moment neither of them moved. Mist curled away behind them. Somewhere out on the water, faint as memory, Nessa was humming — a ferryman's counting-song, the kind mothers sang to children on night crossings, and Tam sat with his head bowed and listened until it faded.

"She used to sing that," he said finally, "when the fog came in. So passengers wouldn't be afraid." He wiped his face roughly with his sleeve, and when he looked up his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "Right. Solstice. Twenty-six days."

"You don't have to come," Quentin said. "This was the crossing. This was the debt. You're paid, Tam. Go home."

He laughed — a short, broken, genuine sound.

"Go home," he repeated. "To what? A river full of my sister, and twenty-six days until the world forgets her name?" He shouldered his pole. "Besides. Somebody has to keep you lot alive, and clearly none of you can row."

Quentin found she was crying too, a little, and laughing, and colder and lighter than she had been since Thornvale. Ilsa stood at the bow looking back at the water, and raised one dead hand in salute to the mist.

Ahead, the land climbed toward hills the color of old bruises, and on the horizon, faint as a scar, something glinted — rooftops, tin and glass and salvaged steel, catching the light of a sun that seemed already to be thinking about winter.

"Ostmere," Tam said. "City of people from nowhere." He spat over the side, out of habit, then seemed to reconsider, and instead touched two fingers to the water — an old ferryman's farewell to the drowned. "Walk fast. Winter's coming early and wrong, and we've got a country to save in twenty-six days."

They went up the bank together, four of them now — one living, one dead, one grieving, one chosen — and behind them the Greywater slid on, patient and full of voices, carrying its sixty years of names toward a solstice that was coming like a debt called due.
