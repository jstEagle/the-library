## 9. The City That Wasn't

Ostmere announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet stone and something faintly sweet, like honey left too long in a warm room. Then the road crested a low ridge of grey hills, and there it was below them — a town stitched together out of things that had never been meant to sit beside one another.

Quentin stopped at the crest and stared.

She had grown up in Thornvale, where every roof was the same thatch and every wall the same whitewash, where difference was a disease you caught from travelers. She had thought, until this moment, that all towns were like that. Ostmere was not like that. Ostmere was a magpie's nest the size of a small city — slate roofs next to copper ones gone green with age, a bell tower built of pale stone that did not match any other stone in view, doorways carved with symbols in three alphabets she didn't recognize and a fourth she almost did. A bridge crossed the river in seven uneven arches, each arch a different style, as if seven different builders had each contributed one span and refused to speak to their neighbors.

"It looks," Tam said behind her, "like a town that fell downstairs."

"It looks," Quentin said slowly, "like it was *built* from somewhere else."

Ilsa stood at her shoulder, visible now to no one but Quentin, her ghost-light dimmed to almost nothing in the grey afternoon. "It was," she said quietly. "Look at the tower stone. Look at the bridge railings." Her voice caught. "That's Cindral work. That's the old quarter of Vessamyre. They carried it out piece by piece when everything burned."

The map case hung against Quentin's chest under her coat, and even through wool and leather she felt it warm — not urgently, not the way it warmed on the road-that-wasn't-there, but steadily, like a hand pressed flat over her heart. The map knew this place. Or rather, the map knew what this place was made of.

"Refugees," Tam said. He said it the way he said most things, gruff and flat, but his eyes were doing something complicated. "After the war. After the country got — unmade." He still stumbled over that word. They all did. "They couldn't go home because home wasn't anywhere anymore. So they came here, to the border, and they built a town out of whatever they could carry or steal or salvage. And then—" he shrugged, "—the border kingdoms decided an erased country can't have refugees either. Officially, nobody here is from anywhere. You ask anyone in Ostmere where they're from, they'll tell you *here*. Always here. Born and bred, generations deep, in a town that didn't exist forty years ago."

"That's a lie," Quentin said.

"That's survival," Tam corrected. "It's a good lie. It's the kind of lie you build a life inside." He flicked the reins and the cart rattled forward down toward the gates. "Keep your hood up. Don't talk about the map in the open. And for the love of the Greywater, don't let anyone see you talking to empty air."

"I'm standing right here," Ilsa said, wounded.

"You know what I mean."

* * *

Inside the walls, Ostmere was louder than any place Quentin had ever been, and she found, to her surprise, that she didn't hate it.

Thornvale was quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Ostmere was loud the way a market is loud — vendors calling, children shrieking through the lanes, a fiddler playing something in a scale that bent wrong and then righted itself, hammers ringing off anvils, someone arguing cheerfully about the price of eels. The streets were narrow and crooked and smelled of frying onions and tallow and rain on hot cobblestone. People brushed past her shoulders without looking twice. Nobody looked twice at anybody. In Thornvale, Quentin had spent her whole life being looked at — watched sideways, always, in case her strangeness showed. Here she was just another stranger in a city of strangers pretending not to be strangers.

There was a freedom in that which nearly undid her.

They put the cart up at a stable yard off the fish-market square, and Tam went to argue with the stableman about price while Quentin stood in the yard and let herself simply *be* somewhere, anonymous, unnoticed, her hands empty at her sides.

"The bell tower," Ilsa said. She had drifted to the edge of the yard, and her face was turned upward, and the ghost-light around her had brightened despite herself despite everything. "Do you hear it?"

"I don't hear anything. It's not ringing."

"No. Listen anyway. Underneath." Ilsa's voice had gone strange and soft. "When they pulled down Vessamyre, the last thing to fall was the great bell of the Spire quarter. Too heavy to move whole. So they broke it into pieces and carried the pieces out, one family per piece, hidden in carts of grain and coffins and wedding chests. There are pieces of that bell in towers all along the border. And when the wind is right—" she closed her eyes, "—they ring against each other. Not sound exactly. More like memory of sound. The dead hear it. We've been hearing it for sixty years, all across the world, all our broken bell calling us, and no road leading home."

Quentin listened. The wind moved through the yard, and beneath the market noise, beneath the fiddle and the gulls and the hammering, she heard — or almost heard, or imagined she heard — a low bronze hum, like a finger run round the rim of a glass. Like something enormous trying to sing through a cracked throat.

Her eyes stung. She blinked hard and blamed the smoke.

"Quentin." Tam's voice, low, at her elbow. "Stableman says there's a woman two streets over mends fences and does odd ironwork. Name of Brigid. Says if we've got broken gear, she's honest and cheap and doesn't ask questions." He paused. "He also said it the way people say things when they want you to go see someone. Like it matters."

"Why would it matter?"

"He wouldn't say. But he got a look on his face." Tam scratched his jaw. "In my experience, when a man gets a look on his face instead of saying words, the words are the important part."

* * *

Brigid's forge-yard sat in a lane where three crooked streets collided, a chaos of wagons and wheel-rims and fence posts and one entire disassembled gate stacked against the wall like driftwood. The smell of coal-smoke and hot iron rolled out of an open shed, and in the shed, sleeves rolled to the shoulder, a broad-shouldered woman with iron-grey hair cropped short was holding a hinge in the coals with tongs, watching it with the total attention some people give to prayer.

Two children sat on a workbench nearby, legs swinging. A boy of maybe nine, dark-haired, solemn, sorting nails by size with the concentration of a general reviewing troops. A girl of six or seven, red-headed and gap-toothed, who was drawing on the dusty floor with a charred stick — drawing, Quentin saw when they came closer, a house. A very particular house, drawn over and over, getting more detailed each time: a house with a steep roof and a carved door and a garden wall, in a style Quentin had never seen but that made the map warm suddenly against her ribs.

The girl looked up first. She looked at Quentin, and then — Quentin's breath stopped — she looked slightly to the left of Quentin, directly at where Ilsa floated, and frowned, and rubbed her eyes, and went back to her drawing.

"She half-sees," Ilsa whispered. "Oh. Oh, she *half-sees*."

The woman at the forge set down her tongs and turned. Up close she was younger than her hair suggested, forty perhaps, with a sun-browned face and forearms corded with muscle and a small white scar crossing one eyebrow. Her eyes were pale green and very steady, and they went from Quentin to Tam to the cart outside, and then — briefly, quickly, so quickly Quentin might have missed it — to the space beside Quentin's left shoulder.

Something passed over Brigid's face. Not fear. Something older and sadder and more careful than fear.

"You'll be the ones the stableman sent," she said. Her voice was low and roughened, an accent underneath it that Quentin couldn't place — vowels shaped a little wrong, music from a song half-forgotten. "What's broken?"

"The cart axle sings on the left turn," Tam said. "And the right wheel wobbles."

"Bring it round. Axle's likely fine, just dry. Wheel wants re-shoeing." She wiped her hands on her apron, and then, instead of turning back to the forge, she kept looking at Quentin. Looking, Quentin realized, at the shape under her coat. At the map case. "You're carrying something," Brigid said. Not a question.

"A family heirloom," Quentin said, which was true, in a way she hadn't expected it to be.

"Mm." Brigid glanced at the children. "Piet, take Sorcha round the front and count the fence posts in the yard. All of them. Twice."

"We counted them yesterday," the boy said.

"Then you'll get better at counting today."

The children went, the girl Sorcha dragging her feet and complaining, the boy Piet walking with the grave precision of someone who intended to count correctly. When the lane had swallowed them, Brigid shut the shed door and stood facing them in the smoky half-dark, arms folded.

"Now," she said. "Show me what's warming against your heart, girl, before I decide whether to throw you out or kiss you."

Tam made a noise that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Quentin's hands were shaking as she drew out the map case. She told herself it was the cold. She opened the case and lifted the map free, and in the dimness of the forge it seemed to glow faintly, its ink-lines shifting like currents under ice, roads breathing in and out of existence across the shape of a country that was not supposed to exist.

Brigid looked at it for a long time.

Then she sat down. Not gracefully — she simply sat, onto the nearest stool, heavily, the way people sit when their legs make the decision without consulting them. She reached out one calloused hand and hovered it above the map without touching, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping child or a live coal.

"That's the Salt Road," she said hoarsely. "That's — those are the orchard terraces above Kelvarra. My grandmother's house was — there. *There.*" Her finger trembled above a spot near the map's western edge. "I was four when we left. I don't remember the house. I remember the smell of the kitchen and I remember my mother crying into dough so I wouldn't see, and I have spent forty years not knowing whether the house was real or something I invented out of grief." She looked up, and her pale green eyes were wet and furious. "Where did you get this."

Quentin told her. The ford, the frostbitten woman, the leather case, the words *it chose you*, the voice joining the chorus in her bones. She told it plainly, leaving out nothing except the parts she couldn't yet say aloud — the healer's verdict, the wardstones, the rite waiting for her like a knife under snow. Some part of her expected disbelief. Some part of her expected Brigid to be afraid, the way everyone in Thornvale had ever been afraid of her.

Brigid listened with her jaw tight and her hands flat on her knees, and when Quentin finished, she was silent a moment, and then she said:

"And the dead woman. Is she here now?"

Quentin's throat closed. She nodded.

"What's her name?"

"Ilsa."

"Ilsa." Brigid said it carefully, tasting it, and then she bowed her head — actually bowed, a small formal dip, the way you'd greet an elder — toward the empty air at Quentin's left. "Ilsa. My mother's name was Ilse. They took a lot from us, but they never managed to take the names. We handed them down like contraband." She looked back at Quentin, and something in her face had settled, resolved, become fixed as welded iron. "Sixty years my people have pretended to be from nowhere. Sixty years of teaching our children lies about their own blood, because the kingdoms said an erased country can't have refugees, only ghosts. And now a girl walks into my yard carrying the shape of home in a leather tube, with one of our dead at her shoulder, and asks me nothing at all."

"I wasn't asking—"

"You were asking by existing." Brigid stood. She was taller than Quentin had realized. "Here's what happens now. I fix your axle. I feed you supper, because you look like famine walked you here. And then you tell me where this map goes, and what it wants, and whether there's a door at the end of it — because I have two children upstairs who draw a house they've never seen, every day, in the dust, and if there is even one chance in ten thousand that house is real, then I am coming with you, and you do not get a vote."

* * *

Supper was in a room above the forge, low-ceilinged and warm, crowded with a table, a stove, a loom pushed to one wall, and more life than the room should have held. Brigid cooked the way she worked — fast, certain, generous — barley stew with root vegetables and too much pepper, dark bread, honey from a jar with no label. Piet sat across from Quentin and studied her with open, unblinking gravity while he ate, and finally said:

"You've got a person with you. The not-alive kind."

"Piet," Brigid said.

"It's all right," Quentin said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Yes. I do. Her name is Ilsa. She was a mapmaker."

Sorcha, sitting beside her brother, leaned toward Quentin confidingly. "I see them sometimes. Mostly just edges. Like when you look at a fish under water." She pointed her spoon at the air beside Quentin. "Hers is goldish. Is she nice?"

"She's the nicest dead person I know," Quentin said, and Sorcha nodded seriously, satisfied, and returned to her stew.

And Quentin sat there in the steam and the clatter and the firelight, in a room full of people who had learned to carry impossible things without setting them down, and felt something crack gently open in her chest.

This was what it looked like. This was what she had never had and hadn't known to mourn. A family that made room. A table where the strange was just another dish passed hand to hand. Piet counting nails like a soldier; Sorcha drawing her grandmother's house from a memory she'd inherited in the womb; Brigid bowing to a ghost and meaning it. None of them hiding. None of them exiled. Displaced, all of them, from a country that no longer existed — and somehow, impossibly, *home* anyway, because home turned out to be not a place but a practice, something you did daily, like mending fences, like feeding strangers, like handing down names like contraband.

Thornvale had never once made room for her. It had only ever made a space she was allowed to occupy, provisionally, as long as she hid the largest part of herself. She had called that home for twenty-six years because she'd had no other word.

She had another word now.

Under the table, the map case rested against her boot, warm as a sleeping animal. Above the stove, the kettle sang. Ilsa drifted near the window, looking out at the crooked rooftops and the broken bell-tower, humming — very softly, barely a thread of sound — a lullaby in a language Quentin didn't know, and Sorcha's head came up from her bowl, and the child hummed three notes of it back without seeming to notice she'd done it, and Ilsa pressed both her translucent hands to her mouth and wept without sound.

Later, when the children were asleep in the loft and Tam had gone out to check the cart with the thoroughness of a man avoiding gratitude, Brigid spread the map on the scrubbed table and weighted its corners with salt cellars, and Quentin traced the road east with one fingertip, feeling it wake under her touch.

"There," she said. "Past the Greywater, past the hills. The road only exists while you walk it in trust. It ends at a door."

"A door," Brigid repeated.

"To Cindral. What's left of it. Where the dead have been waiting sixty years for someone to carry them home." Quentin looked up. "The winter that's falling early and wrong — that's them. That's the country grieving. And it finishes falling by solstice unless someone opens the door."

Brigid was quiet for a long moment, firelight moving on her scarred brow. Then she said, "Solstice is nineteen days away."

"I know."

"And the road eats doubters."

"So I'm told."

Brigid nodded slowly, and began rolling up her sleeves again, though there was no work left to do at this hour except the work ahead. "Then we leave at first light," she said, "and you'd best sleep, because—"

The knock came at the street door. Three knocks, precise, evenly spaced, the knock of someone who has never in their life needed to ask entry.

Through the shutter slats, Quentin saw lanterns in the lane below — six of them, moving in formation, and men in grey livery with a sigil she didn't know: a crown made of thorns, inverted.

Beside her, very quietly, Ilsa said: "Wardens. *Hide the map.*"
