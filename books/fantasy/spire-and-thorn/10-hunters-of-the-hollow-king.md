## 10. Hunters of the Hollow King

Brigid had mended fences for twenty years, and she knew the sound of something breaking that shouldn't.

It came in the hour before dawn, when Ostmere was at its greyest — a long, splintering crack from the direction of the market square, followed by a silence so complete it seemed to press against her ears. She was already awake. She had been awake most of the night, lying beside Sorcha and little Dev with one hand on each of them, listening to Quentin talk in her sleep in voices that were not her own.

The crack came again. Then shouting — not the ordinary shouting of Ostmere mornings, which was mostly fishwives and carters arguing about axle-widths, but the flat, disciplined barking of men who had been trained to shout together.

She sat up in the dark of the loft above the chandler's shop and felt the cold come through the walls like a message.

*They've found us,* said something in her chest, an old animal knowing, older than thought. *They've finally found us.*

She had spent eleven years in Ostmere teaching herself not to flinch at boots on cobblestones. Eleven years learning to look bored when strangers asked where she'd come from, to say *nowhere, around, doesn't matter* with the right dullness in her voice. She had built a life out of smallness: a fence-mender's trade, two children no one could trace to any registry, a face she kept carefully forgettable. And every night before sleep she did what all of them in Ostmere did, what the whole town did without ever speaking of it — she touched the little knot of Cindrali wool sewn inside her collar, the one thing she'd carried out of the burning, and she whispered the old greeting into the dark.

*Sleep well, home. I remember you.*

The town remembered together, silently, and that shared silence had been armor for eleven years.

It wasn't going to be enough tonight.

* * *

She went down the ladder quietly and found Tam already at the door, dressed, his ferryman's coat buttoned wrong, staring out through the gap in the shutters.

"How many?" Brigid asked.

"Twelve I can see." Tam's voice was gravel over deep water. "Grey cloaks. Iron masks." He swallowed. "Wardens."

The word dropped through the room like a stone through ice. Everyone in Ostmere knew the stories. Wardens served the Hollow King, the power that had unmade Cindral — the hunger that had eaten a whole country off the face of the world and left only refugees and ghosts and a hole in every map. They took the displaced. They took anything that remembered.

And now they had come to a town full of people who remembered nothing out loud, because one woman had walked in off the road carrying a map that shouldn't exist.

"Where's Quentin?"

"Upstairs. Carving." Tam's jaw worked. "Been carving since midnight. Says the bones started screaming."

Brigid closed her eyes. Of course they had. The dead would know before anyone. The dead always knew first.

"Sorcha. Dev." She turned for the ladder, and her heart was already running ahead of her, up into the loft where her children slept. "We're leaving. Now."

* * *

Getting two children out of bed without waking the street was a skill Brigid had hoped never to use again. She woke them with a hand over Sorcha's mouth and a whisper against her ear — *remember the game, remember the quiet game* — and Dev simply opened his eyes, seven years old and solemn as a priest, and nodded as if he'd been waiting all night for this. Children always knew. That was the cruelty of it. Adults could pretend; children just packed their fear away in their faces and waited.

By the time she got them down, Quentin was standing at the bottom of the ladder, pale as tallow, a half-finished bone charm still clutched in one fist. Ilsa stood behind her — the ghost visible now even to Brigid's untrained eyes, a woman made of frost-light and patience, her head tilted toward the window.

"They're not searching house by house," Quentin said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "Not yet. They're reading the streets."

"Reading them how?" Tam demanded.

"The way I read the map." Quentin's fingers tightened around the charm. "Ilsa says the Hollow King's wardens can feel memory. Places where people remember too hard. Ostmere is—" she laughed, a thin, terrible sound, "—Ostmere is a bonfire of remembering, and we lit a torch on top of it. The map. Me. The dead I carry. We're the loudest thing in this valley."

Outside, the disciplined shouting had stopped. That was worse. Men who stopped shouting had started doing.

Brigid did what she had done once before, eleven years ago, on a different night, in a different country that was busy dying. She became very calm. It was a trick her mother had taught her in the last kitchen she ever cooked in: *when the world burns, girl, you get quiet, because quiet is the only tool left.* She banked the stove, threw water on the embers, took down the go-bag she kept behind the flour bin the way other women kept keepsakes — bandages, salt, dried apples, a knife, three charms from the orchard-lore her grandmother had taught her, the kind woven from rowan and grief.

"Tam, back door. Quentin, you carry Dev if he tires. Ilsa—" she hesitated, absurdly, at addressing a ghost, then pushed through it, "—can you see in the dark?"

*I have been dark for eighty years,* Ilsa said, and her smile was sad and bright. *Lead me anywhere.*

They went out through the chandler's yard, past the racks of tallow candles sweating in the cold, into the alley that ran behind the cooperage. Brigid carried Dev on her hip though he was far too big for it; he wrapped his arms around her neck and put his mouth against her collarbone and made no sound at all, which frightened her more than crying would have.

They might have made it. She has wondered since whether they might have, if the wardens hadn't brought fire.

* * *

The first building went up while they were still in the alley — a rush of orange light over the rooftops and a sound like the world inhaling. Then another. The wardens weren't searching Ostmere. They were burning it, block by block, driving everyone into the open like rabbits from a warren, because a crowd of fleeing refugees was easy to read and easy to cut.

"They're flushing us," Tam snarled. "Clever bastards. Herd the whole town and pick out the ones that run *wrong*."

"Then we run right," Brigid said. "Like everyone else. Screaming. Toward the west gate with all the rest."

Sorcha grabbed her skirt. "Mama—"

"I know, love. I know." She hauled both children close. "We're going to play the quiet game in the middle of everything. Can you do that? Loudest night of your life, and you make no sound at all?"

Sorcha set her jaw — Brigid saw her own stubbornness land on the child's face like inheritance — and nodded.

They joined the flood. It was the hardest thing Brigid had ever done deliberately: walking into the smoke, into the press of terrified neighbors, carrying the two most precious things in the world straight through the thing hunting them. Embers came down like orange snow. Somewhere a cow was screaming in a burning byre. Old Wenna from the dye-house stumbled past with her arms full of nothing, just holding air, and Brigid caught her elbow and pointed her west and let go, because she couldn't save everyone, and the arithmetic of that had been killing her slowly since Cindral fell.

Quentin walked beside her, white-faced, lips moving constantly — naming people, Brigid realized. Naming the dead as they happened, under her breath, a litany: *the dyer's man Aldous, the carter's horse, the woman in the blue shawl.* Even now, even here, the girl was carving. Carving with her voice.

"There," Ilsa said suddenly, and her voice cut through the roar like a blade through silk. "The tall one, by the well. Grey cloak. He's not looking at the crowd. He's looking at *her*."

Brigid looked. A warden stood apart from the chaos, iron mask catching the firelight, utterly still amid the running — and his head turned, slow as a weathervane, tracking Quentin through the smoke.

He raised one gloved hand. Two more wardens peeled out of the shadows of the tithe-barn and began to move — not running, *gliding*, the way things move in nightmares, closing on the crowd's edge like scissors.

"West gate's lost," Tam breathed. "They'll have it. North — the tanner's lane, there's a gap in the wall where the palisade rotted, I've poached eels through it since I was a boy—"

"Tanner's lane," Brigid agreed, and swung Dev higher on her hip, and ran.

* * *

The tanner's lane stank of lime and old blood, which was, tonight, a mercy — no one burned it early because no one wanted to stand near it. They went up it single file, Tam leading, the firelight falling away behind them until the dark closed in friendly and complete. Brigid counted footsteps the way she'd learned to count fenceposts: steady, steady, steady, don't think about what's behind you.

Dev was shaking against her shoulder. Not crying. Shaking. She pressed her lips to his hair. "Almost, my heart. Almost."

The gap in the palisade was exactly where Tam said — a rotted seam in the timber wide enough for a slim adult, tight for a man Tam's size. He went through sideways, cursing softly, and reached back for Sorcha.

That was when the warden came over the wall.

No climbing. No effort. One moment the top of the palisade was empty black against the burning sky; the next there was a grey-cloaked figure standing on it, balanced impossibly, iron mask tilted down at them like a bird considering worms. Firelight slid across the mask and Brigid saw, with a lurch, that it wasn't a mask at all. There was no face behind it. Just smooth iron, and a voice coming from nowhere, from everywhere, from the hollow inside it:

"*The map. The carrier. Give them, and the rest may go.*"

"No," Brigid said.

It came out of her before thought, before strategy, before the survival instinct that had governed eleven careful years. Just *no*, flat and final, the same voice she used on bulls and bad weather and men who tried to cheat her over fence posts.

The warden's head cocked. "*Mother. You remember. We can smell it on you. Cindrali wool. Rowan charms. You are a nest of remembering.*" The voice softened into something horribly gentle. "*Give us the map and we will leave your children whole.*"

Every clever lie Brigid had ever told herself about safety — keep small, keep dull, keep silent, and they'll pass you by — died in that moment, burned up like the town behind her. Because this was the truth underneath all those years: they had never passed her by. They had simply not yet wanted anything she had. Safety bought by smallness was never owned. It was rented, and the rent came due.

"You'll leave nothing," she said, "because you're not getting anything," and she shoved Sorcha through the gap into Tam's arms, spun, and put herself between the wall and her son.

The warden came down off the palisade like a dropped stone.

She had a fence-mender's hands, and she used them. She caught the descending arm — iron-cold, impossibly strong — and turned with it the way you turn a falling gate, using its own weight, buying one heartbeat, two. The warden's other hand raked across her side and it felt like being opened by winter itself, cold first and then a hot bloom of wrongness spreading under her ribs, and she heard someone scream and understood distantly that it was her.

But she didn't fall backward. She fell *forward*, deliberately, wrapping her arms around the thing's iron head and dragging it down with her into the mud of the tanner's lane, pinning it under her body for the three seconds that mattered.

"GO!" she roared at Quentin. "Take them THROUGH!"

* * *

Hands pulled her off — Tam, huge and furious, hauling her bodily through the gap while Quentin shoved Dev ahead of her. The last thing Brigid saw of Ostmere was the warden rising out of the mud, unhurried, unhurt, its blank iron face turning to watch them go. Not chasing. Marking. Filing them away.

The woods took them. Black pines, wet needles, the fire turning the low clouds the color of an infected wound overhead. They ran until Sorcha stumbled, until Dev sobbed openly, until Brigid's side screamed so badly that Tam simply picked her up and carried her the way she'd carried her son, and she was too hurt and too proud to protest, and did both anyway.

They stopped in a hollow beneath a lightning-split oak, miles out, where the fire was a red glow on the clouds and nothing else. Tam set her down gently as laying a plank. Quentin knelt, hands hovering over the wound, helpless — a maker of charms, not a healer. Ilsa crouched opposite, frost-lit, her ghost-hands pressed to the torn flesh as if she could hold the cold in.

"Maren would know what to do," Quentin whispered, and then, bitterly: "Maren would call it cleansing."

"It's not poison," Brigid managed. Her teeth were chattering. "I've had worse from a boar." It was a lie and everyone knew it. The wound didn't bleed right. The edges were pale, frost-pale, and the cold was spreading outward from it in fine white filaments, like frost ferning across a windowpane. Like the winter itself had gotten into her.

"The Hollow King's touch," Ilsa said quietly. "It doesn't kill. It *claims*. Slowly. Unless—"

"Unless what?" Tam snapped.

"Unless she reaches warmth that remembers her." The ghost looked at Quentin. "Or unless the map chooses to spend something on her. It does that, sometimes. For its own."

Quentin pulled the map case from her coat with shaking hands. In the darkness the leather was faintly warm, faintly gold-veined, like something alive sleeping. She laid it against Brigid's wound, and the frost-filaments slowed — slowed, but did not stop.

"It's not enough," Quentin said. "It needs somewhere. Some *where*. A place that remembers her hard enough to fight for her." Her eyes went wide, and she looked up, and Brigid watched the idea land on her like a bird landing on a branch. "The Salt Orchard. Ilsa — the orchard remembers everyone Cindral ever grew. If we can reach it—"

"Three days' walk," Ilsa said. "Through country the wardens watch."

"Two, if we don't sleep," Tam said grimly.

Brigid lay back against the split oak and looked up at her strange, impossible family: a gruff ferryman rolling his sleeves, a haunted girl pressing a living map to her wound, a dead cartographer keeping vigil, and her two children — her fierce, terrified, magnificent children — sitting on either side of her, each holding one of her hands, neither willing to let go even to wipe their eyes.

Eleven years of smallness. Eleven years of *don't be noticed, don't be known, don't be worth taking.*

Well. She was worth taking now. She was bleeding proof of it.

"Then we walk," Brigid said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because she was done being quiet. "And when they come again — and they will come again — they're going to learn what a fence-mender does to things that climb her wall."

Above the black pines, the false dawn came up the color of ash, and somewhere behind them Ostmere finished burning, and somewhere ahead, three days through watched country, an orchard of salt and memory waited with the only medicine that could save her.

And in the wound beneath her ribs, patient as winter, something cold began, very softly, to hum.
