## 13. What Winter Wants

Tam Vetch had spent forty years learning the language of water, and water had taught him one lesson above all others: everything that moves wants something. The current wanted the sea. The rain wanted the earth. The flood wanted to take what it could carry and leave the rest rearranged. He had never once trusted a thing that claimed to want nothing.

So when the winter began speaking through his sister's ghost, he did not flinch and he did not pray. He listened, because listening was how you learned what a thing wanted, and knowing what a thing wanted was the beginning of knowing how to fight it.

They were three days out of the salt orchard when it happened.

* * *

The orchard had been a strange softness in the middle of their hard road — Brigid mending coats by firelight, the children shrieking over Tam's crooked card games, Ilsa humming that lullaby that made even the horses go quiet. Tam had hated how much he'd liked it. Softness was a debt you didn't know you'd taken on until the collector came. He'd crossed enough rivers to know that the gentlest banks hid the deepest holes.

Now they were back in the open country, following the map north and east toward the place Quentin called the Traitor's Door, and the cold had changed its character. That was the thing nobody else seemed willing to say aloud. Cold was weather. This was attention.

It came down in long slow spirals, each flake falling as if it had all the time in the world, as if it were looking for something on its way down. The wind didn't howl; it murmured. And at night, when they made camp in the lee of a fallen wall, the frost crept across the ground in patterns too deliberate to be accident — ferns of ice that branched like roads, like rivers, like the veins of a hand.

"Your sister," Quentin said quietly, on the third evening. She was sitting across the fire from him, her carving knife idle in her lap, a half-finished charm of birch wood forgotten beside her. "She's been close all day. Closer than the others."

Tam fed a stick to the flames. "Nessa always did hate being ignored."

"I'm not ignoring her. I just — I can't hear her clearly. The dead are loud here, but she's..." Quentin frowned, searching for the word. "Muffled. Like she's speaking through a wall. Or holding something back."

Brigid looked up from the children's bedrolls. Pip and Wren were asleep already, curled together against the cold like puppies, and Brigid kept her voice low out of habit rather than need. "You think she's trying not to scare him."

"I think," Quentin said, "she's waiting for him to be ready."

Tam snorted. "Nessa never waited for anything in her life. When we were young she once jumped the Greywater on a dare with the ice barely frozen, and when I told her she'd drown she said—" His voice caught, an old rusted hinge. He cleared his throat. "She said drowning was just swimming where the river got to keep you."

The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the light, the snow went on falling with its terrible patience.

"She said that?" Quentin asked softly.

"Nessa said a lot of things." Tam stared into the fire. "Most of them true, which was the worst part about her."

He hadn't spoken of her in years. Not properly. There had been no one to speak of her to — the ferryman's drowned sister was not a story Thornvale or any town along the Greywater wanted told, because Nessa Vetch had died in Cindral's war without ever setting foot in Cindral, and griefs that came from erased countries had nowhere to be buried.

But now there was Quentin, who heard the dead the way Tam heard water. And now there was this strange little family of strays — Brigid with her scarred hands and her borrowed children, Ilsa walking visible as moonlight — and Tam found, to his considerable irritation, that the story was climbing his throat on its own.

"She was older than me by two years," he said. "Stronger, faster, meaner, kinder. When the war came up the river valley — the old war, the one nobody names — refugees came down the Greywater from the north, and half the towns turned them away. Not Nessa. She rowed out to meet every boat herself. Said the river didn't care where you were born, so neither did she."

Ilsa had gone very still by the edge of the firelight. Ghosts didn't feel cold, Tam had noticed, but they could look like they were remembering it.

"The last boat she rowed out to," he continued, "was carrying people from a country none of us had ever heard of. They spoke a language like water going over stones. They had nothing. And Nessa brought them home to our mother's house and fed them, and one of them — a woman with burn-scarred hands — taught Nessa a song. A mourning song, she said. For a country that was dying." He looked up at Ilsa. "That was the lullaby. The one you hum. I'd know it anywhere. Nessa used to sing it after, all wrong, off-key, driving everyone mad. She said somebody ought to remember it, since apparently nobody else would."

Ilsa's expression was the kind of stillness that comes before avalanches. "Your sister carried a piece of Cindral," she said. "All these years. In her voice."

"And then the wardens came," Tam said flatly. "The Hollow King's men, sweeping the river valley for anyone who'd sheltered the exiles. Nessa wouldn't give them names. So they gave the Greywater hers." He turned his hands palm-up, the way he did when he talked about the river, an old ferryman's habit — showing the water he held nothing. "They weighted her and dropped her off my own ferry. My own deck. I was sixteen. I heard the splash and I knew, and I have been crossing that river every day since, because the dead of the Greywater whisper, and I thought maybe someday one of them would be her, and I could at least tell her I was sorry I didn't jump in after her."

"You couldn't have," Brigid said. "You'd have drowned too."

"Aye," Tam said. "That was the general consensus." He let out a breath that fogged in the firelight. "And now she's here, walking beside us in the snow, and she won't come close, and I'd very much like to know why."

Quentin picked up the birch-wood charm. Looked at it. Looked at him.

"I can make you a door to her," she said. "If you want one. It'll cost you. Hearing the dead always costs."

Tam had watched her carve bone and wood for weeks now, had seen the way voices moved through her hands like current through a lock. He knew what it took out of her. He also knew that some debts you don't get to refuse forever.

"Do it," he said.

* * *

She carved while the others slept, and Tam sat watch beside her, which was to say he sat witness. The knife whispered through the birch. Frost feathered up the blade as she worked, though the fire was close, and the shapes the frost made were those branching road-patterns again, and Tam understood suddenly that the winter wasn't falling around them.

It was gathering. Listening. Waiting to see what they would do.

"Ready?" Quentin asked.

"No," Tam said honestly. "Go on anyway."

She pressed the small carved bird into his hands — a river tern, wings folded, because Nessa had loved terns, had claimed they were the only birds honest enough to live on both water and air. The wood warmed against his palms. Then it went cold, colder than the night, and the world tilted the way a boat tilts when a wave comes under it, and Tam Vetch was standing on the deck of his father's ferry again, forty years younger, watching the water close over his sister.

Except this time the water opened.

*Nessa.*

She rose out of the dark like a woman surfacing from a long swim — not the girl he remembered but something truer: drowned and dried and weathered and patient, her hair full of silt-stars, her eyes the grey-green of deep water in winter. She looked at him the way she'd looked at him across the breakfast table every morning of their childhood: as if he were slow, and beloved, and she had all the patience in the world for exactly one of those things.

*Tam.* Her voice was the river's voice, layered, many-currented. *You stubborn old barge-dog. You finally stopped rowing long enough to listen.*

"I've been listening for forty years," Tam said, and his voice cracked like ice in spring. "You never came."

*I couldn't. Not whole. Don't you understand yet? None of us can come whole, because there's no door. There's no home to come TO. We're scattered, Tam. All of us — me, and the ones from the boats, and the ones from the war, thousands upon thousands, drifting in the cold between places. And the winter—*

She stopped. Behind her, in the vision's dark, the snow was falling upward, and Tam felt the temperature drop through his bones into whatever lay beneath bones.

*The winter is here,* Nessa said, and her voice changed — deepened, widened, became not one voice but a chorus the size of a sky. *Ask your question, ferryman. You've been asking it since you were sixteen. Ask.*

Tam's hands shook around the little wooden tern. But he was a ferryman, and ferrymen do not flinch from what the water carries.

"What do you want?" he said. "This winter — the cold, the early snow, the killing season coming down on the whole world — what do you WANT?"

The silence lasted a long time. Long enough for him to hear the fire behind him, the children breathing, Quentin's sharp intake of breath as the cold reached her too.

*Everyone asks that,* the winter said through his sister's mouth, and there was something almost amused in it, vast and weary. *They assume we want destruction. The living always assume the dead want company in ruin. But consider, ferryman: what does a grave want? What does a locked house want? What does a name that has been struck from every book want?*

"I don't know," Tam said.

*We want to be REMEMBERED,* the winter said, and the word came down like an avalanche, like a mountain of snow sliding, and every flake of it was a voice. *Not mourned. Mourned is easy. Mourned is a stone with a name worn smooth. Remembered. Truly. The streets of Ostmere. The salt orchards. The songs. The quarrels. The king who would not stop and the door-keeper who opened the way and the ten thousand small kindnesses of a country that lived for nine hundred years and was unwritten in a single season. We are not angry, ferryman. Anger requires a future. We are GRIEVING. And our grief has become weather, because no one will hold it with us.*

"So the cold," Tam said slowly, working it through the way he'd work a difficult current, "isn't an attack."

*It is a question,* the winter said. *Asked over and over, louder and louder, because no one answers. Remember us truly, or forget us entirely — those are the only doors left. And if the world chooses forgetting, then the snow will fall until it has covered every map, every song, every carrier of the memory. Including you. Including the cartographer's map. Including the girl with the bone-charms. We do not wish it. But a grief unheld must go somewhere, and we are so tired, and the solstice is coming, and after the solstice there are no more questions. Only silence.*

Tam stood in the falling-upward snow and felt the shape of it settle onto him: not doom, exactly. Duty. The worst kind — the kind you couldn't fight, only carry.

"And if we remember?" he asked. "If we carry it? What then?"

*Then,* said the winter, and beneath the vastness of it Tam heard — unmistakably, heartbreakingly — his sister's own voice, young and off-key and alive, singing the mourning song for a country she'd never seen, *then the grief has somewhere to go. Then the snow can stop falling. Then we can rest, or cross, or stay — but CHOSEN, ferryman. That is all we ever wanted. To be chosen by memory instead of abandoned by it.*

The vision thinned. Nessa's face resolved out of the chorus one last time, and she smiled at him, and it was sixteen years old and forty years old all at once.

*Tell the girl,* she said. *The keeper. Tell her it isn't a chain she's being offered, whatever the king says. Chains hold. Memory doesn't hold — memory CARRIES. There's a difference, Tam. You of all people should know the difference. You've been carrying me across that river your whole life, and I never once felt chained.*

"Nessa—"

*The Door knows a lie from the truth,* she said, already fading, silt-stars scattering. *And so does the winter. Bring us someone who understands the difference. Bring us—*

The cold snapped shut like a lock. Tam was on his knees in the snow by a dying fire, the wooden tern white with frost in his fists, and Quentin was gripping his shoulder, and Brigid had the children gathered against her, awake now, all of them staring.

* * *

He told them all of it. The words came hard at first and then all at once, the way floodwater comes, and when he finished the camp was silent except for the fire rebuilding itself from Brigid's careful hands.

"So it's not a siege," Brigid said at last. "It's a plea. The whole winter — every storm, every freezing village — it's one enormous plea for witness."

"And if we fail," Tam said, "if the solstice passes without anyone remembering Cindral truly, it stops being a plea. It becomes a burial. Of the memory and everyone carrying it." He looked at Quentin. "Including you. The map chose you. You're a carrier now. Nessa said so."

Quentin was turning the birch tern over in her fingers, her face lit orange and shadow. "The king will offer me a chain," she said quietly. "Ilsa's memories showed me that much. Containment. Safety. Keep the dead locked away forever and the world stays safe and cold." She looked up. "But that's not what the winter wants. The winter wants the opposite of a chain. It wants hands. Open ones."

"Then we'd best make sure you reach that Door with your hands open," Brigid said, and there was iron under the gentleness, the tone she used when she meant a thing past all argument. "Whatever's waiting there. Traitor, king, or grief the size of a sky."

Tam looked east, where the snow fell in its patient spirals, and for the first time in forty years the cold felt less like an enemy and more like a congregation — thousands upon thousands of voices, waiting to see whether anyone would finally say their names.

"We move at first light," he said. "The river taught me one more thing, if nothing else. You don't outrun a flood."

He stood, joints cracking, and nodded toward the darkness where his sister had been.

"You build a bridge."
