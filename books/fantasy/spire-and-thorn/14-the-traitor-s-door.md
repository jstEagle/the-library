## 14. The Traitor's Door

The mountain had been growing in the window of the world for two days before any of us said its name aloud.

It did not grow the way mountains do, by slow approach — it grew the way a bruise does, surfacing out of nothing, as if the land beneath our feet had been carrying it all along and had only now decided to show us. Black stone veined with white, like bone seen through frostbitten skin. And set into its face, half a mile up a stair that switchbacked like a scar, was a door.

Not a door the way houses have doors. A door the way wounds have edges. It stood alone in the rock, taller than three men, made of a wood so dark it drank the light, and around its frame ran carvings so worn they might have been letters or might have been rain damage or might have been both — words rubbed almost smooth by centuries of hands touching them and then never coming back.

"The Last Door," Ilsa said, when we finally stopped at the tree line to look at it. Her voice came through my bones the way it always did now, but there was something new underneath it, something I had not heard from her before. Not fear exactly. Shame has a different weight than fear. Fear is cold; shame is heavy.

"You knew this place," I said. Not a question. The dead are hard to lie to when you carry their names in your hands.

"I guarded it," she said. "For eleven years. Before."

Before. The word hung there with all its cargo. Before the war. Before the Hollow King. Before the door-keeper who opened Cindral's throat from the inside.

Behind me, Tam Vetch spat into the snow, which was his way of saying he'd heard something he didn't like. Brigid said nothing at all, but her hand found the back of Fen's collar and stayed there, the way it did whenever her children were within reach of anything older than they were. Little Sef just looked at the door with the frank, unblinking interest of a seven-year-old who had not yet learned which things were supposed to frighten him.

"It's warm," he announced.

We all looked at him.

"Can't you feel it?" He held up both mittened hands toward the black mass of the mountain, palms open, the way children hold them out to fires. "It's like standing near an oven."

I felt it too, then, once he'd given me permission. A faint pressure against my skin, like the air before a storm, but steady. Patient. The map, strapped inside my coat against my chest, had been warm for days — since the Greywater, maybe longer — and now it warmed further, a slow heartbeat of heat against my sternum.

*Come home,* it seemed to say. *You're nearly here.*

*Home,* answered every ghost I carried, in a hundred voices, in a chorus that had become as familiar as my own breathing. *Home home home.*

* * *

We climbed the stair in the grey hour before dusk, because Brigid refused to arrive anywhere in the dark with children, and because Tam said — gruffly, staring at the middle distance — that his sister's voice had been getting louder the closer we came, and he wanted this over with while he still had daylight and his wits about him.

The stair was cut from the same black stone as the mountain, and it should have been slick with ice, should have been impassable in the deep wrong-winter that had followed us across three countries. But the steps were clear. Swept, almost. As if someone had been keeping them.

"That's wrong," Tam muttered, kicking at a drift that wasn't there. "Snow doesn't respect stairs."

"Someone lives here," Brigid said quietly. She had her mending knife out, the one she used for fence wire, held low along her thigh. She'd been holding it that way since Ostmere burned. "Someone's kept these stairs for a long time."

Ilsa walked beside me, visible now to all of us in the thin places — and this whole mountain was a thin place, the air itself seemed stretched and translucent — her soldier's stride unchanged by death. She had not spoken since the tree line. I caught her looking at the carved frame of the distant door and then looking away, quick, like a woman passing a house where something terrible had happened to her.

Halfway up, we found the first marker.

It was a bone charm, hanging from an iron hook driven into the rock beside the stair. Small. A bird, maybe, or a hand. The carving style stopped my breath, because I knew it — not the specific piece, but the grammar of it. The way the lines curved into each other instead of stopping. The way the maker had left the natural grain to do half the work.

It was the way I carve.

"Quentin." Brigid's voice, careful. "Keep walking."

But I couldn't. My fingers were already rising toward the little bone bird, and when they touched it, the whisper came — faint, threadbare, a voice worn down to almost nothing:

*—cold, so long, so cold, is anyone—*

I snatched my hand back. The voice cut off like a pinched wick.

"There are more," Sef said, pointing up the stair. He was right. Every twenty paces, another hook, another charm. Birds, hands, doors, small figures with no faces. Dozens of them. Hundreds. All carved in a hand like mine. All whispering, if you had ears for it, the same starving syllable: *anyone, anyone, anyone.*

"He's been listening," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "All this time. He's been leaving charms to catch whatever passes, and nothing ever has, and he just keeps—" I stopped. Swallowed. "He's lonely," I said, and hated how much I understood it.

Tam looked at me strangely. "How would you know that?"

Because I know what it is to shape bone and hear the dead come through. Because I know what it is to do it in a place that calls it sickness. Because I spent thirty years in Thornvale learning to be quiet enough that even I sometimes forgot I was screaming.

"Because the work tells you," I said. "If you've done it long enough."

* * *

The hut sat on the last shelf of stone below the door, squat and dark, roofed in slate, chimney smoking a thin grey thread. Smoke. After everything — after the wardens, after the burning streets of Ostmere, after the Hollow King's hunters who had chased us through two mountain passes — smoke meant a living person, and a living person meant either salvation or the end of us, and I could not tell which from the smell of the woodsmoke.

Brigid positioned herself between the hut and the children. Tam unslung his river-pole, the closest thing he owned to a weapon. Ilsa drifted ahead of us all, because what did she have left to lose, and knocked on the door with a fist that passed through it without a sound.

The door opened anyway.

He was old. That was the first thing, and it shouldn't have been shocking, and it was — shocking the way a landslide is shocking, slowly and then all at once. He was old in a way that seemed structural, like the mountain had decided to grow a man out of its own weathered stone. His beard was white and reached his belt. His hands — and I could not stop looking at his hands — were spotted and knotted and scarred in the particular pattern of a carver's life: nicks across the fingertips, a thickening of the thumb from decades of bracing bone against a blade.

Around his neck, on a chain of dull iron, hung a key the size of a forearm.

"Well," he said. His voice was dry as old glue. "The map finally picked someone."

"You know about the map." I stepped forward before Brigid could stop me. Something was pulling me toward him, some gravity I didn't consent to. "Then you know what it wants. You know about the winter, about solstice, about—"

"I know everything about it." He turned and walked back into the hut, which I suppose was an invitation, because no one who meant to kill you bothers to put a kettle on. We crowded in after him — all except Ilsa, who stopped at the threshold like a dog at a gate it has been beaten behind.

The inside of the hut was the inside of my worst imaginings. Charms covered every surface. Bone birds and bone hands and bone faces hung from the rafters in their hundreds, turning slowly in the draft, and the whispering was deafening to me — a tide of *anyone, anyone, anyone* — though I could see from the others' faces that they heard only wind. On the table lay carvings in progress, tools worn to nubs, a bowl of water gone to ice. And on the walls, drawn in charcoal with a cartographer's precision, were maps. Hundreds of maps. Of valleys, of rivers, of roads that ended nowhere. Maps of countries that no longer existed.

Maps of Cindral. Drawn from memory, over and over, until the memory must have worn smooth as the carvings.

"You're him," I said. "The door-keeper."

The old man set the kettle on its hook and did not turn around. "Vessik," he said. "My name was Vessik. Though I suppose the histories call me something shorter."

*Traitor,* said Ilsa, from the doorway, in a voice like a blade being drawn.

"Yes," Vessik agreed mildly, to her, to the room, to the years. "That too."

* * *

He fed us first. Barley and salt pork and tea brewed from something piney, and the children ate like wolves because children always do, and the strangeness of sitting at a traitor's table while his ceiling whispered *anyone* at me made the food taste like nothing at all.

Then he told it, and I will set it down as he told it, because I have learned that stories are also doors, and you cannot know what's behind one until it opens.

"I was born in Cindral," he said. "In a town called Liss, on the Miren coast, before the coasts went strange. And I had the gift. You know the gift." His eyes found mine across the table, pale as skimmed milk, and I felt seen in a way that made my skin crawl. "Bone-speaking. Dead-carrying. The valley folk where you come from call it sickness, I'd wager. Mine called it worse. In Cindral we called it *door-touched*, and door-touched children were taken from their families at seven years old and raised in the spire, because a gift that hears the dead is a gift that guards the Door. That's the whole architecture of it. The country took me before I could walk properly and made me into a hinge."

"You were the keeper," I said.

"I was *a* keeper. There were nine of us, in my cohort. Nine door-touched children, raised on oaths and cold stone." He turned his mug slowly, not drinking. "And then the war came, and the Hollow King's shadow crossed the border, and the council of Cindral did what councils do. They looked for someone to blame. And there we were — nine children who spoke with the dead, in a country suddenly terrified of what the dead might be saying. They said we were compromised. That the enemy could reach us through our gift. That we were open windows in a house under siege." His voice did not rise. That was the worst of it. It stayed level and dry as dust all the way down. "They sealed us in the spire cells. For our protection, they said. For the country's safety. Eleven months, they kept us, while the front collapsed. And in the eleventh month, the council voted to close the Door permanently — to seal Cindral off from the other side entirely, to let the border provinces burn rather than risk what might come through."

"And you opened it instead," Brigid said. Not accusing. Just placing the stone on the cairn.

"They were going to seal it with us *inside*," Vessik said. "Nine children in cells beneath a door that was about to become a wall. So I opened it. I opened it wide, and I gave the way in to the Hollow King's envoys, and I told myself it was mercy — ours, theirs, everyone's. Let the country die quickly instead of suffocating slow. Let the door-touched go free." He smiled, and it was the most terrible thing I saw that day, worse than the wardens, worse than the burning town, because it was so tired. "The Hollow King thanked me by killing eight of the nine. The ninth was me, spared, because a king understands the value of a hinge. He chained the key to me and set me here to keep the Door shut from this side. Forever. So that what he holds, he holds."

The kettle whistled. Nobody moved to take it off the fire.

"And the whispers?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "The charms on the stair. Who are they?"

"Everyone." He gestured at the ceiling, at the hundreds of turning bones. "Every soul the Hollow King keeps frozen. They press against the Door like moths against glass. I hear them. I have heard them for two hundred years." He said *two hundred years* the way you'd say *last Tuesday*, and I understood that the gift had done to him what gifts do — stretched him out along the seam between living and dead until neither side would hold his weight. "Do you know what they ask? Century after century? Not vengeance. Not freedom, even, not really. They ask if anyone remembers. If anyone outside still says their names. And I could tell them — I am the last man alive who could tell them — and I don't. I carve them birds and hands and leave them on the stair to starve, because if I speak to them, if I let them *in*, then I am a keeper again, and I did not survive two hundred years of exile to spend eternity being useful to the people who threw me away."

Silence. The fire ticked. Above us, softly, ceaselessly: *anyone, anyone, anyone.*

And I looked at him — at the spotted hands like mine, at the loneliness built into the very architecture of his life, at the bitterness grown so old it had become load-bearing — and I felt the floor of my self tilt.

Because I knew him. Not his name, not his crime. *Him.* I knew the shape of a person whose gift makes a country flinch. I knew the arithmetic of exile: *they feared me, therefore I owe them nothing.* I had done that sum myself, in the dark, in Thornvale, ten thousand times, over the grandmother's grave, over Maren's verdict, over the wardstones I slipped past with one bag and a stolen future. *They cast me out first. Whatever comes next is theirs.*

Vessik had done that sum and let it run for two centuries, compounding, until it devoured a country.

And the horror was not that he was monstrous. The horror was that he was legible. That I could read every line of him the way I read bone. That if the winter lasted long enough, if the loneliness went deep enough, if the world gave me two hundred years to ripen my grievance — I might sit in a hut full of whispering birds and refuse to answer them, and call it justice.

"Quentin." Brigid's hand closed over mine on the table. Warm. Callused. Alive. "Where'd you go just now?"

"Nowhere," I lied, and then, because she had mended my coat and taught her children cards' worth of trust in me: "There. Into him. It's a short road, Brigid. That's what frightens me. It's a very short road from what was done to me to what he did."

Vessik watched us with those milk-pale eyes, and something moved behind them — not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of a man seeing his own reflection in water and resenting the interruption.

"You think you'll choose better," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I think," I said, "that you never had a Brigid. Or a Tam. Or a Sef who tells you the door is warm." I stood, and the map against my chest beat hot as a second heart. "You've been alone with your grievance so long you've forgotten it's not the truth. It's just the only voice that stayed."

Something cracked in his face — fast, buried instantly, but I had ears for cracks. I had spent my whole life hearing what bones keep under the surface.

"The king knows you're coming," Vessik said flatly, all tenderness gone, the hinge speaking again. "The wardens turned back at the pass an hour ago. Summoned. Which means he's finished waiting, and it means the offer he means to make you, he means to make tonight." He rose, joints crackling like green wood, and lifted the great iron key away from his neck — held it out over the table, chain pooling, his hand trembling with what I chose to believe was fear.

"Take it," he said. "Walk up to the Door. Knock. And pray, girl, pray harder than I ever did, that whatever answers you is kinder than what answered me."

Outside, the light was failing, and on the mountain above the hut, the Last Door stood open.

It had not been open when we arrived.
