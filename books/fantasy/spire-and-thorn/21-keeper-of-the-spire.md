## 21. Keeper of the Spire

The first year, I counted everything.

I counted the days until I stopped expecting a knock at the spire door — forty-one. I counted the letters I did not write to Thornvale — all of them. I counted the names as they came through my hands, one by one, in the long blue evenings when the dead of Cindral crossed over or chose their quiet rest: nine hundred and six souls carried like river stones through my palms, each one warm for a moment, each one heavier than it looked.

On the four hundred and twelfth day, I stopped counting.

Not because the work was done. The work is never done; that is what a keeper is *for*. But somewhere between the last hard frost and the first green push of thorn-vine up the spire's eastern face, counting became unnecessary. You do not count your own heartbeat. You do not count the way light comes through a window you have finally stopped flinching at.

You just live inside it.

* * *

Cindral does not look like a country that died.

That was the thing no one told me — not Ilsa, not the map, not even the whispers in my bones. A country unmade should be ash and absence, a wound in the world. But grief made weather, and weather makes soil, and under all that long falling snow the land had only been sleeping, holding its shape the way a held breath holds a voice. When the winter broke, Cindral exhaled. Rivers remembered their courses. The salt orchard blossomed into something sweeter than salt, though the trees still wept faintly on humid days, and Brigid swears the fruit tastes like someone else's childhood.

The spire stands at the center of it, black stone grown through with white thorn since the solstice, as if the country itself decided to hold its own heart in a fist of brambles. From the topmost room I can see everything: the Greywater running silver and honest now, Tam's ferry crossing it twice a day with actual passengers who pay in eggs and gossip; the road south where Ostmere used to pretend it wasn't built from salvage, its people drifting east in ones and twos once word spread that there was a door open and a keeper who would not ask where you'd been; the rolling meadowland where the king dissolved into first light, which blooms every spring with a flower no botanist can name and which the children call *the king's apology*, because Petal decided so and Petal decides these things.

My rooms are on the third floor. There is a hearth that draws well, a table scarred by other hands before mine, and a window seat deep enough to sleep in, which I know because I have slept in it more nights than I will admit to anyone, including myself.

Below, the village grows the way villages grow when no one is watching: crooked, stubborn, alive.

* * *

They stayed. That still surprises me, some mornings, when I come down the spire stairs and smell woodsmoke and frying onions and hear Tam swearing at a hinge.

I thought they would go home. Home, for them, meant Ostmere, meant the fence-mending life Brigid had built out of salvage and stubbornness, meant the small safe pretending that had kept them alive through the long erasure. I stood in the doorway of the spire the morning after the Door opened, hollowed out and ringing like a struck bell, and waited for them to gather the children and walk away from the strange woman who had become a country's hinge.

Brigid looked at me over the top of Petal's head — Petal asleep against her shoulder, Wren asleep against her hip, both of them filthy from the solstice and dreaming hard — and said, "Well? Are you going to stand there all day, or are you going to show us which room has the best light?"

Tam grunted. Tam's grunts are a whole language; this one meant *she's right and I hate it.*

"We're not staying for you," he added, in case I got ideas. "Winter's coming again someday, ordinary winter, and I'd rather see it fall on a country that deserves it. Also the fishing here is obscene. Nobody's fished these rivers in a hundred years. It's like robbing a church."

"Tam," Brigid said.

"It's true."

So they stayed. And then others came — not many, at first. A widow from Ostmere whose grandmother had hummed a Cindrali lullaby she never knew the words to; she came asking if I could find them, and I could, and she stayed. Two brothers who had spent thirty years being from nowhere, carefully, deliberately nowhere, and who walked through the Door and sat down in the road and cried like children. A warden — an actual Hollow King's warden, gray-cloaked and hollow-eyed — who arrived in the second spring carrying his cloak folded over his arm like something surrendered, and asked, in a voice like a man testing ice, whether a person who had guarded a prison could learn to guard a garden instead.

He mends the spire wall now. His name is Aldous. He is terrible at cards and Tam will not let him quit.

We are eleven households. We are a village the size of a held breath. We have no name yet, though Petal has proposed *Thornhollow*, *Spirestead*, and, twice, *Petalville*, and been voted down with love each time. Brigid says a place gets its name the way a child gets theirs — not chosen but recognized, one morning, suddenly obvious. I believe her. She is usually right about how things grow.

* * *

Thornvale sends no one to fetch me.

I want to be clear about that, because I spent the first months listening for it — for hoofbeats on the southern road, for Maren's brisk shadow, for some delegation of the righteous come to drag me back to the valley that raised me and nearly unmade me. I listened the way you listen for a storm you've decided not to fear anymore. Not one rider came. Not one letter. Not one rumor, even, carried up the ferry by Tam's passengers, of the valley speaking my name at all.

At first I read it as confirmation of everything I'd feared: that I had been so easy to cut away. That the wardstones had closed behind me like water over a dropped stone, and Thornvale's great virtue — its quiet, its cleanliness, its proud magiclessness — extended to forgetting.

Then, one evening in the second autumn, I understood it differently.

I was carving. I carve every evening now, not charms but names — the spire's thorn-grown stones take ink poorly and chisel beautifully, and the dead of Cindral deserve to be written somewhere the rain can't argue with. My tools moved through a piece of antler, shaping the name *Sera Voss, who taught children to whistle through grass,* and the whisper came through my hands the way it always does, gentle now, gentle as family visiting, and Sera lingered after her name was set, the way some of them do, and asked me about my home.

"The valley," I said. "It wasn't really my home. It was just where I hid."

And Sera — who had been a teacher, and had the teacher's gift for questions that sound simple and are not — asked: *"Did it send anyone?"*

"No."

*"Then it isn't holding you anywhere, is it."*

I sat with that a long time, the antler warm in my lap, the fire ticking. Because she was right, and I hadn't seen it. Exile requires two parties. Banishment requires someone to keep the door shut. Thornvale had not sent riders because Thornvale was not holding anything of mine. It never had. I had been carrying my whole self out of that valley for years before I ever walked past the wardstones — in my pocket, in my bones, in the secret language of bone and whisper. They didn't exile me. They just finally noticed I'd already gone.

I send nothing back either. Not spite — I checked, honestly, the way you check a wound for heat. Just nothing. There is no letter I could write that wouldn't be a lie or a plea, and I am done with both. If Maren ever wonders what became of the Halloran girl with the sickness, she may wonder. Wondering is free. So is being wrong.

Some nights, though — I'll say this here, where only the thorns and the dead can hear — some nights I dream of my grandmother's kitchen. The smell of tallow and tea. Her hands over mine on the little carving knife, guiding, patient. *Inheritance, not sickness,* she said, the only person in that whole clean valley who ever said it. In the dreams she doesn't speak. She just smiles, and her hands are warm, and somewhere far off a door stands open.

I think she knew. I think she stayed anyway, all those years, because someone had to plant the seed in me before the frost came. Grandmothers are like that. Countries too, maybe.

* * *

Spring came early this year, and wrong in all the right ways — crocuses pushing up through old snowmelt, the Greywater loud with meltwater and fish, the king's-apology flowers opening across the meadow like a slow purple applause. Wren turned seven and demanded a birthday party with everyone in it, which in our village means eleven households plus whatever dead happened to be lingering politely near the windows, and I'll tell you, there is a particular kind of peace in watching ghosts hover at a birthday party like fond aunts, invisible to everyone but me, commenting on the cake.

"Sera says the frosting is too sweet," I reported.

"Tell Sera the frosting is perfect," Petal said with the gravity of a monarch.

"She says that's what she told her students, and they were also wrong."

Wren laughed so hard he fell off the bench. Tam caught him without looking up from the cards. Aldous, who still startles sometimes at sudden joy, smiled into his cup where he thought no one saw him.

I watched them from the window seat later, after the cake and the games, the fire low, the household settling into that particular hush of a full house going sleepy. Brigid was mending — she is always mending, coats and fences and people, she mended my coat again last month and I let her pretend I didn't notice the new stitching shaped like thorn vines. Tam was teaching Petal a card trick involving cheating, openly, as pedagogy. The children glowed. The spire hummed around us, that low stone note it makes at night, which I have decided is contentment.

Once, belonging was a thing I begged for. I begged Thornvale for it in a thousand small ways — extra shifts at the healer's, extra kindnesses, extra silence about the whispers in my hands. I thought belonging was granted, issued like ration bread to those who complied. It took a dying cartographer, a vanished country, a grieving king, and a family that broke into a frozen court armed with nothing but salt charms and stubbornness to teach me otherwise.

Belonging is claimed. You walk in and sit down and refuse to leave, and one morning the place looks around and realizes it has rearranged itself around you, and neither of you remembers when that happened.

Cindral chose me. I chose it back. Between those two choices, a whole country learned to breathe.

* * *

Tonight I climb to the top of the spire, as I do most nights, to check the Door.

It doesn't need checking. That's the joke Aldous makes — *keeper, thy door is fine* — but I check anyway, the way you visit a grave that holds no one sad, just to sit a while. The Last Door stands in the spire's heart chamber, and since the solstice it has been less a door than a threshold of light, open in both directions, humming softly. Through it, the dead pass when they are ready. Some cross entirely — wherever the dead go when they're done, a country I don't map. Some stay a while in Cindral's soft air, resting, visiting, being weather. All of them pass through my hands first, if they want naming, and most do. Nine hundred and six in the first year. Fewer now. The backlog of a century's exile is easing, soul by witnessed soul.

Ilsa was the last of the old company to go. She stayed three seasons, walking the country she had mapped from memory, correcting the landscape gently — *that hill was taller; the ford was further east; there was a bell tower here, ring it for me once.* I rang it. Then one evening at the salt orchard, now an apple orchard with one tree that still weeps, she kissed my brow — cool as riverstone, brief as a blessing — and said, "You were the best thing I ever carried," and went through the Door like light going home.

I did not count the days after that either. But I remember that one.

Tonight the Door is calm. Tonight the wind comes up from the south smelling of thaw, and the thorn vines on the spire's face have put out their first white flowers, and far below, lamplit windows dot the dark like a constellation someone is still drawing.

And then — as I turn to go, as I'm pulling my coat close and thinking about the kettle and the antler piece waiting on my table — I hear it.

Hoofbeats. On the southern road. One rider, moving fast, past the hour when anyone travels, toward the spire light.

I stand very still at the top of the tower, an old habit rising in me like floodwater — *they've come, they've finally come, Maren, the rite, the cleansing* — and then I let the habit go, breath by breath, because I am not that woman anymore and this is not that valley. Whoever rides toward Cindral now rides toward a door that opens. Whatever they carry, they will carry it through my hands if they need to.

The hoofbeats slow. Stop. Below, a figure dismounts in the spire's shadow — young, by the posture, swaying with exhaustion — and lifts a face toward my window, toward the light.

And the wind carries up to me, thin and cracked and unmistakable, a single word in a valley accent I would know anywhere, the accent of my childhood, of tallow-smoke and wardstones and everything I left:

"*Halloran?*"

I put my hand on the windowsill. The thorn vine beside it, blooming, leans toward the stranger the way the dead lean toward warmth.

I go down to answer the door.
