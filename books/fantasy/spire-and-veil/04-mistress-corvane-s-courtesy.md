## 4. Mistress Corvane's Courtesy

The summons came at the ninth bell, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes.

"Mistress Corvane will see you now," he said, and then he was gone down the corridor of the Ledger Hall, quick as a dropped coin rolling toward a drain.

I set my pen in its rest with the care of someone who has practiced not shaking. Around me the clerks bent over their desks like a field of wheat bowing to weather no one could see, and not one of them looked up. That was the first courtesy of the Gilded Veil: when one of your own was called upstairs, you gave them the mercy of pretending you hadn't noticed. The second courtesy was that they'd all know by supper anyway.

I had been summoned before, of course. Twice for arithmetic errors — mine, both times, and both times I'd confessed within a breath and been forgiven with a smile that cost more than any fine. Once to identify a bottle whose seal had been broken in transit. Corvane had watched me hold it, watched my face while the memory inside whispered against the glass, and afterward she'd said, *You have an honest face, Ravensworth. In this house that is either a virtue or a resource.* I still didn't know which she'd meant, and I had spent three years deciding it was safer not to find out.

But those summons had come at midmorning, casual as asking for tea. This one came at the ninth bell, after dark, when the guildhouse went quiet and the lamps in the upper corridors were lit with the expensive oil that burned without smoke. Nobody was invited to Sable Corvane's evening hours by accident.

The map was under the third floorboard of my cell, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath a false layer of inventory receipts I'd written myself — forty lines of bottled griefs and borrowed summers, each entry perfectly formed, because if I was going to hide a thing behind paperwork, the paperwork would be flawless. It was the only kind of armor I owned.

Still, walking up through the guildhouse, past the cases where impossible things glowed softly behind glass — a jar of last words, a ribbon tied around somebody's forgotten birthday, a key that opened a door in a house that had burned down decades ago — I felt the floorboard pulling at me like a wound under a bandage. *She knows,* said some cold clerk-voice in my chest, the voice I used for balancing columns. *She has always known. She is simply waiting to see what you'll do about it.*

*Then she can wait,* I answered, and climbed the stairs.

* * *

Her study sat at the top of the guildhouse, in the round room under the lantern tower, and it was the warmest place in Vellmark. Not fire-warm. There was no hearth. The warmth came from the things themselves: shelves of memories radiating their little stolen summers, bottles of festival days and harvest afternoons and one entire shelf, cordoned off with velvet rope, of what the catalog called *green*. Spring mornings. Cut grass. The smell of rain on warm stone. Things from before the winter, or from far south of it, hoarded here like a dragon's gold while the city outside scraped ice off its windows and remembered green the way the devout remember heaven.

Corvane stood at her window with her back to me when I entered, which was itself a message. You did not turn your back on a clerk unless you wanted them to understand how little you feared them.

"Noor Ravensworth." My name in her mouth sounded like an entry in a ledger — accurate, complete, filed. "Sit. Would you like something warm? I have real tea. Not the chicory the Hall drinks."

"Thank you, Mistress. Chicory is fine."

"It is not fine, it is chicory." She turned then, and poured two cups anyway, and handed me one, and I understood that refusing would have been the error. Her hand, when our fingers nearly touched over the porcelain, was steady as a scale.

She was not what strangers expected. They expected the mistress of the Gilded Veil to be gilded herself, lacquered and jeweled, and instead she was a small neat woman in gray wool with silver hair cut short as a soldier's and eyes the pale brown of watered ink. She looked like somebody's patient aunt. She looked like a woman who would remember your birthday and also exactly how much you owed.

"You buried a courier yesterday," she said.

My cup paused halfway to my mouth. I made it finish the journey. Drink, swallow, set it down on the saucer with a small click that I hoped sounded bored rather than terrified. "He wasn't mine to bury, Mistress. The enforcers took charge of the body."

"The enforcers took charge of the body," she agreed. "You took charge of everything else. The blanket from the receiving room. The coin you put in his hand — a Veil coin, Noor, which means it comes out of your own wages, since we do not reimburse funerary gestures. And you closed his eyes." She sipped her tea. "Vosk's mercy, but you closed his eyes. Why?"

"Because they were open."

"And?"

"And it seemed rude to leave him looking at the snow."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An entry being made somewhere behind her eyes. "Rude," she repeated, tasting the word. "You are a very careful person, Ravensworth. Did you know that? I have read your work. Your columns balance to the half-copper across three years of ledgers. Your hand never varies. When you are tired, your letters get smaller rather than worse — did you know you do that? Smaller and smaller, tighter and tighter, as though you're trying to take up less of the page." She set down her cup. "Careful people interest me. Careless people steal from me and I hang them, and that's simple. But careful people — careful people only break when something matters more than their care. So I find myself wondering what mattered to you enough, yesterday, to stand in the snow longer than anyone else. To touch a dead stranger's face."

"He was frostbitten before he reached the door," I said. "He'd come a long way. Someone should notice that."

"Someone should," she agreed pleasantly. "We did notice. He carried guild seals forged well enough to pass my own doorwardens, which means someone with access to a press of ours, or a very good copyist, wanted him to look like ours. His boots were southern make. His teeth said he'd grown up eating soft food, which is odd for a courier. And his hands—" she lifted her own, elegant, dry, "—his hands were stained with mapmaker's ink under the nails. Old stains. A courier carries maps; he doesn't draw them. Unless he was more than a courier once." She tilted her head. "Does none of this interest you? You've gone very still. Stillness is another of your tells, incidentally. When you lie, you stop moving entirely, like a hare under a hawk. When you tell the truth you fidget with your cuff. You're fidgeting now, so I'll believe the next thing you say. Choose it carefully."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us, the guildhouse settled against the cold with a sound like a long exhale.

"I don't lie to you, Mistress," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and even, the ledger voice, and hated it. "I keep accounts. That's all I am here for. A man died in our alley and I did the decent thing over the body because nobody else was going to. If there's something strange about him, that's a question for your enforcers, not your clerks."

"Mm." She rose and walked along her shelves, trailing one finger above the bottles without touching them, and the memories leaned toward her finger like plants toward sun. "Do you know what I admire most about you, Noor? You survived Aerhallow."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped weight. I kept my face still. Hawk overhead. Hare frozen.

"They exiled you for a storm that killed eleven people, and you walked out of that city at nineteen with nothing, and you came here, to the one guild in the north that trades in what people most want to forget, and you asked for work keeping records of other people's erasures." She glanced back at me. "That is either profound penance or profound irony, and I have never decided which. But here is what I know about survivors of Aerhallow. They flinch at thunder. They count exits. And they lie beautifully, because they learned young that the truth gets people hanged." She let that sit. "So when I ask you my next question, I want you to fidget with your cuff."

She waited. I realized my hand had already found the fabric.

"Did the courier give you anything?"

And there it was. The whole evening balanced on it, the way a column balances, every number above the line waiting on the one below. I thought of the floorboard. The oilcloth. Forty flawless lines of invented inventory standing guard over a bleeding piece of vellum that hummed when I came near it.

I could say no. I could say it in a still voice, hare-still, and watch her file the stillness away, and spend the rest of my life waiting for the night she sent someone to lift my floorboards while I slept. Or I could say yes and hand her the Spire, and hand her whatever war slept underneath it, and become the clerk who balanced that column too.

Or —

"There was a moment," I said slowly, "when he was dying. He grabbed my wrist. He tried to say something. I couldn't make it out." My fingers moved against my cuff. Fidget. Truth-shaped. "It might have been a word. It might have been nothing. People dying say a lot of things, Mistress. I've catalogued enough last words to know most of them are names."

"That's true," she said softly. "Most of them are names." She studied me for a long moment, and the lamplight made her pale eyes look almost colorless, almost like ice over deep water. "You didn't answer my question."

"You asked if he gave me anything. He grabbed my wrist. That's what he gave me. Bruises. They've faded."

A silence. Then she laughed — a short, genuine, terrible laugh, the first honest sound she'd made all evening. "Oh, you are wasted on the shipping ledgers. Do you know, I believe you. That's the remarkable thing. I believe that you're lying, and I believe that I cannot prove it, and I believe that you know I cannot prove it, and that you have constructed your answer so precisely that tearing it apart would cost me more than it gains." She refilled my cup unasked. "Your exile taught you arithmetic of a very high order, Ravensworth. Pity Aerhallow wasted it on storms."

She returned to her desk and drew a paper from a drawer — heavy stock, gilt-edged, the kind the Veil used for contracts that outlived their signers.

"A promotion," she said. "Effective immediately. Senior Reconciler, First Rank. You'll have your own office on the third floor, a wage doubled, and access to the Deep Archive — the old ledgers, the founding collections, everything back to Vosk's day." She slid it across the desk, and a pen, and the inkwell with the silver cap. "You'll report directly to me. Weekly. Privately."

Every instinct I had, every careful hair-trigger instinct, screamed that this was the trap laid open, the cage with its door politely held wide. The Deep Archive. Where the oldest ledgers lived. Where anything the courier had been carrying would eventually be traced, if it traced anywhere — and where, if she suspected me, she could watch me walk into the evidence with my own feet.

"Why?" I asked. It came out before I could dress it up.

"Because you're the best clerk I have, and I am about to lose several worse ones." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "Enforcers ride north within the week. Whatever our dead courier was carrying — or wasn't — it came from the direction of the Sunken Spire, and my mistress-before-me spent thirty years and four lives trying to chart that coast and got back nothing but drowned men. If there is something out there worth forging our seals for, I intend to have it. And while my dogs run the passes, I want my sharpest pen here, close to me, reconciling the accounts." She smiled, and it was the aunt-smile again, patient, bottomless. "Unless you'd prefer to stay in the Hall. Columns are comfortable. Comfortable things, though — they do tend to get audited."

There it was. Not a threat. An invoice.

I picked up the pen. My hand, I noted distantly, had stopped fidgeting altogether — perfectly still, hare-still — and so I made myself write my name with a small flourish, a careless human flourish, a liar's flourish, and slide the contract back across the wood.

"Thank you, Mistress."

"Thank you, Senior Reconciler." She stood, and walked me to the door herself, which no one did, ever, and at the threshold she touched my elbow — light as a signature. "One more thing. The bottle you catalogued Tuesday. Lot 114, the sailor's memory, the one that hummed." Her voice stayed pleasant as a fireside. "It's missing from the case. Taken sometime between the ninth bell and morning. Whoever took it walked past three wardens and a warded door, which narrows the field considerably." She released my arm. "If it turns up on your rounds, bring it to me personally. Some sympathies, once started, are very hard to quiet."

The door closed behind me with a sound like a ledger shutting.

I stood a long time in the corridor, listening to my own heart conduct its irregular audit. She knew. Not everything — maybe not even much — but she knew the shape of the hole where the truth should be, and she had just built her cage around it and hung the door with gilt and called it a promotion. The Deep Archive. Weekly. Privately. She hadn't offered me a position; she'd offered me proximity, so that whatever I did next, she'd be near enough to feel the heat of it.

And yet. Under the fear, unwelcome as a splinter, something else had stirred when she said *the founding ledgers, everything back to Vosk's day.* Because there was a question I had not let myself ask since the courier died with the word *Spire* going out of him like a lamp — a question about the vellum that bled, and why a map drawn a century ago should hum in sympathy with a sailor's bottled memory that had passed through *my* hands, of all the clerks in Vellmark, mine.

The Deep Archive held the founding ledgers.

And I had an appointment to keep there in the morning.

I went down through the sleeping guildhouse, past the glowing cases, past the wardens who nodded to me differently now — senior, they'd have heard by morning, promoted, dangerous — and knelt at the third floorboard of my cell, and lifted the oilcloth, and the map lay there in the dark with its routes faintly rearranged, patient as a debt.

"Well," I told it quietly. "She's invited us both."

Somewhere far off, thunder rolled across the frozen harbor — impossible thunder, out of a clear and freezing sky — and I pressed my palm flat against the floor until it passed, and told myself I hadn't recognized the sound.

I was lying. But at least, this once, I fidgeted while I did it.
