## 5. The Thief with Borrowed Faces

The face was the problem.

Noor had spent three days telling themselves it wasn't — that the man who'd been loitering outside the Salt Door, across the fishmonger's row, at the mouth of Candlewick Lane, was simply a stranger with an unfortunate resemblance. Grief did that. Grief made faces out of nothing. They'd buried Tam Vessell last spring in a pauper's row behind the guildhouse, a clerk who'd miscounted one ledger too many and been found floating in the harbor with his hands tied, and Noor had stood at the graveside in the rain because someone from the counting floor always did, and they had looked at that dead face for a long time before the gravediggers covered it.

They knew the shape of Tam's jaw. They knew the small scar splitting his left eyebrow where he'd walked into a shelf of bottled years as a boy. They knew the way his mouth sat slightly crooked when it wasn't doing anything at all.

The stranger across the market square had all of it. Every line. Even the crooked mouth.

So Noor did what a careful person does. They finished their errand — two vials of borrowed courage for a shipping magnate who wanted to face his creditors — they logged the delivery in their head rather than on paper, and they took a different route home, doubling back twice through the tannery district where the stink discouraged followers.

It didn't discourage this one.

* * *

They came at dusk, in the alley behind Noor's lodging house, which told Noor something already: the stranger had watched long enough to learn where home was, and had chosen the place where Noor slept rather than the place where Noor worked. That was either courtesy or a message, and Noor suspected both.

"I wouldn't run," the stranger said, stepping out of the shadow of the rain barrels. "You're not built for running. I've been watching you walk for three days. You walk like a man balancing a full tray."

Tam's voice came out of Tam's mouth, and Noor's stomach dropped through the cobbles.

"Who are you," Noor said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes, because that was how clerks fought.

"That's the trouble with this face." The stranger touched their own cheek almost tenderly, the way a musician touches an instrument. "Everyone asks about the face first. Wren Ashgrave." A shallow bow. "And before you ask the second question everyone asks — no, I'm not wearing him because I killed him. He was dead when I took it. Drowned men keep well in cold water; it's practically a service to them, being remembered."

"You stole his face."

"I borrowed it. There's a difference, and the difference is that I intend to give it back someday, which is more than the Veil ever intends for anything it takes." Wren tilted their head, and the gesture was wrong — too fluid, too curious, nothing like Tam's careful economy of motion — and somehow that wrongness was worse than the likeness. Like watching a puppeteer forget to be still. "You knew him. You cried at his grave. Don't bother denying it, I was there. In the tree line. I'd just taken the face and I wanted to see if it fit anywhere."

Noor's hand had drifted, without permission, toward the knife they kept in their coat for cutting wax seals. Wren noticed. Wren smiled with Tam's crooked mouth.

"The courier," Wren said. "Three nights ago. Frostbitten, half-dead, pressed something into your hands in the alley behind the guildhouse. And then, minutes later, four of Corvane's hounds arrive as if summoned by the smell of dying. You told them you saw nothing."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "But you said it too fast, and your ears went red, and one of the enforcers — the big one, Hesk — wrote down the time anyway, because enforcers write down everything, it's the only poetry they know. So here is where we find ourselves." They counted on fingers, an oddly bookish gesture for a face-thief. "You have a map you shouldn't have. Corvane thinks you might have a map you shouldn't have. I need the map you shouldn't have. And you—" the crooked mouth curved further, "—need someone to tell you what's on it, because I've seen you at your candle three nights running, and you haven't slept, and you still don't know what you're holding."

The alley was very quiet. Somewhere above, a gull complained about the winter.

"How do you know about my candle," Noor said slowly, "if you've been watching me for three days in a face I would have recognized?"

Wren's expression flickered — the first honest thing they'd shown. Something like respect.

"There it is," they said. "The ledger-keeper's mind. Yes. I wore other faces. A fishwife Tuesday. A lamplighter Wednesday. Today I got lazy, or sentimental, depending on whether you believe me, and put Tam back on." They spread their hands. "Ask your real question, Ravensworth. The one under the others."

Noor asked it. "Why should I believe anything a memory-thief says? You deal in stolen goods. You are stolen goods, walking around in a dead clerk's skin."

"Because," Wren said, and stopped smiling, and for a moment the borrowed face went hollow as a mask hung on a nail, "the courier was my brother."

* * *

They ended up in Noor's rooms, which Noor would later identify as the second-worst decision of the week, after lying to enforcers and before everything that followed. But at the time it seemed simple: better to hear whatever Wren Ashgrave had come to say indoors, with a door bolted and a candle lit, than in an alley where voices carried and knives were cheap.

Wren sat on the one chair like they owned it, legs crossed, turning a coin over their knuckles — a habit, Noor noted, not Tam's, something older and more restless underneath the stolen surface.

"His name was Lachlan Ashgrave," Wren said. "He was a courier for the Veil for eleven years. Fast, discreet, and stupid in exactly one way: he believed the guild's promises. He thought if he ran far enough, fast enough, clean enough, they'd eventually sell him back the years they held as collateral. Everyone who works for the Veil has collateral, did you know that? Your wages are good because your memories are the deposit." They flipped the coin. Caught it. "Lachlan carried something north six weeks ago. Something he wasn't supposed to look at. He looked anyway. Then he ran south with it instead of delivering it, which tells you what he saw."

"He was dying of frostbite," Noor said. "In a warm city. In autumn."

"Frostbite from the inside," Wren said. "I've heard of it once before. When something old looks at you, really looks, the cold goes in and stays. He made it two hundred miles holding that cold off. He was going to bring it to me — I'd have known what to do with it, I always know what to do with things — but he saw the guildhouse first and changed his plan. Gave it to a stranger. A clerk. Someone gray enough to be invisible." Their eyes, which were not Tam's eyes, which were pale green and quick, fixed on Noor. "He chose you. I'd love to know why."

"I don't know why."

"Mm." Wren clearly didn't believe that, but let it lie, which Noor was learning was a Wren tactic: set down a weight, walk away, let you pick it up yourself. "Here's what I know about the map, since you're wondering whether I'm worth the trouble. It's drawn on vellum that isn't vellum. It moves. And it's the only surviving chart of the Sunken Spire, which every child in this city learns is a sailor's fable, and which Sable Corvane has spent — conservatively — forty years and an ocean of gold trying to find." The coin stopped moving. "She hasn't spent that kind of money on a fable. Neither will I."

"And what do you want with it? Sell it to her? You could name your price."

Something moved through Wren's face then, fast, like a fish under ice — grief, maybe, or its counterfeit, and Noor realized with a lurch that they couldn't tell the difference, and neither, possibly, could Wren.

"My brother died carrying it," Wren said. "That's reason enough for some people. For me—" the coin resumed, faster, "—for me the reason is that everything Lachlan ever was, everything he remembered, is sitting in a vault under the guildhouse as collateral against wages he never lived to collect. Corvane holds my brother's whole life in a bottle, Ravensworth. She holds mine too. And if that map leads where I think it leads, it leads to the place where the Veil learned how to bottle people in the first place." The coin stopped again. "I want to burn the vault down. Metaphorically. Or literally. I'm flexible."

Noor stood at the window with their back to the room, which was a habit, giving your back to dangerous people, because it made them talk louder. Below, the streetlamps were being lit one by one, little orange defeats against the early dark.

"If Corvane even suspects I have it," Noor said, "she'll take it apart to get to me. Slowly. Politely. She'll promote me until there's nowhere left to stand."

"Yes," Wren said cheerfully. "She will. She's very good at it. She did it to me once, years ago — offered me a counting house of my own, a title, a key to the inner vaults. I said no, so she burned down the building where I kept my stock and blamed a lamp-oil accident, and I lost eleven years of inventory in one night. Eleven years, Ravensworth. Do you understand what I mean by inventory?" They didn't wait for an answer. "So. Here is my offer, and you should take it, because it's the only one on any table."

"Which is?"

"We hold each other hostage. You give me access to the map — I don't take it from you, I'm not a fool, you'd go straight to Corvane and trade me for a promotion — and in exchange, I give you the one thing you can't get anywhere else." Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the scar above Tam's eyebrow. "I can open a memory bottle without killing what's inside. It's the family trade. And you, ledger-keeper, catalogued a shipment on Tuesday — fourteen bottles, sealed, unregistered origin — and one of them has been humming at your map ever since. You've heard it. Don't lie, you have the same look Lachlan had. Whatever's in that bottle knows what's on that vellum. And I can crack it open without spilling a drop."

The room seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a ship tilts.

"You want me to help you steal from the guild," Noor said, "using merchandise I inventoried."

"I want us to help each other steal from the guild," Wren corrected. "Using merchandise you inventoried and a map you're hiding. Mutual blackmail, mutual benefit. It's the only kind of partnership that lasts — no trust required, just consequences." They rose, smooth as poured water, and paused at the door with one hand on the bolt. "Midnight tomorrow. Bring the map and the bottle. Wear a face you don't mind losing."

Then they were gone, and Noor stood alone in their small gray room, listening to the gulls and the lamplighters' fading footsteps, and became aware of two facts settling into them like stones into a riverbed.

The first was that they had agreed to nothing, and yet everything had already been decided; Wren Ashgrave had simply been polite enough to let Noor pretend otherwise.

The second was that, beneath the fear — beneath the cold arithmetic of blackmail and the image of Corvane's patient smile — some small traitorous part of them had felt, for the first time since Aerhallow, awake.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so Noor did what they always did with sleepless hours: they worked. They pulled the shipment ledger from under the floorboard — not the guild copy, the private one, the one that would hang them if found — and turned to Tuesday's entry by candlelight.

Fourteen bottles. Unregistered origin. Sealed in black wax stamped with a sigil Noor hadn't recognized at the time: a spiral, broken by a single vertical line.

They recognized it now. It was pressed into the corner of the courier's map, small as a thumbnail, in the same black wax.

Noor sat very still and listened, the way you listen for a sound you've been pretending not to hear. From the hiding place beneath the floorboard, faint as a pulse through a wall, the seventh bottle was humming.

And beneath the humming — so low it might have been imagination, might have been blood in the ears, might have been the winter itself pressing its face against the window — Noor could have sworn they heard something else.

A voice. A woman's voice. Singing.

It sounded, though Noor would spend years refusing to admit this, exactly like their mother.
