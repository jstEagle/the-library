## 8. The Village That Sold Its Summer

The village announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and tallow and something under it all like old ice, the scent of a place that had been cold so long the cold had become part of its architecture.

Noor smelled it from the ridge while the light was still gray, and stopped walking without deciding to.

Below, in a bowl of hills that should have been farmland — *should have been*, the map's first route said, crossing land marked in the cartographer's confident hand as barley and orchard country — sat a cluster of low stone houses with turf roofs furred white. Smoke rose thin and straight from a dozen chimneys. A frozen stream stitched through the middle of everything, and along its banks stood what remained of an orchard: forty or fifty apple trees, black-limbed, dead for decades, their branches raised like the arms of people surrendering to something that never came down out of the sky to take them.

"Brackhollow," Wren said, reading it off the map over Noor's shoulder. Their breath fogged. "The ledger lists it. I've seen the entry."

Of course they had. Wren Ashgrave had spent years inside the Gilded Veil's records wearing other people's faces, and they quoted the ledgers the way priests quoted scripture — flatly, precisely, with no visible belief.

"What entry?" Noor asked, though they already knew. They had catalogued enough impossible things to know how this story went.

Wren pulled their collar up against the wind coming off the ridge. "Contract of seasonal transfer. Brackhollow, forty-one years ago. The village sold its summer." A pause, the kind Wren used when they wanted you to ask. "All of it. Every warm day, in perpetuity, to the Veil. In exchange for—" They consulted nothing, reciting from memory. "Guaranteed grain allotments. Winter cloth. A tithe exemption. And one bottled memory per household, per generation, of the buyer's choosing."

"The buyer's choosing," Noor repeated.

"The Veil likes its purchases to be remembered fondly."

They went down into the village because the map's route went through it, and because the passes north would close within days, and because there was nowhere else to go. Noor told themselves all three reasons twice on the way down, and noticed, with the part of their mind that kept ledgers even now, that none of the three was *we want to see*.

* * *

Brackhollow's streets were packed snow over cobbles, swept clean by brooms worn down to nubs. The houses were built close and low, doors facing each other across lanes barely wide enough for a cart, as if the whole village were huddled around some shared warmth that hadn't existed in forty-one years. There were no dogs. Noor noticed that before they noticed anything else. Dogs bark at strangers, and strangers had not come here in a long time.

The first person to speak to them was a woman mending a net outside the fish-smoking shed, though there was no open water within a day's walk. She looked up, saw two travelers in road-worn cloaks, and her face did something complicated — surprise, then fear, then a careful flattening of both.

"You'll be wanting the headman," she said. Not a question. "Tam Vess. The long house, by the well."

"We're just passing through," Noor said. "North. We need passage over the ice road, or a guide to the coast road."

The woman's needle stopped. She looked at Noor the way people look at someone who has said something obscene in a language she half understands.

"There's no north," she said gently, as if correcting a child. "Not this time of year. Not any time of year, past the high fields." Then, because hospitality was apparently a law older than winter: "You'll eat. Tam will decide about the rest."

Inside the smoking shed it was warm enough that Noor's fingers ached as they thawed. Fish hung in ranks, split and amber-dark, and the smoke stung their eyes until they wept without meaning to, which was either the smoke or the last three days catching up with them. The woman — Maren, she said, Maren Vess, the headman's sister — fed them smoked fish and barley bread and tea brewed from pine needles, and asked no questions, and answered several Noor hadn't asked.

"How many people live here?" Noor asked.

"Hundred and ten. Was four hundred, when my grandmother signed."

"*Signed.*"

Maren's hands kept working the net. "There was a famine year. Crops failed three seasons running, and the Veil's factor came up from the coast with a contract and a wagon of grain, and he sat at our table and ate our last chicken and explained, very kindly, that we could keep starving honestly or we could sell something we weren't using." She tied off a knot. "He said *summer* like it was a coat we'd grown out of."

"And your grandmother signed."

"She was the headman. It was her name on the line, and everyone thanked her for twenty years, and then everyone's children grew up never seeing a green thing, and they stopped thanking her." Maren looked up, and her eyes were steady and terribly tired. "You're guild folk. Don't insult me by saying you're not. You've got the look — the counting look. So I'll tell you what I'd tell the factor himself if he ever came back: the grain came every year, right on time. The Veil honors its contracts. That's the worst thing about them. They always honor their contracts."

Noor thought of the ledgers. Thousands of pages, columns of entries in their own neat hand: *one summer, perpetual, Brackhollow; consideration rendered.* They had written *consideration rendered* about villages like this one and felt nothing, because the entries were numbers, and numbers were safe, and safety was the entire architecture of their life.

They ate the fish. It tasted like smoke and guilt.

* * *

The children found them at dusk.

Noor heard them before seeing them — a shrieking that for one horrible moment sounded like violence, until Wren, who had gone still in the doorway with a knife already palmed, relaxed by a single degree and said, "Children. Playing."

Playing. Noor had almost forgotten the sound existed.

There were six of them, ranging from maybe five to eleven, chasing each other through the blue snow-light between the houses, and they stopped dead when they saw the strangers, and then approached with the boldness of children who have nothing new to lose. They wore layers of patched wool and fur, cheeks scoured red by wind, and they stared at Noor and Wren with open hunger — not for food, though Noor suspected they were hungry for that too, but for novelty. For anything that wasn't the same hundred and ten faces and the same black trees and the same white ground.

"Are you from south of the pass?" the oldest asked. A girl, gap-toothed, maybe ten.

"Farther than that," Noor said.

"Is it true there are places where the water runs in summer?"

"It's true."

"Did you *see* it?"

"I've seen rivers," Noor said carefully. "I've seen rain that didn't freeze. I've seen—" They stopped, because the next true thing was *grass*, and the word caught in their throat like a bone.

"Grass," the girl said, tasting it. "Maren has a memory of grass. She lets us sit with it sometimes, on the long nights. It's in a bottle with a green seal." Her voice changed when she said *green*, went reverent and slightly confused, the way children talk about miracles they've been told about but can't picture. "It's mostly a feeling. Warm on your feet. But there's a color in it too, at the edge. Nobody can say what the color looks like because you can't say a color to someone who hasn't—"

"Tamsin." One of the smaller boys tugged her sleeve. "Ask them."

She turned back to Noor, suddenly shy, which somehow made it worse. "What does green *do*? When you look at it. What does it do to you?"

Noor opened their mouth and found nothing. What did green do? It did nothing. It simply was, everywhere, unremarkable, the color of the world's ordinary mercy — and Noor had walked through it every day of their life in Aerhallow and later in the guildhouse gardens without once thinking *this*, without once understanding that this was a thing that could be taken, itemized, entered in a ledger, *sold*.

"It doesn't do anything," Noor said finally. "It's just there. All the time. Like air."

The children considered this with the seriousness of scholars.

"That sounds like a lie," the small boy said, not unkindly. "A nice lie, though."

They ran off then, called away by a bell — a real bell, cracked, rung by a man going door to door for the evening meal count — and Noor stood in the cold watching them go and felt something shift in their chest with an audible weight, like a ledger being closed hard.

They had catalogued bottled memories for six years. Memories of weddings, of dead parents, of first loves and last fights. They had priced them. They had never once wondered what it meant that the Veil could reach into a village and take the *seasons*, and leave the people standing in the hole where warmth used to be, teaching their children to revere a color secondhand, from a bottle with a green seal.

*Consideration rendered,* Noor had written. Forty times, sixty times, in a fair and careful hand.

* * *

Tam Vess received them in the long house after supper, and he was his sister made older and harder: same steady eyes, same tiredness worn like a tool. He listened to their request — guide to the coast road, or leave to travel the ice road north — with his hands folded, and then he named his price, and the price was not coin.

"No coin leaves this village," he said. "Coin goes out and comes back as contracts. We learned." He nodded at Wren. "Your friend there moves like guild muscle, and you move like guild paper, and I'll thank you not to deny it. If you're running from the Veil, that's your business, and I won't ask. But passage costs, and the only currency we hold is what's in the strong room."

"The bottles," Wren said.

"The bottles." Tam's mouth twisted. "One per household, per generation, buyer's choice. Forty-one years of them. Some are memories the Veil took as part payment and left behind as trinkets — a wedding song, a dead man's laugh, useless to them, precious to us. And some—" he paused, and Noor watched him decide to say it, "—some are the ones the factor chose for us. Kindnesses, he called them. Memories of the signing, softened. Memories of the first famine year, blurred at the edges so it doesn't hurt so much to remember. We keep them locked up because half the village would trade a child's ration to feel them again, and a village that lives inside a bottle stops being a village."

"So what do you want from us?" Noor asked.

"A bottle you carry. Any one. Something from outside, something real, something none of us has lived. Payment for the guide — my nephew Oskar knows the ice road, and he wants out of here anyway, which is its own sadness — and payment for the truth, which is that I'd let you pass free if I could, because you've got the faces of people carrying something heavy, and heavy things belong in Brackhollow." He unfolded his hands. "But I can't afford sentiment. Sentiment is how my grandmother sold the summer."

* * *

That night, in the loft above the long house where they'd been given blankets that smelled of sheep and smoke, Noor opened their pack and looked at what they carried.

Three bottles remained from the courier's satchel, besides the map wrapped in oilcloth beneath the false bottom. Two were sealed with plain wax, contents unknown. The third was the one that had hummed in sympathy with the map back in Noor's candlelit room — the one labeled in faded ink, in a clerk's hand generations old: *Aerhallow, the harbor bells, midsummer.*

Their own city. Their own bells. A memory of home, bottled by someone, decades ago, and passed through the Veil's warehouses until it had landed, by chance or by the map's strange gravity, in a dying courier's hands.

Noor held it up to the lamplight. Inside, the memory moved like weather — a slow golden swirl, and somewhere in it, faint as a heartbeat, the sound of bells. Harbor bells, ringing over water, in summer, in a city that had exiled Noor for killing the wrong people with a storm and had then erased them so thoroughly that Noor sometimes doubted the storm had ever been theirs.

They could give the village one of the unlabeled bottles. That would be safer. Kinder, even — kinder to themselves. Whatever those held, it wasn't home. It wasn't the sound of a place that had cast them out, ringing in a season they'd been barred from.

But Tam had asked for something real. Something from outside. And the unlabeled bottles might hold anything — a stranger's grief, a torturer's satisfaction, a Veil factor's kindly poison. Noor knew exactly what the harbor-bell bottle held, and it was real, and it was beautiful, and it was the single most valuable thing they carried except the map.

They hated themselves for the arithmetic even as they did it. *Value given, value received. Consideration rendered.* Six years of guild training, and it still worked in them like a reflex, pricing the world before feeling it.

But they also understood, sitting in the dark with the bottle warm in their palms, that this was the first honest trade they'd ever been party to. The Veil had taken summers from these people and called it commerce. Noor could give back one small piece of summer — anyone's summer, a dead stranger's summer — and call it the same thing, and mean it better.

In the morning, they gave Tam Vess the bottle of Aerhallow's harbor bells, and watched him carry it to the strong room like a man carrying an infant, and heard him tell Maren, "Green seal. We'll open it at midwinter feast," and had to walk out into the cold and stand among the dead apple trees for a long time, breathing, because if he had thanked them directly Noor was afraid of what noise they'd have made.

Oskar Vess left with them an hour later, a wiry, silent young man with a coil of rope and an ice-pick, and the three of them took the north road out of Brackhollow across ground the map insisted had once grown barley.

Behind them, the village shrank into its bowl of hills, smoke rising straight from its chimneys into a sky the color of dishwater, and Noor did not look back, because looking back meant seeing the orchard again, forty black trees with their arms raised, and they had made a vow somewhere between the strong room and the gate — a vow with no witness and no contract, which made it the only kind Noor trusted anymore — that when this was over, whatever else happened, whoever they had to burn down to do it, the Gilded Veil was going to give this village back its summer.

They were a quarter mile out when Wren fell into step beside them and said, quietly, without preamble:

"The courier wasn't my brother."

Noor stopped walking. The snow creaked under their boots. Ahead, Oskar kept on, unaware, his breath streaming.

"I told you he was," Wren said, watching the road, not Noor. "And part of me believes it, and that's the problem. Because I don't know if the grief is mine or if it was *sold* to me, and I've been carrying it for three weeks like it's proof of something, and you deserve to know what you're traveling with before the ice road gets narrow."

The wind came down off the high fields, and somewhere behind them, faint and impossible, Noor would have sworn they heard bells.
