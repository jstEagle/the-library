## 9. Wren's Stolen Childhood

The village that sold its summer was three days behind us when the snow finally caught up.

It came down soft at first, the kind of snow that pretends to be gentle, and we made camp in the shell of a waystone shrine — one of those squat stone huts the old kingdoms built for travelers, back when travelers were worth building for. The roof had half fallen in. The walls still held the wind off, mostly. Noor got a fire going with the flat efficiency of someone who has made a thousand fires and enjoyed none of them, and I sat against the far wall with my boots off and my feet screaming, and I watched them.

I'd been watching them for days. That was the problem.

"You're staring," Noor said, without looking up from the fire.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring *assessingly*." They fed a stick to the flames. "Ask whatever it is you're circling around, Ashgrave. You've been orbiting since the marshes. It's making me nervous, and I don't like being nervous. Nervous people make ledger errors."

I laughed. It came out wrong — too sharp, too quick, the laugh of a person buying time. Because I *was* circling something. I'd been circling it since the salt door, since the moment I put Tam's face on and watched Noor flinch, and every mile north had wound the question tighter instead of looser.

The truth was simple. The truth was that I didn't know what I felt about my brother, and I was about to say so out loud to a near-stranger, because they were the only person alive who might understand, and because the snow was coming down like a curtain closing on the world, and because if I didn't say it soon I was going to carry it into the Spire and let it kill me there.

"Tam," I said. "My brother. The courier."

Noor looked up.

"The one whose face I wore when I found you." I turned my hands over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. "You asked me once — back at the salt door, before you lied to Corvane's enforcers for me — you asked how long he'd been dead. And I told you two weeks. That part was true. But you looked at me like you were waiting for something else, and I gave you the grief instead, and you took it, and we've been traveling on it ever since. Like coin."

"And?"

"And I don't know if it's real." The words came out steadier than I deserved. "The grief. I don't know if it's mine."

* * *

Let me tell you how the Veil buys a childhood, since Noor keeps ledgers and might appreciate the accounting.

It doesn't send thugs. Thugs are for people who have things worth protecting and know it. For people like my family — marsh-folk, eel-catchers, poor in the way that poverty is a weather rather than an event — the Veil sends a courteous man with a courteous smile and a contract written in a hand so beautiful it looks like mercy.

I was nine. Tam was twelve. The winter had already been long for thirty years by then; we were children of the frost, we'd never known anything else, and the courteous man explained — kindly, patiently, the way you explain arithmetic to the slow — that our mother's debt could be settled one of two ways. Her fingers. Or our summers.

Not our memories, not at first. That came later, when the interest compounded. At first it was just the warm days. Every day above freezing, every green morning, every afternoon the sun spent on our backs — sold forward, all of them, until the end of our lives. We wouldn't miss them, he said. We'd never had them. You cannot grieve what you never held.

He was almost right.

We signed. My mother pressed her thumb into the wax and cried without sound, which was her way, and Tam put his arm around me and said it didn't matter, summer was just a story anyway, stories were for rich kids in port towns. He believed it. He was very good at believing things so I didn't have to.

And then, when I was fourteen, the Veil came back for the principal.

That's how they do it. The first sale is never the last, because the first sale teaches them what you have. What we had was each other — our childhoods, our mother's face when she sang, the name of the island where our father drowned, the smell of eel-smoke, the particular gray of Tam's eyes before he laughed. A boyhood, itemized. The courteous man — a different one, or maybe the same one wearing a different courtesy — spread the inventory out on our table and explained that the debt had grown, and that memory was the only collateral left that the Veil considered liquid.

My mother refused. Of course she refused. So they took hers instead — all of it, everything, a whole woman poured out of herself and carted south in bottles — and left her sitting in our kitchen, breathing, blinking, polite as furniture. She lived eleven more years like that. She died not knowing my name, and I stood at her funeral grieving a mother who had already been taken from me twice, and I remember thinking: *at least this loss is honest.*

Then the Veil came for mine.

* * *

"They took yours," Noor said quietly. Not a question. The fire cracked and spat. Outside, the snow kept up its soft, endless lying.

"Most of them." I pulled my knees up. "Childhood through sixteen. Everything before the collateral call. They left me the debt years — isn't that generous? Left me the hunger and the cold and the running. Kept the parts worth bottling." I heard my own voice go flat and clerical, Noor's own register, and hated it. "I've seen some of them since. On shelves. In the vaults under the guildhouse, when I was — well. When I was doing what I did. There's a bottle with my label on it. *Ashgrave, Wren. Summer days, assorted. Father, drowning of.* Assorted. Like buttons."

Noor said nothing. They had gone very still, the stillness of a person doing sums they don't want to finish.

"So here's the arithmetic," I said, and my voice finally broke, right on cue, right where I'd been dreading it would. "Everything I remember starts at fourteen. Everything before that is a hole with edges. And Tam — Tam is *in* the hole. He's on both sides of it. I have him after: the thin angry boy teaching me to gut fish, the courier's license, the last time I saw him alive. But the Tam before — the one who carried me on his shoulders, the one whose jokes I supposedly stole, the whole deep shelf of him — that's in a vault in Vellamere, catalogued, priced, probably sold to some merchant's wife who wanted to feel a poor girl's summer afternoons on her skin."

The fire popped. I wiped my face with my sleeve, roughly, furious at the tears for being so ordinary.

"So when I found his body at the salt door, and the grief hit me — that huge, black, floor-dropping grief — I couldn't tell. I lay awake that whole first night asking myself: is this real? Is this what losing a brother feels like? Or is this what losing *half* a brother feels like, the half I was allowed to keep? Would it be bigger if I had all of him? Is it smaller? Is the shape of it even *right*, or am I grieving the way the Veil left me able to grieve?" I laughed again, and this time it sounded like what it was. "They took my childhood and now I can't even trust my mourning. That's the trade, Noor. That's what the Gilded Veil sells. Not memories. *Certainty*. And what it buys is everyone else's."

* * *

For a long moment Noor didn't speak, and I braced for pity, which I would have walked out into the snow to avoid.

It didn't come. What came instead was worse and better.

"When I was exiled from Aerhallow," they said slowly, "the storm killed eleven people. That's the number in the record. Eleven names, read aloud at my trial, and I stood there and felt — nothing. Not denial. Nothing. Like reading a list of goods in someone else's shipment." Their hands were flat on their knees, pressing down, as if they might otherwise float away. "Everyone said that was guilt. Shock, then guilt. The magistrates said a person who felt nothing was worse than a person who felt too much. And I believed them. I've spent six years believing that the emptiness was proof of what I am."

"Wren—"

"But what if it wasn't?" They looked up, and the firelight did something terrible to their face, stripped it down to the question underneath. "What if the nothing wasn't absence? What if it was a *hole with edges*? What if someone went into me the way they went into you, and took the part that could feel those eleven names, and filed it somewhere, and the exile — the exile was the receipt?"

The wind found a gap in the stonework and moaned through it, low and rising, and for one absurd second I thought: *storm-blood answering storm-blood.* Then it passed.

"Your ledgers," I said. "Chapter Six, as it were. Your handwriting in the old book. Entries dated after your exile."

"After my exile." They nodded, once, jerky. "I told myself it was forgery. Someone framing me, or copying my hand, or — I'm good with numbers, Ashgrave, I can make any explanation balance if I try hard enough. That's what a ledger-keeper does. Makes it balance." A breath. "But your face at the salt door. Tam's face. You wore it and I knew — not knew, *felt* — that faces can be worn and lives can be shelved and a person can be edited down to what remains profitable. And ever since, I've been doing the sum I've avoided for six years. If the Veil can take a childhood—" they gestured at me, at all of me, "—then it can take a guilt. It can take a grief. It can take whatever part of a person is inconvenient to the account."

"So you think they took yours."

"I think," Noor said, with the terrible precision of a clerk reading a figure they've checked four times, "that I was in Aerhallow on a night I have no record of. I think my own hand wrote about it. I think the guild erased my past once already, and I never noticed, because they're careful, because erasure done well leaves no scar — except you." They pointed at me across the fire. "You're the scar. You're what it looks like from the inside. Twenty years of living around a hole and calling the hole yourself."

I hadn't thought of it that way. I'd thought of myself as a thief with borrowed faces, a clever ghost, a survivor. I had not thought of myself as evidence.

"It doesn't get better," I said. "Knowing. I want to tell you it does, but it doesn't. You just get a new thing to carry alongside the old ones."

"I'm not looking for better." Noor fed the fire another stick, and their hand was steady, and I understood suddenly that the steadiness cost them everything. "I'm looking for the entries. All of them. Whatever's in the vaults under the guildhouse — mine, yours, your mother's, the eleven names if they're in there — I want the full ledger, Wren. Before Corvane gets to the Spire. Because if she opens it with my blood and I don't know what my blood is *for*—"

They stopped. Outside, the snow thickened, and somewhere down the valley a tree cracked under its weight, sharp as a snapped seal.

* * *

We didn't talk much after that. We banked the fire and took watches, and I lay in my bedroll listening to Noor breathe and turning one fact over and over like a stone in the pocket.

I believed them. That was the strange part. Somewhere between the marshes and this ruined shrine, between their lie at the salt door and their confession tonight, I had stopped keeping score of whether Noor Ravensworth was a mark or a partner, and started simply trusting them — not because they'd earned it, but because they were the only other person I'd ever met who'd been *audited*. Who understood that the self is a set of books, and books can be cooked, and you can spend your whole life balancing figures someone else entered.

"Hey," I whispered across the dark, because I was a coward about everything except this. "If we get into the vaults. If we find your missing night, my missing years — my mother, if there's anything left of her to find. What do we do with it? Drink it? Smash the bottles? Some of it won't fit back in. Some of it's been out too long."

Noor was quiet so long I thought they'd slept.

"Then we read it," they said finally. "Even if it doesn't fit. Even if it changes nothing. A thing that happened deserves to be recorded accurately. That's the whole religion of ledger-keeping, Ashgrave. Everything that happened, written down, in the right hand, under the right date." A pause. "Even when the right hand turns out to be someone else's."

I fell asleep smiling, which should have warned me.

Because at dawn, when the snow eased and we broke camp and came down out of the shrine onto the frozen road, there were hoofprints in the drift ahead of us. Fresh ones. A single rider, moving fast, headed the same direction we were — and beside the road, pinned to a waypost with a silver guild-spike, a folded paper bearing the Gilded Veil's seal.

Noor took it down and opened it, and I watched their face do something I'd never seen it do.

It was addressed to them. By name. And beneath the signature — a signature I recognized, elegant and patient, the courteous hand of Mistress Sable Corvane herself — was a single line:

*I have your mother's bottles. Come alone, or I sell them to the highest bidder in Vellamere by month's end.*
