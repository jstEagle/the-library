## 10. Enforcers on the Frozen Road

The waystation had been a monastery once, before the war made prayer expensive and the Veil bought the deed for a song. Now it was a stone shell with a broken bell tower and a hearth that smoked more than it warmed, crouched on a shelf of mountain where the pass narrowed to a throat. The map's second route ran straight through it.

"We stop here or we die stupid," Wren said, stamping snow off their boots in the doorway. "Those are the options."

"I prefer dying stupid," Noor said. "It's honest."

But they went in anyway, because the wind had teeth now — real winter teeth, not the polite frost of the lowlands — and because Wren's hands had gone the color of tallow two miles back, though they'd never once complained. Noor was learning that about them: Wren Ashgrave would let their fingers blacken and drop off before admitting to a weakness. It was a kind of honesty too, maybe. Or a kind of wound.

Inside, the waystation smelled of old incense and older stone. Someone had left firewood stacked against the far wall, someone long dead; the wood had gone gray as bone but it caught, eventually, after Wren fed the hearth like a nurse spooning broth to a stubborn patient. Noor sat with the map across their knees and did not look at it, because looking at it never helped. The routes moved when unobserved. It was like trying to befriend a cat that hated you.

"The pass closes in three days," Wren said. "Maybe four."

"Corvane's riders closed it behind us at the marshes. They'll come through the north col." Noor traced the map's edge without touching its face. "They're not chasing us, Wren. They're herding us. This is the only warm room between here and the icefall."

Wren went still in the way they did when something landed — a small, total stillness, like an animal deciding whether to bolt. "You think she wants us stopped here."

"I think she wants us *found* here." Noor looked up. "There's a difference. Stopping costs men. Finding just costs patience."

Outside, the wind changed its mind about something.

Noor felt it before they heard it — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper coins on the tongue, the particular hush that came before weather broke bad. They'd felt it all their life, even in Aerhallow, even as a child, the sky leaning down to whisper through them like they were a door left unlatched. In Aerhallow they'd been taught to open that door. Here, alone, they'd spent six years teaching themselves to keep it barred.

"Wren." Their voice came out flat. "Get away from the windows."

* * *

The enforcers came at dusk, which was either tactically sound or theatrically inevitable, depending on how much you believed Corvane planned anything she didn't also stage.

There were six of them, coming up the switchbacks in pairs, dressed in the Veil's traveling grays with white hoods against the snow so that from a distance they looked like nothing at all — like drifts that had learned to walk. Noor watched them from the bell tower's broken arch while Wren doused the fire below, and the cold came in fast behind the dying heat, and Noor thought: *this is what my whole life has been. Watching trouble arrive and calculating how small I can make myself.*

Six was too many. Two might have been negotiable. Six meant someone had told Corvane exactly where they were, which meant the map had been read by someone other than Noor, which meant—

Which meant later. Everything could be later if you survived the present tense.

"They'll take the door," Wren whispered, climbing up beside them, breath ragged. "Frontal. Corvane's people don't do subtle indoors — too many witnesses in guildhouses, so they never learned."

"And out here?"

"Out here they do whatever they want." Wren drew a knife, then a second knife, then seemed to consider whether a third knife was greed. "There's a back stair into the crypt. Old monasteries always have one. We go down and out under them and we're gone before—"

The front door didn't splinter. It simply ceased to be a door, kicked inward with a sound like a bone breaking in a large animal, and torchlight poured across the flagstones, and the moment for clever plans ended.

What followed took less time than it takes to tell. That's the thing about violence nobody warns you: it's over faster than narrative can dress it. Wren went down the inside stair two steps at a time with both knives working, and Noor followed, and the first enforcer reached the landing and met Wren's blade and folded around it with a grunt of pure surprise, and then there were five, and the stairwell was narrow enough that numbers stopped mattering quite so much, and for perhaps forty seconds Noor allowed themselves to believe in the back stair and the crypt and the clean escape.

Then one of them threw something.

Not a knife. A bottle — a squat glass thing wrapped in oiled leather, guild-issue, and Noor knew what it was before it shattered because they'd catalogued four hundred of them in the Gilded Veil's cellars. Bottled cold. Winter in suspension, harvested off the glacier fields and sold south to vintners who wanted ice wine in June. When it broke on the stairs it broke *open*, and the temperature fell through the floor of the world, and frost raced up the walls in ferns, and Wren slipped on the new ice and went down hard, knife ringing away into the dark, and an enforcer stepped over them with a hooked pole — not a weapon to kill with, Noor understood dimly, a weapon to *restrain*, because Corvane wanted them alive, wanted the map, wanted Noor breathing and pliable and delivered.

The hook caught Wren across the shoulder and dragged.

And Noor stood three steps above it all with their back against the frozen wall, and the storm-door inside them rattled on its bars, and they kept it shut.

That was the truth of it, the ugly arithmetic they'd carry out of this night: Noor Ravensworth stood there and did nothing. Not for a breath. Not for ten. Long enough for Wren to twist under the hook, long enough for blood to show black on the ice, long enough for a second enforcer to reach the bottom of the stair and start up toward Noor with a net of weighted cord unrolling from his fist. Long enough to count every reason not to act, and every reason was the same reason wearing different clothes.

*Aerhallow.* The name arrived the way it always did, without permission. The square. The bells. The wrong people, standing where the lightning fell, people who had names and mothers and no part whatsoever in what Noor had been angry about. Six years of exile built on one thrown bolt, and the ledger-keeper in Noor had balanced those books a thousand times: *if you open the door again, you cannot choose what walks through. You are not a person who calls storms. You are a person storms happen to use.*

The enforcer with the net was close enough now that Noor could see his eyes above his hood. Young. Bored. Doing a job.

Below, Wren made a sound Noor had never heard from them — small, strangled, furious at itself for existing — and the hook tightened, and something in Noor's chest did not rattle this time.

Something *broke*.

* * *

Afterward, Noor would try to reconstruct it and fail, the way you fail to reconstruct a fall. There was the breaking, and then there was standing in the courtyard amid the wreckage of the moment, and the middle was gone.

Here is what the middle held, as far as anyone could piece together:

Lightning, indoors. Lightning called down through the broken bell tower as if the tower had been waiting six years for exactly this invitation, as if the whole ruined monastery were a keyhole and Noor the key finally turned. It came down the stairwell ahead of any decision, wearing Noor's anger like a borrowed coat, and it found the enforcer with the net and the enforcer with the hook and the two at the door and it did not distinguish among them, because lightning has never once in the history of the world cared whose fault anything was.

It found the bottled-cold frost on the walls and turned it to steam. It found the bell tower's last beam and split it lengthwise. It found the sixth enforcer running for the treeline and chased him fifty yards through falling snow and dropped him screaming into a drift, alive — Noor would check, would need desperately to check — alive, smoking, cursing, alive.

And through all of it, underneath the thunder, Noor heard themselves laughing.

That was the detail that survived. Not the light, not the smell of scorched wool and ionized air, not Wren's face turned up toward them from the ice with an expression Noor had no category for. The laughter. High and delighted and entirely unfamiliar, rising out of some cellar of the self Noor had never been given a key to, and it sounded — this was the unbearable part — it sounded *happy*. It sounded like Aerhallow's storm-priests on festival nights, when the calling was good and the sky answered and the whole city rang like a struck bowl. It sounded like belonging.

Then it was over. Snow drifted down through the hole where the roof had opinions. Steam curled off the stones. Somewhere in the trees, the sixth enforcer moaned and dragged himself deeper into the dark, and Noor let him go, and was grateful to have a reason to let him go.

"Noor." Wren's voice, hoarse. They were sitting up against the stair rail, one hand pressed to their shoulder, blood between the fingers. "Noor. Look at me."

Noor looked at the courtyard instead. Four bodies. One crawling away. One tower burning quietly at its crown where the beam smoldered. And themselves standing in the center of it untouched, unmarked, hair lifted as if the air still couldn't decide whether to obey them, feeling —

Feeling *wonderful*.

That was the horror. Not guilt — guilt they knew, guilt was an old roommate, guilt paid its share of the rent. This was worse. This was the discovery that some locked room inside them hadn't been suffering all these years behind its bars. It had been *waiting*. Patient as the Veil. Hungry as interest compounding. And now it had eaten, and it lay back satisfied, and it was already, already, already wondering when the next meal might come.

"Noor." Wren got upright by way of the wall, swaying. "You enjoyed that."

It wasn't an accusation. That was the strange mercy of Wren Ashgrave — they didn't deal in accusations, only observations, each one laid on the table like a card they expected you to pick up. Noor picked it up.

"Yes," Noor said.

The word hung in the freezing air. Above them, snow fell through the broken roof onto the dead, gently, impartially, covering everything the way the long winter covered everything, the way it had covered the war and the Spire and every grave the last century had dug. Noor watched it land on an enforcer's open eyes and thought about ledgers. About how every entry had two sides. About how they had spent six years recording only the debit column — the exile, the guilt, the dead in Aerhallow square — and had never once written down what the credit side looked like.

Now they knew. The credit side looked like lightning. The credit side sang.

"I enjoyed it," Noor said, "and I want to do it again, and I don't know which of those facts is the dangerous one." They turned to Wren, and their voice came out steady, which frightened them most of all. "We need to move. The noise will carry down-valley. Whatever else Corvane sent, the noise will bring it."

Wren stared at them a moment longer — cataloguing, Noor realized. Adding a page to whatever private ledger the memory-thief kept. Then they nodded, once, and bent to search the nearest body with their good arm, and said nothing else about it, which was its own kind of covenant.

From the dead man's coat Wren took a flint, a purse, and a letter bearing the Veil's wax seal — black wax, stamped with the sigil Noor knew from a thousand shipping manifests: a hand holding a key upside-down. Wren cracked the seal and read by the light of the burning beam, and Noor watched their face do something complicated.

"What?"

"It's not orders." Wren held it out. "It's a receipt."

Noor took it. The handwriting was precise, unhurried, familiar from a hundred courteous summons:

*Bought and paid: passage of two (2) persons, Ravensworth and companion, via the north col, upon condition of arrival intact. Expenses authorized. Do not harm the ledger-keeper. — S.C.*

Noor read it twice. The snow kept falling. Somewhere below them in the dark, the road south waited, and the icefall beyond it, and the Spire beneath the sea, and a woman two centuries old who had apparently purchased this ambush the way one purchases wine — in advance, at a fair price, expecting delivery.

"She knew we'd stop here," Noor said slowly. "She sent them to *test* us. To see what I'd—" The sentence refused to finish. What Noor had done. What Noor had *been*.

"To see what you'd cost," Wren corrected softly. "Everything with her is pricing." They looked up at the burning tower, then at Noor, and for the first time since the salt door, Wren Ashgrave looked genuinely uncertain. "So the question isn't whether she's hunting us anymore. The question is whether we're doing what she bought — or something she hasn't paid for yet."

Noor folded the receipt into their coat, next to the map, next to the humming memory bottle, next to the growing file of things they'd have to answer for eventually. Inside their chest, the storm-door stood open for the first time in six years, and the wind of the world moved through it, and it felt, appallingly, like home.

"Then we invent something new," Noor said, and turned south, toward the icefall, toward the dark.
