## 13. The Bargain at the Icefall

Wren had worn a hundred faces and never once been afraid inside them. A face was armor. You put it on, you became someone with fewer debts, and the fear stayed outside like weather.

But standing at the edge of the icefall with Corvane's enforcers fanning out across the glacier above them, Wren Ashgrave felt fear crawl back into their own borrowed skin and make itself comfortable.

* * *

It had started well, which should have been the first warning. Nothing in Wren's long career of theft had ever started well and ended better.

They'd made the pass by dusk, Noor setting a pace that was half flight and half fury, the map pressed flat against their chest like something they were afraid would hatch. The mountains here were old and mean, all black stone and hanging ice, and the trail narrowed to a shelf above a frozen waterfall — the icefall, the map called it, in letters that shifted when Wren wasn't looking directly at them. Below it, the glacier dropped away in blue steps toward the coast road. Above it, the waystation they'd fled that morning sat burning against the snow like a wound.

Noor had set the fire. Wren still wasn't sure how they felt about that. The quiet clerk who catalogued bottled memories for a living had lit three enforcers' quarters ablaze without breaking stride, and then stood watching the flames with an expression Wren recognized from mirrors: appetite, quickly buried.

"We camp below the fall," Noor said now. "The map shows a hollow behind it. We'll be invisible until dawn."

"The map also showed us walking off a cliff two days ago," Wren said. "I checked."

"It corrected itself."

"Maps don't correct themselves, Noor."

"This one does." Noor's breath came out white and fast. "Everything about this one does. Come on."

The hollow behind the icefall was real — a pocket of air walled in by frozen water thick as cathedral glass, glowing faintly where the last light came through. Inside, the cold was different. Older. Wren built a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, using charcoal and a trick of flint they'd stolen along with a smuggler's hands two winters ago, and for an hour they almost rested.

Noor unrolled the map across their knees and went silent in the particular way that meant thinking too hard.

"You're doing the ledger face," Wren said.

"I don't have a ledger face."

"You absolutely do. I've watched you inventory stolen childhoods with it. Something's bothering you besides the obvious."

Noor's finger traced the route south. "The routes are converging. Look. Every path the map offers — every one — bends toward the harbor. Even the ones that started pointing west." A pause. "It's not showing us options anymore. It's herding us."

Wren looked at the vellum. It did look like herding. And herding meant a shepherd, and there was only one shepherd who'd spent two centuries arranging this journey, according to the cartographer's grave and its cheerful little epitaph about apprentices.

"She knows where we are," Wren said.

"She's known since the tomb."

"Then why hasn't she—"

The avalanche answered before the question did.

* * *

It didn't come from above. That was the horror of it. It came from the sides — two slopes flanking the icefall, snowfields that had hung there for centuries, released together with a crack like the world's spine giving way. The sound arrived first, then the white wall of it, and Wren was moving before thought caught up, dragging Noor deeper into the hollow as the world outside turned to thunder.

The icefall held. That was the miracle — or the design. The frozen waterfall took the avalanche's weight and shrugged it into the gorge below, and when the roaring finally faded, the hollow remained intact, sealed behind thirty feet of fresh-packed snow, and the two of them remained alive inside it.

Trapped.

"No," Noor said, to the situation, to the map, to the universe. "No, this is—"

"A trap," Wren finished. "A very old, very patient trap. She didn't even have to send anyone down the mountain. She just waited for us to walk into the jar and put the lid on."

The fire guttered. In its light, Noor's face had gone the color of the vellum. "She could have buried us."

"She could have buried us at the waystation. At the marshes. In your sleep, any night since the salt door." Wren heard their own voice go flat and professional, the voice of a thief assessing a lock. "She wants us alive and she wants us desperate and she wants us to know she could have killed us a hundred times. That's not hunting, Noor. That's negotiation."

As if summoned by the word, the snow sealing the hollow began to move.

Not slide — open. Melted through from the outside in some impossible warm channel, steaming, until a corridor of clear air bored through the drift and stopped six feet short of them. Standing at the end of it, framed by white, was a woman in a grey traveling coat with frost in her silver hair and courtesy in every line of her body.

Mistress Sable Corvane had come alone, exactly as the grave had promised, and somehow that was worse than the enforcers.

"Noor Ravensworth," she said warmly. "And the Ashgrave thief. What a small world the north is becoming."

* * *

Wren had planned, in the abstract, for this meeting. The plans had involved knives, exits, and other people's faces. None of the plans survived contact with the actual woman, because Corvane didn't move like a threat. She moved like a hostess. She stepped into the hollow as if she'd been invited, brushed snow from her coat, and regarded their tiny fire with mild approval.

"May I?" She didn't wait for an answer. She sat on a ledge of ice as though it were a chaise, and the cold seemed to negotiate around her rather than with her.

"You buried us," Noor said.

"I protected you. There's a difference, though I understand it's a subtle one." Corvane spread her gloved hands. "There were eleven enforcers on those slopes, Noor. My enforcers, yes, but enforcers nonetheless — men and women who believe the map must be burned and its bearers with it. I sent them east into the pass yesterday with orders I knew they'd misread. The avalanche was mine. It was the gentlest available option."

"You expect us to believe you saved our lives."

"I expect you to verify it, because you're a ledger-keeper and verification is your religion." Her eyes — pale, patient, older than they had any right to be — moved to Wren. "You, I expect less of. But you've surprised me already, so perhaps we'll both be surprised tonight."

"What do you want?" Noor asked.

"The same thing you want. To reach the Spire." Corvane let that sit. "You found Vosk's grave. You know what she was. You know what she did — what she's still doing, up on that drowned rock, dreaming the winter to keep the war asleep. I want to open the gates. So do you. The only question is the terms, and I've come to discuss terms like a civilized person."

"Terms," Wren repeated. "From the woman who runs a guild that sells people's memories as collateral."

"Who told you that?"

"Wren," Noor said carefully, "don't—"

"Your friend here has a personal grievance," Corvane went on, silk over steel. "Sold memories. Stolen faces. A brother dead on the salt door step. I know the whole sad inventory, Ashgrave; I approved the original transaction twenty years ago. Your family defaulted on a loan of borrowed years. The Veil took payment in kind. That's arithmetic, not cruelty — but I'm not here to defend arithmetic." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught something in her face that was not kindness and was not cruelty either, but hunger wearing both as masks. "I'm here because I can give it all back."

The words dropped into the hollow like stones into a well.

"Every memory the Veil holds," Corvane said. "Bottled, shelved, collateralized — thousands of them. Forty years of harvests. Every stolen childhood, every sold summer, every name that was traded away. I hold the keys to the vaults, and I am prepared to open every single one."

"Why?" Noor's voice was very quiet.

"Because I'll need a guild worth ruling when this is over, and a guild built on hostage memories is a guild built on the Spire's methods. I've had two hundred years to learn where those methods lead." She smiled, briefly, and it was the loneliest expression Wren had ever seen on a human face. "Also because I need you, Noor. Storm-blood opens the gates. You know that now. And you'll do it more willingly if your conscience is clean — and your conscience, I've noticed, is a loud and inconvenient instrument."

"And if we refuse?" Wren asked.

Corvane gestured at the walls of snow. "You're welcome to dig."

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about bargains, having made most of the bad kinds: the devil is never in the details. The details are fine. The devil is in the thing nobody says — the clause written in the silence between sentences, the price that gets named later, when it's too late to walk away.

So while Noor and Corvane talked terms — memory vaults, escort arrangements, the question of whether Wren counted as a hostage or a guest — Wren watched. Watched the woman's hands, which never gestured without purpose. Watched her eyes, which tracked the map on Noor's knees whenever it shifted, quick as a hawk's. Watched the way she said *the Spire* — softly, the way other people said the names of the dead.

Two hundred years, the grave had said. Apprentice, the grave had said. Wren had worn enough faces to know what lived under all of them: wanting. Corvane wanted something from that prison with her whole ancient self, and it wasn't the winter's end.

But.

But she was offering the vaults. And Wren's own childhood was sitting in one of them, in a bottle on a shelf in the Gilded Veil's deep cellar, filed under a number instead of a name. Twenty years of not knowing whether the grief for their brother was real or purchased, whether the face they'd been born with had ever fit, whether there had ever been a *they* underneath all the thefts — all of it locked in glass, and here was the keyholder saying *open*.

Brittle hope, Wren thought. Hope you could hear cracking if you squeezed it.

"Noor." They cut across the negotiation. "A word."

Noor glanced up, grateful for the interruption or pretending to be. They stepped to the far side of the hollow, close to the humming ice, voices low.

"You're going to say take the deal," Noor said.

"I'm going to say take the deal *and* keep your hand on the knife. Both things." Wren crossed their arms. "She means the vaults. That part's true — I've lied professionally for fifteen years, I know true when it's spending itself on me. She wants the Spire opened more than she wants anything, and she'll pay real coin for it."

"But?"

"But she said *a guild worth ruling*. Not ending. Ruling. And she looked at that map like a woman looking at a deed." Wren held Noor's gaze. "Whatever's sleeping in that Spire, she doesn't want it dead. She wants it inherited. And you're not her key, Noor. You're her heir apparent. Ask yourself why she needs storm-blood specifically. Ask yourself why your memories were taken in the first place, and who signed the order."

Noor had gone very still. "You think I'm—"

"I think nothing. I think the Veil doesn't erase memories for free. Somebody paid for yours to be taken, or somebody was afraid of what was in them. Either way—" Wren shrugged. "Either way, we go down the mountain, we watch her like hawks, and the moment she reaches for whatever she actually wants, we remember that we never trusted her. Deal?"

For a moment Noor almost smiled. "You've gotten frighteningly good at this."

"I've had frightening teachers."

They returned to the fire. Corvane waited with the patience of furniture.

"Terms," Noor said. "One: every memory in the Veil's vaults goes free — not sold, not traded, freed. Publicly. Before we open anything. Two: Wren walks with me into the Spire or I don't walk in at all. Three: the moment the gates are open, your claim on me ends. Whatever you think my blood makes me, I'm not an asset on your ledger."

Corvane considered. Wren could see her weighing each term like a jeweler, finding flaws, deciding the flaws were affordable.

"Accepted," she said. "With one amendment. The freeing of the vaults happens when the gates open — not before. I won't empty my guild's heart on a promise. You'll have my oath sworn on my own name, and where we're going, names have weight. You know that better than anyone now, Noor."

Noor flinched — the gate that costs a name, the map had warned, and Wren saw them touch their own throat without meaning to — but they nodded.

"Then we have a bargain," Corvane said, and rose, and with a gesture the melted corridor through the snow widened into a stair.

* * *

They descended toward the coast under armed truce, which is to say: three people walking in a line through the blue dusk of the glacier, everyone smiling, nobody turning their back.

Corvane led. Of course she led. She walked the ice like a woman strolling her own garden, and behind her came Noor with the map, and behind Noor came Wren, counting exits out of habit and finding none, and finding, beneath the fear, something worse.

Hope. Brittle, cracking, ridiculous hope. Because somewhere south of here was a frozen harbor, and past the harbor a singing prison, and past the prison — if the arithmetic held, if the oaths held, if Noor's storm-blood and Wren's stubbornness and two hundred years of Corvane's patience all added up right — a cellar full of bottles, and in one of them, a child with Wren's actual face, waiting twenty years to be poured back into the world.

Behind them, far up the slopes, Wren heard it: a whistle, thin and carrying. Eleven enforcers, regrouping, finding the pass blocked and their mistress gone ahead without them.

Corvane didn't turn. But Wren saw her smile in profile, small and satisfied, and heard her say, mostly to herself:

"They'll follow. They always follow. Let them watch what I've waited for."

Below, past the glacier's toe, the land fell away toward a darkness that was the sea, and under the sea, faint as a pulse through the soles of their boots, Wren felt the ice humming — the Spire, singing to the one bloodline that could wake it.

Noor stumbled, just once, and pressed a hand to their chest as if something in there had answered.

Wren reached out and steadied them, and kept walking, and hoped.
