## 14. Harbor of the Drowned Bells

The harbor town was called Bellhaven, though Wren learned later that this was a courtesy. The real name had drowned with the bells.

They came down off the ice road at dusk, three travelers under an armed truce that frayed a little more with every mile: Noor in front, hollow-eyed and storm-marked; Corvane behind, walking like a woman out for a constitutional, which Wren found more frightening than any ambush; and Wren themselves, carrying the map and every stolen face they owned folded up inside them like letters never sent.

The bells were the first thing they heard. Or rather, the absence of bells — a harbor town should have been loud with them, and instead there was only wind moving through rigging gone stiff as wire, and beneath it a low tolling that came from under the ice. Once every few minutes. Patient. Like something counting.

"The drowned bells," Corvane said, pleased. "They ring when the tide moves beneath the harbor. The locals think it's the sea praying."

"What's it praying for?" Noor asked.

"Forgiveness," Corvane said, "or an ending. With congregations it's usually both."

Wren did not like her voice tonight. It had too much warmth in it, the way a trap has warmth if you're the bait.

Bellhaven resolved out of the gloom by degrees: a crooked skyline of salt-bleached houses, nets hung frozen on racks like the town had been interrupted mid-breath, and everywhere ice — ice on the roofs, ice sheathing the boats in their cradles, ice over the harbor itself, thick and white and scarred with old auger holes. The whole place smelled of fish oil and woodsmoke and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a bell.

People watched them come down the one street. They didn't call out. They didn't bar their doors either, which Wren thought was worse. Doors meant fear of strangers. This was something else — this was a town deciding what it was looking at.

"They know," Noor said quietly. It wasn't a question. The storm-calling at the waystation had lit up half the sky; word traveled faster than they did.

"They know," Corvane agreed. "Good. Saves introductions."

Wren caught Noor's sleeve. "We don't have to stop here. There'll be another—"

"There won't," Corvane said. "This is the last harbor before the sea ice. Everything east of here is the Spire's country." She smiled at them both, gracious as a hostess. "And we need provisions, and sledges, and a guide who knows where the pressure ridges open. Unless one of you has learned to walk on water since the mountain."

She was right, which Wren hated. Three days of hard travel had burned through their supplies, and the map's final route began at the harbor's edge, at a marker stone carved with a spiral that matched the seal on Vosk's tomb. The Spire lay somewhere out under all that white, singing to anyone with ears tuned wrong.

Noor had ears tuned wrong. Wren had watched them flinch all day whenever the wind shifted, like the ice below was calling a name they couldn't quite hear.

* * *

The inn was called the Last Anchor, and it was warm the way a held breath is warm. A dozen locals sat around the long stove without talking, and every face turned when the door opened, and every face stayed turned.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with frost-scars across her knuckles, old ones, the kind you got working lines as a child. She looked at Corvane longest — people always looked at Corvane longest, some animal part of them recognizing money or age or appetite — but it was Noor she spoke to.

"You're the one," she said. Not asking.

"I'm a clerk," Noor said.

"You're the one the sky broke for." The woman set down her rag. "Three nights past. Green fire over the mountains. My grandmother saw green fire once, sixty years ago, the night the winter came down. She said it was the world's wound showing." She looked around the room, gathering the others into her certainty the way a net gathers fish. "Storm-witch."

The word went around the room like a coal passed hand to hand. Wren saw Noor go very still, the stillness of someone doing arithmetic they already dread the answer to.

"We're paying customers," Wren said, and put coin on the bar, guild-minted, heavy. "For rooms, food, sledges, and a guide. We leave at first light."

The innkeeper didn't look at the coin. "You'll take no guide onto the ice. The ice is holy ground. Under it sleeps the Punished One, and the bells are her penance, and every soul who walks out there walks on God's own prison floor." She leaned forward. "We don't cross it. We keep it. That's the covenant. Our grandmothers signed it in blood and hunger, and we keep it."

"Then sell us sledges and stay home," Wren said.

"And let the witch wake her?" Now the room moved, chairs scraping, the crowd becoming a crowd. "The green fire was the lock rattling. We've kept the winter a hundred years so the Punished One could finish her sorrow, and now this one comes walking with storm in their blood to break her rest early?" The innkeeper's voice dropped, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "The old books say what stops a storm-witch. The sea takes what the sky sent. We give the storm back to the water, and the water keeps it, and the winter holds."

Wren had seen mobs before. Had been mobs, once or twice, wearing borrowed faces, back when theft required a body. You could read a room the way you read a ledger, and this ledger was already balanced toward blood. What they needed was time, or a lie, or a miracle, and they had none of the above.

What they had was a satchel of memories.

Corvane, Wren noticed, had not moved. She stood by the fire with her hands folded, watching, and her expression was the expression of a woman checking her watch. Whatever happened here, she had decided, would be instructive. Wren made a private note to survive her.

The innkeeper's sons came around the bar. Big men, rope-scarred, moving without hurry because hurry wasn't needed — there were twelve of them and two of them, and the third was Corvane, who would help exactly no one.

"Outside," the innkeeper said. "There's a hole in the harbor ice. Old one. Ceremonial. The water under it is black and it doesn't freeze, and my grandmother said it goes all the way down to the bells." She took Noor's arm, almost gently. "You'll go in quiet, witch. The sea will do the rest."

"Noor—" Wren started, and a hand like a dock cleat closed on their shoulder.

It happened fast after that. Too fast to plan, which was how Wren worked worst. They were marched out the back door into the blue dark, torches blooming along the street as neighbors fell in, and the crowd had that terrible festival quality now, hushed and certain, everyone knowing their part in a rite rehearsed for generations but never performed. Children peered from windows. An old woman threw salt.

The hole in the ice was maybe thirty feet out, a circle of open black water steaming faintly, ringed with stones and frozen garlands and small bones — fish bones, bird bones, Wren hoped — and the tolling of the drowned bells was louder here, close enough to feel in the teeth.

They put a rope around Noor's waist. For the weights, someone said. So the sea holds what it's given.

Noor looked at Wren across the crowd. Their face was calm in the way of ledgers, in the way of someone cataloging their own death because panic wouldn't balance the column. But their eyes said: *do something*, and then, worse, said: *don't. Let them. Maybe they're right.*

"Don't you dare," Wren breathed.

The innkeeper raised her arms. The crowd knelt. The bells tolled, and tolled, and tolled.

And Wren Ashgrave, memory-thief, coward, survivor, opened their satchel.

* * *

Here is what Wren had never told anyone, not even in the confession on the frozen road: among all the faces and years and small bright thefts in their keeping, there was one bottle they had never sold, never traded, never even opened to look at. It had come to them eleven years ago, in the first months, when they were still learning the trade and still stealing only what they could carry. A dying woman in a burning border town — a Veil job gone wrong, a purge dressed as collection — had pressed it into their hands along with her blood.

Her name had been Sefa. She had been nobody. A cook, a mother, a woman who sang off-key while she worked. And the bottle held the last summer of her childhood: a river, a willow, a sister splashing her, sun so warm it had weight, the taste of stolen plums, laughter that came out of her whole body. The single most ordinary afternoon ever lived, and Wren had carried it through eleven winters because it was proof. Proof that warmth had existed. Proof that the world before the winter had been *like that* — green and stupid and golden and full of plum-stones spat into rivers.

On the coldest nights, Wren took it out and held it and did not open it, because opening it would spend it, and spending it would mean admitting that someday even proof runs out.

They had planned, in the dishonest ledger of things they'd tell themselves, to return it to Sefa's family someday. Find the sister. Give the summer back.

The sister was ash eleven years gone. The river was probably under a glacier. And Noor Ravensworth was standing on the edge of a hole in the world with a rope around their waist.

Wren walked forward into the kneeling crowd, and the crowd let them, because the crowd hadn't decided what a thief was yet.

"Holy woman," Wren said to the innkeeper, loud enough to carry. "Before you give the sea its due — the old rites require payment. Even your grandmother's books say it. Nothing given to the water is given free, or the water doesn't hold it." It was a lie. It was absolutely a lie, delivered with the flat confidence of a customs form. "I have a relic. Warmth from before the winter. If the sea is to take a storm-witch, let it be paid in sunlight."

Murmuring. The innkeeper's eyes narrowed, hungry and suspicious in equal measure. Towns that worship winter dream constantly of summer; Wren had counted on that the way they counted on everything, coldly and precisely.

"Show me," the innkeeper said.

Wren drew out the bottle. Small, plain glass, cork sealed with wax gone amber with age. Inside, impossibly, warmly, the liquid light of a July afternoon moved like a goldfish in a bowl, and the crowd made a sound Wren had never heard a crowd make — a soft, involuntary moan, the sound of people remembering that they remembered.

A child near the front reached out toward it. Her mother pulled her back, then couldn't help herself and reached out too.

"It's true," someone whispered. "Summer was true."

"Open it," the innkeeper said hoarsely. "At the water. Let the sea see what it's owed."

So Wren walked to the edge of the hole, past Noor, whose eyes had gone wide and stricken, whose lips formed *no, no, Wren, don't* — and knelt at the black water, and pulled the cork.

The summer came out like a bird.

Heat rolled across the harbor, actual heat, a pocket of July twenty paces wide, and the snow on the nearby stones hissed and slid, and for one impossible moment the whole town of Bellhaven stood inside a fragment of the world as it had been. Wren felt it wash over them — river-water, plum-sweetness, a sister's laugh, sun with weight — and they fed it deliberately, generously, into the air, into the crowd, letting the memory pour out and over everyone like wine, because a thing given freely reads as holy, and a thing spent grudgingly reads as fraud, and Wren Ashgrave had never once in their life done anything this freely.

The crowd wept. Grown rope-handed men wept. The child laughed — actually laughed, hearing laughter for the first time, laughing at laughter — and that sound went through Wren like a harpoon.

Then the summer guttered, thinned, and was gone, and the cold rushed back in like water into a hull, and the bottle in Wren's hands was empty glass, and would always and forever be empty glass.

The silence afterward was total. Even the bells seemed to hold their stroke.

"The debt is paid," the innkeeper said at last, unsteadily. "The sea has seen sunlight. The offering—" She faltered, looking at Noor, at the rope, at the hole. "The offering can be... commuted. A tithe instead of the whole. Salt and silver and a sworn passage, marked by the covens, safe-conduct over the holy ice."

"A tithe," Wren agreed instantly. "Generous. Traditional. Very old."

It was very new. Nobody cared.

* * *

Later, in a room with a locked door and a stove, Noor sat across from Wren and did not speak for a long time. Corvane had taken the other room, and through the wall came nothing at all, which meant she was awake and listening, which meant everything.

"You gave it up," Noor finally said. "Your proof. Your — you said it was the realest thing you owned."

"It was a bottle."

"It was the only warm thing about you."

Wren almost smiled. "That's why it worked. Holy relics have to cost the giver. Everyone in that square knew it, even if they didn't know they knew it. If I'd burned a stranger's memory, they'd have smelled the difference and drowned you anyway." They turned the empty bottle in their fingers. Light moved in it still, faintly, the way an echo moves. "Besides. I stole it from a dying woman. It was never mine. I just... kept it longer than thieves keep things."

Noor crossed the room and sat beside them, shoulder to shoulder, and neither of them said anything else, because there was a language of silence between them now that had been bought at the waystation and the ice road and a square full of kneeling strangers, and it said enough.

But sleep didn't come, and near midnight Wren heard it — Noor sitting up suddenly, head cocked, listening to the harbor.

"Do you hear that?" Noor whispered.

Under the ice, beneath the town, beneath the black ceremonial water, the drowned bells had changed their rhythm. All night they had tolled slow and patient, a penance-count. Now they rang quick and bright and glad, over and over, like a rung change, like peals, like —

Like bells answering a question asked for the first time in a hundred years.

And far out on the sea ice, at the edge of torchlight, the snow stood up. Not drifted. *Stood.* A figure, tall and thin, facing the town, facing Noor's window, motionless in the wind.

Noor's breath fogged the glass. Outside, the figure raised one hand — not a threat, Wren thought, watching over Noor's shoulder with their heart going like a drum. More like a wave. Like a greeting.

Like a grandmother who has waited a very long time, and has just heard her grandchild's footsteps on the road.

"She knows we're coming," Noor said.

The bells rang on, joyful and drowned, and out on the ice the figure turned and walked away into the dark, leaving footprints that glowed faintly green, and did not fill with snow.
