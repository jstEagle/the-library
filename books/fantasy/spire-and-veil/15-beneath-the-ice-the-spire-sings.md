## 15. Beneath the Ice, the Spire Sings

The sea ice began three days out of the harbor, and it did not look like anything Noor had a name for.

They had expected white. What they got was color — vast sheets of green and violet glass, layered like the memory bottles in the Veil's vaults, so that walking across them felt like walking through someone else's stored years. Below their boots, in places, dark shapes moved: fish frozen mid-turn, a whale suspended the size of a chapel, its eye pressed to the underside of the ice like a thing peering into a window.

"Don't look down," Wren said, for the fourth time that morning.

"I'm not looking down."

"You're looking down."

Noor looked up instead, at Corvane, who walked ahead of them all with her hands folded behind her back as though the sea ice were a corridor in her guildhouse and the wind a minor clerical error. She had kept her word about the memories — or claimed to. Somewhere in the sled she'd pulled herself, wrapped in oilcloth and wax seals, were crates of bottles: the freed hostages of the Gilded Veil's arithmetic, every one of them destined for whatever village or family still existed to receive them. Noor had checked the manifest twice. It was correct to the last entry. That frightened them more than any lie would have.

Corvane was honest the way an avalanche was honest.

Behind them came the harbor folk's parting gift: they had wanted to burn Noor on the ice as a storm-witch, and Wren had bought mercy with a memory — burned it, actually burned it, feeding their most precious stolen possession into a brazier while the crowd watched the smoke and called it penance. Noor still didn't know which memory it had been. Wren wouldn't say. That was the shape of their friendship now: a debt neither of them could price.

The map — what remained of it, the fragment that had survived the tomb and the ambush and the burning of the cartographer's grave — pointed south across the ice toward a smudge of darkness on the horizon that might have been weather or might have been architecture. The Spire. *Sunken*, the old texts said, but from here it looked less sunken than drowned upright, a black needle standing in the sea itself, half-swallowed, humming.

Noor had been hearing the hum since dawn.

At first they'd blamed the cold. Cold sang; everyone knew that. It sang in rigging and in teeth and in the space between words. But this was pitched differently — low, patient, almost melodic, rising and falling in long slow phrases like something breathing in its sleep. And it was getting closer, which was impossible, because sound didn't work that way over ice, and because nothing else about this journey had respected the rules of sound or distance or sense.

By midday the others heard it too.

One of Corvane's two remaining enforcers — Brannic, the one with the frost-scarred jaw — stopped dead and cocked his head like a hound. "Mistress. You hear that?"

"I hear it," Corvane said, and for the first time since the icefall, something in her voice was not entirely controlled. "Keep walking."

"It sounds like—"

"*Keep walking.*"

But Wren had gone pale under their borrowed face — the face of the clerk Noor had buried last spring, worn now so long it seemed more real than whatever lived underneath — and they fell into step beside Noor and said, quietly, "It's singing. Actually singing. Do you know the song?"

"No."

"I think I do." Wren's breath came out in silver plumes. "That's the worst part. I've never heard it before and I know how it goes. There's a verse coming. I can feel it waiting, like a word on the tip of the tongue." They glanced sideways. "Is that what your storms feel like?"

Noor didn't answer. But yes. Yes, exactly that.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a pressure ridge, walls of shattered ice reared up around them like the ribs of something enormous. Corvane set watches. The enforcers slept in shifts with their weapons unsheathed. The fire was small and blue-burning, fed from a pouch of chemical bricks the guild used when there was no wood for a thousand miles, and it gave off no warmth worth mentioning, only light enough to see each other's faces and pretend that mattered.

Noor took first watch, alone at the edge of the camp, facing south.

The Spire stood against the last of the twilight like a splinter of night. Closer now. Close enough that Noor could see it wasn't smooth — it was carved, or grown, covered in ridges and hollows, and the hum came off it in visible waves, shivering the snow-mist above the ice into patterns.

And then, somewhere between one breath and the next, the hum became a voice.

Noor knew the voice before they understood it, the way you know a room before you remember its name. A voice pitched low and warm, roughened at the edges, with a habit of rising at the end of sentences as if everything were a question worth asking. A voice that had sung working songs over a washbasin. That had said *come inside, love, the sky's in one of its moods* a thousand times in a city where the sky was always in one of its moods.

Aerhallow. Their mother. Dead eleven years, drowned in the flood that Noor's own storm had summoned — the wrong storm, the wrong people, the ledger-entry that had ended their old life and begun this colorless one.

"No," Noor said aloud, to the ice, to the dark, to themselves. "No, that's not—"

*Come inside, love,* the ice sang. *The sky's in one of its moods.*

They were on their feet without deciding to stand. Behind them the camp stirred; Wren's voice, sharp with sleep: "Noor? What is it?"

"Don't follow me." Their own voice sounded far away, borrowed, as if someone else were wearing it the way Wren wore faces. "I have to— it's calling, it knows my—"

They walked out onto the open ice.

Later, they would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. There was the green-violet glass underfoot going strange beneath them, deepening, darkening, until they were walking on black water locked under a finger's width of clear ice, and below the ice — below, impossibly far below, at the bottom of the drowned world — lights. Not fish-lights. Windows. Streets. A city under the sea, laid out in a grid they knew, *knew*, the way you know the layout of a childhood home: the terraced rows of Aerhallow's lower quarter, the market square, the seawall where they'd kissed someone whose name was a blank spot in their chest, all of it drowned and lit and *preserved*, every wave of the flood frozen mid-catastrophe, figures in the streets with their arms raised, mouths open, caught forever in the moment the water came.

The war, Vosk had frozen. But this — this was something else. This was a memory so large it had become a place.

*You're the only one who can hear me properly,* the voice said, and it was gentle, so gentle, the way she'd been gentle when Noor was small and feverish, sitting up all night with a cool cloth. *The others hear weather. You hear me.*

"You're not her." Noor's voice cracked on the ice like a whip-crack. "She's dead. I killed her. I know the arithmetic. I've done it ten thousand times in ledgers that don't lie."

*I never said I was her.* A pause, and the tenderness in it was worse than cruelty. *I said I needed a voice you couldn't refuse.*

The lights below shifted. The drowned streets rearranged themselves — not Aerhallow anymore but something older, vaster, a spiral descending into darkness, and at its heart a point of white light like a held breath. The Spire's interior. Its heart. The place where an old woman dreamed a winter and would not wake.

*Eleven years ago,* the voice said, *you stood in a tower in Aerhallow and called a storm. Do you remember why?*

"I remember—" Noor reached for it and found the familiar hole, the smooth-walled absence where the reason should have been. "I don't. They took it. The Veil took the why along with everything else."

*Did they?* The voice was almost amused. *Or did someone take it from the Veil? Think, ledger-keeper. Who has the power to reach into a guild vault and lift a single memory out of a sealed bottle? Who has walked through the Gilded Veil's wards for two hundred years without leaving a footprint?*

The cold went through Noor then, deeper than the ice, deeper than the sea.

*Your mother's mother's mother,* the voice continued, soft as snowfall, *was born on the Spire's threshold. Ilyen Vosk had a daughter before she built her prison. The daughter fled north. The blood thinned and hid and forgot, generation by generation, the way blood does. Until it pooled again in a child in Aerhallow who could call lightning out of a clear sky and didn't know why the storms answered like family.*

"No."

*Why do you think the courier found you? Of every clerk in every guildhouse on the coast, why press the map into your hands? Why do you think the map bleeds when you cut it? It's vellum from the Spire's own skin, keeper. It's been waiting two centuries for blood it recognizes. Yours.*

"No." Louder. Their hands were shaking, and beneath their feet, faintly, the ice hummed back — a sympathetic vibration, a tuning fork finding its note. Frost crawled out from their boot-heels in feathered stars. "The Veil erased me. Corvane said—"

*Corvane arranged it.* Now the voice was urgent, leaning close. *Who do you think sold your memories to the vault? Not the guild's routine confiscation — a private transaction, signed in a hand two hundred years old. She took your past and locked it away because she needs you empty. An empty vessel opens the gate. A full one might refuse. She has spent eleven years curating you, keeper — the exile, the guilt, the careful little ledgers — building you into exactly the key she requires. And the gate is three days ahead of us.*

The ice sang. The drowned city glowed. Somewhere behind Noor, footsteps — Wren, running across the glass, shouting their name, and Corvane's voice too, calm and terrible, telling everyone to stop, to let the keeper walk.

*So here is what I offer,* the voice said, and it was her mother's voice offering the last sweet roll, offering the window seat, offering everything with that same rising question at the end. *When you stand at my gate, remember: she wants a key. I want a successor. One of us is lying, and you have three days to decide which.*

The lights went out all at once, street by street, like a city closing its eyes.

Noor came back to themselves kneeling on the black ice, Wren's hands on their shoulders, shaking them, saying their name over and over in a voice scraped raw. The pressure ridge and the blue fire were thirty yards behind. Corvane stood at the camp's edge, watching, her expression unreadable, her breath steaming in the wind that had begun — Noor realized with a lurch — to circle *them*, gently, obediently, the way a dog circles its master.

"Noor." Wren gripped harder. "You walked out here asleep. Eyes open, completely asleep. What did it say to you?"

And Noor, looking up at Wren's borrowed face, at the friend who had burned their truest memory for a stranger's sake, understood that the ground under their whole life had just become sea ice: load-bearing, beautiful, and one false step from opening.

They thought of the smooth-walled hole where the reason for the storm should be. They thought of Corvane's manifest, correct to the last entry. They thought of a hand two hundred years old signing away a child's past.

They thought: *she curated me. Every colorless day. Every careful ledger. Even my guilt was hers.*

"Noor," Wren said again. "Talk to me."

Noor stood. The wind settled around their shoulders like a mantle, patient and adoring, and they hated it, and some traitorous chamber of their heart preened under its attention.

"It told me who I am," they said. "And Wren — I don't know yet whether it lied."

Three days to the Spire. Three days to decide whether they were a person or a key.

Behind them, Corvane smiled, and began to walk.
