## 17. Ilyen Vosk, Still Dreaming

The Spire's heart was a cathedral of ice, and it was breathing.

Noor felt it before they saw it — a slow tidal pull in the air, the whole chamber expanding and contracting like the chest of something vast and sleeping. The walls here had given up pretending to be stone. They were memory made solid, layered in translucent strata: a burning fleet, a city's bells ringing alarm, a field of soldiers mid-charge with frost climbing their throats. The war, frozen at its worst hour, still fighting itself in miniature.

And at the center, suspended in a column of blue-white ice that rose from floor to vaulted dark, hung a woman.

She looked asleep. That was the horror of it. Not dead, not preserved — *asleep*, the way Noor's mother used to sleep on storm nights, one hand open, trusting the weather to be kind. Her hair drifted around her face as if she floated underwater. Her lips moved, faintly, shaping words no one would ever hear, or everyone would.

"That's her," Wren whispered. Their borrowed face — the clerk Aldous, dead these ten months — had gone pale and wrong, the stolen features slipping like a mask in rain. "That's Ilyen Vosk."

"She looks young," Noor said. It came out stupid, small. Two hundred years of winter, and the woman responsible had the face of someone who'd fall asleep reading.

"Time doesn't spend itself in here." Corvane stood apart from them both, hands folded, watching the ice column with an expression Noor had never seen on her: not ambition, not calculation. Something older and wearier. Grief, maybe, or its fossil. "I told you. She isn't imprisoned. She's *working*."

The singing Noor had heard since the harbor — their mother's voice, threading through the sea ice — swelled as they approached the column. It wasn't coming from Vosk. It was coming from the ice itself, from every frozen moment in the walls, and it was all the same voice, all the same song, and Noor understood with a lurch that the Spire had learned it from her, long ago, the way a child learns a lullaby.

*Come in,* the song said. *Come home.*

"No," Noor said aloud, and the word cracked the silence like a dropped glass.

Vosk's eyes opened.

They were gray, the gray of old sea ice, and they found Noor across the chamber with terrible precision, the way a compass finds north. Her lips kept moving behind the ice. And though no sound crossed it, the voice arrived anyway — inside, behind the eyes, in the register where memories live.

*You have your grandmother's stubbornness. I can see it. You walk like you're apologizing for taking up space, and then you plant your feet like the ground owes you rent.*

"Stop that," Noor said.

*I've been alone with my own thoughts for two centuries. Forgive me for being glad of company.* A pause, and something almost like humor moved through the cold. *You brought Sable. Of course you did. She was always going to bring herself eventually. I only wondered which of you she'd send ahead.*

Corvane stepped forward. "Mistress."

*Apprentice,* said the voice in Noor's skull, and heard Corvane flinch at hearing it aloud in her own memory. *Still calling me mistress after two hundred years. Still waiting for permission.*

"I'm not here for permission." Corvane's voice was level, but Noor saw her hands, white-knuckled at her sides. "I'm here to finish what you couldn't."

*What I wouldn't,* Vosk corrected gently. *There's a difference, and the difference is the whole world.*

* * *

Wren had stopped listening to the exchange. Or tried to.

The walls were doing something to them. The frozen moments nearest where they stood had begun to shift — not thawing, but rearranging, the way a dream reshuffles when you look away and back. A burning fleet became a signing table became a mother running with a child in her arms, and in each scene, briefly, Wren saw faces they knew. Not knew — *had*. Faces from bottles. Faces they'd worn.

"There you are," Wren murmured to a soldier frozen mid-scream, a man whose smile Wren had borrowed for three weeks in Callowmere while hiding from Veil enforcers. "What did they do to you?"

The soldier didn't answer. The soldier was two hundred years dead, his last moment stretched thin as gold leaf and nailed to a wall. But the question sat in Wren's chest like swallowed ice, because it was their question too, had been since the marshes: *what did the Veil do to us, and how much of what we feel is ours?*

Their grief for Tam. Their brother. The courier who'd died pressing a map into a stranger's hands.

Wren had told Noor the truth of it on the frozen road — that their childhood memories had been sold as collateral, that they didn't know if the grief was real or purchased, installed like furniture in an empty house. Standing here, in the place where memory was the literal architecture, the doubt got worse instead of better. Because the walls remembered everything. Every moment anyone had ever sold to the Veil, every year borrowed and never returned, was somewhere in this ice, filed and catalogued, and somewhere in these corridors was a boy called Wren Ashgrave laughing at something, and Wren could go stand next to that moment and watch themselves be happy and still not know if the happiness had been real.

*It was real,* said a voice, and Wren spun, hand going to the knife they'd taken off a dead enforcer.

The ice column. The gray eyes. Looking at them now instead of Noor.

*Your brother carried the map because I asked him to. In a dream. He thought it was a dream. He volunteered anyway.* The voice softened into something Wren hadn't heard in years and had never once heard directed at them: tenderness without transaction. *He did it because he loved you. That part wasn't purchased. Nothing about you two was purchased. The Veil took your past, child, but it couldn't make a new one. What you feel for him grew in the gap it left. Things that grow in gaps grow crooked, but they grow true.*

Wren's throat closed. They turned away so neither Noor nor Corvane could see their face, and watched the frozen war instead, and let their shoulders shake exactly once.

* * *

"You want me to kill her," Noor said.

They had walked closer to the column without deciding to. Close enough now to see the fine detail of Vosk's face — the lines at her eyes, a scar through one eyebrow, the ordinary human architecture of a person who had done an enormous thing and was still doing it, breath by breath, century by century.

*I want you to understand the choice,* Vosk said. *Sable will frame it her way. Let me give you mine.*

She drew a breath — the first Noor had seen her take — and the whole chamber breathed with her, the walls flickering, the frozen soldiers shuddering in their ice.

*The winter is my dream. Every snowfall, every frozen harbor, every village that sold its summer — it's all held in place by my sleeping mind, the way a dam holds a river. I started the war. Did Sable tell you that? I built the Veil to fund it, traded in impossible things until impossible things were all anyone had left, and when I finally understood what I'd made, I couldn't unmake it. So I froze it. I froze the war and I froze the world to hold the war, and I have been dreaming this winter for two hundred years to keep eight million people's last moments from finishing themselves.*

Her eyes closed. Opened.

*If I die, the dream ends. The winter breaks. Spring comes — real spring, green things, ships moving, children seeing color. Everything you've wanted since you first understood what the world had lost.*

"And?" Noor said, because there was an *and*, there was always an *and*, they were a ledger-keeper, they knew that nothing in the Veil's books balanced without one.

*And the war wakes with me.* The voice didn't waver. *Every army in these walls completes its final charge. Every fire finishes burning. The fleets were mid-engagement, Noor. The cities were mid-slaughter. I didn't end the war. I paused it, and a paused thing does not stay paused out of gratitude.*

The chamber was very quiet. Even Corvane had stopped moving.

"So there's no clean answer," Noor said slowly. "Thaw the world and wake the slaughter, or keep the peace and keep the winter forever."

*Now you see why I chose sleep over decision.* Something bitter moved through the voice. *I told myself it was sacrifice. Some nights — some decades — I even believed it. But you should know what your grandmother's blood bought you the right to judge: I am not the solution. I am the procrastination.*

"You could have asked someone," Noor said. "Two hundred years. You could have—"

*Asked whom? The guild I corrupted? The kingdoms I bankrupted?* A flash of the old fire. *Or perhaps I should have waited for a descendant I deliberately blinded, whose memories I paid the Veil to erase, so that no one could follow the bloodline to my door and wake me by accident?*

The words landed like a blow. Noor took a step back.

"My memories. *You* took them."

*I asked Sable to arrange it. I'm sorry. It was the cruelest kindness available. If you had known what you were — known the storm in your blood, known whose blood — you would have come here years ago, full of purpose, and opened this door, and we would be having this conversation with the war already half-awake.* The gray eyes held them. *Instead you came as a clerk. Careful. Colorless. A person who counts things before touching them. I could work with that. The world might survive that.*

"You don't get to sculpt people," Noor said, and their voice shook, and they hated that it shook. "You don't get to erase someone and call it mercy."

*No,* Vosk agreed quietly. *I don't. Welcome to the family trade.*

* * *

Corvane had heard enough.

Two hundred years. Two hundred years of serving the Veil, of building it in her teacher's image, of telling herself the founder would return and set everything right — and here was the truth, delivered in a dead woman's dreaming voice: Ilyen Vosk had no plan. Had never had a plan. Had simply lain down in the middle of history and pulled the sky over herself like a blanket, leaving her apprentice to sweep up.

*I can hear you thinking, Sable,* the voice said wearily. *Whatever you've decided, decide slower.*

"No." Corvane stepped forward, and Noor saw her draw something from her coat — a slender blade, veined with the same translucent stuff as the walls, a thing that drank the candlelight. "I've decided slower than anyone alive. That was the problem."

*Sable—*

"You froze the war because you couldn't win it and couldn't bear to lose it. Fine. I can bear it." Her voice stayed gentle, which was worse than shouting; Noor had sat across ledgers from this woman and knew that the gentler Corvane's voice, the more arithmetic was happening underneath. "You built the Veil on a single principle: everything has a price, and the person willing to pay it owns the outcome. You taught me that. So I'm paying."

*Mistress—*

"*Don't* call me that." For one instant the courtesy cracked and Noor saw what was underneath: two centuries of hunger compressed to a point. "You slept while the world rotted. You erased a child rather than face your own blood. You are not the keeper of the winter. You are the reason for it. And I am done inheriting your cowardice."

She raised the blade.

Noor moved without deciding to — the same way they'd called lightning on the mountain, the body acting on knowledge the mind refused. They threw themselves between the blade and the ice, arms spread, and felt the point stop a finger's width from their sternum, cold bleeding through their coat.

"Wait," Noor said. "Just — wait. Listen to her. There's no clean answer, she said it herself, so killing her isn't one either—"

"The answer doesn't have to be clean," Corvane said softly. "It has to be *mine*."

Behind Noor, the ice sang louder. The walls began, very faintly, to warm.

And in the column, Ilyen Vosk looked at her apprentice over the shoulder of her last descendant, and said, in the voice that lived behind the eyes:

*Then you'd best be quick, Sable. Because the moment my heart stops, everything I'm holding lets go — and you have perhaps a minute to become whatever you intend to become before the war remembers what it was doing.*

Corvane smiled.

"I've waited two hundred years," she said. "A minute is nothing."

The blade came down.
