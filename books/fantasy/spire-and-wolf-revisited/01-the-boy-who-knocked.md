## 1. The Boy Who Knocked

The storm had been building since noon, and by the time the light failed it had teeth.

Yara Marchetti stood at the narrow window of the old watchtower with a clay mug of nettle tea going cold in her hands, watching sleet drive sideways across the shoulder of Thornfell. The wind came down off the high crags like something alive, something that had opinions about her. It found every gap in the mortar, every crack she hadn't yet stuffed with moss and tallow, and played them like a broken flute. Below, the valley was already white, the pines bent and groaning, the smugglers' road buried under a foot of ice-slick snow that no one in their right mind would cross tonight.

She liked it that way. The bad nights were the safe ones. No one climbed to the tower when the mountain was angry — not the tax collectors from Dunmarrow, not the woodcutters who thought the place cursed, and certainly not the men she'd once called family, who would sell her location for the price of a good horse and think themselves generous at the exchange.

Three years she'd lived here, in the stolen shell of a watchtower the king's garrison had abandoned a generation ago. Three years of learning which stones sweated before frost, which beams held weight and which only pretended, how to stretch a winter's stores into a spring's starvation rations. She'd learned the tower the way you learn a wound: intimately, daily, without ever quite making peace with it.

It was not the life she had imagined for herself. It was the life left over after everything else had been taken.

Her mistress had taught her that distinction, though neither of them had named it aloud. Maren Solenne — hedge-witch, herb-woman, keeper of a grimoire bound in grey leather soft as moth wing — had taken Yara in off the streets of Vell's Landing when she was eleven years old and all elbows and hunger and fury. For seven years she had been apprentice, daughter, servant, student; the categories blurred until they stopped mattering. Then the witch-hunters came, and Maren burned on a pyre outside her own cottage while Yara watched from the tree line with smoke in her throat and a satchel of stolen pages against her spine, and the categories collapsed into one word that she carried now like a stone in her chest: *gone*.

She set the cold tea down. The fire needed feeding. That was the whole of her evening's ambition — feed the fire, eat the barley porridge, read three pages of the grimoire by rushlight, sleep. A small life, measured out in small tasks, and she had made her peace with its smallness the way you make peace with a limp. You don't forgive it. You just stop expecting it to heal.

Then the knock came.

Not a knock, exactly. Not knuckles on oak. More a thudding, small and desperate and arrhythmic, the sound of a fist too tired to make a proper fist, striking the door twice, failing, striking again. In a storm like this, at this height, at this hour, no living thing should have been on her doorstep. Wolves didn't knock. Bears didn't bother. And men — men who meant harm kicked doors in; they didn't tap politely and wait.

Yara went still, mug halfway to the shelf, breath caught somewhere between ribs.

The knocking came again. Weaker now. The kind of weak that meant whoever was behind it had very little time left to be weak in.

She told herself all the sensible things. It could be a trick. It could be a trap — Brannoc's crew knew where she was, more or less, and Brannoc had a talent for turning kindness into leverage the way other men turned ore into coin. It could be a hunter's decoy, a child used as bait because even the king's men had learned there were things Yara Marchetti would open a door for.

She opened the door anyway.

Later, she would try to explain this to herself and fail. She would say it was the sound of the knocking, too small for a grown man, too human for an animal. She would say it was the storm, and some old superstition about turning away travelers in weather like this — a superstition Maren had held fiercely, *the mountain keeps score*, she used to say, *and it counts what you refuse*. But the truth, if Yara was honest in the privacy of her own skull, was simpler and worse: she opened the door because the last time someone knocked on a door for her, needing shelter, needing warmth, needing *her*, she had still been someone who believed doors were for opening. She wanted, just for a moment, to be that person again. Even if it killed her.

The cold hit first — a wall of it, sleet hissing past her into the room, snuffing the rushlight to a blue ghost of flame. And then, huddled against the doorframe in the lee the tower offered, half-buried already in the drifts piling against the threshold:

A boy.

Small. Eight years old, maybe nine, though it was hard to judge through the shivering and the sleet-matted hair. Barefoot. Both feet bare on the frozen stone, toes blackening at the tips with cold, and no shoes anywhere near him, no pack, no cloak, nothing but a ragged shirt that might once have been white and was now the color of dirty snow. His skin was pale in a way that wasn't quite human pallor — something faintly luminous under it, like moonstone held up to a candle, though the storm-light made liars of all colors. His hair was dark, soaked flat to his skull. His lips were blue. His eyes—

His eyes were what stopped her hand halfway to grabbing him by the collar and hauling him inside.

Grey. Not the grey of storm-cloud or slate or ash. A grey with silver moving in it, slowly, the way frost-flowers grow across a window in time-lapse — patterns forming, dissolving, reforming, as though the color itself were thinking. She had seen eyes like that exactly once before, in a page of Maren's grimoire, in a woodcut illustration of a creature the text called *Spire-born*, and she had assumed then, as any sensible person would, that the engraver had simply been showing off.

The boy looked up at her. He did not beg. He did not cry. He said something — a few words, spoken straight into the freezing dark between them, in a language Yara did not know and could not even guess at the shape of. It sounded like water running over stones. Like wind through a hollow reed. Like something older than the mountain itself, older than the idea of mountains.

Then he swayed, and his knees buckled, and he began to fall forward across her threshold.

She caught him. She had to lean hard to do it, both arms around his chest, and he weighed almost nothing — a bundle of sticks and wet cloth, a bird's skeleton wrapped in rags. She dragged him inside one-handed, hooked the door shut against the wind with her heel, and got him down onto the hearthstones where the residual heat of the day's fire still lingered in the rock.

For a long moment she simply crouched there beside him, hands shaking, heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out, and watched the boy breathe. Shallow. Fast. The breathing of a body spending its last reserves. His bare feet were the worst of it — the cold had gotten deep into them, past feeling, and she knew enough field-craft to know that the line between *numb* and *lost* was thinner than most people believed and crossed faster than anyone wanted to admit.

She worked. Warm water from the kettle, not hot — never hot, not for frozen flesh, Maren had drilled that into her until it lived in her reflexes deeper than her own name. Wool blankets from the chest. Her own spare shift, dry, wrapped around him over the blankets because wool alone wasn't enough and pride was a luxury for people who weren't dying. She rubbed his feet, working warmth back into them centimeter by centimeter, watching the black tip of the smallest toe fade toward pink with a relief so sharp it felt like fear in reverse.

The boy did not wake. He drifted somewhere between sleep and something deeper than sleep, eyes closed, breath gradually slowing as his body remembered how to spend less of itself on staying alive. He did not stir when she moved him. He did not flinch when her fingers probed the bruises along his ribs — old bruises, yellow-green, faded, the marks of someone who had been hurt days ago and kept walking anyway.

Days. In this cold. Barefoot.

Yara sat back on her heels and looked at him, really looked, and felt something she had not permitted herself to feel in three years.

Worry. Unbudgeted, unpractical, unguarded worry about another living thing.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You don't know what he is. You don't know who sent him. You don't know what walking through your door costs.*

But her hands were already heating more water, and her stores were already being mentally inventoried for something a starving child could keep down, and the argument was over before it started, and she hated herself a little for how little she minded losing.

* * *

He woke near midnight, when the storm had settled into its long grinding monotony and the tower creaked around them like an old ship.

Yara was mending a blanket by rushlight, mostly to have something to do with her hands, when his eyes opened — those impossible grey-silver eyes, fixed on her across the small room with none of the confusion waking usually brings. No panic. No tears. Just a long, level look, as though he were checking something against a list only he could see.

Then he spoke again, and this time she was close enough to hear it properly, and hearing it properly changed nothing and everything.

The words had structure. That was the first thing she noticed — they weren't the random vocalizations of a fevered child or a mind gone strange. They rose and fell in deliberate cadence, syllables grouped and stressed, a grammar moving underneath whether or not she could see it. The sounds themselves were unlike anything in the trade-tongue, unlike the lowland dialects, unlike the clipped northern speech of the mountain villages. Soft consonants. Long vowels that seemed to be shaped as much by breath as by tongue. When he finished speaking, the silence afterward felt different — fuller, somehow, as though the words were still in the room, decaying slowly like the ring of a struck bell.

She tried the trade-tongue on him. *What is your name? Where are your people?* Nothing. She tried the lowland dialect, the northern speech, the handful of smuggler-sign phrases she knew. Nothing — except that on the third attempt, something flickered in his expression. Not understanding. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing a familiar song played badly on a broken instrument.

He answered her with more of the old language, patient, unhurried, as though the fault were obviously hers and he was willing to wait for her to correct it.

*I don't understand you,* she said, and heard how useless the words were the moment she said them, and laughed — a short, cracked, exhausted laugh that startled her more than the boy did.

He watched her laugh. Then, deliberately, he reached out one thin hand and touched the grimoire where it lay on the table, and looked at her with a question in his face so plain it needed no shared language at all.

*No.* She pulled the book away, fast, an instinct older than thought. It was the one thing she owned that mattered. Every page of it had cost blood — Maren's blood, eventually all of it. Strangers didn't touch the grimoire. Strangers didn't touch anything of hers; that was the entire architecture of her survival.

But the boy didn't reach for it again. He drew his hand back, folded it in his lap, and sat there in the blankets looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse — not hurt, not offended. Something quieter. Something almost like recognition, aimed not at her but at the book itself, at the grey leather cover with its worn sigil stamped blind into the hide.

And then, as she sat glaring at him across the firelight, daring him to try something, the boy began to hum.

It took four notes.

Four notes, and Yara Marchetti forgot how to breathe.

She knew the melody. Not vaguely, not distantly — she knew it the way you know the texture of your own childhood, the way your body knows the shape of a stair in the dark. It was a lullaby. A slow, descending line, six phrases, the last one always sung softer than the rest, trailing off like something sinking gently beneath still water. Maren had sung it. On bad nights, when the coughing was bad and the pain in her joints was bad and the world outside the cottage walls was worst of all, she had sung it to herself in the kitchen while Yara pretended to sleep in the loft, listening through the floorboards, memorizing it without meaning to, the way children memorize the sounds of the people keeping them alive.

Maren had never told her what it was. Where she'd learned it. Why a hedge-witch in a fishing town on the edge of nowhere carried a lullaby that sounded like nothing else in the world — older than the trade-tongue, older than the roads, older, Yara realized with a chill spreading up her spine, than anything else in her mistress's considerable store of old things.

Maren had died with it unexplained, like nearly everything else.

And now a barefoot child who spoke a language no map recorded was sitting on her hearthstones humming it back to her, note-perfect, in the middle of a sleet storm at the top of a mountain everyone said was empty.

*Where did you learn that song?*

She asked it aloud, uselessly, in the trade-tongue, her voice coming out rougher than she intended. The boy stopped humming. He looked at her — through her, almost, at something standing behind her eyes that only he could see — and he said a single word in the old language. One word, soft, unhurried, weighted with a gentleness that had no business surviving whatever had put those bruises on his ribs.

She didn't know what it meant. She would find out later, much later, and when she did she would understand why he had chosen that word above all others, and it would undo her completely.

But that night she only heard the sound of it, and the sound was enough to break something loose in her chest that three years of careful living had kept mortared shut. Grief, maybe. Or the memory of grief — the shape grief leaves, the way a hand leaves its shape in wax. She turned away from him quickly, busied herself with the fire, with the kettle, with anything, because her eyes were doing something treacherous and she would rather have bitten through her own tongue than let a stranger — especially this stranger — watch Maren Solenne's lullaby walk out of her past and sit down at her fire.

The boy, for his part, seemed to understand perfectly. He lay back down among the blankets, curled small, and within moments he was asleep again — truly asleep this time, the deep, trusting, boneless sleep of the young and the utterly spent, one bare foot still poking out from the wool despite everything she'd done to prevent it.

Yara sat up the rest of the night.

She told herself it was caution. It was partly caution — she kept the knife loose in its sheath and the grimoire under her hand and one ear cocked for the storm hiding any sound that wasn't storm. But mostly she sat because she couldn't stop looking at him. At the faint luminescence under his skin, visible now in the steady light, stronger along his forearms and throat, like veins of moonlight quarried into flesh. At the faded bruises. At the total absence of calluses on his bare feet — feet that had walked for days over frozen ground without shoes and without the hardened skin that should have bought him, and hadn't, as though the mountain itself had declined to roughen him. As though the stone had recognized something and stepped softly.

Outside, the sleet ticked and hissed against the shutters. The wind moaned through the beacon-platform overhead, a sound the old garrison soldiers must have slept beneath for a hundred years, waiting for fires that never came.

Somewhere below the cloud deck, miles down the valley, the world went on — the free cities with their locked gates and their ledgers, the king in his capital counting armies, the smugglers on their frozen road counting profits. None of it knew that in a stolen watchtower on the shoulder of Thornfell, a dead woman's lullaby had come back from extinction wearing a child's body, and the last person alive who might recognize what that meant was sitting awake beside it with a knife in her lap and her heart in ruins.

Toward dawn, the storm began to lose its grip. The wind dropped to a mutter. Grey light seeped through the arrow-slits, and in that light Yara finally admitted what she had been refusing to admit all night: the boy's eyes, the song, the language, the skin like veined moonstone — it all pointed one direction, and the direction was a page in Maren's grimoire she had read a hundred times and never once believed.

*Of the Spire-born it is written: their blood remembers what the realm forgets.*

She looked at the sleeping boy. She looked at the grimoire. Outside, far down in the valley, thin and almost lost in the morning haze, a smudge of smoke rose from the direction of the lowland villages — too steady, too disciplined, too deliberate to be a chimney.

Witch-hunter smoke.

Yara reached for the grimoire and opened it to the page she had never believed.
