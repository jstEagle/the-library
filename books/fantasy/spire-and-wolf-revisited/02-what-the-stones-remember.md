## 2. What the Stones Remember

The grimoire did not want to be opened to that page.

Yara had learned its moods over six years of tending it — the way the spine resisted certain chapters like a horse balking at a bridge, the way the vellum went cool and unwilling under her thumb when the knowledge between the leaves was of the kind that got people burned. Mistress Odile had called the book *contrary* and kissed its cover anyway, every night, the way one kisses a difficult parent. Yara had inherited both the book and the habit without ever quite understanding either.

Now she pinned the pages flat with two river stones from the windowsill and made herself read.

*Of the Spire-born it is written: their blood remembers what the realm forgets.*

The script was Odile's — no, older than Odile's, a hand copied down through hands, each scribe leaving small tremors in the letters like fingerprints in wax. The ink had gone brown as old blood. Around the central passage, marginalia crawled up the edges in three different scripts: translations, corrections, warnings. One note, in Odile's own sharp slant, simply read *verify before believing*.

Yara looked at that note for a long time.

Behind her, the boy slept in her blanket nest by the hearth, breathing in the slow tidal rhythm of the truly exhausted. Sleet ticked against the shutters. His feet, she had washed them last night with snowmelt and felt how cracked and blue they were — were wrapped now in strips of her spare shirt. He had not woken when she touched them. Children who had run as long as he had ran past exhaustion into some country beyond it, where the body simply did what it was told and asked no questions.

She turned back to the page.

*The covenant was sworn in blood freely given,* the old hand wrote, *and so it can only be known by blood freely given. No scrying, no divination, no cunning of art will reveal the heir. Only the stones themselves may know him.*

Below that, a diagram: three circles arranged in a triangle, connected by lines, and at each point a symbol Yara recognized from waystones she'd seen ruined along the high passes — the spiral-and-tower mark that lowland folk called *the devil's knot* and spat over their shoulders at. She had seen it once, weathered nearly smooth, on the broken pillar at Ashfall Pass, half-swallowed by lichen. She had been fourteen. Brannoc's crew had camped there overnight, and Brannoc himself had drunk too much plum brandy and told a story about the Spire-folk that ended with all of them dead and deservedly so, and everyone had laughed, and Yara had laughed too because she was fourteen and wanted to belong to something.

She thought about that laughter now and felt it curdle.

*Verify before believing.* Fine. She would verify.

* * *

The rite, such as it was, took less preparation than making breakfast.

That was the first thing that frightened her — how little it required. A bowl of snowmelt. Three drops of blood, freely given, hers or the child's, though the text was specific that the blood of the tested must be the blood of the tested, and no substitution would serve. A waystone fragment, or failing that, any stone that had stood within sight of one. She had that. She had collected it years ago from the cairn below the watchtower without knowing why, the way she collected other useless beautiful things, and it sat in her herb box among the dried yarrow with its spiral mark worn but legible.

The second thing that frightened her was what the text promised the rite would show. Not merely identity. Lineage. And lineage, in the Spire-blood, meant something specific, something the grimoire circled back to again and again in language that grew more urgent with each copyist:

*For the covenant is not a wall but a wound, bound by living flesh. While an heir of the Spire lives and remembers, the mountains keep their own counsel and the armies of the lowlands cannot pass. But if the line should thin — if the remembering should fail — then the wound opens, and what was shut out is shut out no longer.*

Dated marginalia followed. Odile's handwriting again, cramped, hurried: *Third check of the passes this year. Still quiet. Still holding. How much longer?*

And beneath that, in fresher ink, almost a whisper: *They are dying out. I am nearly sure of it. Gods forgive us all if I am right.*

Yara set the book down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Outside, the smoke in the valley had thickened.

* * *

She woke him gently, which surprised her. Her first instinct had been to wake him the way Brannoc woke new deckhands — a boot to the sole and a question you already knew the answer to. But something about the boy's sleep argued against violence. He lay curled with his fists near his chin, and even unconscious he listened; twice in the night the wind had shifted the loose shutter and both times his eyes had snapped open, taken her measure, and closed again without a sound. That was not the sleep of a child. That was the sleep of prey.

"Hey," she said. "I need your hand. Just your hand. Nothing else."

He watched her from under the blanket, and she watched him back, and the silence stretched until she understood he was deciding something — weighing her, the room, the exits, the odds. Then he uncurled and held out his hand, palm up, an offering or a dare.

His palm was clean; she had seen to that in the night. She took the lancet she used for bleeding fevers and pricked the pad of his thumb, quick as a snakebite, murmuring an apology he wouldn't understand. He didn't flinch. He watched the bead of red well up with an expression she couldn't read — recognition, maybe. Recognition was the wrong word. *Homecoming*, perhaps. As if his own blood were a language he hadn't heard spoken in a long while.

Three drops into the snowmelt. The stone beside the bowl. She spoke the words of the rite as written, stumbling over the Old Tongue, feeling foolish, feeling the syllables move through her mouth like fish through fingers — and nothing happened, and she was about to laugh at herself with relief when the boy said a single word.

Softly. Correcting her.

The last syllable of the invocation, the one she'd flattened, and he gave it its true shape — a falling tone, a sound like a door closing — and the water moved.

Not violently. It shivered, the way a puddle shivers when a hoof strikes ground far away, and then the surface drew tight and still as glass, and in it Yara saw —

— a tower. White stone veined with silver, rising out of cloud, impossibly tall, its crown lost in light rather than sky. Figures on its balconies, slender and dark-eyed, robed in colors she had no names for. And threaded through everything, visible the way current is visible in water, a great slow turning pattern — the spiral-and-tower mark, alive, revolving, binding tower to mountain to valley to the far white teeth of the Thornfells, a web of light laid over the whole range like a hand laid over a wound.

Then the vision narrowed. Drew inward. Found a woman of the Spire, young, laughing, pressing a bundle into the arms of a human smuggler with a scarred face and desperate eyes — and the bundle was a child, and the woman's mouth formed words the water would not carry, and behind her the tower was burning, the silver veins running black, the great spiral stuttering like a heart losing its rhythm —

The boy made a sound. Small. The water shattered into droplets and lay flat and ordinary, reflecting the rafters.

Yara realized she was shaking. She realized, distantly, that she was crying, which annoyed her more than frightened her, because she had not cried since Odile died and had built quite a fortress around that fact.

The boy was looking at the empty bowl. Then at her. His lips moved around a word — his name, she understood suddenly, he was telling her his name, or trying to, offering the only thing he had.

"*Vael,*" he said.

The grimoire, open on the table, rustled its pages as though a draft had passed — though every shutter was closed. When Yara dared look down, the book had turned itself, or seemed to have, to a genealogy she had skimmed past a hundred times without reading: the line of the Spire's wardens, name after name in the Old Tongue, each with its dates, each ending in the same grim shorthand the copyists used for the dead. And at the bottom, the last entry, the ink barely dry-looking after all these years, added in a hand she knew better than her own:

*Vael. Last of the line. Whereabouts unknown. May the mountains hide him better than we could.*

Odile had known. Odile had written his name in her book and gone looking for him, or waiting for him, or both — and had died on the winter road with the answer sitting somewhere out there in the snow, barefoot, alone.

"Oh," Yara said, to nobody, to the rafters. "Oh, you impossible old woman."

* * *

She fed him barley porridge with honey because she didn't know what else to do with her hands. He ate the way he did everything — watchfully, precisely, wasting nothing — and between spoonfuls he studied the grimoire with an intensity that made her uneasy, until she understood: he was reading it. Not guessing at pictures. Reading. His eyes tracked left to right through passages in the Old Tongue that had taken Yara months to parse, and occasionally his brow furrowed at some error or archaism, and once he reached out and tapped a word twice, frowning, as though the book had misspoken.

"You can read this," she said, uselessly, in Trade Tongue. "You're — what, seven? Eight?"

He tilted his head at her voice the way a bird does, catching the shape of it if not the sense. Then he pointed at himself. "*Vael.*" Pointed at the book. Said a longer phrase, gentle and explanatory, the tone of a patient adult, and she caught perhaps one word in ten — but one of them was *Spire*, and another, repeated twice with emphasis, was a word she did know, because it appeared in the covenant passages more than any other.

*Covenant*. Or close enough. The Old Tongue word was older, rounder, meant something more like *the promise that binds the wound*.

"Yes," she said. "I know. I'm starting to know."

She spread the diagram of the three circles on the table and weighted its corners. Three waystones. Ashfall Pass to the west — she'd seen it broken with her own eyes. The Hollow Root, somewhere under the eastern forests, location marked only by a riddle in verse she'd never solved. And the third, the Weeping Crown, drawn at the top of the map as a summit shrine above the high glaciers, past anything Brannoc's routes had ever touched.

Broken. All three broken — the text was clear on that, and clearer still on the consequence. The covenant was not a thing that failed all at once. It frayed. It thinned like ice in spring, holding here, giving way there, letting through first seepage and then cracks and then, one bright cold morning, the whole rotten sheet going at once.

And the smoke in the valley rose steady and disciplined and deliberate.

She went to the window while Vael ate. The haze had lifted with the morning and the view south was cruelly clear: the grey ribbon of the King's Road, the lowland villages huddled along it, and above the nearest of them — Hallowmere, or what had been Hallowmere — a column of smoke that did not behave like cooking fires. Too straight. Too black. Burning wet things, green things, things that smoked because someone wanted the smoke. Witch-hunters signaled their arrivals that way. *We are here*, the smoke said. *Bring us names.*

Serane's people. Everyone whispered about Serane's people the way one whispers about weather coming — Inquisitor Serane of the King's Writ, who interviewed villages and left them shorter. Yara had never seen her. She had only seen the results, once, passing through a market town a season after the Writ had visited: the empty stalls, the shuttered chapel, the way people answered questions a half-beat too quickly and watched the roads while they talked.

They were hunting covenant-blood. That was the other thing the grimoire said, in Odile's most recent entries, the ones written in the last two years of her life, the handwriting deteriorating as her health did. *Aldric's Writ has agents in every city now. They call it purging heresy. They are looking for Spire descendants, however diluted. They have lists. They have methods. If they find the true heir before the stones are mended—*

The entry stopped there. The next page was a supply list. The page after that was blank. Some entries never got finished; that was the truth of keeping a dead woman's book.

Yara counted what she knew. A boy whose blood sang to the stones. Three broken waystones. A ritual she half-understood that demanded — she went back and checked, hoping she'd misread, knowing she hadn't — *a memory freely given for each stone, of the giver's choosing, surrendered wholly and forever*. A witch-hunter inquisitor methodically combing the valleys. And winter coming down the passes like a debt collector.

Against all of it: herself. An apprentice runaway with a stolen watchtower, a dead mistress's book, and a bow she was fair-to-middling with.

Vael tugged her sleeve. She looked down. He was pointing at the map, at the western circle — Ashfall Pass — and then at the window, toward the pass itself, and then he looked up at her with those dark Spire eyes and said something short and certain in the Old Tongue, and though she couldn't translate it, she didn't need to. Some sentences carry their meaning in their shoulders.

*West. Now. Before they come.*

"I know," she said. "I know."

But she stood at the window a moment longer, watching the black column rise off Hallowmere, and let herself feel it — really feel it, without the armor of practicality — what it was to be small, and hunted, and responsible for the continued existence of everything.

Then she packed the grimoire, strung her bow, and started counting arrows.

* * *

They left the watchtower an hour past midday, moving north first to false the trail, then cutting west through the pine shadow toward Ashfall Pass. Vael walked beside her without being told to, matching her pace, and if his barefoot-wrapped stride faltered on the scree he never once complained, and Yara found herself revising upward every estimate she'd made of him.

Halfway up the first ridge she paused, breath steaming, and looked back. The watchtower sat on its crag, small and blind. Beyond it the valley lay in afternoon gold, peaceful as a painting — except for the smoke, which had multiplied. Two columns now. Three. Moving, she realized with a lurch. Not village fires at all. Signal smokes, relaying along the road, passing something east to west.

Passing it toward the passes.

Toward them.

Vael touched her arm. He pointed at the smoke, then at his own chest, then made a gesture she'd seen hunters' children make — two fingers walking, then stopping, then folding flat.

*They know something is here. They are coming to end it.*

"Yeah," Yara said, and pulled her hood up against the wind. "Then we'd better be somewhere else when they arrive."

They climbed. Behind them, faint on the wind, came a sound she pretended not to hear: horns, far down in the valley, calling to one another in the disciplined, deliberate tongue of the King's Writ.
