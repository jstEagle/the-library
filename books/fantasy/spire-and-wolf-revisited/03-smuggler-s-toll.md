## 3. Smuggler's Toll

The world was too loud.

Vael had known loudness before—knew it as the hum of the Spire's deep roots, a sound like honey poured over stone, like ten thousand voices singing one note so low it lived in the marrow rather than the ear. But this was different. This was noise with teeth in it.

*Saltmarsh*, the woman said. The word meant nothing and everything. It came apart in his mouth when he tried to taste it: *salt*, which he knew because the wind off the low country carried it now, stinging the cracks in his lips; *marsh*, which his blood translated as *water that cannot decide what it wants to be*. He liked that. Water that could not decide. He understood water that could not decide.

He rode pressed against Yara's back on a borrowed mule, and her spine was a wall of tension he could read the way his people once read the grain of mountain-stone. Her heartbeat spoke to him through layers of wool and leather—a quick, guarded drum. She had not slept. He could smell it on her skin, something sour and bright beneath the woodsmoke smell, the smell of a body keeping watch against itself.

*Yara.* Her name was the first human word he had ever loved. It sat strangely in the Old Tongue when he turned it over in his mind—no root for it, no echo of stone or star or root-system. A name that belonged to no older word. A name that had made itself, the way humans made everything: badly, bravely, out of nothing at all.

Below them, the saltmarsh opened like a grey hand.

* * *

There were birds here that screamed like the dying.

Vael flinched at each cry until Yara's hand found his wrist—not gripping, just resting, the way you rest your hand on a door that might blow open. *Waders,* she said. *Just birds.* But the word she used was not the word the marsh-folk used; he could hear the difference, hear how her mouth shaped the sounds of somewhere else, somewhere with hard winters and harder vowels. Thornfell. The mountains. His mountains, though he did not know yet why they were his, only that when she spoke of them something behind his ribs leaned toward the sound like a plant toward light.

Language was a strange country. He was learning to walk in it.

Some words arrived whole, wearing their meanings like clothes: *fire*, *food*, *hide*, *run*. Others came broken and had to be mended by context. When Yara muttered *toll* under her breath as the mule picked its way down a causeway of packed black mud, Vael understood only that it was a thing taken from you, a tooth pulled from the mouth of a journey. Later he would learn it meant payment. Later still he would learn that some payments are never finished being paid.

The causeway ended at a village built on stilts above the waterline, houses like herons standing on one leg. And there—there was the first thing in the human world that made him feel less afraid rather than more.

Smoke.

Not witch-hunter smoke, which Yara had taught him to dread, smoke that rose straight and hungry and organized. This was cooking-smoke, lazy and blue, smelling of fish and peat and something sweet. Humans gathered around it. Humans always gathered around fire; even in the Spire, in the long ago that lived in his blood like sediment lives in river-water, there had been fires. Fire was the oldest covenant. You give it wood; it gives you back light. Everything after that was argument.

A man stood at the end of the dock, waiting for them.

Vael felt him before he saw him clearly—the way you feel a change in weather through an old wound. The man was big in the way that driftwood is big: weathered, hollowed, lighter than he looked. His beard held things in it. Actual things. A fishhook, a twist of wire, what might have been a bird's bone. His eyes were the color of old ice and they moved across Vael the way a merchant's hands move across cloth, testing the weave, judging the weight, calculating what the bolt would fetch in a better market than this one.

*Brannoc,* Yara said, and her heartbeat changed its rhythm. Slower. Careful. The rhythm of someone walking on a frozen lake.

*Little Yara Marchetti,* the man said, and his voice was warm the way a trap is warm—you do not notice the warmth until your leg is in it. *Thought you were dead. Everyone thought you were dead. That was the point of you leaving, wasn't it?*

*I need passage north,* Yara said. *Up the estuary. Quiet.*

*Everything I do is quiet,* Brannoc said. *That's what people pay me for.* And then his eyes came back to Vael and stayed there, and stayed there, and Vael understood, in the way he understood things then—not in words but in pressures, in temperatures—that he was the cargo.

* * *

They sat in a room that smelled of tar and old fish and waited while men argued about them in the next room.

Vael listened with the part of himself that was not words. He heard Yara's voice go flat and hard, a blade laid on a table. He heard Brannoc's voice roll and dip, generous-sounding, each offer wrapped in laughter like poison wrapped in honeycomb. He heard other voices too—three, four—and mapped them without meaning to: a thin nervous one who clicked his teeth; a woman who never spoke but whose boots shifted weight from foot to foot, restless, unhappy; a fat man who laughed at all the wrong moments because laughing was cheaper than thinking.

And underneath the voices, the room itself spoke. That was the strangest thing about the human world, Vael was learning: it was full of speech that humans could not hear. Walls remembered hands. Floors remembered feet. The tarred beams overhead held the ghost-echo of a hundred years of songs sung badly by drunk men, and if he let his attention soften, he could almost pick the melodies out, half-heard lullabies and work-chants layered like shells in a midden.

One of the songs was wrong, though. One of the songs, buried deepest, was not human at all.

It was four notes, rising, falling, rising again—the opening phrase of a Spire lament, worn down by time into something the singers no longer understood, a prayer become a drinking song become nothing at all. His people's music, fossilized in the bones of a smuggler's den. He put his palm flat against the floorboards, very quietly, and the wood gave the song back to him like a hand giving back a coin it has held too long.

*What are you doing?* Yara whispered.

*Listening,* he said. It was one of his best words. *The house is singing.*

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide whether to be frightened. He was learning to watch humans decide things. It happened in the face first, then the hands. Fear entered through the eyes and left through the fingers, or didn't.

*Houses don't sing,* she said finally, but softly, and she did not move her chair away from his, and he counted that as a kind of promise.

* * *

Night came down on the marsh like a lid.

Vael did not sleep well in the human world. Sleep, for him, was not darkness; it was descent. He went down through layers of memory that were not his own—or were they? He could no longer always tell. He dreamed of a tower white as a held breath, of stairs that sang underfoot, of a gate closing and closing and closing, and every time it closed he woke with salt on his face that was not from the marsh air.

In the small hours, the house-talk woke him again. Not the dead songs this time. Living voices, low, in the room below.

He should have woken Yara. He knew that even then, some animal part of him knowing it the way a deer knows a snapped twig. But the boy he was—the boy who had walked out of the high snows barefoot, who had knocked on a stranger's door because a lullaby had led him there—wanted, just once, to understand the humans on his own. Without translation. To stand inside their language instead of outside its windows.

So he lay on the pallet and listened through the floor.

*...the witch-hunters pay in crown silver,* Brannoc was saying. Crown silver. Two words Vael knew separately and together: metal, and the man who wanted him dead. *And they pay for information, girl, same as anyone. Same as everyone. You think I run this crew on gratitude?*

*You run it on other people's trust,* Yara said. Her voice was very quiet. Vael had learned that her quietest voice was her most dangerous one. *I'm asking you to remember who pulled you out of the Kettersby canal with two knives in your back.*

*I remember everything,* Brannoc said, and for one moment—one only—the warmth fell out of his voice entirely, and what was underneath was old and tired and cold as the bottom of the estuary. *That's my curse, little Yara. I remember everything, and it's never once stopped me doing what needed doing.*

Silence. Then the click of the nervous man's teeth. Then the restless boots, stopping.

*He's not cargo, Bran,* the woman said. First words Vael had ever heard from her. Her voice was rough as rope. *Look at him. Whatever he is, he's a child.*

*Everything's cargo,* Brannoc said. *The only question is what it costs to carry.* A pause, long enough for the tide to turn. *We sail on the morning ebb. Both of them. North, like she wants. And we'll see what the road gives us.*

Vael lay in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird, and he did not understand all the words, but he understood the shape of them. Shapes were easy. Shapes were the oldest language. And the shape of what Brannoc had said was the shape of a hand opening—palm up, friendly, empty—

—and then closing.

* * *

Dawn on the marsh was the color of a bruise healing.

They loaded onto a flat-bottomed barge that smelled of eel-nets and tar, and Vael sat in the bow where the mist was thickest, because mist was the closest thing this soft country had to his snows. Yara sat beside him, sharpening a knife she did not look at, watching Brannoc at the tiller the way a hawk watches a field.

*Boy,* Brannoc called, cheerful as a market bell. *Come here. Let's have a look at you.*

Yara's knife stopped moving.

Vael went. Not because he trusted the man—he did not, had not since the closing-hand shape of the night-talk—but because he was learning another lesson of the human world, one that sat cold in his stomach: sometimes you walk toward the wolf so it does not learn that you know it is a wolf.

Up close, Brannoc smelled of pipe-smoke and brine and, faintly, of the wrongness Vael had tasted in the house-song—old contact with things Spire-made, or Spire-broken. There was a ring on his thumb, green copper, worked in a pattern Vael's blood recognized and recoiled from: a waystone sigil, defaced, its lines crossed out like a face scratched from a portrait.

*Where did you get that ring?* Vael asked.

The marsh went quiet around them. Even the screaming birds seemed to lean in.

Brannoc looked down at his thumb as though noticing it for the first time in years. Something moved behind the old-ice eyes—quick, submerged, gone. *Took it off a corpse in the high passes, twenty years back,* he said. *Dead man in the snow, dressed finer than any lord. Why? You know him?*

*No,* Vael said, which was true, and also the most terrible lie he had ever told, because some part of him, some sediment-deep part, was almost sure that he did.

Brannoc smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. *Strange child,* he said, ruffling Vael's hair with a hand heavy as a mooring line. *Strange, quiet, valuable child.* And the last word dropped between them like a coin into deep water, and Vael watched it fall, and fall, and never hit bottom.

Behind him he heard Yara rise, boards creaking, her heartbeat loud enough now that he wondered the whole marsh couldn't hear it.

Ahead, far up the grey estuary where the water narrowed toward the hills, a thread of smoke stood against the morning sky. Straight. Hungry. Organized.

Brannoc saw it too. Vael watched him see it. Watched the old-ice eyes flick from the smoke, to the boy, to the woman, and back to the smoke—and watched, with a sinking that felt like the barge itself settling lower in the water, as the merchant's arithmetic completed itself behind them, silent as falling snow.

*Well now,* Brannoc said softly, to no one, to everyone, turning the tiller a degree or two off true. *Looks like the road's already making us offers.*

The barge slid north into the mist, carrying them toward the smoke, and Vael pressed his palm flat against the cold deck and asked the wood, in the Old Tongue, what it remembered of betrayal.

The wood answered: *everything.*
