## 4. The Usurper's Writ

The village of Marrow's Bend had one well, two streets, and forty-one souls, and Inquisitor Serane catalogued all of it before her boots touched the mud.

She counted from the saddle, the way she counted everything: roofs thatched and rotting, a shrine to the Nine with its paint peeling like sunburnt skin, a boy of perhaps ten splitting kindling who dropped his axe when he saw the black-and-silver of the King's writ on her banner. Forty-one souls. One of them, by the ledger in her saddlebag, was lying.

"Goodwife Hessa," she said, dismounting without waiting for help. Her boots sank an inch into the muck, and she noted that too—Marrow's Bend in thaw, mud to the ankle, which meant no rider could approach unseen, which meant whoever had passed through here recently had been seen by everyone. "You received my letter."

The goodwife was a broad woman with flour to her elbows and fear already sweating through her apron. She'd received the letter; Serane could see it in the way her eyes went first not to Serane's face but to the wax seal, still clutched in one red fist as though she'd been holding it since dawn. People held the seal like that. As if the paper might bite.

"We did, lady. We've nothing here for the likes of—"

"You have a girl." Serane drew off one glove, finger by finger. The cold bit at her knuckles and she let it, because cold kept the mind clean. "Sixteen, perhaps seventeen years. Dark-haired. Trained hands—not a farm girl's hands, a scholar's or a hedge-witch's. She would have come down out of the high passes sometime in the last season, buying salt, buying iron, asking questions about the old stones." She paused. "Or she would not have come at all, and you will tell me so, and I will believe you, and we will all be warm tonight."

Goodwife Hessa's mouth opened. Closed. The kindling-boy had stopped chopping entirely.

"Lady," said a man's voice, and the smith came out of his forge wiping his hands, a big man doing his best to look bigger. "We're loyal folk here. King's peace, king's taxes, and no trouble come through this bend in twenty years."

"Twenty-three," Serane said mildly. "Your bridge toll records show a crossing in the autumn before last. A woman, alone, with a mule and too many books. She paid in silver minted in Callowmere, which is curious, because Callowmere hasn't minted coin since the Spire fell." She watched the smith's face do what faces did—the small collapse around the eyes, the flick toward his wife. "She stayed three days. She bought lamp oil. Lamp oil, master smith, in a village where every soul has burned tallow since the war. Who burns oil in the mountains? Someone reading old script by lamplight. Someone copying maps."

"We never saw her again," the smith said. "She went up the pass and—"

"And someone else came after her." Serane put the glove back on, slowly, and now she let the cold into her voice as well. "Someone young. And someone came asking about *her*, and this village—which remembers everything, because villages forget nothing—decided to remember nothing at all. That is a choice, goodman. Choices have prices under the King's writ."

The forge-fire crackled. Somewhere a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

"She bought bread," Goodwife Hessa said suddenly, the words tumbling out like something vomited rather than spoken. "Bread and dried apples and a needle. She had a cut on her arm she wouldn't let me look at. She asked—" the goodwife swallowed— "she asked if anyone still sang the mountain songs. The old ones. I told her nobody remembered them proper. She looked so sad, lady. Like I'd told her the whole world was dead."

Serane nodded once. "Which way?"

"Up past Greywater Ford. Toward Thornfell." The goodwife's eyes were wet now, furious with herself for being wet. "She was just a girl. Whatever she's done—"

"What she has done," Serane said, gathering her reins, "is exist. That is the whole of her crime, and the whole of yours, and the whole of mine. Remember that when you pray tonight."

She rode out of Marrow's Bend without looking back, though she heard the goodwife begin to cry, and she filed the sound away with all the others.

* * *

Inquisitors did not sleep in beds, as a rule. Beds softened the dreams, and Serane needed hers sharp.

That night, in the waystation above the ford, she sat wrapped in her cloak before a fire she permitted herself only because her hands were stiffening, and she opened the small lacquered box she carried against her ribs, and she looked—as she looked every night—at the shard inside.

It was no larger than a knife-blade. It caught the firelight wrong: light went *into* it and did not come back out, or came back changed, dimmed, the way a voice sounds through a wall. A piece of the Spire. One of seven known fragments in the world, and the only one that answered to Aldric's hand.

*You are tired,* the shard said, in the voice she had given it, which was her own voice, younger. *Put me away.*

"I am working," Serane said.

*You are remembering.* The shard's glow pulsed, slow as a sleeping pulse. *Remembering is not working.*

She put it away.

But the memory came anyway, the way it always did when the shard spoke, because the shard was made of memory—it was crystallized covenant, the old magic of binding and oath—and it pulled things up out of her like a hook pulling weed from a pond.

She was nineteen again, standing in the great hall of Callowmere, the year of the Breaking. She had been a novitiate then, grey-robed, holding candles for the older priests, and she had watched from behind a pillar as Prince Aldric—young then too, golden, laughing, beloved by everyone who didn't know him—had knelt before the Spire-folk envoy and sworn the renewal oaths with a smile that never reached his eyes.

She hadn't understood, then, what she was watching. Nobody had. The Spire-folk had come down from their mountain once a generation to renew the covenant: blood freely given, stone freely raised, and in exchange the free cities kept their walls unbreached and their children unbled by the things that lived in the deep places. It was old law, older than the Nine, older than names. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows the sky is blue—without ever wondering why.

Aldric had wondered why.

She remembered the night he summoned her—her, a novitiate of no family and less fortune, precisely because she was nobody, because a nobody could be taught and a somebody would have to be bribed. He had shown her the shard, then whole and singing, torn from the Spire's root by miners who thought they'd found ore.

"Do you know what this is?" he'd asked.

"A relic, majesty."

"It's a leash," Aldric had said, turning it so the light drowned in it. "The whole mountain is a leash, and we are dogs on it, and they call it protection. They take our grain, our gold, our sons for their rituals—and in return they keep us safe from monsters of their own describing. Have you ever seen one of those monsters, Serane? Have you ever seen anything come out of the deep places?"

"No, majesty."

"*No one has.*" He'd smiled at her then, and it had been the most frightening thing she had ever seen, because it was warm. "Because there is nothing there. There never was. There is only them, and their stone, and their hunger dressed as holiness. And I am going to show the world."

And he had. Gods forgive the world, he had.

The Breaking had taken eleven years. Plague in the Spire's lower halls—Aldric's gift, delivered through a traitor whose name Serane had never learned but whose grave she had visited, once, out of some need she didn't examine. Trade embargoes dressed as piety. Assassinations dressed as accidents. And always, always the whispering: *why do we pay them, what do they give us, have you ever seen a monster, have you ever seen anything at all.*

By the end, the Spire-folk were dying faster than they could breed, and the covenant-stones were cracking for want of renewed blood, and the free cities—cowardly, practical, hungry—signed Aldric's Concordat and called it liberation.

The last heir of the Spire had been a child. Serane knew this because she had been in the room, thirty years ago, when the order was given: *no survivors, and bring me whatever bleeds strangely.* She had been the one who reported, later, that the child's body was never found. She remembered Aldric's face when she told him. Not rage. Something worse. Patience.

"He will surface," the King had said. "Blood calls to blood. Stones call to stones. Find him when he does, Serane. You are the only patient thing I own."

Thirty years. Thirty years of quiet inquiries, of ledgers and toll-records and villages that forgot inconvenient girls, and now—a girl in the high passes asking after old songs, buying lamp oil, following her dead mistress's map toward broken stones. And now, if the latest reports were true, smoke rising near Ashfall Pass where no smoke should be, and a waystone that had been dead for a lifetime suddenly warm to the touch.

The covenant was waking. Which meant the boy was alive. Which meant—

Which meant her king's patience was about to be rewarded, and Serane intended to be the instrument of it.

* * *

The problem, she reflected on the third day, was that everyone she interrogated lied to her, and almost none of them knew they were doing it.

She had taken over the toll-house at Greywater Ford, installed her four witch-hunters in the loft, and begun the slow work of unpicking the river traffic. The ford was the throat of the high country; everything going up to Thornfell passed her window eventually. She interviewed raftsmen and herb-gatherers, a tinker, two pilgrims, and a woman who sold eels, and from each she took not confessions but details—the way a jeweler takes settings apart, screw by tiny screw, until the lie reveals itself as a gap.

The eel-woman was the one who broke.

Not by confessing. By being honest about the wrong thing.

"There was a boat," she said, in answer to her ninth question, "poling upstream two nights back. Odd cargo. Smuggler-built, that shallow draft, but flying no colors. And there was a boy aboard."

Serane set down her pen. "Describe him."

"Barefoot. Barefoot in sleet-water, lady, and he wasn't shivering. Sat at the bow like a figurehead, staring at the water like he was listening to it. Young lad, maybe twelve winters, hair pale as ash. Pretty, in an uncanny way, if you take my meaning." The eel-woman wiped her hands on her skirts. "The smugglers were nervous. Kept their hands near their knives whenever the boy moved. You don't do that with cargo you trust."

No. You don't. Serane wrote the date, the direction, the description, in her precise, joyless hand. *Ash-pale hair. Unbothered by cold. Listens to water.* Every report for thirty years had carried fragments like these—sightings that dissolved, rumors that curdled—but never assembled, never *confirmed*. And now here it was, floating up her river in a smuggler's boat, headed for the broken stones with a runaway girl and, presumably, her mistress's grimoire.

"Where do smugglers land above the ford?"

"Nowhere good, lady. Old quarry steps, mostly. Or—" the eel-woman hesitated, and Serane waited, because waiting was a weapon too— "or Brannoc's crew uses the hollow under Widow Crag. But you didn't hear it from me."

"I heard it from no one," Serane agreed, and meant it; the woman would be safer unnamed, and Serane wasted nothing, not even mercy.

That evening she stood on the toll-house porch and watched sleet come down the valley like the sky shaking out ash, and she thought about the girl.

Yara Marchetti. The name had cost her two weeks and a burned archive to recover—apprentice to Maga Odile Voss, hedge-craft licensed under the old concordat, both presumed dead in the Callowmere purge six years past. Presumed. But Odile Voss had been cleverer than the purge, and somewhere between her death and now she had hidden her apprentice in the one place Aldric's writ ran thinnest: the Thornfell heights, where the old covenant still slept in the stones like embers under snow.

And the apprentice had found the boy. Of course she had. Blood called to blood; the mistress had planned it that way, probably, before she died. Everything about the girl's movements had the shape of a dead woman's design, and Serane respected that shape the way a fencer respects a good blade—even as she prepared to break it.

What she did not respect—what she found, if she was honest, faintly obscene—was the sentiment attached to it. The goodwife crying over bread. The villagers choosing ruin to protect a stranger. Love, loyalty, grief: the whole soft apparatus that humans built around themselves and called meaning. Serane had watched that apparatus fail a thousand times. It failed predictably, at predictable pressures, and her entire craft was knowing the pressures.

Except.

Except that the girl had survived six years alone in the high country, which took more than craft. Except that the boy sat barefoot in sleet like the water was singing to him, and something in Serane's chest, some room she kept locked, rattled its door when she imagined that. Except that she had served a hollow man for thirty years because he had once smiled at her warmly, and she had mistaken the warmth for a soul.

She went inside. She did not open the box. She slept, and her dreams were sharp, and in them a pale-haired boy turned his face from the water and looked at her with her own younger eyes, and said, in her own voice: *you are remembering. Remembering is not working.*
