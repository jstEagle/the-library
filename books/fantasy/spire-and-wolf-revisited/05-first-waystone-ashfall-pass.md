## 5. First Waystone: Ashfall Pass

The mountain did not want them to pass.

Yara felt it in the way the wind turned against them at every switchback, in the way the sleet came sideways for two days, needling through every seam of her oilskin as though the sky itself had been paid to slow them. Behind them, three valleys back and a day's hard walking down, the smoke of witch-hunter fires still smudged the horizon like a bruise that would not fade. Ahead, according to her mistress's grimoire—and according to the boy who walked beside her with bare feet that never seemed to bleed—lay Ashfall Pass, and the first of the three broken waystones.

Vael had not complained once. That was the thing about him that unsettled her most, more than the Old Tongue, more than the blood-test she had run on the second night with a silver needle and a page of her mistress's cramped handwriting. He simply walked. He walked as if the mountain were speaking to him and he was too polite to interrupt.

"Tell me again what it says," Yara said, when they stopped at midday beneath an overhang furred with frost-killed lichen. She tapped the grimoire's open page. "In words I know."

Vael crouched beside her, studying the faded ink as though it might study him back. His Common Tongue had grown faster than she'd expected—days, not weeks—but it still came out of him strangely, each word placed like a stone in a wall he was building carefully and slightly wrong.

"The stone is sleeping," he said. "Not dead. Sleeping things can be waked." He touched his own wrist, where the needle-mark had already vanished. "My blood is a key. Your hands are the lock-turner."

"Because I'm human."

"Because you *remember*," he said, and something moved behind his eyes—something old and sorry. "The covenant is made of remembering. That is what my mother told me, before—" He stopped. He often stopped there, at the edge of before, the way other children stopped at the edge of a cliff.

"Before the Spire fell," Yara finished for him.

"Before," he agreed quietly, and said nothing else.

They ate hardtack softened in snowmelt and pressed on.

* * *

Ashfall Pass earned its name honestly. The mountainsides on either side were grey with ancient cinder, ground so fine that their boots left prints like bruises in ash, and the wind carried a smell Yara knew from her earliest years with her mistress—the smell of burnt magic, old and cold, the way a hearth smells hours after the fire has died. Somewhere above them, half-lost in cloud, stood the shattered crown of a peak that had once, the grimoire claimed, held one of the great waystones: anchor-points of the covenant, strung across the Thornfells like beads on a thread, binding mountain-magic to the lowland realms so that no army could cross without the free cities' leave.

King Aldric had found his leave another way. King Aldric had broken the thread.

Yara saw the first waystone an hour before sunset, and her breath went out of her all at once.

It had been tall once—taller than any watchtower she'd ever slept in, taller than the smugglers' masts at Greywater. Now it lay broken across the pass like a fallen god, its upper third sheared away and lying in the cinder-field fifty yards off, its surface crazed with cracks that glimmered faintly, even now, even broken, with a light like moonlight trapped under ice. Carvings covered every reachable inch: spirals, running script in the Old Tongue, figures that might have been people or might have been mountains wearing people's faces. Where the stone had cracked, the carvings had split too, and through the splits leaked a thin, continuous sound—a note too low to hear properly, felt instead in the teeth, in the breastbone, like a bell struck once a hundred years ago and never allowed to finish ringing.

Vael made a sound Yara had never heard from him. It was small and raw, the sound a child makes finding a wounded animal, and he ran—not walked, *ran*—across the ash, bare feet kicking up grey puffs, and pressed both palms flat against the fallen stone's base.

The low note changed. Deepened. The glimmering in the cracks brightened, then guttered, like a lamp running out of oil.

"It knows me," Vael whispered. He looked up at her, and his face was wet, and Yara realized with a jolt that he was crying without seeming to notice. "It knows me and it cannot hold on. Yara. It is *dying* while it sleeps."

She knelt beside him and opened the grimoire to the page her mistress had marked with a pressed sprig of rowan, forty years dried to lace. The ritual was there in her mistress's hand, precise as a surgeon's chart: the circle of salt and iron filings, the placement of the heir's blood at four points, the words—which Yara could read now, haltingly, because three nights ago Vael had begun teaching her, syllable by patient syllable, the way you teach someone to swim by holding them up in shallow water.

And there, at the bottom of the page, in smaller writing, almost an afterthought:

*The stone will take its price. It always takes a price. Choose what you offer before you begin, or it will choose for you.*

Yara read that line three times. Her mouth had gone dry.

"What price?" Vael asked, watching her face.

"It doesn't say." She turned the page over, as if the answer might be hiding there. It wasn't. "Mistress Ilvane never wrote anything she didn't mean, but she wrote this like she wanted it missed."

"Maybe she hoped no one would have to do it."

"Maybe." Yara looked at the dying stone, at the boy kneeling against it with his hands still pressed flat, giving whatever warmth he had to something that couldn't thank him. "Or maybe she hoped whoever did it would be brave enough to start anyway."

* * *

They built the circle as the light failed.

Salt from her pouch, iron filings from the bottom of her kit, laid in a ring around the point where the fallen stone met the earth—a junction of root and ruin, thick as a wagon-wheel, veined with that trapped-moonlight glow. Vael opened his palm with the same unnerving calm he gave everything, and let her draw the blood: four drops, four points of the compass, each hissed faintly where it struck the stone like water on a hot pan.

Then the words.

Yara spoke them badly. She knew she spoke them badly—her tongue tripped on the deep vowels, her breath broke phrases that should have flowed unbroken—but the stone did not seem to care. With each line, the glow in the cracks strengthened. The unfinished bell-note rose, resolved, became almost melody. Frost retreated from the stone in a slow tide. And beneath her knees, through the ash and the frozen ground, Yara felt something vast shift its weight, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice calling their name.

The last word left her mouth.

The world went white and silent.

Not dark—white. Yara hung in a place that was not a place, and something enormous regarded her. Not with eyes. With attention. It was the difference between being watched and being *read*, and she was being read cover to cover, every page, including the ones she had hidden even from herself.

***A price,*** said the stone, in a voice like her own memory of her own voice. ***A freely given memory. One. To bind the first knot.***

Her mind flew to the obvious things—to Vael, to the grimoire, to Mistress Ilvane's face—and slammed against a wall of instinct so fierce it felt physical. No. Not those. Never those.

But the stone was reading, reading, patient as winter, and Yara understood with sickening clarity that it would find what it needed whether she pointed or not. Better to choose. Her mistress had written that much: *choose, or it chooses for you.*

So Yara Marchetti, runaway apprentice, thief of a watchtower, keeper of a dying covenant, reached into herself and took hold of the oldest grief she owned.

Her mother. Dead of fever when Yara was six. Dead so long ago that the grief had worn smooth as river glass, carried so long it had become part of her skeleton rather than a wound upon it. What remained was small: a kitchen, steam on a window, a voice humming, brown hair, warm hands—

Warm hands. Brown hair. A voice.

*A face.*

"I give you her face," Yara said aloud, into the whiteness, and her voice cracked in the middle. "Freely given. Take it. Leave me the rest."

The stone considered. The stone accepted.

The pain was not pain. That was the horror of it. There was no tearing, no burning—there was simply a moment when Yara reached for her mother's face and found a smooth, featureless warmth where it had been, the way a word sits on the tip of the tongue and will not come. She remembered everything else. The kitchen. The fever. The funeral, such as it was, in a smuggler's camp where nobody cried because crying cost energy and everyone was hungry. The humming. But the face itself—the nose, the eyes, the particular set of the mouth—was gone, scooped out clean, and in its place was only knowledge: *she had a face; faces matter; this loss is mine and I chose it.*

Then the whiteness folded shut, and Yara was on her knees in the ash, and the first waystone was *singing*.

Light poured up through the cracks like dawn forced backward into the stone. The broken upper section, fifty yards distant, groaned—actually groaned, a sound of stone remembering it was stone—and dragged itself a handspan across the cinder field, reaching toward its base the way a drowning man reaches for a rope. The bell-note completed itself at last, a full resonant chord that rolled down the pass and out into the valleys beyond, and Yara felt it in her teeth, in her scars, in the hollow place where her mother's face used to live.

High above, along the ridgelines, lights kindled. Small at first. Then more. Mountain-fires? Ward-lights? Something older than either, answering a call that had gone unanswered for forty years.

Vael was staring at her.

He had seen. Of course he had seen—he had been inside the circle, bound to the working, unable to look away. He had watched the whiteness take her, watched her come back, and now he looked at her the way he had looked at the broken stone: as at something wounded that did not yet know it was wounded.

"What did it take?" he asked. His Common Tongue had never sounded so careful.

"A memory." Yara got to her feet. Her legs held. That surprised her. "That's the price. That's what your covenant runs on. Memories, freely given." She managed something that was almost a smile. "Cheap at half the cost, if it keeps Aldric's hunters off these slopes."

"What memory?"

She should have lied. She was a smuggler's apprentice; lying was a tool she kept sharp. But the boy's eyes were wide and horrified and honest, and the lie died somewhere in her throat.

"My mother's face," she said. "I don't remember what she looked like anymore. I remember everything else. Just not that."

Vael crossed the space between them and took her hand—her scarred, salt-burned, capable hand—in both of his small cold ones, and held it against the singing stone.

"Humans pay with *themselves*," he whispered. "For everything. Every step. Every kindness." His voice shook. "I didn't know. I thought—I thought survival was just survival. My mother never—" He stopped, jaw tight. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. It should have been mine to give. It's my blood, my covenant—"

"And it'll ask you soon enough, I expect." Yara squeezed his fingers. "When it does, you'll choose well. That's how we'll get through this. Choosing well, one terrible price at a time."

Above them the ridge-lights burned steady now, a constellation answering a constellation. The stone's glow settled into something sustainable, a heartbeat rather than a flare. One knot tied. Two remained.

Yara looked south, down the long throat of the pass, and her stomach dropped.

Far below, winding up the valley road like ink spilled on snow, torches. Dozens of them. Moving with the unhurried discipline of soldiers who believe no one is watching.

Witch-hunters. And they had found the pass.

Vael followed her gaze, and the chord of the awakened stone seemed suddenly very loud, very bright, very visible in the gathering dark.

"They saw the light," he said quietly.

"Yes," Yara said, and reached for her pack. "Run."
