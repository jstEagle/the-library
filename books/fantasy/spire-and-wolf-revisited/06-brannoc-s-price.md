## 6. Brannoc's Price

The smugglers' camp smelled of wet wool and burnt tallow, and it was the smell that betrayed them before anything else did.

Yara had known that smell since she was nine years old—the reek of the river barges where Brannoc's crew dried their cloaks over charcoal braziers, the smell of men and women who lived half their lives in fog and the other half in lies. She had loved it once. She had thought of it as the smell of home.

Now she crouched on the ridge above the camp with Vael pressed against her side, watching the firelight gutter among the pines below, and the smell made her stomach turn.

"They're here," she murmured. "Good. That means we eat tonight."

Vael said nothing. He had learned to be quiet in the way that wild things are quiet—not trained silence but a stillness so complete it seemed he might dissolve into the dark. Only his eyes moved, tracking the shapes below: six figures around the fire, a seventh walking the perimeter with a crossbow cradled like a sleeping child. Brannoc sat apart from the rest on an upturned crate, turning something in his thick fingers. Even at this distance, in this light, Yara knew what it was. A coin. He always turned a coin when he was calculating.

She should have felt afraid. Instead she felt a terrible, familiar warmth, the warmth of coming back to people who had fed her when she was starving and taught her which knots held and which slipped. Three days she had run with Vael through the high passes since Ashfall Pass, three days of cold rations and colder wind, and the thought of Brannoc's fire had been the thing that kept her legs moving. Passage downriver. Supplies. A face or two between her and Serane's witch-hunters. That was what family was for.

"Stay behind me," she told Vael. "Say nothing. If anyone asks, you're my sister's boy. Mute. Simple. Whatever keeps their eyes off you."

Vael looked up at her. In the starlight his eyes were pale as frost on slate, and there was something in them—pity, maybe, though for whom she couldn't have said—that she chose not to see.

They went down.

* * *

Brannoc saw her before she reached the fire. He always did; it was one of the things she'd admired about him, that watchfulness, that refusal to be surprised by the world. He rose from his crate slowly, and the firelight caught the grey threading through his beard now, more of it than there had been two years ago.

"Yara Marchetti." He said her whole name the way he always had, rolling it out like a rope he was testing for rot. "Thought you were dead."

"Thought you'd be glad I'm not."

"I'm deciding." But his mouth twitched, and some of the crew laughed, and just like that she was inside the circle again, hands pulled toward the fire, a tin cup of something harsh pressed into her fingers. Dalla the rope-witch squeezed her shoulder. Little Fen, who'd been a cabin boy when she left, stared at her with open-mouthed admiration, as if she were a story come off the page.

"You look like hell," Brannoc said.

"I've been in the mountains."

"Nobody *lives* in the mountains, girl. People hide in the mountains." His eyes went past her shoulder, casual as a man checking the weather, and found Vael standing at the edge of the light. "Who's this?"

"My sister's boy. He doesn't talk."

"Doesn't talk." Brannoc came closer, unhurried, and Yara watched him look at Vael the way she had watched him look at cargo a hundred times—at bolt after bolt of stolen silk, at a cage of river-otters, at a frightened girl once, years ago, before Yara understood what that particular stillness in his face meant. "Strange-looking child. Where's his mother?"

"Dead."

"And your mistress. Dead too, they say. The old woman in Thornfell, the one who took you in." He said it lightly, but his eyes never left hers. "Word travels strange ways, Yara. Word says soldiers burned her tower. Word says someone walked out of that fire."

"The word is right."

"Mm." He turned the coin. Heads. Tails. Heads. "And where is the boy's father, then? Sister's boy. Sister dead, mother dead. Father?"

"Brannoc." She kept her voice easy, kept the cup steady in her hand. "I need passage down the Greywater. Two seats on whatever barge you're running. And supplies—a week's worth. I can pay."

"With what?"

"With what I've got." She didn't name it. She didn't have to; they both knew what a runaway apprentice carried in her pockets. Silver candlesticks. A ring too fine for her hands. The small portable wealth of a dead woman's house.

Brannoc smiled, and it was almost the smile she remembered, the one that had meant safety when she was nine and orphaned and stealing bread off his barge. "We leave at first light. You'll ride with Fen's skiff. And the boy—" the coin stopped turning, "—the boy eats first. Growing lads eat first. That's the rule."

It wasn't the rule. It had never been the rule. But Vael was already being drawn toward the fire by Dalla, who was pressing bread into his hands with a gentleness that surprised Yara, and the warmth in her chest was so sudden and so stupid that she let herself believe it.

That was her mistake. That was always her mistake.

* * *

She woke to the sound of a coin dropping on wood.

Not a real sound—a memory-sound, the ghost of Brannoc's habit, and she lay rigid in the lee of Fen's skiff with her heart hammering before she understood why the sound had followed her into sleep. Then she heard the voices. Low, across the embers of the dead fire, where two figures stood beyond the ring of sleeping forms.

"—not asking you to like it," Brannoc was saying. "I'm asking you to count."

Yara slid from her blankets without moving the ground under her, an old smuggler's trick, and drifted through the trees with her breath shallow and her feet finding the silent places. The second figure stood with her back to the fire's ashes, and even hooded, even in the dark, Yara knew the set of those shoulders. She had seen that silhouette in three villages now, always a day behind her, always leaving ash.

Serane.

The witch-hunter's voice was soft and precise, a blade being oiled. "The writ specifies covenant-blood. Living proof that the old magic survives. The King pays for the child alive and unmarked. The woman is incidental—but she has been seen with him, which makes her either hostage or accomplice, and I find I don't care which."

"He's not for sale," Brannoc said. "He's for *delivery*. There's a difference in the price."

"There is no difference in the outcome."

"There's a difference in the price," Brannoc repeated, stubborn as a mule in mud, and Yara heard the coin flip, caught, flipped again. "Two hundred crowns for the pair. Four hundred if you want them separated and delivered quiet."

"Three hundred for the boy delivered quiet. The woman walks free—or doesn't. Your choice to make, since you know where she sleeps."

Silence. Long enough for an owl to call somewhere up the valley, long enough for Yara's hands to go cold in a way that had nothing to do with the night air.

"Done," Brannoc said.

Yara did not remember walking back to the skiff. She remembered lying down again, pulling the blanket to her chin, staring up at the wheeling stars through the pine branches while her mind did what it had been trained to do: calculate. Not grief. Grief could wait; grief was a luxury that got people killed. Calculate.

Serane would take him at dawn, probably—no, before dawn, these inquisitor types liked the dark for their work. Which meant Yara had hours, not until morning. Which meant waking Vael without waking the camp, which meant the perimeter sentry, which meant—

Which meant the sentry was already dead.

She found him slumped against a pine with a crossbow bolt through his throat, and for one absurd moment she thought *Vael*, thought the boy had killed to protect her, and something in her chest lurched between pride and horror. Then she saw the fletching. Grey goose-feather, red-waxed thread. Serane's own mark. The inquisitor hadn't waited for dawn. She had opened the camp's throat herself, quietly, professionally, and now—

Now the camp was waking. Shouts. Firelight blooming. And through it all, cutting across the chaos like a keel through chop, Serane's voice, clear and cold:

"The boy! Take the boy alive!"

Yara ran.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do.

She reached the skiff as the first of Brannoc's crew came blundering out of the tents with blades drawn and no idea who the enemy was. Vael was awake—he was always awake, she would learn, always half-coiled in sleep like a heron—and his hand shot out and gripped her wrist with a strength no child should have had.

"They sold us," he said. It was the longest sentence she had ever heard from him, and his accent was wrong, flattened and human, and she understood dimly that he had understood everything, had perhaps always understood everything, and had lain in the dark beside her letting her believe otherwise.

"Yes," she said. "Run."

They ran. Upstream along the shingle, because downstream was the river road and the river road was death. Behind them the camp dissolved into screaming—Serane had not come alone, and whatever bargain Brannoc had struck, it apparently hadn't included terms for what happened when her hunters finished the job early. A crossbow bolt splintered the driftwood an arm's length from Vael's head. Yara grabbed his collar and hauled him into the treeline as a second bolt buried itself where she'd been standing.

Then hands on her from the side—Dalla, rope-witch, rope-callused fingers closing on her arm, and for one heartbeat Yara thought *help*, thought *family*, and then she saw Dalla's face and knew better.

"Girl," Dalla breathed, "girl, give me the boy and walk away. Brannoc will let you walk away. It's four hundred crowns, Yara. It's four hundred crowns and a pardon and none of us swing for the Thornfell job. Don't make us—"

Yara broke her nose with the heel of her hand. It was not a skill her mistress had taught her; it was a skill the barge had. Dalla went down howling, and Yara stepped over her without looking back, because looking back was another luxury, and she was done buying luxuries.

Vael kept pace with her through the black pines, barefoot on frozen ground, breathing easily where she wheezed. Twice more the bolts came—one from ahead, forcing them downslope, herding them, Serane was *herding* them—and twice the mountain itself seemed to intervene, a rockslide of old snow, a ravine where the trail should have been. Later, much later, Yara would wonder whether the boy had called those things. That night she only ran.

They came out of the trees above the Greywater gorge with the river roaring below and torchlight spreading through the forest behind them like a stain. Ahead, the only crossing for ten miles: the old pack bridge, rope and plank, swaying in the wind off the water.

Halfway across it, silhouetted against the stars, stood a figure with a crossbow.

Fen. Little Fen, who had pressed bread into Vael's hands and looked at Yara like she was a legend. He raised the crossbow, and his voice cracked across the gorge, young and miserable and absolutely certain.

"Don't. Please don't, Yara. Brannoc says—I'm sorry. I'm sorry, but four hundred crowns, and he has my brother's debt, and I—please just stop walking."

Yara stopped walking.

The gorge wind tore at her hair. Below, the river smashed white against the rocks, loud enough to swallow a scream, loud enough to swallow a body. Behind her, the torches were closing. Ahead, a seventeen-year-old boy with a crossbow and tears freezing on his cheeks, and the arithmetic of love and money balanced so finely in his young face that Yara could see it trembling.

She thought about her mistress then. Not deliberately—it came on her like weather, the memory of the old woman's hands grinding herbs, of the lullaby, of *you are worth more than what they'll pay for you, little wolf*. She thought: *this is what she saved me from. This exact bridge. This exact arithmetic.*

And she thought: *I am worth more than what they'll pay for me.*

"Fen," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Put it down or don't. But know that if you loose that bolt, you're not Brannoc's anymore. You're hers. And she burns villages."

Fen's crossbow dipped. Rose. Dipped again.

Vael moved.

She never saw him decide to do it. One moment he was beside her, the next he was walking forward onto the bridge, straight down its center, arms loose at his sides, barefoot on the frost-slick planks, walking toward the crossbow the way water walks toward a crack in stone. Fen made a sound like a kicked dog. The bow shook. And then the boy began to speak—not human words, not the flat trade-tongue, but the Old Tongue, the language of a dead realm, syllables that made the gorge air feel suddenly thin and the hairs on Yara's arms stand up, and the wind changed.

It came up the gorge, hard, a fist of river-cold air, and it hit the bridge sideways. The ropes groaned. Fen dropped the crossbow to grab the rail, and the weapon skated across the planks and vanished over the edge, and Vael walked past him without a glance, the way you pass a fencepost, and reached the far side.

He turned. Held out his hand to her across the swaying gap.

Yara crossed. Her legs were shaking so badly she barely felt the planks. When her feet touched solid rock, she looked back once—once only—at the torchlit chaos, at the camp that had fed her and named her and sold her, at Fen clinging to the railings and weeping, at the darkness where Brannoc was presumably already renegotiating.

"Supplies," she said aloud, stupidly, to the wind. "I lost the supplies."

Vael tilted his head at her, that birdlike tilt, and said something in the Old Tongue, soft. She didn't know the words yet. But she knew the tone. Everyone knew that tone; it was universal, older than any language. It was the tone of someone saying: *I know. I'm sorry. Keep walking.*

So she kept walking.

* * *

Dawn found them in a hollow beneath a granite overhang, ten miles upstream and higher than any road, sharing the last of everything: half a heel of bread, three strips of dried fish, a mouthful of melted snow each. Yara inventoried her pockets with the grim thoroughness Brannoc had taught her and arrived at the total. One knife. Flint and steel. The grimoire, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Nothing else. No food for tomorrow. No rope. No silver to buy any.

Behind them, smoke still rose faintly from the direction of the camp. She didn't let herself think about what that smoke was.

"They were my family," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came out anyway, scraped raw. "Nine years old, starving, stealing off his barge—he could have thrown me in the river. Any captain would have. He fed me instead. Taught me knots. Gave me a bunk and a name and—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Four hundred crowns. That's what nine years weighed."

Vael watched her with those frost-pale eyes. Then he did something he had never done before: he reached out and took her hand. His grip was warm, startlingly warm, warmer than any child's had a right to be, and he pressed her knuckles once, briefly, against his forehead—an old gesture, she would learn later, the Spire-folk's sign between equals in grief.

When he spoke, it was in trade-tongue, clumsy and deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Not family. Family does not sell." A pause, searching for the next piece. "You... make new. Better."

Yara laughed. It came out broken, half sob, but it was a laugh, and it startled birds out of the pines above them.

"Make new," she echoed. "Right."

She looked east, where the sun was coming up over the shoulders of the Thornfells, gilding the peaks she would have to cross. Somewhere out there was the second waystone, and beyond it the third, and beyond them a king's army and an inquisitor who did not tire and did not bargain badly and did not stop. She had no food, no rope, no silver, no family, and no plan past *keep walking*.

But she had the grimoire against her chest, and the boy's warm hand in hers, and a fury in her belly that burned cleaner than any brazier Brannoc had ever lit.

"First lesson," she said, standing, brushing the frost from her knees. "You wanted to teach me your tongue. We start today. Every word I learn unlocks more of her book, and I need that book, and I need you to teach me fast—because the next people who find us won't be offering passage."

Far below, on the grey ribbon of the river, a single barge was making its way upstream against the current, poling hard, flying no colors at all.

Vael saw it too. His eyes narrowed, and he said one word in the Old Tongue, low, and though Yara didn't yet know what it meant, she would remember the sound of it for the rest of her life.

It meant *deepwood*.
