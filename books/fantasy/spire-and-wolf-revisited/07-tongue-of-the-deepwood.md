## 7. Tongue of the Deepwood

The forest-keeper's house was not a house at all, or not in any way Yara had been taught to understand houses. It grew. That was the only word for it. A great fallen oak had rooted itself again where it lay, its branches driven down into the earth like the fingers of a hand pressed flat against the world, and beneath that living archway someone had built walls of woven hazel and clay, a roof of turf sown with thrift and small blue flowers, a chimney of stacked stone that breathed smoke as gently as a sleeping thing exhaled.

Yara stopped at the edge of the clearing and put her arm across Vael's chest before she could think better of it.

"Wait," she said. "Watch first."

They watched. She had learned watching from her mistress—*the mountain gives you ten breaths, girl, take all ten before you spend one*—and in those ten breaths she saw: a goat-shed built into the roots, two goats regarding them with flat yellow indifference; a herb garden gone half wild but deliberately so, rows of feverfew and comfrey threading themselves through the nettles; a line of washing strung between two saplings, shirts and a long grey dress, all of it mended, none of it fine.

And she saw the keeper himself, standing in his doorway as though he had known they were coming for three days, which he probably had. He was old in the way that stones are old—not bent by age so much as weathered smooth by it—and he wore his white hair braided back from a face seamed like bark. His eyes went to Vael first. They stayed there a long moment.

Then he spoke, and the language he spoke was not the common tongue of the valley roads, nor the trade-speech of the smugglers' camps, nor anything Yara had a name for. It moved like water over stones. And Vael answered.

Yara stood with her pack straps cutting her shoulders and listened to a conversation she could not follow, held between a boy she had known for less than a season and an old man she did not know at all, and felt something twist in her chest that was not quite jealousy and not quite fear. It was the feeling of standing outside a lit window on a cold night.

The old man looked past Vael at last, at her, and said something else—one word, twice—and beckoned.

"He says you look hungry," Vael translated. His voice had changed since they'd left the pass behind them; it was steadier now, deeper, as though the deepwood itself were teaching him how to stand inside his own words. "He says hungry people make bad guests."

"That's true enough," Yara said, and her voice cracked on it, because she was suddenly, stupidly near tears, and she didn't know why until she understood: no one had fed her without wanting something in return since her mistress died. Brannoc's crew shared their fire, but the ledger was always open. The smugglers' kindness came with interest.

The keeper's name, when he gave it, was Ossen. He would not say more than that, and Vael explained, haltingly, that in the Old Tongue a name was not a label but a history—that *Ossen* meant something like *he who remained*, and that the rest of a man's naming was private, kept for those who earned it.

"What do I call you, then?" Yara asked him that first evening, over a soup of nettle and smoked fish that tasted like the forest floor made edible.

Ossen smiled with very few teeth. "Old man," he said, in accented but serviceable common speech. "That will do. Names can wait. Most things can." His eyes flicked to Vael, and something in them was old grief wearing patience like a coat. "Some things cannot."

* * *

Vael dreamed badly the first nights under Ossen's roof.

He dreamed of the Spire—not the ruin of it, not the fall, but the Spire as it had been, which was worse, because his dreaming mind had begun to furnish the memory with details his waking self had lost. Blue light running through crystal corridors like blood through veins. The hum of the covenant-stones, felt in the teeth rather than heard. His mother's hands, cool as riverwater, turning his face up toward hers.

In the dreams she spoke the Old Tongue, and in the dreams he understood every word, and when he woke the understanding clung to him like dew, burning off slowly in the morning light of the human world.

It was in that half-dreaming hour, on the fourth morning, that he found Yara already awake, sitting cross-legged by the cold hearth with the grimoire open on her knees, her lips moving.

She was trying to read it aloud.

He should have laughed—he almost did—but then he saw her face. She wasn't performing. She was fighting. Her finger traced the spidery glyphs of the Spire script, and her brow was furrowed with the particular ferocity she brought to everything she could not yet do, and her mouth shaped sounds that were almost right and therefore wrong in ways that made his skin prickle, the way a song sung slightly off-key makes a dog lift its head.

"You're saying *fire*," he said quietly. "You're trying to say *ash*."

She looked up. For a heartbeat he saw her decide whether to be embarrassed, and then watch herself decide not to bother. "The grimoire opens differently than it used to," she said. "Since the first stone. Since—" she touched her temple, where the memory of her mother's face had been, though of course she couldn't remember what was missing, only that there was a smooth place in her past where something ought to be. "Pages that were blank before have writing on them now. But it's the old script. My mistress taught me letters, trade-letters, valley-letters. Not this."

"No one teaches this," Vael said. "You're born into it, or you're not."

"Then I'm not." She closed the book. Not slammed it—closed it, carefully, which was somehow worse. "Well. We'll manage. We always—"

"*Ash*," Vael said.

She stopped.

"The word you were trying to read. *Ash.*" He crouched down across the hearth from her, awkward, his knees cracking the way Ossen's goats sounded when they rose. "In the Old Tongue it's *saer*. But it isn't only ash. It's what remains after burning. What the fire leaves behind that still remembers being wood." He hesitated. "My mother used to say that people were *saer*. That grief is just love with nowhere to go, sitting in you like ash in a grate."

Yara stared at him. Then, slowly, she opened the grimoire again and found the page, and traced the glyph, and said "*Saer*"—and got it nearly right on the second try.

And the page turned itself.

Not dramatically. There was no wind, no thunderclap. The parchment simply stirred, the way a sleeper turns over, and settled onto a new leaf, and the new leaf was covered in writing that had not been legible to her an hour before—or perhaps had not existed at all—and she made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half sob.

"It listens," she whispered. "The book listens to whether I understand."

"The book was written for people who speak," Vael said. "Everything in my country worked that way. Nothing was locked. Things were just... waiting for the right mouth." He looked at his own hands, bare and brown and human-looking, and something moved behind his eyes that Yara couldn't read. "I thought all of that was dead. Buried with everyone who spoke it."

"And now?"

"Now there's you," he said simply, as if it were obvious, as if it were nothing, and Yara Marchetti—who had survived smugglers' camps and witch-hunter fires and the loss of her own mother's face without weeping—had to turn away and pretend to fuss with the fire.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm, the three of them, the way water falls into a channel it has cut for itself.

Mornings, Ossen set them tasks. Vael carried water and split wood and learned, with enormous concentration, the proper way to milk a goat, which involved being kicked into the straw twice and emerging triumphant on the third day with a full pail and a bruised shin and an expression of such fierce pride that Yara laughed until she had to sit down. Yara dug the winter bed for the leeks and re-thatched the lean-to and rendered fat, and discovered that Ossen watched her hands when she worked and nodded to himself sometimes, and that his nodding made her feel more approved-of than any praise ever had.

Afternoons, lessons.

Vael taught the way he did everything—without system, following some inner tide—but Yara learned that his tides were reliable. He'd give her a word when a word was needed: *dolm*, *root*, when she was weeding; *shirr*, the sound rain makes on leaves, when the afternoon storms came; *vess*, the space between two people who trust each other, which he taught her blushing and staring at the floor, and which she repeated back to him with deliberate gravity until he threw a pinecone at her.

Each word opened the grimoire further. She began to keep a slate, scratching the glyphs beside their meanings, filling both sides, then washing and beginning again. Her handwriting in the Old Tongue improved faster than her speech, and Vael told her that was correct, that the Spire-folk had trusted writing more than speaking, that words carved in stone outlasted words breathed in air.

"Is that why your people wrote a covenant?" she asked one evening, unthinking, and saw him go still.

"Yes," he said, after too long a pause. "That's why."

Evenings, Ossen told stories—but never in common speech, and never finished. He'd begin a tale in the Old Tongue, letting Vael translate in fragments, and stop at the crux, and refuse to continue. "Come back tomorrow," he'd say. "Stories keep. So does bread. So does grief." Yara suspected the withholding was pedagogy—the fastest way to make her learn fifty new words was to leave her desperate for the ending of the tale of the fox who stole the moon—and she resented it and admired it in equal measure.

On the ninth evening, the story was different. Ossen sat with his hands around his cup and said, in slow, careful common speech, as if translating for himself:

"There was a keeper here before me. My teacher. When the Spire burned, the mountains screamed for a night and a day. Every stone in every wall rang like a struck bell. Birds fell out of the trees. And then silence, and the covenant broken, and the lowlands open to whoever wanted to walk in." He drank. "My teacher walked to the first waystone and sat before it for a year. Waiting for an heir to come. He died waiting. I have sat here forty years doing the same, and I am not ashamed to say I had stopped believing." His eyes found Vael. "Then the stones woke in Ashfall Pass, and the birds came back to this wood in a week, and I knew."

"Knew what?" Yara asked.

"That the last heir was walking toward me," Ossen said, "and that I would live long enough to feed him soup." He smiled his few-toothed smile. "A man could ask worse of a life."

Vael looked down into his cup. And Yara, watching him, saw the shadow cross his face again—the same shadow she'd seen at the ford, at the pass, whenever anyone spoke of what the covenant required—and she reached across the table and put her hand over his, and he turned his hand palm-up beneath hers and held on, hard, like a drowning man, and neither of them said anything, and the fire ticked, and that was *vess*, she thought. That was exactly *vess*.

* * *

It rained for two days, and on the second day of rain Vael found Yara in the goat-shed, sitting on an overturned bucket, crying.

Not loudly. She was good at not loudly. But her shoulders were shaking, and when she heard him she wiped her face with her sleeve with furious efficiency and said, "Go away."

He did not go away. He came and sat down on the straw beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and waited, because Ossen had taught him that much about humans: sometimes the gift is the waiting.

"I tried to remember her today," Yara said finally. "My mother. The one whose face I paid at the pass. I thought—if I try hard enough, maybe the memory isn't gone, maybe it's just folded up somewhere, maybe—" Her breath hitched. "There's nothing. There's a hole with edges. I know I loved her. I know she must have had a face. I can't even tell if I miss her, because missing needs a shape to miss, and mine has no shape." She laughed, wet and awful. "I sold my own mother for a rock in a mountain pass, and the worst part is I don't even feel bad about it, because I can't feel about it. It's just a fact. Like the number of legs on a spider."

Vael was quiet for a long time. Rain drummed the shed roof. One of the goats chewed thoughtfully at his hair.

"When the Spire fell," he said at last, "I was small. Small enough that most of it went out of me, like heat from a cup. I remembered loving them. I couldn't remember them. Faces, voices—all *saer*. All ash." He turned to look at her, and his eyes in the dimness were the color of deep water. "So I know the hole with edges. And I know what you'll say next, because I said it too. You'll say, *at least you chose yours.*"

"At least I chose mine," Yara whispered.

"But here is what I learned, in the caves, in the dark, with nothing but the hole." He took her hand, the way she'd taken his at the table, palm-up, holding on. "The love doesn't live in the memory. I thought it did. I thought when the faces went, the love went with them. But it didn't. It just stopped knowing where it came from. It became—" he searched, and gave up on translation, and said the Old Tongue word instead: "*Saer.* What remains after burning. Still remembering being wood."

Yara looked at their joined hands. Something in her chest that had been clenched since Ashfall Pass loosened, not all the way, but a little—a door unlatched rather than opened.

"I'm going to get them back," she said. "The memories. Somehow. Whatever it takes."

"All right," Vael said gravely, as if this were a reasonable plan and not a mad one, and that, more than anything, was what undid her. She leaned sideways until her head found his shoulder, and he went stiff with surprise and then, carefully, as though handling something breakable, let his cheek rest against her hair, and they sat in the straw while the rain came down and the goat chewed his hair, and it was not romantic, nothing in it was romantic, it was fiercer than romance—it was the love of two creatures who had each crawled out of their own ruins and recognized the other's rubble as home.

"We're going to be all right," Yara said, into his shoulder. "Tell me we're going to be all right."

Vael was silent a moment too long.

"We're going to be together," he said. "That's the truer thing."

She was too tired to hear what lived underneath it.

* * *

On the twelfth morning the frost came early and hard, silvering the clearing, and Ossen stood in his doorway longer than usual, reading the sky the way other men read letters.

"Early winter," he said. "Hard one. You should stay. There's room, and work, and I've forty years of the Old Tongue left to teach, and the boy—" he glanced at Vael, who was carrying wood with a new steadiness, a new height, growing almost visibly in the mountain air "—the boy is stronger here. The stones feed him. Another winter and he'd be ready for whatever comes."

Yara felt the offer land in her like warmth. Stay. A roof. A teacher. A whole season of safety, of soup, of slate-words and unfinished stories. She caught herself already rearranging the journey in her mind—*we could leave at thaw, the stones aren't going anywhere*—

And then she saw the smoke.

It was far off, south and east, past the shoulder of the ridge, a single thread rising from the valley where the villages huddled. Too thin for a chimney in this cold. Too straight. And as she watched, a second thread joined it, and a third, spaced with terrible regularity, like stitches.

Like a search line moving through the valley, setting signal fires as it came.

"Ossen," she said quietly. "How many roads lead to this clearing?"

The old man followed her gaze. He was silent for a long moment, and when he spoke, all the patience had gone out of his voice, leaving something older and harder underneath.

"One road in," he said. "And the woman asking knows how to find it. Witch-hunters always do. They smell the old words on the wind." He turned and looked at Vael—at the boy who had grown stronger, taller, brighter with every restored stone, whose power now showed faintly in the dark like banked coals through a grate. "Get your things," Ossen said. "Both of you. Now. And pray your book has taught you enough, child, because the lesson after this one will not be given gently."

Far to the south, the third signal fire climbed into the frost-white sky.
