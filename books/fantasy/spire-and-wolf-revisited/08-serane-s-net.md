## 8. Serane's Net

The smuggler's name was Doss, and he had lasted four hours before he began to weep.

Serane Voss watched him from the far side of the brazier, her hands folded in her lap, her face arranged into an expression of patient sympathy. It was a face she wore the way other women wore jewelry—chosen for the occasion, polished to a shine. The cellar of the tollhouse at Greywater Ford was cold enough to see breath, and Doss's breath came fast and ragged now, pluming above him where he hung by his wrists from a roof beam.

"You're doing well," she said gently. "Better than most. Most men lie for six hours and then tell me nothing worth having. You've given me names already. Brannoc. The Marchetti girl. And now this—" she lifted a scrap of parchment between two fingers, careful as a priest handling relics, "—this lovely little map of your routes through Thornfell."

"I told you everything," Doss whispered. His voice had gone thin as winter ice over running water. "I swear it on my mother's grave, Inquisitor, I've told you—"

"Your mother," Serane said, "is alive. She sells eels at the Wednesday market in Lowmarsh. I checked." She let that sit in the cold air between them while his face did what faces did when they discovered their last coin was counterfeit. "You see why I don't put much stock in swearing, Doss. Swearing is just lying with better posture."

She rose, unhurried, and crossed to the brazier. Among its coals sat a slender iron rod, its tip glowing a dull orange, patient as a liturgy. She did not pick it up. That was the art of it—the rod was never for using, not really. It was for being *near*. Fear did the work; the iron merely gave fear something to look at.

"Tell me again about the glade," she said. "The forest-keeper. Slowly, this time. And Doss—" she turned, and let him see her eyes, which were pale grey and entirely without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty, "—tell me true. Because the writ I carry says *covenant-blood*, and covenant-blood is the one thing in this world I cannot come back empty-handed for."

He told her about the glade.

It lay two days' hard walk into the Deepwood's southern skirt, past a stream that ran backward under a shelf of stone—a thing Doss described with the superstitious shudder of a man who had seen too much of the mountains' oddness and understood none of it. A forest-keeper lived there, one of those half-mythical recluses who tended the old groves and asked no toll but courtesy. Old, Doss said. Older than old. The kind of old that made even Brannoc take his hat off, and Brannoc took his hat off for no one living or dead.

"And the girl went there," Serane said. "With the boy."

"With the boy." Doss's eyes flicked sideways, toward the door, toward anywhere. "Inquisitor, I never saw the boy myself. But Brannoc talked about him after. Talked like a man who'd handled something he shouldn't have. Said the boy spoke backwards-talk, said the stones near him hummed like struck bells. Brannoc sold the route anyway. Sold it to the witch-hunters first, and then—" his voice cracked, "—then he sold it to you. Same road, twice. He always said a road could be sold as many times as there were buyers."

"Yes," Serane said softly. "Brannoc has always been very clear about what things are worth. Including himself." She studied the map again. Two days into the Deepwood, along a route only smugglers knew, to a keeper's glade—and beyond it, if the grimoire fragments were honest, the second waystone, sunk somewhere beneath the roots of the mountain itself. The Hollow Root, the old texts called it, in a script that predated Aldric's grandfather's grandfather.

The pieces were assembling themselves. They usually did, if you were patient. People believed interrogation was about pain, and pain had its uses, but truly it was about arithmetic. One captured smuggler plus one frightened crew equaled a route. One route plus one grimoire equaled a destination. One destination plus the writ of a king who had waited thirty years to finish what he'd started—

"Cut him down," she said to the soldiers at the door. "Feed him. When he can walk, send him south with the supply train. He'll serve out his debt hauling chain for the army."

Doss stared at her from the floor, wrists bleeding, face wrecked, unable to comprehend mercy arriving from the direction it least expected. "You—you're letting me go?"

"I'm letting you live," Serane corrected. "Those are different things. Go home to your mother, Doss. Sell eels. You'll be better at it than smuggling—you haven't the stomach for selling people, though apparently you have the stomach for watching it done." She gathered her maps and her seal and moved toward the stairs, then paused. "One more thing. The keeper at the glade. What would he do, do you think, if armed men came up his stream?"

Doss swallowed. "Die badly," he said. "They all say the keepers are magic, but I never saw one do anything but grow vegetables and talk to trees."

"Mm." Serane climbed the stairs into the grey afternoon light, and thought: *every legend dies badly, if you bring enough men.* It was almost a comfort, how reliably the world obeyed that rule. Almost.

* * *

They marched at dusk.

Serane rode at the head of twenty picked men—not witch-hunters, whose piety made them noisy, but quiet professional soldiers out of Aldric's own garrison, men who had burned villages in the lowlands and felt nothing about it, which she considered the highest military virtue. Behind them came four handlers with dogs, and behind the dogs came a hooded man chained to a mule: Tam Vess, another of Brannoc's crew, taken off a river barge three nights ago, and useful now for exactly one purpose.

The Deepwood received them the way deep water receives a stone—without comment, closing over the noise of their passage, swallowing the hoofbeats into moss and root. Serane disliked forests. They were dishonest terrain, full of cover and ambiguity, places where the small and quick defeated the many and armored. Mountains at least were honest: they killed you openly. But the writ did not care about her preferences, and neither did the king, and so she rode with her hand near her sword and her mind moving ahead of the column like a spider down a thread.

Tam Vess walked where she told him to walk, and at each fork of the trail he confirmed what Doss had already given her, and each confirmation cost him less than the last. By midnight he was answering before she asked. By the second dawn, when the trees began to change—older, wider, hung with lichen like funeral banners—he was walking with the eagerness of a man approaching salvation rather than betrayal. Serane noted this and filed it away. Betrayers always found a theology eventually. It was how they slept.

"There," Tam breathed, as the stream appeared, running backward beneath its stone shelf, silver water climbing uphill against every law God and gravity had agreed upon. "Past that. The glade opens just past the shelf. You'll hear the bees before you see it. He keeps bees."

Bees. Serane almost smiled. Somewhere in the Deepwood, an ancient creature out of the covenant-age kept bees and grew vegetables, and Yara Marchetti—clever, desperate, grieving Yara Marchetti—had run to him for shelter, believing that the world still had corners in it where the old powers would shelter the hunted.

The world had corners. That was true. Serane had spent her life finding them.

"Halt the column," she said. "No horns. No fire. We go in on foot, wide, in a ring. Nobody touches the boy. Wound the girl if she runs—that's an order, not a suggestion—but nobody lays hands on the heir. If a single hair of his head is harmed by our side, I will personally see the man responsible flogged through three garrisons." She looked around at their faces, hard and blank and obedient. "The king wants him breathing. Breathing, gentlemen. Everything else in that glade is negotiable."

"And the keeper?" asked the sergeant, a scarred veteran named Hult.

Serane considered the question the way she considered all questions: on its merits, without sentiment. "If he resists, kill him. If he surrenders, bind him. If he knows anything about the waystones—and he will know everything about the waystones—I want him talking before nightfall." She paused. "But understand this. Keepers are not smugglers. Smugglers break. Keepers *choose*, and a man who chooses is harder to turn than any man who merely breaks. So don't waste tenderness on him. Tenderness is for after."

* * *

The trap closed at midmorning, when the light slanted gold through the high branches and the bees were working the late flowers.

Serane heard it begin as a wrongness in the birdsong—a silence spreading outward from the ring of soldiers like ink through water. Then a dog whined. Then a man's voice, low and urgent, cut off mid-word.

She came into the glade itself last, as was her habit. Let others breach; she would observe.

It was beautiful, damn it all. That was the first thing she felt, and she hated herself for feeling it. A clearing full of soft green light, a low house built of living wood that had been *persuaded* rather than cut, gardens in crooked abundance, hives standing like grey temples among the bean rows. And in the center of it, standing very straight despite his years, the keeper—an old man in undyed wool, white hair loose to his waist, holding nothing but a wooden staff and an expression of profound, unhurried sorrow.

He looked at the ring of soldiers the way a man looks at weather. Not surprised. Grieved, perhaps, in the way one is grieved by a long-expected frost.

"Inquisitor," he said, and his voice carried across the glade without effort, reaching her as clearly as if she stood beside him. "You've brought a great many boots into a place where boots were never welcome."

"Sorrel," Serane said, reading the name off the intelligence scroll in her head—three decades of Aldric's records, and even the keepers had entries, thin ones, neglected ones. "Keeper of the southern groves. You're harboring fugitives under the king's writ. A woman called Marchetti. A child of covenant-blood."

"The child is not a fugitive," Sorrel said. "The child is a guest. There is a difference, though I don't expect your king's paper to know it."

"Where are they?"

"Gone." And he smiled then, faintly, and Serane's stomach tightened, because it was not the smile of a man bluffing. "Gone since before your dogs crossed the backward stream. Did you think the woods wouldn't tell me? The woods tell me everything, Inquisitor. They told me you were coming two days ago. They told me how many. They told me the color of your horse." He leaned slightly on his staff. "I stayed anyway. Someone had to slow you. And someone had to make sure the children reached the stone."

Something cold slid down Serane's spine, and she recognized it, distantly, as the feeling of a plan going quietly wrong. "Hult," she said. "Sweep the glade. Cellars, hollows, tree-boles. And find me their trail."

The soldiers moved. And the keeper watched them move with that same terrible patience, and then he did something no one expected, least of all Serane.

He set down his staff.

He laid it in the dirt, deliberately, the way a soldier lays down a sword at surrender, and he raised both hands, empty, palms out, and he began to speak—in the Old Tongue, the language of the Spire-folk, the language that was supposed to be extinct, rolling out of him in cadences like water over stone. And the glade *answered*.

Serane felt it before she understood it: a pressure, a rising hum, the hair on her arms lifting. The grass lengthened visibly, greedily, weaving itself into ankle-high tangles around the soldiers' legs. Roots heaved under the packed earth of the garden paths. The great trees at the glade's edge leaned inward, groaning, their branches interlacing overhead like fingers closing into a fist. The bees poured from their hives in a golden river, and the horses outside the treeline began, horribly, to scream.

"*Take him down!*" Serane shouted, and the world went to chaos.

An arrow took the keeper through the chest before he finished the second phrase. He staggered, blood flowering on the undyed wool, and *kept speaking*, louder, his voice cracking but relentless, and the glade kept answering—brambles erupting in thorned walls, a soldier vanishing to the hips in ground that had decided to become mud, Hult bellowing orders that no one could follow because the geometry of the clearing was rearranging itself around them like a dream arguing with itself.

Serane drew her sword and forced her way through the brambles, cutting a path by main strength, and reached the keeper as he sank to his knees among his ruined beans. Blood at his mouth now. His eyes found hers, and they were clear, and they were sorry for her, which was the worst thing she had ever seen in a dying man's face.

"You're too late," he said, in the common tongue now, wet and labored. "And you will always be too late. That is the covenant's whole gift to the faithful, Inquisitor. The stones wake faster than your king can ride." He coughed, and gripped her wrist with shocking strength, and pulled her close, and said, barely a whisper: "He speaks to the mountain now. Can't you hear it? Even here. Even through your iron and your paper. The mountain is *waking up*—"

The arrowman put a second shaft through him, and the voice stopped, and the glade's fury stopped with it—not gradually, but all at once, like a held breath released. The brambles sagged. The trees sighed back upright. The bees settled, dazed, onto the broken hives. Silence rushed in where the Old Tongue had been, and it was the loudest silence Serane had ever heard.

She knelt there a moment in the trampled garden, the dead man's grip still on her wrist, and made herself look at his face. She had learned long ago to look—it was discipline, not sentiment; a commander who refused to see what her orders did would soon refuse to see anything at all. The sorrow was still on his face. The sorrow, and something worse underneath it: pity. Pity for *her*.

"Get a count," she said hoarsely, standing. "Casualties. And someone find me that trail, or I'll start finding it myself, and none of you want that."

They found it within the hour: two sets of prints, one small and bare, heading east-northeast, toward the high shoulder of the mountain. Toward the Hollow Root. The keeper had bought them hours—perhaps a day—with his life, and he had known he was doing it, and he had done it anyway.

Serane stood at the edge of the ruined glade while her soldiers regrouped among the broken hives, and she looked east at the mountain, at its cloud-wrapped shoulders, and she listened—as much to humor the dead man as anything—to the wind coming down off the heights.

Wind. Only wind.

Probably only wind.

"Break camp," she said. "We march in ten minutes. And double the watch tonight." She swung back into the saddle, and did not look again at the keeper lying among his beans, because looking had already done its work, and the work it had done was making her afraid, and fear was a luxury she had promised King Aldric she would never again permit herself.

Behind her, the bees began, very softly, to sing against the broken hives—a sound no beekeeper in the free cities had ever reported, a sound the old texts called *mourning*—and the column rode east, into the shadow of the waking mountain, while somewhere beneath its roots, in the dark beneath the second waystone, a boy and a woman who loved him ran for the deep places of the earth, carrying with them a dead man's gift of time.

Time, Serane knew, was the one currency she could not torture out of anyone.

She meant to spend hers wisely.
