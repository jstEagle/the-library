## 9. Second Waystone: The Hollow Root

The mountain swallowed them at dusk.

Yara had walked past the hollow a dozen times in her years of running the Thornfell trails, and never once seen it. It opened beneath a shelf of granite furred with moss, a seam of darkness no wider than a coffin lid, and only when Vael pressed his palm flat against the stone did the seam breathe open—slowly, grudgingly, like an old man deciding whether to rise from his chair. Cold air rolled out, smelling of wet iron and deep earth and something older underneath, something that reminded her absurdly of her mistress's writing desk: ink, dust, and time.

"Down," Vael said. His Common Tongue was better now—weeks of the road had roughed it into something usable—but he still chose words the way a man chooses stepping-stones across a river.

"Down," Yara agreed, and lit the stub of candle she'd saved from Brannoc's drowned stores, and went first into the dark because that was what she did now. That was who she was becoming. The person who went first.

The passage descended in spirals cut by no human hand. The walls were not carved but *grown*, she realized—the stone bore whorls and ridges like the rings of a tree, and here and there thin veins of pale mineral ran through it, glowing faintly where Vael passed, dimming again behind him. He was a lamp walking. She tried not to think about what that meant for how long they could stay hidden from Serane's hunters. A boy whose blood lit the dark was a boy who could be followed by anyone who knew to look for light.

They climbed down for hours. Twice the passage narrowed until Yara had to turn sideways, the rock pressing her ribs, the candle guttering in her cupped hands. Twice Vael stopped and laid his ear against the wall, listening to something she could not hear, and both times he said, "It remembers us," in that tone he used for things that were not questions but were not quite statements either.

"It," she said. "The mountain."

"The stone under the mountain." He considered. "The same thing. Different words."

*Different words.* In the Old Tongue, she was learning, there were often five words where Common had one, each shading a different kind of being. Her mistress's grimoire had been full of them, and for years she had read those pages as decoration, as the ornamental nonsense of a dead woman's scholarship. Now every word she coaxed out of Vael unlocked another line, and the lines were not decoration at all. They were instructions. They were warnings. They were, increasingly, accusations.

Because she had read the second waystone's rite by candlelight on the trail above, and she knew what it asked.

Not just memory this time. *Chosen* memory. The first stone had taken from her like a thief in the night—she had woken on the cold stones of Ashfall Pass without her mother's face, reaching for something warm and finding only a smooth blank place where warmth should have been. But the Hollow Root, the grimoire said, was older and more courteous. It would show the giver the well of their remembering and let them draw the bucket themselves.

She had not told Vael yet. She told herself it was because he would argue, because he would offer his own memories, because he was already carrying too much of what the stones showed him. She told herself many things. The candle flame held steady in the still air, and she watched it instead of looking at him.

* * *

The passage opened without warning into a cavern so vast the candle could not find its ceiling or its far wall. Yara stood on a ledge above a sunken floor, and in the center of that floor rose the waystone.

It was nothing like the first. Ashfall's stone had been a broken finger of rock, weathered, half-buried, human-scaled enough to touch. This one was a root—a petrified root thicker than a watchtower, plunging down through the cavern floor and up into darkness overhead, branching as it rose into a canopy of stone limbs that spread across the whole vault of the cave. It looked less like something built than like something that had once been alive and had refused, even in death, to stop holding the world together.

And it was dying. She could see it the way you can see a room has been empty a long time—not from any single sign but from the whole of it. The pale veins in the stone glowed only fitfully, pulses of light traveling up the great root and guttering out before they reached the branches. Where the first stone had hummed under her hands, this one was silent. It had the silence of a house after a funeral.

Vael went down the ledge ahead of her, sure-footed on stairs that had not existed a moment before, appearing out of the rock as if the mountain were making room for him. When he touched the root, the veins flared—one pulse, weak but real—and the light came up through the floor in slow rings, and the cavern filled with shadows that moved though nothing moved to cast them.

Then the visions began.

* * *

Yara had expected pictures. What came instead was *weather*—the past arriving over her like a change in the sky, pressing on her skin, filling her lungs.

She stood in a green country. Not Thornfell as she knew it, all scree and black pine, but Thornfell clothed in oak and meadow, rivers running silver, and rising from the valley floor a spire of white stone so tall its crown was lost in cloud. Around it, people. Tall people, narrow, with eyes the color of deep water, moving among shorter figures—human figures—with the easy intimacy of neighbors. Not masters and servants. Neighbors. She saw a Spire-woman teaching a human child to shape a word in the Old Tongue, the child laughing at the feel of it in his mouth. She saw humans bringing grain and honey up the spire's stair, and Spire-folk sending down light captured in glass, and neither side counting what they gave.

And between the spire and the mountains, the waystones stood unbroken, a chain of them, each one humming, each one fed. She understood without being told: the covenant was not a wall. It was a *circulation*. Blood and memory flowing outward from the spire through the stones, and the mountains' old strength flowing back, and the free cities sleeping safe inside that circulation the way a child sleeps safe inside a heartbeat.

Then the sky changed.

It changed the way weather changes in these visions—no single moment, just a wrongness gathering. She saw men come over the passes in armor the color of dried blood, Aldric's colors, Aldric's host, decades ago, young Aldric still hungry rather than hollow. And she saw the spire's defenders go out to meet them, and she saw the waystones flare—

—and gutter.

One by one, like candles pinched by invisible fingers. The chain broke from the outside in, stone after stone going dark, and the mountain strength that should have risen against the armies simply *did not*, and the green country burned.

Yara wept without knowing it, watching fields she had never walked burn with a grief that felt borrowed and enormous. She turned to Vael—to show him, to share it, because surely he was seeing it too—

But the vision had shifted. It was showing her something else now. Something smaller. A room.

A study. Candlelight. A desk heaped with books, and at the desk a woman Yara knew better than she had ever known her own mother—knew, she corrected herself, *had known*, because the first stone had already taken things and there were evenings now when she reached for her mistress's voice and found it thinner than it should have been, worn like a coin passed through too many hands.

Mistress Ilvane. Sitting across from a man in blood-colored armor, and on the desk between them a book bound in white hide, and the man's gauntleted hand resting on it.

Yara could not hear the words. The vision gave her faces and gestures and the terrible grammar of a bargain, and she understood it anyway, understood it the way you understand a blow before the pain arrives. Ilvane had secrets. Ilvane had spent years among the Spire-folk's scattered remnants, collecting their lore, their rites, the true names of the waystones—and Aldric wanted them, wanted the covenant's architecture laid bare so he could break it properly, break it *permanently*, and be certain no heir would ever reforge it.

And Ilvane—her Ilvane, who had pulled Yara out of a smuggler's gutter and taught her letters and hedge-craft and how to hold a knife and how to hold grief—Ilvane slid the book across the desk.

In exchange, the vision said, in the language of tilted heads and steepled fingers: her life. Safe passage. An old woman's quiet death in a warm room instead of a witch-hunter's fire.

The book crossed the desk. The man smiled with no warmth in it at all. And somewhere in the burning green country below, the last waystones went dark, because the man now held the map of every vein in the body he meant to bleed.

"No," Yara said aloud. Her voice cracked against the cavern walls. "No, that's not—that isn't—"

But the vision was not finished. It turned the scene like a key turning, and showed her the rest: Ilvane afterward, alone, in the very tower where Yara had found her, sitting with a new book—a grimoire, *this* grimoire, the one strapped to Yara's back right now—writing in it through the night. Writing not the Spire-folk's secrets, which she had sold, but their *hope*: the rite of reforging, the three stones, the price of memory. Writing it in cipher and hiding it where only a clever, desperate apprentice would ever find it.

An apology in advance. A debt marked down to be paid by someone else's hand.

*If you are reading this,* the vision seemed to say, in Ilvane's dry voice, *then I am dead, and you know now what I was.*

The light withdrew. The cavern settled. Yara found herself on her knees on the stone floor with no memory of falling, and Vael beside her, his hand hovering near her shoulder, not touching, because he had learned that about her—when she broke, she broke alone, and touching made it worse.

"She sold them," Yara said. The words came out flat, factual, which was somehow worse than screaming. "My mistress. The woman who raised me. She sold the last of the Spire-folk to Aldric to buy herself a bed to die in."

Vael was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his Common was careful, each word set down like a cup that might crack.

"I saw my people die," he said. "In the stone. I see it every time I sleep." He looked up at the vast dark branches overhead. "I did not know *why* the stones failed. Now I know the why had a face. A human face." He paused. "Your teacher's face."

"And you're not angry?"

He considered this with the grave slowness he brought to everything, and Yara braced herself for mercy or fury, and got neither.

"I am sad," he said finally. "Anger is for things that can be fought. She is ash. Aldric is not ash yet." Something moved behind his eyes then, something he folded away before she could read it. "The stone asks its price now. You knew this. You have known since the pass above."

So he had seen her avoiding it. Of course he had.

* * *

The rite was simple, as the deep things always are. She knelt at the root of the great stone with her palms against it and spoke the opening words of the Old Tongue—her accent still poor, Vael's patient corrections still ringing in her ears—and the stone opened her the way the first one had, except this time it did not take. This time it *showed*.

Her memories rose around her like fish in a dark pool, each one faintly luminous, each one hers. Her mother's face—no. That was gone; where it had been there was only a soft absence, a scar of light. But around that absence the pool teemed: the smugglers' campfires of her childhood, Brannoc's laugh, the taste of stolen plums, the first knife Ilvane put in her hand, the tower, the sleet, the knock at the door, the boy humming a lullaby in a dead tongue.

The stone waited. Courteous. Horrible.

The grimoire had been clear: the Hollow Root required a memory freely chosen, and the choice itself was part of the payment. A memory given unwillingly bought nothing. A memory surrendered knowingly, named, weighed, released—it bought a great deal. The stone did not want her suffering. It wanted her *consent*. That was the whole theology of the covenant, she was beginning to understand: the old magic ran on what people gave, not what was seized. Which was exactly why Aldric's thefts had curdled it, and exactly why Ilvane's sale had broken it beyond mere damage. Every taking weakened the web. Only giving strengthened it.

She could give something small. A day. The taste of plums. Brannoc's laugh—though after the ambush, after the betrayal, that laugh had already soured on its own; giving it away would cost almost nothing, and the stone would know it, and the rite would run thin.

Or she could give something that mattered.

She thought of Ilvane. All of her. The lessons, the scoldings, the rare devastating warmth, the lullaby—the lullaby that had brought Vael to her door, that had been the first thread between them. If she gave Ilvane entirely, she would stand in this cavern having forgotten the one person who had ever chosen her, and she would not even know what she had lost. She would be a woman with a hole shaped like a mother in the middle of her, and no name for the hole.

But if she kept Ilvane—if she paid with small coin—the stone might fail, the way the first one had barely held, the way the covenant was failing everywhere, thread by thread. And Vael would die for it. Vael, or the cities, or the mountain folk gathering somewhere behind them in the dark like a tide coming in.

"You can choose a small one," Vael said quietly. He was watching the luminous pool of her memories too—he could see them, she realized; the stone let the witness see. "I would not blame you. No one should have to—" He stopped, started again. "The first stone took your mother. To lose the other one also—"

"That's exactly why I have to choose it myself." She heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. "Don't you see? If I pay with scraps, I'm doing what she did. Trading small truths for safety. Selling the covenant by the ounce." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Gods. She trained me for this my whole life and I never noticed. Everything she taught me—hedge-craft, the grimoire, the trails—she was building the person who'd come down here and clean up her sin."

"Not clean," Vael said. "*Pay.* There is a word—" he said it in the Old Tongue, a word with roots in both *debt* and *gift*, a word that meant a debt paid so fully it became a gift. "You are not cleaning. You are paying forward."

"Same direction, different words."

"Different words," he agreed softly, "mean different things."

She looked into the pool a long time. Then, deliberately, the way you lift a hot iron knowing it will brand you, she reached in and gathered Ilvane to her—not one memory but the whole bright shoal of her, every lesson and lullaby and long winter evening, and she held it all in her mind's hands and looked at it one final time.

*I forgive you,* she thought, not sure if she meant it, not sure it mattered, sure only that it needed saying. *And I'm paying your debt. Watch, wherever you are, and learn what it costs.*

"I give this freely," she said in the Old Tongue, stumbling only once. "The woman who taught me. All of her. For the stone. For the covenant. For him."

She let go.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the cruelest part. The memories simply drained out of her like water through open fingers, and the luminous shoal that was Ilvane dimmed, thinned, and went out, and the pool closed over the place where she had been, smooth as a healed wound.

The root blazed.

Light roared up through the petrized wood, vein after vein igniting, racing up the trunk and out along the stone branches until the whole cavern vault burned white, and Yara heard—or felt, or became—a sound like a great held breath releasing after forty years. Somewhere far above, she knew, snow was sliding off peaks that had held it too long. Somewhere, water was finding old channels. The second stone lived.

And Yara Marchetti knelt before it, weeping, and did not know why.

That was the worst of it—not the loss but the *evidence* of loss, tears with no story attached, grief with no face. She wiped her cheeks and found Vael watching her with an expression she couldn't parse, and she said, "Why am I crying?" and heard how strange her voice sounded asking it.

"You gave something," he said carefully. "Something large."

"Did I? What?"

His jaw worked. Whatever answer he had, he swallowed it. "Something that was yours to give," he said. "That is all the stone will tell either of us."

He helped her up. Her legs shook. The grimoire strap bit familiarly into her shoulder, and she adjusted it without thinking, and did not know whose handwriting filled its pages, only that it was important, only that she must protect it, only that some absent hand had once rested on her head in blessing and the shape of that blessing remained after the hand was gone.

Above them, muffled by a hundred yards of living rock, the wind changed. It came down the mountain smelling of woodsmoke and horses—many horses—and under it, faint but unmistakable, a horn call in a pattern Yara's body recognized before her mind caught up: three notes, repeated. Serane's hunters. Signaling to each other. Signaling that the quarry was located.

Vael's head came up sharply, the light of the awakened stone reflected in his deep-water eyes.

"They've found the hollow," he said. "We cannot go back the way we came."

Yara looked at the blazing root, at the dark passages branching away from the cavern floor—passages that had not been there an hour ago, passages the mountain seemed to be offering—and felt the strange new hollowness inside her where a life used to be.

"Then we don't go back," she said. "We go through."

The mountain, it seemed, had been waiting for exactly those words. On the far side of the cavern, a passage opened onto a stair going down and eastward, and from somewhere along it came a draft of air that smelled not of stone but of pine and rain and, very faintly, of woodsmoke from a hearth where someone was waiting.

The grimoire, when her hand found it, fell open on its own to a page she had never read, in that handwriting she no longer knew, and the first line said:

*When the second stone wakes, go down to the valley and ask the free cities for aid, though they will refuse you. Ask anyway. Let their refusal teach you whom to trust.*

Yara read it twice, feeling the words land on ground that had been cleared of everything else, and took the stair.
