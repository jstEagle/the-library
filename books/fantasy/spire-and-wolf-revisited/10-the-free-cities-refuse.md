## 10. The Free Cities Refuse

*To the Council of Vellamare, from Yara Marchetti, formerly of the Thornfell watchtowers, by hand and by peril delivered:*

*I write to you because no one else will.*

*Three weeks ago I stood at the first waystone of Ashfall Pass and watched it wake after sixty years of sleep. I have seen the Old Tongue spoken by a living mouth. I have felt the covenant move beneath my feet like a heartbeat under snow. The mountains are waking, honored councilors, and King Aldric's witch-hunters are already in the high passes burning what they find. When the last stone fails—and it is failing—the roads through Thornfell open to his host, and his host will not stop at your walls out of courtesy.*

*I ask for men, for grain, for arms, for anything. I ask even only that you believe me.*

*Come to Ashfall Pass. See the stone burn blue. Then decide.*

*—Y.M.*

The letter arrived in Vellamare on a morning of gulls and fish-stink, carried up from the harbor by a courier whose boots were still crusted with mountain mud. It was read aloud in the Marble Hall before the full Council of Nine, in a voice that made the words sound smaller than they were, the way a bell sounds smaller when you ring it underwater.

Lord Chancellor Edevane let the silence sit for exactly as long as dignity required.

"A child's tale," he said at last, folding his hands upon the armrests of his chair like a man closing a ledger. "Waystones. Covenant-blood. A boy who speaks a dead tongue." He smiled the thin smile he kept for bad harvests and worse petitions. "I have heard this story before. My nursemaid told it with better pacing."

"Serane's hunters *are* in the passes," said Lady Corvane, who was old enough to remember when Aldric had been merely a general and not yet a king. "That much is true."

"Serane burns smugglers and hedge-witches because Aldric tells her to," Edevane replied. "It keeps her busy and keeps the border provinces frightened, and frightened provinces pay their taxes promptly. As for this Marchetti woman—" he tapped the letter once, delicately, as one taps an insect, "—she asks us to arm a rebellion against an anointed king on the strength of a glowing rock. Gentlemen. Ladies. We are merchants. Let us behave like merchants."

What he proposed instead was written down that same afternoon in the Council's own careful hand: an embassy to Aldric's court bearing silver, shipping rights, and a treaty of non-aggression sealed with enough gold to make war against Vellamare more expensive than peace. The free cities had bought off conquerors before. It was, Edevane observed, considerably cheaper than dying for a myth.

Only Lady Corvane voted against it, and she did so quietly, looking out the tall windows toward the mountains she could just see on clear days, blue and far away, like something remembered rather than seen.

She did not say what she was thinking, which was this: *my grandmother sang a lullaby in a tongue no one could translate, and swore it came from the Spire-folk, and swore the stones were real.* She did not say it because she was eighty-one years old and had learned, across eight decades of councils, that belief is a currency that buys nothing in a room full of merchants.

But she kept the letter. She folded it small and hid it in her sleeve, and that night she wrote in the margin, in a shaking hand:

*If the girl is lying, no harm done. If she is not, may whatever gods remain forgive us the speed of our cowardice.*

* * *

*To the Merchant-Prince of Corveth, greetings, from one who has walked the Thornfell road and seen what walks behind her:*

*You will have heard rumors. Let me tell you what I saw with my own eyes. A waystone at Ashfall Pass, broken for sixty years, standing whole again and burning blue as glacier ice. A boy of perhaps ten years whose blood, spilled on granite, woke carvings dead since before your city had walls. Witch-hunters under the Inquisitor Serane burning villages in the high country for sheltering "covenant-blood"—her word, not mine, and she has writs signed by Aldric's own seal.*

*The covenant held the mountain passes closed to conquest for four hundred years. Your grandfathers' grandfathers slept safe beneath it without ever knowing its name. It is breaking. I am trying to mend it. I cannot do it alone, and I am being hunted while I try.*

*Send aid. Send soldiers. Send bread. Or send nothing, and read this letter again in five years, when Aldric's banners come down the Thornfell road, and understand that I warned you and you chose your ledgers over your lives.*

*—Yara Marchetti*

In Corveth, the letter was not read aloud at all.

The Merchant-Prince, a soft-spoken man named Halveric who had risen from nothing by never once mistaking sentiment for profit, read it alone in his counting-house, twice, then set it beneath a paperweight of green glass and summoned his factor for the northern routes.

"What do we know of this woman?" he asked.

"Smuggler's brat, by the reports. Ran with Brannoc's crew until recently. Now she's gone up into the high country with a boy nobody can account for, and Serane wants them both very badly." The factor hesitated. "There's also this. Our people in the valley towns say the mountain folk have started leaving offerings at the old stones again. Milk and honey, that sort of thing. First time in living memory."

Halveric considered this. Outside his window, Corveth glittered—thirty thousand roofs, the richest harbor on the western coast, every tile of it purchased by the simple discipline of never betting on miracles.

"Offerings," he said. "Superstition follows fear the way gulls follow the fishing boats. And fear follows Serane." He drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself. "We will send the Marchetti woman a courteous reply and a purse of twenty crowns, which is more than her story deserves and less than it would cost us if we took it seriously. And we will send Aldric's chamberlain a gift of Corveth steel, finest blades, with our compliments and our hopes for a long and prosperous reign."

"And if she's telling the truth?"

Halveric looked at the factor for a long moment. "Then in five years," he said, "we will be trading with whoever holds these walls, whoever they are. Cities endure, Master Fenwick. That is what cities are *for*. Kings come and go. Mountains—" he almost smiled, "—mountains are someone else's problem. They always have been."

He wrote the reply himself, in his beautiful merchant's hand:

*Mistress Marchetti—Corveth thanks you for your vigilance and commends your courage. Be assured your report has received the fullest attention. Enclosed find a modest token of the city's regard. We remain, as ever, devoted to the peace and prosperity of all the realm's peoples.—H.*

Twenty crowns. He weighed the purse in his palm before sealing it, and found it satisfyingly heavy, the exact weight of a conscience salved at market rate.

* * *

Ostmere did not even receive a letter, because there was no one left to carry one.

The easternmost of the free cities, Ostmere sat closest to the Thornfell passes, close enough that its watchtowers could see the high snowline on winter mornings. It was also the poorest, having been squeezed between Aldric's tariffs and the mountain tolls for a decade, and its council met now in a hall with a leaking roof, nine gray men and women arguing about grain quotas while the world ended politely around them.

Yara's petition reached them secondhand—a copy, transcribed by a sympathetic friar, passed along the pilgrim road. The council read it by candlelight because the city could no longer afford lamp-oil in quantity.

"It's true," said Councilor Maren, who had a brother-in-law in the valley towns. "All of it. The stones, the hunters, the burning. My wife's cousin saw Ashfall Pass stone lit up like a star fell and stuck. Half the valley's talking about it."

"Half the valley also believes in river-spirits and red-haired ghosts," said Councilor Doss. "What Maren says changes nothing. Even if the covenant is real—even granting the whole fairy tale—what would you have us do? Ostmere fields four hundred spears on a good year. Aldric brings ten thousand. You want to spend our four hundred on a *mountain*?"

"I want to spend them on something," Maren said bitterly. "Because if the girl's right, spending them on our own walls later won't help. The passes are the walls. They've always been the walls. We just forgot because someone else's blood paid for them."

"Whose blood?" asked old Councilor Ashe, who was nearly deaf and missed nothing.

No one answered him.

In the end Ostmere did what poor cities do: it barred its gates—not against Aldric, whom it could not hope to bar against, but against refugees, against rumor, against the girl herself should she come. A proclamation was nailed to the south gate in letters a child could read:

*By order of the Council: the city of Ostmere is closed to all travelers from the mountain provinces until further notice. No aid will be given to persons bearing petitions of any kind. This for the health and safety of the citizenry.*

A tinker's boy read it aloud to a knot of drovers gathered below, and an old woman in the crowd spat, and said, "Closed to petitions. Closed to beggars. Not closed to Aldric, though, eh? Not closed to *him*." And the drovers laughed, because it was either laugh or weep, and laughing was cheaper.

That night, above the gate, the wind came down off the Thornfell snows carrying a smell none of them could name—cold stone and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. The old woman looked up at the dark mass of the mountains and crossed herself with the wrong hand, the old way, the way her grandmother had taught her, and went inside, and barred a door that would not matter.

* * *

Yara learned of the refusals the way she learned most things now: slowly, painfully, and from strangers.

The first answer came at a crossroads shrine two days' walk below Ashfall Pass, where a peddler pressed a sealed letter into her hand and vanished into the fog without waiting for payment. She broke the wax by the shrine's cold flame and read Corveth's beautiful, empty courtesies twice. Twenty crowns spilled into her palm when she turned the envelope over, each coin stamped with the Merchant-Prince's smiling profile.

Vael watched her face from beneath the hood of the cloak she'd cut down for him. His speech had grown surer since the first stone woke—words coming to him now in clusters rather than single drops—but he knew the shape of grief in any language.

"They said no," he said. Not a question.

"They said *thank you*," Yara said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Which is worse. A no you can hate. A thank-you just sits there." She weighed the purse in her hand. Twenty crowns. She thought of the keeper's glade, of the forest-keeper's grave they'd had no time to dig properly, of Serane's writs fluttering behind them like crows. Twenty crowns was the price of a conscience in Corveth, and business was good, because everyone in the free cities was buying.

The second refusal needed no letter. They saw it from the ridge above the pilgrim road at dusk: Ostmere's gates shut, torches on the walls, and the proclamation nailed up where the last light caught it. Vael read the large letters haltingly, sounding them out—he had learned his letters from Yara, from her mistress's grimoire, in stolen hours by fires—and when he finished, he was quiet for a long time.

"*Closed,*" he said finally. "The cities were built inside the covenant's shadow. Four hundred years the stones kept their roads safe, their harbors full, their children unafraid. And they never learned the words. Never even learned the *name*." He looked at his own hands, at the faint blue tracery the restored stones had begun to raise along his veins, visible now whenever he was angry or afraid. "My people died holding a door they never knew existed."

Yara pulled the hood lower over his hair. Below them, the valley towns of the mountain province glimmered with their small honest lights—the only places, she realized, that had believed her. Farmers. Drovers. An old woman at a gate who had crossed herself the old way. The free cities had refused her with committees and seals; the mountain folk had answered with milk and honey left on cold stones.

"Let them buy their treaty," she said, and heard how hard her voice had become, how much of the girl who'd knocked on doors was gone from it. "Brannoc sold us. Corveth bought itself. Ostmere locked its gates against beggars. Fine. We stopped needing anyone the day the first stone lit." She turned up the trail, toward the high country, toward the second waystone sleeping somewhere beneath the roots of the world. "We'll reforge it ourselves. Me and you and whatever's left of the grimoire. And when Aldric comes down out of the passes someday—if he comes—they can read about it in their ledgers."

Vael followed her. But at the bend of the trail he stopped, once, and looked back at the free cities' distant glow, and said something in the Old Tongue, softly, the way one speaks over graves.

Yara didn't ask him to translate. Some part of her, the part that still remembered her mistress singing lullabies in a tongue no map recorded, understood it anyway—or would have, if she'd let herself.

*The door was never ours to keep,* he had said. *But it was theirs to lose.*

They climbed. Behind them the valley lights burned on, warm and blind, and ahead the mountain waited with its second secret, and its price, still hidden in the dark beneath the roots.

And far to the east, in a tent lit by a stolen shard of Spire-glass, King Aldric read his ambassadors' letters from Vellamare and Corveth, and smiled, and set them aside, and gave Serane new orders: *the girl, the boy, and the stones—all three, before the moon turns. The cities have already surrendered. They simply don't know it yet.*
