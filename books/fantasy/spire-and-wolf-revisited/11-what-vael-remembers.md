## 11. What Vael Remembers

The words came back to him the way water comes back to a dry riverbed—not all at once, but finding its old channels, widening them, wearing them deeper with every passing day.

Vael noticed it first in his hands.

They had been clumsy things since the night he knocked on Yara's door, these human-shaped hands, all knuckle and fumble, unable to hold a spoon without spilling or a thought without dropping it. But now, as he sat cross-legged before the hearth of the forest-keeper's glade, turning a smooth river stone over and over, the stone seemed to *listen* to him. It warmed where his fingers pressed. When he whispered to it in the old tongue—*silvren, silvren, be still, be still*—it grew quiet in a way stones were not supposed to grow quiet, settling into his palm like a sleeping bird.

He looked up. Yara was across the room, hunched over her mistress's grimoire by candlelight, mouthing the syllables he had taught her that morning. Her brow was furrowed. She held her lips wrong for the deep vowels—the tongue was not made for human mouths, it had been made for throats like wind through high places—but she persisted, and there was something in her persistence that made his chest ache in a way he did not yet have words for. He had words now for many things. That was the trouble.

*Yara*, he thought, testing the name against everything he was remembering. *Yara, who feeds me first and eats last. Yara, who flinches when she thinks of her mother's face and does not know I saw what the stone took. Yara, who is teaching me to be a person again, one word at a time.*

The keeper's glade smelled of pine resin and drying herbs, and outside the snow came down soft and endless, muffling the world until it felt as though they were the only three living things beneath the sky. The old forest-keeper, Maerlin, dozed in her chair by the fire, her breath whistling through ancient teeth. Safe, this place felt safe. Vael knew better than to trust the feeling. Safety was a coat that never quite fit him; somewhere beneath it, the cold shape of what he was waited patiently to be put on again.

He closed his eyes and let the memory come.

* * *

It had been waiting for him since the second waystone. The Hollow Root had poured itself into his blood along with Yara's memory, and ever since, the past rose in him at odd hours—in dreams, in the space between waking and sleep, in the moment after Maerlin said a certain word and before the fire popped. He could no more stop it than he could stop his own heartbeat. And so, in the blue hour before dawn, while Yara slept curled like a comma in the corner bed, Vael lay awake and *remembered*.

He remembered the Spire.

Not as the grimoire's diagrams showed it—a tapering needle of white stone crowned with light—but from inside. From below. He remembered being small enough to ride on his mother's shoulder, her hand steadying his back, the whole city of the Spire-folk rising around them in terraces of singing glass. The Spire-folk had not built their tower to touch heaven. They had built it to *hold* heaven—to anchor the covenant, the great weaving of mountain magic that kept the lowlands free and the free cities free and the wolves unchained and the rivers running sweet. Every Spire-child learned the covenant's first verse before they learned to walk:

*We are the hinge. We are the door. What we hold open, none may close; what we hold closed, none may open.*

His mother had sung it as a lullaby. That was the terrible joke of it, Vael thought now, staring at the ceiling beams. The most powerful working in the world, the seal on which an entire age of peace had rested, and they had set it to a tune you could hum a child to sleep with. He had hummed it himself, once, half-frozen on a stranger's doorstep in a sleet storm, because it was the only thing left in his head that was wholly his.

And then he remembered how it ended.

Fire first—or what passed for fire among things that hunted Spire-folk, a light that ate light, pouring down the terraces of singing glass. His mother's shoulder under his cheek going rigid. Her voice, no longer a lullaby: *Run down, little one, run down and out, do not look back, do not sing, be nothing, be nobody, be a human child if you can manage it.* Hands pushing him into a smuggler's cart, into straw that stank of pig and tar. And through the slats, because he had disobeyed, because he was small and foolish and four years old—he had looked back.

He had seen the Spire go dark from the top downward, floor by floor, like a candle being pinched out section by section. He had seen figures in the light-that-ate-light moving through the terraces with a writ sealed in black wax. And he had heard—this was the part that lived behind his eyes like a splinter, the part that woke him some nights with his own scream caught in his throat—he had heard the covenant *tear*. Not a sound. A silence. A hole in the world's music, right where the hinge had been.

After that: years of fog. Years of being passed between smugglers who spoke around him in clipped trade-tongue, of learning to be small and silent and strange, of forgetting on purpose because remembering hurt too much and made him do things—made stones tremble, made dogs howl, made superstitious men reach for knives. Forgetting had been survival. Forgetting had been mercy.

Now the stones were giving it back to him, and he understood why the old ones had called memory a burden you carried so others would not have to.

* * *

"Your mouth is doing it wrong again."

Yara glanced up from the grimoire, affronted. "I'm saying it exactly as you said it."

"You are saying *vael*—" he tapped his own chest, "—when you mean *va-el*. Two hearts in one word. You are swallowing the second heart."

"There's only one of you," she said dryly. "One heart seems sufficient."

But she tried again, and this time she got it, and the word came out of her mouth rounded and warm and true, and Vael felt it land somewhere beneath his ribs like a struck bell. *Va-el.* Beloved-of-the-door. The name his mother had given him because every Spire-name was a small covenant, a promise folded into sound. He had not known that until three days ago. Now he could not un-know it, and every time Yara said his name correctly—which was often now, deliberately often, he suspected, just to watch him wince at her accent—some quiet part of him broke and healed at once.

"You're getting stronger," Yara said. It was not a question. She had been watching him the way she watched weather. "Since the second stone. You stood in the doorway yesterday and the frost on the glass melted in a circle around your hand."

"I am sorry. I will stand elsewhere."

"That's not what I—" She exhaled, set down the grimoire. "Vael. When the covenant is reforged. At the third stone. What happens to you?"

The question hung in the smoky air of the glade. Outside, the snow went on falling. Maerlin snored gently. The fire popped.

And here it was, Vael thought. Here was the moment. The words were in him now, mature and clear, the Old Tongue and the trade-tongue both, and he could feel the truth sitting in his chest like a stone he had swallowed, heavy and smooth and waiting to be given up. *The third waystone demands a Spire heir,* he could say. *Willing blood, willing sacrifice. Either I die, or I become the new Spire—stone and silence and covenant forever, a hinge that thinks but does not live, does not laugh, does not learn new words, does not sit across from Yara while she mangles vowels and secretly loves it.*

He opened his mouth.

"I don't know," he said.

* * *

The lie sat in him afterward like a swallowed coal.

He had never lied to Yara. In the beginning he had been incapable of it—not from virtue but from poverty, lacking the words to construct anything but plain truth. And later, when the words came, he had found he did not want to. Lying to Yara felt like lying to the person who had pulled him out of a river. But he lay awake that night, listening to her breathe, and told himself the lie was a kindness, and knew even as he told himself this that it was also a wall.

Because he had read further in the grimoire than he had let her see. Because the Hollow Root had shown him visions it had not shown her—it had recognized its maker's blood in him, and it had been generous where it was cruel to her. He knew the ritual's full architecture now, knew it the way you know a house you grew up in: the three stones were anchors, yes, but the third was different. The third was not an anchor. The third was a *keystone*, and a keystone must be shaped from living material, and the only living material of the right kind left in all the world was sleeping ten feet away from him, breathing slow, dreaming whatever humans dreamed.

*Either the heir gives his life,* the old verses ran, *or the heir gives his selfhood, and becomes the door that others pass through.*

There was a third option. There was always a third option; the Spire-folk had been engineers of covenants, and every structure had its tolerances, its hidden load-bearing paths. But the third option required something he did not have and could not manufacture: another will, freely offered, bound to his by love. The verses were explicit and cold about it. *No heir may substitute another's sacrifice, nor ask it, nor hint toward it. The door must choose its own opening.* To tell Yara the truth would be to ask. Even to let her guess might poison the working, turn freely-given into guilt-driven, and guilt-driven magic failed, or worse, it *held*—held wrongly, crookedly, a covenant built on coercion, which was precisely the thing King Aldric had broken the old one to escape.

So Vael lay in the dark and did the arithmetic of love.

If he told her nothing, she would grieve him. She would rage at the stones, at the mistress whose grimoire had led them here, at the world. But she would live. She would walk back down the mountain with her memories spent and her name intact, and someday, perhaps, she would teach hedge-craft to some other runaway apprentice, and she would say his name correctly, two hearts in one word, and keep a small part of him alive that way.

If he told her everything—

He turned the possibility over and found he could not finish the thought. It was not that he feared she would refuse. It was that he feared, with a certainty that felt like memory itself, that she would not.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the frosted window. Vael rose before Yara, as he always did now, and went out into the white silence of the glade to practice.

Maerlin found him there, standing among the pines with his bare feet sunk in snow that steamed faintly around his ankles. The old keeper lowered herself onto a stump with the groaning deliberateness of ancient wood and watched him work: he was coaxing a dead branch, fallen in autumn, to remember spring. Buds swelled along its length, tentative, green-veined.

"The stones are feeding you," she said. It was not a question either. Everyone in this story asked questions they already knew the answers to, Vael had noticed. Perhaps that was what wisdom was.

"They are giving back what was taken."

"Mm." Maerlin's eyes, pale as skimmed milk, settled on him. "My grandmother's grandmother saw the Spire burn. Told it to me as a child, the way you tell children terrible things so they'll recognize them later. Said the last heir was carried out in a smuggler's cart no bigger than a coffin." She paused. "Said the heir was four years old."

Vael let the branch go. It stood in the snow, impossibly budding, a small green impossibility between them.

"You know what the third stone asks," Maerlin said.

"I know what it asks."

"And you've told the girl."

Vael said nothing. The silence stretched, and Maerlin studied him with those milk-pale eyes, and whatever she saw there made her sigh—a long, rattling sound, like wind through a hollow tree.

"My husband was a soldier," she said at last. "Died at Greywater Ford, thirty years back, holding a bridge he had no orders to hold. I've had fifty years to decide whether knowing he'd do that would have been worse than not knowing." She pushed herself up off the stump, joints crackling like green wood in fire. "I still haven't decided. But I'll tell you what I decided about myself: I decided that people get to choose their griefs. Take that choice away from someone who loves you, boy, and you haven't protected them. You've only made certain they grieve alone."

She shuffled back toward the cabin, leaving him alone with the budding branch and the falling snow.

Vael stood a long time in the cold. The truth was a door, Maerlin had essentially said, and doors were his inheritance, his name, his whole cursed lineage—and here he was, last of the door-makers, nailing his own shut.

But the verses were clear. The verses were *cold*. And the covenant could not be rebuilt on a coerced foundation, and Yara's love, offered freely as it was, would curdle the instant it learned the price—

Wouldn't it?

He thought of her face in the firelight, mouthing *va-el*, getting it wrong, getting it right. He thought of the way she had screamed when the first stone took her mother's face, and how she had paid anyway, and paid again at the Hollow Root, choosing her losses like a woman rationing her own heart. Yara did not flinch from prices. Yara had never once flinched from a price. Which meant—

Which meant the cruelest thing he could believe about her was that she was weak, and he did not believe it, and that was exactly why he could not risk the asking.

The snow thickened. Far down the valley, so far it might have been imagination, a thread of smoke rose against the grey—too straight, too disciplined, too deliberate to be any hearth-fire. Witch-hunter smoke. Serane was climbing.

Vael looked at it for a long moment, and then he turned and went inside, and began, quietly, to pack.

When Yara woke, he was already dressed, the grimoire wrapped in oilcloth, the route to the third waystone marked in charcoal on a scrap of hide. She blinked at him, sleep-rumpled, and asked what had gotten into him.

"Serane is in the valley," he said. "We leave within the hour."

He handed her the map. Their fingers touched over it, hers chapped and strong, his faintly luminous, and neither of them mentioned it.

And when she turned away to gather her things, Vael looked at the back of her head—at the person he had chosen to lie to, the person he was choosing to die for—and added, silently, in the Old Tongue, in the language of doors and hinges and things held open:

*I'm sorry. Watch me, and don't believe me. Please don't believe me.*

Down in the valley, the smoke multiplied.
