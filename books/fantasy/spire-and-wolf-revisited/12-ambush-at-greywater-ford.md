## 12. Ambush at Greywater Ford

The ford announced itself long before they saw it, the way grief does.

First there was the sound—the river running wide and shallow over grey stones, a sound like a thousand small voices all saying the same word in different tongues. Then the smell: cold water and wet iron, willow-bark and mud. And last, when they came down through the alder scrub with the morning fog still clinging to their knees, the sight of it—Greywater Ford, half a mile of braided channels and gravel bars, spanned at its narrowest point by an old smugglers' bridge that Brannoc's people had built from stolen timber and older lies.

Yara crouched at the treeline and did not move for a long time.

Vael crouched beside her, mirroring her stillness the way he had learned to mirror everything she did, as if her body were a text he was teaching himself to read. He had changed since the Hollow Root. The restored waystone's power ran in him now like light through water; his eyes caught the grey morning and gave it back faintly silvered, and where his bare feet pressed the frost, the ground remembered spring for a heartbeat before winter took it again. He could not hide what he was anymore. Neither of them could pretend he was anything but a beacon.

*They will see you coming,* the stone under the mountain had shown them. *You are a lamp walking through a dark country.*

"We cross here or we don't cross," Yara whispered. "There's no other bridge for thirty miles, and the high passes are already snowed shut. Serane knows it too. If she's going to corner us anywhere—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Vael's hand found her sleeve, and his fingers were cold as the river would be.

He spoke in the Old Tongue first—it came to him more easily than their shared trade-speech, especially when he was afraid—and then caught himself, and translated, word by careful word, the way she had taught him and he had taught her, both of them building a bridge between languages the way other people built bridges between shores.

*"Water remembers,"* he said. *"My people said that. Water remembers everything that crosses it."*

"Then let's hope it forgets us by sundown."

She checked the straps on her pack out of habit rather than need. They had almost nothing left to strap down. After Brannoc's betrayal at the ash camps—after the ambush she had crawled out of with a knife-wound in her side that had only just stopped weeping—they had lost the supplies, the maps, the oilcloth-wrapped grimoire pages she'd spent three years copying. What remained was Vael, the second waystone's mark burning faintly on her palm where the ritual had branded her, and a stolen hunting bow with six arrows, two of them hers.

Six arrows against whatever Serane brought.

She thought of the forest-keeper's face in the moment before the glade burned. She thought of the keeper's hands, gentle with bees and bitter herbs, and how quickly fire made nothing of gentleness. Grief had stopped being a wave that passed through her and become something structural, load-bearing, a beam she walked across every day because the alternative was falling.

"Stay behind me at the water," she said. "If I say *run*, you run upstream along the bank and don't look back. Not once. Promise me."

Vael looked at her with those silvered eyes, and for a moment she saw him weighing the promise the way he weighed all human words now—testing each one for the hollow places where lies lived. He had learned about lies from watching humans make them. He had learned them from Brannoc's crew, from the free cities' council clerks, from her own careful omissions.

"I promise," he said, "that I will run if you say run." A pause, precise as a knife-point. "I do not promise to not look back."

It was so exactly, stubbornly *him* that Yara laughed—a short, cracked sound, surprising them both. Then she kissed the top of his head, the way her mistress used to kiss hers, in the time before she knew what her mistress had traded away, and stood up out of the ferns.

* * *

The crossing began well, which should have warned her.

The fog was lifting off the braided channels in slow rags, and the water came up only to their shins at the first bar, shockingly cold, pulling at them with the patient strength of something that had all the time in the world. Vael moved through it easily, barefoot, unbothered, and Yara wondered—not for the first time—if the old covenant ran in the river itself, if every stream off the Thornfell massif carried some thin thread of the Spire-folk's making. The water seemed to lean toward him. Seemed to recognize him.

They were halfway across the widest channel, the smugglers' bridge looming ahead on its rotting pilings, when the fog tore open on the far bank and showed her the trap.

Witch-hunters. A dozen of them at least, in Aldric's grey-and-crimson, spread in a crescent around the near end of the bridge, crossbows levelled, and behind them, on a black horse that stood in the shallows like something carved rather than bred, Inquisitor Serane herself.

Yara froze mid-river. The current pushed at her back, insistent, indifferent.

"There she is," Serane called, and her voice carried across the water with terrible clarity, pitched the way a bell is pitched, to be heard and obeyed. "Yara Marchetti. Apprentice-thief. Covenant-blood's keeper." She said the words like charges being read. "And the boy. Come out of the water, child. Both of you. The King's writ extends to the far bank and no further—I am authorized to take you alive, which is more mercy than my writ requires. Walk to me and the boy dies painlessly. Run, and I cannot promise what my crossbows promise."

*The boy dies painlessly.* As if death were a coin she could mint and spend. As if Vael were a debt to be settled.

Yara's hand found Vael's shoulder and turned him, slowly, so that her body was between him and the crescent of crossbows. The water swirled around her thighs. Her wounded side throbbed in time with her pulse.

"You've read the wrong book," she called back. "The covenant doesn't die with him. Kill him and the mountains wake up angry, and your king's precious armies learn what the Thornfell did to the last man who tried to take it."

Something flickered across Serane's face—not doubt, nothing so useful as doubt, but calculation. Yara watched her weigh it, watch her decide, with the cold arithmetic Yara had come to know in the space of one breath, that the risk was acceptable.

"Loose on my mark," Serane said quietly. "Aim for the woman."

And that—that was the mistake. That was the hinge on which everything after turned. Because a voice from the bridge above them, rough as gravel and shaking in a way Yara had never heard it shake, shouted:

"*No.*"

Brannoc stood at the near end of the smugglers' bridge with a crossbow of his own, and he was not aiming it at them.

* * *

Later—much later, in the years when she told this story and the years when she refused to—Yara would try to understand what she saw in those next few minutes, and she never fully would. But this is what happened:

Brannoc had sold them once. He had taken Serane's gold for their route, their habits, their weaknesses, and he had watched his own people ambush the girl he'd fed from her ninth winter, and he had told himself it was business, the way he told himself everything was business, because Brannoc without the story of business was just an old man who had done an unforgivable thing.

But he had also, in the ambush's aftermath, gone through Yara's abandoned pack looking for valuables—and found, tucked beneath the false bottom where she kept her most dangerous papers, a letter. Her mistress's letter. The confession. The map to the third waystone. And Brannoc, who could barely read, had carried it anyway, had asked a fence in Lowmark to read it to him twice, had heard the words *covenant* and *Spire* and *the price may be paid by any who love the heir*, and had understood, in the slow grinding way that guilt understands things, that he had sold not a runaway girl but the last door standing between the world and whatever Aldric meant to do with it.

So he had followed them. Alone, on foot, three days behind, an old smuggler with bad knees chasing two children through the high country, telling himself the whole way that he'd decide what to do when he got there.

He decided at Greywater Ford.

"No," he said again, louder, and stepped out onto the bridge, into the open, between the crossbows and the children in the water. "Not these two, Serane. Find another writ to serve."

One of the witch-hunters lowered his crossbow slightly, confused. "That's Brannoc. That's the informant—"

"I know who I am," Brannoc said, and there was something in his voice Yara had never heard in twenty years of knowing him—not the smuggler's easy charm, not the bargainer's warmth, but something raw underneath, something that had been waiting its whole life for this exact moment and hated it. "I'm the man who sold you the trail. And I'm the man who's telling you the trail ends here."

Serane's horse shifted in the shallows. Her expression did not change at all, which was how Yara knew she was furious.

"Step aside, old man. You're owed payment, not martyrdom."

"I'm owed nothing." Brannoc spat into the water. "That's the whole of it. I'm owed nothing and I paid myself anyway, years ago, out of other people's accounts. This—" he hefted the crossbow, and Yara saw his hands were trembling, "—this is the first honest work I've done since I met the girl's mistress, and she was a liar too, gods rest her, so maybe honesty runs in the trade."

"Loose," Serane said.

The world became noise.

Crossbow strings snapped in a ragged volley, and Brannoc roared—not a scream, a roar, the roar of a man spending everything he had in one place—and threw himself flat across the bridge's narrow throat with his own weapon firing into the crescent of hunters. Two went down. The rest scattered for cover among the bridge pilings and the alder scrub, and Serane's horse screamed and wheeled as a bolt meant for her buried itself in its neck, and the black rider went down into the grey water with a splash that sounded almost gentle.

"RUN!" Brannoc bellowed, and this time the word was for her. "UPSTREAM, GIRL! DON'T YOU DARE LOOK BACK!"

Yara grabbed Vael's wrist and ran.

The river fought them every step—water to their waists now, the current shoving, the gravel rolling underfoot, Vael dragging against the pull with a strength that wasn't entirely human, his free hand cutting the water and *parting* it, the Old Tongue spilling out of him in gasping fragments, and where he spoke, the river listened. Channels bent around them. Currents slackened. The water that remembered everything chose, in that moment, to forget them faster.

Bolts hit the river around them—one close enough that Yara felt it comb her hair—and then the shooting faltered, because Brannoc was still roaring on the bridge, still holding it, still making himself the loudest thing in the world so that every crossbow on that bank had to choose between the fleeing targets and the old man who would not stop.

She looked back.

Of course she looked back. She was Yara Marchetti, and she had promised Vael she wouldn't look back herself, and she broke her own promise in the same breath she demanded he keep his.

Brannoc was on his knees on the bridge planks, a bolt in his shoulder and another in his chest, and he was laughing. Actually laughing, blood on his teeth, waving his empty crossbow at the hunters like a drunk waving at old friends. "Is that all?" he shouted. "Is that the best the usurper's gold can buy? I've been cheated better than this by churchmen!"

Then Serane, on foot now, soaked to the waist, walked past her struggling soldiers, drew her long knife, and ended it.

Yara turned away. Too late, always too late, redemption arriving the way weather arrives—you see it coming for days and it still catches you in the open.

* * *

They came out of the river a mile upstream, both of them bleeding—Yara's knife-wound torn open again, Vael's feet laced with cuts the water had not healed because even Spire-blood has limits—and collapsed in the hollow beneath a leaning birch, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

The river talked instead. It talked and talked, carrying its cargo of memory downstream toward the ford, toward the bridge, toward the body that the hunters would drag off the planks and leave, probably, for the crows, because Serane did not waste burial on traitors.

"He held it," Vael said at last. His trade-speech was thick, clumsy; he reached for the Old Tongue and translated for her anyway, because she needed the words in her own language, needed them shaped like hers. "The bridge. He stood on the wood and he would not move. Why? He sold you. He—" Vael searched for the word, found it, flinched from it. "*Betrayed.* He betrayed you. Betrayal and the bridge. Both true?"

"Both true." Yara pressed her palm hard against her reopened wound and breathed through the pain until she could speak steadily. "Humans are both true, Vael. Most of us, most of the time. We hold bridges we have no right to hold, and we hold them badly, and late, and it counts anyway." Her voice cracked. "Or it doesn't count. I don't know. Ask me in fifty years."

Vael considered this with the grave attention he gave everything now, the restored stones having made him old in ways his body refused to match. Then he did something he had never done. He leaned against her shoulder, deliberately, the full weight of him, the way a person leans—not the way a frightened child leans, but the way family leans, the way you lean on someone whose bones you trust.

"The water remembers him," he said softly. "Greywater remembers. Every river in the Thornfell comes from our mountain. So somewhere in the water, forever, there is a man on a bridge who turned around." A pause. "In the Old Tongue there is a word for that. It means: *late, but arriving.* It is a good word. It is a word we give to heroes."

Yara closed her eyes. Fury and sorrow fused in her chest into something that had no name in either language—a heat that was also a grief, a grief that was also fuel. Brannoc had sold her, and Brannoc had died for her, and both facts sat inside her like stones in a riverbed, unmoving, permanent, worn smooth eventually or not at all.

"We can't stop," she said finally. "She'll sweep the banks. We have to keep moving."

"I know."

"And Vael—" she opened her eyes, and looked at him, and the question came out before she could soften it, the question that had been growing in her since the Hollow Root, since she'd seen him calculating what to tell her and what to withhold. "What happens at the third stone? What aren't you telling me?"

His face did something very small and very controlled. The silvered eyes went still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"When we reach it," he said, "you will understand why I waited."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer I have that is not a lie." He looked away, upriver, toward the white shoulders of the mountain showing through the trees. "Yara. There is a letter. Your mistress wrote a letter, and the smuggler carried it, and it burned with the pack, and I saw it burn, and I have not told you what was in it, because—" his voice caught, the first crack in him she had ever heard, "—because I was afraid that if you knew, you would pay a price I cannot bear to watch you pay."

The river ran on below them, remembering everything, forgetting nothing, carrying one dead man's name down out of the mountains toward the sea.

Yara stared at him. Somewhere behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound of horns—Serane, regrouping, sweeping, patient as water.

"What price," Yara said quietly, "and what letter?"

And Vael, heir of the Spire-folk, last speaker of a dead tongue, looked at her with all the terrible tenderness of someone about to break a promise, and said: "Get up. Walk while I tell you. Because if we live to hear the end of it, you'll want to be moving when you hear it."

They rose together out of the birch-hollow, two wounds and one secret, and climbed.
