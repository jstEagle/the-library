## 13. The Mistress's Last Letter

They buried Brannoc where the river bent, in the grey light before dawn, because Yara could not carry him any farther and would not leave him for the crows.

The ground at Greywater Ford was stony and half-frozen, and her shovel was a broken oar-blade she'd pried from an abandoned skiff. She dug until her palms blistered and split, and Vael knelt beside her without being asked, scooping earth with his bare hands as though it were nothing, as though his fingers were made of the same patient stone as the mountains. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the digging did not say better.

Brannoc had died facing downstream, sword in hand, holding the narrow span of the ford against six of Serane's hunters so that two fugitives could scramble up the willow-choked bank and vanish into the dark. He had turned his coat twice in one life—first selling their route to the Inquisitor, then spending himself to buy back the debt—and Yara had watched him fall from thirty yards away, too far to help, close enough to see the moment the light went out of him. He had been looking upstream when he fell. Looking toward her.

She laid him down in the grave with his sword across his chest, the way smugglers were meant to be laid down, though she suspected no smuggler had ever earned it quite the way he had.

"He was not good," Vael said quietly, in the Old Tongue, and then, laboriously, in Common, testing each word like ice on a pond: "But he was *ours*. At the end."

Yara looked at him. The boy was nine years old by the count of anyone who counted such things, and he had just spoken a eulogy more honest than any priest could manage.

"Yes," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "At the end."

She covered his face last.

* * *

They sheltered that day in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy high above the ford, a low stone cell with a collapsed roof that still left one dry corner, big enough for two people and a fire small enough to hide. Yara's left arm was bound where a crossbow bolt had torn through the meat of her forearm—not deep, but deep enough to make everything clumsy, every movement a negotiation. Vael had washed the wound with melted snow and packed it with moss he'd chewed into poultice, muttering Old Tongue words over it that made the pain recede like a tide going out. His power was stronger now. She could feel it in the air around him, faint and constant, like warmth rising off a sun-baked wall. The second waystone, reforged beneath the Hollow Root, had poured something back into him—or perhaps he had simply grown into what he had always been, the way a river grows into its canyon.

She was grateful for the numbness. It let her think about other things.

Namely: the satchel.

It lay between them now, Brannoc's satchel, oilskin-wrapped and river-stained, which she had cut from his body before burial because leaving it would have felt like leaving part of him unburied. Inside, she expected, would be the usual cargo of a smuggler's life: coin, false papers, a flask of something harsh, maybe letters of credit from fences in three cities. She intended to take what they needed—food, the papers, anything that might get them past a checkpoint—and burn the rest, or leave it, or simply not look at it.

Because there was a knot in her chest when she thought about Brannoc, and the knot had two strands braided together: grief and fury, fury and grief, wound so tight she couldn't tell which was which anymore. He had sold them. He had sat across a fire from her, passed her stew, called her *little wren* the way he had since she was eleven years old and starving on his crew's doorstep—and all the while he'd been counting the price of her head. And then he had died for her anyway, and she did not know what to do with a man like that, a man whose treachery and loyalty lived in the same house and shared the same bed.

"Open it," Vael said. Not a command. An invitation. He had learned, these long weeks, to read her silences the way she had learned to read his.

"I know what's in it."

"You do not." He tilted his head, that birdlike tilt he had, dark eyes catching the firelight. "You are afraid of what is in it. That is different."

She almost laughed. Almost. "When did you get wise?"

"From watching you pretend not to be."

So she opened it.

Coin first, as expected—a leather purse heavy with silver marks stamped with the free cities' faces, and beneath it a bundle of documents: passage writs under three different names, a bill of lading for a shipment that had never existed, a letter of introduction to a fence in Carrowmere signed with a name she recognized from her smuggling days. All of it useful. All of it Brannoc, the professional man, the man who moved goods and people through the world's cracks.

And beneath that, wrapped separately in waxed linen, tied with cord gone soft with age—

A letter.

Not Brannoc's hand. She knew that before she touched it, knew it the way you know a voice through a wall. The wrapping was old, the wax seal broken and resealed a dozen times over the years, the paper inside yellowed and creased along fold-lines worn nearly to threads, as though it had been carried everywhere, taken out, read, put away, read again. Ten years of reading. Fifteen.

Her hands began to shake.

"Yara." Vael's voice, gentle. "Whose—"

"Mine," she said, though it wasn't, not yet. "*Maeve's.*"

* * *

Mistress Maeve Corvane. Dead four years now, drowned in her own bed by a heart that simply stopped, found in the morning by an apprentice who had screamed until the neighbors came. Buried in Thornfell under a stone Yara had carved herself, badly, because grief makes poor chisels of us all. The woman who had bought her off the streets, fed her, taught her letters and hedge-craft and the difference between a warding sigil and a curse. The woman whose grimoire now rode in Yara's pack and had led her up this mountain after a feral boy who sang her lullabies.

The woman who, Yara had learned beneath the Hollow Root, had helped break the covenant. Had traded Spire secrets to Aldric's agents for her own life, decades ago, when the usurper's knives were out and the old order was falling. Yara had stood in the stone-dark with visions burning behind her eyes and understood that everything she loved had been built on a foundation of betrayal—that her rescue, her apprenticeship, her whole saved life, had been purchased with the blood of a murdered people.

She had paid the waystone a memory rather than sit with that knowledge a moment longer. She had chosen what to lose, and lost it, and told herself she was done learning terrible things about Maeve Corvane.

The letter in her hands said otherwise.

"Read it aloud," Vael said. "If you can. If you want. Two ears hold more than one."

So she broke the cord, and unfolded pages gone soft as cloth, and read in the lamplight of a dying fire while sleet ticked against the ruined roof, in a voice that only shook a little:

---

*My Yara,*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you have gone through my things despite my explicit instructions, which I confess I am counting on, because you never once obeyed me when it mattered—or you have taken this from Brannoc, which means he is dead too, and I am sorry for whatever it cost you to learn what he truly was.*

*You will hate me when you finish this letter. I need you to do it anyway. Hate is a door that swings both ways, and on the other side of your hatred is the thing you must know to survive what is coming.*

*I betrayed the Spire-folk. You learned this already—I know you did, because you are reading this, which means you reached the Hollow Root, which means the stones have shown you what they showed me. I traded their secrets to Aldric's men for my life. I told myself I had no choice. This was true and it was also a lie; we always have a choice, we simply choose the ones we can live with, and I chose wrongly, and children burned in the Spire because of what I sold.*

*Here is what the stones will not show you, because stones remember deeds but not intentions: I spent every year afterward trying to unmake what I had done. Every ward I taught you, every hedge-craft, every charm against witch-hunters—all of it was Spire knowledge, salvaged, hidden, seeded into the world's cracks the way a smuggler seeds contraband. I could not restore the covenant. But I could keep its embers alive until someone came who could.*

*I believed for many years that someone would be me. Then I grew old, and cowardly in the specific way the old grow cowardly, and I understood it would have to be you.*

*Now listen. Listen carefully, because I will say this only once, and the ink is the only witness.*

*The third waystone stands at the Weeping Crown, above the Serpent's Stair. I hid its approach from even my own notes, because if Aldric ever learned its location he would garrison it, and if he garrisoned it, all hope dies. The stair begins behind the falls at Greywater's source. You will know the door by the wolf-mark.*

*And here is the secret I paid more than my life to learn, the secret I carried like a stone in my belly for forty years:*

*The ritual demands a memory freely given for each stone. So much is true, and true also is that the final sealing asks a price beyond price—the heir himself, willingly given, transformed or extinguished. The stones showed me this and I despaired of it, and I searched thirty years for a loophole, and I found one, and I paid a witch of the Deepwood everything I owned to confirm it.*

*The covenant does not care whose memory pays. It does not care whose blood seals. What it requires is love—love freely given, love that chooses the other over the self. The heir may pay with himself. But so may any who love him. A memory given for him. A life offered in his place. The magic reads the heart, Yara, not the ledger. It always has. That is why Aldric can never hold it, no matter how much of it he steals—the stolen shard will eat him alive, because there is nothing in him but hunger, and hunger is not love.*

*Do you understand what I am telling you? You do not have to lose the boy. If it comes to the final stone, and it will come to the final stone, there is another way—but it will cost whoever loves him everything they are, and I cannot ask that of you, and so I am not asking. I am only telling you, because you deserve to choose with open eyes, and I robbed you of so many choices already.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Either way, climb the Stair.*

*All my love, which was never enough but was always real,*
*Maeve*

---

The fire had burned down to embers. Yara realized she was crying only when a tear struck the page and bloomed there, spreading slow through the old ink, and she snatched the letter back with a strangled sound, terrified of ruining it, of losing one word of it, pressing it flat against her breastbone with her bandaged arm and rocking forward over it like a woman sheltering a flame from wind.

Vael said nothing. He simply moved closer, and put his small hand on her shoulder, and let her shake.

* * *

Later—much later, when the sleet had thickened to snow and the bothy's single window showed a world erased in white—she read it again. And again. By the fourth reading her tears had stopped and something else had arrived in their place, something quiet and enormous, sitting in her chest like a banked coal.

Hope. Hope kindled inside grief, the way a new green shoot comes up through ash.

*Any who love him.* She turned the phrase over and over. Maeve had spent thirty years searching for a loophole and had found it, had confirmed it, had written it down and hidden it in the keeping of a treacherous smuggler because Brannoc—faithless, venal Brannoc—had been the one person Maeve trusted to survive long enough to pass it on. Had he known what he carried? Had he read it? Had it weighed on him through all those years of moving contraband, all those fires where he'd passed her stew and called her little wren?

She would never know. That was the shape of Brannoc, in the end: a locked room with the key thrown away. He had sold them and saved them and died for them, and the letter in his keeping proved that some thread of the old Maeve's cunning had run through his whole rotten, loyal life, waiting.

"What does it say?" Vael asked softly. "Truly. Not the parts you read aloud. Your face changed at the end. It changed like weather."

Yara looked at him across the ember-light—at this boy who had lost everyone, whose people were ash, whose destiny was a knife pointed at his own heart. He didn't know yet. She was certain of it now, certain in the way you become certain of a thing you've been refusing to see: he had learned at the Hollow Root what the final ritual required, and he had said nothing, and he had been walking toward the Weeping Crown with the calm of someone arranging his own funeral.

He thought he had to die. He thought that was the price, and he had decided, alone, silently, to pay it, because that was what heirs did.

"No," Yara said aloud, to the boy, to the letter, to the dead woman, to the mountain itself. "No, you don't."

Vael blinked. "I don't—what?"

She folded the letter with hands that no longer shook and tucked it inside her shirt, against her skin, where it could not be lost. Outside, the snow kept falling, erasing the road, erasing the blood at the ford, erasing everything except the way forward.

"The third stone," she said, standing, wincing at her arm, feeling the coal in her chest catch and brighten. "Maeve left us a map to it. Behind the falls at Greywater's source. And Vael—" she held out her good hand to him, and he took it, and she pulled him up, "—when we get there, nobody is paying with their life. Not you. Not anyone. I've just read the terms of the contract, and I intend to renegotiate."

Above them, far up the valley, the falls thundered under the snow, and somewhere beyond them the Serpent's Stair waited, coiling up through Aldric's patrol country toward the Weeping Crown—and for the first time since the Hollow Root, Yara Marchetti climbed toward it not as a mourner, but as a woman carrying a weapon no witch-hunter could confiscate: the truth, written in a dead woman's hand, that love was legal tender, and she had more of it than any king alive.
