## 14. Serpent's Stair

The mountain did not want them to climb it quietly.

Yara felt that truth in her bones before she saw a single soldier. The path they had chosen—the old pilgrim road her mistress's maps called the Serpent's Stair—switchbacked up the eastern face of the Thornfell massif like a scar of white stone, exposed to the sky for its whole length, and every step of it was visible to the watchtowers Aldric had planted along the ridgeline. Three days of climbing, three days of open ground, and somewhere above them, the third waystone waited at the Weeping Crown with its price still unpaid.

And Vael glowed.

There was no gentler word for it. Since the Hollow Root, since the second stone had drunk its memory and woken, the boy's skin carried a faint luminance, as if moonlight had gotten under it and could not get out. In shadow it was subtle—a pearl sheen along his knuckles, a cold silver at his throat where the blood ran close to the surface. But under the open sky, in the thin bright air of the high country, he shone like a lamp left burning in a window.

"You should walk behind me," Yara said, for the fourth time that morning. "In my shadow. If a patrol—"

"They will see me anyway." Vael spoke the trade-tongue now with only the ghost of an accent, each word placed carefully, like stones laid across a stream. "I am a beacon on a dark hill, Yara. Walking behind you changes nothing." He paused, and something moved through his face that was too old for it. "It changes less than you hope."

She hated when he did that—reached into the language and pulled out exactly the right phrase, exactly the right grief. Two months ago he had been a feral child humming lullabies in a dead tongue. Now he stood straighter than she did, and when he looked at the distant watchtowers he did not look afraid. He looked like someone reading a letter he already knew by heart.

"Then we move at night," she said.

"The Stair is ice at night. The Stair is death at night."

"Then we move at dawn, when the fog comes off the valley, and we pray the fog is thick."

Vael turned to look at her fully, and the light under his skin seemed to answer something in the morning—brightened, just slightly, the way iron answers a lodestone. "You keep saying *we pray*," he said. "You do not pray. You told me so. You said your mistress taught you that prayer was what people did instead of thinking."

"I say a lot of things I don't mean."

"Yes," he said, gently. "That is also something you have taught me about humans."

* * *

They made the first switchback before noon, hugging the inner wall of the Stair where the rock overhung, and it was there, on a shelf of stone barely wide enough for two, that Yara heard the singing.

Not Vael's singing. Not the Old Tongue, either—or not entirely. It was a shepherd's song, one of the high-pasture melodies the mountain folk used to call their flocks home, but threaded through it, woven into it like silver wire through wool, ran phrases of the Old Tongue that no shepherd should have known. Phrases *she* knew now, because Vael had spent weeks teaching her, word by patient word, in the forest-keeper's glade and after, in caves, in ditches, in the long grey hours of flight: *elun-vara*, light-of-the-blood. *Seth-arien*, the-waking-stone.

The singer rounded the bend below them and stopped dead.

He was an old man, weathered to the color of the mountain itself, driving three goats and carrying a crook that had been mended more times than it had been carved. His eyes went from Yara—wary, hand drifting toward her knife—to Vael, and stopped there, and stayed.

The light of him. The old man was looking at the light of him the way men look at the first star after a long storm.

"*Seth-arien*," the shepherd whispered. Then, in his own tongue, halting and reverent: "The waking stone walks again."

"We're not—" Yara started.

"Grandfather." Vael stepped forward, past her knife-hand, past all her caution, and inclined his head in the manner of the Spire-folk, palm open, heart exposed. "Your song kept our words alive. Four hundred years in the mouths of shepherds. We thought ourselves the last keepers of them." His voice caught, just once. "We were wrong."

The old man dropped to his knees. The goats, indifferent to history, began cropping the scrub at the trail's edge.

"No," Yara said quickly, reaching for him. "Get up. Get *up*—do you understand what you're doing? Kneeling in the open on a patrolled road? There are witch-hunters within a day's walk, there are watchtowers, there is an inquisitor named Serane who has burned three villages for less than a song—"

"Let him kneel," Vael said quietly.

"He'll die for it!"

"Yes." Vael crouched, took the old man's shoulders, and raised him anyway, gently, the way you lift something you are afraid of breaking. "Many will die for this, I think. That is why we must not waste their kneeling." He looked at Yara then, and the light under his skin pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat. "You taught me that humans pay for what matters. I am learning to ask them what they wish to buy."

The shepherd pressed his crook into Vael's hands—not offering it, Yara realized, but *giving* it, the way you give a sword or a banner—and said something rapid and low in the mountain dialect. She caught only fragments. *The high passes. The freeholds. Word will run ahead of you faster than any horse.* And then, pressing his forehead briefly against Vael's chest, heedless of the glow, heedless of everything: "*Elun-vara*. We have waited four hundred years for you to be born tired enough to need us."

By the time the bend of the Stair swallowed the old man and his goats, Yara's hands were shaking.

"Do you understand what you've done?" she whispered.

"I have accepted a crook I do not know how to use."

"You've become a *banner*. Don't you see? Every village between here and the Crown is going to hear that the waking stone walks, and half of them will kneel, and the other half will sell us to Serane for a pardon, and either way—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Either way, everyone who sings that song is marked. Everyone who helps us dies. The keeper died. Brannoc—"

"Brannoc chose," Vael said. "At the ford. You know he chose."

"That's not the point!"

"It is entirely the point." For a moment the boy was gone and something older stood in his place, looking out through a young face with terrible patience. "The keeper died buying us an hour. The shepherd just spent his life's safety on a song. They are not dying *for* me, Yara. They are dying *for the covenant*—for the idea that the mountains belonged to everyone who loved them, before your king put a fence around the world." He softened. "I did not choose this. You know I did not choose this. But it has chosen me, and I would rather carry it standing than crawling."

Yara looked at him—at the impossible, glowing, stubborn boy she had dragged out of a sleet storm because she'd been too lonely and too soft to turn him away—and understood, with a sensation like a stair step missing in the dark, that she was no longer leading him anywhere.

She never had been, maybe. She'd just been walking beside him while he grew.

"Dawn fog tomorrow," she said finally. "We move fast and we don't stop for kneelers."

Vael smiled, and it was the old smile, the feral one, all teeth and no court. "We will see," he said.

* * *

Serane watched the mountain through a spyglass of blackened brass and did not like what she saw.

The report had come in at midday, passed hand to hand up the relay of watchtowers until it reached her field desk in the garrison at Greywater: a shepherd on the eastern Stair, detained and questioned, who claimed—he claimed, with tears streaming down a face seamed like old leather—that he had seen the covenant-child walking in the light of his own body. The shepherd had been released. Released! Because half the garrison had heard the tale by the time the questioning began, and Inquisitor Serane had learned long ago that a secret tortured out of one man in front of forty witnesses was worth less than no secret at all.

Now the tale was everywhere. It moved through the high villages like fire through dry grass, and it had grown in the telling, as tales did. The child walked wrapped in the light of the old kings. The child had raised the ford at Greywater from its bed. The child's blood sang the mountains awake. In three days, the story had acquired a miracle, two martyrs, and a chorus.

Superstition. All of it, superstition—and yet.

She lowered the glass and looked east, where evening was coming down the valleys like spilled ink, and thought about King Aldric waiting in the lowlands with his stolen shard of Spire magic strapped to his breastbone like a reliquary, and about what the king would do to the messenger who reported that the covenant-blood had become a *religion* before anyone had managed to put a knife in it.

"Inquisitor." Her lieutenant, Dain, hovered at the tent flap with the particular stiffness of a man bringing news he hopes will be someone else's problem. "The freehold villages on the upper Stair. They're... refusing the levy."

"Refusing."

"Not rising, my lady. Nothing so useful as rising. They simply—" he consulted his notes as if hoping the words had changed, "—will not open their gates. They say the mountain provides. They say the waking stone walks. They say—" he faltered.

"Say it."

"They say the king's writ ends where the old words begin."

Serane set down the glass with great care. Twenty years she had served the writ, twenty years of smoke and ledgers and the arithmetic of fear, and she knew precisely what she was looking at, because she had read the old accounts of the Spire-folk's last days. This was how it began for them too. Not with armies. With songs. With shepherds. With ordinary people deciding, all at once and without any order given, that a thing was true.

"Double the patrols on the Stair," she said. "Burn the freehold at Crag's Foot if it shelters them—I want the smoke visible from the valley. And send a rider to the king." She hesitated, hearing how the next words would sound. "Tell him the covenant-blood moves toward the Weeping Crown, and tell him the mountain is waking ahead of him."

Dain lingered. "My lady—if the stories are true, if the boy is what they say—should we not withdraw? Regroup? A power like that—"

"A power like that," Serane said, "is a boy who bleeds. Everything bleeds. That is the first lesson of the writ, Dain, and the last." She picked up the glass again, though the light was nearly gone. "Find me where he sleeps."

* * *

They slept that night in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, a fire the size of two cupped hands, and Vael woke near midnight because the mountain was talking to him.

It had begun doing that since the Hollow Root—not words, exactly, but pressures, tides, a slow attention turning beneath the stone like a great fish beneath ice. Tonight the attention had a direction. Tonight it pulled upward, toward the Crown, and also outward, down every goat track and sheep path radiating from the Stair, and when Vael opened his senses to it he found the reason:

People were moving.

Small knots of them, in the dark, on paths that had been empty for generations. A widow from Crag's Foot with a lantern and her husband's old sword. Two brothers driving a mule loaded with barley and arrowheads. A girl of perhaps fifteen walking alone with a sling and a sack of bread, following the song every child in the high country apparently now knew. None of them converging on Vael and Yara. All of them converging on the same point ahead: the Weeping Crown, the third stone, the place the covenant would stand or shatter.

They were not fleeing with the fugitives. They were *following* them. Ahead of them, even. Pilgrims, walking toward a shrine that had been silent for four hundred years, because a shepherd had sung a song and meant it.

"Yara," he whispered.

She came awake instantly, knife already loose in her grip—two months of flight had taught her body things her mind hadn't approved. "What. Patrols?"

"Pilgrims." He told her what he had felt, and watched her face go through disbelief, denial, and arrive at last at a grim, exhausted acceptance that looked very old on her.

"So we lead a procession up a patrolled mountain to the most visible shrine in the Thornfells," she said flatly, "with an inquisitor doubling her patrols behind us and a usurper king marching his host into the foothills ahead of us. Wonderful. Truly, the covenant could not have chosen two better fools."

"You could turn back."

The silence stretched. Below their hollow, far down the black shoulder of the mountain, a watchtower burned its signal fire, and the light of it touched the snowfields red.

"No," Yara said at last, and it came out of her like something torn. "No. I gave two memories to these stones already. My mother's face. My—" she stopped, because the second memory was a shape with no name anymore, a hole in her past she had learned to walk around. "Whatever's left of me belongs to that summit. I'm not losing a third piece for nothing."

Vael reached across the tiny fire and took her hand. His skin was warm now—it had always been cold, that first night in the watchtower, cold as riverbed stone—and the light under it flickered in time, he realized, with both their pulses.

"When this is done," he said, "when the covenant stands—whatever it costs—you will not remember why you climbed. Do you understand that? You will stand on the Crown hollowed out, and you will look at me, and you will not know me, and you will not know yourself, and the mountain will be saved and you will be a stranger inside your own life."

"I know."

"And you offer anyway."

"I said *no*, didn't I?" Her voice cracked, and she laughed at herself, wetly. "Gods. Listen to me. My mistress would say I've gone soft. She'd say—" Yara stopped. Frowned. Something flickered behind her eyes, some reflex reaching for a memory and finding the shelf empty, the way it always did now, the way it broke his heart every time.

"She'd say what?" Vael asked softly.

"I don't remember," Yara said. "Isn't that the whole cursed point of me?"

Below them, faint on the wind, someone in the dark was singing. The shepherd's song, or one like it, climbing the Stair from camp to camp, voice to voice, the Old Tongue worn smooth as river stones in human mouths. *Elun-vara. Seth-arien.* Light of the blood. The waking stone.

Vael listened until the song faded, and then he lay back and let the mountain's attention wash over him, and in the tide of it he felt the third waystone waiting at the summit—felt it the way you feel a held breath—and beneath that feeling, older and colder, something else. Something at the Crown that was not stone at all.

Something that had been watching them climb for three days.

And it was hungry.
