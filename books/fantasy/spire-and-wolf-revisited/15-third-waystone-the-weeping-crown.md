## 15. Third Waystone: The Weeping Crown

The mountain stopped pretending to be kind two thousand feet below the summit.

Yara felt it in her knees first, then in her lungs—the air thinning like broth stretched too far, each breath a negotiation. The pilgrims who had gathered behind them over the last three days of climbing could go no further; she heard them singing behind her in the wind, low voices braided with Vael's Old Tongue, and then even that faded, swallowed by the shriek of sleet against stone.

Vael walked ahead of her, and the mountain let him.

She had noticed it first at Greywater Ford, in the terrible hours after Brannoc fell—how the river's ice groaned but did not crack beneath the boy's bare feet, how the wind parted around him as though he were a stone in its stream. Now, climbing the Weeping Stair, the effect was unmistakable. Frost bloomed where he stepped and melted behind him. The sleet turned to rain in a halo a hand's width from his skin. He was becoming something the weather recognized, and Yara did not know whether to be comforted or terrified.

*He is the seal,* her mistress's letter had said. *And the seal must be pressed into the wax.*

She climbed.

The stair was old work, older than any kingdom—steps cut from living rock by hands that had not needed chisels, each one wide enough for three and worn smooth down the center by centuries of feet that had stopped coming long before Yara was born. Lichen glowed faintly along the edges, a blue-green light like drowned stars, and where the light touched the ice it softened, so that the stair seemed to breathe beneath them, rising and falling with some vast slow pulse.

"The mountain's heart," Vael said. His voice had changed too, over these weeks. It was still a boy's voice, but there were harmonics in it now, echoes arriving a half-beat after the words, as if the stone itself repeated everything he said in a language just below hearing. "My mother told me of it. Before the Spire fell, you could hear it from the valleys. A drumbeat once an hour. The whole realm set its clocks by it."

"Your mother," Yara said, and immediately wished she hadn't. He never spoke of her willingly, and when he did, his eyes went somewhere she couldn't follow.

"She sang on this stair." He said it simply, without grief, which frightened her more than weeping would have. "The whole procession sang. Thousands of voices. When they reached the top, the Crown would weep, and the weeping was the covenant renewed." He glanced back at her, and in the lichen-light his eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone. "You will see."

They climbed until the sky changed.

It happened gradually, so gradually she might have missed it if her body hadn't been screaming: the grey ceiling of cloud above them thinned, then tore, and beyond it was not the pale winter sky she expected but a darkness scattered with stars—at midday, or what passed for midday under the storm. The stars were wrong, too close, too bright, arranged in patterns no navigator of the free cities would know. And at their center, directly above the peak they climbed toward, hung a single point of light that was not a star at all but a shard of white fire, motionless, patient.

"The Weeping Crown," Vael whispered.

Yara looked up at it and felt something in her chest answer—a pull, gentle and absolute, like the tide answering the moon. Her scars ached. Her teeth ached. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where her mistress's voice still lived in memory, something leaned toward that light like a plant toward sun.

*Soon,* the light seemed to say. *Soon.*

They reached the summit at dusk, if dusk still meant anything under those alien stars.

* * *

The shrine was a ring.

That was all—no roof, no walls, no idol. A circle of standing stones perhaps forty paces across, each taller than two men, each carved so densely with script that the surfaces looked furred rather than fluted. The Old Tongue covered every inch of them, and Yara, who now carried perhaps two hundred words of it in her head, could read almost none of it—and yet she understood it anyway, the way one understands a face. The stones were not writing. They were *waiting*. Each character was a held breath, a word spoken ten thousand years ago and never permitted to finish.

At the ring's center stood the waystone itself.

It was smaller than she had imagined, after two ruined cousins in Ashfall Pass and the Hollow Root. Barely taller than she was, a single pillar of white stone shot through with veins of silver, and atop it, balanced impossibly, a crown of ice that had never melted—not in ten thousand years, not through the fall of the Spire, not through Aldric's wars or the long forgetting. Meltwater ran down the pillar's sides in thin bright threads, endlessly, silently, pooling around its base in a basin carved with the same waiting script.

The water was warm. She knew it would be before she touched it. It was the temperature of tears.

"It's been weeping since the covenant broke," Vael said softly. He stood at the ring's edge, and Yara saw that he was afraid—for the first time since the ford, truly afraid, his small hands opening and closing at his sides. "Every drop is a year the realms have been unsealed. Every drop is a door Aldric could walk through, if he knew the way." He looked at her. "He knows the way. Serane carries a shard. I've felt it getting closer for days, like a cold spot moving through the world."

"Then we finish this tonight."

"Yes." But he didn't move. He stood there in the starlight, and the harmonics in his voice trembled, and Yara realized with a lurch that he was trying to say something and failing. "Yara. If—if the stone asks for more than you expect. If it asks for—"

"I know what it asks for." She said it gently, because she had known since the Hollow Root, since the visions showed her the original rite in all its terrible clarity: three memories freely given, each greater than the last, until the giver stood empty before the stone and the stone could pour itself full again through the hollow that remained. She had paid her mother's face at Ashfall Pass. She had paid, by deliberate choice, the memory of the day the smugglers took her in—her last illusion that found-family came free—at the Hollow Root. What remained in her well was shallow now, and she knew exactly what lay at the bottom of it.

Her mistress. Everything. Maerlin Ishikawa, who had pulled a half-starved child out of a gutter in Corvess and taught her letters, herb-craft, the names of stars, the difference between loneliness and solitude. Maerlin, whose betrayal of the Spire-folk Yara had learned in the dark beneath the second stone and forgiven anyway, because love was not a ledger. Maerlin, whose lullaby had called a lost boy through a sleet-storm to her door, and whose footsteps Yara had followed ever since.

"I know," she said again. "And I give it freely. That's the whole point, isn't it? That's why it has to be me."

Vael crossed the ring to her. Up close, in the light of the impossible stars, she could see that he was crying without seeming to notice—tears running down his face and turning to tiny crystals before they left his jaw, as if the mountain's magic had already begun claiming him. "There might be another way," he said. "The letter said—"

"The letter said anyone who loves the heir may pay." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile steady. "I've thought about that every step of this climb, Vael. I've turned it over like a coin. And here's what I found: if someone else pays my price, then my memories stay mine, and mine are of a woman who helped break your people. Who do you think the stone would rather have? Her sins, burned clean? Or—" she tapped her own chest, "—this. A debt I owe and choose to pay. Freely given means *mine* to give. No one else can carry it for me. That's not the loophole, love. That's the lock."

He stared at her. Behind him, the Weeping Crown shed its endless bright threads into the basin, and the sound of it was suddenly very loud, a sound like all the weeping the world had ever done, compressed to a whisper.

"You sound like her," he said at last. "In the memories you've told me. You sound like Maerlin."

"Good," Yara said. "Then let's make sure the mountain remembers her too. All of her. Even the parts that shame us. Especially those."

She walked to the basin.

* * *

Kneeling in the meltwater should have been cold. It was not. It was blood-warm, and where it soaked through her trousers and sleeves she felt it sink deeper than skin, sinking along paths she hadn't known her body contained, filling channels that had been dry her whole life and were suddenly, unmistakably, *meant* for this.

The waystone woke.

Light rose through the silver veins like dawn moving up a valley floor, and the waiting script on the surrounding monoliths began, at last, to speak—all of it at once, ten thousand years of unfinished sentences completing themselves in a chorus that was not sound so much as pressure, meaning arriving whole inside her skull. She caught fragments. *We were two peoples and one roof.* *Let the high places hold the low.* *A memory for a memory, freely given, forever given.*

And beneath it all, addressing her alone, in a voice like water finding a crack in stone:

***What will you give?***

She had rehearsed this. On the stair, in the caves, in a hundred sleepless camps, she had built the memory up carefully in her mind like a shrine of its own, so that she could look at it one final time before offering it: Maerlin in her kitchen, flour to the elbows, laughing at her own terrible jokes. Maerlin on the roof teaching her constellations, her breath smoking in the cold. Maerlin dying, and the last thing she'd said—*you were the best thing I ever stole*—and the guttering candle, and the silence after.

She reached for all of it. Every room of the house they'd shared. Every lesson. Every quarrel. The smell of the grimoire's leather. The weight of the woman's hand on her hair, the only touch that had ever felt like belonging.

***Freely given?***

"Freely given," Yara said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it crack, because the stone deserved honesty more than it deserved dignity. "All of her. Take the whole of it. She saved my life and she broke your people and I loved her and I am done carrying her alone. Let the mountain hold her. Let the mountain remember her better than I could. Take it *all*."

***Then open,*** said the stone, ***and be poured.***

It did not feel like losing.

That was the horror of it, later, in the fragmentary way she would ever after understand *later*: it did not feel like loss at all. It felt like being *emptied*, like a well giving up its water to a thirstier ground, and the water going gladly, gratefully, each memory rising out of her and streaming upward into the silver-veined stone as a thread of light. She watched them go. She watched Maerlin's kitchen rise glowing out of her chest and unravel into the pillar. She watched the rooftop, the stars, the lessons, the laughter. She watched the deathbed go last, the candle, the words, the silence—

—and she felt nothing.

Not numbness. Nothing so crude. She felt *full*, paradoxically, filled with a vast quiet tenderness that had no object and no history, a love with no beloved attached, like sunlight in an empty room. The grief was leaving her and the love was staying, and she could not have said, even then, whom the love was for. It simply was, the way the mountain simply was. It had no name anymore.

The last thread of light left her. The basin went still. The Weeping Crown stopped weeping.

For one heartbeat the summit was silent as the inside of a sealed tomb.

Then the ice crown shattered—not violently, but the way frost leaves a window: all at once, everywhere, into light. The shards did not fall. They rose, spiraling up into the black star-scattered sky, and where they passed, the wrong stars *moved*, sliding into new arrangements, and Yara understood with a certainty that came from somewhere below thought: the sky itself was a lock, and it had just turned.

The waystone blazed. The monoliths rang like bells struck by a giant's finger. Far below, muffled by miles of rock, Yara felt—or heard, or became—the mountain's heart resume its beat. Once. Twice. A drumbeat, slow and immense, setting its clock by nothing now except itself.

The covenant held.

By a thread—but it held. She could feel the thread, actually feel it, a filament of silver light running from the stone through her emptied chest and out into the world, thin as spider silk, humming. One witch-hunter's blade, one stolen shard, one desperate king, and it would snap. But tonight, tonight it sang.

She became aware that Vael was holding her. When had he crossed the ring? She was on her knees in the basin and his arms were around her and he was saying her name over and over—*Yara, Yara, Yara*—in the Old Tongue and the common speech both, as if making sure she existed in either language.

She looked at him, this boy she had sheltered, bled for, climbed a mountain for, and felt the fierce familial love she had carried since the night he knocked—and beneath it, where a name should be, a soft and total blankness, smooth as snow.

"Why am I crying?" she asked him.

Her cheeks were wet. The tears kept coming, warm and endless, and she had no memory attached to any of them, no story to tell herself about why. They were simply there, the way the meltwater had been there, ten thousand years of grief finding its way down a face that no longer knew its source.

Vael's face crumpled. "Because you gave everything," he said. "You gave her, all of her, and the stone took it, and you're free, and freedom *hurts*, Yara, I'm so sorry, I'm so—"

"Hush." She touched his crystalline tears with one finger, wondering at them. "Don't be sorry. Look." She turned his face toward the pillar, toward the blazing script, toward the sky rearranging itself into a shape that meant *kept*, that meant *whole*, that meant *two peoples, one roof*. "Whatever I lost—I can feel that it was worth it. The stone knows it. The mountain knows it. So it must be true."

She smiled at him, and the smile was steady, and she did not know whose smile it was. Some habit of the face, some inheritance from a woman whose name had gone up in light. It fit her perfectly anyway.

Far to the south, past the singing stones and the resumed heartbeat of the world, torchlight was crawling up the lower passes like a spill of embers—hundreds of fires, thousands, a host on the march, and at its head something cold that made the new-made thread in Yara's chest shiver like a plucked string.

Vael saw it too. He went very still against her.

"They're coming," he said. "Aldric is coming. Tonight."

Yara wiped her face with the back of her hand and stood, knees steady, heart empty and burning, and looked down the mountain at the approaching dark.

"Then we'd better teach them what the mountain remembers," she said.
