## 16. Aldric's Host

The road to Thornfell was paved with the bones of the old kingdom, and Aldric rode over them without looking down.

He had ordered the highway built himself, thirty years ago, in the first flush of his reign—flagstones quarried from the ruins of a Spire outpost, laid by prisoners who had once been freeholders of the mountain vales. It had seemed fitting then. Let the old magic carry the new king's armies, he had thought. Let the conquered stone bear the conqueror's weight. He had been younger then, and the hunger in him had still felt like ambition rather than appetite.

Now he rode at the head of four thousand men through a country that remembered too much, and the hunger had teeth.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the shadow of Greywater Ford, or what remained of it. The bridge was broken—recently broken, its timbers splintered as if by something enormous passing through—and the ford itself ran high and brown with snowmelt. His engineers were already arguing about pontoons. His outriders had found bodies: smugglers, by their dress, left where they'd fallen, and among them one man still breathing who babbled about a boy and a woman and a bridge that would not let his comrades cross.

Aldric listened to the report standing at the edge of the water, letting the cold air off the river find the gaps in his furs.

"A boy," he said.

"Ten years old, perhaps. Or twelve. The survivor is not—" The captain hesitated. "He is not well, Majesty. He keeps saying the water stood up."

"The water stood up," Aldric repeated. He turned the phrase over the way a jeweler turns a suspected stone, looking for the flaw that proves it false. He found none. That was the trouble with this country; every lie here came dressed as truth, and every truth wore the rags of legend. "And Serane? Where is my Inquisitor?"

"Gone ahead, Majesty. With her writ and six riders. She said the trail was fresh."

Of course she had. Serane did not wait for armies. Serane moved through the world like a needle through cloth, and everything she stitched came apart in her hands afterward. He had given her the writ himself—the black seal, the authority to burn, the power to question any soul between here and the summit—and some nights he wondered whether he had handed a leash to a wolf or simply shown the wolf where the sheep were penned.

"Find me a crossing," he said. "The army moves at first light."

"And the prisoner, Majesty?"

Aldric looked at the smuggler a long moment. The man was shivering, half-mad, clutching at a soldier's sleeve and whispering about standing water. There had been a time—a lifetime ago, before the crown, before the covenant broke—when Aldric would have knelt beside such a man and given him wine from his own flask.

"Give him dry blankets," he said. "And if he speaks sense by morning, bring him to me."

The captain blinked. Kings were not known for mercy toward smugglers. But the captain bowed and went, and Aldric turned back to the river, and in the brown rushing water he saw—not reflected, but remembered—a face he had spent thirty years trying not to see.

* * *

His tent that night was hung with maps, and the maps were wrong.

That was the thing no cartographer would admit: the mountains resisted being drawn. Roads wandered off the parchment into blankness. Valleys appeared and vanished between surveys. And always, at the center of the highest range, there was a space where the ink refused to sit—a pale circle like a scar, which the mapmakers called *the Spire's Footprint* and then, when they thought no one important was listening, *the Empty Crown*.

Aldric sat alone with those maps while his army slept around him in its city of canvas, and he took from a locked case at his wrist the thing he had ridden all this way to spend.

It was the size of a knucklebone. That was all. A shard of white crystal, veined with light the color of dawn on snow, warm as a living thing against his palm. His alchemists called it a resonance fragment. His priests, the few he had not yet burned for honesty, called it blasphemy. The smugglers who had sold it to his agents—for everything in this world was for sale, that was the lesson of his long reign—had called it *spireglass*, and had gone pale saying the word, and had taken the gold without counting it.

It had come out of the ruins of the Spire itself. Out of the fall. Out of the night, thirty years ago, when the last great working of the old people had cracked like an egg and the covenant that shielded the free cities had died with it—or so everyone believed.

Everyone except Aldric.

He held the shard up to the lamplight and watched the vein of light pulse, slow, patient, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. It had been pulsing stronger these past weeks. Stronger with every league he marched into the mountains. As if it knew the way home.

"You want to go back," he murmured to it. "I know. So do I."

There it was. The truth he kept even from Serane, even from himself on most days, folded small and carried in the hollow place beneath his breastbone where his heart used to be. He was not marching to destroy the covenant. Destruction was easy; he had proven that decades ago. He was marching to *take it*. To swallow the old magic whole, to pour the covenant through himself instead of through stones and bloodlines, to become the thing the Spire-folk had never dared become: a single vessel, undying, unaccountable, answerable to nothing because there would be nothing left outside him.

Immortality. The word sounded noble if you said it quickly.

He closed his fist around the shard and felt its warmth bite into his palm, and he thought—as he thought every night, whether he willed it or no—of his brother.

* * *

Corin had been the elder. Corin had been the beloved. Corin had sat at the feet of the Spire envoys when they still crossed the lowlands in their grey robes, learning their tongue, laughing at their jokes in a language Aldric could not parse, while Aldric stood at the edge of the firelight doing sums and holding grudges.

Their father had meant to bind the family to the covenant. That was the old plan: a mortal house sworn to the waystones, keepers and kept, generation after generation, humble before the mountain. Corin had been born for that role. Gentle Corin, patient Corin, who spoke to shepherds and smiths as though they were interesting, who had once given away his winter cloak to a beggar child and walked home blue-lipped and glowing with some private joy.

Aldric had been born for nothing. He knew that early. Second sons are born for nothing; it is the first cruelty life teaches them, and some men spend their whole lives proving their fathers right.

So when the plague year came—when the sickness swept the lowlands and the Spire-folk sealed their passes and let the valleys burn rather than open their sanctuaries to the dying—Aldric had seen his chance dressed as a wound. The people needed someone to blame. The covenant needed a face to hate. And Corin—gentle, hopeless Corin—had chosen that exact season to stand in the market square and defend them. *They have their reasons*, his brother had said, to a crowd that had buried three of its children that week. *We must trust the old wisdom.*

Aldric had not killed his brother. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven kept. He had only stopped defending him. He had only stood aside, eloquently, while other men did what other men do to traitors' brothers in plague years. And afterward, when the mob's fury was banked and usable, he had led it up the mountain passes with fire and iron, and the Spire had fallen, and the covenant had broken, and the free cities—cowardly, practical, grateful—had opened their gates to the man who promised order instead of mystery.

Thirty years of order. Thirty years of granaries full and roads safe and no grey-robed envoys appearing out of nowhere to remind the world that some things were older than kings.

And thirty years of waking at night with the taste of ash in his mouth, reaching for a brother who had been dead before Aldric ever gave the order that mattered, and finding instead the hollow place, and the hunger, and the shard of white glass pulsing on the table beside his bed like a second heart.

*They have their reasons.*

He had hated Corin most for being right. The Spire-folk had had their reasons. The plague had come *from* the lowlands, his scholars now admitted—from a pit of his own making, though none dared say so—and the sealing of the passes had bought the mountains time to craft a cure that had saved ten thousand lowland lives within a generation. If the covenant had held. If Aldric had not broken it. The arithmetic of his reign, done honestly, came out in red.

But honest arithmetic was for monks. Kings dealt in other sums.

* * *

Serane returned near midnight, slipping into his tent with snow in her dark hair and something bright and terrible in her eyes, and Aldric knew before she spoke that she had found them.

"They crossed Greywater," she said. "Upstream of the ford. Something cleared the ice for them—I have six witnesses who will swear the river parted." She smiled, thin as a blade's edge. "The mountain is choosing sides, Majesty. It has not done that in thirty years."

"Your report, Inquisitor. Not your poetry."

Her smile did not fade; it merely sharpened. She unrolled a leather case on his map table and laid out her evidence with the tenderness other women showed to infants: a scrap of cloth burned at the edges, a wax rubbing of a waystone sigil, a list of villages interrogated, a tally of the confessed and the condemned. "The boy is real. Vael—that is what the forest-keeper called him before she burned. Last heir of the Spire line. The woman with him is Yara Marchetti, apprentice to Maerlin of the Hollow Root—you remember Maerlin."

"I remember Maerlin sold me secrets for twenty years and died before I could collect the last of them." Aldric studied the sigil rubbing. Three interlocked arcs, the mark of the covenant. "You believe she means to reforge it."

"I believe she already has begun. Two stones answered, by my count of the tremors. The third sits at the Weeping Crown, above the snowline, and if they reach it—" Serane's voice went quiet, which was how she said the most frightening things. "Then the covenant stands again, Majesty. Whole. And every army you march into these mountains will break against it like weather against cliff-face. Not defeated. *Refused.*"

"And you propose we reach it first."

"I propose we reach it *with* you." She touched the locked case at his wrist, lightly, familiar in a way no one else alive dared be. "The shard responds to proximity. You've felt it. Imagine what it does at the Crown itself, with the third stone awake under your hand. The covenant is a lock, Aldric. You are carrying the key. We don't need to fight our way up that mountain. We need to arrive."

He looked at her—at this woman he had raised from a gutter orphan into an instrument, this creature of method and appetite whom he had long suspected was the only honest mirror he owned—and he saw, with the clarity that comes only at midnight and at the end of things, exactly what she was offering. Not victory. Communion. She wanted him to drink the covenant as much as he wanted it himself, and her devotion was not loyalty at all but hunger recognizing hunger, the way two fires lean toward each other across a burning room.

"And the boy?" he asked. "The heir. When we arrive—what happens to the boy?"

Serane shrugged, a small elegant motion. "What happens to any key once the door stands open? Nothing. Or anything. Keys are not people, Majesty. You taught me that."

Had he? He tried to remember teaching her that and could not, which frightened him more than the parted river did. Perhaps she had learned it from watching. Perhaps he had never needed to say it aloud; perhaps his whole reign had been the lesson, delivered daily, in a language plainer than words.

"Sleep," he told her. "We cross at first light."

She bowed and withdrew, and Aldric sat alone with his maps and his shard and the memory of his brother's voice saying *we must trust the old wisdom* in a market square full of grieving people, and he understood—he had always understood—that he was not marching to save his kingdom from the covenant.

He was marching to save himself from the arithmetic.

* * *

They crossed at dawn, and the river behaved.

Four thousand men forded Greywater in ranks, boots on gravel, breath steaming, banners stiff with frost, and the brown water rose no higher than their knees and ran no faster than a millstream. The soldiers laughed about it. Good omens, they said. The mountain yields. Aldric rode the current's edge and felt the shard against his wrist beating faster, faster, like a bird against a window, and he alone understood what the calm water meant.

It was not yielding. It was *watching*.

Beyond the far bank the road climbed into pine and shadow, and above the pines the peaks stood white against a hard blue sky, and above the peaks—faint, visible only when the light struck true, visible only to a man carrying a piece of the old country in his pocket—there rose a shimmer like heat-haze in winter air. The Spire's Footprint. The Empty Crown. The place where the world had been sewn to itself once, and might be sewn again, by hands willing to pay.

His horse stumbled on the far bank, and Aldric steadied it, and for one strange moment he looked back downstream at the broken bridge and the scattered dead smugglers and the one mad survivor being helped along by soldiers, and he felt—an old feeling, unwelcome as a scar in rain—the shape of pity. For the boy on the mountain, who would learn what keys were worth. For the woman beside him, who loved something doomed. For Serane, who mistook appetite for faith. For the four thousand men behind him who believed they followed a king into glory, when in truth they followed a hollow man into his own devouring.

Pity, inside horror. He recognized the arrangement. He had lived inside it for thirty years.

"Forward," he said, to no one, to everyone, to the shard that pulsed against his skin like an answering heart.

The column moved. The mountain waited. And high above, past the tree line, past the snow, past the sight of any living thing below, the Empty Crown caught the morning sun and held it, the way a cup holds wine—patiently, and for someone.
