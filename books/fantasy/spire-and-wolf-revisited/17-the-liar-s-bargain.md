## 17. The Liar's Bargain

The net came down on Vael in the grey hour before dawn, when the mist lay so thick along the river meadows that the world seemed made of wool.

He had left Yara sleeping in the hollow beneath the alder roots—she needed the rest; the wound at her shoulder had gone hot again—and he had told himself he was only walking the perimeter, only listening to the stones the way she had taught him, the way the Old Tongue taught him. But some part of him, the part that remembered the Spire falling, knew that a boy who could feel the covenant waking in his own blood would not sleep soundly while Aldric's host climbed the mountain roads below. So he walked. And while he walked, they took him.

The net was weighted with iron beads, small ones, each no larger than a fingernail, and where they touched his skin they burned like brands. He went down hard into the wet grass, and the world became a confusion of boots and lantern-light and voices speaking the trade-tongue too fast for him to follow. He did not struggle. Struggling was what prey did, and he had decided, somewhere between the second waystone and the ford at Greywater, that he was finished being prey.

They bound his wrists with iron wire and carried him slung over a horse's back like a sack of grain, and through the swaying curtain of his own hair he watched the stars wheel slowly overhead, indifferent as ever, and he thought: *Yara will wake alone. Yara will be afraid.* And then, more quietly, in the language of the Spire that no one living but her had begun to learn: *Good. Let her be afraid. Afraid people run.*

* * *

Inquisitor Serane woke before her soldiers, as she always did, and dressed by candlelight in the small stone chapel her men had commandeered in the village of Lowmere. The chapel's saint had been chiseled off the altar years ago, on the King's orders, and the empty niche suited her fine. She had never cared much for saints. Saints were what people invented when they wanted their suffering to mean something.

She ate a boiled egg and half a loaf, standing up, and reviewed the night's reports by the light of a tallow candle. The boy was secured. The girl had slipped the net—for now. The King's vanguard was two days behind her, moving slow through the flooded lowlands, and His Majesty's patience, which had never been generous, was wearing thin as old rope.

Serane did not fear the King. That was the secret of her usefulness, the thing that set her apart from the fawning courtiers and the ambitious generals who orbited Aldric like moths around a lamp that had long since stopped giving warmth. She did not fear him, and she did not love him, and because of this she saw him clearly: a man who had eaten something enormous thirty years ago and had been trying to digest it ever since. The stolen shard of Spire magic sat against his heart like a swallowed coal, and it was burning him alive from the inside, and he called the burning *immortality* because the alternative was to call it what it was.

She had seen the same hunger in smaller men. It always ended the same way.

At first light she went down to the chapel crypt, where they were keeping the boy.

He sat on the floor against the wall, wrists wired, ankles wired, the iron beads from the net piled in a bowl beside the door like an offering. He should have been gray-faced with pain. Iron burned covenant-blood the way frost burned green shoots; she had seen grown men, half-Spire bastards with barely a drop in them, weep and babble under far lighter restraints. This boy sat straight-backed in the cold, watching her come down the stairs with eyes the color of deep water over slate, and he did not weep, and he did not babble, and Serane felt the small cold thrill she always felt when the world confirmed her expectations.

"You don't speak the trade-tongue," she said. "Or you pretend not to. Your companion translates for you. I'm told you've been teaching her bits of your filthy old language in return."

The boy said nothing. But his chin lifted—a fraction, no more—and Serane smiled without warmth.

"I'll speak plainly, then, and you can pretend all you like." She drew a stool from the corner and sat, arranging her grey cloak about her knees, a woman settling in for a long conversation with a child. "You are Vael. Last heir of the Spire-folk. You are the living seal on a covenant your people died making, and in three days' time, if nothing changes, my King will reach the Weeping Crown and take what's left of your blood out of you the way one takes marrow from a bone. I have read the old writs. I know what he intends. Do you?"

Something moved behind the boy's eyes—not fear. Calculation. Serane catalogued it and moved on.

"Here is my offer," she said. "Not the King's. Mine. The King wants you screaming. He wants ritual, and spectacle, and the free cities watching from their walls as the last of the old magic is unmade in front of them. I have no use for spectacle. Spectacle is waste." She leaned forward slightly. "Tell me where the girl is. Tell me how far she's gotten, which stones are lit, what she paid. Do this, and I will kill you tonight, cleanly, quickly, before the King arrives. A knife, once, in the right place. You will feel cold, and then nothing. No furnace. No altar. No centuries of being pulled apart thread by thread while a dying man tries to weave himself a new life from your guts."

She let the silence sit. In her experience, silence did most of the work.

"You think I'm cruel," she said, when he still said nothing. "I'm not. Cruelty is inefficient. What I am offering you is the only mercy anyone in this war has offered you, boy, and I am offering it because I have read the prophecies too, and I know what happens to the last heir of a fallen people. There is no version of your story that ends with you growing old. There is only the question of how much of you gets used up on the way there." She stood, smoothing her cloak. "Think on it. I'll return at midday. Give me the girl, and I'll give you a death worth having. Refuse, and I'll give you to the King, and you can spend eternity regretting your loyalty to someone who will forget your name within a year of eating you."

At the foot of the stairs she paused, because the boy had spoken.

His voice was low and rough, an instrument played rarely, and his accent was strange, vowels shaped like wind through high places—but the words themselves were trade-tongue, clear enough, each one placed like a stone in a wall.

"The wolf does not bargain with the snare."

Serane turned. The boy looked back at her, and for just a moment she saw something ancient looking out through the young face, patient and cold and very, very awake.

"No," she agreed softly. "But the wolf in the snare dies all the same. Midday, then."

She climbed the stairs into the pale morning and found her lieutenant waiting, a heavy man named Doss with a face like a closed fist.

"Well?" he asked.

"He'll talk," Serane said. "They always do. The loyal ones just take longer, because they have to lie to themselves first." She glanced back down the stairwell, at the faint shape of the boy sitting straight against the crypt wall. "Double the watch on him. And send riders to the King's column—I want His Majesty to know we have the heir, and that I will deliver him personally at the Crown. Let him chew on that while he drowns in his own floodwater."

* * *

They lied to themselves, Serane had said. She was right, in her way. Vael had spent the whole of the night doing exactly that.

Lying in the dark of the crypt, listening to the soldiers above shift and cough and mutter in their sleep, he had built the lie carefully, the way Yara built a fire: small things first, dry things, the kindling of small truths. *It is true that I cannot escape. True that the iron burns me. True that Yara is wounded and slow and hiding somewhere in the river country with no supplies and no allies.* All true. And then, laid over the truth like fat over lean meat, the lie itself: *If I give her to them, she dies quickly instead of badly, and I die clean, and that is the best either of us can hope for.*

It was a good lie. It even tasted almost true. But underneath it, older than it, ran the other current—the one the Spire had put in his blood, the one that whispered in the Old Tongue when the iron burned worst: *A seal is not broken by force. A seal is broken by consent. Never let them have your yes.*

Yara had taught him human cunning in the months since Ashfall Pass—how to read a road, how to lie with your shoulders, how to make a pursuer believe you were slower and stupider and more frightened than you were. *The best trap,* she'd said once, crouched over a deadfall in the Deepwood, resetting the trigger-stick with quick brown fingers, *is the one the animal thinks it sprang on purpose.* He had not understood her then. He understood her now.

Because here was the thing Serane did not know, the thing Vael had kept behind his teeth even from Yara until the pilgrims came: the mountain folk had been following them since the Serpent's Stair. Not as hunters. As hearth-keepers. Old women and shepherd boys and one one-eyed ferryman, dozens of them now, drifting up the mountain roads in the wake of the two children who were lighting the old stones, carrying sickles and sling-stones and the fierce dumb courage of people who had spent thirty years kneeling and had finally remembered they owned knees. And where the pilgrims walked, the old stones woke. The boundary-markers. The oath-circles. The ring of standing rocks on the hill above Lowmere that the villagers called the Grey Court and had not dared approach in living memory.

Vael had felt it in the night, through the iron and the stone walls—the Grey Court humming like a struck bell, drinking the moonlight, hungry for what the old law said it must be fed.

*Consent,* whispered the Spire in his blood. *Never let them have your yes. But oh, child—oh, last of us—there is more than one kind of yes.*

At midday, Serane came down the stairs again, and Vael looked up at her with the face of a boy who had spent twelve hours losing an argument with himself.

"You said a knife," he said. "Once. In the right place. Cold, and then nothing."

"I keep my bargains. It's why I'm still alive."

"Then hear mine." He let his shoulders sag against the wall, let the exhaustion show, let the iron-weariness pool in his eyes where she could see it. "The girl is in the alder hollow by the river bend, two miles east. She sleeps at night and limps by day; her shoulder is bad. She keeps the grimoire wrapped in oilcloth against her belly. If you go at dusk, you can take her before she wakes." He held her gaze, and put everything he had into the next words, and what he had, after the Spire's fall and the caves and the ford and the weeping crown, was considerable. "And when she is taken—you kill me first. Before she sees. Promise it."

Serane studied him for a long moment. He watched her weigh it: the boy's surrender, the detail of the wound, the grimoire, the timing—all of it fitting together like a lock accepting a key. He watched her decide that grief had done her work for her, that the last heir of the Spire-folk had simply run out of the strength to be noble.

People always believed that story. It was the easiest story in the world to believe about another person.

"Dusk," Serane said. "You'll ride with us to the hollow. When the girl is secured, you get your knife." She turned to Doss at the top of the stairs. "Twenty men. Quiet approach. And bring the boy—he knows the ground."

* * *

They came up through the mist at dusk exactly as he had known they would, twenty soldiers moving soft through the bracken with the boy slung riderless on a lead-rope in their midst, and Vael counted the ground beneath his bare feet the way his people had once counted stars: the flat shelf of limestone, the ring of grey monoliths rising out of the fog ahead, the hum in the earth that had become, over three days of pilgrimage, as loud to him as a choir.

The Grey Court. Nine stones in a ring, older than Aldric, older than the free cities, older than the word *king*. The villagers feared it. The pilgrims had cleaned the moss from its faces and left offerings of bread and cut hair in its center, and the old law had woken in it like a sleeper turning over, and the old law was simple, the old law was beautiful, the old law was this:

*No hand raised within the circle shall fall. No oath sworn within the circle shall be broken. And any who enter bearing chains and malice shall find the circle's judgment waiting.*

The soldiers crossed the boundary stones without knowing it. Vael felt the moment they did—a shiver going through the fog, through the limestone, through his own marrow, the Grey Court opening its attention like an eye.

"Here," he said aloud, in the trade-tongue, so they would all understand. "This is the place. She sleeps beyond the third monolith."

Serane stepped past him into the ring, sword drawn, twenty men flowing in behind her, torches hissing in the damp. The fog thickened. The torchlight shrank. And Vael, standing at the circle's edge with the lead-rope slack in a dead soldier's grip, spoke the words Yara had drilled into him until they lived in his mouth like his own heartbeat—the formal words, the binding words, the words that made a stranger's presence into a summons and a summons into a trial:

"*Grey Court, Grey Court, wake and see: chains and malice walk in thee.*"

The stones lit.

Not with flame—with memory. Light poured up out of the monoliths like water running backward, and in the light every soldier saw, for one endless heartbeat, the Spire whole and burning-bright and full of singing, and the covenant as it had been, and the price of breaking it, written in the old script across the inside of their own skulls. Men screamed. Men dropped swords. Doss swung at a monolith with his axe and the axe came away from the stone ringing and shattered, and the recoil threw him to his knees.

Serane alone did not scream. She stood in the center of the blazing ring with her sword up and her face white as the limestone, and she looked—at last, truly looked—at the boy at the circle's edge, and understanding moved across her features like weather.

"The snare," she said quietly. "You gave me the girl's location, and it was this. You gave me *yourself*."

"I gave you my yes," Vael said. "That's what you came for. That's what the King sends you to harvest. Consent." He let the lead-rope fall. "You should have asked what I was consenting *to*."

The Grey Court judged. It was not cruel—it was never cruel; cruelty was inefficient, and the old law was older than cruelty—but it was thorough. Chains rusted to red dust. Swords fused to scabbards. Twenty soldiers walked backward out of the ring on legs that would carry them no faster than a walk for a month, and their malice went with them, wrung out of them like water from cloth, and several of them sat down in the bracken afterward and wept without knowing why, feeling suddenly, unbearably light.

Serane came out last. Her sword was gone; her hands, when she looked at them, were empty, and the writ she had served for thirty years felt suddenly like paper in her memory, just paper, just a dead king's hunger given letters. She stood facing the boy across the boundary stone, and the fog curled between them, and neither of them moved.

"You knew," she said. "Before I ever came down those stairs. You knew what you'd do."

"I had a good teacher." Vael's voice cracked on it, young again, exhausted, thirteen years old and carrying a nation's ghost. "Go back to your King, Inquisitor. Tell him the stones are waking. Tell him the wolf is out of the snare." He turned toward the mountain, toward the distant smudge of the Weeping Crown against the last light, and the pilgrims were already emerging from the mist around him, silent, hundreds strong now, falling into step.

Behind him, Serane stood a long moment in the ruined dusk. Then she turned south, toward the floodwaters and the King's advancing banners, and began to run.

Two days, she calculated. Two days before Aldric reached the summit—and now she understood, with the terrible clarity of a woman whose certainties had just been burned out of her, that he would not stop, could not stop, that the coal in his chest would drag him up that mountain whether he willed it or not.

She ran faster.
