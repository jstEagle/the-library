## 18. Spire Ascendant

The mountain had been singing for three days, and King Aldric Vane was tired of the song.

He heard it even through the walls of his command tent pitched at the shoulder of the Weeping Crown, where the pilgrim road bent upward through fields of grey scree. It was not a melody so much as a pressure—a low, patient thrum beneath the world, like a heart buried under stone that had finally remembered how to beat. His soldiers heard it too. He could see it in the way they hunched around their cookfires, in the way they did not sing marching songs anymore, in the way they glanced up at the summit where the third waystone stood like a broken tooth against the bruised sky.

They called it the mountain's voice. Serane called it the covenant waking. Aldric knew better.

It was the sound of something being taken back from him.

"Your Grace." Serane entered without waiting for the sentry's word—she never waited anymore, not since Greywater Ford, not since the boy had looked at her with those pale Spire eyes and walked out of her net as if nets were a thing invented by lesser creatures. Her armor was scorched along one arm where the stone-trap had breathed fire. She had not let anyone treat it. "The vanguard is ready. Two thousand on the upper road, eight hundred holding the pass. The pilgrims have stopped throwing stones."

"They've stopped doing anything," Aldric said. "That's what worries me."

Below them, strung along the switchbacks like beads of dark glass, his host waited in the pre-dawn cold. Above them, the summit shrine glowed faintly—not with torchlight, though torches burned there, but with something older, a light that came from inside the rock itself, seeping out through the cracks the way water seeps through a dam. And around the shrine, motionless as carvings, stood the pilgrims. Hundreds of them. Mountain folk in rough wool, free-city refugees with their bundles still tied, old men and children and one one-armed woman who had walked out of Thornfell with a shepherd's crook held like a spear.

They had not fought when the vanguard advanced yesterday. They had simply stood in the way, singing in a tongue no soldier could name, until the soldiers' nerve broke and the column withdrew to wait for dawn and orders.

"They think the boy will save them," Serane said.

"The boy thinks he'll save them." Aldric rose and buckled his sword—the stolen shard set into its pommel, humming in discordant answer to the mountain's song. Through the leather of his glove he felt it pulse, hungry, eager, and he hated how much the eagerness sounded like his own. "There is a difference, Inquisitor, and I intend to teach it to everyone on this mountain before the sun sets."

Serane studied him with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing. "And if the covenant takes you instead?"

Aldric smiled, and knew it was the smile that had made grown men sign away their cities. "Then I will have died reaching for eternity instead of kneeling here waiting for death to find me. That is the whole difference between us and them, Serane. They kneel. I climb."

* * *

Yara woke on the summit with frost in her hair and no idea why her face was wet.

She wiped it absently, the way you brush away rain, and looked out over the sea of clouds below the shrine, pink-edged now with the coming sun. The third waystone rose beside her, taller than two men, its surface crawling with script that glowed and dimmed like slow breathing. The Weeping Crown, the mountain folk called this place, because the stone wept constantly—a thin film of moisture that ran down its flanks and pooled at its base in a basin carved by hands older than any kingdom. The water tasted of lightning. Vael had drunk from it once and wept for an hour afterward, and would not say why.

Vael. She turned to find him standing at the shrine's edge, looking down at the army coiled on the roads below, and her chest did the thing it always did now—that tightening, that fierce and nameless ache, love with no history attached to it, love that had arrived fully formed the way a wound arrives fully bleeding. She did not remember learning to love him. She did not remember much of anything: a name she'd been told was hers, a mistress whose face was a blank wall in her mind, three stones paid for with pieces of herself she could no longer count. But him. Him she knew the way the mountain knew its own weight.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I don't need to anymore." He said it lightly, and it was a lie, and she had learned to hear his lies—they had a particular texture, like silk drawn over stone. Since the second waystone he had been carrying something, some knowledge folded small and hidden deep, and every night she watched him carry it and said nothing, because whatever it was, he was not ready to give it, and she had learned that too: you cannot take a gift before it's offered or it breaks.

"The stone's been talking all night," she said instead, laying her palm against the waystone's flank. It was warm. It had been getting warmer for days, and the script beneath her hand moved now when she wasn't looking, rearranging itself like ants disturbed. "It keeps saying the same thing. *The door opens from either side.* I don't know what that means."

"It means," Vael said quietly, "that what happens today can go more than one way."

She looked at him sharply. The light from the stone caught his face, and she saw it again—the change that had been growing on him since the Hollow Root, since the second payment. His skin had begun to hold light the way the stone did, faintly, from within. When he moved quickly, she sometimes saw afterimages. The mountain folk had started calling him *the young spire*, and touching their foreheads when he passed, and each time they did, something in his expression closed another door.

"Vael." She crossed to him, took his hands. They were cool as riverbed stones. "Whatever you're planning—whatever you decided down there in the dark that you haven't told me—I want you to know that I can't remember my own mother's face. I can't remember the woman who raised me, except that I loved her so much it left a hole shaped exactly like her. I gave those things away because they were mine to give. That was the whole point. Freely given." She gripped harder. "So whatever the price is today, you don't get to pay it alone. That's not how giving works."

For a moment something cracked open in his face—grief and gratitude and terror all at once—and then horns sounded from the lower road, and the moment was gone, swallowed by the day.

* * *

Aldric climbed.

He had ordered the ascent at first light, expecting resistance, and the resistance came—but not the kind his generals had war-gamed around the fires. As the vanguard reached the last switchback below the shrine, the mountain itself began to move.

Not earthquake. Not landslide. Something subtler and far worse. The scree fields shifted, stones rolling uphill against their own nature. Springs burst from dry rock and flooded the road in channels that curved, deliberately, around the pilgrims and swallowed soldiers' feet to the ankle. The wind reversed direction twice in an hour, carrying voices—old voices, multitudinous, speaking in a language that made the horses scream and two hundred men break and run back down toward the pass, dropping shields as they went.

And everywhere, the song grew louder. The covenant's heartbeat, quickening.

Aldric climbed through it with the shard-sword drawn, and where the blade pointed, the mountain's magic recoiled. He could feel it now clearly, the stolen fragment of Spire power in the pommel straining toward the summit like a dog toward home. Good. Let it strain. He would follow it up the mountain, and at the top he would drive the shard into the covenant's heart, and everything the Spire-folk had been—everything they had hoarded for a thousand years while kings like him scraped by on bread and iron—he would take into himself, and he would never again be a man who could be starved out, betrayed, buried, ended.

His father had knelt to the old covenant. Had paid tribute to mountains that never once answered a human prayer. Aldric had been seven years old when he'd understood that the world was divided into those who owned magic and those who rented the ground it sat on, and he had sworn, at seven, that he would rather be devoured than rent-paying.

He had not expected, at forty-odd, to feel so hollow about keeping the vow.

"Your Grace!" A captain stumbled up the switchback, blood sheeting down one side of his face. "The rocks—the rocks are *fighting* us, Your Grace, we can't—"

"Then stop fighting the rocks," Aldric said, "and fight the people. They're softer."

* * *

They came at noon, and Yara learned what a battle between an army and a mountain looked like.

It looked like chaos wearing order's clothes. The pilgrims did not charge; they sang, standing in crescents around the shrine, and the mountain answered them—stone rising where they willed it, wind screaming down the switchbacks, fog boiling up from nowhere to swallow whole companies. But Aldric's soldiers were many, and brave enough, and desperate, and the shard in the king's sword cut through the mountain's workings like a hot knife through frost. Wherever Aldric advanced, the magic died. Grass browned in his footprints. Waystone-light guttered.

Yara fought the only way she knew: hedge-craft and a knife and pure fury. She had no memories worth the name, but her body remembered things—how to throw salt into a man's eyes, how to fall and roll, how to run downhill without breaking her neck. She saved a shepherd-boy from a spear and nearly lost her own scalp to an axe. The one-armed woman with the crook fought beside her for a while, laughing like this was a festival, until a crossbow bolt took her through the throat, and Yara dragged her body behind the shrine wall and screamed at the sky, and the sky answered with thunder that had no clouds in it.

Through it all, Vael stood at the waystone, and the waystone drank him in.

She saw it happening and could not stop watching. With each hour the light in his skin brightened; with each hour the stone's script flowed faster, spiraling inward toward a single character at its heart that pulsed like a door about to open. And Vael's face—gods, his face. He looked the way she imagined a man looks stepping off a cliff he has spent his whole life climbing toward. Resolved. Grieving. *Ready.*

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to him, to the stone. "*No.*"

* * *

By late afternoon the battle had become a siege in miniature. Aldric's host held the lower slopes; the mountain held the summit; between them lay fifty yards of churned ground and several hundred dead who no longer cared whose side they'd been on. The king himself had fought his way to within thirty yards of the shrine, and nothing seemed able to touch him—the shard drank every working thrown at it, and men who charged him found their courage curdling into terror, their limbs forgetting obedience.

Yara crouched behind the shrine wall, bleeding from somewhere she couldn't identify, and watched Vael walk out into the open ground.

He walked slowly, arms loose at his sides, light pouring off him now like heat off summer stone. The fighting stuttered around him. Soldiers fell back. Even Aldric paused, sword lowered, staring.

Vael stopped ten paces from the king, and his voice carried across the whole battlefield, in both tongues, the Old Tongue and the trade speech, and Yara understood every word of both.

"You want what the Spire had," Vael said. "I am what the Spire has left. Come and take me, Aldric Vane, and see if it fits you."

"*Boy*—" Aldric began, and something in his voice was not entirely contempt, and Yara would think about that later, in the space between heartbeats.

But Vael was already turning, already walking back toward the waystone, already raising his hand to the pulsing character at its heart, and Yara understood—finally, completely, with the terrible clarity of a person who has nothing left to lose—what he had been hiding since the Hollow Root. What the final ritual required. What he had decided in the dark beneath the earth, alone, because he thought she would try to stop him.

*The new Spire must be a Spire heir, freely given, forever.*

Forever. Not death. Worse than death. An eternity of standing-stone, of consciousness locked in rock, of watching centuries with roots for veins. He meant to become the fourth pillar of the covenant. He meant to leave her.

She was moving before she knew she'd decided, out from behind the wall, across the killing ground, through a hail of arrows that the mountain batted aside like flies, and she caught his wrist just as his fingers touched the stone.

"Don't," she said.

"Yara." His voice cracked. "There's no other way. The letter—"

"I read the letter." Her own voice surprised her—steady, ringing, louder than it had any right to be. "Every word. You thought I wouldn't understand it, because I don't remember her, because I don't remember anything. But I remember *this*. I remember you." She pressed his hand flat against the stone and put hers over it, and the waystone shuddered beneath their palms like a living thing startled awake. "Her letter said the price can be paid by any who love the heir. *Any who love him.* Not just him. Not just blood. Love, Vael. Love is the currency. And I am—" her voice broke, and she let it, and kept talking anyway, shouting now over the roar of the battle, over the mountain's song, over the drums of her own heart, "—I am *made* of love for you. Every piece of me I have left. So take it. Take all of it. Take my name, take my past, take whatever's left in the well when the sun goes down—but you are *not* going into that stone alone, and you are *not* going into that stone forever, because the covenant doesn't need a god. It never needed a god. It needs two peoples who choose each other. So *choose*, damn you. Choose me, and let's pay for it together."

The battlefield had gone silent. Two armies stared. Somewhere below, Aldric Vane stood frozen with his stolen shard half-raised, watching a memoryless girl and the last heir of a murdered race hold hands against a glowing stone.

And the waystone, ancient and patient and starving, opened its character like a door—

and asked, in a voice like ten thousand years of falling snow:

***What do you offer?***

Yara drew breath to answer, and found she already knew the shape of the price, and that it was larger than anything, and that she would pay it anyway.

Beside her, Vael turned his hand over and laced his fingers through hers.

"Together," he whispered—in the Old Tongue, and then, for the first time in front of armies and kings, in hers.
