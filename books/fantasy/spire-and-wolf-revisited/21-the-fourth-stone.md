## 21. The Fourth Stone

The watchtower had been hers once by theft and was hers now by something slower and harder to name. She had stolen it in a winter of hunger, sleeping in its broken stairwell with a knife under her cheek and her mistress's grimoire wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs. She had come back to it in the spring after the war that never quite happened, and this time she had brought mortar, and timber, and Vael.

They rebuilt it from the top down, because that was how Vael insisted on doing things. *You start where the sky comes in,* he said in his careful, accented Common, *and you work your way toward the ground.* It made no sense to her as masonry logic, but she had learned by now that most of what he said made no sense until it did.

So they roofed first—new beams of mountain larch, slates pried and patched from the old guardhouse below—and then the walls, stone by stone, some of them hauled up from the collapsed outbuildings on a sledge Vael had taught two patient mules to pull. His hands had changed over the year since Greywater Ford and everything after. They were still slim, still marked faintly at the wrists with the silver filigree of Spire blood, but they cracked now in cold weather. They bled when a chisel slipped. He aged the way she did, a little more each season, and every time she noticed a new line at the corner of his eye she felt something complicated move through her—not grief exactly, not relief, but the shape of a bargain kept.

He was twenty-three years old now, or looked it. He would look thirty soon, then fifty, then old. He had chosen that. He had chosen it standing on the summit with Aldric's host breaking like a wave against the awakened mountain, and he had chosen it again afterward, quietly, when everyone who mattered had stopped watching, because—as he told her once, in the Old Tongue, translating for her word by word so she could hold each syllable up to the light—a gift that costs nothing is not a gift; it is only weather.

Yara thought about that sentence more than she admitted. She thought about it especially on mornings like this one, hauling slate up the outside stair in a shoulder-basket while frost still clung to the rope, when her body ached in ways that reminded her she was mortal all the way through, and when the empty places in her memory ached in ways that reminded her she was mortal in other directions too.

Because there were holes in her. She knew their shapes even if she could not see into them.

She knew she had been an apprentice. She knew she had run. She knew her mother's face was gone—not dead, just gone, like a room in a house she'd been locked out of—and she knew, though she could not have said how, that she had loved someone enormously and paid for the world with that love. The knowing sat in her like a scar sits in skin: proof of a wound, without the wound itself. Some nights she woke weeping and could not have told anyone why, and Vael would sit beside her without touching her, humming the lullaby she no longer recognized, and wait for the storm in her to pass.

*You gave too much,* he said once, after such a night.

*I gave what the stones asked.*

*The stones asked for three memories.* He had looked at her then with an expression she couldn't read—something between wonder and reproach. *You gave them four. You gave them your name's history twice over. I watched you do it and I could not stop you, because you were already gone into it.*

She hadn't answered. What was there to answer? The covenant held. The free cities had opened their gates. Children who would have been witch-hunter kindling were learning letters in Thornfell instead. If the price had been her past, then her past had been cheap, and she meant to keep thinking of it that way for as long as she could manage.

* * *

They found the fourth stone on a Tuesday, in the way that important things always arrive on unremarkable days.

It happened because of the cistern. The tower's original water system had been a marvel of the old garrison engineers—rain caught on the roof, fed through clay channels into a covered tank built into the bedrock beneath the ground floor—but centuries of neglect had filled it with rubble and root. They had been clearing it for a week, passing buckets of wet stone up a chain of hands, Vael below with a lantern because he was thinner and didn't complain about the dark.

Yara was on the ladder with a full bucket when she heard him go quiet.

Vael going quiet was different from other people going quiet. Other people's silence was absence. His was attention—the whole boy suddenly turned into an ear. She climbed back down and found him kneeling in the muck at the cistern's bottom, lantern raised, staring at the wall where the bedrock curved away into shadow.

There was a stone set into it that did not match.

It was waist-high, roughly square, and it should have been invisible—one block among hundreds—but the lantern light did something wrong around it. It fell *into* the stone rather than onto it, the way light falls into deep water. And across its face, half-erased by mineral stain, ran lines of carving: the interlocking knotwork of the waystones, the same pattern she had burned into her hands at Ashfall Pass and the Hollow Root and the Weeping Crown.

Her mouth went dry. "That's not possible."

Vael touched it with two fingers, the way he touched everything new—gently, as if asking permission. "It's warm," he said. Then, softer, in the Old Tongue: something she only had half the words for. *Stone-that-* something. *Remembers* something.

"Her letter," Yara said. Her voice came out strange. "Mistress Ilsevele's letter. She wrote about three stones. Three broken waystones, three memories. She drew me a map." She had burned that map into her mind so thoroughly that losing everything else hadn't touched it; the map lived somewhere below memory, in the marrow. "Three. There were three."

"There were three she knew of," Vael said.

They worked the rest of the day without speaking much, which was its own kind of language between them by now. They cleared the rubble from the stone's face. They found, behind a fall of older masonry, a channel cut into the bedrock running from the cistern floor up into the tower's foundations—a vein of the same pale quartz-thread that ran through all the waystones, dim as a dying ember but present. Alive. Waiting.

By dusk they had it exposed to its full height, and Yara stood in the cold cistern with her lantern and let herself feel what she felt, which was fear wearing the mask of curiosity.

Every other stone had taken something from her. What would this one want?

* * *

She dreamed of her mistress that night, which had never happened before.

Not a memory—she had none left to dream with. This was something else, a visitation shaped like memory, the way a mask is shaped like a face. Ilsevele stood in the tower's upper room as she had stood a hundred times in life, grey-robed, ink-stained, impatient, and she did not speak. She only pointed—at the floor, at the foundations, downward—and her expression was not warning. Yara searched it desperately for warning. It was not there.

It was, of all things, *apologetic*. And underneath the apology, faintly, unmistakably: hope.

She woke before dawn with tears drying on her face and no idea whose grief they belonged to, and went down to the cistern with the lantern while Vael still slept.

The stone was warmer than yesterday. She was sure of it. Warm as a hand.

"I don't know what you are," she told it. Speaking to stones was a habit the last two years had beaten into her; hedge-wives talked to their kettles, and she talked to covenant-stones, and both of them were probably mad, and both of them got results. "She never wrote about you. She hid you, maybe. Or forgot you. Or—" and here the thought arrived whole, the way true thoughts do, "—or she couldn't face you."

The quartz-thread pulsed. Once. Slow. Like something turning over in sleep.

Yara knelt. She pressed her palm flat against the carved face, expecting the old cold pull, the hook behind the navel, the sensation of a memory being drawn out of her like thread from a spool. She braced herself to lose something else—her knowledge of knots, the smell of the sea, whatever small coin she had left.

Nothing left her.

Instead, something *arrived*.

It came up through her arm like warmth returning to a frozen limb—painful, prickling, too much at once. A smell: woodsmoke and tallow. A sound: a woman's voice saying *again, Yara, your stitching is crooked again, patience is also a craft.* A flash of a kitchen, a table scored with knife-marks, a pair of hands—older hands, capable ones—guiding her smaller ones through a hem.

Her mistress's hands. Her mistress's kitchen. The first week of her apprenticeship, which she had known she must have lived and had known, with a specific and private agony, that she could not remember living.

Yara snatched her hand back and sat down hard in the cistern muck and shook.

When Vael found her there an hour later, she was still sitting, and the stone's light was fading back to ember-dim, and she managed only one sentence:

"It doesn't take. It *keeps*."

* * *

They studied it together for a month, and the shape of the truth emerged slowly, the way a figure emerges from fog—if you stare directly it vanishes, and if you live alongside it patiently it becomes undeniable.

The fourth stone was not part of the covenant. It was older than the covenant, or parallel to it, or perhaps simply *beside* it, the way a hearth is beside a house. It was a thing the Spire-folk had made long ago, Vael believed, for exactly the purpose it now served: a vessel of trust. When the old rites demanded payment of memory, the Spire-folk had not simply surrendered their pasts into darkness. They had given them *somewhere*. Deposited them, the way a farmer deposits seed grain against a bad winter. Held in trust. Recoverable.

"Not recoverable by taking," Vael said. He had spent whole evenings with his palm on the stone, listening in the Old Tongue to whatever the stone said back, and translating for her in fragments, and she could tell when he reached the edge of his own knowledge because his Common went simple and careful. "Recoverable by *earning*. That is what the carvings say. Memory is not property. You cannot steal it back, buy it back, force it back. It returns the way—" he searched, and smiled ruefully, "—the way trust returns. One kindness at a time. Each act that proves who you are unlocks a little of what you were."

"And who decides what counts as kind?"

"The stone." He shrugged. "Or perhaps the person you were decides, from inside the stone. Ilsevele's texts say the Spire-folk argued about it for three generations and never settled it."

Yara laughed—short, disbelieving, close to tears. "Three generations of philosophers and the answer is *be good and find out*."

"The best answers usually are," Vael said.

But she saw him watching her afterward with that unreadable expression again, and finally she made him give it words. They were on the roof, laying the last course of slate as autumn came down the valley in gold and smoke, and she asked him plainly, because they had earned plainness between them the hard way.

"You think I shouldn't try to get them back. My memories."

He set his hammer down. Below them the mountain road wound away, and on it, small in the distance, a party of pilgrims walked upward toward the summit shrine, singing—one of the covenant songs now, in both tongues, the way they were always sung now.

"I think," he said, "that the woman who reforged the covenant, who out-thought Serane, who stood on the Weeping Crown and emptied herself for strangers she will never meet—that woman exists because of what she gave up. I am afraid that if you take yourself back, piece by piece, you will become someone who *wouldn't have done it*. Someone who would have kept her mistress and let the cities burn." He met her eyes. "I am afraid of losing you to getting you back."

The wind moved through the unfinished eaves. Yara looked at her hands—scarred, competent, a stranger's hands in the sense that she could not remember earning half these scars.

"That's not how the stone works," she said at last. "I've been reading the carvings. My Old Tongue is better than yours thinks." He huffed, conceding. "It doesn't return memories to make you who you were. It returns them to show you *why you became who you are*. That's the difference. Taking them back isn't going backward. It's—" she groped for it, "—it's inheritance. You don't inherit your parents' house to become your parents. You inherit it to know what you're building on."

Vael was quiet a long moment. Then he picked up his hammer, and knocked the next slate home, and said, "Then we build the tower first. All of it. Roof tight, walls true, water clean. And when it's done—" the ghost of a smile, "—when it's done, we'll see what the stone thinks of us."

* * *

Winter came, and the tower stood finished, and they lit a fire in the great hearth on the first night of snow and called it, half-joking, half-not, a housewarming.

Pilgrims came up the road the next morning and left bread at the door, because that was what people did now for the keepers of the watchtower—the Wolf and the Spire, the children called them, though neither of them had chosen those names and both privately hated them. Yara handed out tea and splinted a pilgrim's wrist and sent two lost brothers from the lowlands back down with directions and a lecture. Vael sat by the fire teaching a shepherd's daughter her first three words of the Old Tongue, laughing when she mangled them, correcting gently.

And on the third evening of snow, alone at the cistern, Yara put her hand on the fourth stone and received, unasked, a memory of her mistress teaching her to make tea properly—which leaf, which heat, which silence—and understood that the stone had been watching. Not judging. Watching, the way a grandmother watches a child at her first honest work.

She wept a little, and laughed a little, and went up to the fire.

* * *

Spring found them re-hanging the tower's ancient door, which they had salvaged, planed, and bound in new iron. It was heavy work, awkward work, and they were arguing cheerfully about hinges when the knock came.

Not on their door—they had no door hung yet. On the *air*, if anything. A pressure, a shiver in the quartz-threads of the world, the feeling Yara had learned at three waystones and hoped never to feel again: magic moving with intent.

Vael went still, head cocked, listening with that total-body attention. "Below," he said. "The valley road. Something is coming up the mountain. Not pilgrims." His face had gone pale in a way she hadn't seen since the war. "Yara—it's *singing*. In the Old Tongue. Something is coming up the mountain singing the covenant songs wrong."

She took down her staff from beside the hearth, the one banded with waystone-quartz, and felt it hum against her palms in answer.

Together they stepped out onto the landing, into the bright spring air, and looked down the mountain road to see what the fourth stone's sisters had woken—and what, after everything, had come looking for the people who had rebuilt what was broken.

The road was empty to its first bend. But the bend itself was glowing.
