## 22. Spire and Wolf

### Yara

The morning the first pilgrims of spring reached Thornfell, Yara was teaching a girl of nine how to charm fever out of well water.

"Again," she said, folding the child's small hands over the cup. "The words are only half of it. The other half is meaning them."

"I do mean them," the girl protested.

"You mean you want to be done with your chores." Yara smiled and adjusted the girl's grip until her thumbs crossed, the old way, the way her mistress had once corrected her own clumsy hands in a kitchen that smelled of tansy and woodsmoke—a memory that lived now in the fourth stone at the foot of the tower, held in trust, returned to her in fragments whenever she earned one. She remembered the kitchen. She did not remember the woman who had stood beside her in it. She had learned to carry both facts without letting either crush her.

The girl scowled, breathed, tried again. This time she spoke the words as if they were true, and the water in the cup went still and clear as mountain glass.

"There," Yara said. "You see? The craft doesn't care where you were born or what name the cities gave you. It only cares whether you're honest."

That was the difference, she supposed, between hedge-craft taught in cellars and hedge-craft taught on an open hillside. For years after the war that never quite happened—after Aldric's host broke like a wave against the awakened mountain, after the free cities opened their gates to folk they'd once barred—she had kept her teaching hidden, out of habit, out of fear. Then one market day in Greywater she had caught herself whispering a charm behind her hand and thought: *I bled for this. I emptied myself for this. I will not whisper it.*

So she built a schoolroom against the rebuilt watchtower's eastern wall, with windows facing the valley, and hung no shutters over them at all.

The girl ran off with her charmed water, and Yara stood in the doorway a while, watching the road. It was a good road now—graded and graveled by pilgrim labor, marked with cairns every mile, each cairn topped with a chip of white quartz so it caught the light. Ten years ago it had been smugglers' track and witch-hunter ground. Now it carried farmers' daughters and city scholars and one memorable delegation of free-city aldermen who had come expecting a shrine and found instead a woman who made them weed her garden before she'd answer their questions about immortality.

She still didn't remember why she wept, sometimes, at the summit. But she had stopped needing to know.

Footsteps on the gravel—unhurried, familiar, the gait of someone who had walked this road more times than any living person could count. She didn't turn around.

"You're late," she said.

"The road was crowded." Vael came up beside her, and she looked at him sidelong the way she always did, checking, the way one checks a scar. He was forty-one years old this spring, though he'd been born—or made, or grown from Spire-light, the stories disagreed—more than three centuries before that. Mortality sat on him gently but unmistakably now: silver threading his dark hair at the temples, lines at the corners of his eyes, a knee that ached before rain. He wore it like a coat he'd chosen himself. "There's a family from the coast. They want to see the summit. And there's a boy asking questions I don't have answers for."

"You always say that. You always have answers."

"Not for this one." His voice had gone quiet in the particular way it did when the Old Tongue was moving underneath his human words. "He asked me what the spire dreams about."

Yara turned to look at him fully. Above the watchtower, above the terraced gardens and the schoolhouse and the pilgrims' hospice, the Spire itself rose from the summit crag—no longer the broken fang of ancient architecture but something whole again, something *grown*, its surface veined with living stone that warmed in sunlight and glimmered faintly at dusk. Vael's blood ran through it. Not all of him, not the way the old covenant would have demanded—all of him, forever, a boy dissolved into godhood—but enough. A willing tithe, renewed each year on the night of the long moon, finite and shared, exactly as he had insisted on the summit while Aldric's ash was still settling on the snow.

"And what did you tell him?" she asked.

"The truth. That I don't know. That it isn't mine to know anymore." He smiled, tired and real. "He seemed satisfied. Children usually are. It's adults who need the spire to be a god."

"It used to be worse than a god. It used to be a cage." She said it lightly, but she felt the weight of it—the grimoire's cold arithmetic, the original covenant written in master-and-ward, in keeper-and-kept, in a people sealed away to protect a realm that forgot them. They had reforged it differently. Two peoples. Equal voices. "Come inside. There's bread, and I want to hear about the coast family before we go up."

"We're going up today?"

"It's the equinox." She raised an eyebrow at him. "You forgot."

"I've been mortal for eleven years, Yara. I forget things now. It's one of my favorite features."

But he was smiling as he followed her in, and later, when they shouldered their packs for the climb, he took the steeper path without being asked—the one that wound past the fourth stone—because he knew she liked to stop there, and he never made her ask.

### Vael

The fourth stone stood where the old watchtower's shadow pooled longest, a waist-high pillar of grey rock that no mason had cut and no chisel had ever touched. Its face was smooth as river-worn glass, and if you laid your palm against it and waited, it showed you nothing at all—unless the stone judged otherwise.

Yara pressed her hand to it, the way she did every time they passed, and Vael watched her face with the patience of long practice.

For a moment—only a moment—her expression changed. Something moved behind her eyes like a fish under ice: a kitchen, perhaps, or a lullaby, or a woman's laugh. He knew which memories lived in the stone because she had told him, once, in the early years when she was still mapping the shape of her own absence: her mother's face, paid at Ashfall Pass; the choosing-memory, paid beneath the Hollow Root; and last and largest, given freely at the Weeping Crown, everything of her mistress—the person who had saved her, taught her, sung to her, betrayed her, and died for her, all tangled into one love she could no longer feel but had somehow, impossibly, not lost.

"She's showing me something new," Yara murmured. Her breath fogged on the cold air. "A door. A green door, and rain on it."

Vael felt the words land somewhere below his ribs. "I don't know that door."

"No. Neither do I." She took her hand back and flexed her fingers, and her face settled into its usual calm, the calm of a woman who had made peace with being a house with some rooms locked. "One act of kindness at a time. That's the price of entry. The stone is patient."

"The stone learned that from you."

"Don't flatter me, heir of the Spire. Walk."

They walked. The road climbed through pine shadow and then broke into open scree where the wind came off the high snowfields carrying the smell of stone and sun. Pilgrims dotted the path below them—a knot of coast-folk with their bright inland clothes, two hooded sisters from the free cities, a shepherd driving his flock along the lower switchbacks with the serene entitlement of a man whose family had grazed these slopes since before Aldric and would graze them long after everyone currently alive was dust. One of the shepherd's lambs broke loose and came bounding up toward them, and Vael crouched and let it butt its woolly head against his hand.

"They follow you," Yara said, catching up. "It's uncanny. Animals, children, that boy this morning."

"Lambs follow anyone with warm hands."

"Mm." She looked at him with the flat skepticism she'd perfected over a decade of not believing his deflections. "Say it in the Old Tongue."

He sighed. Then he said it—not the lamb, but the thing underneath, the thing she was actually asking. *They remember what I am. Even when I am mostly not that anymore.*

"I know," she said, though she couldn't have understood a word of it. Eleven years of lessons and her accent was still atrocious and her vocabulary still stopped short of anything requiring a subjunctive. But she knew. That was the covenant too, the one they'd never written down: *equal voices* meant he told her things in the language of his dead people and trusted her to hold them without translation.

They climbed. By midday the summit shrine came into view—the ring of standing stones around the Spire's base, each carved now in both scripts, the common letters and the flowing deep-cut glyphs of the Old Tongue side by side, so that no visitor could read one without seeing the other. Pilgrims had left offerings at the ring's edge: bread, salt, a child's knitted mitten, a soldier's broken sword hilt from the war that never quite happened. Vael touched each stone as he passed, and each one hummed faintly under his palm, recognizing him, the way a dog knows its household.

At the ring's center, the Spire rose white and faintly luminous against the blue. Once it had been a fortress-prison, a seal, a wall between peoples. Now it was simply itself: tall, alive, dreaming whatever it dreamed, keeping nothing out and holding everything together.

"Ready?" Yara asked.

"Whenever you are."

### Yara

They spoke the covenant at the hour when the shadows of the standing stones lay longest across the snow, pointing outward like the spokes of a wheel, the way they did twice a year, the way they had since the day the mountain woke.

Yara spoke first, in the common tongue, her voice carrying in the thin bright air:

*"Stone remembers. Blood is given, not taken. No people shall be sealed away for another's safety; no power shall be hoarded against another's hunger. What binds us binds us both. What frees one frees all."*

Then Vael answered in the Old Tongue, and even after eleven years she could only follow the shape of it, the rise and fall, the words like roots going down into the mountain—but she knew it matched hers line for line, because they had built it that way, sentence answering sentence, neither version primary, neither tongue the master's and neither the ward's.

When he finished, they spoke together—one voice in each language, overlapping, equal—and the Spire answered.

It always answered. A slow pulse of light down its length, a warmth in the standing stones, a sound below hearing like a great bell struck underwater. The pilgrims at the ring's edge bowed or wept or simply stared, according to their natures. The coast family's youngest child laughed out loud with pure delight, which Yara privately considered the most correct response of any ever offered at this spot.

And then it was done, and the silence came back, the good kind, the kind with wind and distance in it.

Yara sat down on the snow-wet granite, pulled her cloak around her, and looked out. From here she could see all of it: the road unspooling down the mountain, the watchtower with its schoolroom windows, the smoke of Greywater Ford rising peaceful where Brannoc had once held a bridge with his dying body. Beyond, the free cities glittered in the haze, gates open, walls more ornament than defense now. Somewhere south, the villages Serane had once interrogated with fire were planting their spring fields. Serane herself lived in the second of those villages, gray and quiet, growing herbs, sending Yara a letter every solstice that said little and meant, Yara suspected, everything an apology could hold without breaking apart.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. She didn't have to specify. It was an old question, worn smooth as the stones.

Vael considered it honestly, as he always did. "Sometimes I miss it. Not the power. The certainty. When I was the last heir and the covenant was failing, I knew exactly what I was for." He turned his hand over, studied the human lines of his palm, the callus from ten years of garden work. "Now I'm for whatever I choose. It's terrifying. I recommend it."

She laughed, and the sound went out over the snow, and somewhere below a bird startled up from the pines.

"Your turn," he said. "Regrets?"

She let the question sit. Below them the world went on—pilgrims descending, lambs bleating, the school waiting with its chalk and its fever-charms and its stubborn honest children. She had given this mountain everything: her mother's face, her chosen losses, the whole well of the woman who made her. In exchange she had received a life she could not fully account for, built on foundations she could not see, loved by a brother she had chosen without knowing why the choice had felt like coming home.

"No," she said, and found it was true. "Ask me again next equinox."

* * *

### Vael

They came down in the gold end of the afternoon, and at the fourth stone Yara stopped, as she always stopped, and laid her hand against the glass-smooth face.

This time she went very still.

"What is it?" Vael asked.

Her eyes had gone wide and wet, and she was laughing—laughing and crying at once, helplessly, the way people do when joy arrives wearing grief's coat. "A song," she whispered. "It's giving me a song. I don't know the words yet, but I know—I know it's a lullaby, and I know—" Her hand pressed harder against the stone. "Vael, I know I've heard it before. From her. I can almost—"

And there it was, the thing he had wondered about for eleven years and never dared to say aloud: whether the stone gave back only what was earned, or whether, sometimes, it gave back what was *wanted*. Whether memory could be inheritance rather than possession, passed hand to hand like flame.

"Sing it anyway," he said quietly. "Sing what you have."

So Yara Marchetti—who had emptied herself to the dregs to save two peoples, who taught fever-charms in open windows, who had forgotten her own history and built a better one—stood in the shadow of the watchtower with her hand on the fourth stone and sang a lullaby in a language she did not speak, in a voice that shook, while the last light of the equinox slid down the Spire like a blessing.

Vael joined her on the third verse. He knew the words. He had known them before he had a name.

And far below, on the road, the pilgrims stopped to listen, and the wolf that had run free on this mountain since the covenant broke and broke again and was remade lifted its head from the treeline and sang back—two voices, then three, then many, rising through the blue dusk, equal and uncounted, all the way home.
