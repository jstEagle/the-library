## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgot things. That was its whole purpose, though most of the people who lived in it had never thought about it that way.

Hollowmere sat in a bowl of green hills two days' ride from anywhere that mattered, and at the ridgeline—where the pines thinned and the wind came through the pass smelling of snow and distance—something happened to the world. Travelers who crossed down into the valley said their headaches eased. Old wounds ached less. A woman once swore her husband's fever broke the moment their cart rolled past the boundary stones, and she'd wept and kissed the ground, which everyone agreed was excessive.

What nobody in Hollowmere said aloud, because saying it aloud would have been dangerous, was that the forgetting worked both ways. Magic could not follow you down into the valley. It pooled on the far side of the ridgeline like rain against a roof and slid away. And anything magical already inside when the boundary settled—generations ago, or last week, no one knew—faded. Slowed. Went quiet.

Zephyrine Thornbury had spent twenty-two years being very, very quiet.

* * *

Morning began the way every morning began: with Marigold Dunmore's fist on the door of the healer's cottage and a voice like gravel in honey.

"Up. The Hedley boy took a fall off the mill dam. His mother's carrying him here now."

Zephyrine was already up. They were always already up; they'd learned young that the best way to be invisible was to be useful, and the best way to be useful was to never make anyone wait. They pulled on the smock that hung by the bed—too big, sleeves rolled three times—and went out into the grey-gold light of early autumn.

Master Dunmore was already at the worktable, laying out splints with the brisk economy of a woman who had set every bone in the valley at least twice. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a fence post, with hands that looked soft and were not.

"Mill dam," Zephyrine said.

"Mill dam." She didn't look up. "You'll hold his leg. You'll not flinch when he screams, because he'll scream, and if you flinch he'll think there's something to be afraid of."

"There is something to be afraid of."

"Of course there is. He doesn't need to know it." She glanced up then, and her eyes—pale as well water—did the thing they sometimes did, the quick assessing flicker, as if she were reading something written on Zephyrine's face that Zephyrine couldn't see. "Wash your hands. Boiled water. Go."

The Hedley boy was eleven and had broken his leg in two places, and he screamed exactly as predicted, and Zephyrine held the leg and did not flinch, and afterward they sat on the step behind the cottage with their heart still hammering and watched the swallows turn over the mere.

That was the day. That was what the days were: bones and fevers and birthing babies and drawing teeth, the long patient apprenticeship of being needed without being noticed. Master Dunmore had taken them in when they were seven, an orphan from down-country with no family and no history anyone cared to check, and she had taught them everything she knew except the one thing she didn't know she was teaching: how to live beside people without ever quite touching them.

Because at night, when the cottage lamp went dark and Master Dunmore's breathing went slow and even through the wall, Zephyrine got up, and went out, and spoke.

* * *

The first time they'd done it, they'd been four years old, before Hollowmere, before any of it. They'd looked at a carthorse sweating under too-heavy loads and said, in words that weren't words but arrived in their mouth anyway, *that hurts you*, and the horse had turned its head and looked at them with one wet brown eye, and something had passed between them that Zephyrine had no name for yet.

They learned the name later, the hard way. Beast-speaker. Traitor's gift. In the lowlands they burned for it—the crown riders made a public lesson of it, whenever they passed through, whenever someone talked. Zephyrine's mother had put them on a cart heading north with a farmer's wife and a lie about a dead aunt, and Zephyrine had never seen her again, and had spent the rest of their life understanding, in their bones, exactly why.

So: Hollowmere. Where magic died at the ridgeline. Where a gift like theirs went quiet—not gone, never gone, but muffled, like shouting through a wall. Down in the valley they could speak to the miller's dog and the dairy cats and the fat comfortable hens, and the animals heard them the way you hear someone calling from another room. Faintly. Patiently.

But up on the ridge, where the boundary thinned—

Up on the ridge, Zephyrine could *hear*.

* * *

They climbed at dusk, taking the shepherd's path behind the quarry, the one that wasn't on any map because shepherds don't need maps. The air cooled as they rose. The pines gave way to bare slopes of bracken gone copper, and the sky did its enormous evening business overhead, and Zephyrine walked the way they always walked up here—quickly, guiltily, like a thief going to a hoard.

At the saddle below the crags, they stopped, and breathed, and listened.

It came in slowly, the way feeling comes back to a cold hand. First the near things: a fox's mind moving along the slope below, bright and sly and uninterested in them. Then farther: ravens arguing over a kill on the high scree, their voices sharp as struck flint. Then the deep slow presences—the red deer in the high corrie, the old badger clan under the hawthorn roots, all of it layered and interwoven, a whole conversation the world was having with itself, and Zephyrine standing in the middle of it like a deaf child let into a concert hall.

*Little shadow,* said a voice above them, dry as paper. *You're late.*

Zephyrine looked up. The crow dropped out of the darkening sky and landed on the boundary stone—a lichen-crusted marker older than the village—and folded itself with the smug economy of its kind.

"I'm not late," Zephyrine said. "There's no appointed hour."

*There's always an appointed hour,* the crow said. Its name, as much as crows have names, was Scratch, and it had adopted Zephyrine three winters ago for reasons it had never adequately explained, though Zephyrine suspected it was mostly boredom. *And tonight you're late, because I've been sitting on this stone since sundown waiting to tell you something, and my patience is a finite resource.*

Zephyrine felt it then, the way you feel weather change behind your back. They sat down slowly on the cold grass. "Tell me."

Scratch hopped along the stone, head tilting, one eye black and wet and ancient. *Riders,* it said. *On the north road. Grey cloaks, iron hats, six of them, maybe eight. They stopped at the shrine at Coldwell Ford and burned it.*

"The ford's a day's ride from the pass."

*I know arithmetic, little shadow. I also know what grey cloaks mean.* The crow's voice lost some of its dryness. *I've flown over the lowlands. I've seen the posts at the crossroads. Your kind burns well, they say. That's what the soldiers say. Burned things burn.*

The wind moved through the bracken. Somewhere below, the valley lamps were coming on one by one, small and warm and utterly unaware. Zephyrine looked down at them—at the cottage where Master Dunmore would be banking the fire, at the inn where the miller would be drinking his two ales, at all of it, the whole fragile arrangement of a life built like a wall around a secret.

"They can't come in," Zephyrine said. It came out half a question. "Magic dies at the ridgeline. Their witch-finders, their charms, their—whatever they use to smell us out. None of it works here. Everyone knows that."

*Everyone knows that,* Scratch agreed. *And yet.*

"And yet."

*They're not riding fast,* the crow said. *Riders in a hurry are raiders. Riders slow are surveyors. They're counting chimneys, little shadow. Counting heads. Someone has told them there's something in this valley worth counting.*

Zephyrine's hands had gone cold. They tucked them under their arms and made themselves breathe the way Master Dunmore had taught them—slow, from the belly, the breath you use when the bleeding won't stop.

"Maybe they're just tax men."

*Maybe,* said Scratch, in the tone of a creature humoring a fool. *Tax men don't burn shrines.*

Below them, a dog barked in the village—ordinary, distant, saying nothing. But Zephyrine heard it now with the ridge's clarity, and beneath the bark was the simple animal truth of it: *strangers, strangers, strangers*, a thread of unease running through the valley's dogs like a current under ice. The horses in the Hedleys' field were restless. Even the dairy cats were jumpy.

The animals had known before Scratch told them. The animals always knew first.

"How long," Zephyrine said quietly, "before they reach the pass?"

*Two days,* Scratch said. *Three if it rains.* A pause, feathers shifting in the wind. *What will you do?*

What would they do. What could they do. They were a healer's apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a borrowed name and a gift they'd spent eighteen years burying so deep that some mornings they woke up genuinely unsure whether they'd dreamed the whole of it. There was nothing to do. There was nothing to be. There was only the valley, and the wall of silence around it, and the hope—the desperate, threadbare hope—that the forgetting held.

"Nothing," Zephyrine said. "I do nothing. I go home, and tomorrow I grind herbs and set bones, and if riders come asking questions I'm an orphan who talks to no one, because that part isn't even a lie."

Scratch regarded them for a long moment. Crows could not sigh, exactly, but this one managed something in the vicinity.

*You know,* it said, *I've watched you come up this hill three hundred nights, give or take. And every night you sit right there, right on the edge where the world gets loud, and you listen like a starving man at a window. And every morning you go back down and fold yourself up small enough to fit in that valley.* The crow hopped closer, its head low, one eye fixed. *One day the folding won't work. One day the world comes over the hill whether you've folded yourself or not. What then?*

"Then I suppose I find out how small I really am."

*That's not an answer.*

"It's the only one I have."

Scratch clicked its beak—an oddly gentle sound—and lifted off the stone, spiraling up into the last violet light. *Two days,* it said, dropping the words back like coins. *Sleep badly, little shadow. It's what I'd do.*

Then it was gone, and the ridge was just a ridge, and the great conversation of the world dimmed and dulled as Zephyrine walked back down toward the treeline, toward the boundary, toward the muffling silence that had kept them alive for eighteen years and had suddenly begun to feel less like a wall and more like a lid.

* * *

Master Dunmore was awake when Zephyrine slipped in past midnight. Of course she was. She sat by the banked fire with a cup of willow-bark tea she wasn't drinking, and she didn't ask where they'd been, because she never asked, and the not-asking was its own kind of conversation—one they'd been having for fifteen years, in which each of them pretended not to know what the other knew.

"Mrs. Aldous brought a ham," she said. "For the Hedley boy's leg. We'll eat half and she'll expect the other half back, so don't finish it."

"Noted."

Silence. The fire ticked. Zephyrine stood in the doorway with their cloak still damp from the valley dew, and found, to their surprise, that the words came out on their own.

"There were riders on the north road today. Grey cloaks."

Master Dunmore didn't startle. She set the cup down with a small precise click, and looked into the fire, and was quiet for so long that Zephyrine thought she might simply refuse the subject altogether.

"Betha Tull saw them too," she said finally. "Came by at dusk, all of a flutter. Says they turned off at Coldwell." Her jaw worked. "Elder Cassock's called a meeting for tomorrow night. Whole village."

"A meeting about what?"

"About what it always comes down to." She looked up at Zephyrine then, and her face in the firelight was old and tired and fond in the way that hurt worst of all. "About who to point at. There's always someone to point at, child. That's how these things work. The riders come, and the village asks itself who among us is worth less than the rest of us, and it answers, and it sleeps soundly after."

"You'd point at no one."

"I'd like to believe that." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the window, and looked out at the dark valley as if she could see the north road through the hills themselves. "I'm old, Zeph. I remember the last time riders came through, thirty years back. Good people pointed. People I'd delivered, people I'd sat with while their mothers died. They pointed, and they slept soundly, and I set the broken fingers of a girl they'd dragged through the square, and the next spring those same good people bought bread from me like nothing had happened." She turned. "People survive by forgetting. This whole valley is built on it. Maybe that's not wickedness. Maybe it's just what staying alive looks like, out here."

Zephyrine thought of Scratch on the boundary stone. *One day the folding won't work.*

"And if they point at me?"

Master Dunmore held their gaze, and did not look away, and did not promise anything, which was somehow the most honest kindness Zephyrine had ever been given.

"Then you run," she said. "Down the sheep track, over the saddle, before the sun's up. There's coin in the blue jar and my mother's ring under the floorboard by the hearth, and you don't come back for me, and you don't say goodbye, and you live." Her voice didn't shake. "Promise me the living part. The rest is details."

Zephyrine promised. They lay in their narrow bed after that and listened to the house settle, and did not sleep, and somewhere in the small hours heard, faint and far off, the dogs of the valley begin to bark—not all together, not in alarm, just one and then another, a ragged relay running down the length of Hollowmere like a rumor.

Strangers. Strangers. Strangers.

Two days, Scratch had said. Three if it rains.

It did not rain.
