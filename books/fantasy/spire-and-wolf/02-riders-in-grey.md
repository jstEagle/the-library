## 2. Riders in Grey

The crow came back at dawn, and it was wrong.

Zephyrine knew its voice the way they knew their own heartbeat—Bramble's caw had a scratch in it, a burr like a thorn caught in a wool sleeve, and for three years that scratch had been as familiar as the smell of the tannery or the bell of the chapel calling the valley to market. But this morning the crow landed on the sill of the stillroom window and made no sound at all. It just stood there, feathers slicked flat, eyes black and fixed, and when Zephyrine opened the shutter it hopped once closer and said, in the flat tone birds used only for facts too large to decorate:

*Riders. North road. Grey cloaks. Many.*

"How many is many?"

*More than trees have leaves on them.*

Crows exaggerated. That was the trouble with talking to crows; they were gossips and dramatists, and half of what they told you had to be wrung through a cloth of skepticism before you could drink it. But Bramble wasn't performing now. The bird's head was low, its wings tucked tight against its body, and there was a tremor running through it that Zephyrine had felt only twice before—once during the rockslide that closed the eastern pass, and once the night old Marta's barn burned with her ox inside.

Zephyrine looked out over Hollowmere.

Frost silvered the rooftops of the village below the healer's cottage, and woodsmoke rose straight up into windless air, forty threads of grey climbing toward a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Someone was already at the well. Someone else was arguing cheerfully with a mule by the mill. The baker's chimney smelled of rye and honey, and down in the paddock the goats were complaining to each other about the cold in their small bleating way, and none of it—none of it—knew yet.

Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years of this: frost and smoke and rye bread, the ordinary blessed machinery of an ordinary morning. Magic died at the ridgeline. Everyone said so, everyone believed it, and so the world beyond the pass—the wars, the purges, the burning times—had never followed them down into the valley. Hollowmere forgot things. That was its gift. It forgot travelers' faces within a season, forgot grudges by spring, forgot, generation by generation, that anything outside itself mattered.

Zephyrine had built a life inside that forgetting. Healer's apprentice by day: grinding willow bark, lancing boils, holding the hands of the dying while Master Ellery murmured the old prayers he only half believed. And by night—

By night, the other life. The true one. Sitting on the millstone under the moon while the valley's creatures came to them: foxes with torn ears wanting nothing but company, owls reporting the movement of voles as though it were court news, the old blind badger who liked to be told about the stars because she could no longer see them. A secret so complete that even here, even in a village that forgot everything, not one living soul suspected. Not even Ellery. *Especially* not Ellery, who had lost two friends to the pyres in his youth and taught Zephyrine, without ever saying it plainly, that silence was the first medicine.

"Riders," Zephyrine said again, tasting the word. "Grey cloaks."

Grey was not the crown's color. The crown rode in scarlet and gold, trumpets and banners. Grey was what riders wore when they didn't want to be seen until they chose to be seen.

Bramble shifted on the sill. *They stopped at the shrine on the ridge. They are reading the stones.*

"Reading—" Zephyrine went cold. The boundary stones. Old, older than the village, carved with spirals that no one in Hollowmere could translate anymore. Everyone said they marked where magic died. No one had ever asked what else they might mark.

*I will watch,* the crow said, and lifted off into the pale air.

Zephyrine stood at the window a long moment, watching the smoke rise, and then did the thing they had rehearsed in their head a hundred times across twenty-two years and never once believed they'd do: they began packing.

Not much. You couldn't take much and move quietly. The good knife. Dried herbs wrapped in waxed linen—yarrow, comfrey, the bitter green paste that pulled fever out of flesh. Their mother's ring, which they wore anyway. Bread. And then, because their hands kept shaking and the shaking needed somewhere to go, they sat down on the edge of the cot and pressed their palms against their knees and breathed until the room stopped tilting.

It might be nothing. Riders came through sometimes—tax collectors, surveyors, once a whole company lost in a snowstorm who'd stayed three days and left without remembering the village's name. Hollowmere forgot. Hollowmere always forgot.

But Bramble hadn't come home yet.

* * *

They came at noon, and the crow had been right about the number, and righter than any crow should be.

Thirty of them, maybe more, riding down the north road in a column so disciplined it looked poured rather than ridden. Grey cloaks, grey horses, faces beneath steel caps that gave away nothing—not cruelty, not mercy, just the blank patience of men being paid. At their center rode a woman in a grey cloak trimmed with white, and above the village, carried on a pole like a standard, hung a proclamation nailed to a board.

The valley gathered. Of course it did. Curiosity is the one thing Hollowmere never forgot how to feel. Farmers and millers, the baker with flour still on his forearms, children hoisted onto fences—all of them lining the road as the riders drew up before the moot-oak, and the woman in white trim dismounted, unrolled a scroll, and read in a voice trained to carry:

"By decree of Her Majesty Vessane, First of Her Name, Protector of the Realm: let it be known that beast-speakers—those abominations who traffic in the voices of animals—are traitors to the crown and enemies of all honest folk. They are to be taken alive. Any who shelter them share their guilt. Any who name them earn the crown's mercy."

A pause, measured like a dose of medicine.

"The crown knows that such creatures nest in remote places, among simple people deceived by generations of superstition. The crown does not blame the deceived." Her eyes moved across the crowd, unhurried, cataloguing. "The crown rewards honesty."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on a crowd like snow on a roof. Zephyrine stood at the back, hood up, heart going so hard they could feel it in their teeth, and watched their neighbors' faces—and that was the worst part, worse than the proclamation, worse than the thirty grey cloaks. Because the faces weren't horrified. They were thoughtful.

Old Tam the miller glanced at his wife. The cooper's daughter whispered something behind her hand. These were people Zephyrine had bandaged and bled and sat with through fevers. People whose dogs Zephyrine had secretly soothed through thunderstorms, whose cattle Zephyrine had calmed by whispering to them across a fence line in words no human ear could catch. And every face in the crowd was doing arithmetic.

Master Ellery pushed forward through the press.

He was seventy-one and walked with a stick and had the kind of face that made authority itch—he'd been arguing with tax collectors since before the woman in white was born. "We're healers and farmers here," he said, loud enough for the whole road. "We don't hold with abominations. Never have. If there were any such creature in Hollowmere, we'd have burned it ourselves, crown or no crown."

Something in Zephyrine's chest cracked, quiet as an ice sheet shifting.

Because it was a lie, and it was meant kindly, and it was exactly the lie Zephyrine had spent twenty-two years building their life upon. *We're not like that. We're not your monsters.* Ellery was protecting the village the only way he knew how—by throwing the idea of the gifted to the wolves so the wolves would look elsewhere. It was strategy. It was survival. It was also, Zephyrine understood with terrible clarity, the sound of a community deciding, in advance and out loud, what it would do if one of its own was ever named.

It would do this. It would say *we'd have burned it ourselves.*

The woman in white smiled without warmth. "How reassuring. We'll begin with the outlying farms."

* * *

They took Wren Halloway at dusk.

She was the shepherd on the high pasture, sixty years old, weathered to leather, and she had never in her life hidden anything from anyone—which was why everyone assumed she had no secrets, and why no one had ever guessed that the reason her flocks thrived, the reason her dogs were the best-trained in three valleys, the reason sheep walked calmly to her knife instead of bolting, was that she talked to them. Had talked to them all her life. In the open, careless, thinking herself safe inside the valley's forgetting.

Zephyrine learned of it the way the village learned of it: as spectacle. The riders brought her down from the pasture bound at the wrists, walking between two soldiers, and set her before the moot-oak where the whole village had been summoned by the ringing of the chapel bell—the same bell that called them to market, to weddings, to the blessing of the fields.

Wren didn't cry. She stood there in her mud-caked skirts with her chin up and looked out at the faces she'd known for sixty years, and Zephyrine, pressed into the crowd beside the cooper's cart, saw her find her neighbors' eyes one by one. Looking for something. Not finding it.

"You all know me," Wren said. Her voice was steady. "I've lambed your ewes. I've pulled your children out of the river. Two of you I've pulled out of the river twice."

Nobody spoke.

"I talk to my sheep," she said simply. "I always have. It's not devilry. It's just—it's how I'm made. Like Tam whistles, like Ellery prays. It's how I'm *made*."

The woman in white stepped forward. "You confess freely?"

"I confess to what I am. I won't call it a crime."

"That distinction," the woman said, "will be weighed elsewhere." She turned to the crowd, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost kind, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing Zephyrine had heard in twenty-two years. "This woman lived among you for decades. Think on what that means. Think on who else might be hiding. The crown's mercy is not infinite, but it is real, and it is available *now*, tonight, to those who come forward. Tomorrow it will cost more."

Elder Maren stepped out of the crowd.

Maren was eighty, wide as a doorway, keeper of the village rolls, arbiter of boundary disputes, the closest thing Hollowmere had to a government. She had held Zephyrine as a baby. She had paid for Zephyrine's apprenticeship when their mother died. She walked to the front with her hands folded, and she bowed her head to the woman in white, and she said, in the reasonable tone she used for settling arguments over fence lines:

"There's no need for threats, mistress. Hollowmere is an honest village. We'll give you names."

The world went very far away and very quiet, the way it did when Zephyrine's patients were dying—everything receding except the essential thing, except the single fact at the center of the noise. Maren was talking. Maren was listing. Zephyrine heard the words *the shepherd* and *there was talk, years ago, of a tinker's boy* and *old stories, old rumors, we keep the rolls, we remember* and understood that the rolls—the precious, meticulous, beloved rolls—contained every oddity the valley had ever noticed and filed away and forgotten. Filed away. Forgotten. Hollowmere forgot things, yes.

Maren wrote them down first.

Zephyrine's name was not in the list. They heard that too, each name falling like a stone into a well, waiting for theirs, and it never came—because twenty-two years of silence buys you that much, at least; because a healer's apprentice who kept their mouth shut around animals was invisible even to a woman who remembered everything. Zephyrine stood in the darkening crowd and listened to their own death sentence being read out for other people, and felt the most shameful relief of their life pour through them like warm water, and hated themselves for it, and could not stop it.

Then Maren paused. Looked down at her own memory. And said, "And there's the herbalist's household. Ellery took in an orphan, years back. Strange child. Always off alone with the beasts. Always *too* good with them."

The world came back all at once.

Ellery moved before Zephyrine could even breathe. The old man planted himself between Maren and the riders, stick raised, and his voice cracked across the square like a whip: "That child is my apprentice and my responsibility, and they are no more a beast-speaker than you are, Maren, you foolish, frightened woman—"

"They healed the Hendersons' horse," Maren said, not looking at him, looking at the woman in white. "When the vet couldn't. It bit everyone else. It never bit them."

"It's a *healer*," Ellery roared. "That's what healers *do*—"

"Take him too," the woman in white said mildly. "Harboring."

What happened next happened fast and slow at once, the way disasters do. Soldiers moving through the crowd. The crowd parting—parting, that was the thing, nobody's shoulder accidentally blocking anyone, the whole village flowing aside like water around stones. Ellery swinging his stick and connecting once, gloriously, with a helmeted jaw before three men bore him down into the mud. Wren shouting something defiant that the wind took. The chapel bell still faintly ringing its summons to a congregation assembled to watch its own soul sold.

And Zephyrine standing frozen in the middle of it, hood up, invisible, alive.

*Run,* said a voice in their skull, and it took a moment to understand it was their own. *Run now, run while they're busy, run and live, this is what all those years of silence were FOR—*

But their feet wouldn't move, because Ellery was face-down in the mud of the village he'd served for fifty years, and he turned his head as they dragged him up, and his eyes swept the crowd once—quick, professional, the look of a man checking a wound—and found Zephyrine's.

He didn't call out. He didn't say their name. He looked at them for one second, mud on his face, blood in his mouth, and his eyes said with perfect clarity everything his mouth would never risk: *Go. Don't be a fool. Go north, go over the ridge, go anywhere, and never come back, and live.*

Then a soldier's fist ended the lesson, and they threw him in the wagon next to Wren Halloway, and the wagon started rolling toward the north road, toward the ridge, toward whatever waited beyond the valley where magic could follow.

The villagers stood in the square and watched the wagon go. Some of them wept. Zephyrine noted that, filed it away in some cold new room in their mind: *they wept*. As if tears settled anything. As if grief were a payment that excused the debt. Maren had traded names for mercy and gotten it—the woman in white had promised the village would be left in peace, mostly, provisionally—and the crowd was already dispersing, already murmuring about supper, because that was what communities did. They survived. They forgot.

Hollowmere forgets things. That was its gift.

Zephyrine walked home through the blue dusk with the packed bundle already waiting, and did not light a lamp, and did not weep, because weeping was a luxury with a price tag and they had finally, at twenty-two years old, read the full account of what their life here had cost. The valley had never been theirs. That was the truth sitting in their chest now, huge and cold and clean. They had been tolerated inside it the way a wild thing is tolerated at the edge of a field—welcome so long as it stayed invisible, hunted the moment it showed its shape. Twenty-two years of hiding had bought them nothing but a longer fall.

At the cottage, Bramble was waiting on the windowsill, feathers ruffled, eyes bright with fear.

*The wagon goes north,* the crow said. *Over the ridge. There is nothing over the ridge for captured ones. You know what happens to captured ones.*

"I know," Zephyrine said aloud, to an empty room, in a voice they barely recognized.

They slung the bundle over their shoulder, took the good knife, and looked once around the stillroom—at the mortar and pestle worn smooth by Ellery's hands, at the bundles of hanging herbs, at the small window where they'd sat a thousand nights listening to the valley breathe. Then they blew out the last ember of the fire, put the darkness on like a cloak, and slipped out the back door toward the pass.

Toward the ridge. Toward the place where magic died—or where, if the boundary stones meant what Bramble feared they meant, it had been waiting all along.

Behind them, Hollowmere's chimneys smoked peacefully into the night, forgetting already.
