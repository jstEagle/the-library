## 3. What the Wolf Carried

Zephyrine ran the way they had never been allowed to run in Hollowmere—loudly, carelessly, branches whipping their arms, breath tearing out of them in white rags. Behind them the valley lay dark and quiet, the way it always did, as if nothing had happened. As if Master Ellery had not been dragged from her own herb-garden by men in grey.

The elder had given up three names. Zephyrine's had not been one of them—but it would be. It was only a matter of hours before someone remembered the healer's apprentice who talked to crows, who was seen at odd hours on the ridge, whose mother had come from outside and never spoken of why. Fear made memories sharp. By morning the riders would have a fourth name, and Zephyrine intended to be miles gone when they came for it.

The pass opened ahead like a wound in the mountain. In summer it was a sheep-track; in late autumn it was a throat of grey scree and stunted pine, climbing toward the ridgeline where, everyone said, the world simply stopped mattering. Nothing crossed it. Nothing needed to. Hollowmere had chosen its forgetting a long time ago, and the mountains had agreed to keep the secret.

Zephyrine climbed until their legs burned and their lungs tasted of iron, and then they heard the sound.

At first they took it for wind moving through the pines—a low, broken whining that rose and fell. But wind did not stop when you stopped. Wind did not carry, underneath it, something that sounded horribly like *pain*.

They found the wolf thirty feet off the track, wedged into the hollow beneath a leaning slab of stone, as if it had crawled there deliberately to die out of sight. It was big—bigger than any wolf Zephyrine had seen even in the old woodcuts Master Ellery kept locked in her chest—and its coat was the colour of wet slate, except where the blood had soaked it black along its flank.

The bolt stood out just behind its shoulder. A crossbow bolt, fletched in white and gold.

Crown colours.

Zephyrine should have run. Every instinct Hollowmere had drilled into them for twenty-two years said *run*—a wounded wolf was death, a wolf shot by crown bolts was worse than death, it was *implication*, it was a thread leading straight back to the village and everyone in it. They took two steps backward. The wolf's head lifted.

Its eyes were amber, and they were wrong. Not animal-wrong. They were focused, deliberate, and they fixed on Zephyrine with an intensity that made the hair rise along their arms.

And then the wolf spoke.

"*Healer.*"

The word arrived not through Zephyrine's ears but somewhere behind them, the way all beast-speech did—arrived whole, without grammar, carrying more than it said. Zephyrine had heard fear from foxes and grief from cows and the dry gossip of crows their entire life. They had never heard a voice like this. It was disciplined. It was *hurting*. And it knew what Zephyrine was.

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, stupidly, to a wolf. "No, I'm not—I don't know what you—"

"*You hear me,*" the wolf said, and there was no mistaking it now, the words pressing against the inside of Zephyrine's skull like fingers against a bruise. "*Twenty years I have carried this. Twenty years looking for the one thing this dead valley was supposed to contain. Do not waste my blood telling me lies.*"

The wolf tried to rise. Its forelegs shook, held for a moment, and then it slumped back against the stone with a sound that was almost human. The blood beneath it had spread. It had been lying here long enough to soak the ground, which meant it had been lying here since before dawn—which meant it had come over the ridgeline in the night, past wards and warnings and twenty-two years of careful nothing, dragging a crown-fletched bolt in its body.

"How," Zephyrine whispered. "How did you get in? Nothing crosses. Magic doesn't—"

"*Magic does not follow,*" the wolf said. "*I know. I walked in anyway. What hunts me does not need magic to kill. Only silver.*" Its muzzle dipped toward the bolt. "*It is not the wound that will end me. It is what the wound carries. Cold iron core, blessed in a queen's chapel. It eats outward. You can feel it if you put your hands on me. And you are going to put your hands on me, healer, because you have already decided to.*"

Zephyrine looked at their own hands. They had, apparently, already knelt.

"I'm an apprentice," they said. "Herbs. Poultices. Setting bones. I've never—" They stopped. Lied to a dying wolf about the one thing that mattered. "There's nothing I can do for a wound like that."

"*There is,*" the wolf said. "*Not here. Here your gift is a candle in a cellar. Beyond the ridge—"*

"Don't." The word came out cracked. "Don't say beyond the ridge."

"*You know what waits there.*"

Of course they knew. Everyone in Hollowmere knew, in the way children know things they're told never to think about—that past the pass the world was wide and loud and full of the gifted, and full too of the people who burned them. That the valley's silence was a mercy and a shroud both. Zephyrine had spent their whole life standing on the near side of an invisible line, pressing their palm against it, feeling nothing, being nothing, safe.

"You want me to carry you over it," Zephyrine said. "Out of the valley. With crown riders behind me and a crown bolt in you. You want me to walk into the exact world my mother died keeping me out of."

"*I want you to listen first,*" the wolf said. "*Then decide. I am owed that much. He is owed that much.*"

"He?"

The wolf was quiet a moment. When it spoke again, its voice had changed—lower, slower, weighted like something carried too far.

"*Aldric,*" it said. "*King Aldric of the Ninefold Crown. My master. My—*" A pause, and Zephyrine felt, bleeding through the beast-speech, something enormous and grief-shaped that the wolf could not find a word for. "*He is alive. He is alive and he is screaming, and no one in his kingdom can hear him, because the man wearing his face has ordered every ear that might listen burned out of the realm.*"

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere below, very far away now, a dog barked in Hollowmere, and Zephyrine thought of Master Ellery's hands tied, of the elder's bowed head, of the proclamation nailed to the meeting-house door. *All who hold speech with beasts are traitors to the crown.*

"The king," Zephyrine said slowly, "is in the capital. Everyone knows the king. He rode through the lowlands last spring—the Harvest Progress. He gave a speech about unity. About cleansing." Their stomach turned as they said it. "That's the man who sent the riders."

"*That,*" said the wolf, "*is not the man. That is Vessane. She wore his face across four hundred miles and no one saw, because seeing is the one thing glamour cannot survive and she made sure no one dared look. The real Aldric has not left his own body in two years. She bound him inside it—wove the binding from his own crown, from his own oath, so that it cannot be cut by steel or unmade by sorcery. He watches her rule through his eyes. He hears her speak with his mouth. And he screams, healer, he screams day and night, and the scream goes nowhere because she killed or bought every voice that might have carried it.*"

"*Every voice but mine,*" the wolf added, softer. "*She could not buy what she could not see. Hounds do not count as voices, to queens.*"

Zephyrine sat back on their heels. The scree was cold through their trousers. This was madness—this was exactly the kind of story the valley told to frighten itself, usurpers and bindings and kings in cages, tales from the outside world where everything was large and terrible. Except the wolf was real. The bolt was real. The white-and-gold fletching was real, and the blood soaking into the mountain was real, and the voice in their head was real in a way nothing in Hollowmere had ever quite been.

"Why me?" they asked. "Why does it matter if I hear? I'm nobody. I'm an apprentice who talks to chickens."

"*Because the binding has an anchor,*" the wolf said. "*Vessane is clever. She anchored it in living flesh—not hers, not his, something she believed no one would ever reach, no one would ever pity, no one would ever *speak to*. Sorcery cannot touch it. Steel cannot. But flesh answers to flesh, healer, and there is one kind of voice that can ask a suffering creature to let go.*" The amber eyes held Zephyrine's. "*Only a beast-speaker can reach the anchor. And I have crossed half a kingdom and taken a bolt to find the last one alive who does not yet know they are hunted.*"

"That's not true," Zephyrine said. "Master Ellery—"

The wolf was silent. And in the silence Zephyrine understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, why the riders had come to Hollowmere at all. Why now. Why the proclamation, why the names, why the sudden ferocity of a crown that had ignored the valley for generations.

They weren't hunting beast-speakers because of an old law.

They were hunting because one was missing.

"They felt you," Zephyrine whispered. "Crossing the line. Your magic—it rang like a bell, didn't it? And she's been searching ever since, burning every valley, every hedge-witch, every child who whispers to cats—looking for *you*, or whatever followed you in."

"*Yes,*" the wolf said simply. "*So you see. There is no staying. There is no hiding longer. The valley was yours for twenty-two years because no one was looking. Now they are looking. Now, healer, you are the most valuable living creature in the Ninefold Kingdom, and the second most valuable is dying under this stone.*"

Zephyrine's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against their knees and breathed, the way Master Ellery had taught them—*in for the wound, out for the fear*—and looked at the wolf properly for the first time. At the grey coat matted with blood. At the great ribs working like bellows. At the bolt, white-and-gold, riding in the muscle like a splinter of the whole rotten world.

"What's your name?" they asked, because it seemed suddenly important, and because asking it was easier than deciding anything.

Something flickered through the beast-speech—surprise, maybe, or the ghost of an old pleasure.

"*Later,*" the wolf said. "*Names are for the living. Get me over the ridge, and I will give you mine.*"

"And if I don't? If I leave you here and go north alone, over the top, and never think about kings again?"

"*Then I die,*" the wolf said, "*and the message dies with me, and Aldric screams until Vessane tires of his body and burns it, and then she marches on the free cities with a bound king's face and an army that believes it serves him. And you live, healer. You hide, and you survive, and some winter night twenty years from now you will hear a dog dreaming and understand every word, and you will remember that once, on a mountain, something asked you for help and you chose the cellar over the sky.*"

The wolf laid its head down on its forepaws. Not surrendering, Zephyrine realized. Waiting. It had crawled over a mountain range with a blessed bolt eating toward its heart, and it had done it on the strength of one certainty, and now it had delivered its message and it would wait, patiently, for a frightened apprentice to decide whether the world was worth the trouble.

Zephyrine thought of Master Ellery's face as they led her away—she had looked back, once, directly at Zephyrine, and her expression had not been fear. It had been instruction. *Go. Be what you are somewhere it won't kill you.*

Well. Somewhere it wouldn't kill them had just stopped existing.

"The healing," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Beyond the ridge. I've never worked with— with actual power. Only herbs and hands. I don't know what happens to me out there."

"*Neither do I,*" the wolf said. "*But I know what happens to me here.*"

Zephyrine stood. Below them, Hollowmere was a smear of smoke and lamplight in the gathering dusk, small enough to cup in two hands, and they understood that they were looking at it for the last time. Grief hit them sideways—grief for the garden, the quiet, the crow who brought them buttons, the whole careful architecture of a hidden life—and they let it come, and they let it pass, because there would be time for grieving later or there would be nothing at all.

They crouched and slid their arms under the wolf's body, and the wolf weighed nothing at all, which was its own kind of terrible.

"Hold on," Zephyrine said. "Both of us. We hold on."

The wolf's breath stirred their hair—warm, ragged, trusting.

"*North,*" it murmured, as Zephyrine turned toward the ridgeline and the last light beyond it. "*Toward Marrowgate. Find the smuggler called Sable. And healer—walk quickly. The bolt is colder than it was an hour ago.*"

Zephyrine climbed.
