## 5. Road of Ash

The lowlands announced themselves by smell before anything else.

For three days Zephyrine had been descending out of the high country, Fen limping at their side, and for three days the wind had carried it: woodsmoke, yes, but under the smoke something sweeter and fouler, like meat left too long in the sun. The wolf's nose knew it first. Each morning he would stop at the crest of a hill, nostrils working, ears flat, and look back at Zephyrine with an expression that needed no beast-speech to translate.

*Turn around,* that look said. *Go back up where the air is clean.*

But there was no going back. Hollowmere had traded its names to grey riders, and the pass behind them was crawling with soldiers who had orders to take beast-speakers alive — which meant, Zephyrine had learned from the crow's gossip on the ridgeline, that they were taken alive exactly as long as it took to get them somewhere with a fire and an audience.

So they went down.

The road itself told the story before any town did. It was a good road, old and well-kept, running through orchards gone wild with late autumn apples, and it was empty. Not quiet-empty, not winter-empty. *Fled*-empty. A cart stood abandoned in a ditch with its axle snapped, still half-loaded with household goods — a copper pot, a bundle of children's clothing, a wooden horse with one leg chewed smooth by some small mouth. Zephyrine walked past it and made themselves not look. Then looked anyway. Then hated themselves for looking.

"They'll come back for it," they said aloud, because saying things aloud was a habit they were trying to build, a substitute for all the conversations they'd spent twenty-two years having with creatures who never judged a human voice. "People always come back for—"

Fen made a low sound in his chest, not quite a growl. The wound in his shoulder had closed now, knit by the borrowed magic of the ridge-top, but the memory of it was written in his gait, a hitch on the left side that would probably never leave him. He looked at the cart, then at Zephyrine, then down the road toward the smoke.

He didn't believe people were coming back either.

* * *

They found the first burned farmstead at dusk. Whoever had fled had done it recently enough that the rafters were still smoldering, and whoever had burned it had done it thoroughly enough that there was nothing left to come back for. In the yard, arranged with a deliberateness that turned Zephyrine's stomach cold, someone had nailed a dead rooster to the barn door. Beneath it, painted in something dark across the planks:

**BEAST-FRIEND**

Zephyrine stood in the yard a long time, until Fen pressed his warm flank against their leg.

"It's just a bird," they said. Their voice came out wrong. "It's just paint."

It was not just paint. It was a grammar lesson. It was the lowlands teaching Zephyrine, in the plainest terms available, what the word *gifted* meant out here where magic could reach — not whispered about, not hidden, but nailed to doors. In Hollowmere, the valley's silence had been a shield. Out here the silence was a grave being dug in advance, one farmhouse at a time.

They slept that night in the root cellar of the ruined house, curled against Fen's side in the dark among the smell of earth and ash, and Zephyrine lay awake listening to the wolf's heartbeat and thinking: *this is what I ran toward.* Not freedom. Just a bigger burning, seen from further away.

* * *

The market town was called Bracken Hollow, according to a leaning signpost, and they smelled it before they saw it — bread baking, tallow, river mud, and under everything again that sweet foulness, stronger now, close. Zephyrine wanted desperately to go around. They needed food; their supplies had run to a heel of hard cheese and a handful of dried plums, and Fen needed meat the way a healing body needs it, constantly, hungrily. But towns meant people, and people meant questions, and questions meant—

"We go in," Zephyrine said, mostly to convince themselves. "We buy food. We keep our head down. We don't talk to anyone except to pay them. We don't—" they glanced at Fen, "—and you stay in the trees outside. I mean it. A wolf walking through a market square is a proclamation all by itself."

Fen gave them a look of profound wolfish insult, as if to say that he had survived twenty years at a king's side without needing lessons in discretion from a herb-girl who talked to chickens. But he peeled away into the hedgerow at the town's edge, and Zephyrine went in alone.

Bracken Hollow was holding its market day the way a man holds a wound closed — grimly, knowing it might split open at any moment. The square was full enough, stalls enough, but the noise was wrong. Markets should roar. This one muttered. Farmers sold fast and wouldn't haggle. Mothers kept hands hooked in their children's collars. And everywhere, everywhere, people glanced north up the river road, toward the sound.

Because there was a sound. Faint, rhythmic, felt more than heard through the soles of Zephyrine's boots. Drums.

Zephyrine bought bread and a string of dried fish from a woman whose eyes never quite met theirs, and was turning to leave when the pigeons spoke.

Not in words — pigeons rarely bothered with words — but in the raw flood of panic that was their native tongue, pouring off the rooftops in a grey clattering wave: *wings-wings-wings-up-up-the-grey-men-the-grey-men-coming-running-running-* 

Zephyrine froze in the square with bread in their fist while every pigeon in Bracken Hollow took to the air at once.

Then the drums stopped, and the screaming started.

* * *

They came up the river road in column, maybe forty of them, and they were not the grey riders from Hollowmere. These wore black and white, chevroned in a pattern that hurt to look at directly, and at their head rode a woman in a mask of white porcelain shaped like a serene human face — no features but two eye-holes and a small smiling mouth. Behind the riders came a wagon, and on the wagon was a cage, and in the cage was a man.

Zephyrine understood, in one sick lurching instant, what kind of purge this was. Not a sweep for the hidden. A *demonstration*. Vessane's armies were marching on the free cities, and the roads between here and there were full of towns like this one, full of ordinary frightened people who needed to be taught what happened to those who harbored the gifted. You didn't burn a witch in secret. That taught nothing.

The caged man was shouting something. The crowd couldn't hear it over the drums' final flourish, but Zephyrine could feel it coming off him in waves — not words, not gift, just raw animal terror, the same terror Zephyrine had heard from foxes in snares and cattle in slaughter lines. The soldiers hauled him down and chained him to a post that had been set in the square's center, freshly planted, the earth around it still dark and turned.

A herald read from a scroll. The words rolled out flat and official: *by order of Her Majesty Queen Vessane, rightful regent of the realm, the crime of beast-craft is treason, the punishment is fire, and the harboring of the gifted is treason also.* The porcelain-masked woman dismounted and walked a slow circle around the crowd, and wherever she walked, dogs flattened. Zephyrine saw a carter's mastiff — a big confident brute — press its belly into the mud and wet itself, and the masked woman paused beside it almost fondly.

She was gifted herself. Zephyrine realized it with a horror that felt like falling. Whatever she was, she stank of power the way Fen had stank of silver, and she was using it to hunt her own kind.

"Any who shelter the marked share the marked's fate," the herald finished. "Witness, and be instructed."

They brought torches to the post.

And the crowd — this was the part Zephyrine would carry forever, the part that woke them for years afterward — the crowd did nothing. Worse than nothing. They *watched*. A baker's boy climbed onto a barrel for a better view. A woman shushed her crying child. Somewhere in the press of bodies, someone said, "Well, if he's one of them," in the tone of a person agreeing the weather had turned.

The caged man stopped shouting and started praying, and the torches went up, and Zephyrine's whole body became one clenched refusal — *move, do something, call the horses, stampede the wagons, scream, burn, DO SOMETHING* — and they did nothing, because doing something meant dying, meant Fen dying, meant the message in the wolf's head dying with them both, meant Aldric rotting in his own stolen body while Zephyrine's ashes drifted over a market square full of people who would say afterward, *well, if she was one of them.*

Invisibility had a price. Here it was, itemized.

The man screamed. The smoke rose straight up in the windless air. And Zephyrine stood in the crowd with their hood up and their teeth buried in their own lip until they tasted blood, and learned precisely what it cost to stay invisible while others burned.

* * *

It was almost over. The soldiers were remounting. The masked woman was back in her saddle. The crowd was already dispersing with that terrible fluid speed of people who want very much to have not seen what they saw — and that was when Zephyrine heard the child.

Not crying. Crying was everywhere, mothers hushing children all through the square. This was different. This was a small voice talking — *talking* — in the bright unselfconscious way of a child narrating the world to themselves, and the world the child was narrating included the dog.

"You're scared," the little voice said clearly, from beside a shuttered stall near the post. "I know. I'm scared too. But we can't run yet, my mama said wait till—"

The masked woman's head turned.

Zephyrine saw it happen — saw the porcelain face swing toward the stall, saw the woman's gloved hand rise, saw two soldiers peel off from the column and start walking, unhurried, toward a girl of perhaps six years standing next to a terrier-mongrel with its hackles up. The mother wasn't there. The mother had been in the crowd, and the crowd had closed behind her, and the child had been left at the edge of it like driftwood after a tide.

"—and your heart goes *fast-fast-fast*, I can hear it—"

"*There,*" the masked woman said, and her voice was pleasant, almost gentle, and it carried across the whole square. "You see how they hide among us? Even the smallest viper." She nudged her horse forward. "Bring the little one."

Something in Zephyrine broke cleanly, like a bone.

Later they would not remember deciding. They remembered the bread dropping from their hand. They remembered stepping out of the crowd's edge, and hearing their own voice — the voice they had spent twenty-two years burying, the voice that was a pyre waiting to happen — go out ahead of them in a low, carrying tone pitched exactly for one set of small ears:

"*Run to me. Now. Don't look back.*"

The girl's head snapped around. Children trust the voice that understands the dog; it's a rule older than kingdoms. She bolted, terrier at her heels, straight into Zephyrine's arms — and Zephyrine scooped her up and did the only thing left, the mad thing, the thing that painted a target on their own back: they spoke again, loudly, to the terrier, in front of forty soldiers and a whole muttering town.

"*Gate. Now. Run.*"

The dog shot across the square toward the south gate like a thrown stone, and every soldier watched it go, and in the half-second of collective confusion Zephyrine turned with the child in their arms and walked — walked, not ran, because running draws the eye — toward the alley behind the shuttered stall.

"Hold," the masked woman called, and her pleasant voice had frost in it now. "Where are you taking that child?"

Zephyrine did not answer. Answering was for people who existed. They put a cart between themselves and the square, then another, then slipped into the alley as the porcelain voice rose behind them, no longer gentle: "*Stop them.*"

Boots. Shouting. Zephyrine ran, the girl's arms locked around their neck, her breath sobbing hot against their throat, the terrier streaking ahead through gaps in fences as if it had lived in these alleys all its life and knew every gap — and it did, because Zephyrine had asked it, and it had answered, and the answering was a rope pulling them both toward the gallows.

"*Left,*" Zephyrine gasped, feeling the dog's route through the fence-lines, "*left, then the low wall, don't stop—*" and the girl, terrifyingly, obeyed, adjusting her grip, trusting absolutely, and they went over the low wall together as a crossbow bolt splintered the wood where their heads had been.

Beyond the wall was the riverbank, willows, mud. Beyond that, the hedgerows, the orchards, the darkening hills. And leaning over the wall ahead of them, ears pricked, was a grey wolf with a limp, wearing an expression of absolute fury.

"Fen—" Zephyrine panted.

He dropped back down on the far side without a sound. The terrier poured itself over the wall after him, and the girl reached down and caught the scruff of its neck, and Zephyrine ran with the child bouncing against their hip until the shouts faded and the drums did not resume and the only sound was their own ragged breathing and the river sliding past in the dark.

* * *

They walked most of the night. The girl — Maren, she said, when she finally spoke above a whisper; her mother had a stall, her mother had told her to wait, her mother had told her — fell asleep against Zephyrine's shoulder somewhere past the second mile, the terrier curled in the crook of her other arm. Fen ranged ahead and behind, silent, watchful, and twice brought them wide around farmhouses where dogs barked at their passing.

At dawn they found a shepherd's hut, empty, its flock scattered or stolen. Zephyrine got a fire going small and smokeless in the hearth, fed the child dried fish and the last of the bread, and sat looking at her across the flames while the terrier slept in her lap.

Her mother might find her. Word traveled; markets emptied along known roads; a woman looking for a daughter would ask at farms. Zephyrine wrote directions in charcoal on the hut's door — *south fork, shepherd's hut, two miles past the burned mill* — and hated every letter of it, because the hut was a landmark and landmarks drew soldiers, and if the wrong people read that door first—

No. Stop. One cruelty at a time.

When Maren woke, Zephyrine knelt to her level. "Your mama will come here. Do you understand? You wait inside, door shut, and Biscuit—" the terrier's name, extracted through careful questioning, "—Biscuit stays with you. Can you do that?"

Maren nodded solemnly. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "You talked to Biscuit. Like I do."

Zephyrine's chest went tight. "Yes."

"Mama says never let anyone hear me." The girl's chin trembled, just slightly. "That man at the post, he got heard."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "So listen to me, Maren. What you have isn't bad. It isn't wicked. The people who say so are afraid, and afraid people do cruel things, and the only way to beat them is to live. Live long, and get strong, and find the others. There are others. I promise you there are others." They heard their own voice shake and steadied it. "Can you be brave and quiet for me? For both of us?"

Maren considered this with the gravity of a queen reviewing troops. Then she leaned forward and kissed Zephyrine on the cheek, quick and fierce, the way children give blessings.

"You talk nice," she informed them. "Biscuit says so too."

They left her there with the fire banked and the door barred, and Zephyrine did not cry until the hut was long out of sight, and even then only for a minute, walking, because there was no time and the road was long.

* * *

Fen found them at midday, sitting on a hilltop overlooking the river road, watching the smoke of Bracken Hollow still smudging the southern sky. He sat down heavily beside them, wound-side to the wind, and leaned his weight into their shoulder the way he did when words — his or theirs — were beyond either of them.

"He was just a man," Zephyrine said at last. "The one they burned. Maybe he wasn't even gifted. Maybe somebody just *said* he was." They picked at the bark of the fallen log beneath them. "I stood there, Fen. I stood in that crowd and I did the sums. His life against yours. His life against the message. Against Aldric. Against all of it. And I chose, and the sum came out the way it came out, and I hate that I can do that sum at all."

Fen made a soft sound, deep in his chest.

"And then I turned around and threw it all away for one child in ten seconds flat." A laugh came out of Zephyrine, cracked and joyless. "So much for the sums."

The wolf regarded them steadily with amber eyes, and then he did something he had never done. He lifted his head and touched his nose, briefly, deliberately, to Zephyrine's cheek — the place Maren had kissed — and held it there.

Zephyrine closed their eyes. When they opened them, they were looking north, up the river road, toward the free cities and the war and the king bound in his own skin, and on the road below, dust rising.

Not a patrol. Too orderly for a patrol, too large. Wagons, banners, outriders — an army's advance elements, moving with the unhurried confidence of a force that owned every road it touched. And riding at the head of the column, white as a bone in the grey afternoon, a single figure on a pale horse, wearing a porcelain mask that caught the light and threw it back smiling.

The masked woman. Heading the same direction they were headed. Toward Marrowgate.

Zephyrine sank down behind the hillcrest, heart hammering, and felt Fen go rigid beside them, a low continuous growl building in his chest like distant thunder.

"She's going to the free cities," Zephyrine whispered. "Whatever's coming — whatever the loyalists are planning — she's going to be there first."

Below, the column stretched toward the horizon, and the road ahead ran straight as a blade between the burning fields, and there was no other road.
