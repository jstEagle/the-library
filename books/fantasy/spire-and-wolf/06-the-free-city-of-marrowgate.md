## 6. The Free City of Marrowgate

The road to Marrowgate was a river running the wrong way.

Zephyrine had never seen so many people in one place, and every one of them was moving away from something. Families with carts stacked past their axles. Old men leading goats that were too thin to milk. A woman carrying a loom's worth of thread on her back because she hadn't been able to carry the loom. Nobody spoke much. The road made its own sound—a shuffling, coughing, wheel-grinding murmur that went on for miles and swallowed individual voices whole.

Fen walked at their side with his head low and his ears flat, doing his best impression of a dog. It was a good impression. Zephyrine had spent three days coaching him on it: *no looking at people like you're measuring them for supper.* He'd taken it with the wounded dignity of a creature who had once been a king's hound and was now pretending to be a mutt of no particular ambition.

*How much farther,* Fen asked—not spoke, not in words, but in that pressure behind Zephyrine's eyes that was becoming as familiar as their own heartbeat.

"See the smoke?" Zephyrine kept their voice under the road's murmur. "That's Marrowgate. Free city. No crown law inside the walls—that's what the loyalists said before we left the pass."

*Free,* Fen repeated, in the tone of someone who had heard that word used as bait before.

"Yeah," Zephyrine said. "We'll see."

They had come down out of the high country four days ago, and four days had been enough to teach them what Vessane's purge looked like from the ground. Not armies. Not battles. Just absence. Villages where the smithy was cold and the door was nailed shut. A shrine on the roadside with its carved beasts chiseled off the lintel—someone had taken hammers to the old protective figures, leaving only smooth scars. And once, a gibbet at a crossroads with three bodies swinging on it, each wearing a placard: BEAST-LOVER. WITCH-KIN. TRAITOR'S BREED.

Zephyrine had made themselves look. That was the discipline they'd set for themselves after the market town—the one where they'd pulled a child out from under a collapsing stall while witch-hunters turned the square over, and nearly undone themselves by whispering to the man's terrified mule to keep it calm. *You look,* they told themselves now, walking past the gibbet with their fists in their pockets. *You look at all of it, or you start believing you're the only one it's happening to.*

But looking cost something. By the time the walls of Marrowgate rose out of the afternoon haze—grey stone, black with old rain, crowned with watchtowers flying no banner at all—Zephyrine felt scraped hollow.

The crowd thickened as the road bent toward the gates. Then stopped thickening, and simply stopped.

Zephyrine pushed forward through the press until they could see why. The gate stood open—actually open, which surprised them—but between its jaws stretched a line of humanity half a mile long, coiled back on itself like a snake swallowing its own tail. At the head of the line, city guards in mismatched armor checked papers, took coins, turned people away. Even from here Zephyrine could hear the rhythm of it: the plea, the refusal, the next plea.

"They're not letting anyone through," said a voice beside them. A drover, grey-bearded, with a cart full of children who weren't his—Zephyrine had noticed him two days back, collecting strays along the road the way some men collected debts. "Not without silver, and not fast even then. My cousin says the queue's been there since dawn yesterday."

"How long have you been waiting?"

"Since yesterday." He said it without bitterness, the way you'd report weather. "There's another way in. There always is. But it costs more than I've got."

Zephyrine looked at the wall. It ran away in both directions until haze ate it, but walls were walls—they ended somewhere, or they had doors, or they had cracks. And beneath them, Zephyrine could feel it already: rats. Hundreds of them, living their small busy lives in the foundations and the drains and the middens just outside the gate. The city breathed rat-noise like a sleeper breathes.

It would be nothing to ask one of them where the wall was thinnest. Nothing at all.

And that was exactly how people ended up on gibbets.

"No," Zephyrine murmured to themself, and Fen's ear flicked toward them, hearing what wasn't said.

*You could,* Fen observed.

"I know I could. That's the problem." They scratched him behind the ear, feeling the ridge of the old bolt-scar under his fur. "Every time I use it, it gets louder. You told me that yourself. The healer in the pass said my gift was deepening. If I go asking rats to map a city wall for me, somebody's going to feel it. Somebody always feels it."

*Then we wait in line.*

"With what money? We've got eleven coppers and a button." Zephyrine leaned against a milestone and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding. Eleven coppers bought bread, maybe, if they stretched it. It did not buy passage through a gate guarded by men whose wages came from a queen who burned beast-speakers. "We wait in line, we get to the front, they ask questions. Where are you from? Hollowmere. Never heard of it. Why are your papers wrong? Because I burned them crossing the pass. Who's the wolf?"

*Dog,* Fen corrected.

"Who's the enormous dog with the scar tissue and the eyes like a tax assessor?" Zephyrine sighed. "One good question and we're done."

Fen considered this with his chin on his paws. *There is another option.*

"There's always another option. That's what the drover said. What is it?"

*The smugglers.*

Zephyrine had heard the word whispered along the road for two days, always in the same hushed register people used for plague and pregnancy. *Smugglers move people through the water-gates. Smugglers charge an arm. Smugglers don't care what you're fleeing from, only what you can pay.* Half the stories called them saviors; the other half called them slavers with better manners.

"You want us to sell ourselves to smugglers."

*I want us to stop being proud,* Fen said, *while we still have something left to be proud with.*

Zephyrine laughed despite everything—a short, cracked sound that made the drover glance over. "Fine. Fine. But if they turn out to be slavers, you bite first and apologize never."

*That has always been my policy.*

* * *

Finding the smugglers was easier than finding bread, which should have been the first warning.

Zephyrine followed the rat-chatter—not asking anything, just listening, the way you'd listen to neighbors talk over a fence—and the rats near the eastern wall had opinions about a certain tinker's cart that parked by the tannery ditch every evening and sold tinware nobody ever seemed to buy. The rats thought the tinker smelled interesting. Of iron filings, lamp oil, and underneath it all, of other animals—dog, horse, hawk—coming and going.

Zephyrine found the cart at dusk. The tinker was a slight figure wrapped in a patched coat, sitting on the cart's tail with a mug of something steaming, watching the refugee fires scattered across the ditch-side with the mild attention of a person counting sheep.

"Tin," Zephyrine said. "I need tin."

"Do you." The tinker didn't look up. Voice low, unplaceable—neither young nor old, neither rough nor fine. "What sort?"

"The sort that opens gates."

Now the tinker looked up. Eyes dark and quick, face unremarkable in the deliberate way of someone who had practiced being forgettable. "Bold, talking like that to a stranger. Bold gets people drowned in ditches."

"So does waiting in that line with the wrong answers in my mouth."

A long pause. The tinker took a slow sip from the mug, and Zephyrine watched the quick eyes do a full inventory: boots worn but well-mended, hands clean-ish, accent valley-flat, no jewelry, no papers visible, a very large dog sitting with suspicious discipline. When the eyes came back up, something in them had shifted from assessment to interest.

"Wrong answers," the tinker said. "Such as?"

"Such as where I'm from. Such as why I'm here. Such as—" Zephyrine hesitated, then decided that hedging with this one would be worse than honesty. "Such as what I am."

The tinker went very still. Not alarmed-still. Interested-still. "And what are you?"

"Someone the queen's writ calls a traitor. Someone who can talk to animals." Zephyrine held the dark gaze. "And before you decide whether that's worth coin or worth reporting, ask yourself why a beast-speaker would walk up to a stranger by a tannery ditch and say so out loud."

"Because you're desperate."

"Because I'm careful," Zephyrine said. "Desperate people grab the first hand offered. I'm asking whether yours is worth grabbing."

Something flickered across the tinker's face—surprise, quickly buried, and under it something that might have been approval. The tinker set down the mug and stood, unfolding to a height barely past Zephyrine's shoulder.

"Sable," the tinker said. "That's the name you get. I move people. In through the water-gates, out through the culverts, sometimes both in one night. I don't ask your story and you don't invent me one. Price is eight silver for a single, twelve for a pair, and the dog counts as a pair because he eats like one and looks like trouble."

"We have eleven coppers and a button."

Sable's expression didn't change. "Then we have a problem."

"We have a negotiation," Zephyrine said. "I can't pay silver. But I can pay in kind. Your cart smells like every animal you've ever moved through this city—and I'd bet my button that half your routes depend on keeping horses calm in the water-gates and dogs quiet in the culverts and hawks from screaming when the guard walks past. I can make sure of all of it. Every run. No spooked animal betrays you, no barking dog brings the watch down on your cargo. That's worth more than eight silver, and you know it, because you've lost runs to exactly that."

Silence. Somewhere across the ditch, a refugee fire crackled and a child cried and was hushed. Sable studied Zephyrine for a long moment, and Zephyrine had the uncomfortable sense of being read like a ledger—every asset weighed, every liability noted, the sum total written down somewhere behind those dark eyes.

"That's a dangerous offer," Sable said finally. "You're describing your gift like it's a trade skill. Like a cooper describing barrel-making."

"It is a trade skill. It's also the thing that gets me killed. Both are true."

"And if I say no? If I decide a beast-speaker is worth more to me sold to the witch-hunters than working for me?"

"Then you'll be richer for about a week," Zephyrine said quietly. "Because the witch-hunters pay informers, and then they burn the informers too. Everyone knows it. Everyone does it anyway. You seem like someone who does the math all the way to the end."

For the first time, Sable smiled. It was a thin thing, more bared teeth than warmth, but it reached the eyes. "Everyone on this road does the math to the end. Most of them don't like the answer." The tinker jerked a thumb at Fen. "Does it talk back?"

"He talks plenty. Mostly criticism."

*Mostly accurate criticism,* Fen said.

Sable's eyebrows rose slightly, reading something in the exchange that Zephyrine couldn't hide. "Huh. It really does work both ways." The tinker folded arms into patched sleeves. "Here's terms. You work for me—animal-wrangling on runs, nothing else. I feed you, I shelter you, I move you north when there's a run going that direction, which won't be soon, so don't ask. You don't use your gift except when I say. You don't tell anyone what you are. And if the watch takes you, you never heard my name."

"And if you sell me instead?"

"Then you'll have been right about the week," Sable said. "Deal?"

Zephyrine looked at the small figure in the big coat—at the sharp eyes and the tin cart and the smell of iron filings and hidden animals—and felt the familiar cold calculus of survival settle over everything. This was not trust. This was a contract with teeth on both sides. Every instinct that had kept them alive for twenty-two years in Hollowmere said: *don't tie yourself to anyone, don't owe anyone, don't let anyone hold your name.*

But twenty-two years in Hollowmere had also taught them what solitary survival cost. It cost the mentor in chains. It cost the child in the burning market town. It cost the gibbets at the crossroads, seen alone, carried alone, grieved alone.

"Deal," Zephyrine said.

Sable spat in a palm and held it out—an old road custom, older than contracts. Zephyrine took it. The grip was dry and strong and brief.

"Don't sleep in the ditch," Sable said, turning back to the cart. "Tannery ditch after dark is where people go to disappear, and not in the fun way. You're with me tonight. There's a stable two streets in—I have an arrangement. Dog sleeps in the loft, you sleep in the straw, and tomorrow you learn what a water-gate smells like at three in the morning."

Zephyrine blinked. "Just like that? You're taking us in?"

Sable paused, one foot on the cart's wheel, and gave them a look that was almost pitying.

"Course not 'just like that.' You're an investment. Investments sleep indoors so they don't die of fever before they earn." The tinker climbed onto the bench and gathered the reins. "Besides—you did the math to the end. People who do the math to the end are rare. Rare is useful."

The cart rolled off through the dusk, and Zephyrine walked beside it with Fen pacing at their heel, leaving the refugee fires and the coiled queue and the eleven coppers behind. Ahead, the walls of Marrowgate went black against the last light, and somewhere beyond them, in the dark between the stones, hundreds of rats went about their business, and one of them, Zephyrine was almost certain, was already telling the others about the stranger who could listen.

They were three streets inside the walls, breathing air that smelled of smoke and fish and too many lives pressed together, when Sable spoke again, without turning around.

"One more term. Didn't think of it till now."

"Name it."

"If anyone ever asks you what you'd sell your gift for—" the tinker's voice was flat, unreadable, aimed at the road ahead, "—the answer's nothing. Whatever they're offering. However bad it gets. Because the day you trade it, you become the kind of person I'd have to lose sleep over. And I don't sleep much as it is."

Zephyrine stared at the patchwork back, at the small figure holding the reins of a tin cart full of secrets, and understood that somewhere in that strange bargain, without either of them naming it, a line had been drawn that wasn't in the contract at all.

"I don't sell what I am," Zephyrine said.

"Good." Sable clicked their tongue at the mule. "Then maybe we'll both live long enough to regret this."

Behind them, the gate guards were still turning refugees away by lamplight, and above the eastern wall the moon was rising thin and white as a bone—and Zephyrine, walking into the free city that wasn't free, felt for the first time since Hollowmere burned behind them that they were not entirely alone in the dark.

Which was, they were beginning to understand, exactly when the hunting got serious.
