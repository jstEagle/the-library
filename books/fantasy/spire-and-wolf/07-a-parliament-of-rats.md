## 7. A Parliament of Rats

The rats told me about the stranger before I ever saw their face.

That's the thing about Marrowgate nobody understands until they've lived in its guts. The city has a thousand eyes that don't blink and don't sleep and don't care about your bounty boards. Rats in the granaries. Rats in the sewers. Rats in the walls of every tavern where the Queen's men drink themselves stupid on confiscated wine. I've spent eleven years learning to listen to them—not the way a beast-speaker listens, I want to be clear about that, because if I could actually *talk* to the little bastards I'd be dead or rich by now. No. What I have is patience, a pouch of dried fish, and a memory for pattern that would frighten you if I showed you the half of it.

The rats know where the patrols walk. The rats know which cellars have grain and which have corpses. And the rats know, with the blunt certainty of creatures who have never once lied to me, that something had changed in the tanner's quarter.

They'd been moving wrong for two days.

* * *

Let me back up, because you'll want to know who's telling you this story, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from the rumors, which get everything wrong except the parts that ruin you.

My name is Sable. It's the only name I've got that's mine—everything else was given, taken, or traded away before I was old enough to know the difference. I run people. That's the trade. Not flesh—gods, no, never that, whatever the harbor rumors say—but people who need to be somewhere they aren't. Gifted folk mostly, since Vessane's purges started, though I moved smuggled silk and stolen relics in the lean years before. I've got three routes out of the city that the witch-hunters haven't found, a network of safehouses held together with bribes and blackmail and one genuinely sincere innkeeper, and a rule I've kept since the beginning:

Never be the most interesting thing in the room.

It's kept me alive through two regimes and one very memorable fire. And it's the rule I was breaking, two nights ago, when I followed a river of rats through the tanner's quarter to see what had them so stirred up.

* * *

Here's what I knew going in. For three days, the rats of the lower city had been behaving like soldiers. Moving in lanes. Avoiding the open ground of Tanner's Square. And—this was the part that made me put down my cup and put on my boots—*reporting*. I don't know another word for it. A rat would pop up on a wall, look at a patrol, and then scurry off, and ten minutes later the whole colony's pattern would shift around whatever it had seen. They were running information. Somebody was asking them for it.

There are maybe four people in Marrowgate who could do that, and I know where three of them sleep. The fourth was supposed to be a story.

So I followed. Down through the tannery rows where the smell of lime and hide scrapes the paint off your lungs, along the drainage cut where the water runs grey as old dishcloth, to a collapsed smokehouse at the end of Gutter Lane where the rats were flowing like a dark tide into a gap in the wall no wider than my forearm.

And out of that gap, a voice.

Not a shout. Not the chanting you get from the street prophets or the hissing of the poison-peddlers. Just—talking. Low, even, a little rough at the edges, the way you'd talk to a frightened animal, because that's exactly what it was. I couldn't make out the words at first. I pressed myself into the shadow of a rain barrel and I listened, and what I heard made the hair on my arms stand up like a dog's hackles.

The voice was saying *thank you*.

To the rats.

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt in that moment, because everyone who writes about the gifted writes about fear or worship, and both of those are lies of convenience. What I felt was recognition. Cold, unwelcome, and total.

I've spent my whole life translating. That's the smuggler's trade under the trade—you read a guard's shoulders, a magistrate's pause, a client's hands, and you say the thing that keeps the door open. I have been reading bodies since before I could read letters. I know what a lie looks like in the set of a jaw. I know what fear smells like on a person who's smiling. I built an entire life out of the space between what people say and what they mean, and here was someone standing in a ruin at midnight, holding a conversation with creatures nobody else even counts as alive, and I thought:

*That's me. That's what I do. They just do it better, and to a different audience.*

Then I thought: *and they're worth more than a shipload of silk to the right buyer, and dead to the wrong one, and either way I need to know which side of that line I'm standing on.*

I'm not going to pretend the second thought didn't come. It always comes. The difference between me and the people I move is that I've made peace with the ugliness in my own head. I just don't let it drive.

I waited until the voice went quiet and the rats dispersed—flowing back out through the wall in ones and twos, businesslike, a workforce dismissed—and then I watched the stranger come out into the moonlight, and I got my first look at the person the whole city's underworld was about to be talking about.

* * *

They were young. That was my first note. Young and tired and carrying themselves like someone who'd learned recently that the world had teeth, in the specific way of a person who used to think they were safe. A healer's build—lean, quick hands, the calluses you get from grinding herbs rather than hauling rope. A wolf at their side.

The wolf is its own paragraph, so let me give it one. It was big—not monstrous, but big, grey and silver with a healing wound high on the shoulder that it was favoring like a veteran favors an old campaign. And it was *watching me*. Not the way a dog watches a stranger. The way a soldier watches a treeline. It had marked me before I'd marked it, and it was deciding.

The stranger's head came up too, and their eyes found my barrel in the dark like the wolf had told them exactly where I was.

"Come out," they said. "Or don't. But stop breathing like that—it's upsetting the rats."

* * *

I stepped out. I had a knife on my hip and three more on my person, and I made sure the posture said *armed* while the voice said *harmless*, which is my native language.

"Evening," I said. "Nice night for a chat with the local government."

They stared at me. The wolf made a sound low in its chest—not quite a growl. A question.

"Rats," I said. "The city's real administration. You've been holding council. I'd know—I pay their salaries." I shook the pouch of dried fish so it rattled. "In fish."

"You *feed* them." The stranger's voice had gone careful. Wary the way I was wary, which meant they were smarter than the average fugitive. "Why?"

"Same reason you talk to them. Information keeps you alive down here. I just pay for mine instead of charming it out." I let a beat pass. "You're new. Off the ridge road, by the mud on your boots. And you're in trouble, or you wouldn't be sleeping in a smokehouse."

"And you're a stranger asking questions," they said. "In a city where asking the wrong questions gets you a hood over your head."

"True." I crouched, slow, and scattered a handful of fish at the edge of the lamplight. A rat came out of the wall within ten heartbeats—bold as a tax collector—and I gestured at it. "See that one? That's Pinch. She's been running the Gutter Lane colony for three years. She tells me you've got the whole lower quarter's rats organized into a watch rotation. Patrols, safe routes, the lot." I looked up at the stranger. "I've been running people out of this city for eleven years, friend, and I have never seen anyone do that. So you understand why I'm standing in a tannery yard at midnight instead of asleep."

The wolf had moved. I hadn't seen it move. It was just suddenly closer, between me and its person, and its teeth were showing in a way that was not a question anymore.

"Fen," the stranger said quietly. "No." And the wolf—stopped. Backed off. Not happy about it. I noted that too: one word, no gesture, and an animal that size chose obedience over instinct. That's not training. That's something else.

"You have a name?" I asked.

A long pause. I watched them weigh it—watched the exact moment they decided that I already knew too much for a lie to help. I've seen that moment on a hundred faces. It's the moment my business runs on.

"Zephyrine," they said.

* * *

We talked until the sky went the color of dirty pewter. I bought us a corner in the back of the Drowned Bell, which is the only tavern in Marrowgate where the owner is sincere and the ale is terrible and both facts keep the wrong crowd out. Zephyrine drank like someone who'd forgotten drink existed. The wolf lay under the table with its jaw resting on my boot, which I chose to interpret as a compliment and Zephyrine chose to apologize for.

They gave me the shape of it, not the details. The valley. The riders. The wolf shot with silver and carrying something that mattered—something about the king. I didn't push. Details are currency and I don't spend other people's.

What I gave them in return was the city. Vessane's purge schedule, as much as anyone outside the palace knows it. The bounty boards—new ones going up every week, and the prices climbing. The three routes out of Marrowgate that still worked, and the four that used to. The market town of Ashford, two days south, where I'd heard the witch-hunters had done their work in the open, in daylight, with the whole town made to watch.

Zephyrine went very still when I said Ashford.

"You know it," I said.

"I passed near it." Their voice had flattened out, the way voices do when someone is sitting on something hot. "There was a boy."

Ah, I thought. There's always a boy.

"Here's what I don't understand," I said, leaning forward, because this was the question I'd carried since the smokehouse. "You can speak to every animal in this city. You could raise an army of rats and pigeons and stray dogs that outnumbers the Queen's garrison ten to one. You could find every hidden cellar, every secret route, every informer, before breakfast. And instead you're hiding in a ruin, keeping your head down, asking the rats to *warn you* instead of asking them for anything." I tapped the table. "Why?"

The look they gave me was long and level and older than their face.

"Because the last time I used it where people could see," Zephyrine said, "my mentor was dragged out of her house in chains. Because every person who's ever been visible is dead or running. Because—" they stopped, and their hand found the wolf's ear, and the wolf leaned into it. "Because being invisible is the only thing that's ever kept me alive."

"Yeah," I said. "I know how that goes."

And here's the thing—I did. That was the whole problem with Zephyrine Thornbury, from the moment I met them. Every instinct I had said *this person is a walking bounty, walk away*. And underneath that instinct, quiet as a rat in a wall, was the other thing. The recognition. Eleven years I'd been the only person in Marrowgate who spoke fluent body, who lived in the gaps between what the world said and what it meant, who was useful to everyone and known by no one. I'd told myself it was a strategy. Sitting across from someone who'd never had the option of pretending, I started to suspect it had just been loneliness with good tradecraft.

* * *

"I need to get north," Zephyrine said, eventually. "There are people I have to find. The king's loyalists—the real ones, the ones who went to ground when Vessane took the throne. The wolf carries proof of where they can be reached, but the proof is—" they glanced at Fen. "It's not a letter. It's not something I can show a stranger on the road. I need someone who knows how to move quietly and who knows who's still worth trusting."

"North is where the bounties are thickest," I said. "And the loyalists, if they exist, are buried so deep even I've only heard rumors. You'd need a guide. Guides cost."

"I don't have much."

"You have something better than coin." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "You have the rats. You have the dogs. You have every pigeon on every rooftop in a city where my entire trade depends on knowing things before the other side does. You're not a passenger, friend. You're the best asset that's walked into my operation since the fire."

They flinched at *asset*. I saw it land and I didn't take it back, because I don't lie to clients about what they are. But I leaned in, and I lowered my voice, and I said the true thing, which I almost never do on a first night.

"Look. I move the hunted. That's the work. Every person I get over the wall is a person Vessane doesn't get to burn, and I've stopped pretending that's just business to me. You want north? I'll take you north. Not because you're useful—though you are, gods, you are—but because there's a boy in Ashford who's going to be on a bounty board by spring if nobody moves him, and a hundred like him, and I can't be in two places at once." I sat back. "You help me, I help you. That's the whole contract. No oaths. Oaths are for people who plan to break them."

Zephyrine looked at me for a long time. The wolf's eyes were on me too, and I had the distinct, unsettling sense that they were both deciding.

"Pinch," Zephyrine said at last, nodding at the wall behind me, where a rat had appeared, whiskers twitching. "She's been sitting there for ten minutes. She says you've never once lied to her."

"Fish is fish," I said. "Lying to your grocer is bad economics."

A corner of their mouth moved. First almost-smile of the night. "She also says you cried when the tannery dogs drowned. Three years ago. The flood."

The tavern noise seemed to get louder. I took a slow drink of terrible ale.

"Rats," I said finally, "are gossips."

"They're witnesses," Zephyrine said. "It's not the same thing."

And that was it. That was the moment, if you want it—the moment I decided. Not because of the asset, not because of the routes and the rumors and the reward at the end of whatever this wolf was carrying. Because they'd said *witnesses*, and meant it, and I realized I was sitting across from the first person in eleven years who understood the job the way I understood it: that the whole world is talking, all the time, and almost nobody listens, and the listening is the work.

"Tomorrow night," I said, standing, dropping coins on the table. "There's a cellar under the Old Tannery—your old tannery, the one you've been sleeping behind. Be there at moonrise. There are people you need to meet, and if half of what the wolf is carrying is true, they've been waiting for you a long time."

Zephyrine stood too. Up close they were younger than I'd thought, and more frightened, and more stubborn, all three at once.

"Why help me?" they asked. "You don't know me."

"No," I agreed. I pulled my hood up and headed for the door, and then I paused, because I figured they'd earned one honest sentence before I went back to being a professional.

"But the rats vouch for you," I said. "And in this city, that's a better reference than the Queen's seal."

* * *

I was three streets away, moving through the wet dark with my collar up, when I felt it—the small tug at the edge of my awareness, the scritch of claws on a windowsill. Pinch, pacing me along the rooftops, keeping easy distance.

I stopped. She stopped. We regarded each other across four feet of night air, one of us a smuggler with a price on her old life and the other a rat with fish breath, and I swear on every god I don't believe in that she was *smirking*.

"Yeah, yeah," I told her. "Tell your boss I'm in."

She flicked her tail and poured herself off the roofline into the dark, and I stood there a moment longer than was strictly safe, grinning like an idiot in a city that eats grinning idiots.

Somewhere south, a boy in Ashford was about to have a very bad week. Somewhere north, a buried king was waiting on a wolf. And somewhere in between, against every rule I'd kept for eleven years, I had just made myself the second most interesting thing in the realm.

The cellar meeting was at moonrise. I intended to be early.

I intended, for the first time in a long time, to bring someone with me.
