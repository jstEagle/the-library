## 8. The Loyalist Cellar

The tannery stank like a promise kept badly.

Fen and I came in from the river side, through a maze of drying yards where hides hung like flayed ghosts, and the smell of lime and rotting flesh was so thick I could taste it on my teeth. Fen didn't complain. Wolves don't, but he pressed close against my leg the whole way, which for him was the same as complaining.

"You're sure this is the place?" I murmured.

He huffed. In wolf-speech it was less a word than a shape of certainty: *the rat said under the vats, past the salt store, knock twice and once.* Rats give directions like they're narrating a fever dream, but Fen had made me repeat it three times until we both had it cold.

The door was low and iron-banded, sunk into the hillside behind the salt store like something buried alive. I knocked twice, then once. Long enough passed that I started composing my own eulogy—*here lies Zephyrine Thornbury, killed by a door*—before it swung inward on darkness and the business end of a crossbow.

"Name," said the darkness.

"The rat sent me."

A pause. "That's not a name."

"It's what I've got."

The crossbow lowered an inch. "The rat also said you'd bring a wolf."

"He insisted."

Another pause, longer, and then the man holding the crossbow stepped aside with the weary air of someone whose night had already gone wrong and was committed to seeing it through. "In, then. Both of you. Touch nothing, speak to no one until Ser Halvard says otherwise, and if your beast makes water on anything down here, I swear by every dead god I will make you lick it up."

"Fen's house-trained," I said, and slipped inside before he could decide whether that was insolence.

The stairs went down farther than any cellar had a right to. Torches burned at intervals—not many, just enough to make the dark feel deliberate—and the air changed as we descended, from tannery-stink to stone-cold and old, old smoke. Someone had lived down here a long time. Someone had *burned* things down here, more than once.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a vaulted chamber that had probably been a crypt once and was now something stranger: maps nailed to the walls, a long table scarred by candle-wax, racks of weapons that had been oiled recently and often. And people. Seven of them, maybe eight—a couple dozing on bedrolls against the far wall, one sharpening a sword with the patience of a woman who'd done it ten thousand times, and at the head of the table, a big grey-bearded man with a face like a cliff that had been arguing with the sea for forty years and losing slowly.

Ser Halvard, presumably. He looked up when we came in, and his eyes went straight past me to Fen, and stayed there.

"Well," he said. "It's true, then."

"What's true?"

"A beast-speaker." He said it the way other men said *a comet*, or *a plague*. "I'd begun to think the rats were embellishing. They do that." He waved at a stool. "Sit. And the wolf can—"

"He stays next to me."

Something flickered across the old knight's face—not offense. Recognition, maybe. "Of course he does." He waited until I sat, then folded his huge hands on the table. "Before we begin, understand what you've walked into. Everyone in this room is attainted. Traitors, by crown law. If Vessane's witch-hunters find us, we die badly, and so do you. There's no clever way out of that arithmetic. Do you still want to hear why you're here?"

"I walked through a tannery," I said. "My standards for suffering are very high right now."

One of the dozing knights snorted awake. Halvard almost smiled. Almost.

"Then let's not waste the candle." He pulled a cloth off the table's center, and underneath lay a crown.

Not a real one—a copy, worked in lead or pewter, dull and heavy-looking, its circlet ringed with nine spikes. But even the fake version did something to the room. The sharpening woman stopped. The dozers sat up. Fen's hackles lifted along his spine, a slow ripple, and a growl started somewhere deep in his chest that I felt through the floor before I heard it.

"That's—" I began.

"King Aldric's crown," Halvard said. "Or its likeness. We had a goldsmith among us, before. He worked from memory." His voice went flat and careful, the way voices go around wounds. "Three years ago, Aldric rode north to put down the Redwater rebellion. He came back eleven days later wearing his own face and speaking his own voice, and within a season he had dissolved the council, purged the courts, and named himself regent over a king who was, officially, 'seized by a wasting illness.' Except there was no illness. Because there was no king. Not anymore."

The torches crackled. Nobody moved.

"The thing that came back from Redwater," Halvard continued, "wears Aldric's face. A glamour—the deepest working I've ever seen or heard tell of. It doesn't sit on the skin; it sits in the eye of the beholder. You could cut that face open and see the skull beneath and still swear you were looking at your king. Half our order served him twenty years. We *know* him. We know how he stands, how he laughs, the scar on his left forearm from a boar at Caelmoor." He turned his hand over, showing an old white line across his own wrist. "And I have stood in an audience chamber not ten feet from the thing wearing him, and every part of me screamed *that is Aldric*, and I believed it, gods help me, I believed it for two years."

"And then?" I asked quietly.

"And then a kitchen boy saw something he shouldn't have." Halvard's jaw tightened. "A scullery lad, simple-minded, sweet-tempered. He told anyone who'd listen that the king smelled wrong. That the king's shadow moved a half-beat late. They flogged him for it. He kept saying it. They hanged him for it." Silence took the room for a moment. "His death bought us the first doubt. Doubt bought us a defector from the court—a laundress, then a clerk, then a captain. And the captain brought us this." He touched the leaden crown. "The truth of how it was done."

"How?" My mouth had gone dry.

"The binding isn't woven on the king's body. Any binding on flesh can be found and broken—that's the first law of the craft, and Vessane's sorcerers know it better than anyone. So they wove it elsewhere. Into something living, something that would never be searched, because no one thinks to look." He leaned forward. "They bound the spell into an animal. A creature kept close to the king, fed on his presence, tied to him blood and bone. While it lives and suffers, the binding holds. The king's soul sits chained behind his own eyes, watching everything, saying nothing, while that *thing* upstairs wears him like a coat."

Fen's growl had stopped. That was worse than the growling. He stood utterly rigid beside me, ears forward, staring at the leaden crown, and when I reached down to touch his shoulder his fur was damp and trembling.

"Fen," I whispered. "What is it?"

He didn't answer in words. He answered in images, pushed hard and fast into my mind the way he only did when something mattered more than pride: *a hall of banners. A fire. Running. Running. A door closing. A smell of iron and old magic. A stag—*

I went cold all over.

"There's a stag," I said. My voice came out strange. "In the royal menagerie. Isn't there? The false king keeps it. Everyone says it's a pet, a curiosity—he feeds it himself, they say, they say it like it's charming."

Every head at the table turned to me.

"Our captain reported the same," Halvard said slowly. "The menagerie stag. White-flanked, old, half-starved no matter how much they feed it. The court thinks the king is eccentrically fond of it." He studied me the way you study a map for a road you missed. "You know something."

"My wolf knows something." I swallowed. "He's not— he's not just a wolf. He was the king's hound. He was *there* when it happened. He's been trying to tell me since I found him, but he's hurt and the memories are—" I stopped, because Fen had laid his great head on my knee, deliberately, heavily, the way he did when he wanted me to stop talking and start listening.

Through the touch, clear as a bell rung underwater, came the rest.

*I ran. I should have stayed. The door closed and the screaming started and it wasn't screaming out loud, it was screaming inside, and I couldn't get back in. Three winters I've carried it. Three winters I've looked for a speaker who could hear me. The bolt caught me two valleys from finding one.*

"Oh," I said. Just that. Oh.

Halvard watched us both with the expression of a man watching weather turn. "Tell me," he said.

So I did. All of it—the crow on the north road, the riders in grey, Marta seized in her own doorway, the silver-cored bolt that should have been impossible inside Hollowmere's dead air, the message Fen carried from the king who was locked inside his own body. *Find the speaker. Find the anchor. Free the beast, free the king.* Some of it I'd pieced together myself over the weeks since; some of it Fen gave me now, deliberately, formally, holding nothing back for the first time since I'd known him. When I finished, the cellar was so quiet I could hear the candle-wicks drowning in their own wax.

"The wolf carries the king's own message," Halvard said at last, hoarse. "Sent out through the binding's cracks, the only way left to him. Gods. Twenty years I served that man, and his last act as himself was to trust his life to a dog and a stranger."

"He's not a dog," I said, sharper than I meant to. "He's a knight. He just happens to have four feet."

That got a real reaction—someone laughed, short and startled, and the sharpening woman set down her whetstone and looked at Fen with new eyes, and bowed her head slightly, the way you'd acknowledge a fellow soldier. Fen's tail moved once against the floor. Accepting it. Needing it, maybe, more than he'd ever admit.

"Here's the problem," Halvard said, dragging the conversation back like a man hauling on a rope. "We've known about the stag for a year. Do you know what we've done about it? Nothing. Because the binding is a craft-working, and none of us are craft-workers, and the two hedge-mages we trusted to try died screaming before they got within a league of the palace. The spell doesn't just *sit* in the stag—it's grown into it, root and branch. To unbind it you'd have to speak to it. Hear its pain, find the weave's knots, unpick them without killing the creature, because if the anchor dies wrong, the binding collapses inward and takes the king's mind with it." He spread his hands. "There may be four people alive with the gift to attempt it. Two are ash. One is in Vessane's dungeons. And one—"

"—walked into your cellar tonight," I finished.

"You see why the rats were excited."

I did. That was the problem. I stared at the leaden crown and felt the whole shape of it settle onto my shoulders: not a mission, a *keystone*. Every plan these people had bled for, every smuggled letter and burned safehouse and hanged kitchen boy—all of it funneled down to one point, and the point was me. A healer's apprentice from a valley that forgot magic, who'd spent twenty-two years learning to be nobody, sitting in a traitor's crypt being told she was indispensable.

Indispensable. It sounded like a word for candles burning at both ends.

"If I fail," I said, "the king dies. If I'm caught, the king dies *and* you all die, and Vessane learns exactly where the last threat to her comes from. Have I got the arithmetic right?"

"You have."

"And if I refuse?"

Halvard held my gaze without flinching. "Then we'll find another way or die trying, and I won't think less of you. No one in this room has the right to. We ask because there's no one else. That's not leverage. It's just the truth."

I looked down at Fen. He was watching me with those amber eyes, and through the bond between us—not a command, never a command, just an offering—came a single image: a meadow in spring, green going gold, and a man laughing as a wolf circled him, and the feeling underneath it, vast and specific, of *home*.

*He waited three winters for you,* the image said. *But it's your choice. It was always going to be your choice.*

I exhaled. "All right," I said. "I'll need to get close to the stag. Closer than a visitor. Which means getting inside the palace, which means—"

"Which means a way in that leaves no record and no witness," Halvard said. "Yes. We've been working that problem for a year. Our best answer involves a smuggler."

"Sable," I said.

"You've met."

"She robbed me, negotiated with me, and saved my life, roughly in that order."

"Then you know her about as well as anyone does." Halvard rose, joints cracking like green wood, and for a moment he just looked tired—an old man in a hole under a tannery, carrying a kingdom on a bad back. "Rest tonight. We move on the palace within the fortnight, before the winter court fills with Vessane's northern lords. After that, the windows close until spring, and spring may be too late."

People were moving, dousing candles, rolling bedding. I stayed at the table a moment longer, looking at the leaden crown, at the nine spikes that stood in for a kingdom's grief.

Fen nudged my hand. *Well?*

"We just volunteered to break into a palace," I said, "guarded by a sorceress-queen, to talk to a starving deer."

*When you say it like that it sounds mad.*

"When I say it any way, it sounds mad."

*Good,* Fen said, laying his head on my knee again. *Mad plans have better odds than hopeless ones.*

I was still turning that over—deciding whether it was wisdom or wolf nonsense—when the lookout on the stairs hissed down the passage, sharp and low, and every candle in the cellar went dark at once.

Boots above us. Many boots. And beneath the tannery-stink, threading down through the floorboards, a smell Fen knew and I was learning to fear:

Iron. Old magic. Witch-hunters.

* * *

Someone grabbed my arm in the blackness—Halvard, his breath hot against my ear. "The bolt-hole, behind the salt racks. Go now, go quiet, and whatever you hear behind you—"

Above, the trapdoor at the top of the stairs splintered inward, and light poured down the stairwell like something spilled.
