## 9. Hunted Through the Warrens

The cellar smelled of tallow and old blood, and Zephyrine had almost stopped flinching at the sound of boots overhead.

Three days they had been beneath the tannery, learning what the loyalists knew. Ser Aldous Vane—gray-bearded, one-armed, a knight who spoke of the king the way men speak of dead brothers—had drawn maps in spilled salt. The binding. The crown. The anchor that no blade could reach but a beast-speaker might. Around the scarred table sat seven of them: exiles in borrowed clothes, knights without a country, and one healer's apprentice from a valley that forgot.

Zephyrine was learning to sleep in snatches, the way Fen did. The wolf lay against the far wall with his ears up even in rest, and when the boots came—not the usual shuffle of the tannery workers above but the hard, synchronized tread of soldiers—Fen was on his feet before the sound resolved into meaning.

*Hunters,* he said. *Many. And something else with them.*

Zephyrine felt it a heartbeat later: a wrongness pressing down through the floorboards, cold and greasy, like a hand sliding along the back of the neck. Witch-finders carried charms that stank of stolen magic. Zephyrine had never felt one before. Now they understood why the gifted ran from these men rather than fighting.

"Ser Aldous." Their voice came out low and steady, which surprised them. "They've found us."

Vane didn't waste time asking how they knew. That was the thing about exiles—they believed bad news fast. "Back way," he said, already moving. "Through the curing racks, up the drain tunnel. Marta, take the ledgers. The rest of you—"

The door at the top of the stairs exploded inward.

It went fast after that, and it went badly. Not because the hunters were skilled—though they were—but because there were twelve of them and seven exiles and a narrow stairway that funneled everything into slaughter. Vane killed the first two with a sword he'd hidden inside a rolled mattress, and then a crossbow bolt took him through the throat and the cellar became screaming and smoke and the greasy cold pressing harder, harder, a voice like wet gravel scraping across Zephyrine's mind:

*There. The speaker. Take the speaker alive.*

Zephyrine did not remember deciding to run. They remembered Fen's shoulder slamming their hip, steering them toward the drain tunnel while steel rang behind them. They remembered Marta falling. They remembered the witch-finder's charm flaring white as they passed beneath it, and the gravel voice rising into something like joy.

Then they were in the tunnel, crawling through filth by Fen's glow-less, sightless certainty, and behind them the cellar burned.

* * *

They came up through a grate in a cobbled yard behind a chandler's shop, and Marrowgate at night was nothing like Marrowgate by day. By day it was refugees and queues and Sable's smugglers moving crates of people like contraband. By night it was a city holding its breath—and tonight the breath came out as horns.

"They'll have the warrens sealed," Zephyrine said. Blood on their hands—Marta's, though they hadn't been able to stop the bleeding, wouldn't be able to stop anything if the hunters caught them. "Alleys, cellars, the rat-runs. They know this city better than we do."

Fen stood in the yard's shadow, hackles lifted, nose working. *Then we don't use their roads.* He looked back at Zephyrine, and in the dark his eyes caught no light at all, just depth. *Use yours.*

"I can't call them all." The words came out before Zephyrine had decided to say them, which was how they knew they were true. "Fen, I've called birds. A few dogs. Never—I couldn't hold a whole city."

*You held me,* Fen said. *When I was dying on that mountainside with silver in my chest, you held me together with your voice alone. You were half-trained and terrified and you did it anyway.* He stepped closer, and his breath was warm against their wrist. *I am not telling you to be brave. I am telling you what I know.*

Above them, somewhere past the rooftops, the horns changed pitch. Spreading. Coordinating. The witch-finders had dogs too—real ones, charmed ones, animals twisted until their noses hunted magic instead of scent. Zephyrine could feel them even now, a wrongness moving through the streets like oil through water.

And underneath the wrongness, everywhere, the city's true life: rats in the walls by the thousand, patient and clever. Dogs chained in yards and sleeping under carts. Pigeons roosting in the eaves of every church and granary, thousands upon thousands, dreaming bird dreams. All of it humming along beneath Marrowgate like a second city, unseen, unheard.

Unspoken to. For twenty-two years Zephyrine had kept the gift small enough to hide. Even in the valley, where magic died at the ridgeline, they had whispered to crows and moths and once, memorably, an angry goose. Small calls. Careful calls. The gift deepening since the pass, since the healing, since they'd poured borrowed power into Fen's torn chest—but still held on a short leash, because a long leash meant burning.

The horns sounded again, closer now.

*They will find us,* Fen said simply. *Or you can stop hiding.*

Zephyrine closed their eyes and let go of the leash.

It was like opening a door in a house they hadn't known they owned. The gift rushed out of them—not pushed, *poured*—down through the cobbles and out along the night like a bell struck in a cathedral of dark. And the city answered.

Rats first, because rats are always listening. They poured from the walls in silver rivers, hundreds, then thousands, whiskered faces turning toward the chandler's yard as one. Then the dogs—chained dogs barking, stray dogs howling, lap dogs shrieking in their masters' houses, every canine voice in the warrens rising at once into a chorus that had no fear in it at all, only attention. Then the pigeons burst upward off the rooftops in a storm of wings so vast it blotted the stars, wheeling over the streets in a living cloud that turned the night pale.

Zephyrine stood in the middle of it with their arms out and their heart hammering and felt—for the first time in their life, for three glorious, terrible seconds—*huge*. Not the healer's apprentice hiding in a valley. Not the mute scullery girl or the nameless refugee. The whole city listening to them. The whole city *theirs*, briefly, the way a wave belongs to the sea.

*This way,* they said to the rats, and ten thousand rats moved. *Cry there,* they said to the dogs, and the dogs' voices scattered east and west, north and south, a hundred false trails singing through the dark. *Blind them,* they said to the pigeons, and the great wheeling cloud dropped low over the witch-finders' lanterns, wings battering light into chaos, feathers and screams and sudden darkness.

The hunters' coordinated advance dissolved into cursing confusion. Charmed hounds spun in circles, overwhelmed—their handlers had made them hunting instruments, and instruments had no defenses against a choir. Somewhere a witch-finder screamed about devils. Somewhere else, a sergeant started shouting orders nobody could hear.

Zephyrine laughed. It came out of them wild and unrecognizable, and they ran with it, Fen loping beside them through streets the rats had cleared and lit—no, not lit, *described*, every shadow and gap and handhold whispered to them a heartbeat before they needed it. They ran through Marrowgate like a fish through water that loved them.

For three blocks. Maybe four.

Then the cost arrived.

It came as cold, first—a cold that started in the bones of their hands and spread inward, toward the center of them, toward whatever it was that made Zephyrine *Zephyrine*. The gift wasn't a muscle; it was more like blood, and they were bleeding it out by the gallon. They staggered against a wall. The pigeons wheeled drunkenly overhead. A dog's voice cut off mid-bark, confused, its thread slipping from Zephyrine's grip.

*Enough,* Fen said sharply. *Let them go. You're tearing—*

"I can hold—" But they couldn't finish the sentence, because holding was exactly what they couldn't do anymore. The rats were still flowing around their ankles, waiting, faithful, and Zephyrine loved them so much in that moment it hurt, and they said, *Go home, go home, thank you, go home,* and released them strand by strand, feeling each release like a small death.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Not true silence—the city still breathed and muttered and scratched—but the absence of that vast attention, that choir, that sea. Zephyrine slid down the wall into the gutter and shook. Fen pressed against them, solid and warm, and above them the last pigeons settled back onto their roosts, and somewhere in the distance the horns began again—but confused now, searching, arguing with themselves.

*They know,* Fen said quietly. *What you are. What you can do. There will have been survivors, and witch-finders write everything down.*

"I know." Zephyrine's voice was ash. "I know."

They had bought the escape. They had spent the cellar's secrets doing it—no, worse, they had spent *themselves*, loudly, publicly, unmistakably. Every hunter in Marrowgate would know by morning that a beast-speaker of unusual power was loose in the warrens. Not a rumor. Not a whisper. A demonstration.

Exhilaration curdled slowly in their chest, going sour and heavy. They had felt like a god for four seconds and had paid for it with the only currency that mattered: invisibility. Twenty-two years of careful smallness, spent in one night.

*We need Sable,* Fen said.

"We can't trust—"

*We cannot walk,* Fen said, *and we cannot hide, and come morning every wall in this city will carry your face. We need someone who knows how to move hunted things out of cities. That is the entire shape of her life.*

Zephyrine leaned their head back against the cold brick and looked up at the stars, wheeling, indifferent, beautiful. Their hands had stopped shaking. That was somehow worse.

"Find her, then," they said to Fen. "Before the city does."

Far off across the rooftops, a single crow lifted from a chimney and turned toward the river—and Zephyrine, giftless-tired, hollowed out, heard nothing at all when it cried.
