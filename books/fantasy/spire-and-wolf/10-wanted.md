## 10. Wanted

The bounty notice reached Sable before breakfast, which was how Sable knew it was serious. Nothing in Marrowgate moved that fast unless someone had paid for speed.

It came folded inside a bolt of salt-stiff canvas, delivered by a boy with a chipped tooth and no memory of who'd handed it to him—standard practice for the Queen's informants, who liked their couriers stupid and deniable. Sable read it standing in the doorway of the tannery's back office, one boot on the threshold, the other still in the reek of the curing yard, because some part of them wanted to be able to run before they finished the first line.

*By order of Her Majesty Vessane, rightful sovereign of the realm: the person depicted herein is sought alive for crimes of sorcery against the crown. Reward of two hundred crowns royal, payable upon delivery to any witch-finder or garrison captain. The bearer of true information leading to capture shall receive fifty.*

Two hundred crowns.

Sable had smuggled people out of burning towns for less than a tenth of that. Had moved whole families through the marsh roads for what it cost to feed them. Two hundred crowns would keep the network running through winter. Would buy passage for every soul currently stacked three-deep in the cellar beneath the tannery, waiting on boats that hadn't come. Would replace the coin Captain Verren had bled out of them in bribes, the coin the raid at the loyalist safehouse had burned, the coin that was always, always the difference between someone living and someone not.

And below the words, rendered by a court artist with a careful hand, was Zephyrine's face.

They'd drawn it from witnesses, obviously—the warrens had been full of eyes during the escape—but whoever held the brush had caught something true. The set of the mouth, stubborn even in ink. The way the eyes looked slightly past the viewer, as if listening to something behind the wall. *Healer's hands,* the description read. *Travels with a grey wolf, unusually large. May attempt to pass as mute. Speaks with an accent of the northern valleys when pressed.*

*May attempt to pass as mute.* Sable almost laughed. Almost. The laugh died somewhere behind their ribs, where it had been living for days now, ever since the raid, ever since they'd watched Zephyrine stand in a street full of screaming and call every rat in Marrowgate to war.

* * *

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

Brannic, the tannery foreman, filled the doorway the way he filled most doorways—all shoulders and suspicion. He'd worked for Sable four years and trusted them about as far as he could throw the building, which was the correct amount of trust for this business. He nodded at the paper. "That the healer?"

"That's the healer."

"Two hundred." Brannic whistled low. "Queen's paying real money now. Not the usual finder's fee."

"The usual finder's fee gets you a drunk stablehand dragged out of a tavern." Sable folded the notice along its creases, slowly, like closing a wound. "This one gets you a witch-hunter escort and a cell under the Spire. They want this one bad enough to make it public. That means the garrisons have copies. The gate guards. The river pilots." They met Brannic's eye. "Every boat I've got lined up just became a trap with sails."

Brannic scratched his jaw. "You going to turn them in?"

The question landed the way questions like that always landed—flat, casual, aimed at nothing, while the man asking watched your hands instead of your face. Four years of working together, and this was the conversation they were finally having.

"No," Sable said.

"No," Brannic repeated. "Just like that."

"Just like that."

"Huh." He shifted his weight. "Only, see, I've got eleven families in my drying sheds right now eating soup made of soup, and you've got two hundred crowns in your hand, and the last time we talked numbers you told me we were nine days from broke. So when you say 'no' like it's simple, I'd like to hear the arithmetic."

Sable looked down at the folded paper. The crease ran across Zephyrine's face like a scar.

"The arithmetic," Sable said, "is that the moment I sell one person to save the rest, everyone we've ever moved finds out about it. And then nobody runs to us again. And then the network isn't worth two crowns, let alone two hundred." It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. It was also the answer Sable had prepared in the four seconds between reading the bounty and hearing Brannic's boots on the stairs, which meant it was an answer built for other people's ears, and Sable knew the difference better than anyone alive.

Because here was the thing Sable did not say, the thing they kept in the same locked room where they kept their mother's name and the price they'd once taken for a job they still dreamed about: the arithmetic didn't matter. They would have said no if the reward had been two thousand. They would have said no if it had been two.

* * *

The trouble with principles, Sable thought that evening, is that they don't keep the boats floating.

They sat in the loft above the rope-walk with the ledger open and the lamp turned low, doing sums that refused to come out kind. Nine days from broke had been optimistic. Seven, maybe, if the grain prices held—and grain prices never held in wartime; they climbed like cats up curtains. The free cities were closing their harbors one by one. Vessane's purge had turned every road south into a checkpoint, every checkpoint into a toll, every toll into a bribe, and bribes were the only currency Sable had ever been rich in until suddenly they weren't.

Fifty crowns for information. Two hundred for delivery.

Sable closed the ledger and pressed both palms flat against it, as if holding the numbers down.

Old survival instincts are funny things. They don't announce themselves. They don't arrive wearing villainy like a costume. They arrive reasonable. They arrive as *prudence*, as *the greater good*, as *one life weighed against forty*. They arrive speaking in your own voice, because they've lived in your head so long they know all your arguments, and they know exactly which one will work: *you can't save anyone if you're dead, and you can't save anyone if you're broke, and Zephyrine Thornbury is one stranger you've known for six days.*

Six days. Sable had known people for six years and sold them for less.

But Zephyrine wasn't a cargo. That was the problem. Cargo you loaded and shipped and forgot. Zephyrine had sat in this same loft three nights ago, cross-legged on the floorboards with Fen's huge grey head in their lap, picking burrs out of the wolf's ruff with a patience Sable found frankly obscene, and had asked—not demanded, *asked*—whether Sable ate enough. Whether there was food for the families in the sheds first. Whether Sable slept, ever, or just moved from one crisis to the next like a stone skipping water.

Nobody asked Sable those questions. People asked Sable for things. Prices. Passages. Miracles. Zephyrine had asked after *them*, in that quiet, careful voice, the voice of someone who had spent twenty-two years learning to watch people closely enough to survive them—and had apparently decided that what they saw was worth worrying about.

And then there was the wolf. Fen had looked at Sable that night with pale amber eyes that understood every word spoken in the room, and Sable had felt, for the first time since childhood, genuinely *seen*—not the smuggler, not the fixer, not the price-list, but the person underneath, the one who'd started this whole mad enterprise because a girl with burn scars had once begged them at a dockside and they'd said no, and the saying-no had followed them ever since like a debt collecting interest.

One life weighed against forty. Except the forty weren't saved yet, and might not be, and the one was already saved, already here, already sleeping in the hay with a wolf's chin on their knee, trusting Sable with the unguarded face of someone who had never once been safe.

Sable blew out the lamp.

* * *

They went looking for Zephyrine at midnight, because some decisions you make in daylight and some you have to make standing in front of the person they'll hurt.

The hayloft over the tannery stables was warm with animal heat and smelled of leather oil and straw. Zephyrine was awake—of course they were awake; Sable suspected they slept the way cats did, in shallow watchful laps—and Fen rose to his feet the moment Sable's head cleared the ladder, silent, enormous, watching.

"It's me," Sable said, unnecessarily. The wolf knew. The wolf probably knew what Sable had eaten for dinner.

Zephyrine sat up in the straw, hair loose, eyes finding Sable in the dark with unsettling ease. "Bad news travels fast in this city," they said. "What is it?"

Sable held up the folded notice. "This came today. Court artist did a decent job of you, all things considered. Two hundred crowns alive. Fifty for information." They tossed it across the straw rather than hand it over, because handing it over felt too much like presenting evidence. "Garrisons have copies. River pilots too, likely. Every route I had planned for the next month is compromised, and I need to know—" and here Sable stopped, because the sentence they'd rehearsed was *I need to know if you trust me to move you*, and the sentence that came out was worse, truer:

"I need you to know that I'm not going to take it. The money. And I need you to know that I *thought about it*, because I think about everything, it's what I do, and I'd rather you heard it from me than found out some other way that your face has a price and the person keeping you has debts."

Silence. The stable horses shifted below. Fen hadn't sat down; he stood like a drawn bow, but he hadn't growled, and Sable took that as the endorsement it was.

Zephyrine unfolded the paper and studied their own face for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was quiet. "Twenty-two years," they said. "I hid so well I started to believe the hiding was the whole of me. And now there's a picture of my face nailed to every post from here to the capital, and do you know what the strange part is?" They looked up. "It's almost a relief. Being seen. Even like this. Even hunted." A pause. "Almost."

"They'll double it," Sable said. "After the cellar raid, after the rats—they know what you can do now. This is the opening bid. Within a fortnight there'll be witch-hunters in this city specifically for you, and they don't pay tolls and they don't take bribes, which breaks half my playbook right there." Sable crouched down at the edge of the straw, arms on knees, close enough that honesty couldn't hide. "So here's where we are. I can't move you south. Can't move you east. The loyalists you were meant to reach are scattered or dead or hiding deeper than I can dig. Which leaves north. Through the high passes. In winter. With a wolf, no papers, and a face worth more than my entire operation."

"That sounds like a terrible plan," Zephyrine said.

"It's a catastrophic plan. It's the worst plan I've ever voiced aloud, and I once moved a fugitive ox-cart through a royal wedding procession." Sable exhaled. "But the king's men—the ones who survived the raid—went to ground in the old quarry road beyond Northgate. If anyone can still get us to them, it's a guide named Wren Halloway who owes me a favor so large she pretends it doesn't exist. She moves at night. She hates the Queen more than she loves money, which in my experience is the only reliable kind of ally."

Zephyrine was quiet for a moment. Then: "You said 'us.'"

"I beg your pardon?"

"'Get *us* to them.'" Zephyrine's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You're coming."

Sable opened their mouth to deliver the speech—the one about how their place was here, with the network, with the forty families, with the boats—and found that the speech wouldn't come. Because it was a lie, and Zephyrine would hear it as one, and worse, Sable would hear it as one.

"If I stay," Sable said finally, "I spend seven days watching the gates for witch-hunters and then I either sell you out or die refusing to, and I find I've lost my appetite for both. Besides—" they jerked a thumb at the wolf, who had finally, gracefully, lain back down in the straw, though his eyes stayed open and fixed on Sable with what could only be called *supervision* — "your dog doesn't trust me yet. I intend to fix that. I have a professional reputation."

"He's not my dog," Zephyrine said softly, one hand settling into the grey ruff. "He's his own. But he agrees with you, for whatever that's worth. He says—" a small huff of laughter, surprised out of them— "he says you smell like honest fear. He considers that a recommendation."

"Wonderful. Endorsed by a wolf on the basis of terror. My mother would be proud."

Something crossed Zephyrine's face at the word *mother*—a flicker, quickly banked—and Sable filed it away, because filing things away was what they did, but gently now. More gently than before.

"We leave tomorrow night," Sable said, standing, brushing straw from their coat. "Dark moon. Wren meets us at the tanner's sluice gate. Sleep if you can." At the ladder they paused, one hand on the rail, and said the last part without turning around, because it was easier said to the dark:

"For what it's worth—I've been offered a lot of money for a lot of people. You're the first one I ever had to talk myself out of selling. Everyone else, I never even considered it." A beat. "I haven't decided yet whether that makes you special or makes me getting soft. Ask me again when we're over the mountains."

Behind them, in the warm dark of the hayloft, Zephyrine laughed—a real one, short and startled—and Fen's tail thumped once against the boards like a judge's gavel coming down on the side of mercy.

Sable went down the ladder into the cold, and outside, pasted to the tannery gate where someone had nailed it within the hour, a fresh copy of the bounty fluttered in the wind, two hundred crowns staring at the street with Zephyrine's patient, listening eyes.

Sable tore it down, folded it into quarters, and put it in their pocket—next to the ledger figures, next to the debts, next to the decision already made.

North, then. Through the passes. With a wolf, a fugitive, and everything they owned riding on the worst plan of their life.

At dawn, a boy with a chipped tooth would bring a second message: *Wren Halloway hasn't been seen in three days, and her house on Coil Street is burned to the foundation stones.*
