## 11. The Wolf's Name

For six days the wolf did not speak.

It walked beside me through the grey throat of the north road, close enough that its shoulder brushed my knee when the wind gusted, far enough that I understood it was a choice, not a habit. It ate what I scavenged—half of everything, no more—and drank from streams only after I'd drunk first, which I found strange until I realized it was watching for poison. Watching over me. A creature the size of a yearling calf, scarred across the muzzle like an old soldier, deferring to a healer's apprentice who had never been further from home than the valley ridgeline.

I talked to it anyway. That was the thing about me; I had never learned how not to talk.

"You're favoring the left foreleg," I said on the fourth morning, crouching in the frost-stiffened bracken while it stood patient as furniture. "The bolt wound closed clean, but there's a splinter of the silver core still in there. I can feel it when you walk. Don't give me that look—you think I can't feel it? You've been limping since the pass."

The wolf gave me the look anyway. Wolves have a limited repertoire of expressions and this one managed to convey *mind your own business* with remarkable economy.

"I'm minding my own business," I said. "You're my business now. Apparently. Nobody asked me, mind. You just bled out your message and collapsed on top of my escape route and now here we are, walking to a war I don't understand, carrying a king's plea inside a body that should have died three times by now."

Its ear flicked. That was all. Six days of flicked ears and lowered lids and once—a moment I kept returning to—it had pressed its cold nose against my palm as we sheltered from sleet under a fallen beech, and held it there, and looked at me with amber eyes that held something too deliberate to be animal and too wounded to be anything else.

The message was burned into me now. I heard it every time I closed my eyes: *If any speaker hears this—Aldric is bound. The one who wears his face is not Aldric. Find the loyalists at Marrowgate. Tell them the hound lives.* And then, fainter, fraying like a thread pulled to its last inch: *Tell Fen he was right.*

*Fen.*

I had turned the name over for six days like a stone in my pocket. The hound lives. Tell Fen he was right. And the wolf before me, who would not speak, who walked north without being asked, whose wounds I had closed with borrowed magic that sang strangely in my blood ever since the ridge—

"Your name is Fen," I said, on the sixth morning, because I could not hold it another hour.

* * *

We had stopped at midday in the shell of a coaching inn, its roof burned to black ribs, its well choked with ash. Vessane's purge had passed through here weeks ago; the walls were scrawled with the crown's new sigil, a spire impaling a crescent moon, and beneath it someone had painted, in a hand shaking with fury or fear or both: *SHE IS NOT THE KING*. Someone had tried to scrub it away. They hadn't finished.

I sat against the inn's cold hearthstone and shared out the last of the smoked eel Sable's people had pressed on us at the Marrowgate docks—*for the road, and don't come back, both of you*, Sable had said, which from Sable was practically a sonnet—and watched the wolf watch the road.

"Fen," I said again. "That's what the king said. *Tell Fen he was right.* And you haven't told me your name, but you flinched. Right there. Your left ear went back when I said it." I tore the eel and offered half. "You're Fen."

The wolf took the eel with exaggerated gentleness, teeth careful of my fingers even now, even after everything. Then it lay down across my feet—all of it, the whole warm weight of it, which it had never done—and sighed, a long sound like wind through a hollow tree.

And spoke.

"Yes."

One word. But oh, the voice of it—not sound alone, though my ears caught something low and resonant that no ordinary wolf could make, but meaning pouring directly into that place behind my breastbone where the gift lived, where all speech-without-speech arrived. The word carried weather in it. Carried years.

"You knew," the wolf said. "You've known since the message."

"I hoped. Hoped and dreaded it, because if you're Fen, then you're his hound, and if you're his hound, then whatever bound him—" My voice cracked. I hated that. "What bound him might have touched you too."

"It did." Fen laid its great head on my knee. This close, I could see the scars properly: not the random marks of a wild life but patterned ones, ritual ones, thin lines crossing the shoulders in a shape I almost recognized. "Sit, Zephyrine Thornbury. This is a long story, and you will want to be sitting."

"How do you know my—"

"The rats of Marrowgate gossip worse than washerwomen. Sable sold your name to half the city and gave it free to the other half." Something moved through the voice that I slowly recognized as humor, dry as old bone. "Also, you talk in your sleep. You apologized to a hedgehog."

"That hedgehog was rude."

"*There.*" The wolf's tail thumped once against the floorboards, and the sound of it was so absurdly domestic that my eyes stung. "That is the tone. That is the voice the king loved. Now listen, because I have carried this story alone since the night they took him, and a story carried alone grows teeth."

So I sat, with a wolf's head heavy on my knee, in a burned inn under a traitor's sigil, and listened.

* * *

"I was whelped in the royal kennels," Fen said, "but I was not raised to be a kennel hound. There is an old compact, older than the crown itself, between the kings of this land and the wolves of the northern fells. When an heir takes the throne, a wolf is chosen. Not tamed—*chosen*. We swear at the coronation, in the old tongue, before the old stone. Bodyguard, huntsman, witness. The king's second set of eyes and the realm's first alarm." A pause. "Aldric was nine when he met me. I was the runt of my litter. He picked the runt because nobody else wanted her, and he said, *I know what that's like.*"

I thought of a boy-king choosing the unwanted animal, and my chest hurt.

"He grew up crooked and kind. Do you know how rare that is, in a palace? He snuck me into council meetings in his fifteenth year and the chancellor never noticed for two months because Aldric would answer questions in the chancellor's own words, having heard them through my ears the night before. He laughed at his own jokes before he made them. He remembered the names of scullery maids." The head on my knee grew heavier. "He was a good king. Not a great one yet—he was young, he was learning—but good, and getting better, and then his sister came home from the southern wars."

"Vessane."

"Vessane." The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well. "She had always been brilliant. Cold as deep winter, but brilliant, and he loved her the way you love a fire that has burned you before—carefully, from a distance, hoping. And she studied him for a year. His habits, his guards, his affections. And she studied something else. There are books in the deep vaults that no king has opened in three hundred years, and she opened them. Binding-craft. The oldest and foulest of it—the kind that needs a living anchor, the kind that feeds."

"The stag," I whispered. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. In the loyalist cellar, old Ser Brandt had spoken of rumors: the binding anchored to a living creature kept in pain. "The menagerie."

"You know some of it already. Good. Then hear the part you don't." Fen lifted its head, and in the dim light of the burned inn its eyes caught what little there was and held it. "The binding needed two anchors, not one. A beast in torment to feed it—and a beast in *loyalty* to seal it. The spell is woven from love, Zephyrine. That's its genius and its obscenity. It uses the truest feeling in the room as its thread. They couldn't bind Aldric with fear or hatred; he had none for them worth using. So they used mine."

My hands had gone cold around the eel I wasn't eating anymore.

"They took me the night of the solstice feast. Drugged meat, thrown over the kennel wall—I should have known, I *did* know, I smelled the sorcery and ate it anyway because it smelled faintly of him, of his hand, and I thought he was calling me." The wolf's voice did not break. Wolves do not break. But it went somewhere flat and terrible. "They chained me in a chamber beneath the menagerie and made me watch while they worked the first knots into the stag. And then they worked the rest into me. Every oath I had ever sworn, every year of loyalty, every memory of a boy picking the runt—they braided it all into cordage and used it to tie the king shut inside his own skin. He is in there, Zephyrine. Awake. He has been awake for two years, watching his sister wear his face and sign his orders, screaming into a body that will not obey him. And the only reason he has not gone mad is that twice a year, on the old feast days, the binding loosens by a hair's breadth, and I am permitted to speak to him through the stone. Through the anchor. We keep each other sane, he and I, in the dark, in two cages."

The hearth ash shifted in a draft. Somewhere outside, a crow called, and I flinched at crows now, would probably flinch at them forever.

"*Tell Fen he was right,*" I said. "What was he right about?"

Fen was quiet a long time. When it answered, the voice had changed—smaller, somehow, though nothing about the wolf was small.

"In the last hour before they took him, the binding half-formed, his mind surfacing and sinking like a drowning man. He looked at me through the bars of his own skull and he said, *Run, Fen. Get out. Save yourself.* And I said no. I said, *Where you go, I go. That was the oath.* And he said—" the wolf's breath shuddered, an entirely human sound from an entirely unhuman throat, "—he said, *Then you're right, and I'm sorry, and one day someone will have to be braver than both of us.*"

"So you stayed. Let yourself be bound. To stay near him."

"To stay *with* him. There's a difference. And then, when the loyalists finally got a runner into the castle last autumn—when we learned there were still people willing to act—we spent four months preparing the one working we could manage. The message. A speaker's-craft so old I barely remembered it, taught to me by my dam before the compact, pushed through the binding's seams at the cost of—" it gestured, with its muzzle, at the silver-bolt scar, at the ritual lines across its shoulders, "—most of what I had left. The witch-hunters tracked the working. They shot me at the ridge. The rest, you know. You were there."

"I was there," I said softly. "I pulled a splinter of the first magic to enter Hollowmere in sixty years out of a dying wolf, and I used power I didn't know I had to knit the wound, and I felt something in me wake up and stretch, and I've been frightened of myself ever since."

"I know," Fen said. "I've been waiting for you to say it."

* * *

The afternoon light was failing when we left the inn, and the road north ran white and empty toward hills that were becoming mountains. We walked. At some point, without either of us deciding it, the wolf stopped walking beside me and started walking pressed against my leg, its warmth soaking through my coat, and I put my hand on its neck, into the coarse ruff, and it leaned into the touch the way a tired child leans into a doorframe.

"My mother died when I was seven," I said, to the hills, because it was easier than saying it to Fen. "Before we came to Hollowmere. My father followed three years later, and after that it was just me and the mistress and the village that tolerated me the way you tolerate weather. I used to think the animals were the only family I'd get. The crow who brought warnings. The fox who stole my gloves and left mushrooms in exchange, which I choose to believe was sincere. And I told myself that was enough. I built a whole life on *enough*." I scratched behind the wolf's ear, carefully, where the fur grew over old ritual scarring. "Then the riders came and the village sold my mentor's name for mercy, and I learned that *enough* built on hiding isn't a life. It's a holding pattern."

"And now?"

"Now I'm walking to a war with a wolf who calls a king by his childhood mistakes." I laughed, and it came out wet. "Now I have a pack of one, and it talks in its sleep less than I do."

"Wolves don't sleep-talk."

"Hedgehog-apologizers shouldn't throw stones."

Fen made a sound I had never heard from it before, a huffing, chuffing bark that took me a full three steps to identify as laughter. And something in my chest, something that had been clenched since the night the crow screamed *riders* over Hollowmere, loosened by a single degree. Not healed. Loosened. Family, I was beginning to understand, was not a thing you lost once and mourned forever. It was a thing that could be rebuilt in stranger shapes—a burned inn, a shared eel, a hand in a ruff, a voice in the blood.

"Fen," I said. "When we find the loyalists. When they tell us what they want. The cellar people spoke of reaching the anchor, of a beast-speaker unbinding the spell. You know what that means better than anyone. You know what it'll cost."

The wolf walked three strides in silence.

"I know what it cost to send one message," it said at last. "I know what it will cost to unravel two years of binding-work fed on pain and love. And I know that you felt your gift deepen when you healed me, and I know you're afraid it will consume you, and I will not lie to you and say you're wrong to be afraid." It stopped, and turned, and looked up at me with those amber eyes that held a boy-king's whole history. "But I will tell you what I told him on the last night. *Where you go, I go.* Whatever you choose at the end of this road, Zephyrine Thornbury, you will not choose it alone. That is my oath. The old one, and a new one."

I knelt in the frozen mud of the north road and put my arms around a wolf's neck, and a wolf put its chin on my shoulder, and we stayed like that while the light died, two creatures with nothing left to hide from each other.

It was Fen who broke the silence, ears swiveling south, hackles lifting along its spine.

"Riders," it said. "Behind us. Moving fast. And ahead—" its nose worked the wind off the mountains, and the growl that came out of it was the growl of the king's hound, of the realm's first alarm, of every oath it had ever sworn, "—ahead, on the pass road, there's smoke. A lot of smoke. And under the smoke, Zephyrine—under the smoke I smell *witch-hunter's oil*."

Ahead of us, between us and the loyalists' mountain roads, something was burning.
