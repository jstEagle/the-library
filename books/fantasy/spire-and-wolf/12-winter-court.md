## 12. Winter Court

The palace of Vhalderan had been built to be looked up at. Its spires climbed like frozen flames, its windows threw back the winter sun in sheets of gold, and its gates—tall enough to admit a war-elephant standing on its hind legs—were worked with the faces of every king who had ever ruled the realm. Zephyrine passed beneath them in a cart of turnips and did not look up.

Looking up was how you got noticed.

They wore a scullery hand's rough smock over three layers of stolen wool, their hair cropped short and greased flat, their hands blacked at the nails with ash so they read as a lifetime of labor. The mute part was easy. Sable had drilled it into them for two days straight: *You're from the plague villages south of the marshes. Throat took your voice when you were six. Nobody asks a mute servant questions—they talk around you like you're furniture. Furniture survives.*

*And if someone does ask?*

*Then you're furniture that can't answer.* Sable had grinned, sharp as a fishhook. *Best disguise there is.*

Now Sable was somewhere above them, in the servants' corridors, wearing a steward's grey and a forged seal, moving through the palace the way water moves through cracks. Fen was outside the walls, denned in the tannery district with the loyalists' other assets, hating every minute of it. Zephyrine had felt his reluctance through the bond they'd built across the miles—a low, constant pressure, like a hand on their shoulder saying *wrong direction*.

*I'll come back*, they'd told him before the walls swallowed them.

He hadn't answered. He never answered words. But he'd pressed his forehead against theirs, and what came through it wasn't doubt. It was a promise: *I will tear down these walls stone by stone if you do not walk out again.*

The cart rattled through the inner gate. A guard prodded the turnips with a spear-butt, found nothing but vegetables and misery, and waved them through.

Zephyrine kept their eyes down and began, quietly, to listen.

* * *

A palace is an animal. That was the first thing they understood within an hour of carrying slop buckets through its guts. It had a heartbeat—the great kitchens, always pounding—and a hundred smaller pulses: laundry steam, guard rotations, the bell that marked the hours. And like any animal, it could be spoken to. Not with the gift. With patience. With the small languages everyone spoke without knowing: where the guards got bored, which corridors the stewards avoided, which doors the maids knocked on twice before entering because something behind them made them nervous.

Something behind those doors made everyone nervous.

Zephyrine heard it first in fragments, while scrubbing the flagstones of the west gallery. Two laundresses, whispering over their baskets:

"—won't sit for the portraitist. Says the light's wrong. It's been wrong for a month."

"—the king ate nothing at the feast. Watched the wine being poured like he thought it'd bite him."

"The king doesn't eat. The king *presides*. You've been here four years and you still don't know that?"

But the older one did know, and her voice dropped further, until Zephyrine only caught the shape of it: *that isn't how he used to be. I served under Aldric. That isn't—*

A steward's footsteps. Silence, folded away like contraband linen.

Zephyrine wrung out their rag and kept scrubbing. Their heart was going hard against their ribs. *That isn't how he used to be.* Of course not. Because the man on the throne was a wound dressed as a king, and the thing wearing his face—

They didn't let themselves finish the thought. Finishing thoughts was dangerous in this place. Here you kept your mind like a closed fist.

* * *

Three days of scullery work taught them the palace's rhythms, and on the fourth day the rhythm delivered them what they'd come for: the king would hold open court in the Great Hall at midwinter's approach, receiving petitions before the court departed for the winter progress north. Every servant in the palace would be needed. Even the mute ones.

Even, specifically, the ones who could stand at the edge of a hall full of power and pour wine without trembling.

Sable had gotten them the assignment through channels Zephyrine didn't ask about. Now, in the grey hour before dawn, they stood in the servants' passage beside the Great Hall while a housekeeper with a voice like gravel raked them with her eyes.

"You'll stand by the east pillar. Wine for the high table. You do not speak—you can't, we all know, spare us the tragedy. You do not look at the king. You do not look at anyone above the third rank. You pour, you bow, you vanish. If you spill anything on anyone, I will have you flogged and then I will have you sent home to your plague village, and we both know which fate frightens you more."

Zephyrine bowed. Kept their face empty. Inside, a cold clear voice said: *soon*.

The hall filled like a lung drawing breath. Courtiers in furs the color of foxes and snow, petitioners in their one good coat, guards along the walls with halberds polished to mirrors. Banners hung from the rafters—the crowned stag of House Aldricson, gold on green—and beneath them, braziers burned sweet-smelling wood that did nothing against the hall's deep chill. Everything gleamed. Everything was wrong. Zephyrine couldn't have said how they knew; they just knew, the way an animal knows a snare painted to look like a branch.

Trumpets. The far doors opened.

And the false king came in wearing Aldric's face.

Zephyrine had braced for it. They'd told themselves: *you know what you'll see. A man. A body. The glamour sits on top like frost on glass.* They had not braced enough.

King Aldric—Vessane—crossed the hall the way winter crosses a field. Tall. Broad-shouldered. The famous face, honest and long-jawed, the face from the coins in Zephyrine's childhood pocket. He wore green and gold, the crown sitting easy on his brow, and he smiled at petitioners with warmth that reached his eyes.

It reached his eyes. That was the horror of it. Whatever craft had built this mask, it was perfect down to the smallest human detail. There was nothing to see.

Which was why Zephyrine stopped looking with their eyes.

They let their gift rise—not the speaking, not yet, just the listening, the animal-deep attention they'd carried since childhood, the sense that had once told them a crow's anger from a crow's fear at forty paces. They turned it on the throne like a lantern turned on a dark room.

And beneath the glamour, beneath the smile, beneath the warm strong voice dispensing justice and mercy—

*screaming.*

Not sound. Something deeper than sound, the way pain is deeper than the wound. A man's voice, worn down to a thread, screaming without pause, without hope, without even the mercy of echo. It had been screaming so long it had forgotten anything else. It screamed the way a chained animal screams on the third day, and the tenth day, and the hundredth—past rage, past bargaining, into a place where screaming is simply what a soul does now, forever, because no one is coming.

Zephyrine's hands went still on the wine jug.

The hall went on. A petitioner knelt, pleading a land dispute. The false king listened, nodded, ruled wisely. The court murmured approval. Somewhere a clerk scratched his quill. And underneath it all, threaded through every word the mouth spoke, the prisoner inside screamed.

*He's in there.* The realization landed in Zephyrine's chest like a stone dropped down a well. *He's alive in there. He feels his own hands ruling. He hears his own voice lying. He has watched—* How long? Months. A year. Longer? *—he has watched this thing wear him, and he cannot close his eyes, because where would he close them to?*

The wine jug trembled. Zephyrine locked their wrists. A page glanced over; Zephyrine bowed their head, poured, moved on. Furniture. Furniture survives.

But inside, something in them had torn open, and what came flooding through was not the terror they'd expected. It was worse. It was compassion, vast and helpless, the compassion they'd felt as a child holding a broken-winged sparrow and knowing exactly what it suffered because they could hear it asking *why*.

*Aldric.* They didn't send the thought—they weren't fool enough to use the gift here—but it rose in them anyway, wordless, aching. *I hear you. Do you understand? Someone hears you.*

And then—

The screaming stopped.

One instant. One held breath in an eternity of torment. And in that gap, fainter than memory, thinner than a dying candle flame, something turned toward Zephyrine. Not words. Not even a voice anymore. Just attention—desperate, disbelieving, starved attention, the way a man lost in a blizzard turns toward a single point of light.

It *felt* them.

Zephyrine's knees nearly buckled. They caught the pillar, disguised it as adjusting their grip on the tray, and kept their eyes fixed on the flagstones while their whole body shook. Across the hall, on the throne, the false king paused mid-sentence.

Paused.

The court waited. The petitioner waited. For three heartbeats the most powerful man in the realm stood silent, his head tilted very slightly, like a hound catching a scent on the wind.

Then he smiled and continued his ruling, and the screaming resumed beneath it, and Zephyrine understood with absolute clarity that they had just done the one thing they must never do.

They had touched the binding. And the binding had felt them touch it.

* * *

Afterward, in the scullery, with steam rising off the wash-tubs and their hands raw from water too hot to bear, Zephyrine stood very still and made themselves think.

The false king had sensed them. Which meant the binding was aware—which meant the witch-hunters' raids, the purges, the burning of every beast-speaker in the realm were not merely cruelty or policy. They were a hunt with a purpose. Vessane was killing the gifted because a gifted one was the only creature alive who could reach the anchor and unmake her. She wasn't cleansing the realm.

She was searching it.

And today, on her throne, she had brushed against exactly what she was searching for.

*You have days,* Zephyrine thought. *Maybe less. She'll pull every thread. The mute scullery hand who stood by the east pillar. She'll question the housekeeper, the pages, the guards. She'll—*

"Oi." A scullery boy nudged them, jerking his chin toward the door. "Steward wants you. Grey coat, the one what came with the kitchen lot. Says you're wanted upstairs."

Zephyrine's blood went to ice.

*Sable.* Either rescue or ruin, and no way to tell which until they walked through the door. They dried their hands, bowed their head, and followed the boy out—past the wash-tubs, past the whispering laundresses, up the stairs toward whatever waited, while behind and below them, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the screaming of a king went on and on and on.
