## 13. The Anchor Beneath

The dream came again on the third night in the servants' quarters, and this time Zephyrine did not fight it.

They had learned to stop fighting it after the first night, when they'd thrashed so hard they'd cracked their elbow against the bedframe and woken half the scullery with their shouting. Mute scullery hands did not shout. Mute scullery hands did not do anything that drew the eye. So on the second night Zephyrine had tied their own wrists to the bedposts with strips of blanket, and on the third night they simply lay still and let the memory pour through them like cold water through a sieve.

Fen's memory. Fen's eyes, twenty-two years ago.

Snow. A courtyard Zephyrine had never seen but now knew stone by stone—the crooked flagstone by the well, the drain grate shaped like a sunburst, the archway hung with winter holly because it had been near midwinter then too. A young king walking fast, his breath smoking, a grey wolf loping at his heel. And ahead of them both, at the far end of the courtyard, past a door the king's guards were not supposed to open—

A sound. That was what the memory was, mostly. Not an image. A sound.

Zephyrine had heard suffering before. Hollowmere had sick animals, trapped animals, animals caught in snares that bit down wrong. But this was different. This was suffering with no end in sight, suffering that had been going on so long it had stopped hoping to stop, and it came through the door in a long, low note like a bow drawn across a string made of pain, and the young king stopped walking as if he'd walked into a wall.

*What is that?* Aldric had said, in the memory, in words Fen heard and Fen gave to Zephyrine whole. *What in the gods' names is kept back there?*

And the steward with him—smooth-faced, quick-eyed, a man whose name Zephyrine didn't know but whose gait they'd seen a hundred times in the palace corridors these past two weeks, worn now by a body that wasn't his—had smiled and said: *Nothing that concerns you, Majesty. An old beast. Dying by inches. The menagerie keeps it for its antlers.*

The king had frowned. The wolf had growled. And the memory ended there, fraying out into snow and the smell of holly and that note of pain hanging in the air like smoke that wouldn't clear.

Zephyrine woke in the dark with their heart slamming and their hands already moving, finding Fen's ruff in the blackness beneath the cot where the wolf slept pressed against the floorboards like a shadow pretending to be a dog.

*The menagerie,* they said, mind to mind, silent as everything between them had to be. *Fen. The menagerie. There's something alive in there that's been hurting since before Vessane took him.*

Fen lifted his head. In the dark his eyes caught no light at all, which was one of the many things about him that should have frightened Zephyrine and instead felt like home.

*I know,* Fen said. *I have known since we crossed the wall. I hoped I was wrong.*

*You're never wrong about smells and sounds. Tell me you're wrong about this.*

*I am not wrong about this.* The wolf's thought was heavy, deliberate, each word placed like a paw on ice. *I smelled it my first night in the kitchens' yard. Old blood. Old fear. Fear that has soaked into stone so deep the stone has forgotten anything else. It comes from the north range. Past the aviaries. Past the lion house.* A pause. *Past the place where the guards change twice as often as anywhere else in the palace, for a yard that holds nothing but old animals.*

Zephyrine lay back and stared at the ceiling beams and let the shape of it assemble itself in the dark, the way they'd once assembled the bones of a broken-winged kestrel on their mentor's table, piece by piece until the bird stood up in the mind's eye.

The loyalists had told them the binding was woven from the king's own crown. They'd told them it was anchored—to something. To somewhere. Ser Osric, the exiled knight with the burn-scarred hands, had said the anchor would be *close to the throne, close to the flesh, because bindings that travel far from their anchors fray*. They'd assumed it meant a room. A vault. Something under the throne itself.

But a binding woven from a crown needed feeding. Every spell did; that was the first thing the hedge-wives of Hollowmere taught, the first law of working: nothing comes from nothing. Power had to come from somewhere, and the kind of power that could hold a grown man prisoner inside his own body, that could sustain a glamour strong enough to fool a court and a kingdom for twenty-two years—that kind of power didn't trickle. It poured. Continuously. From a source.

And there was a source in the north range of the palace that had been pouring pain since before the usurpation.

*It was always meant to be found,* Zephyrine said slowly. *That's why it's been there all along. Vessane—or whoever she was then, whoever wore her face before she wore his—she built the binding around a living anchor and put it in the one place no one looks. Who visits a menagerie? Who asks questions about a dying animal?*

*The king asked,* Fen said. *Once.*

*Once.* Zephyrine tasted the bitterness of it. *And then he woke up one morning inside a prison, and the question died with him.*

Fen said nothing, but his warmth pressed against Zephyrine's shin through the thin mattress, and that was answer enough.

*Tomorrow,* Zephyrine said. *Tomorrow I carry slops to the north range. Tomorrow I see it.*

*We see it,* Fen corrected.

*You can't come. Dogs aren't allowed past the aviary gate, and you are a very unconvincing dog.*

There was a silence with teeth in it. Then: *I will be at the drain grate by the sunburst flagstone. If you are not back by the changing of the noon guard, I will stop being a convincing dog entirely.*

Zephyrine smiled in the dark, alone in the way that mattered least.

*Agreed,* they said.

* *

The morning came up white and hard, frost furring the iron gates of the kitchen yard, and Zephyrine went through the motions of the scullery hand they were pretending to be: hauling water, scraping pots, keeping their eyes down and their mouth shut, which was easy because their mouth was shut, which was the whole architecture of the disguise. Sable had forged the papers. Sable had coached them in the walk, the stoop, the way mute servants moved through a great house like furniture that had learned to apologize. *Never meet anyone's eyes,* Sable had said, *but never dodge them either. Dodging is a story. Blankness is a wall.* Zephyrine had practiced blankness in a shard of mirror until even they couldn't find themselves in it.

At midmorning the under-cook barked the day's assignments, and Zephyrine's name—for they had a name here, Marren, a good grey nothing of a name—came up with the north range run. Slops to the menagerie keeper. Feed scraps to the aviary. Zephyrine took the buckets without looking eager, which cost more effort than any magic they had ever worked.

The path wound up through the service corridors, past the laundry steaming like a bog, past the guard post where two soldiers in the false king's grey played dice and didn't look up. Then out along a covered walk where the wind off the mountains cut through every gap in the stones, and the aviaries rose on the left—a long colonnade of cages, hawks and ravens and a pair of eagles that turned their heads to track Zephyrine as they passed.

Zephyrine did not look at them.

That was the discipline of it, the daily discipline, harder than hauling water or holding the stoop: walking past caged birds with their gift folded up small inside them like a fist in the belly, feeling the birds feel them, feeling the flicker of attention, the faint puzzled hope—*one of ours? one who hears?*—and giving them nothing. Nothing, because a witch-hunter's net was strung through this palace, and the hunters didn't look for faces. They looked for ripples. For the moment when a hundred pigeons turned their heads at once. For the stillness of a startled rat.

So Zephyrine walked past the eagles and gave them a wall.

Past the aviaries the walk narrowed, and the quality of the palace changed. It was subtle. Zephyrine might have missed it a month ago. But a lifetime of reading places through their animals had taught them that buildings had bodies too, and this stretch of the north range had the body of a thing long untended—not ruined, not neglected exactly, but *avoided*. Moss grew thick on the shaded flags. The gutters had not been cleared. A spider had built across the doorway of a disused storeroom and the web was old enough to have caught three generations of flies. Servants, when they passed at all, passed quickly, eyes forward, the way people pass a grave.

And the guards. Two at the gate to the inner yard, standing straighter than the dice-players below, wearing the same grey but wearing it differently—newer cloaks, cleaner steel, and a badge Zephyrine hadn't seen elsewhere: a crown inverted, points down, like a chalice. Vessane's own. Her personal detail, this far from her apartments, guarding a yard full of dying animals.

Zephyrine kept walking. Buckets. Slops. A wall behind the eyes.

The menagerie yard opened beyond the gate, and it was worse than the memory had promised.

Not because it was cruel-looking. Because it was *tidy*. Cages in rows, swept gravel, water troughs rinsed clean. A keeper's shed with tools racked neatly. Everything maintained, everything ordered, everything arranged around the central fact of the place, which was that every animal in it was old, or lame, or strange, or dying—and every animal in it was watching Zephyrine with the same expression.

Hope. Faint, banked, ashamed of itself, but hope.

An old bear, grizzled grey around the muzzle, lifted its head from where it lay. A lynx with a clouded eye pressed its face to the bars. A crane stood on one leg in a cage too small for its wings and did not dance, because cranes only dance when they believe someone is watching, and this crane had stopped believing years ago—but its eye followed Zephyrine across the yard with terrible patience.

*One of ours,* said the bear, in the low slow way bears think, and Zephyrine felt the words hit the walls they'd built and crack them, just slightly, just enough. *One of ours has come at last.*

*I'm not—* Zephyrine began, and strangled it. Not here. Not with inverted-crown guards thirty paces away. They set down the slop buckets by the keeper's shed with hands that wanted to shake, and made themselves look around the yard with the dull incurious gaze of a servant counting minutes.

And that was when they saw the structure at the yard's center.

It was not a cage. That was the first wrongness. Every other enclosure in the menagerie was a cage—bars, locks, obvious. This was a building. Low, long, stone-roofed, windowless except for a single slit high on the north wall, and around it ran a moat of sorts—not water but a channel of bare earth, maybe four feet wide, utterly empty, utterly clean, as if nothing living ever crossed it or was permitted to. The door was oak, iron-banded, and on the door, bolted rather than carved, hung a plate of some pale metal etched with lines that hurt to follow, that slid away from the eye like fish.

Wards. Written wards, real ones, the first Zephyrine had ever seen outside a story. Hollowmere killed magic at the ridgeline; Zephyrine had grown up in a world where power was rumor. But they knew what they were looking at anyway, the way a person born blind knows a flame is hot. The air above the channel of bare earth shimmered very slightly, like heat in reverse, like cold given a shape.

The keeper found them there. An old woman, leathery, missing two fingers on her left hand, with the flat competent stare of someone who had done one job for forty years and expected to be left alone while doing it.

*Slops?* she said. *Leave them. And don't stand gawking at the longhouse. It's bad luck.*

*Bad luck,* Zephyrine mouthed, playing the mute, tilting their head.

The keeper spat to the side, an old gesture against evil. *Beast in there. Older than the queen's grandmother, they say. Been in there longer than anyone can remember. Fed once a day, never seen. My mother's mother tended it before me, and hers before that, and none of us ever laid eyes on it, and none of us ever will, gods willing.* She took the buckets. Her voice dropped, not to a whisper—there was no one to hear—but to the tone people use for facts they've stopped questioning. *It screams sometimes. Winter nights mostly. You'll hear it if you're here at midwinter. Sounds almost like a man. Almost.* She shrugged. *Old beasts do that. I've a rooster crows like a bishop.*

She shuffled off toward the shed, and Zephyrine stood in the swept gravel of the tidy yard, surrounded by the banked hope of a dozen caged animals, and felt the cold clarity descend over them like the frost coming off the mountain.

Almost like a man.

*Fen,* they said, reaching down the bond, careful, careful, a thread not a shout. *It's not a beast. Whatever's in the longhouse—it was made to be called a beast. It's the anchor. It has to be. Fed once a day, never seen, warded, guarded by the queen's own—twenty-two years of screaming that sounds almost human.*

Silence down the bond. Then Fen, very quietly: *I remember the night they took him. I remember being dragged from the king's side, and I remember that before the dark closed over me, I heard a stag scream from somewhere beneath us. I told myself it was a stag. I have been telling myself it was a stag for twenty-two years.*

*A stag.* Zephyrine closed their eyes. Of course. Of course it was a stag. The crown of the realm bore a stag's head crowned; the royal house had taken the white hart for its sigil six hundred years ago; every child in the free cities knew the story of the first king who was saved from wolves by a great stag that knelt and let him ride. Bindings fed on meaning as much as pain. What better anchor for a stolen crown than the realm's own sacred beast, starved and chained in the dark, its suffering a fire that never went out?

*The binding drinks it,* Zephyrine said, and the clarity was cold now, cold all the way through, the kind of cold that makes things simple. *Every hour of its hunger, every year of its pain—it feeds the spell that holds Aldric. As long as it lives like that, he stays buried. Which means—*

*Which means you cannot simply free it,* Fen said. *If the anchor dies in torment, the binding may snap back and kill the king. Or worse. Bindings that break sudden—*

*I know. I know.* Zephyrine opened their eyes. Across the yard, the old bear was still watching them, and the lynx, and the one-legged crane, and the clarity turned another degree colder, because they understood now why the animals looked at them that way. Not just hope. Recognition. Every creature in this yard had spent its life being a thing used by humans, a thing whose suffering served someone's purpose, and every one of them knew, the way animals know weather, that the thing in the longhouse was the same and worse, and that the person standing in the gravel smelling of the wild hills was the first visitor in twenty-two years who might care.

The keeper came back out, wiping her hands. *Done? Off you go, then. And mind—" She paused. Her flat eyes narrowed, just slightly, at Zephyrine's stillness, at the way the yard's animals had all gone quiet and attentive as a congregation. "Mind how you go," she finished, and something in her voice suggested she had decided not to notice whatever it was she'd noticed. Forty years in this yard. Perhaps that was its own kind of survival.

Zephyrine bowed their head and walked.

At the edge of the yard they risked one glance back. The longhouse sat in the middle of its dead channel of earth, blind-windowed, patient. High on the north wall, the single slit vent was dark. But as the wind shifted, as Zephyrine passed down the covered walk with their empty hands and their wall behind their eyes, the wind brought them one thread of scent from the vent, one thread of sound too low for human ears—

Hunger. Ancient, hollowed-out hunger, and beneath it, beneath everything, a heartbeat slower than any healthy heart had a right to beat, enduring.

*Midwinter is in nine days,* Zephyrine said to Fen, at the drain grate by the sunburst flagstone, where the wolf waited in the shadows exactly as promised. *The binding will be strongest at midwinter. The loyalists say that's when it must be broken—when the weave is taut enough to find every strand. Nine days.*

*Then we have nine days to learn how to free a starving god-beast without killing the king it feeds,* Fen said. *And to get you into a warded longhouse guarded by the queen's own, past wards written by sorcerers, carrying enough mercy to unmake twenty-two years of pain.*

*Yes,* Zephyrine said.

Down the bond, Fen's thought came quiet and fierce as a held breath: *Then we had better start tonight.*

Above them, unseen in the grey afternoon, every pigeon on the palace roofs turned its head south at once—and somewhere in the north range, behind oak and iron and wards older than the usurpation, the stag screamed, almost like a man, and waited.
