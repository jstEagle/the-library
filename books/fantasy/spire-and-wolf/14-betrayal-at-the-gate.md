## 14. Betrayal at the Gate

Sable had survived twenty years in Marrowgate by learning one lesson early and never forgetting it: everyone has a price, and the ones who swear they don't are the most expensive of all.

So when Captain Dren Varro asked to speak with them alone—away from the cellar, away from the tannery's reek, away from Zephyrine and Fen and the exiled knights who called themselves the Crown Remnant—Sable felt the old cold slide down their spine like a knife laid flat against skin.

They met in the loft above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane, where Sable kept a bolt-hole nobody knew about. Not even the rats. Especially not the rats. Varro stood by the window with his back straight the way soldiers stand when they're trying to remember who they used to be, and he wouldn't look at Sable directly.

"You've been asking questions," he said. "About the menagerie rotations. About which guards draw midwinter duty."

"I ask a lot of questions. It's how I stay alive."

"This one you shouldn't have asked." He turned then, and Sable saw it in his face before he said a word—the particular grayness around the mouth of a man carrying something heavy and rotten. "Vessane's witch-hunters came to me three days ago. They have my sons."

Sable kept still. Keep still, keep talking, keep the exits mapped. "How old?"

"Nine and twelve." His voice cracked on *twelve* and he hated it, Sable could see him hating it. "The younger one draws pictures of wolves. The older one wants to be a knight. They took them from my sister's farm in Corrowdale and they sent me a drawing. In charcoal. A child's hand. And a note that said I would get them back when I delivered the beast-speaker and the wolf."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones, and a chandler's boy sang a song about spring that nobody believed in anymore.

"Varro." Sable made their voice flat, uninterested, the voice of a merchant discussing freight. "You know what happens to those boys either way. You know what she does to people who've served their purpose."

"I'm choosing to believe otherwise." His jaw worked. "I'm choosing it because the alternative is that I burn the world down for nothing."

"And so you came here to tell me. Why? You could've just done it. Walked me into the palace under your captain's seal, collected your sons, been done."

"Because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I watched you in the cellar. When the knight was describing the anchor, the stag. You weren't listening like someone planning a rescue. You were listening like someone planning to pay for it. Whatever it costs." His eyes finally met Sable's, and there was something in them Sable hadn't expected: shame, yes, but underneath it, a terrible kind of respect. "People like that don't come along often. People willing to spend themselves. I've spent twenty years watching everyone hoard themselves instead."

Sable said nothing. The old instinct screamed: *run, now, out the window, across the roofs, vanish.* But running meant leaving Zephyrine without warning, and leaving Zephyrine without warning meant the plan died here, in this dusty loft, along with a king and a realm and whatever small stubborn thing had started growing in Sable's chest since a rat told them about a healer who talked to wolves.

"How long do we have?" Sable asked.

"They move tomorrow night. Midwinter vigil. The hunters will come to the tannery an hour after the bells—I'm supposed to lead them myself, so no one can claim I warned anyone."

"Supposed to."

"Supposed to." Something almost like a smile. "But I'm a poor liar, and worse at betrayal. So here is what I can give you: the hour, the number, the names of the two hunters who'll actually fight rather than posture. And one piece of advice."

"Give me all three."

"The advice first. Don't run through the warrens. They've seeded it with hounds that smell magic—real ones, not ours. Go up. Rooftops, bell towers, the old aqueduct. Nobody watches the sky."

"And the third thing?"

Varro looked at the window, at the gray light dying over Marrowgate's crooked skyline. "Tell your beast-speaker that some betrayals are just love wearing armor that doesn't fit. They'll understand. They talk to animals. Animals know all about that."

He left without another word, boots quiet on the ladder, and Sable sat alone in the loft for a long time, doing arithmetic with lives.

* * *

The cellar beneath the tannery smelled of lye and old blood, as always, and tonight it held seven people, one wolf, and a plan balanced on a knife's edge. Sable laid it out plainly, because plainness was mercy: the raid coming, the hour, the route up and out.

"We scatter," said Ser Aldous, the oldest knight, gray-bearded and granite-faced. "Regroup at the aqueduct shrine in three days."

"No." Zephyrine spoke from the corner where they sat with Fen's head in their lap. Their voice had changed since Sable had known them—harder, quieter, like water cutting stone. "We don't scatter. We don't have three days. Midwinter is *tomorrow night*. If we miss it, the binding renews itself for another year, and Aldric spends another winter screaming inside his own skull while Vessane wears his face to war."

"So we go now," said Ser Maren. "Tonight. Through the gates before they close."

"Through gates crawling with hunters who already know about this cellar?" Sable shook their head. "No. We go tomorrow, but we go *loud*. I give them something to chase."

Every head turned. Zephyrine's eyes narrowed—they'd spent enough time around smugglers to do the math fast. "You mean you."

"I mean me." Sable kept their voice light, which was the hardest thing they'd ever done. "I'm the one whose face isn't on any bounty board yet. I walk in the front gate of the tannery district tomorrow night, loud, drunk, conspicuous, carrying papers that look like the cell's real plans. They take me. They interrogate me. While they're busy with me, the rest of you go up—rooftops, aqueduct, Varro's route—and you're inside the palace grounds by midnight."

"Absolutely not." Zephyrine was on their feet. Fen rose with them, hackles lifting. "You'd be handing yourself to Vessane's witch-hunters. Do you know what they do to—"

"I know exactly what they do. I've moved people out from under them for six years. I know the inside of their cells better than they do." Sable met Zephyrine's eyes and held them. "Listen to me. This whole plan rests on one person reaching that stag, and that person is you. Not me. I'm a fence-runner and a liar. You're the only beast-speaker in a thousand miles who can hear what that animal's saying. My life buys yours a clear road. That's not a hard trade. That's the easiest trade I've ever made."

"It's not yours to make alone!"

"Neither was trusting me with the rats' parliament. Neither was walking into Marrowgate with a price on your head and nowhere else to go." Sable softened, just slightly. "Zeph. Six months ago I'd have sold you for less than what Varro's getting. I know exactly what I am. But somewhere between the cellar and the road north, you stopped being cargo and started being—" They stopped. Swallowed it. "Started being mine to look after. Let me do my job."

Fen padded forward and pressed his great gray head against Sable's chest, once, briefly—a gesture Sable had never seen the wolf offer anyone but Zephyrine. Then he stepped back.

"He says you're stubborn," Zephyrine translated quietly. "And that he'll find you. After."

"Tell him I'd be insulted if he didn't."

* * *

Midwinter Eve came down cold and starless, the bells tolling nine across a city holding its breath. Sable walked into the tannery district wearing their loudest coat and their best drunk, singing a smuggling ballad at full volume, a satchel of forged plans slung carelessly over one shoulder.

They made it forty feet past the district gate before the nets came down.

They fought—not to win, never to win, but to make it look real, to sell the capture, to buy every extra minute of confusion. A hunter's boot found their ribs. Someone wrenched their arms back and cinched iron around their wrists, and Sable laughed through bloodied lips because the laughter was part of the performance too.

"Well, well," said the hunter-captain, hauling the satchel open. "The ghost of Marrowgate's underworld, delivering herself. Who else is in your little cell?"

"Just me and my bad decisions," Sable said. "Though I'll warn you—I'm much more fun than I look."

They were dragged toward the wagons, and as the hood came down, Sable caught one last glimpse over their shoulder: rooftops dark and empty, the aqueduct shadow silent, and—just for a heartbeat—a gray shape motionless on the ridge of the chandler's shop, watching.

*Going,* Sable thought, as the hood swallowed the world. *Good. Run.*

The wagon lurched forward. Somewhere behind them, faint and fading, a wolf began to howl—one long note that didn't sound like grief at all.

It sounded like a promise.
