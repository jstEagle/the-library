## 15. Alone With the Storm

The blizzard came down off the mountains like something with intent.

Zephyrine watched it swallow the road behind them, then the walls of the capital, then the last grey smear of the city itself, until the world was nothing but white noise and the sound of their own breathing inside a stolen shepherd's cloak. Fen moved ahead, a dark shape flickering in and out of existence, and Zephyrine followed by faith alone.

The menagerie sat on the palace's western edge, beyond the formal gardens, beyond even the servants' sprawl—a walled compound of iron and timber that Vessane had no reason to visit and every reason to forget. The king's collection. His father's, really: bears and lynxes and a pair of snow leopards bought at ruinous cost from traders who had taken them as cubs from mothers that still remembered their names. The old king had loved his beasts. Vessane, whatever she was, did not love anything.

Fen found the gap in the wall where a drainage channel ran beneath the foundations, choked now with ice but passable if you were willing to crawl through a throat of frozen filth. Zephyrine was willing. They had been willing to do worse things lately.

They came up inside a yard buried to the knee in snow, and the first thing that reached them—not through ears, not yet, but through the gift, through the low constant hum they had carried since childhood—was grief.

It rolled over them like weather. Dozens of small hungers, dozens of larger despairs, all pressed together in this square of iron and silence. Zephyrine stood in the falling white with snow gathering on their shoulders and felt the whole compound breathing around them, slow and starved and waiting for nothing, because waiting was the one luxury the caged could not afford.

*You shouldn't be here,* Fen said. He stood close, flank against their leg, fur crusted with ice. *This place is wrong. It hums wrong.*

"I know." Zephyrine's voice came out rough. "That's why we're here."

They had come for shelter, yes—for one night out of the wind before the final push toward the menagerie's true prize, the thing Fen's memory kept circling like a moth at glass. But there was more than strategy in it. Zephyrine would not have admitted it aloud, not even to Fen, but some part of them had needed to be here. Among the caged. Where the air matched what they felt.

*Then let me go first.*

"You always say that."

*And you always ignore me.* But he went first anyway, nose low, reading the snow, and Zephyrine followed him past the bear pit where a brown shape lay so still they thought it dead until its ribs moved—once, twice, a breath budgeted like coin. Past the lynx enclosure where two pale eyes tracked them from a hollow of shadow. Past cages whose occupants they couldn't see at all, only feel: a coil of serpent-sleep, a flutter of bird-panic, something heavy pacing somewhere in the dark with the patience of the long-imprisoned.

The storm thickened. It came down so hard that the far wall vanished, and the world shrank to a circle ten paces wide, and in that circle there was only Zephyrine and Fen and the breathing of beasts.

Zephyrine found an overhang beside the feed store, a lean-to of rotting planks where the snow couldn't reach, and sat down with their back to the wood. Fen folded himself against them, a furnace of fur and heartbeat. For a long time neither of them spoke. There didn't need to be words. That was the strange mercy of Fen—that silence with him was never empty.

But tonight the silence filled itself up with everything Zephyrine had been refusing to look at since the cellar beneath the tannery.

*Say it,* Fen said at last.

"Say what?"

*The thing you've been carrying since Marrowgate. Since before Marrowrine. I can smell it on you. Fear has a smell, and yours has gone stale from being held too long.*

Zephyrine almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was quieter than the snow. "When I called the warrens, Fen. All those rats, all those dogs, every pigeon in half the city. When I pulled them to me like—" They stopped. Started again. "Like pulling threads. Like I was the loom."

*I remember.*

"So does everyone else. Half the witch-hunters in the capital are hunting a ghost that can speak to every living thing at once." Zephyrine stared out into the white. "But that's not what frightens me. What frightens me is that it didn't feel like enough. Do you understand? I stood in that cellar with the door coming down and I reached for everything alive within a mile, and some part of me—the part that's supposed to be me—wanted to reach further. Wanted to keep reaching. And when I finally let go, when the rats scattered and the dogs went home, I felt it close behind me like a door. And I don't know, Fen. I don't know if next time I'd let it close."

Fen was quiet a moment. Snow ticked against the lean-to roof.

*My father's pack had a saying,* he said. *A wolf who howls too long forgets which voice is his.*

"That's not comforting."

*No. It isn't meant to be.* He shifted, pressing closer. *But hear the rest. A wolf who never howls at all—isn't a wolf. Just a dog waiting to die.*

Zephyrine looked down at him. Ice had gotten into the fur around his wound—the old wound, the silver-cored bolt that had started all of this—and he was thinner than he'd been in autumn, all of them thinner, worn down by roads and fear and the particular exhaustion of carrying a secret the size of a kingdom. The bolt scar showed pale through the broken fur. He had died for this message once already. He had let Zephyrine carry him across the ridgeline knowing what it would cost them both.

"The loyalists said only a beast-speaker can reach the anchor," Zephyrine said. "Only someone like me can unbind Aldric. And the unbinding—" Their hands curled in their lap. "Sable heard them talking. Everyone hears them talking; they don't bother whispering around the mute scullery girl. The binding is woven through a living creature, Fen. To unravel it I'll have to pour myself into it. Every strand of the gift, all at once, held open while it tears free. And the knights talk about it like arithmetic. Cost of doing. Price of victory." A breath. "Nobody says the obvious part out loud. That when it's done, there may not be enough of me left to walk away."

*There will be.*

"You don't know that."

*No,* Fen admitted. *I don't. But I know you. And I know what I felt the night you dragged me through the pass with a bolt in my chest and magic in your hands for the first time. You were terrified. You were grieving. You were more alone than any creature I have ever known.* His amber eyes caught what little light the storm allowed. *And you still stopped. You still chose, every moment, how much to give. Power doesn't take you, Zephyrine. You give it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.*

"And if I give too much?"

*Then I will be there to bite your hand and remind you whose hand it is.*

Despite everything, Zephyrine laughed—a short, cracked sound, swallowed instantly by the snow. Fen's tail thumped once against the frozen ground, satisfied.

"It's not just the unbinding," Zephyrine said, after a while. Quieter now. The truth under the truth. "It's before that. Tonight. Tomorrow. Every day between here and midwinter. Because I can feel them, Fen. Right now. All of them. This whole yard full of starving hearts, and every single one of them is calling, and they don't even know they're calling, they're just—" They pressed the heel of a hand against their sternum, where the hum lived. "I could open the cages. Right now, in the storm, before anyone knew. I could speak to every beast in this compound and they would follow me out through the drainage channel and into the hills and half of them might even live. And the other half would freeze, or starve, or lead hunters straight to us, and I know that, I know it, and I still want to. Can't you see? That's the thing I'm afraid of. Not the power. The wanting."

Fen rose. He did it slowly, the way he did everything now, joints stiff with cold and old damage, and he turned to face Zephyrine and sat down again directly in front of them, blocking their view of the storm, so that there was nothing in the world but his face.

*Look at me.*

"I'm looking."

*You opened the cages in the warrens. Hundreds of animals, chaos, fire, witch-hunters dying in the streets. And do you know what you did afterward?*

"I ran."

*You wept. For three hours, behind a tanner's shed, you shook and wept for rats you'd never met, because two of them died in the crossing and you counted them. Two rats.* His voice, in that place behind Zephyrine's eyes where his words lived, was absolutely steady. *Vessane burns cities. You count rats. Whatever you are afraid of becoming, little speaker, you are very bad at becoming it.*

The snow fell. Somewhere across the compound, the heavy thing that paced had stopped pacing, and Zephyrine realized with a lurch that it was listening—that it had been listening for some time, the way caged things listen, with their whole bodies.

*And you are not alone with it,* Fen went on. *That's the other thing. You keep building this fear a private room and locking yourself in with it, and I am done standing outside the door. So hear me now, and hear me all the way: whatever you pour out at midwinter, I will be there. If your gift tears loose strand by strand, I will feel every strand with you. If you fall, I will catch what's left. If there is nothing left to catch—* a pause, and for the first time his steadiness wavered, just slightly, like ice flexing over deep water— *then I will lie down beside what remains of you, and I will stay. That is not devotion, Zephyrine. Wolves don't do devotion. It's simpler than that. You are mine, and I am yours, and that is the whole of the law.*

Zephyrine's throat worked. The storm howled against the walls of the menagerie, and inside the walls, all those small starved hearts beat on, and none of it—not the usurper, not the armies, not the binding or the price or the terrible arithmetic of midwinter—none of it was as large as a wounded wolf sitting in the snow telling them they belonged to each other.

"I'm scared," Zephyrine said. Plainly. No armor on it.

*I know.* Fen leaned forward and pressed his forehead against theirs, fur against skin, breath against breath. *Be scared. Be scared and do it anyway. That's all courage has ever been—we just gave it a nicer name so we could pretend it costs less.*

They stayed like that while the blizzard spent itself against the compound walls. At some point Zephyrine slept, or half-slept, wedged against Fen's warmth in the lee of the feed store, and dreamed of nothing—which was its own kind of mercy.

They woke to a changed silence.

Not peace. Attention. The whole menagerie had gone quiet in the way a forest goes quiet, and Zephyrine came fully awake in a single heartbeat, hand finding Fen's ruff, every nerve singing.

Across the yard, in the largest enclosure—a long cage of black iron that the old king had built for something grander than anything it now held—a shape stood at the bars.

Tall. Thin. Too thin, grotesquely, starvation carved into every line of it, antlers like bare winter branches pressed flat against its skull. It stood in the falling snow and looked at Zephyrine with eyes that were not animal eyes, had never been entirely animal eyes, and when it spoke, its voice arrived without sound at all, straight into the hollow of Zephyrine's chest:

*You came at last. I have been waiting so long to be spoken to.*

Zephyrine's blood went cold and then colder, because they knew—they knew with absolute certainty—what stood before them. Not a stag. The anchor. The suffering heart of the binding, the creature whose pain held a king prisoner inside his own body.

And it had seen them. It had heard them. Which meant—

*Yes,* the stag said, and there was something in its voice that might have been hope, worn smooth as a river stone by years of captivity. *She knows you're here too. She felt you cross her threshold an hour ago. Run, little speaker—or finish what you came for. Either way, the storm is about to break.*
