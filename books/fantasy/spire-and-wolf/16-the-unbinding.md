## 16. The Unbinding

The menagerie at midwinter was a graveyard that breathed.

Zephyrine moved between the cages with a lantern hooded to a sliver of light, and every animal watched them pass. Not the dull watching of beasts accustomed to captivity—something worse. A watching full of recognition. They knew what Zephyrine was. They had known since the first night, when Zephyrine had pressed a palm to the bars of the wolf pen and heard not barking but *names*, each animal introducing itself in the old tongue of hunger and fear: *I am Ash. I am Bracken. I am the one they took from the northern forests when my coat was still milk-soft.*

They had all introduced themselves. None of them had ever asked Zephyrine's name. The animals of Vessane's menagerie had stopped believing names mattered.

Fen padded at their heel, grey as the snowfall sifting through the roof's broken slats. The stag's enclosure stood at the far end, apart from the others, as if even the keepers had sensed there was something wrong about it. Wrong was too small a word. As Zephyrine drew closer, the air itself changed—thickened, sweetened, like the moment before lightning. Fen's hackles rose in a ridge down his spine.

*There,* Fen said. His voice in Zephyrine's skull had grown familiar over these weeks on the road, low and dry as river stones. *That is where he put it. That is where Aldric's crown went to die.*

The stag lay in the trampled snow of its pen, flanks heaving, ribs standing out like the frame of a burned house. It was enormous—a white stag, or it had been once; its coat had gone the yellow of old bone. Its antlers were magnificent and terrible, grown wrong, curling back toward its own skull as though trying to return inside it. And around its neck, sunk into the flesh so deeply that fur no longer grew there, was a circlet of iron worked into the shape of intertwined thorns.

The crown. Not the crown Aldric wore in portraits—the true one, the binding-crown, the anchor. Sable's smuggler contacts had whispered of it for weeks before the loyalists confirmed it: a spell this size needed a heart, and a heart needed to suffer steadily, endlessly, to keep the knot tied. Vessane had chosen well. A stag was a king-beast in the old stories. A starving stag, caged in sight of the palace windows, its pain feeding the glamour that let her wear her brother's face—

Zephyrine knelt in the snow outside the bars.

The stag lifted its head. Its eyes were the worst thing. Not empty. *Patient.* An animal that had suffered so long it had filed suffering under weather, under winter, under things that simply were.

"Hello," Zephyrine said aloud, and then, because the gift deserved better than human words, said it the other way—in the speech that lived below language, in the grammar of breath and stance and open hands. *I see you. I know what you are carrying. I have come to take it.*

The stag did not answer at first. Then, faintly, in a voice worn down to thread:

*You are the speaker. He told us you would come. The wolf told the rats, the rats told the sparrows, the sparrows told me. I did not believe. Nothing comes here. Nothing has ever come here.*

"I'm here."

*Then you are either a lie or a miracle,* the stag said, *and I am too tired to care which.*

Fen pressed against the bars, and the stag's nostrils flared. *Hound,* it said, with something almost like warmth. *You smell of him still. Of the king. Is it true? Does he scream?*

*Every hour of every day,* Fen said. *He screams, and she smiles with his mouth, and the world bows to a mask. We are going to end it tonight.*

Zephyrine examined the lock—iron, cold, ordinary. The cage was never meant to hold magic; it was meant to hold a starved animal too weak to fight. Their picks trembled. Not from cold. From the thing they had agreed to do, waiting inside them like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the truth Sable had made them say out loud, two nights ago, over a guttering candle in the tannery cellar: unbinding the spell would require a power equal to the power that made it. The binding had been woven by seven sorcerers over a year, fed by a stag's endless agony. Zephyrine had one source of power. One. Themselves.

*It will take your voice,* Fen had said, the night he finally admitted it. *All of it. Every strand. You will speak to animals for the last time, and then you will be like everyone else, and you will live.*

*Or you fail halfway,* Sable had added, practical to the marrow, *and lose the voice anyway AND leave the king enthralled. So aim well.*

Now Zephyrine set down the picks, and the lock gave, and the gate swung open on silence.

The stag did not rise. Zephyrine crawled to it through the snow, laid both hands flat against its heaving side, and felt the binding thrum beneath the hide like a second heartbeat—vast, intricate, patient. Threads of it ran up through the antlers, out through the palace stones, across the city, into the great hall where Vessane stood tonight in Aldric's body, addressing ten thousand soldiers before the march on the free cities. Midwinter. The longest night. The night the binding was woven, and the only night it could be unwoven.

*It will hurt,* the stag said. *When you pull it out of me, it will hurt more than anything. It has been hurting me for six years. I would like it to stop. I would like it to stop even if stopping kills me.*

"It won't kill you," Zephyrine said. "I promise you spring."

*I don't remember spring.*

"You will."

Zephyrine closed their eyes and opened the gift.

Not a crack this time, not the careful trickle they'd used to calm horses and question rats. They opened it the way a door opens in a flood. Twenty-two years of concealment came off in a single motion—every word bitten back in Hollowmere, every conversation smothered before it began, every night spent whispering to foxes with one ear turned toward the house—and Zephyrine let it all pour out at once into the stag's wound.

The binding *noticed*.

It came for them like a net coming for a fish. Zephyrine felt it map them, taste them, find the bright tangled root of the beast-speech coiled at the base of their soul—and seize it. Good, some distant part of them thought, cold and clear. *Pull.* They pulled.

The first strand tore away like a nail leaving wood.

Zephyrine screamed. There was no dignity in it, no transcendence yet—just pain, white and total, the sound of a lifelong sense being ripped out at the root. The stag screamed too, a sound like a tree splitting, because the binding fought its extraction by tightening everywhere at once. Snow leapt off the ground around them. Fen threw himself against the far wall of the pen and howled, and his howl was not an animal sound but a word repeated over and over in the old tongue: *hold, hold, hold.*

Second strand. Third. Each one took something specific, and Zephyrine felt each loss individually, catalogued in the instant of its going: the speech of birds, gone, a whole sky going silent. The speech of the deep water-creatures, gone, though they'd only ever touched it once, at nine years old, listening to a pike tell ghost stories about the drowned. The speech of wolves—Fen's voice fraying at the edges of hearing, and Zephyrine clung to that one, hoarded it, made it the last.

*Let go of me,* Fen said, and his voice was already thinning, going papery. *Save your strength for the knot. Don't spend it on goodbyes.*

"No," Zephyrine gasped. "You're last. You're *last*. I get to keep you longest."

Fourth strand. Fifth. The binding was shrieking now, a sound with no air in it, a sound made of law and geometry, and somewhere above the menagerie the whole palace shuddered on its foundations. Lights bloomed in the windows. Bells began—no, not bells. Alarums. Somewhere in the great hall, ten thousand soldiers were watching their king stagger mid-sentence, watching the face on their king's skull ripple like water.

Sixth strand. The speech of stags themselves, and the stag beneath Zephyrine's hands sighed, long and shuddering, as the thorn-crown loosened its grip on its neck for the first time in six years. Blood welled black in the starlight where the iron had sat. The stag's eyes cleared. For one moment they were young eyes, forest eyes.

*Oh,* the stag said. *Oh, it's quiet. It's so quiet. I had forgotten there was a me underneath it.*

Seventh strand. The last. The knot itself, the heart of it, the place where seven sorcerers had tied a king's soul to a cage. Zephyrine poured everything remaining into that single point—all of it, the whole twenty-two years, Hollowmere and the valley that forgot, the healer's garden, the crow on the north road, the market town burning, Sable's crooked grin, Fen running beside them through the snow—and tore.

The world went white.

*

Zephyrine came back to themselves in stages. First: cold. Second: weight, the weight of a stag's head resting in their lap, breathing slow and deep and easy. Third: silence—not the binding's silence, which had always been a held breath, but real silence, plain silence, the silence of an ordinary winter night.

And inside that silence, where the beast-speech had lived for as long as Zephyrine could remember, there was nothing. A smooth blank wall. A room with the hearth swept cold.

They tried to reach for it anyway, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. Nothing answered. No rustle of sparrow-minds, no rat-chatter in the walls, no Fen—

"Fen." Aloud. Human words, clumsy, all they had left. "Fen, can you hear me?"

The wolf limped over on legs that shook. He looked down at Zephyrine for a long moment, and his amber eyes were wet, and whatever he said—or didn't say—reached Zephyrine only as a soft whine, a tilt of the head, a paw placed carefully on their knee.

Gone. All of it. Zephyrine put their arms around the stag's neck and the wolf's shoulders together and wept without sound, because even crying felt loud now, felt like spending something they couldn't replace.

Above them, the palace erupted.

*

They saw it from the menagerie door, because neither of them could stand, and because the whole city seemed determined to show them.

The great hall's high windows blazed against the dark—and then, in the space of three heartbeats, the blaze *changed*. Light that had been warm gold curdled, flickered, split. Even at this distance, even through snow, Zephyrine could see the figure on the dais convulse—could see the shape of the man who ruled the realm buckle like a banner in wind.

The glamour didn't shatter dramatically. It *peeled*, the way ice peels off a window in thaw, in long curling sheets, and what stood revealed beneath it was a woman in a stolen body that no longer fit her—Vessane, wearing Aldric's face the way a thief wears a coat two sizes wrong, and the face was melting, sliding, becoming hers and not hers, becoming the truth. Ten thousand soldiers saw their king come apart in front of them. Ten thousand soldiers saw a stranger wearing him.

And then another figure rose on the dais. Thin, grey-faced, moving like a man learning to walk again after a long illness—but moving as *himself*, his own posture, his own ruined grace. King Aldric, awake in his own body for the first time in six years, gripped the edge of the dais, looked out at his army, and spoke.

No words reached the menagerie. But the sound reached them—the roar, rising off the hall like heat off a fire, rolling across the courtyard, shaking snow from the rooftops. Ten thousand voices discovering, all at once, what they had been made to cheer.

Fen leaned hard against Zephyrine's side. Zephyrine buried a hand in his ruff, feeling only fur and warmth and the steady drum of his heart, understanding nothing he might be saying, understanding everything anyway. Some things had never needed the gift. The way he pressed close. The way he had refused, all these miles, to walk behind.

"He's awake," Zephyrine whispered. "We did it. She's finished."

But even as they said it, they saw it: at the hall's far doors, movement. A wedge of black-armored figures cutting through the chaos with terrible discipline—the elite guard, Vessane's own, forming a ring around their mistress as she staggered free of the collapsing glamour. Not surrendering. Retreating. And beyond them, in the courtyard below the menagerie, horses being saddled in the dark, quietly, by men who moved like people who had planned for this night to go wrong.

The stag struggled upright beside them, swaying, gaunt, alive, its bare neck bleeding freely into the snow. It looked at the gates, and then at Zephyrine, and made a sound—not speech anymore, just a sound, but Zephyrine read it easily enough in the set of its ears and the angle of its skull.

*She runs,* the stag's body said. *And she knows who unbound her.*

Down in the courtyard, a rider swung up onto a pale horse, and even at this distance, even half-dissolved, Zephyrine knew the set of those shoulders. Vessane wheeled toward the north gate—toward the road, the river, the armies still loyal to her—and paused, one heartbeat, two, her head turning slowly until her gaze climbed the wall and found the open door of the menagerie and the figures standing in it.

Across the snow-dark distance, across the falling night, the false king's borrowed eyes met Zephyrine's.

Then the north gate crashed open, and she was gone into the storm, and the hunt began.
