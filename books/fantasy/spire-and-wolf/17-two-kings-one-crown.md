## 17. Two Kings, One Crown

The silence after the glamour broke lasted three heartbeats.

I counted them. I had nothing else to do—my hands were still pressed flat against the stag's bare ribs in the frozen menagerie, my gift was a raw socket where it used to live, and somewhere above me, through stone and snow and a hundred feet of castle, the false king's voice had just torn open like wet paper mid-sentence.

Then the screaming started.

Not one voice. Hundreds. The great hall above us must have been packed for the midwinter address—lords and ladies, ambassadors from the free cities, the whole glittering machinery of Vessane's court—and every one of them had been looking at the throne when her face came apart. When the man wearing Aldric's skin stopped being whatever she'd made them see and became what he actually was: a stranger. A hollow thing with the king's cheekbones and no king behind his eyes.

The sound that rolled down through the floors wasn't panic yet. It was the sound people make when their minds are trying to catch up to their eyes.

Fen's shoulder hit mine. *Up,* the wolf said—but not in words anymore. That was the strange arithmetic of what I'd done. The binding was unmade, the stag was free, and the voice that had been mine for twenty-two years was gone with it. What remained was older and simpler: the pressure of Fen's body against my leg, the angle of his ears, the meaning in the set of his hackles. *Up. Now. They'll seal the menagerie.*

He was right. He was always right about this—the practical shape of danger, the geometry of escape. I hauled myself up the ladder after him while below us the stag, gaunt and trembling and free for the first time in years, took one step, then another, then bolted through the shattered gate into the blizzard like a brown ghost returning to some story older than crowns.

We came up into chaos.

The servants' corridors were full of running bodies. Scullery maids with their caps askew. A chamberlain shouting orders nobody heard. Two of Vessane's grey-cloaks shoving through the crowd toward the hall, then—this was the part I would remember later, in the quiet, when I let myself think about it—stopping. Looking at each other. And turning around, quietly, like men deciding which way the wind had shifted and choosing not to be downwind of it.

Because word travels faster than soldiers. By the time we reached the gallery overlooking the great hall, half the castle already knew what had happened there, and the other half knew something had happened, and both halves understood that the next hour would decide whose side survival lay on.

We looked down through the carved screen into the hall itself.

* * *

Vessane was gone.

That was the first thing I registered—not the overturned tables, not the candelabra guttering where they'd been knocked, but the empty space at the head of the room where she should have been standing. Her elite guard had formed a ring around the dais and then around nothing at all, and now they were backing toward the west doors in perfect formation, shields out, faces blank beneath their helms, retreating from a hall full of people who had watched their queen's face dissolve.

And on the steps below the throne, half-kneeling, half-supported by two men in livery who had clearly decided in the last five minutes that they were loyalists after all, was King Aldric.

He looked terrible. That was my first thought, and shame followed it, and then I thought it again because it was true. Twenty years of enthralment had left him like a house long shut up—structurally sound, faintly ruined, everything inside faded. He was thinner than the coins and portraits made him look. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way no glamour would have permitted. And his eyes—

His eyes were awake. That was the thing. Whatever Vessane had worn him as, whatever puppet-skin she'd stretched herself into, the man underneath had been screaming the whole time—I'd heard him, that night in the scullery, heard his voice like a drowning man's under ice—and now the ice was broken and he was looking at his own hall, his own stolen throne, his own terrified court, with an expression I recognized from the mirror on hard days: grief and fury holding each other upright.

"Bring me light," Aldric said.

His voice cracked on the second word. It didn't matter. The hall went quiet enough to hear candles burning.

"Bring me light," he said again, stronger, "because I have spent twenty years in the dark, and I will not give another speech in it."

They brought torches. And by that light, leaning on the shoulders of men whose names he somehow still knew—twenty years imprisoned in his own body, and he remembered their fathers' names—Aldric denounced her.

I won't pretend I caught every word. The gallery was thick with fugitive servants, and Fen was a low growl against my knee, and my own ears were still ringing with the tearing sound of the glamour breaking. But pieces came down to us in fragments, passed mouth to mouth along the crowd like coins:

"—wore my face to my own army—"

"—the purges were signed with my hand I never held—"

"—the free cities bled while I watched from inside my own skull—"

And then the fragment that mattered, the one that ran through the crowd and came back changed every time but never lost its spine:

"—I do not ask you to believe me. Belief is earned. I ask you only to look at what she did and ask yourselves what else she lied about. Justice begins tonight. It does not end tonight. Go home. Hold your houses. And when her soldiers come to your doors and tell you the king commands you—*look at the king they mean.*"

It wasn't a rousing speech. It was better than that. It was a tired man telling the truth badly, and the hall believed him precisely because of it.

Then the west doors opened, and the Grey Guard came back in.

* * *

There were perhaps forty of them—Vessane's elite, the ones who'd guarded her body since before anyone could remember, the ones whose oaths were said to be written in things older than ink. They didn't charge. They formed a wedge across the western end of the hall, and through their parted ranks came a herald carrying a banner I'd seen once before, burning over a market town square: the crowned wolf reversed, the sigil of the regency.

The herald read the proclamation in a voice trained for distance. That Vessane remained the rightful regent. That the man claiming kingship was an impostor, a traitor, possibly enchanted. That all who aided him shared his treason.

It might have worked. Fear is a strong glue; it had held her realm together for twenty years. But the herald made one mistake. He looked at Aldric while he read it.

Everyone saw him look. Everyone saw the flicker in his eyes—the same flicker I'd seen in the grey-cloaks in the corridor, the calculation of a man realizing the story had changed under his feet. And Lord Cassery—I learned his name later, the old knight from the tannery cellar, the one whose network Sable had nearly died to protect—stepped out of the crowd with his sword drawn and said, mildly, the way you'd correct a child:

"Son, you're reading that to the wrong king."

The hall laughed. It was a dangerous laugh, a tipping-point laugh, and the Grey Guard commander knew it. Their wedge tightened. Somewhere a woman screamed. I felt Fen's muscles coil under my hand and thought, absurdly, *not here, not now, we're so close—*

And then the bells started.

Every bell in the capital, all at once. Not the alarm pattern—the *answer* pattern, the one rung when a city calls its people to its walls. The loyalists hadn't just infiltrated the court. They'd reached the bell-towers. And with the bells came something else, rolling in from beyond the walls, faint but unmistakable even through stone: horns. Many horns. From the north road, from the river gate, from the east—from the direction of Marrowgate and the free cities, from the direction of everything Vessane had burned, converging.

She had marched on the capital expecting a garrison. She had gotten a reckoning.

The Grey Guard commander made the only choice available to a professional facing arithmetic. The wedge turned. The banner withdrew. And Vessane's elite guard carried their empty dais-protection out of the hall at a disciplined march, and the moment the doors closed behind them, the great hall of the capital erupted—not into battle, but into something stranger and louder: argument, weeping, oath-swearing, lords falling over each other to kneel to a king they'd cheered for twenty years, and Aldric standing in the middle of it looking like a man watching a flood and trying to remember which way was uphill.

* * *

He found us an hour later.

Not me specifically—he found the stag's empty pen, or rather his men did, and followed the trail of the unbinding backward through the snow, and Fen refused to leave my side long enough for me to slip away, so when the torchlight came down into the menagerie's ruins I was sitting on a feed barrel with a blanket someone had given me, listening to the blizzard, feeling the enormous new silence inside my chest.

Aldric came down the steps himself. His guards tried to flank him and he waved them off at the door—a small gesture, sharp, the reflex of a man who'd spent twenty years unable to control who stood near him.

For a long moment he just looked at the wrecked pen. The shattered chain. The blood-flecked straw where the stag had lain starving for years, feeding its pain into a spell.

"Twenty years," he said. His voice was rough. "Do you know what I remember? Everything. Every purge. Every hanging. Every time she smiled with my mouth at some grieving mother. I was in there for all of it. Watching." He turned. "You heard me."

It wasn't a question. I shook my head anyway, then stopped, because lying to this man seemed suddenly obscene. "In the scullery. Under the glamour. I heard—" My throat closed. There was no voice to reach him with anymore, no way to say it in the language that would have made it easy, and I discovered that grief for a gift includes grief for every sentence you can no longer build in it. "I heard you screaming. Yes."

"Then you know." He stepped closer. Up close he smelled of cold and candle smoke and, faintly, of the menagerie—as if some part of him had been kept here too. "I owe a debt I don't have words for. So I'll say the plain thing instead." His eyes went to Fen, and something passed between them that I couldn't read—old recognition, deep and bruised. "Whatever you need. Whatever you've lost. The crown owes it."

*I lost the only thing I ever wanted to keep,* I didn't say. What I said was: "Finish it. That's the debt. Don't stop at the walls."

Something moved in his face—surprise, maybe, that a person standing in front of him asking for justice instead of gold.

"It's not finished," I said. "She's still out there. Her guard is still armed. Half the realm still thinks she's the king. Tonight isn't justice. Tonight is the beginning of it."

Aldric looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a man accepting terms.

"The bridge," he said. "Her army will come for the river bridge before dawn—if they're coming at all. If they take it, she escapes south with the treasury and we spend ten more years bleeding." He was already turning, already becoming again the thing the realm needed instead of the ruin it had. At the door he paused. "The smuggler. Sable. My people say the bridge will need someone who knows the underside of this city better than its own guards do."

My stomach dropped. "Sable was captured. Taking the blame for us, at the gate—"

"Was freed during the chaos," Aldric said, and for the first time that night, something almost like a smile touched his ruined face. "By persons unknown. Find your friend, beast-speaker. Tell them the king asks a favor." He pulled his cloak tight. "Tell them the king *owes.*"

Then he was gone, up the stairs, into the noise and the torchlight, and I sat alone in the freezing dark with a wolf's warm weight against my leg and a silence where my life used to be.

Outside, the horns were getting closer. And somewhere south of the river, in the dark, an army was choosing its road.

Fen looked up at me. Ears forward. Body angled toward the door, toward the city, toward the bridge.

*Well?* said every line of him.

I stood up. Justice was beginning. God help everyone—it was beginning without a voice to call it by name, and it needed every pair of legs it could get.

We ran.
