## 18. The Battle of the Broken Bridge

The lock on Sable's cell was a joke. Three tumblers, iron gone soft with rust, and a guard who picked his teeth with the same knife he used to cut bread. Sable had been out of it twice in their head before the screaming started.

When it actually started—boots in the corridor, torchlight strobing through the barred window, someone shouting *she's fled, the false king's fled*—Sable went to work for real.

The hairpin had been a gift from a sympathetic turnkey's wife three days ago, tucked into a bowl of gruel like a flower. Sable had kissed the woman's cheek through the bars and told her she'd be remembered. People liked being remembered. It was the cheapest currency in the world and Sable had been minting it since they were nine years old.

The lock gave with a click that sounded, in that moment of chaos, like the whole city exhaling.

* * *

The tannery cellar was empty when Sable got there. Cold ash in the brazier, chairs overturned, a map of the capital still pinned to the wall with its little wooden markers—the menagerie, the spire, the river. Someone had left in a hurry and left the truth behind, which was more than most people managed.

A boy was crouched in the corner eating hard cheese like it might be taken from him. Sable recognized him: Tam, one of the runner-kids who ferried messages between loyalist safehouses for bread and gossip.

"Where'd everyone go?"

"Bridge." Tam didn't look up. "Vessane's rearguard is pulling out across the Broken Bridge. Aldric's forces are coming down the north bank but they're a day slow. The captain—" he stopped.

"The captain sold us," Sable said. Not a question. They'd seen it coming for weeks, the way you see weather coming. Men with sons in Vessane's prisons always broke eventually; love made worse hostages than fear.

Tam nodded at his cheese.

"And Zephyrine?"

"Gone north with the wolf. Before the raid, even. Nobody knows where."

Something in Sable's chest unclenched and then immediately clenched again about something else. Alive, then. Running. Good. That was the business. You got people out; you didn't get to know how the story ended. That was the whole arrangement with the world, and Sable had signed it gladly twenty years ago.

Except.

Except a mute scullery hand with a wolf the size of a pony had looked at Sable across a smuggler's table and said, in effect, *I have no one.* And Sable had said, in effect, *same.* And somewhere between Marrowgate and this burning city, *same* had turned into something with weight, and now Sable was standing in an empty cellar doing arithmetic about a person instead of a purse.

"Tam. How many people can you raise by dawn?"

* * *

The answer, by gray morning light, was forty-one.

They gathered in the wrecked customs house at the south end of the Broken Bridge—a bridge that had earned its name two generations back when a flood took out the middle span and the city, always broke, rebuilt it with timber and prayer rather than stone. It held carts. It would not hold an army. That was the problem, and also, Sable realized, staring at it while the river ran brown and fast beneath, possibly the answer.

Vessane's rearguard was crossing. Sable could see them from the customs house roof: columns of grey-and-silver winding down from the citadel road, wagons, banners furled because furled banners meant shame or speed or both. Maybe four hundred. Maybe five. And beyond them, on the far bank, the rest of her army regrouping in the hills—and beyond *them*, a day's march north, Aldric's loyalists coming down like winter itself.

If the rearguard crossed clean, Vessane's army stayed whole. If the rearguard got caught on the wrong side of a broken bridge—

Sable had spent a lifetime learning exactly what things could bear before they broke. Locks. Promises. Bridges. People. It was, when you thought about it, the only expertise they'd ever had. Everything else was application.

"All right," Sable said, turning to face the forty-one. Smugglers mostly, faces Sable knew from a hundred dark landings. A couple of freed prisoners still wearing their cell stink. Refugees who'd followed the sound of organization the way cold people follow fire. "Lesson one of my trade: never stand on a structure you might need to drop."

* * *

By noon the bridge had been prepared in ways that would have gotten Sable hanged under any of the last three administrations. The timber spans were sawn ninety percent through and disguised with pitch and dust. Barrels of lamp oil sat tucked in the shadowed cruxes where pier met deck. The old flood-gates upstream—rusting relics nobody had touched in decades—had been winched half-closed by six very cold volunteers standing waist-deep in February water, raising the river a hand's width and doubling its temper.

And along the south approach, behind a barricade of overturned fish carts and a customs wagon, forty-one people who had never defended anything in their lives were handed pikes, crossbows, and the following speech:

"You're not soldiers. Don't pretend. Soldiers die holding ground. We're not holding ground—we're holding *time*. Every minute that army spends looking at us is a minute Aldric's people spend marching. When I give the word, we run, and the bridge goes into the river, and anyone still on it was too slow, so don't be too slow."

A woman near the front—a dyer named Maren whose hands were still blue to the wrist—raised her hand like a child in school. "And if they just charge us?"

"They'll charge us," Sable said. "That's the good news. Charging people is easy. Crossing a bridge under arrows is hard. We only need to be hard for one afternoon."

Someone laughed, high and nervous. Then another. Sable let it run, because laughter was load-bearing too.

* * *

They came at the fourth bell.

Sable watched the grey column halt at the north end, form up, and start across in a column of fours—disciplined, unhurried, contemptuous. Of course they were contemptuous. What was at the south end but fish carts and civilians? A rearguard commander's worst day was supposed to be mud.

At fifty yards, Sable gave the signal, and forty-one crossbows spoke from behind fish carts.

It wasn't a massacre. Sable wanted that recorded somewhere, if anyone ever asked: it was not a massacre. Half the bolts went wide, because these were smugglers and dyers and dock-thieves, not archers. But enough found flesh that the column stopped, and stopping on a bridge is death's favorite invitation. The rearguard commander—brave, stupid, or both—pushed his men forward anyway, and the second volley came lower and closer together, because terror teaches aim faster than any sergeant.

Then the oil went over the middle span, and a fire-arrow from Maren—who, it turned out, had once burned down her own workshop by accident and had learned things since—turned thirty feet of bridge into a line of orange light between the two halves of the fight.

The rearguard was now split. Half on the south side, committed, furious, charging the barricade. Half on the north, watching their comrades shrink behind flame, unable to help without crossing fire.

The ones on the south side hit the fish-cart barricade like a wave hits a seawall, and the seawall bent, and Sable was in it—not commanding anymore, there was no commanding after first contact, only shoving and shouting and driving a pike into the gap where a man in grey kept trying to come through. He was young. They were all young. Sable put the pike in his shoulder instead of his throat, some old instinct overriding every survival rule they owned, and took a sword-cut across the forearm for the courtesy, and screamed, and the line screamed with them, and somehow, impossibly, the line held.

Because here was the thing Sable understood in that red bright moment, pressed against a cart with blood running off their fingers: these people had nowhere else to be. Every soldier on that bridge had an army behind him and orders above him and a home he expected to return to. Every smuggler on this side had nothing but each other and the knowledge—hard-won, bone-deep—that when the lawless band together, it isn't for a cause. It's because the alternative is going back to being alone.

Alone was the only thing Sable had ever truly feared. The army could take everything else.

* * *

The fighting went on past sunset. Twice the south-side soldiers broke through and were thrown back with knives and boat-hooks and, in one memorable case, a barrel of pickled herring wielded by a man named Osk who would dine on that story for the rest of his life. Twice Sable ordered the retreat and twice the moment passed—once because reinforcements on the north bank hesitated at the flames, once because Maren reported the sawn timbers weren't ready to drop and dropping them early would leave half the enemy safely on the far side.

So they held. Bleeding, exhausted, absurd, they held a broken bridge with fish carts and fury, and when full dark came and the fires lit the river like a festival gone wrong, Sable stood on the customs house roof again and looked north along the far road.

Torches. A long, long line of torches, moving fast.

Not Vessane's. These flew no banner Sable feared. The wolf-head standard of House Aldric came down the north road at double-march, and the rearguard trapped on the far bank heard them before they saw them—the sound carrying across the water, horns and hooves and ten thousand voices—and Sable watched five hundred soldiers realize, all at once, that they were on the wrong side of a burning bridge with an army behind them and a mob of fishmongers in front.

"Now," Sable said, to no one, to everyone. "Drop it."

Maren's crew struck the ropes. The charges blew—blackpowder, bought at criminal expense from a man in Marrowgate whom Sable intended to hug and rob in equal measure—and the middle span of the Broken Bridge folded into the river with a groan like a dying animal, taking barrels, timbers, and the last of Vessane's hopes with it.

The soldiers stranded on the south bank threw down their weapons almost gratefully. Forty-one bleeding civilians stood among them, breathing hard, hardly believing it.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, sitting on the customs house steps with their arm bound in a dyer's blue-stained rag—Sable watched Aldric's vanguard pick their way down to the ruined crossing. Engineers were already at the banks, measuring, shouting, planning pontoon bridges by morning. The realm's machinery, grinding back into motion.

Osk lowered himself onto the step beside Sable and offered a flask. Sable drank. It was terrible. It was perfect.

"You know what they're calling it already?" Osk said. "Down the lines. The Battle of the Broken Bridge. There'll be songs."

"There won't. Songs cost money."

"There'll be songs," Osk said stubbornly. "And do you know what they'll say? They'll say the loyalists held the bridge. Knights and banners. Us lot won't get a verse."

Sable thought about it. About forty-one people who'd spent their lives outside the law, outside the walls, outside the story—holding the door of the story open with their bodies while the heroes marched through.

"Good," Sable said finally. "Let them keep the verses. We'll keep the toll rights on the new bridge. Rebuild it, charge a copper a head, retire rich." They raised the flask toward the dark water where their bridge lay in pieces. "That's how the lawless defend a realm, Osk. We invoice it."

Osk laughed until he coughed.

But later still, alone, Sable took out the one thing they'd carried through the cell and the battle and the years: nothing. No token, no keepsake. Smugglers couldn't afford keepsakes. What they carried instead was a memory—a scullery hand with straw-colored hair and a silence like a held breath, saying *I have no one,* and meaning it, and Sable meaning *same* right back.

Zephyrine was somewhere north of here, in a palace full of wolves and worse, doing whatever it was that beast-speakers did when they finally stopped hiding. Probably getting themselves killed. Definitely getting themselves killed.

Sable looked at the north road, black and long, and made the kind of promise they didn't make often, because promises were debts and debts were chains.

"Hold on, whisperer," they muttered. "I'm done letting people walk out of my life into a fire. Next time, I'm walking in after them."

Far to the north, above the palace, the sky was beginning to snow.
