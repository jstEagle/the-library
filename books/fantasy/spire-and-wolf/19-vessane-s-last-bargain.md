## 19. Vessane's Last Bargain

The storm had broken by dawn, and the world outside the menagerie lay white and wounded.

Zephyrine came down from the aviary roof with frost in their hair and Fen's blood-warm flank pressed against their leg, and found the palace gates standing open. Not breached—*opened*, from within, by guards who had spent the night listening to a king scream through a stranger's mouth and could no longer pretend they hadn't heard. Word moved through the capital like water finding cracks. By the time the sun cleared the eastern towers, half the city knew that something had shattered in the great hall at midwinter midnight, and the other half was running toward it to see for themselves.

Zephyrine ran too.

They crossed the outer court with Fen loping beside them—no leash, no command, just the wolf choosing to keep pace—and pushed through the press of bodies at the hall doors. Servants, soldiers, loyalist knights still in their scullery disguises, free-city militiamen who had marched all night on rumor alone. And in the center of it all, where the dais rose beneath the winter court's banners, stood two figures wearing the same face.

One was Aldric. He looked like a man who had been drowning for three years and had only just remembered which way the surface was. He leaned on a knight's arm, his true body gaunt and trembling inside borrowed finery someone had thrown over his shoulders, but his eyes were his own—clear, exhausted, unbearably *present*. When he saw Zephyrine across the crowd, he did not speak. He pressed his hand flat to his chest, once, the way you greet someone whose name you know will break you if you say it aloud.

The other figure was being held between six men-at-arms, and she was not struggling.

Vessane's glamour was gone. What remained was a woman in her forties with grey coming in at her temples and a scholar's narrow hands—the face she'd worn before she ever learned to steal another. She looked smaller without the king's shape. She looked, Zephyrine thought with a strange lurch, like someone's sister. Someone's teacher. That was the horror of it, really: monsters were rarely built to look the part.

Her eyes found Zephyrine through the crowd, and she smiled.

It was not a defeated smile. It was not even an angry one. It was the smile of a woman doing arithmetic, and Zephyrine felt the cold of it slide under their ribs long before anyone spoke to them.

* * *

They kept her in the old map room, because the dungeons had flooded years ago and no one had thought to repair them, and because Aldric refused—refused with a vehemence that startled his own knights—to put her in chains until he understood what she still was. So she sat in a high-backed chair beneath a faded chart of the southern coasts, wrists bound with plain rope, guarded by four men who would not meet her eyes, and when the door opened and Zephyrine stepped through with Fen at their side, she said:

"There you are. I wondered how long Aldric would make me wait."

"I'm not here for him," Zephyrine said. "I'm here because he asked me to come. He wants a witness."

"A witness." Vessane turned the word over like a coin she suspected was clipped. "How very modern of him. And what do you witness, beast-speaker? A prisoner? Or a bargain?"

Fen's growl started low, a vibration more than a sound, and Zephyrine rested a hand on the wolf's neck. *Not yet.* The wolf subsided, though amber eyes never left the woman's face.

"You have nothing I want," Zephyrine said.

"No?" Vessane tilted her head. "Then why are your hands shaking?"

Zephyrine looked down. They were. They hated her for noticing.

"Sit," she said, gesturing as far as the rope allowed. "You've earned the courtesy. Do you know what I felt, at midnight? When your gift tore my binding apart strand by strand? Not pain. Not rage. *Relief.* Three years wearing another person's face, feeling him screaming underneath me every hour of every day, and when it ended—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—I nearly thanked you. Then the doors opened and I understood exactly what relief costs, and here we are."

"Here we are," Zephyrine said flatly. "Say what you brought me here to hear."

"Direct. Good. I've had enough of courtiers this morning." She leaned forward, ropes creaking. "You gave up your gift to unbind him. Every thread of it, poured out into that stag's suffering and spent. I watched you do it. I built that binding, Zephyrine Thornbury—I know its architecture better than the man who wore it as a crown. And I know what you lost, because I know what such workings take." Her voice dropped, went almost gentle, and that gentleness was worse than any threat. "You can't speak to beasts anymore. Can you? You stand next to that wolf and you hear nothing. Silence where there used to be a whole world."

The silence in the room was very loud indeed.

"I can give it back."

Zephyrine didn't move. Didn't breathe. Somewhere behind them, Fen made a sound—not a growl now, something smaller, a warning whine, the sound of a creature who could smell a trap and couldn't warn its person fast enough.

"The binding is broken," Vessane said, "but broken is not destroyed. The threads still exist. Frayed, scattered, sunk into the stones of this palace and the marrow of that stag you freed. I wove them; I can gather them. I can reweave them into *you*—every word you ever lost, every voice from crow to wolf to whale, restored whole. Better than whole. You'd hear things you never heard before. You'd be the greatest beast-speaker this realm has produced in five hundred years." She smiled again, and this time it was almost kind, which was the cruelest thing she'd done all morning. "All I ask is small. A boat. A horse. An hour's head start and one blind spot in whatever guard rotation Aldric dreams up. Help me vanish, and I'll give you back the voice that made you whole."

There it was. Laid out on the map table between them like a merchant's scales.

And Zephyrine stood very still, and let themselves feel the full weight of it.

Because gods, they wanted it. They wanted it with a hunger that scared them. Twenty-two years of hiding a gift, and then one season of finally, *finally* using it—of calling rats through the warrens, of hearing Fen's thoughts move against their own like current against current, of pouring everything they were into the stag's chains and feeling the world's voices tear away strand by strand—and then silence. Silence in the meadow where birds should be singing to them. Silence beside Fen, who had carried them across a kingdom and whose name they had learned in the last days of their hearing and would now never hear spoken in that low, warm, wolfish way again. They woke some mornings reaching for it, the way you reach for a limb that isn't there. They had grieved it. They were grieving it still, and would grieve it for years.

And this woman, this thief, this maker of cages, was offering to hand it back for the price of an hour and a blind spot.

An hour. One hour, and Vessane loose in the world with her knowledge of bindings and glamours and the architecture of enthralled kings. One hour, and somewhere down the road a village burning, a gifted child dragged from a cellar, a new binding woven around some other poor soul wearing some other stolen face. One hour, purchased with the voices of every living thing.

Zephyrine thought about Hollowmere. About twenty-two years of concealment, of a life built so carefully on silence that when betrayal came it barely had far to fall. About the elder trading names for mercy, and the mentor seized, and the lesson underneath both: that survival bought by shrinking always ends in nothing left to shrink.

They thought about Sable, taking the blame in the courtyard so a stranger could run. About the smuggler's creed, delivered once, drunk, on a rooftop in Marrowgate: *Everybody sells out eventually, love. The trick is picking a price you can live with.* Sable had picked wrong once, early, and had spent fifteen years paying it forward. Zephyrine understood that now. Understood it in their bones.

Some prices you can live with. Some prices are the living.

"No," Zephyrine said.

Vessane's expression didn't change. "Think carefully—"

"I have thought. For twenty-two years, I thought about nothing else but keeping what I am hidden, and it bought me nothing but a head start on grief." Their voice was steady. Their hands had stopped shaking. "You're offering me my gift back. I know exactly what it's worth, because I paid more for losing it than you'll ever pay for anything in your life. And I'm telling you it isn't worth an hour of you. It isn't worth a single burned village. You want to know what I learned, speaking to beasts my whole life? Animals don't bargain away each other. A wolf doesn't trade the pack for its own warmth. That's the whole of what you never understood, sitting in your tower weaving your clever bindings—you built a spell out of a starving stag because you thought suffering was fuel. It isn't. It's just suffering. And I won't be your fuel either."

For the first time, something moved behind Vessane's eyes that wasn't calculation. It might have been surprise. It might even, in some buried chamber of her, have been respect.

"You'd stay silent forever," she said quietly, "rather than deal with me."

"I'd rather be whole *without* my gift," Zephyrine said, "than complete with your price attached. There's a difference. You wouldn't recognize it. Everything you ever made whole, you made out of pieces of other people."

Fen rose then, slow and deliberate, and crossed the space between them, and stood over Vessane—not snarling, not baring teeth, just *standing*, a wall of grey muscle and ancient patience, close enough that the woman had to tilt her head back to hold the wolf's gaze. And whatever passed between them, prisoner and wolf, it made Vessane look away first.

"That," she said softly, "is the most frightening thing I've seen in three years."

* * *

Aldric received her judgment in the great hall at noon, before the assembled captains of the loyalists and the militias of the free cities, before servants and soldiers and the ragged tide of refugees who had followed the army through the gates.

But he did not pass it himself.

Zephyrine stood near the dais and watched him do it—watched the thin, hollow-cheeked king rise on legs that shook, and look out at a hall full of people who had every reason to demand vengeance, and say, in a voice roughened by three years of screaming behind someone else's face:

"She wore my crown. She wore my *face*. If I judge her, every enemy I ever make will learn that a king can be replaced and the replacement judged by the man she supplanted—that justice is just the strongest survivor's opinion. I won't build my second reign on that foundation." He turned. "She harmed the free cities most. Marrowgate burned. Ashford burned. Let their delegates sit in judgment. Let the realm watch justice done *openly*, by those who suffered, under law—not by a king settling a private score. I am owed a debt. I decline to collect it."

The hall erupted—some cheering, some furious, a captain of the loyalists shouting that mercy was weakness—but Aldric stood through all of it with the stubborn patience of a man who had already screamed himself empty of fear.

Vessane, flanked by guards, was led toward the side chamber where the free cities' delegates waited with their lists of the dead. As she passed Zephyrine, she slowed.

"For what it's worth," she murmured, "you'd have made a magnificent monster. You have the one thing I never managed." Her eyes flicked to Fen, pacing at Zephyrine's heel. "Something that follows you *freely*. Guard it. It's rarer than any gift."

Then they took her away, and the doors closed, and the hall began the long, loud business of deciding a realm's future.

Zephyrine slipped out a side door into the cold, because halls full of shouting had never been where they belonged, and found the balcony above the inner garden empty except for one figure leaning on the rail, wrapped in a guard's cloak, watching the snowmelt drip from the bare trees.

Sable glanced sideways. "Heard you told her no."

"Heard fast, around here."

"Sable hears everything. Occupational hazard." The smuggler studied them. "She offered you the gift back, didn't she. That was the play. I'd have offered the same, in her position."

"How do you know?"

"Because I know what it is to stare at a price that would fix everything and cost everything." Sable's voice was quiet, stripped of its usual sardonic edge. "Fifteen years ago I sold a route to Vessane's people for enough coin to save my network. Two of the families I moved that season didn't make it out. I've been buying that back ever since, one refugee at a time, and I'll die owing." They shrugged. "So. You stared at it. What happened?"

Zephyrine looked out at the garden, at Fen below, nose down in the melting snow, following some scent with total, uncomplicated joy. They couldn't hear the wolf's mind anymore. They never would again. But they could read the set of the shoulders, the looseness of the tail, the way Fen kept circling back toward the balcony door every few minutes—not commanded, not bound. Just checking. Just making sure.

"I said no," Zephyrine said. "And it turns out it wasn't even hard. Not once I understood what she was actually selling."

"Which was?"

"Her freedom, priced in everyone else's." Zephyrine exhaled, breath fogging. "I spent my whole life believing the gift was what made me worth keeping. Turns out I was worth keeping anyway. That's the bargain I made instead. Cheaper than hers."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then: "The delegates start questioning her tomorrow. Marrowgate wants you as a witness—they asked by name. Aldric agreed." A pause. "You don't have to. Nobody would blame—"

"I'll testify," Zephyrine said. "As a witness. Not an accuser. Somebody has to say what she built and what it cost, and it should be someone who was offered the easy way out and knows exactly how heavy the hard one is."

Below, Fen lifted their head, found Zephyrine on the balcony, and held the gaze a long moment—amber eyes, steady, certain. Then the wolf turned and trotted for the garden door, unhurried, unmistakably heading up.

Coming to them. Choosing to.

Zephyrine watched, and waited, and discovered that the silence inside them had changed its shape sometime in the last day without their noticing. It was not an absence anymore. It was simply room—room for whatever came next, for testimony and rebuilding and a spring that was still weeks off but already, faintly, on its way.

The door opened behind them. Wet paws on stone. Warm weight against their leg.

"Alright," Zephyrine said softly, to the wolf, to the city, to the year turning over. "Let's go tell the truth."
