## 21. Silence and Song

The first morning without the voice, Zephyrine woke reaching for it the way a tongue reaches for a broken tooth.

They lay in the narrow bed they'd been given in the loyalist quarter—above a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and honey—and listened to the city wake. Gulls on the rooftops. A dog barking itself hoarse three streets over. Horses in the courtyard below, stamping against the cold.

All of it just sound now. No words folded inside the words. No *feed me*, no *the grey men are coming*, no small gossip of sparrows passed along the eaves like coins. Just noise, and Zephyrine alone inside their own skull for the first time since childhood.

They had known it would be like this. They had chosen it, strand by strand, pouring twenty-two years of gift into the unbinding until the last of it tore loose and went screaming out of them like a bird released from the hand. They had felt Aldric wake. They had heard—through Fen's howl, through the roar of the crowd—the moment Vessane's borrowed face came apart in front of ten thousand soldiers.

It had been worth it. They would do it again. They had told Vessane as much to her face, and meant it.

And still, in the grey hour before dawn, Zephyrine lay in the dark and grieved.

Not quietly, either. That was the strange part. For years they had done everything quietly—hidden magic, hidden fear, hidden even from themselves the shape of what they were. Now there was nothing left to hide and no strength left to hide it with, and so they wept like a child, face pressed into the rough blanket, hands curled around nothing, mourning a voice the way you mourn a person: not because it was good, but because it was *theirs*.

Fen was there before the second sob.

The wolf came up onto the bed—a thing no hound of any king should do—and folded himself around Zephyrine's shaking body, chin over their shoulder, breath warm against their ear. He made no sound. He didn't need to. His heartbeat was slow and enormous against their back, and his tail swept the blanket once, twice, patient as tide.

*I'm here*, said everything about him except words.

Zephyrine laughed through the tears, wet and cracked. "You see? I can still hear you."

Because that was the joke of it, the cruel bright joke: Fen had never needed the gift to speak. Not really. The gift had been translation, yes—but a translation only works when both sides are already trying. And Fen had been trying for years. The tilt of his ears. The set of his shoulders. The way he looked at doors when he wanted them opened and at Zephyrine's hands when he wanted them still.

Zephyrine knew all of it. Had always known it. The gift had simply been too loud to notice how much they already understood without it.

But knowing it at dawn, wrapped in a wolf's warmth, was not the same as believing it. Belief would take longer. Belief, Zephyrine suspected, would take the rest of this chapter of their life, and they were too tired to begin it before breakfast.

* * *

The city was still half-mad with celebration, which is to say: frightened, and pretending otherwise.

Vessane was in custody. Her elite guard had scattered or surrendered; her armies were still out there, leaderless but not harmless, and everyone in the capital seemed to understand that the hard part hadn't ended so much as changed shape. Banners of the true king hung from windows alongside the old free-city colors, sometimes on the same line, an argument in cloth. Children chalked wolves on doorsteps for luck. Someone had already written a ballad about the unbinding, and it was wrong in every particular except that a beast-speaker had saved the king, which was the only part anyone remembered.

Zephyrine kept their hood up and did the thing they knew how to do: work.

The granary cells had been emptied two days after the trial began. Forty-one gifted taken alive by Vessane's commissions, hidden beneath the wheat where no transcript would ever find them—children mostly, some of them so young they flinched from kindness. Zephyrine had been there when the doors opened. Had watched a boy of maybe eight walk out blinking into torchlight, a sparrow perched on his shoulder like a small brown conscience, and had felt the absence of the gift like a missing tooth one more time.

But the boy had looked at Zephyrine—at the hood, the tired eyes, the wolf at their side—and known anyway. Children like that always knew.

"Are you empty now?" he'd asked, with the terrible directness of the young.

"Yes," Zephyrine had said.

"Does it hurt?"

"Every day. Less each day. That's what people promise, anyway."

The boy had considered this with great seriousness. "My mam says the same thing about my da." Then he'd gone off toward the light, sparrow and all, and Zephyrine had stood in the cold wheat-dust and thought: *well. There it is. Grief has company everywhere.*

Now, a week on, the work was quieter. The freed children needed food that didn't come from requisition stores. The freed knights needed their wounds seen to. The city needed someone to sit with its animals while their owners stood in bread lines, and Zephyrine—giftless, useless by every measure that had ever mattered to the realm—turned out to be very good at it.

Because here was the thing they discovered slowly, then all at once, the way you discover a room you've lived in your whole life has a window you never opened:

They still understood.

Not the words. Never again the words. But the mare in the chandler's stable who swung her hindquarters away from her stall's corner—she'd been stabled somewhere cramped, somewhere she'd learned that corners were traps. The butcher's dog who carried his food away from the bowl before eating—he'd been fed in competition once, maybe for years. The old cat on the granary steps who sat facing the wall instead of the street: eyes failing, ashamed of it, hiding the weakness the way cats do, and the way people do.

Zephyrine saw all of it. Had always seen it. Twenty-two years of watching animals had built something in them deeper than magic—something the unbinding couldn't touch, because it had never been the gift at all. It had been attention. It had been love, worn daily, like a path worn through grass.

On the ninth morning, kneeling in the straw beside a carthorse gone lame, Zephyrine ran their hands down the swollen leg and read the whole story in it: the heat, the way the horse held the hoof, the flinch pattern that said *this started days ago and I worked through it because I was told to*. They bound it, poulticed it, talked to the carter—not *for* the horse, the way they used to, but *about* her, translating the old way, the human way, word to word.

"She'll need four days' rest. She's been favoring it since the frost. She kept working because you asked her to, and she loves you, and that's why she's lamed now."

The carter stared. "How'd you know all that?"

"I've been listening my whole life," Zephyrine said. "The gift was just a shortcut."

* * *

Sable found them at dusk on the granary steps, carrying two cups of something hot and alcoholic and sitting down without asking, which was how Sable did everything.

"You look terrible," Sable said.

"Thank you. You look like money that's been through a fire."

"A fair description of my finances." Sable blew steam off their cup. Below them, the city muttered and glowed. Somewhere a fiddle was playing the wrong version of the ballad, and Sable winced at it with professional distaste. "I heard what you're doing. The horses. The dogs. Half the stable-folk in this quarter think you're still gifted and working small. The other half think you're a fraud. Both halves keep paying you."

"Good. Let them argue forever."

"Mm." Sable drank. Was quiet for a moment, which for Sable was practically a speech. "I sold information for eleven years," they said at last. "Names, routes, faces. It's how I stayed alive, and it's how I paid for the ones I moved out of her reach—one betrayal funding three rescues, that was the arithmetic, and I slept fine on it. Mostly fine." A shrug. "When your face went up on those boards, I did the sums again. Your price would have rebuilt my network twice over."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I did the same sums. I'd have understood."

"I know you would have. That's the infuriating part." Sable turned the cup in their hands. "Here's what I figured out, though, standing in front of that bounty board doing arithmetic. Everything I ever bought with betrayals, I bought because I believed I was alone in the world and always would be. That's the whole ledger. Every entry comes down to that one line." They looked sideways at Zephyrine. "And then some fool healer from a valley that forgets things goes and breaks a king's curse and loses the only thing that made them special, and stands there afterward asking if I want tea. And I thought: oh. Oh, hell. It doesn't have to be the ledger anymore."

"Sable—"

"I'm staying," Sable said flatly, before Zephyrine could make it a moment. "Don't make it a moment. The city's going to need smugglers for years—half these freed children will need moving somewhere safe, and the new government will be useless and slow, and I am neither. So I'm staying, and you're going to help me, because you know where the hunted hide better than any spymaster alive, gift or no gift." They drained the cup and stood. "That's not sentiment. That's logistics."

"It sounded a little like sentiment."

"It sounded like logistics," Sable repeated, and stalked off down the steps, and paused at the bottom, and added, without turning around: "The tea was good. Make it again tomorrow."

Zephyrine smiled into their own cup until the steam blurred.

* * *

Winter loosened its grip by degrees, grudgingly, the way old kings give up thrones.

Zephyrine's grief did not vanish. Some mornings it arrived whole and heavy, especially when a raven croaked overhead saying nothing, meaning nothing, or when a child in the refugee houses spoke to a kitten in that wondering half-voice and Zephyrine's chest seized with a hunger that had no name except *again, I want it again.* They let it come. They had learned that much—from Fen, actually, who grieved his dead pack every full moon without shame and without being diminished by it.

But the mornings got lighter. And one raw afternoon in late winter, walking the river path with Fen ranging ahead, Zephyrine caught themselves reading the world fluently and without pain.

A heron stood in the shallows, and Zephyrine knew without words that it had fished unsuccessfully for an hour and was too stubborn to move. Two dogs met at the end of the lane, and Zephyrine watched the whole negotiation unfold—elder deferring, younger testing, the precise choreography of two animals deciding to be neighbors. Fen stopped at a fence rail where a farm cat sat, and the two of them regarded each other across a species line with the weary courtesy of old colleagues, and Zephyrine understood every syllable of it.

None of it required magic. All of it required them.

*Oh*, they thought, standing in the cold with their breath steaming. *Oh, it was never the gift.*

The gift had been a door. But they had built the house themselves, brick by brick, over twenty-two years of watching and loving and learning—and a locked door takes the view away, but the house remains. The understanding remained. The belonging—

The belonging had never come from the voice at all. The valley had never belonged to them because they could speak to its crows; they had belonged to it because they'd healed its lambs and sat with its dying and listened, truly listened, to everything in it. Hollowmere had rejected them out of fear. But the belonging itself had been real, and portable, and it had walked out of the valley with them and survived fire and purge and war and the loss of the gift itself, and here it was, standing on a river path in late winter, wearing a wolf and a smuggler and a lame carthorse and a sparrow-boy's blunt questions.

Fen looked back at them, ears up, waiting.

"Coming," Zephyrine said aloud, and their own voice—plain, human, theirs—sounded less like a loss than it had yesterday.

* * *

They found the summons waiting at the chandler's: heavy paper, the true royal seal, delivered by a knight who waited awkwardly in the doorway while Zephyrine read it twice.

His Majesty requests the presence of Zephyrine Thornbury at the palace, at the holder's earliest convenience. There is a matter His Majesty wishes to discuss in person. — It was signed in Aldric's own hand, and beneath the signature, smaller, almost shy: *No ceremony. Come as you are. Bring the wolf.*

Fen read the look on Zephyrine's face and stood, tail high, already halfway to the door.

"Well," Zephyrine said, folding the letter into their coat, heart beating quick and strange. "Let's go hear what a king wants with an empty vessel."

But some traitor part of them, quiet and warm, already suspected the answer—and suspected that whatever Aldric offered, it would feel less like a reward than like a door finally opening onto ground they'd been standing on all along.
