## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The night market of Vell opened at dusk the way a wound opens: slowly, reluctantly, and with a smell that lingered.

Juniper Vasquez had worked the corner of Tanner's Row and the Ash Steps for six years now, long enough to know which cobblestones wobbled when the frost came, long enough to know that the lamplighter on this stretch drank half his oil ration and lit his lamps with a prayer he didn't believe in — *if there's anyone left up there, mind the flame* — because it was habit, and habits outlive their reasons the way scars outlive their wounds.

They set out their wares in the blue hour, when the last gray light gave way to the first gray lanterns. Luck-knots, braided from tarred cord and river-reed, three coppers apiece. Mildew-charms: little clay tablets pressed with a spiral, baked in a borrowed oven, good for keeping the damp out of a cellar wall or the rot out of a boot sole. Salt-sachets against bad dreams. Knotted threads for safe childbirth, though Juniper always sold those at cost and never met the buyer's eyes.

None of it was magic. That was the whole point. That was what kept them alive.

"Charms," they called, voice worn smooth as river stone by seven years of saying it. "Charms for the house, charms for the road. Knots for luck, salt for sleep. Nothing here but cord and clay and honest work."

The *honest work* was the important part. The Inquisition's patrols liked to drift through the market on slow nights, and the phrase had become a kind of password, a small flag of surrender raised every few minutes. *Nothing here but cord and clay.* Juniper said it the way other people breathed.

Vell was a city that had learned to live without gods the way a body learns to live without a limb — badly, and with a permanent ache where something used to be. Seven years since the Sundering Edict. Seven years since the last choir of oath-bound speakers had been marched to the execution ground beyond the Wall of Chains and put down like dogs, one by one, while the city watched and the sky did nothing. Seven years of silence so total that most people under thirty no longer believed anything had ever answered prayers at all. They thought the old stories were comfort, not history. They thought the shrines were furniture.

Juniper knew better. Juniper had been there.

But knowing better was a thing you buried deep, under layers of cord and clay and honest work, under a name spoken flatly and a face kept forgettable, under the high-collared coat they wore even in summer because the scar on their throat must never, ever be seen.

The market filled around them. Fishwives arguing over eel-buckets. A knife-grinder whose wheel sang a note just shy of pleasant. Two soldiers off duty, spending coin they'd regret tomorrow. The smell of frying onions and tallow smoke and the river's low tide breathing its mineral breath through the streets. Vell at night was gray on gray, but it was alive, and alive was enough.

An old woman bought a mildew-charm and complained about her landlord. A dockhand bought two luck-knots and asked if they worked.

"As well as knots work," Juniper said. "You tie them, you mean them, sometimes the day goes easier. Can't promise more than that."

"Honest, anyway." He grinned, gap-toothed, and moved on.

Honest. Juniper turned the word over like a counterfeit coin. There was a time when they had promised much more than knots, and delivered. There was a time when their voice had been a blade and a bridge and a bell all at once, when they had spoken words of binding into the air and watched the air obey. Oath-bound speaker of the third choir, swordspeaker, sworn at nineteen with a brand pressed hot against their throat and a god's attention settling over them like a mantle of warm iron.

And then the Edict came, and the choice came, and Juniper had made it — the choice that let them stand here selling luck-knots while everyone who had stood beside them was ash on the wind.

They adjusted their collar. The scar beneath it ached, the way it always ached when the nights grew cold. Not pain, exactly. More like an absence with edges. Like a tooth pulled long ago that the tongue still went looking for.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the child came.

She couldn't have been more than six or seven, wrapped in a coat that had belonged to someone bigger, trailing an older brother who was already halfway distracted by the knife-grinder's wheel. She planted herself in front of Juniper's stall with the absolute gravity of the very young, and she stared at the luck-knots as though they might stare back.

"You sell wishes?" she asked.

"Knots," Juniper said gently. "For luck. You tie one around your wrist and think of something good, and maybe the something good finds you faster."

"That's a wish."

"It's a knot."

The girl considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. Then she dug in her pocket and produced a single copper button, a thread still hanging from it, and set it on the table with tremendous ceremony.

"I don't need luck," she announced. "I need my mama to stop coughing. She coughs all night now. Da says the physicker costs too much." Her chin came up, defiant, daring Juniper to make this small. "So I want to buy the one that works."

The market noise went on around them — the wheel, the fishwives, the frying onions — but for Juniper the sound seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. They looked at the button. At the girl's chapped hands. At the fierce wet shine in her eyes that meant she had already cried about this somewhere private and would not cry about it here.

Seven years ago, Juniper could have done something. Could have spoken a word of easing, laid two fingers on the child's wrist, called on powers that had names and faces and patience. Seven years ago, a prayer like this one — a child's plain, unadorned plea — would have gone up like a spark and come down like rain.

Now there was only cord and clay and honest work, and a copper button on a folding table, and the ache under the collar.

Juniper took the button. It was the right thing to do; refusing would break something in the girl that shouldn't be broken. They selected the finest luck-knot on the table, red-dyed reed shot through with a single strand of silver thread Juniper had added for no reason they could defend, and knelt to tie it around the small wrist.

"What do I do?" the girl whispered.

"You hold still. And if you want — and nobody's making you — you can say what you're hoping for. Out loud. Just once. Some people find it helps."

The girl nodded solemnly. She closed her eyes. She laced her fingers together the way children do when they've seen adults pray, even though no adult had prayed in her lifetime, even though the gesture should have died out with everything else.

"Mama," she said, "please stop coughing. Please. I'll be good. I'll eat the fish even when it's yucky. Please."

Her voice was small and clear and utterly without craft, and it rose into the smoky air of the night market like a thread rising off a spool, and Juniper braced themselves for the silence that always followed such things, the great indifferent nothing that had swallowed every prayer in Vell for seven years —

— and the bells rang.

Not the cathedral bells. Those had been melted down in the second year of the Edict, cast into cannon for a war nobody won. This was something else. This was a sound that arrived from no direction and every direction, a peal of small bright bells, silver and glass and frost, ringing once, twice, three times, each note hanging in the air like a held breath, each note landing on Juniper's skin like a hand on a shoulder after seven years alone.

The lamplight flickered. The knife-grinder's wheel fell silent mid-stroke. Down the row, a fishwife stopped with an eel half-lifted from its bucket, mouth open, staring at nothing.

And the girl opened her eyes and laughed — one bright, startled note of her own — and said, "It tickled."

Juniper could not move. Could not breathe. Every instinct honed over seven years of survival screamed at them to look down, to be small, to be cord and clay and nothing — but underneath the terror, underneath the cold flood of *no, no, this is impossible, this is forbidden*, something older and deeper had begun to sing.

Because Juniper recognized that sound.

Every speaker did. It was the sound of a god arriving — not one of the great ones, not a storm-lord or a death-king, but a small god, a hearth-god, a door-god, one of the ten thousand humble divinities that had once crowded the world like sparrows. The sound of something ancient turning its attention toward a mortal voice, and *answering*.

That could not happen. The Sundering Edict had not merely outlawed magic; it had severed the realms. The covenant was broken, the door between here and there nailed shut with law and iron and blood. The gods were not silent because they were sulking. They were silent because they were *gone*, unreachable, sundered, and the choir had died proving how final that severance was.

And yet.

The girl tugged the knot on her wrist. "Did it work? Did you hear it?"

"I heard it," Juniper said, and their voice came out strange, scraped thin.

"Was it Mama?"

"I don't know." Which was true, and also the most frightening sentence Juniper had spoken in seven years, because the honest answer was: *it doesn't matter what it was. What matters is that something answered. Something can still answer. The door isn't shut all the way, and I am standing right beside the crack.*

The girl's brother appeared, tugging her away, complaining about dinner. She waved over her shoulder with the knot-hand, silver thread catching the lamplight, and then she was gone into the crowd, and Juniper was alone at their stall with a copper button and a heart slamming against their ribs like something trying to get out.

They looked down at the button. It sat on the boards, ordinary, thread still attached.

Slowly — so slowly, the way you approach a sleeping animal, the way you approach your own grave — Juniper closed their hand around it.

Warmth. Faint, fading, but unmistakable. The button held warmth the way a stone holds sun after sunset, and threaded through the warmth, almost too faint to catch, was a residue of that bell-sound, a hum, a *listening*. As if the prayer hadn't dissipated into the air but had soaked into the object nearest to it, the way dye soaks into cloth.

As if it had soaked into *them*.

Under the high collar, against their throat, the scar woke.

It didn't hurt — not yet. But it stirred, the way a sleeper stirs before waking, a slow heat spreading across the old cut flesh, and Juniper's hand flew to their collar and pressed down hard as if they could hold it shut from the outside, hold down whatever was rising underneath, and they heard themselves make a small sound they hadn't made since the execution ground. Since the night they had stood in the crowd with their hood up and their oath torn out of them and listened to their fellows sing while the axes fell.

*No,* they thought, with the full desperate force of seven careful years. *No. I buried this. I cut it out myself. I am cord and clay and honest work. I am nobody. I am —*

The bells rang again.

Fainter this time. Farther. But not gone — and not only for the child anymore. Juniper felt it pass through them like a current through wire, felt the scar flare hot beneath their palm, felt — with a clarity that felt like falling — the shape of the answering presence on the other side of the sundered dark. Small. Fading. Straining toward the crack in the door the way a drowning swimmer strains toward light.

*Please,* said the presence, though it had no mouth, and the word was not aimed at the child, and it was not aimed at the mother, and Juniper understood with sick, vertiginous certainty exactly who it was aimed at.

*Please. Someone. Anyone. Hear me.*

Down the Row, a whistle blew — the Inquisition's night patrol, two notes, sharp, the signal for *gather*. Lanterns were converging near the far end of the market. Voices, low and quick. Someone else had heard the bells. Someone with a ledger and a brand-iron and orders written seven years ago that had never actually expired.

Juniper swept their wares into their satchel with hands that shook, dropped the copper button into the girl's abandoned spot — no, no, they couldn't leave it, evidence, warmth, *listening* — hesitated one terrible heartbeat, and pocketed it instead. They pulled their collar high. They pulled their hood higher. They became, with the practiced ease of long survival, exactly the sort of person no one remembers: medium height, medium build, medium gray coat, moving with the market's closing tide toward the dark end of Tanner's Row.

Behind them, the bells faded. Ahead, the whistles multiplied.

And beneath the collar, against the throat, warm as a living thing, the scar where their oath had been cut away began — softly, sweetly, terribly — to burn.
