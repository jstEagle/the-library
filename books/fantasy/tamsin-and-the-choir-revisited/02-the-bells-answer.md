## 2. The Bells Answer

The child's prayer hung in the air of the night market like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Juniper stood behind their stall with a luck-knot half-tied in their fingers and did not move. Around them, the market went on — the fishwife arguing about ice, the tinker winding copper into rings, the gray lanterns hissing their gray light — but Juniper heard none of it clearly. All of it had gone distant and underwater, because a voice made of bells was still fading somewhere above the awnings, above the rooftops, above the soot-colored sky of Vell, and it had said *yes*.

Gods did not say yes. Gods had not said anything in seven years.

The child — a girl with flour on her knuckles and her mother's shawl pinned wrong — looked up at the empty air with an expression Juniper knew too well to bear. It was the expression of someone who has been seen. It was the expression Juniper had worn at nine years old, kneeling in a choir-house in another city, when the oath first took hold of them and the world split open like a fruit.

"Did you hear that?" the girl whispered.

"No," Juniper said, too fast, too flat. "I didn't hear anything."

But their hands were shaking, and the luck-knot slipped from their fingers and unraveled on the table like something dying.

* * *

They should have packed up then. They knew it even as they stayed. Seven years of survival had built a whole architecture of instinct in them: when the wind changed, fold the stall; when a uniform appeared, smile and lower your eyes; when anything impossible happened, be elsewhere before it finished happening. The scar under their left sleeve — where the oath-brand had been cut out of them with a cold knife and a colder prayer — ached the way old wounds ache before storms, and Juniper had learned to trust that ache more than they trusted their own name.

Instead they watched the girl run back to her mother, and they listened.

Not with their ears. That was the horror of it. They listened with the place where the brand used to be, the hollowed-out place that had been silent for seven years, and the silence there was not silence anymore. It was full. It was full the way a room is full after someone has just left it — still warm, still shaped by a presence, every surface remembering.

*She asked for bread,* the hollow-place seemed to say. *Someone answered.*

Juniper tied off the stall's last knot without looking at it. Their fingers remembered the work; the rest of them was somewhere else, standing in a flooded chapel seven years ago, singing, while the knives came down.

* * *

By the time the moon cleared the smoke-haze over Vell, the whole city had begun to hear things.

It started small, the way plagues start. A candle-seller two stalls down swore her dead husband's voice had come out of the gutter-flame and told her where he'd hidden the rent money. A drunk by the fountain wept into his cups because the water had spoken his mother's name. Somewhere in the warren of lanes behind the fish-rows, a dog began to howl at nothing and would not stop, and other dogs took it up street by street until the sound circled the district like a noose.

And the shrines.

Vell was a godless city, but it had never managed to be a shrineless one. The little alcoves survived everywhere — in doorways, under stairs, wedged between chimney-pots — niches where people had once set out milk for the hearth-gods and coins for the door-gods, back before the Sundering Edict had made such offerings a fineable offense. For seven years those niches had held nothing but rainwater and cigarette ends and the occasional desperate flower left by someone who didn't care about fines.

Now they were lighting.

Juniper saw the first one from the mouth of Tanner's Lane: a doorway-shrine no bigger than a cupped hand, its stub of candle burning with a flame that burned *wrong* — too white, too steady, casting shadows that leaned toward it instead of away. An old man stood before it with his hat in his hands, and as Juniper passed, the flame bent toward him like a listening ear, and the old man made a sound Juniper had only heard once before, at an execution, from the crowd: the sound of a person watching something holy and knowing it would cost them.

More shrines. Every few streets, another. Candle-flames standing up straight as soldiers. Rainwater in a basin-shrine trembling in patterns too regular to be wind. A wall-niche where someone had left a chipped clay figure of a door-god, and the figure's painted eyes were wet.

And through all of it, threading under the city like a second river, the bells. Not any bell Juniper could locate — not the cathedral bell, which had been melted down in the first year, nor the harbor bell, which rusted silent — but a bell-sound inside everything, inside the cobbles and the drainpipes and the marrow of Juniper's own bones, a vast and scattered attention turning itself toward Vell like ten thousand faces lifting at once.

*They're answering,* the hollow place in Juniper's arm sang. *They're answering everything.*

"That's not possible," Juniper muttered, walking faster. "You're dead. All of you are dead. I watched you die."

The bells did not argue. That was somehow worse.

* * *

The trouble with answered prayers, Juniper thought, gripping the rail of the Harrow Bridge, is that nobody prays well anymore.

Seven years of silence had made the city's prayers feral. People had forgotten how to ask. They prayed the way drowning men grab — wildly, greedily, all at once, with no shape and no courtesy and no sense of what a thing might cost. And whatever was listening on the far side of the silence was answering everything, granting everything, wild and desperate and *wrong*, and Juniper could feel it going wrong the way you can feel a choir going flat: a wrongness in the harmony, a note that shouldn't exist.

Below the bridge, a woman had waded into the canal up to her waist, arms spread, laughing and sobbing, because she'd asked the water-god to take her grief and the water-god — or something wearing the water-god's drowned face — was *taking it*, pulling it out of her in long silver threads that dissolved before they hit the surface. She was laughing. She was also, Juniper realized with a lurch, forgetting. Even as they watched, her face went smooth and vacant, grief and memory dissolving together, because the prayer hadn't distinguished between them and neither, apparently, had the answer.

"Stop," Juniper whispered. "That's not — you have to ask *properly*, you have to bind it, there are *words*—"

Words. Oath-words. Choir-words. The architecture of asking, the covenant that had once stood between the realms like a load-bearing wall, the thing that made magic lawful instead of merely hungry.

All of it broken. All of it outlawed. All of it executed, singer by singer, on a cold morning seven years ago.

And now the wall was down and both realms were flooding into each other through the gap, and the gods were pouring through unshaped and starving, and every unanswered prayer of seven years was being answered at once, badly, by things that had forgotten the grammar of mercy.

Juniper's scar caught fire.

* * *

There was no gentler way to describe it. The hollow place under their left sleeve — the wound where the Inquisitors' cold knife had carved the oath-brand out of their skin, the price of renunciation, the receipt for their life — ignited. Not pain exactly, or not only pain. It was the feeling of a severed rope suddenly pulled taut from the other end. It was a hand closing around a wrist that had been empty for seven years.

Juniper dropped to one knee on the bridge and tore their sleeve back.

The scar was glowing.

Not the angry red of a reopened wound. It glowed like a coal breathed upon — deep gold at the center, ringed with white — and as Juniper stared, the glow moved, tracing lines that had been cut away, rebuilding the brand out of light alone. The swordspeaker's mark: a blade crossed by a staff, ringed by seven words in the old tongue, the covenant-script that every sworn member of the choir had carried seared into their arm like a signature written by godlight.

They had screamed when it was taken. They remembered that with terrible clarity — screaming while their fellows sang, screaming the renunciation the Inquisitors required, *I unsay my name, I unbind my oath, let the covenant pass me by* — and the words had worked, gods forgive them, the words had *worked*, and the brand had died on their skin and been cut out, and Juniper Vasquez had walked out of the execution yard alive while the others' ashes were still warm.

Now the brand was growing back.

Line by line, word by word, the light wrote it onto their arm as if the intervening seven years had been a clerical error being corrected. And with each stroke of that returning script, Juniper felt the bells louder — felt the whole ruined covenant ringing through them like a wire, felt themselves becoming a tuning fork for a catastrophe.

"No," they said aloud, to the sky, to the bells, to whatever was doing this. "*No.* Find someone else. I unsaid it. You heard me unsay it. Everyone heard."

The brand finished. The glow settled to a low ember-pulse, beating in time with their heart, and beneath the fear Juniper felt something else rise up through the panic like a body rising through dark water — something they had spent seven years drowning.

Duty. The specific, unbearable shape of it. The knowledge that a speaker who hears the realms tearing does not get to walk away, that the oath was never a job or a badge but a *post*, and posts do not care whether the soldier has resigned.

They had buried this. They had buried it deep, under charm-craft and haggling and the small gray commerce of getting through one more day. And now it stood up out of its grave, dusted itself off, and looked at them with their own eyes.

"You're dead," Juniper whispered again, pressing their palm hard against the burning brand as if they could smother it. "I'm dead. We're all dead. Leave the city alone."

Somewhere across the rooftops, a shrine-flare went up — a pillar of white fire visible over the chimneys, and then another, and another, divine attention kindling across Vell like a fever-map. The bells swelled. Under the bridge, the woman in the canal had stopped laughing and started calling for help in a voice that belonged to a much younger person, and Juniper understood with sick clarity that whatever had answered her was still there, still holding her, still taking.

*Someone has to ask properly,* the hollow place said, in a voice that was almost the Tamsin's, almost the old choir-master's, almost their own. *Someone has to speak.*

"Not me," Juniper said. "Anyone but me."

* * *

They smelled the Inquisitors before they saw them: cold iron and sanctified ash, the official incense of the Edict, a smell that had been present at every execution Juniper had ever witnessed. It cut through the canal-stink and the bell-song like a knife through silk.

They came up Tanner's Row six abreast, gray cloaks, iron masks stamped with the sun-and-bar of the Inquisition, staves of blackened steel that hummed with nulling-spells — the only magic the Edict permitted, magic turned against magic, the serpent licensed to eat its own tail. Behind them marched conscripted lamplighters with buckets of sand and salt, and behind *them* came the wagons, and the wagons meant arrests, and arrests tonight — with shrines igniting on every street and prayers tearing holes in the air — would mean something considerably worse than fines.

"By edict of the Sundering Concord," the lead Inquisitor called, voice flattened to a formal drone by the iron mask, "all manifestations are unlawful. All listeners are unlawful. Kneel where you stand. Do not answer what answers you."

The crowd on the bridge froze. The woman in the canal kept calling for her mother in a child's voice. The white flames in the shrines did not kneel.

Juniper got up off their knee and became, through seven years of practiced craft, nobody. Shoulders down. Eyes lowered. Charm-seller's slouch, charm-seller's dull face, the scar covered, the ember-beat of the brand pressed tight against their ribs where no iron mask could see it. They picked up their satchel of luck-knots and mildew-charms and joined the press of people drifting away from the bridge, one gray body among many, heart hammering a rhythm the brand beat right along with.

An Inquisitor paused beside them. Close enough that Juniper could see their own terrified reflection warped in the iron mask's curve.

"You," the mask said. "Charm-seller. Where is your stall?"

"Tanner's Lane," Juniper said. Hoarse. Dull. Perfect. "Luck-knots. Nothing unlawful. Knots and knots."

The masked head tilted. For one endless moment Juniper felt the nulling-staff's hum wash over them like cold water, probing, searching for the spark of oath-magic — and the brand, gods bless it or damn it, dimmed, folding its light down into some depth of them even Juniper couldn't reach, hiding the way a coal hides under ash. Hiding, or waiting.

"Move along," the Inquisitor said at last, and turned to the next person, and the next.

Juniper walked. One step, another. Off the bridge, into the lanes, away from the white fires and the bells and the woman in the canal whose voice was getting younger and younger. They did not run until the third corner, and then they ran — down stairways, through a tanner's yard, along the canal-path where the fog lay thick enough to drink — until the market and the masks and the burning shrines were all swallowed behind them.

Only then, crouched in the reeking dark under the aqueduct with their arms around their knees, did they let themselves feel it. Panic first, huge and animal. And underneath the panic, patient as water finding a crack, the other thing.

The pull.

*Someone has to ask properly.*

"There's no one left to ask," Juniper whispered to the dark. "You killed us. He killed us. It's done."

Under their sleeve, slow and inexorable as dawn, the brand pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a knock at a door Juniper had boarded shut seven years ago.

Like something on the other side, waiting to be let in.

And far off across the city, past the burning shrines and the iron masks and the canal where a woman was forgetting her own name, Juniper heard — or dreamed they heard, or were *made* to hear — a new sound beneath the bells. Fainter than the bells. Older than the bells. A voice like a chapel full of water, saying a word that might have been their name, or might have been the first syllable of a much longer sentence the world had been trying to finish for seven years.

Juniper put their head in their hands and began, very quietly, to laugh — because they already knew they were going to follow it.

They had always known.
