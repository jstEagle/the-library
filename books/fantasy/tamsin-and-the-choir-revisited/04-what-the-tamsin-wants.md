## 4. What the Tamsin Wants

The rain found Juniper before the chapel did.

It came down in gray sheets over the drowned quarter of Vell, where the canals had swallowed three streets whole and no one had bothered to reclaim them. The water was knee-deep and warm as breath, carrying the smell of silt and old stone and something sweeter underneath — incense, maybe, or the memory of it. Juniper waded with their hood up and their brand-scar wrapped in linen beneath their shirt, though wrapping it had done nothing. It burned like a coal sewn under the skin, pulsing in time with a rhythm they could not name.

*Left at the broken arch,* the voice of bells had said, when they'd fled the market with Inquisitor whistles shrilling behind them. *Down through the water. You know the way. You sang it once.*

They hadn't sung anything in seven years. But their feet remembered, turning left where the arch had crumbled into the canal, following streets that existed now only as rooftops and the suggestion of doorways beneath the flood. And that frightened Juniper more than the burning scar, more than the bells, more than the child's prayer answered out of empty air. Because their feet were not deciding on their own. Something was walking them.

The chapel rose out of the water like a drowning animal.

Juniper knew it — had known it, before. The Chapel of the Quiet Hand, a minor shrine to a minor god of mending, one of hundreds the Edict had emptied and the city had forgotten. Its bell tower leaned at an angle that defied collapse. Its doors stood open above the waterline, dark as a mouth.

"You should not have come," Juniper said to the doors, because talking to gods was a habit you apparently never lost, even after seven years of pretending you'd never had it.

The doors said nothing. But the water around Juniper's knees went suddenly, impossibly still — every ripple flattening at once, as if the whole flooded quarter had drawn a breath and held it.

Juniper went in anyway. That was the trouble with oaths, even cut-out ones. They left grooves in you, and things rolled along the grooves whether you wanted them to or not.

* * *

Inside, the chapel was flooded to the waist and lit from below.

That was the first wrongness: the light came *up*, pale gold rising through the water as if the drowned street itself had caught fire, illuminating the vaulted ceiling in wavering ribbons. Ranks of pews floated loose from their moorings, drifting in slow constellations. Along the walls, the old frescoes had begun to peel — saints and hearth-keepers and doorwardens, the small domestic pantheon of Vell, their faces blistered away by decades of damp.

And in the place where the altar should have been, there was a man made of candlelight and erosion.

He sat — or the shape of him sat — on the submerged altar-stone, and he was coming apart even as Juniper watched. His outline flickered like a flame in a draft. Where his hands rested on his knees, they were translucent, worn thin as communion paper. His face kept resolving and losing itself: young, old, neither, a face like a word half-remembered. Around his shoulders hung a mantle of tiny bells, and though the water lay silent all around, Juniper could *hear* them — faintly, distantly, the way you hear snow falling if you stand very still.

"Speaker," the god said. His voice was the voice of bells, but close now, and cracked down the middle. "You came."

"I didn't come," Juniper said. Their voice echoed strangely off the water. "I was dragged. There's a difference."

"There is always a difference." The god smiled, and the smile cost him; his edges frayed worse for it. "The difference is the whole of law, little speaker. Will and compulsion. That is what we are here to discuss."

"I'm not discussing anything with a god." Juniper stayed near the doors, water swirling around their thighs. "Gods are how I got this." They pulled aside their collar, baring the ruined patch of skin where the oath-brand had been cut away — and stopped, because the scar was glowing. Faint gold, pulsing in time with the god's guttering form. Matching him beat for beat, like two hearts learning each other's rhythm.

The god looked at the scar with an expression Juniper couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition. "Yes," he said softly. "I felt it wake. All of us did. Do you know what it means, when a severed oath ignites?"

"It means I'm going to die," Juniper said flatly. "That's usually what it meant before."

"It means the covenant knows your name again." He raised one dissolving hand, and the golden light in the water bent toward him, gathered, shaped itself into images: a bridge of light spanning a darkness too vast to be sky, figures walking it in both directions — human shapes crowned and robed, and shapes that were nothing human at all, vast and bright and strange. "Once, there was a road between your realm and ours. Not faith — *road*. Prayers traveled it like carts on a highway. Blessings came back the same way. We gave, you gave, and the giving was bound by oath so that neither side could take more than it offered. That binding was the covenant. It was older than any of the gods who walk now. Older than your oldest cities."

"And then the Edict—"

"The Edict was a wound," the god said. "Not the blade."

The images shifted. The bridge of light shivered — and broke, not crumbling but *cut*, sheared through its middle as if by a blade the size of a horizon. Both halves recoiled into the dark. The figures on either side scattered like startled birds.

"The covenant was shattered deliberately," the god said, "seven years ago. By hands on both sides of the road. Not to end magic — magic cannot be ended, only orphaned. It was shattered so that it could be *owned*. A covenant binds everyone equally. Its absence leaves a vacancy. And vacancies—" his flickering face turned toward Juniper, and for a moment it resolved completely: a tired, kind, ancient face, "—attract men who wish to fill them."

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. "Corvane."

"You know the name."

"Everyone in Vell knows the name. High Inquisitor Corvane, the Sword of Order, the man who wrote half the Edict's enforcement codes." Juniper laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "You're telling me the Sundering wasn't the Inquisition winning. It was the opening move."

"I am telling you that the gods are returning *wrong*, and you have seen it." The god's voice deepened; the water trembled with it. "A child prays for her mother's fever to break, and something answers — but the road is gone, so the answer comes ragged, unbound, wearing whatever shape desperation gives it. Shrines ignite with attention that has nowhere lawful to land. Small gods are waking from seven years of silence into a world with no rules, and some of them are waking *hungry*, because hunger is all that kept them alive down there in the dark. This is not a return, speaker. This is a symptom. The covenant is decaying, and everything on both sides of the grave is rotting with it."

"And when it finishes decaying?"

The god spread his translucent hands. Around him, the golden light in the water dimmed — visibly, measurably, like a lamp running out of oil. "Then silence. Not peace. Silence. No prayer answered ever again, in either realm. The road doesn't just decay, speaker. It takes the ground with it. Your world will forget how to hope out loud, and mine will forget how to exist."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled across the drowned quarter, and the floating pews knocked against one another like teeth.

"No," Juniper said.

The god tilted his head.

"No," Juniper said again, louder. "Whatever you're about to ask me — I already know what you're about to ask me, because it's written on my skin in a language I spent seven years bleeding out of. I'm not a speaker anymore. I'm a charm-seller. I sell luck-knots that don't work and mildew-charms that mostly do, and I keep my head down, and I am *good* at keeping my head down. Seven years good. Find someone else."

"There is no one else."

"There's always someone else. That's what choirs are *for*."

"Your choir is ash." The god said it without cruelty, which was somehow crueler. "Your fellows burned in the square at Vell while ten thousand people watched and applauded politely. The Edict saw to it that every oath-bound speaker in the realms was executed or renounced. You are the last, Juniper Vasquez. The last swordspeaker. The last living soul who carries the full covenant-oath in their blood — even cut away, even buried, even renounced. Especially renounced." He leaned forward, and his candlelight body guttered hard, and for one terrible moment Juniper saw through him to the altar-stone beneath, black and ancient and carved all over with script. "Do you understand? The covenant cannot be reforged by a god. We are parties to it, not its substance. It cannot be forged by the faithful — they have devotion but not oath. It requires a speaker. An oath-bound life, freely given, holding the whole of the law in one voice. And there is exactly one of those left in all the worlds, and she is standing waist-deep in my chapel telling me *no*."

The silence rang.

Juniper became aware, distantly, that they were shaking. Not with fear — or not only fear. With the awful vertigo of a door you'd nailed shut being opened from the other side.

"I renounced," they whispered. "You don't — I stood in the square and I said the words. *I forsake the choir, I forsake the oath, I forsake the road.* I said it while they—" Their throat closed. While they sang, they had almost said. While my brothers and sisters sang the Lamentation as the fires took them, I said the words that saved my life. "There's nothing left in me to give. You want a sword; someone melted mine down for plowshares. Go find another forge."

"Is that what you believe?" the god asked gently. "That the oath ended when you spoke against it?"

"What else would it have done?"

"An oath freely sworn is not undone by words spoken under the knife." The god rose from the altar-stone, and the water rose with him, and for a moment he was vast — filling the chapel, filling the drowned streets beyond it, a presence like weather. Then he contracted, folding himself back down to the size of a dying man, because he was kind enough to be small. "You survived, Juniper Vasquez. Whatever you told yourself to make the words come out, you survived, and the oath went with you into hiding, the way an ember goes into ash. It has waited seven years. It can wait a little longer while you decide."

"Decide what?"

"Whether survival was the end of your story," the god said, "or only the part where you learned what survival costs — and what it is *for*."

* * *

Juniper didn't answer. They turned for the doors instead, wading through the golden-dimmed water, and the god let them go — let them get one hand on the cold wood of the doorframe, let them stand there breathing the smell of rot and incense, let them look back at the drowned quarter through the doorway at the gray rain and the gray city and the gray life they had built out of the wreckage of themselves.

But the scar was still burning. And beneath the burn, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, was the rhythm of the bells.

"Why me?" Juniper asked the doorway, not turning around. "Really. If the covenant needs a speaker, why the one who ran? Why not raise up someone new, someone clean, someone who never stood in a square and forsook their own family while they burned?"

The god was quiet a long moment. When he answered, his voice had lost its grandeur entirely. It sounded tired. It sounded, absurdly, *grateful*.

"Because the covenant was broken by betrayal," he said. "Deliberately, and from within, and for reasons you do not yet know — reasons that will change what you believe about the night your choir died. It stands to reason it must be reforged the same way. Not by the pure. By the ones who know exactly what breaking costs, and choose the binding anyway." A pause. The water-light pulsed once, weakly. "Come back when the rain stops, speaker. Or don't. The oath in your blood will bring you regardless — it brought you tonight. But I would rather you walked in than washed up."

Juniper stood in the doorway until their knuckles ached against the wood.

Every instinct honed over seven years said *run*. Keep your head down. Sell your charms. Outlive it, the way you outlived everything else. And underneath that instinct, older and deeper, the groove worn into them by years of training and one unspeakable night: the pull of duty, patient as water, waiting for them to stop pretending they couldn't feel it.

"They'll send Inquisitors," Juniper said finally. "They're already sending them. Sable Wren, probably. She'll — " The name caught in their throat like a fishhook. *Former choir-sister. Current hound.* "If I do this, everyone who helps me burns. Everyone who hides me. Everyone who *prays* near me."

"Yes," the god said simply. "That is the shape of the work. I will not lie to you to make it easier. Lying is how we arrived at all of this."

Juniper closed their eyes. Behind their eyelids: the market, the child, the voice of bells answering a prayer that should have died unanswered in the godless dark. The look on the girl's face — not wonder. *Relief.* Like someone who'd been shouting into a well for seven years and finally heard the splash.

The rain drummed on the chapel roof. The scar burned its patient rhythm. And somewhere out in the drowned dark, distantly, a shrine-bell began to ring with no hand anywhere near it — wild, desperate, wrong — and Juniper understood with total clarity that the choice had already been made, had perhaps been made seven years ago in a square full of smoke, and all that remained was to say it aloud.

"Fine," they said, and their voice cracked on it, and they hated that, and said it again anyway. "*Fine.* Tell me where the pieces are."

Behind them, the dying god smiled — and outside, across the flooded quarter, every bell in Vell rang at once.

* * *

The first fragment, the Tamsin said, lay outside the walls, in a place the maps called the bone-orchard and the faithful had once called the Field of Answered Prayer. Juniper left the chapel before dawn, wading back through streets that seemed to remember their feet now, guiding them home.

They did not see the figure in gray standing on the far rooftop, ledger in hand, watching the chapel door with eyes like a winter sky.

But the bells did.
