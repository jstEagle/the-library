## 5. Seven Years of Silence

Sleep did not come to Juniper Vasquez anymore without an escort.

It arrived the way debt collectors arrived — politely at first, then all at once, dragging its chains through the door of their mind no matter how many bolts they had thrown against it. Seven years of practice had taught them every trick: the bitter teas, the exhaustion kept deliberately just shy of collapse, the small gray prayers to gods who could not hear. None of it worked on nights like this one, when the rain came down over Vell in long silver threads and sounded, against the tin roofs of the night market, exactly like a choir warming up.

They lay on their cot above the charm-stall with their boots still on, because a speaker who slept light learned to sleep dressed, and watched the water crawl down the window like slow glass. Below them, the city breathed. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse at nothing. Somewhere a drunk was singing three notes of a song that had been outlawed so thoroughly that even the melody had been confiscated, and Juniper lay very still and let the notes find the scar.

The scar sat where the oath-brand had been cut away: low on the left side of the throat, just beneath the jaw, a pale seam of tissue shaped like nothing at all now, though once it had been a sword laid flat against a circle of seven stars. The Inquisitors' surgeons had been careful. They were always careful. A botched excision killed the speaker outright, and dead speakers made martyrs, and martyrs made songs, and songs were precisely what the Sundering Edict had been written to end.

*We are not killing you,* the surgeon had said, his breath smelling of clove oil and something older, something like incense burned down to ash. *We are unmaking what you agreed to be. Try to understand the mercy in that.*

Juniper had understood. That was the worst of it. They had understood perfectly, and they had opened their mouth anyway, and the words that came out had been the only words in seven years they could never take back.

The rain thickened. The drunk below stopped singing. And sleep, having paid its toll, took Juniper down into the fire.

* * *

Seven years ago, the sky over Vell had been the color of a bruise going gold at the edges, and the whole city had been ordered into the great square to watch the choir die.

Juniper remembered it in pieces, the way a shattered mirror remembers a face — each fragment true, none of them whole. The smell of tallow torches, ten thousand of them, their smoke rising in a column so dense it looked like the sky had grown a black tree. The sound of the crowd, which was not a crowd-sound at all but a held breath, one enormous lungful drawn by forty thousand people and never released. The creak of the scaffold timbers under nine pairs of feet. Nine. There should have been ten.

There should have been ten, and the tenth pair of feet had been Juniper's, and they had not been on the scaffold, because two hours earlier Juniper Vasquez had stood in a cell that smelled of wet stone and clove oil and spoken the renunciation.

They had been given the words on a card. Real paper, real ink, the first real paper Juniper had been allowed to touch in four months of interrogation. The High Inquisitor's clerk had held it out through the bars with the delicacy of a man offering feed to a dangerous animal.

*Read it aloud,* he'd said. *Only that. Read it, and walk out.*

And the card had said: *I, Juniper Vasquez, swordspeaker of the Choir of the Covenant, do here renounce my oath, forsake my god, and swear silence upon my tongue for all the days of my life.* And beneath it, smaller, in the clerk's neat hand: *The Edict requires your voice, not your belief. No one will know. No one will care. The others will die whether you speak or not.*

That last line was the knife of it. Not a threat. An arithmetic. The others would die whether Juniper spoke or not; the Edict had already been signed, the pyres already stacked, the Inquisitors already bored with the paperwork of mercy. Juniper's renunciation would save no one. It would only save Juniper, and everyone in Vell knew it, and that was the design — not to break the choir, but to make certain that whatever survived the choir would be something the city could despise.

Juniper had held the card for a long time. Their hands had not shaken. They remembered being surprised by that — remembering, absurdly, their mother's hands shaking over a bill she couldn't pay, and thinking: *so that's what courage looks like from the inside, it looks like nothing, it looks like a person standing very still in a wet stone room.*

Then they had thought of the Tamsin.

Not by name — names had been forbidden even then, in the last weeks, the Edict's shadow falling forward across everything. But the god they served had always been a small one, a fading one, a god of thresholds and half-lit places, of the moment between stepping off a ledge and landing. The Tamsin had never asked for glory. It had asked, in its bell-voice that was more felt than heard, for one thing only: *Keep the door open. Whatever comes. Keep the door open.*

And Juniper had looked at the card, and at the clerk's patient face, and had understood — with the terrible clarity that comes only at the moments we least want it — that a closed mouth was also a kind of door.

They had read the card aloud.

Their voice had come out steady. That was the second surprise. Four months of interrogation had stripped everything else away — the muscle, the certainty, the easy laughter that Sable used to say sounded like coins dropped in a dry well — but the voice remained, trained by ten years of choir-discipline to carry, to enunciate, to mean what it said. So when Juniper renounced their god, they renounced him *beautifully*. Every vowel clean. Every consonant placed like a stone in a wall. The clerk had actually smiled, as if craftsmanship in betrayal were somehow a comfort.

The brand had come out of their throat like a hook coming out of a fish. They had felt it go — not pain exactly, though there was pain underneath, but a *removal*, a subtraction, the sensation of a word being deleted from the middle of a sentence they were still speaking. The surgeon's blade had followed, cutting the dead mark free, and Juniper had bled into a basin and been bandaged and fed broth, because the Edict wanted its one living witness healthy.

Healthy enough, the clerk had said, to attend the execution.

* * *

They had put Juniper in the front row.

Of course they had. Corvane himself — not High Inquisitor yet, then, only *Inquisitor Corvane*, though he already wore authority the way other men wore armor, fitted close and polished bright — had arranged it personally. Juniper remembered his face in the torchlight, remember thinking it was a strange face for an Inquisitor: too calm, too interested, the face of a scholar watching an experiment conclude rather than a zealot watching heresy burn.

*You will watch,* Corvane had told them, in the antechamber, while the smoke of the torches crept under the door like something alive. *That is the price of your voice. You will stand where you can see every face, and you will remember, and when anyone asks you — in a year, in twenty years, when the old men get sentimental — you will tell them what happens to those who bind themselves to the old gods. You are not our prisoner, Speaker Vasquez. You are our lesson.*

*I'm not a speaker anymore,* Juniper had said, and the title in their own mouth had tasted like blood and river water.

*No,* Corvane had agreed pleasantly. *You're a survivor. Those are far more useful.*

And then the doors had opened, and the crowd's held breath had washed over Juniper like cold tide, and they had walked to the front row on legs that belonged to someone else.

Nine of them on the scaffold. Nine of them in the white robes of the covenant, which the Inquisitors had left unstained — Corvane's order again, Juniper understood later; he wanted the deaths clean, legible, a sentence with no smudged letters. Maerlin the bellspeaker, oldest of them, whose voice could crack frost off a lake in high summer. The twins, Ossa and Ellery, who spoke in harmony so perfect that listeners swore they heard a third voice. Brother Candless, who had joined the choir at fifty after burying a wife and six children and who prayed, always, in the voice of a man apologizing for taking up space. And Sable.

Sable Wren, twenty-two years old, standing at the scaffold's edge with her chin up and her eyes moving across the crowd — across the crowd, not over it — searching. Juniper had known, with a certainty that has never faded in seven years, exactly whom she was looking for. She found them. Of course she found them; she had always been able to find Juniper in any room, any crowd, any dark. Their eyes met across forty feet of torchlight and forty thousand silent people, and Sable Wren did not look away, and she did not curse them, and she did not weep.

She nodded. Once. Small. The way you nod to someone across a sickroom. *I see you. I know what this is costing. Hold on.*

And Juniper had wanted to scream. That was the thing no chronicle would ever record, the thing that lived in them like a swallowed coal: in that moment, offered absolution by the sister they had betrayed before the betrayal was even finished, they had wanted to tear their own throat open on the scaffold nails and shout the truth up at the burning sky — *I didn't have to, I chose it, I read the card, I read it beautifully* — and the wanting had been worse than any torture the Inquisitors had devised, because the Inquisitors had only ever wanted their body broken, and this wanted their soul to lie about itself.

Then Maerlin began to sing.

* * *

They were not supposed to be able to. Everyone knew it. The Edict's binding-collars sat on every throat on that scaffold, gray iron bands etched with negation-sigils, and the Inquisitors had tested them twice, and the collars did not fail. Magic did not fail. What happened that night was not magic failing.

What happened was that nine people who had spent their lives learning to speak to gods decided, in their last minutes, to speak past the collars entirely — to speak *through* the dying, which is the one door no law has ever managed to lock. Maerlin started it. She simply opened her mouth and sang the dawn-hymn, the one that begins *Light, be lawful; law, be light*, and no sound came out of her, and the collar glowed dull red, and she kept singing anyway, silently, her lips shaping each syllable with the precision of a woman tuning an instrument only she could hear.

And the gods heard.

Juniper felt it before they understood it — a pressure behind the sternum, a ringing in the teeth, the particular hum of a threshold straining wide. All across the square, ten thousand torches leaned eastward in a wind that did not exist. The smoke-column bent. The bruised sky went gold at the edges, then white, then a color Juniper had no name for then and has never found one for since, a color like the inside of a promise. And out of that color, faint and vast and everywhere at once, came the answering voices — not one god but the whole scattered parliament of them, the hearth-gods and the road-gods and the drowned saints of the river, all of them singing back to nine bound throats on a scaffold, a call and response seven centuries old, performed one final time with the entire sky for a choir-loft.

The crowd broke. Not into panic — into something stranger, something Juniper has spent seven years refusing to name because naming it would make it true: the crowd broke into *longing*. Forty thousand godless people suddenly feeling, through their skins, what they had outlawed. Grown men fell to their knees in the mud. Women lifted children toward the light. An old soldier near Juniper began, helplessly, to sing along, and his voice cracked on the second line because he had not sung since boyhood and the hymn had changed key in the intervening decades, and his cracked wrong note was the most honest sound in the whole square.

On the scaffold, the nine sang on, silently, and the collars burned, and the fire beneath them caught.

Juniper had been told to watch. They watched. They watched Maerlin's robe catch and saw her keep time with her hands. They watched the twins turn their heads toward each other in the flames, foreheads touching, holding their harmony to the last. They watched Brother Candless — apologetic, gentle Candless — lift his face into the fire with an expression of such naked relief that Juniper's knees went out from under them, and they knelt in the mud of the square, in full view of Corvane and the clerks and the forty thousand, and no one moved to raise them, because everyone in that square was kneeling in some way or another.

And through it all, under the vast singing of the gods, Juniper heard — or dreamed they heard, and has never decided which would be worse — one voice beneath the rest. Small. Fading. A bell rung in an empty house.

*Keep the door open,* it said. Or perhaps: *you closed it.* The acoustics of shame are unreliable. It may have said both.

The light lasted eleven minutes. Juniper counted. Counting was the only prayer left to them.

When it ended, the sky closed like a book, the torches righted themselves, the collars on nine dead throats cooled to ordinary iron, and Inquisitor Corvane turned from the ashes with his calm scholar's face and looked directly down at Juniper kneeling in the mud, and inclined his head — slightly, courteously, the way one acknowledges a colleague after a successful demonstration.

*Remember it well,* his expression said. *You are our lesson now.*

* * *

Juniper woke on the cot above the charm-stall with the taste of smoke in their mouth and their hand pressed flat against the scar on their throat, hard enough to hurt.

Rain still threaded the window. The drunk below had gone quiet. Dawn was a gray suggestion beyond the rooftops of Vell, and the city was beginning its godless morning — shutters banging, carts groaning, the whole gray engine of the place grinding awake without a single prayer to grease it.

Seven years. Seven years of luck-knots and mildew-charms, of keeping the head down and the voice soft and the scar hidden under a high collar, of telling themselves the story that survival was its own penance, that living quietly was a kind of service to the dead. Seven years, and the coal still sat where it had always sat, behind the sternum, glowing.

They sat up in the dark and spoke to the empty room, barely voicing it, the way they did every morning, the ritual no collar required:

"I'll keep it open."

It was a lie of course, or half of one — the door was closed, the gods were gone, the words were habit and nothing more. Habit and ash.

Except that this morning, for the first time in seven years, as the syllables left their mouth, the scar on Juniper's throat grew warm.

Warm — and faintly, impossibly, sweetly — ringing. Like a bell waking in an empty house. Like something far away, in a flooded chapel at the edge of the gray city, turning over in its long death and whispering a name it had not dared use since the fire.

Juniper sat very still, hand at their throat, heart slamming, and listened to a dead god begin, softly, to call them home.
