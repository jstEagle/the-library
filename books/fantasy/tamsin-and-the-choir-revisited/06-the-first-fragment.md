## 6. The First Fragment

The Tamsin woke Juniper before dawn by ringing a single bell inside their skull.

Not painful — that was the strange part. It was the sound of a bell in an empty room at the far end of a long house, heard through three closed doors: soft, round, patient. But it went through them like a fishhook through water, and they came up out of their bedroll in the flooded chapel's vestry with their hand already on the hilt of a sword they did not own.

"You could knock," Juniper said to the dark.

*I have been knocking for seven years,* said the god, from everywhere and nowhere, from the standing water and the cracked saints and the one unbroken bell hanging crooked in the tower above them. *You are a heavy door.*

Juniper sat on the edge of the vestry bench and let the cold of the stone floor climb into them. Three days since the market. Three days since the brand-scar on their forearm had lit up like a coal breathed upon, since they had run through Vell's gray alleys with Inquisitor lanterns swinging behind them and something older than lanterns pulling them forward. Three days of sleeping in the chapel where the Tamsin pooled like rainwater in a cellar, fading, flickering, insisting.

"Tell me again," Juniper said. "Slowly this time."

*There is a covenant,* the Tamsin said, with the weariness of a thing that has explained itself to no one for centuries and is now explaining itself to someone who keeps asking. *It is broken. You will help me mend it. The first piece lies outside the walls, north by northeast, where the road to Harrow Fen used to run.*

"And what's there?"

*A garden.*

"Gardens," Juniper said, "are not usually why I wake up before dawn with my heart going like a drum-line."

*This one is.* A pause, and when the voice came again it had gentled, the way a big animal goes gentle when it scents fear on you. *It is an orchard now, Juniper Vasquez. It grows bones. And something walks in it that used to be worshipped, and is now only hungry, and does not know the difference.*

---

They left Vell through the leper's gate, because the leper's gate had no wardens and the lepers themselves had long ago stopped believing anyone would come to hurt them or help them, which made them excellent at minding their own business. An old woman with a face like crumpled parchment watched Juniper squeeze through the gap in the wall and said nothing, but she touched her forehead, then her breastbone, then her lips — the old sign, the sign of the listening gods, outlawed so long that most people had forgotten it meant anything except bad luck.

Juniper touched their own brand-scar in answer before they could stop themselves.

The land beyond the walls was the land of the Sundering made visible. Seven years ago the Edict hadn't just outlawed magic; it had *unmade the roads to it*, and everything that depended on those roads had begun, slowly, to die of thirst. Fields grew gray crops that fed no one well. Rivers ran the color of dishwater. And along the old pilgrim ways, where prayers had once traveled like carts on rails toward the listening places, the ground had gone strangely quiet — no birdsong, no insect-hum, just wind moving through grass that didn't rustle right.

The Tamsin walked beside them, or rather *arrived* beside them, over and over, a step ahead each time: sometimes a tall figure in a bell-crowned hat, sometimes a shimmer in the air like heat off a forge, sometimes only a pressure at Juniper's shoulder, warm as a hand. Its edges were wrong. When Juniper looked directly at it, pieces were missing — not hidden, *missing*, eaten away, like a page left out in weather.

"You're smaller than you were," Juniper said, somewhere past the second milestone. "In the chapel you filled the whole nave."

*I am spending myself to walk with you.* The god said it lightly, the way you'd mention lending someone a coat. *Every mile costs. Consider it an investment.*

"In what?"

*In the last speaker of swords learning to speak again.* It turned its unfinished face toward them. *You have not drawn your blade once since we left the walls. I can hear it wanting you.*

"I don't have a blade."

*You have the sword your oath bound to you, the night you knelt in the choir-house and gave it your name. Oaths do not die when they are cut away, Juniper. They go underground. They grow back.*

Juniper's hand found the scar on their forearm without permission. Under the wrapped linen, under the skin, under seven years of careful silence, something turned over in its sleep.

"I renounced it," they said. "I said the words. I stood in front of the whole choir-house and I said *I release and am released*—"

*And the words were true, and the cutting was real, and you bled properly, and still.* The Tamsin's voice was very kind and very terrible. *A swordspeaker's oath is not a contract. It is a seed. Ask the orchard, when we arrive. It knows all about things that keep growing after they are cut down.*

---

They smelled the orchard before they saw it.

Not rot — Juniper had expected rot, had braced for it — but something worse: *sweetness*. A thick, cloying perfume like overripe pears left in a hot room, like incense burned past its purpose, like flowers arranged on a coffin. It rolled down the slope ahead of them in visible waves, and the gray grass bent away from it as if in deference or disgust.

The orchard stood in a shallow valley where the Harrow Fen road had been. Rows of trees, straight as soldiers, white as bone — because they were bone. Femurs for trunks, rib-bones arching into branches, finger-bones clustered at the tips like blossom. They had been growing a long time. Some of them were taller than the chapel tower in Vell, and their branches creaked against each other in the windless air with a sound like teeth.

Between the rows, half-buried in pale soil, lay skulls. Thousands. All facing up. All facing the sky that had stopped answering them.

"The executed," Juniper said. Their voice came out flat, which was how they knew how loud the feeling underneath actually was. "The ones the Edict killed. Speakers, hedge-witches, shrine-keepers. Everyone who wouldn't recant."

*Everyone who would not, or could not,* the Tamsin said. *The Inquisition brought them here in the early years, when the killing was too much even for the city to hold. They planted them in rows because order comforts the men who do such work. And the ground here remembered being holy — this was a pilgrimage orchard once, real trees, real fruit, offerings hung in the branches. Holy ground remembers. Holy ground takes what it is given and grows it.*

"So the trees are—"

*Prayers. Grown into bone. Every skull in this valley screamed a prayer at the sky while the axe fell, and the ground drank every one, and this is what it made.* The god's voice had gone very quiet. *Do you understand now why the covenant must be reforged? This is what a world looks like when the line between realms is cut. The prayers do not stop. They only stop going anywhere.*

Juniper stood at the edge of the first row and felt the hair rise on their arms. Because the Tamsin was right, and worse than right: Juniper could *hear* the trees. Faintly, the way you hear the sea in a shell. A vast, slow murmur, thousands of voices braided together, saying the same few words over and over in different mouths:

*Hear me. Hear me. Hear me. Please.*

"Where's the fragment?" they asked, to make the hearing stop.

*At the center. Where the oldest tree stands. And between you and it stands its keeper.* The Tamsin's form guttered, steadied. *I cannot enter the orchard, Juniper. Hollowed ground rejects what remains of me — the keeper would smell my divinity and wake fully, and I do not have the strength left to fight it. This part you do alone.*

"Of course I do." Juniper checked the knife at their belt — ordinary steel, honest, useless — and laughed, short and bitter. "Alone, unarmed, against a hungry dead god. Any other advice?"

*Yes.* The Tamsin leaned close, and its breath smelled, impossibly, of candle smoke and morning. *When it comes for you, reach behind your heart and pull.*

---

The center of the orchard announced itself by silence.

The murmuring of the bone-trees faded row by row until Juniper walked in a pocket of quiet so complete they could hear their own blood. And there stood the oldest tree — vast, silver-white, its trunk wide as a chapel door, its branches spread like arms flung open in surrender or welcome. And set into its trunk, grown over but not swallowed, was a shape that caught the gray light and threw it back gold.

A shard. Long as a forearm, thin as a promise, glowing faintly from within like a lamp behind horn. Even from thirty feet away Juniper felt it the way you feel a struck tuning fork in your own bones: *rightness*. A piece of the covenant, humming the first note of a song everything else had forgotten.

Juniper took one step toward it.

The ground moved.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the horror of it. The skulls between the rows simply rotated, all together, all at once, tracking Juniper like eyes. And from beneath the oldest tree the soil heaved and folded open, and the keeper rose out of it the way a drowned body rises: slowly, reluctantly, enormously.

It had been a god once. Juniper could see the architecture of worship still in it — the suggestion of a crown, the sweep of robes rendered in grave-dirt and bone-fragments, the enormous calm of its proportions. But the worship was gone, and what remained was a hollow: a man-shaped absence twenty feet tall, filled with orchard soil and the packed bones of small animals and, here and there, glinting fragments of votive offerings — tiny copper bells, beads, a child's clay whistle — the last gifts of the last believers, worn like shrapnel in its flesh.

Its face was a skull with a hole where the prayer should come out.

It looked at Juniper. The whole orchard looked at Juniper. And the hollow god opened its mouth, and instead of a voice there came a sound like wind through a rib cage, and Juniper understood it anyway, the way you understand hunger in any language:

*Mine. The bright thing is mine. I kept it. I kept it. No one came, and I kept it.*

"It doesn't belong to you," Juniper said, and their voice shook, and they said it anyway. "Nothing belongs to you anymore. You're empty. Let me take it and rest."

The god tilted its head, and the gesture was so human, so *plaintive*, that Juniper's throat closed. Then it moved.

For something built of dirt and bone it was fast — horrifyingly fast, faster than grief, faster than thought. One enormous hand swept down and Juniper threw themselves sideways, and the impact where they'd been standing cratered the soil and rang like a struck bell through the whole valley. Skulls jumped in their furrows. Juniper rolled, came up running, ducked under a backhand blow that sheared a branch off a bone-tree and sent finger-bones raining like hail.

*Reach behind your heart,* the Tamsin had said.

Juniper ran between the rows, lungs burning, the sweet rot-smell thick enough to chew, and reached.

There was nothing there. Of course there was nothing there. There was a scar and a lie and seven years of—

The hollow god's fist came down through the tree beside them, exploding bone into shrapnel, and a splinter the length of a knife opened a burning line across Juniper's shoulder, and the pain cracked something loose, some door held shut by habit and shame, and Juniper stopped running and stopped thinking and *reached*, the way you reach for a name on the tip of your tongue, the way you reach for a note in a song you sang as a child —

— and the oath was there.

It had always been there. Underground, growing. It came up through their arm like sap through a branch, like fire through a fuse, and it *hurt*, God, it hurt, seven years of denial tearing out in a single motion, and Juniper's hand closed around a hilt that had not existed a moment before and drew a blade made of nothing but spoken intent, silver-white, singing, *the sword that answers when spoken to* — and it was already speaking, it had been waiting seven years to speak, and its voice was Juniper's own voice, younger, unbroken, the voice from the night of the kneeling:

*"Say the word."*

"**Stand,**" Juniper said.

The world changed.

Later, Juniper would never be able to describe it properly — the way the old power woke, the way it felt like the first breath after nearly drowning, like sunrise after a week underground, like every good thing at once and all of it edged in terror. The sword's light poured down their arm and lit the orchard from within, and every skull in every furrow turned toward them, and the murmur of the dead prayers shifted key — from *hear me, hear me* to something else, something wondering and hopeful and unbearable:

*Oh. Oh, it's one of them. One of them came back.*

The hollow god recoiled, hissing like water on iron, and then it attacked in earnest, and the fight became a blur Juniper's body remembered better than their mind did. Their feet found the old forms. Their wrists turned in patterns their hands had learned kneeling in the choir-house at fourteen. The sword spoke only when spoken to — *name the strike and it becomes true* — and Juniper named them, gasping, laughing, crying, all three:

"*Cut.*" The blade took the god's reaching hand off at the wrist and the hand burst into drifting dust.

"*Root.*" A thrust into the ground and a wave of light raced outward, and the skulls sank an inch deeper into their furrows, resting, resting.

"*Open.*" The final blow, both hands, everything Juniper had, driven up into the hollow chest — and the god did not scream. It sighed. Twenty feet of emptiness folded down around the blade like a coat sliding off a shoulder, and the soil spilled out, and the little copper bells and beads and the child's clay whistle settled gently onto the earth, and the wind that had been trapped inside it went free, and the orchard's murmur softened into something almost like sleep.

Juniper stood in the settling dust with a sword of living oath in their hands and their whole body shaking, exhilarated and frightened in equal measure, more alive than they had been in seven years and grieving it, because being alive like this was exactly what they had traded away to survive.

"Oh no," they whispered. "Oh, I missed it. I *missed* it."

*Yes,* said the Tamsin softly, from the edge of the valley, where it waited like a parent at a schoolyard fence. *That is the terrible part. Now you know what you gave up. Come. Take the fragment. We have four more sorrows to gather, and the sky over Vell is starting to bruise.*

Juniper walked to the oldest tree. Up close, they could see faces in its grain — not carved, *grown*, serene, asleep. They laid their palm against the trunk, and the wood was warm.

"I'm sorry," they told it, meaning every skull in the valley, meaning the hollow god, meaning everyone. "I'm sorry we left you."

Then they gripped the golden shard where it stood proud of the bark, and pulled.

It came free with a sound like a word being finished, and light rolled across the orchard, and every bone-tree blossomed at once — brief, impossible, white flowers opening along ribs and femurs, blooming and falling in the space of a breath, the orchard's one and only spring. Then the light faded, the petals became motes, the valley went gray and quiet and at peace.

And far to the south, back toward Vell, a bell began to ring that Juniper recognized with a lurch of cold in the stomach: the great bell of the Inquisition's cathedral, tolling not the hour but the alarm.

Somewhere in the gray city, someone had finally put a name to the charm-seller who fled the night market.

Somewhere in the gray city, Sable Wren was reading it.
