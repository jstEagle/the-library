## 7. Wanted: Speaker of Swords

The posters went up between one bell and the next.

Sable Wren saw the first of them nailed to the customs house at Graywater Gate, still wet with paste, the ink bleeding at the edges as if the words themselves were trying to escape. A face looked out from the paper — a face she knew better than her own reflection, drawn by some clerk's hand that had gotten the jaw too soft and the eyes too kind. Beneath it, in letters tall enough to read from horseback:

**WANTED: SPEAKER OF SWORDS. HERETIC. OATHBREAKER REVERSED.**

*Reward: One hundred crowns. Report to any Inquisitor. Do not approach.*

She stood in the gray morning drizzle and read it three times, and each time the name beneath the face struck her like a fist she had been expecting and had failed to brace against.

*Juniper Vasquez.*

Not crossed out. Never crossed out. Seven years in her ledger, inked in the old choir-hand they'd all learned together, waiting for an execution that had never come — because Juniper had renounced, had recanted, had spoken the words of severance while their brothers and sisters sang. Sable had written the name herself, the night after the executions, when the ledger was passed to her shaking hands and someone said, *record them all, even the coward.* She had recorded it. She had never been able to strike it through.

Now the city would do the striking for her.

By midmorning there were posters on every pillar of the Processional, pasted over the tax notices and the grain tariffs and the proclamations about chimney-sweep licensing, until Vell wore its new law like a rash. By noon the criers had it: *Speaker of swords, seen in the night market, marked by a brand on the left forearm, dangerous, do not listen to anything it says.* By afternoon the word *it* had done its work. You could hear it changing hands in the taverns and the bathhouses and the queue outside the bread-ration office — not *them*, not *that charm-seller from the market*, but *it*, the way you'd speak of a rat in the walls or a fire in a granary.

Sable walked the Processional in her gray coat with the silver clasp and let them look at her. They always looked. The coat did that. And under their looking she heard what they wanted from her, the question they didn't dare ask an Inquisitor: *will you catch it? will you make us safe?*

At the fourth poster she stopped. This one had been defaced — someone had drawn a small, careful bell above the printed face, the kind of bell that hung in the old shrines before the Edict melted them down. Someone had loved this heretic, or feared for them, or simply remembered what answered prayers used to sound like. An Inquisitor's apprentice was already climbing a ladder to tear it down.

"Leave it," Sable said.

The boy froze, paste brush dripping. "Inquisitor?"

"The wind will take it soon enough." It was a lie and they both knew it; the paste would hold for weeks. But the boy climbed down, grateful for any order at all, and Sable walked on with the defaced poster burning a hole in the back of her attention like a coal carried in cupped hands.

* * *

The summons came at dusk, delivered by a novice who wouldn't meet her eyes.

High Inquisitor Corvane received her in the map room at the top of the cathedral spire, where the whole of Vell lay spread below like a circuit diagram of itself — lamplight marking the arteries, darkness pooling in the districts where the poor had learned to bank their fires. Corvane stood with his back to the window, so that the city glowed behind him like something he had already swallowed.

"You've read the broadsheets," he said. No greeting. He never wasted breath on greetings; he considered them a form of flattery, and flattery a form of theft.

"I've read them."

"Then you know the Speaker has been named."

"I know a face has been printed on paper." Sable kept her voice level. "Paper doesn't prove anything. Half the city saw bells answer a child last week. If we're hunting everyone the miracles touched, we'll need a bigger prison than the Gray Cathedral."

Corvane turned from the window. He was not a large man, but he occupied space the way frost occupies a window — completely, and without effort. "The brand on the captured charm-seller's arm," he said, "was identified by three witnesses from the old choir registry. The scar matches the severance record. The market-stall records match the name Vasquez." He paused, letting each fact land like a coin counted onto a table. "And you interviewed the prisoner yourself, Wren. At dawn. On the first day."

"I did."

"And released them."

"I released a frightened charm-peddler with a scar," Sable said. "Scars are common. Guilt is rarer. I prefer to be sure of the difference."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not anger. Interest. That was worse. "You hesitated," he said softly. "My report says you hesitated."

"Your report—"

"My reports are thorough." He came around the table, unhurried, and laid one hand flat upon the great map of Vell, covering the night-market district entirely, as if he could erase it by touch. "I am going to give you something I give no one else, Wren. I am going to give you trust. The Speaker of Swords is yours. Your warrant, your hunt, your hand on the rope. No other Inquisitor touches the case. Whatever you find—" his fingers pressed down, whitening at the knuckle, "—you bring me. Alive, if the covenant fragments require it. Dead, if mercy moves you. Either way, you bring me proof that your hesitation was diligence and not disease."

*Disease.* The old word. Heresy-by-infection, the thing they burned junior clerics for suspecting in themselves. Sable felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath her boots, though the room was still.

"And if I refuse the commission?"

Corvane smiled. It was the worst thing she had ever seen him do, because it was patient. "Then I will know exactly what your hesitation was," he said, "and I will write it in a ledger of my own."

Outside the window, the city glittered on, ignorant, lamplit, full of people pasting up faces and tearing them down again. Sable thought of a small careful bell drawn in ink above a hunted face, and of the sound those bells had made answering a child, seven years silent and then suddenly, impossibly, awake.

"I'll need the execution ledger," she heard herself say. "The original. My copy is incomplete."

"Granted." Corvane lifted his hand from the map. Where it had rested, the lamplight seemed dimmer. "Bring me my Speaker, Wren. Bring me the sword that talks. And bring me whatever god has been whispering in our gutters — I want its name written where I can cross it out myself."

She bowed, because the coat required it, and walked down four hundred stairs with the ledger's weight already settling into her bones like snow into a roof that could not hold much more.

* * *

They found the chapel by falling into it.

That was how Bramble told the story later, and Bramble told everything twice, so Juniper heard it many times: how the rag-and-bone man with the cart had led them through the tannery district at midnight, through a door disguised as a wall, down stairs that smelled of wet stone and lamp oil, until the floor gave way to a flooded nave and Juniper, exhausted, half-asleep on their feet, stepped where the man pointed and dropped six inches into cold black water.

"There," Bramble said, delighted. "Fell right into church. That's how faith works, see? You don't climb to it. You fall."

The chapel had been a shrine to some river-saint before the Edict — the saint's name was gone from the lintel, chiseled away by diligent hands — and now it was a congregation. Forty people, maybe fifty, gathered in the shallows where the flood had never drained, standing ankle-deep and knee-deep among the drowned pews. They had hung lamps on fishing line strung between the pillars, and the light came down doubled, once from the flame and once from the water, so that everyone wore two shadows that didn't quite agree.

Juniper stood dripping in the doorway with their hood up and their scarred arm pressed close, and every eye in the chapel turned to them, and every eye already knew.

"You're the one from the posters," said a woman near the front. Not accusing. Wondering. She held a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth, as if she'd been carrying it toward someone.

"I'm nobody from the posters," Juniper said. "The posters have the jaw wrong."

Laughter — small, startled, quickly smothered, but real. It moved through the water like a ripple. And Juniper felt something crack open in their chest that they had kept boarded shut for seven years, because this was the sound of people unafraid together, and they had forgotten it existed anywhere outside memory.

Bramble — the rag-and-bone man, sixty if he was a day, with a beard full of straw and a voice like a cart on gravel — waded over and gripped Juniper's shoulder. "You fell into church," he repeated, as if this settled everything. "Come sit. Mind the third pew, it bites."

The third pew did bite; its bench-end had rotted to a jagged point. Juniper sat in the fourth, in six inches of holy floodwater, and the congregation closed around them like hands warming around a coal.

They showed Juniper what they kept, there under the tannery, under the noses of the Inquisition. A hearth-god the size of a teacup, carved from chimney brick, that kept the chapel's single brazier lit through anything — Bramble swore it had burned underwater twice. A door-god, nothing but a hinge blessed so long ago the blessing had soaked into the iron, which groaned a warning note whenever soldiers came down the street above. A shelf of forgotten saints in mismatched candle-holders, each one labeled in different handwriting: *for lost keys, for safe births, for rain that stops, for mothers, for dogs.* Small gods. Kitchen gods. Gods too humble for the Sundering Edict to bother hunting, surviving the way mice survive — in walls, in dark, in the spaces power doesn't think to search.

"They're weak," said the woman with the bread. Her name was Odile; she had a burn-scarred forearm of her own, though hers was from a bakehouse, not an oath. "Since the bells started answering wrong, they've gone thin. The little ones especially. They spend themselves answering prayers they can't really keep, because they can't bear not to try." She looked at Juniper with an expression that was not worship and not pity but something harder and kinder than both. "You've met one of the big ones. The Tamsin. What does it say?"

Juniper thought of the flooded chapel across the city, of the fading god's voice like a hymn played on a broken instrument. *Reforge it. Gather what was broken. Before the silence comes for everything, even the mice in the walls.*

"It says the sky is bruising through," Juniper said. "It says the prayers are going unanswered mid-word now. Have you heard that here?"

Silence, except the drip of water. Then, from the back, a girl's voice — twelve, maybe, the age of the child in the night market whose prayer had started everything: "Grandmother prayed for rain for the beans. She got to *please send* and then nothing. Like someone hung up on her." The girl's chin trembled. "She hasn't prayed since. She says why waste it."

Juniper looked at the small gods on their shelf — the brick hearth-thing guttering faithfully in its brazier, the hinge-door god, the saints for keys and dogs and mothers — and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water rising, exactly what was being asked of them. Not by the Tamsin. By this. By forty people standing in a flooded church keeping pilot-lights of divinity alive with cupped hands.

Odile broke the loaf and handed Juniper the larger piece, which was the custom of hungry congregations everywhere and always had been. "We'll hide you," she said. "Feed you, move you when the soldiers sniff around, sew you new coats. We've hidden believers for seven years; we can hide one loud one." Her eyes held steady. "But we want a promise back. All of us. Not thanks. A promise."

"Name it."

"When you reforge this covenant of theirs—" she glanced at the shelf of small gods, and her voice roughened, "—make it lawful. Not free. Lawful. We watched what happened when anyone with a grudge and a gift could bind a neighbor's luck or curse a rival's cow. That's why they let Corvane's lot take the choir, half of them, deep down. Because magic without law is just power with better manners." She took Juniper's hand — the scarred one, deliberately, turning it palm-up in the lamplight. "Promise us oaths that mean something. Promise us that when the gods listen again, they listen to *everyone*, and lie to no one. Can you promise that?"

The water lapped at the pews. Somewhere overhead, faint as a memory, the door-god hummed its warning note and went quiet again — a false alarm, only the wind.

Juniper looked at the promise being asked of them and saw how enormous it was, how it contained whole systems of justice they couldn't yet imagine, and how impossible it would be to keep, and how impossible it would be to refuse while holding this warm bread in this cold water surrounded by these stubborn, faithful, frightened people.

"I promise," Juniper said, "that I won't seal any covenant that serves only the strong. If I can't swear that much into the making of it, I'll die before I finish the work."

It wasn't the whole promise. It wasn't even most of it. But Odile studied their face for a long moment, then nodded once, and the congregation breathed out together, and Bramble clapped Juniper on the back hard enough to slosh the floodwater, shouting, "There! Fell into church, promised in church, that's proper order that is—"

The door-god screamed.

Not hummed — screamed, the blessed hinge shrieking a note that stripped conversation off the air, and every lamp in the chapel guttered at once as forty people went still in the water. Above them, muffled by stone and earth, came the sound the hinge had been warning of: boots. Many boots. And beneath the boots, thin and clear through six feet of Vell's oldest stone, a voice giving orders in the clipped cadence of the Inquisition.

Bramble's hand found Juniper's collar. Odile snuffed the nearest lamp with her bare fingers and whispered, in the dark, in the flooding dark:

"She's asking for you by name."
