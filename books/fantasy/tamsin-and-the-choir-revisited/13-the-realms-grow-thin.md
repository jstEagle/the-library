## 13. The Realms Grow Thin

The god of the third lamp on Candlemaker's Row had been named Wrennel once, by a girl who set him on her windowsill to keep moths from the wax. He was not a grand god. He had never wanted to be. His whole dominion was one lamp and the small circle of light it threw against the glass, and for two hundred years he had kept that circle honest — no moth singed, no flame guttering in a draft, no dark creeping past the sill while the candlemaker's daughter slept.

Now he was going out.

He felt it the way a candle feels the last of the wick: not as pain but as thinning, as though he were being stretched over too little world. The Edict had starved him for seven years — no prayers, no offerings, only the stubborn habit of his own existence — and he had endured that, because enduring was all a door-god knew how to do. But this was different. This was the floor of the world tilting beneath him.

Above Vell, through the smoke-haze, the sky had begun to bruise.

* * *

Sable Wren saw it first from the roof of the Inquisition barracks, where she went when she could not sleep and did not want to lie awake listening to herself think.

She had been free of the cathedral for six days. Six days since Corvane's interrogators had turned her over and found nothing to burn — or nothing they dared burn, yet; mercy-by-heresy was a slow poison in an institution's blood, and everyone knew what she had done in the reliquary, and no one could prove it mattered. They had released her back into service with a smile like a knife laid flat on a table. *We are watching you, Inquisitor. Pray you give us nothing to see.*

So she watched instead. She watched the sky.

It should have been black, that hour before dawn, with the gray smear of Vell's coal-smoke over it. Instead there was a discoloration high above the northern quarter, vast and slow-moving, the color of a healing wound seen from underneath — violet shading into a greenish gold, shot through with veins of darker red. It did not behave like cloud. Clouds moved with the wind. This moved against it, patient as a tide, and when Sable narrowed her eyes she could almost persuade herself she saw shapes in it: towers, perhaps, or trees, or the long spine of something that might have been a mountain range in another world.

*The other realm,* she thought. *Showing through.*

Every child raised in the choir had learned the doctrine in their first year: the realms were two skins of one drum, and the covenant was the tension between them, and so long as the tension held, each skin stayed taut and separate and true. Break the tension and the skins slackened. They touched. They bled into one another.

Someone had broken the tension. Someone had shattered the covenant seven years ago, deliberately, and whatever the Inquisition's official history said about the choir's treason, Sable had read Juniper Vasquez's face in the reliquary and known that they believed the truth ran deeper than any ledger she'd ever been handed.

And now the sky was slackening over Vell like old leather, and every prayer spoken under it was falling through the weave.

She heard the first evidence of that at dawn, in the market off Tanner's Lane, where a fishwife stood in front of a shrine to Saint Maren-of-the-Nets with her hands still folded and her mouth still open around the shape of a word that had died in it.

"—keep my boys safe on the water, please, please—" The prayer simply stopped. Not finished. *Stopped*, mid-plea, the way a rope stops when it snaps. The woman said it again. And again. And on the fourth repetition her voice cracked and she looked up at the little painted saint with an expression Sable recognized from ledgers of executed heretics: the look of someone who has just understood that no one is listening.

Around her, others were trying. An old man murmured grace over bread that would not be blessed. A mother pressed her palm to a doorpost where a household god had lived for three generations and whispered her daughter's name into the wood, and the wood gave back nothing, not even the faint warmth of attention. Small gods were guttering out across the city like lamps in a wind — Sable could feel the absence of them the way you feel a missing tooth, a wrongness in the ordinary pressure of things.

She had spent seven years enforcing a silence. She had told herself it was lawful silence, ordered silence, the quiet of a well-run house. This was not that. This was the silence of a room where the breathing had stopped.

*Corvane knows,* she thought. *He must know. He helped break it — if Juniper's blood told true — and he will have a remedy, and his remedy will be worse than the disease.* She remembered his voice in the interrogation cell, gentle as a confessor's: *Order itself was my god, Inquisitor. I merely gave it form.*

She turned her collar up against a wind that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of flowers from a country that did not exist, and went to find out what the congregations knew.

* * *

Juniper felt the realms thinning in their own body before anyone told them.

They were three days north of Vell, following the Tamsin's fading light along a road that ran beside a canal gone silver-still, when the oath-brand began to ache in a new way. It had burned when the gods returned. It had throbbed when they neared the bone-orchard, flared at the cathedral, screamed in the reliquary. Now it did none of those things. Now it *hollowed* — a cold, drawn-out sensation, like a bell struck and left ringing in an empty room, the sound slowly going somewhere it couldn't be recalled from.

"It's starting," they said to the air beside them, because that was where the Tamsin lived these days: beside them, a shimmer the size of a tall person, visible mostly as a distortion, like heat off summer stone. "Isn't it. The failing."

The distortion rippled. When the voice came, it was thinner than Juniper had ever heard it, a sound like bells heard through deep water. *Yes.*

"How long?"

A pause. Long enough that Juniper stopped walking. The canal lay flat as poured pewter beside them, reflecting a sky that was wrong in the north — bruised, veined, showing through.

*I do not know,* the Tamsin said, and Juniper heard the truth of it, and the truth was terrible: a god who did not know was a god already halfway gone. *Time is behaving badly near the wound. Some hours the skin holds. Some hours it sloughs. Yesterday a village two leagues east prayed for rain and received rain — and also received a river that does not exist, running through their barley for one hour, and then nothing. The realms are bleeding into each other at the thin places. There are more thin places every day.*

"Because the covenant is dead."

*Because the covenant is dying,* the Tamsin corrected, gently. *There is a difference, and it matters. A dead thing cannot be saved. A dying thing can be reforged — but only while some thread of it remains. We are down to threads, swordspeaker.*

Juniper walked on. The fragments rode against their chest in a wrapped bundle — three of them now, bone-pale shards of the old covenant, each humming faintly at a different pitch, and today, for the first time, Juniper noticed the pitches drifting. Sliding out of harmony with one another. Like a choir losing its note.

They thought of the choir. They thought of the ghosts at the execution ground, who had not forgiven them but had taught them anyway, and of the truth they'd learned at the altar-stone: *the choir chose to break it themselves.* Their fellows had shattered the covenant to stop Corvane's first attempt at ownership, and had let the Inquisition's swords fall on them rather than let magic become a state's property. And Juniper — Juniper had renounced their oath aloud in the dark while the others sang, and lived, carrying the whole oath out of the fire without knowing that was what they carried.

"Why me?" they asked quietly. It was an old question, worn smooth from handling. "Why did the oath choose the coward?"

The Tamsin's light flickered — and steadied, deliberately, Juniper realized. Even fading, it spent strength to be steady for them.

*Because the coward stayed,* it said. *The brave died beautifully and were done. You remained. Seven years selling luck-knots in a godless city, hiding your scar, hating yourself — and never once, not once, speaking the renunciation again. Do you understand? You could have made it permanent. One word, freely spoken, and the oath would have left you forever. You never said it. Every day for seven years you chose, without knowing you chose, to keep carrying it.*

Juniper's throat was tight. "I thought I was just too ashamed to finish it."

*Shame is a kind of faith,* said the Tamsin. *It believes you should have been better. Come. The fourth fragment waits, and the door between realms will not hold open much longer even for a god spending its last coin.*

But that night, camped in the shell of a tollhouse with the bruised sky crawling overhead, Juniper woke to a sound that froze them: a prayer, rising from the sleeping countryside, cut off mid-word.

Some farmer, miles away, dreaming or sleepless, whispering to a household saint — *please, let her fever break, let her—*

And then nothing. Not silence exactly. Silence was a thing with edges. This was the absence of edges, the place where an answer should have risen and found no floor to rise from.

Juniper sat up in the dark with their heart slamming and their hand closed around the bundle of fragments, and understood, in their marrow, that the world was running out of time in a way that clocks could not measure.

* * *

In the under-chapel beneath the tannery, Mother Casseline counted her congregation twice, because she did not trust the number the first time.

Forty-one souls. Hearth-believers, door-worshippers, keepers of forgotten saints, smuggled down through the tannery's false wall one family at a time. They had gathered tonight because the signs could no longer be ignored: the shrine-lamps burning blue, the household gods gone cold and silent in their niches, the sky over the grates showing that awful bruise-color even at noon. Old Tomas, who kept the god of his late wife's knitting basket, had come carrying the basket like a coffin. The god inside it had not answered him in four days.

"We need to hear from the speaker," Casseline said, when the hymn was done — sung softly, always softly, though what did softness matter now, when the sky itself had stopped keeping secrets? "Vasquez. The swordspeaker. Has anyone had word?"

"They're north," said Ferro, the tanner, who kept the congregation's roads and rumors. "Chasing the last fragment. But the word that comes back isn't good. The thinning is faster than anyone guessed. My cousin's wife works the river barges — she says the water showed faces yesterday. Faces looking *up* at the boats. From underneath. From the other side."

A murmur went through the chapel, fear moving through it like wind through wheat.

"Then we pray louder," said a young woman near the back — Petra, who tended the shrine of a door-god that had belonged to her grandmother. "If they're dying, we pray them back."

Casseline looked at her with an old woman's patience and an older woman's grief. "Child. They aren't dying because we stopped praying. We stopped praying because the Edict forbade it, yes, but the gods endured that for seven years. They're dying because the thing that connected our prayers to their ears is coming apart. Louder prayers won't mend a torn road." She lifted her hands, and the congregation stilled. "Only the covenant mends the road. Only the speaker can reforge it. And the reforging has a price we all know now, because Vasquez was honest enough to tell us what they learned under the cathedral."

Silence. Everyone knew. *An oath-bound life, freely given.* Everyone had done the arithmetic since, privately, in the dark: *not mine, please, not mine — theirs, theirs, they're the speaker, it should be theirs.*

"And so," Casseline said, "we do the only thing left to us. We make ready. If the speaker succeeds, the gods return, and there must be shrines waiting for them — lawful, open, in daylight, on every street. And if the speaker fails—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it. "If the speaker fails, then let the last thing the small gods hear from Vell be a city full of people saying thank you. Go home. Light your lamps. Say the names of your gods aloud, once, even if no one answers. Let them go out hearing themselves remembered."

They filed up the stairs and out into the bruise-lit evening, forty-one souls scattering to their doorways and windowsills, and one by one across the gray quarters of Vell, small voices began saying names into the thinning air. *Wrennel. Maren. Grandmother's door-god whose name no one living knew, called only "the keeper."*

Most of the names fell into silence.

But in the under-chapel afterward, alone, Mother Casseline lit one last candle before the congregation's oldest image — a crude carving of a god with a sword and a shepherd's crook crossed behind it, the covenant made flesh — and she prayed the way she had not prayed since before the Edict: loudly. Openly. With the door unbarred.

"Hear us, whoever is left," she said. "We are ready to receive what comes. Only come soon."

* * *

High Inquisitor Corvane stood at the top of the Gray Cathedral's bell tower and looked at the wound in the sky, and was not afraid.

That was the difference between him and everyone else in Vell, he reflected. The fishwives wailed. The congregations hid. The Inquisitors themselves had begun — he had seen it, in the averted eyes, in the hastily hidden amulets — to doubt. But Corvane looked at the unraveling of creation and felt only the clean satisfaction of a man watching a forecast come true.

He had known this would happen. He had helped arrange for it to happen, seven years ago, when the choir refused to see reason. Magic could not be permitted to remain wild — wild magic meant plague-cantors, weather-witches holding cities hostage, oath-speakers binding men's tongues with a word. Order required that power answer to structure. The choir had chosen death over structure, and so he had given them death, and he had taken their covenant apart with his own hands, certain that a controlled dissolution could be managed, rationed, *owned*.

He had not anticipated the decay spreading. That was the flaw in his design, and he admitted it the way an engineer admits a stress-fracture: coldly, and with a plan already drafted.

Below him, in the cathedral's great nave, the preparations continued through the night. The congregation captives — forty-one souls taken in the raids on the tannery and the chandler's loft, plus eleven more from the river district — knelt in rows on the altar-dais, bound not with rope but with written oaths, pages of forced confession pinned to their clothing. The counterfeit covenant needed lives. He had told his Inquisitors it was justice. It was, in fact, engineering: enough oath-bound life, freely surrendered under sufficient duress, could anchor a covenant of his own devising — one in which magic flowed only through licensed channels, only at the state's word, forever.

The sky's decay made it urgent. A dying wilderness of magic was more dangerous than a living one; better to bind it now, while it was weak, than fight it later, when it was desperate.

He did not think of it as damming a river while a flood came. He thought of it as building the ark.

"Inquisitor Wren has not reported," said the subordinate at his elbow — Brother Malchus, precise and colorless.

"She will not report," Corvane said. "She has gone over. To the speaker, to the congregations, to whatever superstition is winning her heart. It happens to the best instruments." He turned from the railing. "It changes nothing. Double the watch on the altar. And send word to the border garrisons: if the swordspeaker approaches the city, they are not to engage. They are to observe, and signal, and withdraw."

Malchus frowned. "Withdraw, Your Holiness?"

"The fragment they carry is the last anchor of the old covenant," Corvane said mildly. "I don't want it damaged. I want it *delivered*. Let them bring the reforging to my doorstep. It saves me the trouble of hunting it." He smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "Every covenant needs a sealing. Mine will simply use theirs."

Far below, in the nave, one of the kneeling captives began to sing — very softly, an old choir hymn, half-swallowed, defiant. The guards moved toward her. Corvane listened for a moment with his head tilted, the way a man listens to a clock he intends to stop.

Then he went down to finish his work, while overhead the bruise in the sky widened another inch, and somewhere far to the north, on a dark road, a swordspeaker felt the pull of it in their bones and turned toward the city — toward the cathedral, toward the captives, toward the door between realms that only they could hold open, and only for a little longer.

The Tamsin's voice, when it came that night, was barely a whisper of bells:

*Hurry, Juniper. Hurry.*
