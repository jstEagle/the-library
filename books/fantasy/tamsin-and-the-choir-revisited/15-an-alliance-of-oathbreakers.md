## 15. An Alliance of Oathbreakers

The Tamsin was fading the way a fire fades when no one feeds it — not all at once, but in surrendering pieces of itself to the dark, one coal at a time.

Juniper felt it most at dawn and dusk, the hours when gods were traditionally strongest, because those were the hours when it hurt most to be weak. The god walked beside them along the old pilgrim road out of Vell, or rather *drifted* beside them, its outline gone soft as breath on glass. Where once it had filled the flooded chapel with the smell of incense and drowned bells, now it barely bent the light around itself. Its voice had lost its lower registers. What remained was a sound like wind through the ribs of a wrecked ship.

"You keep looking at me," the Tamsin said, as the sun came up gray behind them. "As if looking will hold me together."

"Would it help if I stopped?"

"No. Keep looking. It's the only prayer anyone's offered me in seven years that I can actually feel."

Juniper said nothing to that. They had learned, over the long miles since the reliquary, that the god's humor was a kind of armor, and that mocking it gently was a kindness the Tamsin would never admit to needing.

They were three days out from Vell, following the fragment-song north toward the threshold place the third fragment had shown them — a pass in the hills where the world wore thin as old cloth. The blood-written map on Juniper's forearm had faded to a brown ghost, but they had memorized it the way you memorize anything written in your own skin: completely, unwillingly, forever.

The road was empty. That was the trouble with roads these days. Since the wanted posters went up — SPEAKER OF SWORDS, ALIVE FOR QUESTIONING, with a charcoal sketch of Juniper's face that made them look either saintly or criminal depending on the angle — the honest travelers had abandoned the pilgrim road, and only the dishonest ones used it after dark. Juniper traveled by day and slept in ditches and did not light fires, and every mile the Tamsin grew thinner, and every mile the brand-scar on their chest burned a little brighter, like a wick drawing down toward the flame.

On the fourth morning, someone was waiting for them at the shrine at Colder's Ford.

* * *

Juniper saw her from two hundred paces and knew her by the way she stood, before they knew her by anything else. Sable Wren had always stood like a drawn blade leaning against a wall — patient, angled, ready to be picked up and used. She stood that way now at the little roadside shrine, its stone saint worn faceless by weather and by the Inquisition's chisels both, and she was not wearing her whites.

She wore travel-gray. No insignia. Her ledger was not in her hands. Her sword was, but it hung point-down from her grip like something she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Juniper's hand went to their own blade — the sword that spoke only when spoken to, which had been sulking in silence for days, offended, it seemed, by the company it kept.

"Don't," Sable said. Her voice carried across the flat ground without effort; choir-training never left you, even when the choir was dead. "If I'd wanted an ambush, Juniper, you'd be in irons already. Or a grave. Corvane argued for the grave."

"And you argued for?"

Sable looked at the faceless saint. "I argued for nothing. I ran. There's a difference between arguing and running, and I've only just learned what it is."

The Tamsin drifted closer to Juniper's shoulder, and though it made no sound Juniper could hear, they felt its attention settle on Sable like a hand on a scale. *She burned eleven shrines,* the god murmured, so quietly it might have been memory. *And she crossed out nine names.*

"I know about your trial," Juniper said. They came forward slowly, one step at a time, the way you approach a wounded animal or a lit fuse. "Word travels. Heresy-by-mercy. They say Corvane interrogated you himself."

"They say true things sometimes. It keeps the lies warm." Sable turned to face them fully, and up close Juniper could see what the posters hadn't shown: she was thinner, and there was a burn scar along her jaw that hadn't been there seven years ago, and her eyes had the particular hollowed look of someone who has read her own heart in an enemy's handwriting and found it legible. "He told me he broke the covenant. Did your god tell you that part? He told me to my face. Order itself was his god, he said. He shattered the covenant because it wouldn't kneel."

"He told me more than that." Juniper stopped ten paces away. Close enough to talk. Far enough that a drawn sword would have to cross distance first, and distance was time, and time was the only currency they had left. "He didn't tell me why the choir died."

"No," Sable said softly. "Neither did I. Not until you bled it onto my floor."

Wind moved through the ford, carrying the smell of the river and the cold iron smell of the coming season. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge. The Tamsin flickered, steadied, flickered again.

"You followed me," Juniper said. "Out of the city. Past the walls. Alone, unarmed except for that—" they nodded at her sword "—and wearing no colors, which means you're not hunting. Which means you're either deserting or defecting, and I've watched you too long to believe you know how to desert."

"I don't," Sable said. "It turns out obedience is the only spine I have, and when I took it out and looked at it, I found someone else's name engraved on it." She laughed, briefly, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the ledger paper she'd burned. "So here I am. Defecting. To a heretic and an oathbreaker and the last speaker of a dead choir." Her eyes came up. "Unless you'd rather I didn't."

* * *

They walked together because there was nowhere else for either of them to go, and because the road north was long and the truth is a thing that wants walking-distance.

For the first hour neither spoke. Juniper catalogued the sounds of Sable moving beside them — the creak of her boot-leather, the small disciplined rhythm of her breathing, the way her sword hilt tapped her hip in time with her stride — and hated how familiar it all was. Seven years of silence, and the body remembers its choir-mates the way a bell remembers its note.

It was Sable who broke first, because Sable had always broken first and called it discipline.

"The night of the Edict," she said. "Tell me how you lived."

The question landed where she'd aimed it, under the ribs, in the scar. Juniper kept walking. The road unspooled ahead through brown fields, and the Tamsin drifted at their flank like smoke deciding whether to be a god today.

"You were there," Juniper said.

"I was in the square. I was fourteen and holding a candle and being very proud of my candle. I saw the scaffold. I heard the singing start. Then the Edict-criers started reading names and the crowd got loud, and after—" her voice did something complicated and settled into flatness "—after, there were six bodies instead of seven, and the seventh name was never crossed out of any ledger. I've carried that ledger for seven years, Juniper. Your name in it, uncrossed, like a debt the Inquisition forgot to collect. So tell me. How did you live?"

Juniper stopped walking.

The field beside the road was stubbled with old wheat, gold going gray, and the sky above it was the color of dishwater, and somewhere far off the other realm pressed against the sky like a bruise pressing against skin. Juniper looked at all of it — the ordinary ruined world — and found that the words, once begun, came out of them like water from a cracked jar.

"I renounced it," they said. "That's how. They gave us the choice, do you remember? Corvane himself stood on the scaffold and offered it. Renounce the oath, speak the words of severance, live. And Maren sang louder. And Osket sang louder. And Ilse, who was sixty and had been speaking oaths since before either of us was born, she looked at me — right at me, in the crowd, she found me somehow — and she smiled, and she kept singing." Juniper's voice went thin. "And I opened my mouth and I said the words. *I renounce the covenant. I renounce the voices. I am no speaker.* And the brand—" they touched their chest, over the scar "—the brand came off my skin like a scald. And they let me go. They pushed me out of the square like rubbish, because a living apostate was worth more to Corvane than a dead speaker. Proof that the oath could be broken. Proof that the gods could be refused."

Silence. The river talked to itself among the reeds.

"I have thought every day for seven years," Juniper said, "that I am the reason the covenant stayed broken. That if I had sung with them, the sacrifice would have been whole, and maybe—"

"Stop." Sable's voice cracked across the road like a whip. "Stop doing that. Stop writing yourself a worse crime than you committed."

"You weren't there."

"I've spent seven years reading the Inquisition's archives." She stepped closer, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "You want to know what I did to obey, Juniper? You want to trade truths? Here's mine. When I was nineteen, they sent me to a village called Harrowmere, where an old woman was leaving bread on her doorstep for a door-god. Bread. On a doorstep. And I burned the shrine, and I confiscated her hearth-stone, and I made her watch while I did it, and I wrote her name in my ledger under *idolater*, and she wept and thanked me, thanked *me*, because she thought mercy was what the burning was." Sable's breath shuddered. "And then I went home and slept soundly, because I was obedient, and obedience is just betrayal with good posture."

The Tamsin made a sound. It might have been grief. It might have been laughter. With dying gods, Juniper had learned, the two are difficult to tell apart.

"So no," Sable said, quieter now. "You didn't break the covenant. You couldn't have saved it by dying, whatever you tell yourself at night. The choir chose what they chose, and it wasn't your choice to make, and surviving isn't the sin. Surviving is just—" she faltered, and for a moment she was fourteen again, holding a candle in a roaring square, watching her sister-friends die for songs she wasn't allowed to learn. "Surviving is just the part that comes before you find out what it was *for*."

Juniper found they were crying. It surprised them; they had thought the well was empty, had thought seven years of careful gray living had salted that ground for good. But grief, it turned out, had only been waiting for permission.

"Sable," they said. "In the reliquary. You had me. Blade at my throat, orders in your pocket, and you let me walk. Why?"

And Sable Wren, who had crossed out nine names and burned her own page and run from the god of order himself, looked at Juniper Vasquez with the exhausted honesty of the newly converted, and said:

"Because you bled the truth onto my floor and it matched the truth in my chest, and I have spent my whole life learning that when those two agree, everything else is paperwork." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, briskly, like settling accounts. "I'm not asking your forgiveness. I want that understood. I haven't earned the standing to ask. I'm telling you what I am now, and you can take it or leave it on the road."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waymarker, an old covenant-stone snapped in half sometime in the first year of the Edict, its oath-script chiseled blind. Juniper built no fire, but Sable produced flint anyway, and after a long look between them — a negotiation conducted entirely in eyebrows — they built a small one, shielded, the kind of fire that apologizes for existing.

The Tamsin sat — there was no better word for it — on the far side of the flames, and by firelight its thinness was terrible to see. Juniper could count constellations through its shoulder. Its face had gone the way of the faceless saint at Colder's Ford: not erased, but worn toward erasure, features softening like wax left in the rain.

"How much longer?" Juniper asked quietly.

"For me?" The god smiled with a mouth that was mostly suggestion. "Long enough. Gods are frugal at the end. We spend ourselves on what matters and go dark between purchases." Its gaze moved to Sable, who was cleaning her sword with the mechanical tenderness of long habit. "You brought a second sword-speaker to the threshold. That was not in any prophecy. Prophecies hate improvisation."

"She's not a speaker."

"Not yet." The Tamsin's voice dropped low, and the fire leaned toward it like a listener. "But the covenant is not picky, child. It never demanded talent. Only consent. That was always the whole of the law: *magic is lawful when the oath is given freely.* Corvane's great sin was never breaking that law. It was believing he could replace consent with command." The god flickered, guttered, steadied. "Remember that, at the end. Whatever else the fragments teach you. Remember that the door only opens outward from the inside."

Juniper wanted to ask what that meant. The god's eyes had already gone distant, fixed on some middle distance where dying gods keep their appointments.

Across the fire, Sable sheathed her sword and looked up. "Your god talks to you."

"When it has the strength."

"What does it say? At night. When you think I'm asleep."

Juniper fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch. "It says the same thing it's said since the chapel. *Reforge it. Reforge it. Someone must carry the whole oath out of the fire.*" They glanced up. "What does your silence say? At night. When you think I'm asleep."

Sable was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Overhead, through the torn canopy of clouds, the bruise in the sky had spread another inch toward the moon, the other realm showing through the world's thin skin like a wound through a sleeve.

"It says I should have crossed your name out years ago," Sable said finally. "And that I should have burned Harrowmere's ledger page. And that obedience was the cowardice I dressed up as faith." She lay back against the broken covenant-stone, arms folded, eyes on the bruised sky. "And it says that tomorrow we walk north, and that whatever's waiting at the threshold, we walk into it together, because I am done being alone inside my own certainties."

Juniper lay back too, on the far side of the small apologetic fire, and found that the brand-scar on their chest had stopped burning for the first time in days. It hummed instead, low and steady, like a struck bell that has decided, for now, to hold its note.

Forgiveness, they thought, drifting toward sleep. They had expected to have to beg for it, or grant it, or perform some ceremony of absolution with speeches and kneeling. Instead it had simply arrived somewhere on the road between the ford and the fire, unasked for, unbargained, the way the old gods used to answer prayers — not because it was deserved, but because someone had finally meant it.

Near midnight, the Tamsin woke them both.

It was standing, and standing cost it visibly — its edges shredded like cloth in wind — and it was facing north, toward the pass, toward the threshold, and its voice when it came had recovered, for one moment, all its drowned-bell depth:

"Runes on the ridge," it said. "Corvane knows where we're going. He's already sealing the road."

And far off in the black hills, faint as a lie, bells began to ring — the wrong kind, the kind that count down.
