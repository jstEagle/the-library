## 17. Raid on the Gray Cathedral

**Juniper**

The Gray Cathedral had no windows. That was the first thing Juniper noticed, standing in its long shadow with Sable at their shoulder and the Tamsin's last warmth guttering in their chest like a coal wrapped in wet cloth. Every cathedral Juniper had ever known — before, when there were cathedrals worth the name — had been built to let light in, to let the other realm look through. Corvane's was a fist of granite and iron, sealed against heaven, because what happened inside it tonight was never meant to be witnessed by anything that could object.

"They're already singing," Sable said.

Juniper heard it then, muffled through stone: not a choir. A choir had harmonies, had breath between phrases, had the human stumble of voices finding one another. This was a single sustained tone, dozens of throats bent to it, held past endurance. The sound of people being used as an instrument rather than being one.

"The congregation," Juniper said. Their mouth had gone dry. "How many did they take?"

"Forty-one names in the raid reports. Maybe more they didn't bother counting." Sable's face was half-lit by the brazier at the service gate, and Juniper saw what seven years had carved into it — the discipline, yes, but underneath, the same girl who had once smuggled honey-cakes into choir practice and blamed the dog from the almonry. "Corvane wants them alive until midnight. After that—"

"After that he doesn't need them breathing."

"No."

The plan was simple, which meant it would probably kill them. The Inquisition's own rite required the officiant's voice unamplified for the binding clauses; that meant a moment, somewhere near the end, when Corvane would stand alone on the dais with his throat bare. They would come in through the ossuary cistern — Sable knew the way, because she had helped design the cathedral's defenses and had spent three sleepless nights teaching herself to hate that knowledge — cut the ceremony apart, and pull forty-one people out of a building built to keep gods out and screams in.

"And if the fragment's really in the altar?" Juniper asked.

Sable looked at them. "Then you take it out. Whatever it costs."

*Whatever it costs.* The Tamsin stirred at those words, faint as a dying man turning over in bed. *The fourth fragment,* it said, in a voice like the memory of bells. *I can feel it from here. It is loud. It is grieving. Be careful what you wake.*

Juniper pressed their palm flat against the scar where their oath-brand had been cut away, felt the answering burn beneath it, and nodded to Sable.

"Together, then," they said. "One more time."

* * *

**Sable**

The cistern smelled of old prayers. That was the thing no one warned you about holy places repurposed: stone remembered. Seven years of enforced silence had soaked into these walls, and now the water lapping at their boots carried the taste of it — ash and incense and the particular mineral tang of fear that has nowhere to go.

Sable went first, lantern hooded, counting arches. Forty paces to the junction. Thirty more to the stair that came up behind the reliquary. She had walked this route a hundred times as Inquisitor, escorting evidence, escorting prisoners, escorting the quiet machinery of a state that had decided God was too dangerous to be left unowned. She had never once walked it toward the singing.

Behind her, Juniper moved like water finding its level. She kept catching herself listening for their footsteps and hearing almost nothing — the swordspeaker's grace, still in the body even after the brand was gone. Even after everything.

"You're staring," Juniper murmured.

"I'm checking for pursuit."

"Mm." A pause, and then, softer: "It's all right to be afraid, Wren. I've been afraid since chapter four of my life."

Despite everything — despite the cistern, despite the tone droning overhead like a wire pulled taut across the world — Sable huffed something that was almost a laugh. "That's not how chapters work."

"It's exactly how chapters work. Ask any novelist."

They reached the stair. Above them, through a grate rusted half-open, the sustained note resolved suddenly into words, and Sable's blood went cold, because she recognized the liturgy. Not the old covenant-rites — those were burned, purged, forbidden — but Corvane's counterfeit, the one drafted in secret and read aloud only to those about to be bound by it. *By consent of the assembled, by authority of the Edict, by order given and order kept—*

Order. His god had always been order. She understood that now, understood it the way you understand a blade only after it's been inside you.

She pulled the grate wide and climbed into the dark behind the reliquary, Juniper at her heels, and together they looked out through the filigree screen into the nave of the Gray Cathedral.

Forty-one people knelt in ranks on the cold floor, chained at the ankle, robed in gray, their mouths moving in the forced chorus. She found faces she knew: Mother Ilse from the door-shrine on Tanner's Row, who had once left bread out for a saint nobody else remembered. The twins from the lamp-god congregation, sixteen at most. Old Piotr, whose hearth-god had been nothing but a chipped figurine and a stubborn kindness, kneeling now with his lips cracked and bleeding from the effort of holding a note that was killing him slowly.

And on the dais, robed in white so clean it hurt, stood High Inquisitor Corvane, arms raised, drinking the sound.

Before him, the altar.

Sable had seen that altar a thousand times and never truly seen it. It was a single block of black stone, older than the cathedral around it, older than the Edict, older — she realized with a lurch — than anything else in Vell. And set into its face, half-swallowed by the rock as if the stone were digesting it, was a shard of something that caught the candlelight and refused to give it back. Something that pulsed, very faintly, in time with the prisoners' song.

The fourth fragment. Taken from the original choir — from the bodies, maybe, or from the place where they died — and buried here, in the heart of the machine that had murdered them, feeding Corvane's counterfeit covenant the way a stolen heart feeds a stolen body.

"There it is," Juniper breathed beside her. Their voice shook. "Oh, Tamsin. There it is."

* * *

**Juniper**

There was no good moment. There was only the least bad one, and it came when Corvane lowered his arms to begin the binding clauses — the moment his amplified chorus faltered for breath.

Juniper went over the screen rail and dropped thirty feet into the nave, sword-first, and the world answered.

Not the sword's answer — the *oath's*. The scar on their arm tore open with light, and every chain in the cathedral shivered. The prisoners' song broke apart into weeping and screaming and, here and there, astonishingly, laughter. Mother Ilse looked up at Juniper falling out of the dark like a thrown spear and said, clear as a bell across the chaos: "The speaker. The speaker's come."

Then the Inquisitors moved, and there was no more time for anything but the fight.

It was ugly. Juniper had fought hollowed god-corpses in bone-orchards and Inquisitor patrols in the gray streets, but this was different — this was forty trained blades in a confined space, defending a man they believed was saving the world, and belief makes soldiers of anyone. Juniper's sword sang only when spoken to, so they spoke to it constantly, a running litany under their breath — *left, high, through, now* — and the blade obeyed like a partner remembering old dances. Steel met steel. Someone's blood — not theirs, not yet — spattered hot across their cheek.

Across the nave, Sable fought in the white-and-gray of her own former rank, and the sight of it broke something loose in the Inquisitors' formation. They hesitated. Hesitation is death in a melee. Three of them went down before the rest understood that Wren had crossed, truly crossed, and after that they stopped trying to capture her.

"THE ALTAR!" Sable shouted over the din. "JUNIPER — THE ALTAR!"

Corvane had not fled. That was the worst of it. He stood on the dais with his hands folded, watching the battle for his counterfeit covenant with the calm of a man reading a ledger, and when his eyes found Juniper's across the burning candles and the falling bodies, he smiled.

"You're late," he called. "The clauses are nearly spoken. Do you know what happens at midnight, Vasquez? Nothing. Nothing happens, ever again, anywhere — except what I permit. I am not destroying magic. I am *domesticating* it. Someone must hold the leash, or the beast drags us all into the dark. You of all people should understand the necessity of—"

Juniper stopped listening. They were climbing the dais steps, cutting down the two guards who thought duty meant dying for a smile, and laying both hands on the black altar-stone, and the fragment inside it was *screaming* — not in rage but in recognition, the way a child screams for its mother across a crowded room.

*I know you,* it sang through the stone, into Juniper's bones. *I know your name. We sang it together. Take me home.*

"Then come home," Juniper whispered, and spoke the old word — the first word of the first fragment, the word the Tamsin had taught them in a flooded chapel while its light went out by degrees — and drove the speaking into the stone like a wedge.

The altar split.

Black rock sheared away from the shard in a spray of dust and light, and the fourth fragment rose free into Juniper's cupped hands — small, warm, impossibly heavy, humming with forty-one borrowed voices and seven years of stolen grief. The moment it cleared the stone, every candle in the cathedral flared white, and the counterfeit liturgy Corvane had been weaving collapsed mid-clause like a rope cut through.

Corvane screamed — not in defeat, Juniper thought, but in *theology*, the sound of a man watching his god die twice.

* * *

**Sable**

Afterward, Sable would remember the retreat in fragments, the way broken glass remembers the window.

The Inquisitors, leaderless — Corvane had fallen to his knees before his dead altar and begun reciting the Edict to himself like a rosary — had lost their shape. Some fought on out of habit. Some simply stopped. The chains on the prisoners' ankles, weakened by the fragment's release, gave way under Mother Ilse's hymn-worn hands, and then it was forty-one people stumbling toward the ossuary stairs with Juniper covering the rear and Sable carrying a boy who could no longer walk.

She was almost to the stairs when the crossbow bolt took her.

It came from the gallery — some faithful remnant, some true believer making his own private last stand — and it went in below her ribs on the left side, and the world tilted, and the boy slid from her arms into someone else's, and she heard Juniper scream her name from very far away and also directly against her ear.

"Wren. Wren, stay with me. Stay—"

"I'm staying," she said, and was surprised by how much effort the words cost. "Someone has to count the survivors. Forty-one. Count them, Juniper. Don't lose any."

"Don't you dare do arithmetic at me right now—"

But she was doing it herself, through the gray closing in: forty-one souls up the stairs, one speaker of swords with four fragments glowing through their clenched fingers like a hand full of captive dawn, one High Inquisitor kneeling in the wreckage of his own certainty and beginning, horribly, to laugh. One bolt in her side. She had read enough ledgers to know how this column summed.

"Juniper." She caught their sleeve as they lifted her. The fragments' light made their face strange, younger and older at once. "Listen. If the price is what you think it is — if it needs a life freely given—" Her breath hitched wetly. "Take mine. Freely. Given. I've been spending lives for seven years on orders I didn't believe. Let mine buy something true for once."

"No." Juniper's voice cracked down the middle. "No, absolutely not, that is the worst offer anyone has ever—"

"Sable," said Mother Ilse gently, from the foot of the stairs, where the whole rescued congregation had stopped and turned, "is bleeding on the threshold of the ossuary, and the threshold is *glowing*, child. Look at it."

Sable looked.

The doorway — the plain, functional, granite doorway between the cathedral and the cistern stairs — was no longer a doorway. Light poured through it from somewhere that was not the cistern, somewhere vast and ringing and almost unbearably tender, and standing in that light, translucent as breath on glass, was a figure Sable had seen only in Juniper's descriptions and her own guilty dreams.

The Tamsin. Faded to almost nothing. Holding the door open with both hands.

*You have all four,* the dying god said, and its voice reached everyone at once, filling the cathedral like water fills a cup. *And the threshold is open, and the hour is close, and the price stands named. Come. Both of you. What was broken by many voices must be mended by more than one.*

Juniper looked down at Sable, at the blood spreading between their fingers, at the door that had opened precisely wide enough for two.

"Hold on to me," Juniper said. "We're going through together, and you are *not allowed* to finish that sentence."

The light took them. Behind, in the ruined nave, Corvane rose to his feet and began to follow.
