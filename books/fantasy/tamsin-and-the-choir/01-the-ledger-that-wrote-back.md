## 1. The Ledger That Wrote Back

The counting house of the Vellum Guild smelled of tallow, ink, and old bone — a smell Lior Thackeray had long since stopped noticing, the way a fisherman stops noticing the sea. It was past the ninth bell, and the clerks had gone home to their suppers and their wives, leaving him alone with the lamps, the ledgers, and the low murmur of the dead who drifted up through the floorboards like damp.

He was carving when the entry appeared.

Later he would try to fix the moment in his memory — the exact angle of the burin in his hand, the curl of shavings at his elbow, the particular quality of lamplight on vellum — but in truth there was nothing to fix. No sound, no cold breath, no warning. He looked down from the knucklebone he was etching for Mistress Corvel (a luck-piece, third one this season; her husband gambled) and the open ledger simply *had* a new line where no line had been.

*The gods are awake,* it said, in his own handwriting. *Payment due.*

Lior set down the burin very carefully, the way his master had taught him to set down tools near frightened animals, though what he meant to keep calm was himself.

His handwriting was distinctive. Twenty years of ledger-keeping had worn it into something like a signature of the whole man: the tall ascenders, the hooked g's, the small precise crossbar he made on every t because Master Ellery had rapped his knuckles with a ruler until precision became reflex. Every clerk in the Guild could pick his entries out of a hundred. And this line — this line was his, letter for letter, down to the way the y's tail dipped below the rule-line just slightly too far, a flaw he'd never managed to carve out of himself.

He had not written it.

He knew this the way he knew his own hands: absolutely, and without any evidence he could show another living soul. The ink was dry. The lamp had not flickered. The room held its ordinary silence, and outside the shuttered window the Guild quarter went about its evening — cart wheels on cobbles, a vendor crying the last of his chestnuts, someone laughing in the taproom across the lane as though the world were a place where laughter still meant anything simple.

Lior read the line again. Then again, a fourth time, hunting for trickery: a forgery, an apprentice's prank, some rival clerk's cruel joke rendered in a fair imitation of his hand. But the ink sat in the vellum's tooth exactly as his ink always did, and the spacing matched his spacing, and the entry was dated in the margin — today's date, tonight's hour — in numerals only he used, the old bone-cutter's notation his mother had taught him before she died, tally-marks crossed in fives that no Guild school would ever sanction.

*The gods are awake. Payment due.*

"Payment," he said aloud, to the empty room, "for what?"

The dead under the floorboards did not answer. They rarely did. They were mostly small deaths, the ones that seeped into the Guild quarter's foundations — a clerk who'd collapsed over his abacus forty years ago, a chimney-sweep's boy, a moneylender whose name had been struck from every record so thoroughly that even his ghost seemed unsure of it. They murmured at the edges of Lior's hearing the way rain murmurs against glass, and he had learned, as a boy in the slums below Scrimshaw Row, to let them wash over him without listening. Listening invited conversation. Conversation invited bargains. And bargains with the dead were the one trade the Vellum Guild recorded but never honored.

He closed the ledger.

That was the sensible thing. Close the book, weight it with the obsidian paper-knife, note in tomorrow's log that an irregularity had been found and corrected. The Guild loved procedure the way other institutions loved scripture; procedure was a wall you could hide behind while the strange thing passed by on the street outside. Lior had hidden behind that wall for twenty years. He was good at it. He was, in fact, the best record-keeper the Guild had ever employed, which was a sentence he could hear Provost Vane saying at some banquet, warm and public and slightly mocking, *our Lior, who never forgets a figure* —

He opened the ledger again.

Because here was the thing about being the keeper of records: you could not unsee an entry. An entry existed to be reconciled. Somewhere in the great web of debits and credits that was the Guild's business — the bottled storms sold to drought-struck provinces, the second shadows purchased by assassins' clients, the year of someone's life traded for a cure — somewhere in all of that, if the gods were awake, then something had been taken from them, and something must be owed, and a debt unrecorded was not a debt forgiven. It was a debt accruing interest.

Master Ellery's voice, dead these fifteen years and buried beneath the Guild chapel, said in his memory: *A ledger is a promise, boy. Every line is a promise that the world can be counted.*

Lior turned back through the pages, looking for the beginning of it. Three days ago: a sale of consecrated candle-smoke to the Alchemist's Conclave, entered and countersigned, ordinary. A week before: nothing unusual. A month: the usual impossible commerce, each line neat as stitching. He went back further. Six months. A year. Five years, his fingers moving faster now, the pages whispering under them like wings.

Nothing. No entry out of place. No transaction involving gods, divine voices, covenant-terms, or anything else that belonged to the category his mind was now, unhelpfully, assembling. The ledger was clean. The ledger had always been clean.

Which meant either the entry was a lie, or the ledger was.

And that thought — that small, cold, perfectly reasonable thought — was the one that finally made him stand up so fast his stool skidded backward across the flagstones.

* * *

He took the ledger to the window, because light matters when you're interrogating a document, and held the page against the moon. Vellum is honest stuff; it remembers everything done to it. Scraping shows. Erasure shows. Ink laid over ink shows, if you know how to look, and Lior had spent a decade teaching apprentices how to look.

The page showed nothing but the entry itself, sitting in its appointed place between yesterday's sale of a bottled lullaby (to a sleepless duchess, price undisclosed) and tomorrow's blank lines waiting like fresh snow. As though the entry had grown there. As though the vellum had been thinking about it for some time and had merely waited for him to be alone.

"The gods are awake," he repeated. His breath fogged the page. He hadn't realized the room had gone cold. "Awake from what? Who sold them? What's owed?"

From beneath the floorboards, faint as a coin dropped in another room, came humming.

It was not the dead's usual murmur. This was melody — actual melody, a rising phrase of four notes and a falling phrase of three, repeated, patient, the way a child hums when they aren't lonely yet but are getting there. Lior stood motionless with the ledger pressed to his chest and listened, and the hair rose along his arms, because he knew the tune.

Every child raised within earshot of the Cathedral of the Quiet Bell knew the tune. It was the Vespers hymn, the plainchant the choir sang at sundown, the one the clergy called *the oldest prayer still in use.* He'd heard it drifting down from the cathedral hill all his life, thin and beautiful and utterly indifferent to the slums below.

But the boy humming it under the floorboards of the Vellum Guild's counting house was singing it wrong. Or rather — Lior's blood went cold and colder — singing it *complete*. There were verses to the Vespers hymn that the cathedral did not sing. Everyone knew there were; the hymnals had gaps in them, margins noting *here the ancient text is lost*, and the choirs skipped those passages with the smoothness of long habit. The boy beneath the floor was singing straight through the gaps, filling them in, note after note after note, as though the missing music had never gone missing at all.

Lior knelt.

It was not a decision so much as a surrender. He put one palm flat against the flagstone, feeling the chill of it, feeling — or imagining, he would tell himself later, *imagining* — a vibration there, like a voice sung into the bones of the building.

"Who's there?" he asked. "What are you?"

The humming stopped.

In the silence that followed he became aware of how alone he was: one lamp, one man kneeling on stone, one ledger full of one impossible line. Somewhere above, the cathedral bell rang the tenth hour, and the sound fell through the room like a stone through water.

Then, directly beneath his palm, in a voice small and clear and terribly young, the dead boy spoke.

"You're the one who keeps the books."

Not a question. Lior swallowed. "I'm the bone-carver. I keep the ledgers, yes."

"They told me you'd come." A pause, and when the voice resumed it had the careful, over-precise quality of a child reciting something rehearsed. "They said: find the one who keeps the books, when the book starts keeping him."

Lior's mouth was dry. "Who said? Who told you?"

"The ones who can't sing anymore." The words came slower now, dragging, as though each one cost the speaker something. "The ones in the wax. In the dark downstairs. You have to count us, master carver. Nobody's counted us. Nobody's counted us in thirty years, and a thing that isn't counted—"

The voice frayed like rope under strain. Lior pressed his palm harder to the stone, absurdly, as if he could hold the boy up through six feet of foundation.

"A thing that isn't counted can be spent," the boy finished, barely a whisper. "Check your ledger. Page nine-and-forty. We're in there. All nine of us. Under *livestock*."

Silence rushed back in, total, the silence of a room that has never held a voice at all. The cold remained. The lamp burned steady. Outside, the chestnut vendor had gone home and the taproom had gone quiet, and the Guild quarter lay under its respectable blanket of night, every debt in it sleeping soundly because every debt in it was written down.

Lior stayed kneeling a long moment. Then he rose, carried the ledger back to the desk, and turned to page forty-nine with hands that were almost steady.

The heading read, in the round confident script of a clerk fifty years dead: *Inventory, Subterranean Vault — Perishable Goods, Assorted.* And beneath it, itemized in columns, nine entries. Nine lots. Each with an acquisition date, a storage location, a condition report updated annually in different hands across the decades — *preserved, intact, holding* — and each with a single-word description that the founding clerks had evidently considered sufficient:

*Soprano. Mezzo. Alto. Tenor. Baritone. Bass. Treble. Countertenor. Descant.*

Nine voices. Filed like tallow. Like wool. Like anything the Guild had ever bought cheap because no one was left to say it wasn't theirs to sell.

Lior sat down slowly. The ledger lay open before him, and the new line gleamed at the bottom of the day's page in his own unmistakable hand, patient as a hook:

*The gods are awake. Payment due.*

He understood, then, with the terrible clarity that comes to men who are very good at their work, that the entry was correct in every particular — and that he was not the one who had written it because he was not the one who was going to pay. The Guild had kept accounts for two hundred years, and accounts, Master Ellery had said, are promises. Somewhere in the dark beneath his feet, nine promises were waiting to be collected, and the collection had begun with a dead boy's song leaking up through the floorboards into the ears of the only man alive who would believe a ledger over his own sanity.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the humming started again — four notes rising, three notes falling — and beneath it, so faint he might have invented it, a second voice joined in. Then a third.

Lior picked up his coat and his carving knife and left the counting house without locking the ledger away, which in twenty years he had never once done. Behind him, in the dark, the Choir went on singing through the gaps in the hymn, and the last thing he heard as the door swung shut was not the dead at all but something older, vaster, awake at last and turning over in its long sleep like a man remembering an insult:

a voice like winter saying, clearly, in the language of covenants:

*Find me a singer.*
