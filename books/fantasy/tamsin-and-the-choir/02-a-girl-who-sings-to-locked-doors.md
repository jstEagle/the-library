## 2. A Girl Who Sings to Locked Doors

The Cathedral of the Quiet Bell had one bell, and it never rang.

Tamsin had asked why, once, when she was small enough to ask questions without being punished for them. Sister Ondile had pressed a finger to her lips and said the bell was not silent because it was broken. It was silent because it was listening. Tamsin had spent nine years in the choir since, and she still did not know whether that was doctrine or a story told to children so they would hush.

What she knew was this: doors opened when she sang.

Not all doors. Not ordinary doors. The heavy oak of the reliquary, triple-locked since before her grandmother's grandmother, had swung open on the third verse of the Vespers Antiphon for Saint Maren — *she who kept the gate* — and Tamsin had stood alone in the dark of the reliquary with forty saints' fingerbones gleaming like wet teeth, until she'd found the strength to sing the antiphon backward and shut it again. Her heart hadn't stopped hammering for an hour.

She'd tested it after that, carefully, the way you test ice on a pond. The crypt door, which no living hand could shift, had groaned open at a low hummed phrase from the Burial Office. The little iron door in the cloister wall that even the provost's own masons swore led nowhere — that one had shivered in its frame when she sang the evening psalm too close to it, rattling like something eager.

And the clergy flinched.

That was the part that frightened her more than the doors. When she sang certain verses — always the same ones, the old verses, the ones printed in faded ink at the bottoms of pages as if they'd been added late or left over from something older — Canon Pell's hands went white on his prayer book. Sister Ondile turned her face away. And once, during the Feast of the Quiet Bell itself, when Tamsin held a high note longer than the music strictly required, the Dean himself had made a sound like a man swallowing a scream, and afterward he'd looked at her across the nave with an expression she couldn't name. Not anger. Something closer to grief, or guilt, or the look a man gives a debt he thought long forgiven.

They didn't tell her to stop singing. That was the strange thing. They gave her solos. They praised her tone, her breath control, her purity. They fed her better than the other choirgirls and let her keep the window room with the view of the river. It was almost as if they wanted her voice exactly where it was. Almost as if she were not a girl in their care but an asset in their keeping, and everyone was very careful not to say so.

Tamsin was seventeen, orphaned, clever, and hungry in ways that had nothing to do with bread. She had learned early that belonging is a currency and she was poor. So she smiled at the Dean. She curtsied to Canon Pell. And at night, alone in her window room, she sang the forbidden verses softly to herself and watched the locks of her own door slide open like animals coming to be petted, and she wondered what she was.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday, in the grey hour between compline and dawn, when the cathedral is at its most honest.

Tamsin woke because the silence changed shape.

She lay still in her narrow bed and listened. The Quiet Bell hung in its tower above her, and she had long ago learned to hear the way it listened — a pressure, a leaning-in, like the whole bronze weight of it bending toward something. Tonight it wasn't leaning toward the city. It was leaning toward the east, toward the Guild quarter, and beneath the pressure of its attention there was something else: a sound so faint she might have dreamed it.

A thread of song. Thin as spider silk. Coming through the floor.

She sat up. The thread pulled.

There was no other word for it. Somewhere below and east, someone was singing — or something was humming — a melody she knew the way you know your own heartbeat, without ever having been taught it. It was built from the same bones as the old verses at the bottoms of the hymnals. It was the music under the music. And it was calling her the way her name called her, gently, insistently, the way a mother calls a child home across a crowded market.

Tamsin dressed in the dark. She took nothing but the small knife she used for sharpening pencils, which tells you how much danger she expected. Then she stood before her chamber door, the one that locked from the outside every night, and sang three notes of the Burial Office under her breath.

The lock sighed open like a lover giving in.

The corridors of the cathedral were cold stone and colder silence. She moved through them barefoot, past the dormitory where the other girls slept in warm heaps, past the chapel where the eternal lamp burned for a god who, everyone said, had gone away. Everyone said the gods were gone. Everyone said it the way you say a thing you've agreed not to examine. *The gods departed. The world is ours to keep.* It was in the catechism. It was carved over the cathedral doors.

But the thread of song pulled east, and the east end of the cathedral was the oldest part, and the oldest stones remembered things, and lately — this was the thing Tamsin could not say aloud, the thing that lived in her chest like a swallowed coal — lately, when she sang, she felt *answered*. Not by the congregation. Not by the acoustics. Answered the way a question is answered: specifically, knowingly, from somewhere that had been waiting a long time to be asked.

She slipped out through the cloister and past the little iron door that led nowhere. As she passed it, the door hummed.

She stopped. Put her palm flat against the cold iron. And for one vertiginous moment she felt the door the way the door felt itself: not a barrier but a throat. A held note. Behind it, far down, something vast and patient was keeping pitch.

"Later," she whispered, and was horrified to find she meant it like a promise.

Then she ran, out the postern gate, into the sleeping city, following the thread.

* * *

The Guild quarter at dawn was all money and mist.

Tamsin had never been here. Choirgirls did not come to the Guild quarter; the Guild quarter was where the world's impossible commerce happened, behind doors with brass fittings, in buildings that smelled of ink and tallow and old decisions. The Vellum Guild traded in everything, the street-sellers said. Land, relics, futures, favors. Things that couldn't be weighed were weighed here anyway, and the scales always tipped toward the Guild.

The thread of song grew stronger as she walked, and stranger. It wasn't human. She understood that now, walking the empty colonnades with her arms wrapped around herself against the cold. Human song has breath in it, mistakes, the small mortal wobble of a body doing its best. This had none. It was perfect the way frost is perfect, and it came from everywhere and nowhere — from the drains, from the gaps under doors, from the space between one flagstone and the next. It was a voice that had been folded up very small and put away, and it was singing to keep from going mad.

Tamsin followed it down a lane between two counting houses, past a shuttered apothecary, to a crooked building whose sign read BONEWORKS & LEDGERS in letters worn nearly smooth. The song pooled there. Under it, fainter, she heard something else now — a boy's voice, a child's humming, coming from beneath the ground, from under the floorboards of the world.

She put her hand on the boneworks door. It was locked, of course. Everything in the Guild quarter was locked; locks were the local language.

So she sang.

Three notes. The old ones. The ones that made Canon Pell's knuckles whiten. The lock turned over with a click that sounded, in the dead morning quiet, like a bone breaking, and the door drifted inward, and Tamsin stepped into the dark smelling of dust and animal fat and ink, and collided with a man coming the other way at a dead run.

They went down together in a tangle of limbs and terror. She struck his chest — hard, all bone and panic — and he made a sound like a startled bird, and then they were both scrambling up in the gloom, and she got her first look at him.

He was young, but worn young, the way tools are worn. Ink-stained fingers. A carver's calluses. His eyes were the worst part: they had the look of eyes that had seen something in the last hour that rearranged the furniture of the soul, and were now trying to decide whether to believe it.

"Don't—" he said, and then seemed to lose the rest of the sentence. He looked at the open door behind her. At her mouth, as if he could see the song still hanging on the air. "You *opened* it."

"You were running," Tamsin said. "From what?"

He laughed, high and unsteady. "The ledger." He said it the way you'd say *the wolf*. "My ledger wrote a line tonight. In my handwriting. I've kept those books for six years and I know my own hand like my own face, and it said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It doesn't matter what it said."

"The gods are awake," Tamsin said quietly. "Payment due."

The man went absolutely still. In the grey light through the dusty window, she watched every drop of blood leave his face.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've been hearing the same music you have," she said. "All night. All my life, maybe. Don't you hear it? Under the floor. Someone's humming."

"I hear him," the man whispered. "A choirboy. Dead sixty years. He's been humming under my floor since midnight, and until tonight he never made a sound, and now—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Now everything is awake and I don't know what I am anymore. I'm Lior. Lior Thackeray. I carve bones and I keep accounts and I am a *sensible* man."

"Tamsin," she said. "I sing."

"Yes," Lior said, looking at the unlocked door again, at her, at the whole impossible architecture of the night. "I'd guessed."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the dead choirboy's humming changed key — and Tamsin felt it in her sternum like a hand turning her by the shoulder, because she recognized the tune now, fully, finally. It was the verse at the bottom of the page. The one the clergy flinched at. The one that had been cut off mid-phrase in every hymnal in the city.

The dead boy was humming the rest of it.

Lior heard it too. She saw it land on him, saw his breath go ragged. He looked at her, and she looked back, and between them passed the wary, electric recognition of two outsiders discovering they had been exiles from the same country all along.

"That song," Tamsin said slowly, "isn't finished."

"No," Lior said. His voice had gone quiet and terrible. "And I think I know who cut it short. There are nine names in a bone downstairs, and a ledger upstairs that writes itself, and I think, Miss Tamsin who sings — I think we've both been keeping books for people who never intended to pay."

Under the floorboards, the humming stopped.

In the silence that followed, every lock in the building turned at once.
