## 3. What the Dead Charge for Answers

The humming did not stop when dawn came.

Lior had spent the night in the counting house with every lamp lit and the ledger locked in the iron chest beneath his desk, and still the sound came up through the floorboards — a boy's voice, thin and patient, working its way through a hymn Lior almost knew. Not the cathedral's version. Older. The melody bent where the modern hymn ran straight, dipping into intervals that made his back teeth ache, that made the lamp flames lean toward the floor as if bowing.

He had not slept. He had carved instead, because carving was what his hands did when his mind refused to hold still. A memorial ring for a widow who wanted her husband's last words set into the bone of his own finger — a common enough commission, though the words themselves had been uncommon: *tell her the debt was never hers.* Lior had cut each letter small and clean, and all the while the humming rose through the boards like water through chalk, and all the while he thought about the entry in his own handwriting that he had never written.

*The gods are awake. Payment due.*

He was a man who believed in accounts. Every trade in impossible things passed through his ledgers, and every ledger balanced, because Lior Thackeray did not permit imbalance. Souls bought and resold at a fair margin. Blessings brokered between dying saints and ambitious merchants. Relics authenticated by the dead themselves, who were, in his long experience, the only honest appraisers left in Vellum Row. He kept the books. He did not ask where the goods came from. That was the arrangement, and it had kept him fed and housed and — he had told himself — clean.

But a ledger that wrote to him unprompted was not a ledger. It was a summons.

When the bells of the Quiet Bell rang matins across the rooftops, Lior put down his knife, knelt on the cold boards of the counting house, and pressed his palm flat against the floor.

"I hear you," he said. "Come up properly or tell me how to come down."

The humming stopped.

The silence afterward was worse — the particular silence of something deciding. Then, from directly beneath his hand, a voice, dry as a page turning:

"You're the keeper."

"I keep the ledgers."

"You keep everything." A pause. "Even what you weren't meant to find."

Lior sat back on his heels. In forty years of hearing the dead — since the fever took his ears' ordinary deafness away at nine years old and left him open to the other traffic — he had learned their habits. They wanted things. Rest, mostly, or revenge, or one more hour in a body they missed. The clever ones wanted trades. And every trade had a price, because the dead understood economy better than any guildsman; they had nothing left to spend but what they'd carried out of life.

"Name yourself," he said.

"Tobias." The word came up faintly, apologetically. "Tobias Wren. I sang treble at the Quiet Bell. Before."

Before. The dead loved that word. It let them stand at the edge of their own ending without falling in.

"How long before, Tobias Wren?"

A silence with arithmetic in it. "Forty-one years. Give or take the ones I don't want to count."

Forty-one years. Lior felt the number settle into him like a stone dropped down a well. Forty-one years ago the Guild had built its first counting house on this very ground — he knew the charter dates the way he knew his own hands. Whatever lay under the floorboards had been here since the beginning.

"The entry in my book," Lior said. "Was that you?"

"No." Something in the voice went careful. "That was them. The ones whose voices are down here with me. They're getting louder now that they're awake, and loud things leak. Your book is old paper, carver. Old paper remembers what it's for." A dry rustle, like a smile. "You wrote it the way a river writes a shoreline. You just haven't done it yet."

Lior did not care for that sentence. He filed it away with the other sentences he didn't care for, in the drawer of his mind marked *pending*, and pressed his palm to the boards again.

"Then tell me about the voices. What voices? Whose?"

And the boy below him said, "The gods'. Who else would be worth a fortune this size?"

* * *

It took until noon to strike the bargain, because Tobias Wren had been a chorister and choristers learned patience the way other children learn walking.

The terms came up through the floor in pieces, each one tested, each one weighed. The dead boy would not simply give away what he knew — knowledge was the only coin he had left, and he had watched men spend it cheaply for forty-one years and regret it. But he was lonely, too. Lior could hear the loneliness underneath the haggling, the way you could hear the sea under a shell.

"You'll have to come down," Tobias said finally. "I can't shout it all through the boards. And there are things down here you'd need to see."

"Down where?"

"The cistern. Under the counting house. Where they put us."

*Us.* Lior turned that word over and found it heavier than it looked.

"And your price?" he asked, because there was always a price, and better to know it before the door opened than after. "Dead men charge. What do you charge?"

The silence went on so long that Lior checked whether the boy was still there — pressed his ear to the boards, heard the faint residual hum of him, a tuning fork someone had forgotten to still.

"My name," Tobias said at last. "Said properly. At my grave, if it's still marked, or anywhere, if it isn't. Nobody's said it in forty-one years, carver. Do you know what that's like? To be a name nobody says?" His voice cracked on a note that had once been treble-pure. "Say it once a year. That's all. That's cheap, and we both know it, and I'm taking advantage of you being kind, and I don't care."

"That's not cheap," Lior said quietly. "That's the most expensive thing anyone's asked me for in twenty years of this work."

He meant it. He had paid the dead in blood-lettings and burned letters, in confessions whispered over graves, in one terrible season in gold. No one had ever asked him only to remember them aloud. It occurred to him, with a lurch, that this was because no one had ever trusted him to remember anything at all.

"Done," he said. "Once a year, your name, spoken properly. Now show me the way down."

* * *

The way down was behind the coal cellar, through a door Lior had walked past ten thousand times and never once seen — which, he understood as his fingers found the seam of it, meant he had been *not seeing* it, professionally, for years. The Guild trained its keepers well. There were doors in every building that the mind agreed not to hold, and this had been one of his.

The stairs were bone-white stone worn hollow in the middle, and the air came up cold and sweetish, like a chapel in high summer. His lamp threw his shadow huge ahead of him, and the shadow trembled, and it took him three steps to realize the trembling wasn't his own hand. The walls were sweating light. Faint threads of it, gold and silver and one deep bruised violet, seeping through the mortar seams like roots through a grave.

The cistern opened around him — a vaulted chamber older than the Guild, older than the city's walls, its arches carved with names in a script that predated writing's manners. Nine alcoves ringed the flooded floor. In each alcove stood a rack, and on each rack sat cylinders of pale carved bone, hundreds of them, thousands, each one etched with a god-name in the old script and sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

They were singing. All of them, faintly, at the edge of hearing — not in unison but in counterpoint, a vast slow chord that had been sounding down here in the dark for forty-one years while the city above forgot what music was for.

Lior stood at the top of the last stair and felt the truth of it enter him the way cold water enters a boot.

"They're bottled," he said. His voice sounded obscene in that place, small and transactional. "Their voices. You bottled them."

"We recorded them," Tobias said, and now the boy was beside him — Lior saw him properly for the first time, a slight figure in a chorister's robe gone grey as ash, his face the face of a child drawn by someone who remembered him fondly but not recently. "That was the word they used. Recorded. Like it was accounting. The gods were going quiet anyway, they said. Fading. Better to preserve the voices than let them waste." His mouth twisted. "Only the gods weren't fading. They were *sleeping*. And a voice is how a god dreams. Take the voice and the god can't wake, can't answer, can't be prayed to. Take enough voices and—" he gestured at the racks, at the whole drowned golden dark, "—you get this. The founding fortune of the Vellum Guild, carver. Sold off in installments. A bishop here, a merchant prince there. Everyone who mattered got a piece of a god, and everyone who mattered kept quiet about the price."

Lior descended the last steps. The water on the floor was ankle-deep and warm, which was worse than cold. He walked the ring of alcoves slowly, reading the names, and the names read him back. *Vael-of-the-Tide. The Mother-of-Embers. He-Who-Counts-The-Stars-Twice.* Nine great presences, reduced to inventory. He thought of the ledger upstairs, of every balanced column, of the word *clean* he had used about himself, and felt it dissolve like sugar in that warm dark water.

"Why show me?" he asked. "Why me? Why now?"

"Because they're waking anyway." Tobias drifted alongside him, bare feet never quite touching the flood. "Bottled songs wear out, carver. The wax is failing. And when enough of them slip free, the gods will wake half-heard and furious, and they will come looking for an accounting." The boy looked up at him with eyes like wet ink. "We need a keeper. Someone who keeps true books. Someone the realms might trust to say what's owed."

"I'm a Guild man," Lior said. It came out flat. "I've kept their ledgers for twenty years. No realm trusts the Guild's own pen."

"Not yet," Tobias agreed pleasantly. "That's the trouble with truths. They cost before they pay."

* * *

They struck the second bargain at the foot of the ninth alcove, and this time Lior named no terms, because he already knew what the boy would ask, and he was right.

"There's a clause," Tobias said. "In the covenant. The original terms — they're carved on a femur in your own archive, third shelf, misfiled under livestock futures, because your predecessors had a sense of humor. Read it tonight. But the clause won't matter unless you understand what was taken. All of it. Every installment, every buyer, every year. And I can't just *tell* you that, carver. Telling slides off the living. It has to go in deeper."

"Then how?"

"A trade. The old kind." The boy's voice gentled, which was how Lior knew it would be bad. "You give me a memory. A real one, held close, load-bearing. One of the ones a person stands on. I'll carry it down here where memories keep, and in exchange the whole history goes into you — written in, not told. You'll know the Guild's founding the way you know your own hands."

Lior closed his eyes. He was not a sentimental man; sentiment was a luxury the ledger did not carry. But there was one memory he guarded the way misers guard the last coin, and he knew — the way you know a thing about yourself that you have never said aloud — that Tobias knew it too.

"My mother's face," he said.

"She'd want you to," the boy said softly, which was a lie, and a kind one, and Lior hated him for both.

He thought about refusing. He thought about the clause, whatever it was, and about finding another way, another dead man, another door. He thought about the entry in his own handwriting — *payment due* — and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the moment of signing, that the payment had been due since before he was born, that he had inherited the debt along with the pen, and that every year he had kept the Guild's clean books he had been drawing down against exactly this moment.

"All right," he said.

Tobias took his face in both small cold hands, and Lior had time to think: *brown eyes, flour on her cheekbone, she smelled of rosemary and rain,* and then—

—and then he was standing in warm ankle-deep water holding a complete and terrible history inside his skull, every installment, every buyer, every signature, and there was a hole in him shaped like a woman's face, and he could not remember what had filled it.

He knew he had loved her. The love remained, vast and directionless, like sunlight in an empty room. He knew she had sung to him. He could not remember the tune. He knew her name was Maren and that she had died when he was nine, and the name was all that was left, a label on an empty jar.

"Oh," he said. It was a very small word for such a large emptiness.

"Now you know what they took from the world," Tobias said, and his voice was gentle again, and Lior understood that the gentleness was real, that the boy had not enjoyed this, that kindness and cruelty could share a pair of small cold hands. "Every prayer that went unanswered. Every god who seemed to have abandoned us. Somebody sold the silence, carver, and called it preservation, and built a fortune on it, and your ledgers kept it tidy." He stepped back, growing fainter at the edges, the lamp light starting to pass through him. "Read the femur tonight. Third shelf. Livestock futures."

"Wait." Lior's voice cracked. "What was her face? Tell me what I gave you. Describe it, so I can—"

But the cistern held only the chord of the bottled gods, rising now, insistent, the wax seals on the nearest cylinder visibly softening, a thread of gold light escaping the crack like smoke from under a door.

Upstairs, in the iron chest, the ledger was waiting. And somewhere above that, in the ordinary morning noise of Vellum Row, a bell was ringing that Lior did not recognize — bright, young, unmistakably alive — a girl's voice carrying a thread of song straight through the streets toward the Guild quarter, toward the counting house, toward him.

The dead boy's last words hung in the warm dark, patient as interest accruing:

*She's coming early, keeper. Best decide what you're going to tell her.*
