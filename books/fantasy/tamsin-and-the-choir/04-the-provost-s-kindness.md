## 4. The Provost's Kindness

They came for her three days after the vespers incident, and they did not come with chains.

Tamsin was scrubbing the cathedral's west steps when the carriage stopped at the bottom — a real carriage, lacquered black with silver fittings, the kind that belonged to merchant princes and never, ever to the Cathedral of the Quiet Bell. The horses were white as altar candles. A footman in grey livery opened the door without a word, and out stepped a man she had only seen from the choir loft: Aldous Vane, Provost of the Vellum Guild.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined the Guild's master as something carved — all angles and appetite, a man made of ledger-lines. Instead he was soft-spoken and round-shouldered, with kind creases around his eyes and a coat the color of good tea. He climbed the steps slowly, as if his knees were old friends who complained, and stopped a respectful distance away.

"You're Tamsin," he said. "The one who sings."

Her hands went still in the wash-water. Word had spread, then. The sisters had whispered about it for days — how the girl's hymn had opened the sacristy door that Brother Anselm had triple-locked after the theft of the reliquary, how she'd stood there mortified while the lock simply *gave up*, like a dog rolling over. The Mother Superior had looked at her afterward with an expression Tamsin couldn't name. Not anger. Something more like fear wearing anger's clothes.

"I didn't mean anything by it," Tamsin said. "The door was—"

"Locked," Vane said pleasantly. "I know. That's rather the point." He gestured to the step beside him, and when she didn't move, he sat down himself, unhurried, his fine coat meeting the cold stone without complaint. "May I tell you what I see, child? I see an orphan girl in a hand-me-down cassock, scrubbing steps in the rain-cold of early morning, in a cathedral that has been quietly running out of money for eleven years. I see someone with a gift so rare that men have killed for less. And I see no one — *no one* — protecting her."

The words landed harder than they should have. Tamsin looked back at the cathedral doors, at the peeling paint on their lower panels, at the bell tower where the Quiet Bell itself hadn't rung in her lifetime.

"The Church provides," she said, but even she heard how thin it sounded.

"The Church provides what it has left." Vane's voice was gentle, almost apologetic, as though he regretted having to say true things. "I've read the ledgers, Tamsin. Both sets — theirs and ours. Your cathedral sold its reliquary lands to my Guild two decades ago to keep its roof on. We have been feeding forty orphans a day since. Did you know that? Every bowl of barley soup you've eaten since you were six years old came out of a Guild account."

She hadn't known. Or she'd known distantly, the way you know the sun is distant — a fact without weight. Now it settled onto her with all its mass.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'd like to offer you a place." He said it simply, no flourish, watching her with those kind creased eyes. "Patronage. Protection. Proper instruction for your voice — not hymns drilled into you by frightened clerics, but training worthy of what you are. Room of your own. Meals. Books." A small smile. "A family, of a sort. The Guild takes care of its own."

*The Guild takes care of its own.* The phrase wrapped around her like a blanket pulled from a warm press. Tamsin thought of the dormitory she shared with nine other girls, of the sister who docked her supper for humming, of the Mother Superior's frightened eyes. She thought of being eight years old and standing at the foundling gate while a clerk wrote her name in a book — *Tamsin, age approx. 6, no surname, voice noted* — and how the ink of that entry had felt like the whole of her identity, one line in someone else's ledger.

Nobody had ever offered to write her into a better book before.

"What would I have to do?" she asked. It was the practical question, the orphan's question, and she hated how quickly she asked it.

"Sing," Vane said. "Learn. Grow. Nothing more sinister than that, whatever the frightened brothers may whisper." He rose, knees cracking, and offered her his hand — dry, warm, ringed with a signet bearing the Guild's quill-and-scale. "Come see us. No vows, no contracts today. Just come and look. A girl should choose her future with her eyes open."

*With her eyes open.* Later — much later — she would understand exactly how carefully those words were chosen. But that morning, cold and kneeling on wet stone, she took his hand.

* * *

The Guild quarter was nothing like she'd imagined.

She had pictured vaults and shadows, bone-dust and locked doors — the stories the choirboys told each other after lights-out, about the counting house where they traded in impossible things. What she found instead was light. Long galleries with windows of real glass, courtyards with fountains, apprentices in clean wool laughing over their copying work. A library whose doors stood open to any member. A dining hall that smelled of roast fowl and fresh bread, where clerks and carvers ate together at long tables and argued cheerfully about interest rates.

"It funds itself," Vane said, catching her stare. "Trade in impossible things sounds like sorcery, but mostly it's insurance, salvage, and very clever accounting. The romance is cheaper than the reality." He walked her through it all himself, refusing his secretary's arm, pointing out the apprentice carvers' benches, the scriptorium, the infirmary. "We employ four hundred people in this quarter alone. Half of them came off the streets. You'll recognize the type."

She did. That was the terrible thing. The hollow-cheeked boy mending ledgers by the window had the same look she saw in the dormitory mirror. The woman teaching penmanship had foundling-calluses on her fingers, the same as hers.

At the top of the central tower — they climbed forever, and Vane climbed it with her, breathing hard but refusing to stop — he showed her the Hall of Records. Rows upon rows of ledgers, bound in vellum and leather, stretching into the lamplight like a congregation at prayer.

"This is what we truly keep," Vane said softly. "Not treasures. Debts and promises. Every covenant in this city, written down so that no one can pretend it was never made. Do you know what the world was like before record-keeping, Tamsin? It was a place where the strong simply said *it was always mine*, and everyone else had to believe them. We are the memory of the city. Memory is a holy work, whatever collar wears it."

From the tower windows she could see the whole city — the cathedral's grey bulk, the river, the slums beyond the bone-carvers' district where smoke stood up straight in the windless evening. It looked, from up here, like a thing that could be tended. Cared for. Ledged.

"Your voice," Vane said, beside her, "is like these books. It makes promises binding. Doors obey it because doors are just promises made of wood and iron — *you shall stay shut* — and your singing reminds them they can be otherwise. That is not a small gift. Left untrained, unprotected, it will be taken from you by someone with fewer scruples than me. And there are always people with fewer scruples than me, child. That is the first lesson of trade."

It sounded like wisdom. It sounded, God help her, like kindness.

* * *

They fed her that night in the Provost's private dining room, just the two of them, which she understood was an honor because the secretary's face flickered when Vane dismissed him. Quail, and pears in wine, and bread with a crust that crackled. Tamsin ate slowly, aware of her manners, aware of the years of barley soup, aware that she might cry if she wasn't careful.

Vane talked. That was mostly what he did — talked while she listened, and she realized somewhere between the second course and the third that this too was a kind of payment, that lonely powerful men bought company with food and attention, and that she didn't care, and hated herself for not caring.

He told her about the gods.

"They were tired," he said, turning his wineglass by its stem. "That's the truth nobody wants. Everyone loves a mystery — *where did the gods go, why did they fall silent* — because mysteries let people feel important. But the truth is banal. They were old, and the world changed, and they chose to withdraw. Gracefully. With terms."

"With terms?" Tamsin asked.

"Covenants. Contracts. Settlements." He smiled. "You may as well call them what they were. The gods sat down across the table from our founders, a hundred years ago and more, and negotiated their retirement. Their voices — their active presence in the world — were ceded over, in installments, in exchange for guarantees. Continuity. Care. The maintenance of their names, their feast-days, their places in the hymnal. My Guild has kept those terms faithfully for generations. We are not thieves, Tamsin. We are executors of an estate. When people whisper that we 'stole' the gods' voices—" he shook his head, sorrowful, reasonable, "—they are confusing inheritance with robbery. If your grandmother leaves you her house, are you a thief?"

"No," Tamsin said slowly.

"No. And yet." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something warmer, confiding. "There are people — frightened clerics, superstitious carvers — who want to undo that settlement. Who claim the gods are *awake again*, that the covenant can be *reforged*, that some singer might unbind everything my Guild has spent a century holding together. Do you understand what unbinding means? Not freedom. Chaos. Every promise in this city coming loose at once. Every debt called, every contract voided, every door open whether it should be or not." His eyes held hers, mild and steady. "Including yours, child. Including the little covenant that says the Guild feeds forty orphans a day. You'd unwrite yourself."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the Guild quarter murmured with evening life — hundreds of people, warm and fed and employed, all of it resting on the words of a man who spoke of covenants the way her Mother Superior spoke of grace.

"And me?" she asked. "What happens to me, if I come here?"

"You become what you were always meant to be." Vane said it without hesitation, without performance, and that was the most convincing thing of all. "The finest-trained voice in the city. Protected. Valued. *Kept* — in the good sense, the way a library keeps its books. And one day, perhaps, when you're ready, you'll help me renew the old settlements properly. A voice both realms trust. There are clauses, Tamsin, old ones, that need a living voice to speak them. I'd rather it were someone I trust. Someone of the Guild."

*A living voice that both realms trust.* The phrase snagged on something in her mind, a burr in wool, but she couldn't have said why, and the warmth in the room smoothed it flat before she could examine it.

"Can I think about it?" she whispered.

"Of course." He said it as if the answer were already known, as if the question were a formality, but kindly, always kindly. "Take a week. Take two. The offer doesn't expire; that's not how family works."

*Family.* The word went through her like a needle finding the one uncalloused place.

* * *

She walked back to the cathedral alone through streets gone blue with dusk, and the Quiet Bell tolled compline above her, and she tried to feel guilty and mostly felt warm instead.

In the dormitory, the other girls were asleep. Tamsin lay in her narrow bed and stared at the ceiling and turned the evening over, examining it for rot the way you check fruit. The Guild fed orphans. The Guild employed foundlings. The Provost had kind eyes and honest creases and had climbed six flights of stairs on bad knees to make a scrub-girl an offer no one else in her whole life had bothered to make.

And yet.

She thought of the sacristy door giving way beneath her hymn — not breaking, but *surrendering*, like something that had been waiting a long time to be asked. She thought of how the senior clergy flinched at certain verses, the old ones, the ones about the Nine who sang the world awake. She thought of the thread of song she'd followed into the Guild quarter weeks ago, faint and wrong, like a choir heard through a wall — and how, standing in the Hall of Records tonight, she had heard it again, thinner, coming from *below*, from under the foundations, many voices now, muffled and patient.

The gods were tired, Vane said. They had withdrawn willingly, with terms.

Then why, Tamsin wondered, staring into the dark, did the voices beneath the Guild sound less like retirees and more like prisoners?

Why did they hum the way the foundlings used to hum, back at the orphanage, in the months before they stopped hoping anyone would come?

She slept badly, and dreamed of doors, and in the morning there was a note waiting for her at the warden's desk, sealed with the quill-and-scale, inviting her to return Sunday next — and tucked inside it, folded small, a single sheet of vellum on which someone had written, in a hand that trembled like an old man's or a frightened one's:

*Ask him what the installments were paid IN. Ask him why the payments stopped.*

There was no signature. But beneath the words, pressed faintly into the paper as if by a seal laid and lifted quickly, was the mark of a small carved bone.
