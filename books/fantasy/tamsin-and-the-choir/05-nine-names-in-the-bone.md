## 5. Nine Names in the Bone

The ledger would not let me sleep.

I had tried. I had banked my fire, shuttered the window against the Guild quarter's lamplight, lain down on the narrow bed above the counting house with my hands folded like a man at his own funeral. Sleep came the way it always comes to me — reluctantly, and never alone. The dead choirboy hummed beneath the floorboards, a thin thread of melody rising through the joists like damp through plaster.

*They are awake,* he had told me, or rather he had made me pay to hear it, and I was still deciding whether the price — a memory I could no longer name but felt the shape of, the way an amputee feels a hand — had been worth it. *The voices were sold. In installments. Over forty years. You keep their receipts.*

So I got up. I lit one candle, the stub I saved for work that shamed me, and I carried the ledger to my bench, and I opened it to the entry that had written itself in my own hand:

*The gods are awake. Payment due.*

A bookkeeper's first instinct is arithmetic. If the entry existed, something had been entered against it. Debits want credits; every debt in the Vellum Guild's ledgers has a counterweight somewhere, a matching line in another hand, another column, another world. I turned back through the pages looking for the other side of that terrible sum.

Forty years of entries slid beneath my thumb. Trades in impossible things: a widow's grief exchanged for three frost-free winters; a hanged man's last word sold to a playwright with more ambition than conscience; bottled luck, secondhand blessings, a saint's patience decanted into a phial and priced by the dram. My predecessors' hands grew older down the page — Quill-master Hesse's cramped italics, then Master Odile's broad merchant's script, then, forty pages deep, the founding hand itself: Provost Corvane Aldery, whose signature still crowned the Guild charter, whose portrait still hung in the counting house with eyes that followed you the way portraits do when they have something to answer for.

And there, in Aldery's confident strokes, an entry dated the year of the Great Silence:

*Received: nine voices, divine, per contract. Consideration rendered. Storage per Schedule K.*

Schedule K. I had catalogued the Guild's schedules once, as an apprentice, all the way from A to J. There was no Schedule K. There had never been a Schedule K. Which meant either that it had been destroyed, or that it had never been paper at all.

I sat very still and listened to the house. Rain on the slates. The candle eating its wick. Beneath everything, faint as a pulse under linen, the boy's humming.

"Schedule K," I said aloud, to the floorboards. "Where do you keep it?"

The humming stopped. Then, in that voice like a wet finger drawn around a glass rim: *You already know. You've held it.*

I looked down at my own hands, ink-stained, knife-scarred, the hands of a man who had spent twenty years carving prayers into bone for clients who wanted their dead remembered. And I thought of the bone store below — the long room where the Guild kept its raw stock, femurs and ribs and skulls in oiled crates, waiting for carvers' commissions. I thought of the crate marked with Aldery's private seal that no apprentice was permitted to touch, that even the provosts stepped around as though it were a sleeping dog.

I took the candle and went down.

* * *

The bone store smells of tallow and cold stone, a smell I have loved all my life the way sailors love tar. Tonight it smelled of a grave opened early.

Aldery's crate sat in its corner under forty years of dust, the seal unbroken, wax gone the color of old teeth. I broke it with my thumbnail because I could not find a better tool and did not trust myself to leave and come back with one. Cowardice, perhaps. Or the instinct of a man who knows that once a thing is done it cannot be entered as undone.

Inside, packed in wool gone grey with age, lay a single human femur.

Not cow, not ox, not any of the honest stock we carve. Human, and old, and dense as riverstone, and carved over every inch of its surface with writing so fine I had to hold it to the candle and turn it slowly, letting the light rake across the grooves, before the letters resolved. Not ink. Carved. Cut into the bone while it was green, I judged, before it dried and hardened — cut by someone who knew that bone remembers what paper forgets.

Nine names.

I will set them down here exactly as I read them, though setting them down felt like handling live coals: **Vessia-of-the-Tides. The Horned Cartographer. Mother-of-Embers. The Quiet Bell. Ash-Who-Listens. The Ninefold Shepherd. Grandmother Winter. The Lantern Swallower. He-Who-Waits-Below.**

Nine names. Nine voices, received per contract. And beneath each name, terms — the covenant's original articles, pressed into the marrow-space of a dead man's leg like a deed into a wall. I read them twice, three times, my lips moving though I made no sound, because some part of me understood that these words were not meant to be spoken carelessly, not anymore, not since the voices they belonged to had been sold out from under them.

*Article the First:* the gods shall walk veiled among mortals, and mortals shall hear them in dream and prayer, and neither realm shall close its door to the other.

*Article the Fourth:* the Guild of Vellum shall serve as steward of the border, keeper of the crossings, taking tithe but never toll of souls.

And then, near the end, in letters cut deeper than all the rest, as if the carver had leaned his whole weight into them:

*Article the Ninth: this covenant endures only while it is renewed each generation, and it may be renewed only by a living voice that both realms trust, speaking the nine names whole. Should such a voice fail to speak, the covenant sleeps — but does not die. It waits for the voice to wake.*

I stood in the cold with a dead man's thigh-bone in my hands and understood, finally, what the Great Silence had been. Not abandonment. Not wrath. A covenant without its speaker, folding itself up like a letter no one would carry — the gods going quiet not because they chose silence but because the law of their own binding required a throat, and someone had made sure no throat remained.

Someone had harvested the voices. Someone had hidden the terms in bone, behind a seal, in a cellar, and called it storage. Forty years of prayers falling on deaf heaven, and the deafness had a ledger line. *Consideration rendered.* The Guild's founding fortune, paid out in installments, in stolen divinity.

The candle guttered. Above me, through the floorboards, the boy began to hum again — and this time I heard it properly, heard the structure under the sweetness, and my blood went cold in a way the cellar's chill could not account for.

It was not a random tune. It was a hymn. One of the cathedral's vespers verses, the old setting, the one the senior clergy flinched from — I had heard it drifting from the Cathedral of the Quiet Bell on my errands, had noted it idly the way I note everything, because noting things is what I am for. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that I knew what to listen for, ran the cadence of the first of the nine names. *Vessia-of-the-Tides*, sung small, sung young, sung by a voice that did not know what it was carrying.

One of the Quiet Bell's choir. A child singing a god's name without knowing it, the way you might carry a spark inside your sleeve without feeling the burn yet.

There was only one singer the dead spoke of lately. Only one voice the floorboards themselves seemed to lean toward. The girl — Tamsin, the carvers' children called her, the orphan who sang to locked doors and found them open — she had collided with me in the Guild quarter two nights past, all wide eyes and wariness, hearing the same forbidden music I fled. I had thought her a curiosity. A fellow listener. A coincidence.

I did not believe in coincidences. I was, professionally, incapable of it. Every entry balances; every effect has its line of cause. A girl born with a voice that both realms trusted was not chance. She was Article the Ninth walking around in a chorister's robe, the renewal clause given lungs, and she was singing the names already, half-asleep, the way a lock picks itself if the key is worn enough.

The covenant was waking up. The ledger had known before I did. *The gods are awake. Payment due.* Payment due — not from me. Through me. Through her.

And here was the arithmetic I could not make kind: the moment I told her what she was, I would paint the Guild's mark on her forehead. Provost Vane was not Aldery; he was worse, because Aldery had stolen from gods who could not protest, and Vane would steal from a child who could. The harvesters kept their knives sharp. The moment the provost understood that a living voice existed which could renew the covenant — and end the Guild's forty-year monopoly on the silence — she would be taken, bottled, shelved in whatever vault held Schedule K's consideration, filed under *assets*. Her voice sold off in installments, verse by verse, until nothing remained of Tamsin but a body that hummed when the wind moved through it.

Tell her, and I signed her death warrant in my own neat hand. Stay silent, and the covenant slept forever, and the dead went on humming under floorboards, unheard, unpaid, unquiet.

I wrapped the femur in the grey wool and carried it back to my bench, and I sat with it between my hands like a thing newly born and already mortal, and I thought: *there must be a third column. There is always a third column.* But my candle burned low, and the boy's hymn rose through the boards, patient as water finding a crack, and far across the sleeping city, in the Cathedral of the Quiet Bell, I knew — the way a bookkeeper knows when a figure is wrong before he can say why — that someone else had begun reading the same accounts.

At dawn, a note came up through my shutters tied to a stone, unsigned, in a clerk's trained hand:

*The provost asks after the choirgirl. Bring the bone to the Vault, or we come for her instead.*

Payment due.
