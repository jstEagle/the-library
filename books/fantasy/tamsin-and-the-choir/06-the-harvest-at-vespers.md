## 6. The Harvest at Vespers

The Cathedral of the Quiet Bell smelled of tallow and old stone on the evening they came for her voice.

Tamsin felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in the air, the way the nave held its breath. The vespers crowd was thin, as it always was on third-days: a handful of widows, two merchants' clerks dozing through the litany, a beggar who slept more devoutly than he prayed. Candlelight pooled in the hollows of the pillars. Above, in the choir loft, the other girls were arranging themselves for the opening hymn, and Sister Mairelle stood among them with her hands folded, her gray braid coiled at her nape like something patient.

*Sing low tonight,* the dead boy had told her that morning, crouched in the cloister shadows where only she could see him. He had a way of appearing when the light went thin — a small figure in a chorister's robe gone gray as ash, his face sweet and terrible, his throat marked with a thin dark line Tamsin tried not to look at. *Sing low, and sing short. They are counting your breaths.*

"Who is?" she'd asked.

"The men with wax in their ears," he said. "They've been standing outside the cathedral since dawn. I counted nine. Nine is a bad number, Tamsin. Nine is how many it takes."

She hadn't asked *nine what*. She'd been afraid the answer would be one of the things she already half-knew: that her voice opened doors, that certain verses made the provost's smile go rigid at its edges, that the hymns she loved best were hymns the senior clergy flinched from like men touching a burn. She had spent three days telling herself that Lior Thackeray — the bone-carver with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a man reading his own funeral — had been mistaken about what she was. That Provost Vane's kindness was kindness. That the warm rooms he'd offered her, the patronage, the promise that she would never go hungry again, were simply what goodness looked like when it had money.

She put on her surplice. She took her place in the loft. She did not sing low.

It was pride, maybe. Or defiance. Or the simple animal stubbornness of a girl who had been hungry too long to make herself small again. The organist struck the first chord, and Tamsin opened her mouth, and the opening verse of the *Canticle of the Unbound* rose out of her — full-voiced, unguarded, the way Sister Mairelle had taught her to let a note stand up straight and own the room.

The candles guttered. All of them, all at once, though there was no draft.

And beneath the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the dead boy began to hum along — urgent now, off-melody, bending her tune into a warning shape. *Stop. Stop. Stop.*

Tamsin faltered. The note wavered, thinned—

—and the great western doors of the cathedral swung open without being touched.

Nine men entered. They wore the plain gray of Guild factors, and their faces were the worst thing about them: not cruel, not eager, merely attentive, the faces of men come to collect a debt. Their ears were stoppered with pale plugs of carved bone. At their center walked a woman in a harvest-master's sash, carrying a case of black lacquer that she set down on the flagstones with the care of a midwife laying out instruments.

The congregation stirred. The dozing clerks woke. One of the widows began to say something about sacrilege, and then stopped, because the harvest-master looked at her, briefly, the way one looks at weather.

Sister Mairelle came down from the loft like a banner unfurling. She planted herself between the doors and the nave, small and iron-spined, and her voice carried to every corner without effort. "This is God's house. Whatever debt you imagine is owed, you will name it to me, and you will name it outside these walls."

"We're not here for the church, Sister." The harvest-master's voice was mild, almost apologetic. "We're here for an asset. A voice, unsworn and unregistered. The Guild has first claim under charter law. We have the writ." She produced a scroll sealed in green wax. "You may verify it with your bishop, if you like. He countersigned it Tuesday."

Tamsin watched the words land on Sister Mairelle like stones dropped into still water. Watched the older woman's shoulders take them. And in the space of that watching, everything rearranged itself: Vane's warmth, Vane's protection, Vane's gentle explanations about gods who had left willingly — all of it tipping over inside her into a new and terrible shape. *He countersigned it.* The comfort had never been comfort. It had been a pen, and she had been the paper, waiting to be written on.

*Tamsin.* The dead boy was beside her now in the loft, close enough that his cold came through her surplice. His humming had stopped; his mouth barely moved. *The side door. The little one by the reliquary stair. It's locked with a song, and you're the key.*

"I don't know any locking songs," she breathed.

"You know all of them. You just don't know you know them. Sing the second verse backward. Slowly. And whatever happens—" his gray face turned toward the nave, toward Sister Mairelle standing alone against nine men, "—whatever happens, don't stop until the door opens."

Below, the harvest-master was speaking again, patient as arithmetic. "The girl will come with us quietly, or the writ permits us reasonable force. I'd rather she came quietly. Voices travel badly when they're frightened."

"They are not taking her." Sister Mairelle's hands had come up, not in surrender but in the old choir-mistress's gesture — the one that said *breathe from here, stand up straight, you are stronger than you think.* "Over my body, madam, and I have buried two husbands and a war, so I am very hard to move."

Something in Tamsin's chest cracked open.

She slipped from the loft while every eye below was fixed on the standoff, down the narrow spiral of the reliquary stair, her hand trailing the cold stone. The little door waited at the bottom — iron-banded oak, older than the cathedral around it, its lock a disc of greenish metal worked with nine small figures that might have been dancers or might have been drowning. It had been locked her whole life. It had probably been locked longer than her whole life.

Above her, muffled by stone, she heard the harvest-master sigh. Heard boots moving. Heard Sister Mairelle say, in a voice gone suddenly fierce and young, "*Now, child—*" and then the sound of a scuffle, and a case of black lacquer clicking open.

Tamsin pressed her palm flat against the door and sang.

Backward. Slowly. The second verse of the *Canticle*, each syllable reversed, each note walked back down the stairs it had climbed. It felt like unswallowing. It felt like pulling thread out of cloth. The nine figures on the lock began — she was nearly sure, she would swear it ever after — to turn, slowly, like dancers changing partners. The greenish metal warmed under her hand. Somewhere behind the door, something vast shifted its weight, the way a sleeping animal shifts, and a smell came through the seams of the iron: rain on far hills, myrrh, the inside of a bell.

The bolt drew back with a sound like a held breath released.

Behind her, above her, Sister Mairelle cried out — not in pain, Tamsin would tell herself later, not exactly, but in the particular outrage of a woman being handled — and then the cry cut off into something worse: silence, and the click of the lacquer case closing, and the harvest-master's mild voice saying, "That will hold her well enough. Wax keeps. Register the substitution; the writ covers any unsworn voice in this building."

*Substitution.*

Tamsin stood in the doorway with cold night air moving past her, out of somewhere deep and old, stirring her hair. Through the door she could see nothing but darkness going down — steps, maybe, or the edge of some country that had no steps. Behind her, above her, the cathedral was already emptying of the thing she loved most. They had taken Sister Mairelle because Tamsin had run. Because Tamsin had sung. Because the writ covered *any unsworn voice*, and a mentor's voice, it seemed, weighed the same as a girl's.

The dead boy appeared beside her, gray in the doorway's gloom. He didn't look at her. He looked back the way she'd come, at the stairs, at everything happening above them, and his small ruined throat moved as if he were swallowing something bitter.

"Now you know what they are," he said quietly. "Go find the bone-carver. He owes the dead answers, and you owe the living a rescue. And Tamsin—" he finally turned those sweet, terrible eyes on her, "—don't blame yourself louder than you blame them. That's how they win twice."

She went through the door. The dark took her gently, smelling of rain on far hills.

But she carried the sound with her — the click of the lacquer case, the word *substitution*, the memory of Sister Mairelle's hands raised in the gesture that meant *you are stronger than you think* — and by the time the dark became a passage and the passage became the Guild quarter's undersides, the guilt had burned itself down to its purest element, which was fury.

Somewhere ahead, in a slum of bone-carvers and lamp-black, a man with ink-stained fingers owed her the truth.

She meant to collect it tonight.
