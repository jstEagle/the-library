## 7. Exiles' Arithmetic

The bone-carver's slum smelled of chalk dust and boiling glue, and Lior had forgotten how much he loved it.

They had come to it by way of the tannery drains, Tamsin's choir robes traded for a fishwife's shawl that made her look twelve instead of seventeen, and now they sat in the workshop of a man called Dree Halloway, who owed Lior three favors and asked no questions about the fourth. The room was low-ceilinged and warm, lit by a single lamp of rendered fat, and every surface was covered in the pale debris of the trade: knucklebones sorted into cups, half-carved prayer beads, a ribcage strung with chimes that clicked when the draft came under the door. Outside, the slum went about its business — knives on bone, the wet rasp of saws, someone singing a work-song in a language older than the Guild's charter.

Tamsin sat with her hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea she hadn't touched. She hadn't spoken since the cathedral. Since Brother Ellery.

"They'll move him to the Vault," Lior said, because silence was worse and because it was true. "If they haven't already."

"I know." Her voice came out flat as slate. "I keep hearing him sing the descant. He always gave me the descant, even when I was too young for it. And I ran."

"You ran because running was correct."

"Brother Ellery would not have run."

Lior set down his knife. "No," he said. "He wouldn't have. That's why they took him and not you."

She looked at him then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and very young, and Lior understood that whatever he said next would either hold them together or scatter them like spilled beads. So he did what he always did. He reached for the ledger in his mind and began to count.

* * *

"Three paths," he said, and pulled a scrap of vellum toward him — real vellum, calfskin, from Dree's offcut bin — and began to write. "We should be honest about all of them before we choose one."

"You're going to make a list."

"I'm going to make an accounting. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

He ignored her and wrote. "Path one: we run. North road, or the river barges, out past the tollhouses where the Guild's writ thins. We're both alive tonight; we can both be alive in ten years. Cost—" and here his pen slowed, because the cost was the whole argument, wasn't it, laid out plain— "cost is Brother Ellery stays in the Vault until they've wrung him dry, and the gods stay voiceless, and Vane keeps everything. Every soul who ever prayed into the dark and got no answer keeps getting no answer. Forever."

Tamsin said nothing. He wrote on.

"Path two: we bargain. Vane offered me terms once already — through an intermediary, before the harvest went wrong. He wants the ledger decoded and delivered. In exchange: Ellery walks free, you get a place in the Guild choir with your voice legally protected, I get my mother's memory back." He heard how it sounded even as he said it. He said it anyway. "Cost is we hand him the nine names, which means handing him the covenant, which means the theft becomes permanent and lawful and no one ever knows. The dead stay sold. But Ellery comes home."

"And path three?"

Lior turned the vellum over. On the back, in smaller letters, he wrote: *The Vault.*

"The Vault sits under the Provost's house. Three locks, each keyed to a different master — Vane holds two keys, the third is held by the Warden of Accounts, who has not left her rooms in eleven years. Inside are the voices. All of them. The god-songs in their wax cylinders, and everything else they've bottled over the decades — including, if the ledger's right, the voice of a boy named Tammas Reeve, who died in the Guild's care forty years ago and whose ghost has been humming under my floorboards long enough to teach me where the bodies are buried." He tapped the page. "We break in. We free them. The covenant reforges itself around a living voice that both realms trust, and there is exactly one of those in this room."

"And the cost?"

"The cost," Lior said, "is that I don't know. That's what makes it path three. Path one costs everything except us. Path two costs everyone except us. Path three—" he set down the pen— "path three probably costs us. But it's the only one where the arithmetic comes out honest."

Tamsin pushed the tea away and stood up. A chime-strung ribcage swayed where her shoulder brushed it, giving off a small dry music, and Lior thought: *even the bones are listening to her.*

"Do you know what I learned in the cathedral?" she said. "Not the hymns. The other thing. The thing they teach the orphans first, before anything else, because it keeps us quiet." She turned to face him. "I learned that some people count you. You're a mouth to feed, a voice to train, a line in somebody's book. And the ones who count you will always tell you it's for your own good. The Provost counted me so kindly, Lior. He counted me like family."

"Tamsin—"

"No. You've had your turn at the ledger." Her voice didn't rise; that was the terrible thing about it, it stayed level as a psalm tone. "You want to weigh Brother Ellery against the world. You want to weigh my voice against his freedom. But people aren't entries. When you do that math, you always find a reason why the answer is the one you were going to pick anyway. Your path two isn't arithmetic. It's just fear wearing a nice coat."

"It's also the only path where he lives."

"Is it?" She stepped closer. "Do you actually believe Vane returns prisoners? Do you believe a man who bottles gods honors a contract with a bone-carver and a runaway? You've read the ledgers. Has Vane ever once paid what he promised?"

Lior opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the answer was in his own handwriting, decades of it, entries upon entries of consideration received and consideration deferred, and the Guild's founding genius had been to discover that a debt never collected could still be recorded as paid.

"He'd give Ellery back," Lior said quietly. "Broken. Or hollowed. Or carrying something inside him that used to be a voice. But he'd give him back, because a returned hostage buys more loyalty than a dead one."

"There," Tamsin said. "That's the truest thing you've said all night, and you had to dig past three lies to reach it." She sat back down across from him, and for a moment neither spoke, and the work-song outside rose and fell like breathing.

* * *

It cracked between them near midnight, and it cracked over the smallest thing.

Dree Halloway brought bread and salt fish and the news that Guild riders were asking after a girl at the tannery gates, and after he'd gone Tamsin said, "We should send word to the cathedral. To the other choristers. If the harvesters come back—"

"With what?" Lior said. "A warning? Signed how? Any message that reaches the cathedral reaches Vane first. You'd be painting targets on children."

"Better than letting them stand in the dark not knowing!"

"Knowing doesn't stop a harvester. Knowing just means they see it coming." He heard his own voice go cold and precise, the counting-house voice, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. "I have spent thirty years learning exactly how much a life weighs against an outcome. It's a vile skill and I'm excellent at it, and right now it is the only thing standing between you and a rope."

"Then put it down." She was crying now, silently, furiously, the way orphans cry — no performance in it, all engine. "Put the skill down for one hour. One. And tell me what you actually want, not what you calculate."

And Lior Thackeray, keeper of the Vellum Guild's ledgers, carver of bones, hearer of the dead, found that when he reached for the answer, the ledger in his mind opened to a blank page.

What he wanted. Not what was owed, not what was prudent, not what the columns balanced to. What he *wanted*.

"I want," he said slowly, "to walk into the Provost's house and take every bottle off every shelf and break them on the floor. I want the gods loud enough that no guild, no charter, no magistrate can ever quiet them again. I want Vane to hear what he's been selling." His hands had closed into fists without his permission. "And I want Brother Ellery back, and I want my mother's face, and I want those last two things so badly I would trade the first two for them, and that is exactly why I can never be trusted to decide alone."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the dead boy hummed — Tammas Reeve, patient as sediment — and the sound came up through the floorboards and through the soles of their feet, a note that was almost a hymn.

Tamsin wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Say that last part again."

"That I can't be trusted alone?"

"That you'd trade the world for your mother's face. And that you know it." She studied him the way she studied a new piece of music, hunting for the false note. "Vane offered you that. Memory. And you told me about it like it was a line item."

"Because if I let myself feel it, I'd take his offer. I know that about myself the way you know a key signature."

"So we watch each other." She leaned forward. "That's the bargain, then. Not yours, not mine. You keep me from doing something brave and stupid, and I keep you from doing something safe and wicked. And when the moment comes — in the Vault, whenever it comes — and one of us reaches for the trade, the other one says no. Out loud. No matter what it costs."

Lior looked at her across the lamp-lit debris of his old trade: this girl who sang to locked doors, who had lost her mentor to a trap set for her, who refused to do arithmetic with lives and was therefore, he suspected, the only person alive fit to carry the covenant.

"It will cost," he said. "Whatever we choose, it will cost more than we have."

"Then we'll owe it," Tamsin said simply. "Together. That's what a covenant *is*, Lior. Nobody ever told you?"

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low. Lior drew the Vault from memory — he had carried its contents in his ledgers for years without ever standing inside it, and the irony of that was not lost on him. Three locks. The Warden of Accounts, who had not left her rooms in eleven years, and whom the ledger showed receiving, every quarter-day, a single payment: *one memory, unspecified.* She was being paid in the same coin they paid the gods. She might be the most hollowed woman in the city, or the only one who remembered why it mattered.

"We'll need her key," Lior said. "Or her absence."

"Or her song," Tamsin murmured, half-asleep against the wall, and the dead boy under the floorboards hummed agreement, and Lior sat sharpening his carving knife by lamplight and understood, with the calm of a man reading his own final entry, that tomorrow night they would go to the Provost's house.

Outside, far off toward the cathedral hill, a bell began to ring — not the Quiet Bell, but one of the others, urgent and wrong, tolling at an hour when bells should sleep.

Lior counted the strokes. Nine.

Then, after a pause, a tenth — a note no bell in the city owned, deep enough to feel in the teeth, ringing from somewhere beneath the earth.

The dead boy stopped humming.

"They've moved him," Lior said, and stood, and reached for his coat. "They've taken Ellery to the Vault tonight. Whatever Vane is afraid of, he's afraid of it *now*."
