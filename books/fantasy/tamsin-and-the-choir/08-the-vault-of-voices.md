## 8. The Vault of Voices

**Lior**

The Vault of Voices sat beneath the Guild quarter like a heart beneath a ribcage, and Lior had spent eleven years walking over it without knowing.

Now he knew. The dead choirboy — Pip, his name had been Pip, though the boy had forgotten it himself until Tamsin sang it back to him — had led them down through the bone-carver's slum, through a drainage culvert that smelled of tallow and old rain, to a door that was not a door. It was a slab of femur, polished white as chapel marble, taller than two men, and it had no handle because nothing inside was meant to come out.

Tamsin put her palm flat against it and hummed three notes.

The bone shivered. Somewhere beyond it, something answered — a low, many-throated sound like wind moving through a forest of hollow reeds, like a congregation breathing in before the first word of a hymn. Lior felt the hair on his arms lift. He had heard the dead all his life; he had thought himself past being frightened of voices.

He had been wrong.

"That's them," Tamsin whispered. Her hand stayed on the door. "All of them. They're still *singing*, Lior. After everything, they're still—"

"Save the song," he said, and hated how rough his voice came out. "Sing when we're inside."

The slab swung inward without a sound, which was somehow worse than grinding stone. Beyond lay darkness that swallowed their lantern light whole, and cold — not the cold of cellars but the cold of high places, of altitude, as if they had descended into the earth only to find themselves standing on a mountain's shoulder under a night sky with no stars in it.

Lior stepped through first. He told himself it was because he knew the Guild's ways better than she did. He told himself a great many things that night, and believed almost none of them.

* * *

**Tamsin**

The Vault was a cathedral built by people who had never loved anything.

That was her first thought, standing in the vast dark while Lior's lantern found the walls: no icons, no windows, no warmth. Just shelves. Rows upon rows of them, rising into blackness, and on every shelf, cylinders of carved bone sealed at each end with pale wax — thousands of them, tens of thousands, arranged with the loving precision of a counting house ledger.

She walked to the nearest shelf and lifted one cylinder. It was warm. That was the thing that undid her — not the size of the theft, not the industry of it, but the warmth. Whatever slept inside that bone had not gone cold. It had waited thirty years in the dark, patient as seed-corn, still alive.

She held it to her ear the way a child holds a seashell.

A voice poured into her. Not words — weather. A voice like summer rain on a hot road, like the smell of cut grass, like every green and growing thing given speech. It sang one line of some song older than any hymn in her cathedral, and then the wax seal dimmed, and the voice folded itself away again, courteous even in captivity.

Tamsin stood very still, holding the cylinder against her sternum, and wept without making any sound at all. She had spent her whole life singing to locked doors. She had never once imagined what it would be to hear a door sing back.

"They can hear you," Lior said quietly behind her. His lantern showed him pale, hollow-eyed, his scarred hands steady around the light. "The whole Vault. I can feel them turning toward you like — like flowers."

"Then let's not keep them waiting."

She set the cylinder back gently, the way you set down something sleeping, and turned to face the center of the Vault, where a single dais rose from the floor, and on the dais—

On the dais stood Provost Aldous Vane, hands clasped behind his back, dressed not in Guild grey but in plain travelling wool, as if he had expected to be taking a walk and not receiving thieves.

"Ah," he said warmly. "There you are."

* * *

**Lior**

Lior had rehearsed this moment in a hundred variations during the long climb down, and in none of them did Vane look *glad* to see them.

"You're early," Vane said, descending the dais steps without hurry. Up close, in lantern light, the Provost looked older than he had in his counting house — the kindness around his eyes thinner, more obviously painted on. "I'd budgeted another hour for your courage to fail. It's gratifying to be wrong about arithmetic for once."

"The harvesters," Lior said. "At the cathedral. Your men took Sister Maerin."

"And released her an hour ago, unharmed, with my apologies and a purse she'll pretend to refuse." Vane spread his hands. "I don't need hostages, Thackeray. Hostages are for men who've lost the argument. I have something better." He turned to Tamsin, and his voice went soft and paternal, the voice he must have used on forty orphans before her. "Child, do you know why the gods left?"

"They didn't leave," Tamsin said. "You sold them."

"I *bought* what was already leaving," Vane said, gentle as a physician. "The covenant was dying. The old gods were fading whether we bottled them or not — ask any historian, any priest. We kept their last songs safe. We kept them *alive*. And yes, we charged for the privilege, because preservation is not free. Someone had to pay the carvers. Someone had to pay the wardens." He gestured at the endless shelves with something close to reverence. "This is not a vault, girl. This is an ark."

"It's a cage," Tamsin said.

"It is a *business*," Vane said, and for one unguarded instant the warmth dropped away entirely, and Lior saw the thing underneath: not cruelty, exactly. Certainty. The terrible calm of a man who has done monstrous arithmetic so long the numbers feel like virtue. Then the warmth returned, seamless, and Vane turned to Lior.

"But you didn't come for a debate. You came for the truth, and I am famously reasonable about truth. So here is mine, offered freely." He reached into his coat and drew out a small case of dark leather, and opened it, and inside, nested in velvet, lay a single wax-sealed cylinder of carved bone. "Your mother's memory, Thackeray. Recovered from the estate of the warden who processed it. Her face. Her voice saying your name. Every detail you traded to a dead boy in a flooded stairwell — restored, whole, yours again."

The Vault went very quiet. Even the shelves seemed to lean in.

Lior looked at the cylinder and felt the shape of the hole inside himself with perfect clarity for the first time in days. He knew his mother had brown eyes. He knew she had died in the fever year, that she had worked thread until her fingers bled, that she had once, impossibly, danced in the kitchen. But her face — the actual face, the living arrangement of her features — was a room with the furniture removed. He could stand in it forever and touch nothing.

"All you must do," Vane said, "is walk away. Take your memory, take the girl if you like, take a Guild pension that will make the pensioners of three kingdoms weep with envy. Walk out that door and never speak of what's down here. In exchange—" he nodded at the shelves, at the ark, at the business, "—the world keeps its stability. Kingdoms run on these voices, Thackeray. Treaties were sworn to them. Tear the Vault open and you don't free gods. You detonate thirty years of contracts written in divine ink. Wars, boy. Famine. The collapse of every covenant law depends upon. Is your *truth* worth that?"

And there it was. Not a bribe — an invoice. Vane had simply shown him the price column and invited him to sign.

Lior thought of his mother dancing in the kitchen. He thought of her face, that bright absent room. He thought of Pip, dead at nine years old, whose name had been sold off in installment four and whose ghost had spent decades humming under floorboards because no one would pay attention long enough to learn it.

He closed the case in Vane's hands.

"No," Lior said.

Vane blinked. "Thackeray—"

"You want me to do arithmetic." Lior's voice came out level, which surprised him; underneath the levelness was a roar. "One memory, weighed against ten thousand stolen songs. One man's comfort against a choirboy who died owing a debt he never incurred. I've kept ledgers my whole life, Provost. I know a false entry when I'm asked to write one." He stepped back, putting himself between Vane and Tamsin, and heard his own heart going like a hammer on a nail. "My mother raised me on stories where people did the right thing badly. She never once told me a story where they did the wrong thing comfortably. I'll keep the hole. It's honest."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Vane's eyes that was not certainty and not warmth. Something almost like grief, or envy, or the memory of a man he might have been before the arithmetic ate him.

Then he sighed, and rang a small silver bell he had carried all along, and the alarms began.

* * *

**Tamsin**

Bells above, boots below, and the great bone door beginning to grind shut somewhere far behind them — Tamsin registered all of it the way you register weather while planting a field. There was work to do.

She climbed the dais. At its summit stood a single pedestal of black wood, and on the pedestal, a cylinder larger than all the rest, its bone carved with nine names in a script that predated the alphabet, its wax seals layered thick as tree rings. The master cylinder. The covenant's original terms, sung by nine throats and bound by a tenth.

The living tenth. Her.

She picked it up. It was warm as a sleeping bird.

"Tamsin." Lior had reached the base of the dais, knife drawn, positioning himself between her and the stairs where torchlight was already boiling upward. Blood ran from a split in his knuckles where he'd struck the first harvester; he didn't seem to have noticed. "Whenever you're ready. Take whatever time you—"

She began to sing.

Not a hymn. Hymns were cages too, pretty ones. She sang the first of the nine names, raw and unaccompanied, and the cylinder in her hands *ignited* — not with fire but with response, the wax seals cracking one by one like ice going out on a river in spring. Down the endless shelves, ten thousand cylinders began to glow and tremble and sing back, a chord building beneath the earth that had no bottom to it, that rose through the bedrock itself, that made the Vault's black ceiling shed dust like snow.

The first god's voice came out of its bottle like dawn coming out of night.

Somewhere above them, the Cathedral of the Quiet Bell — three miles away — began, all on its own, to ring.

Lior braced himself against the dais, bleeding, keeping time with his knife against the stone — *one, two, three, four* — and Tamsin sang the second name, and the third, and the mountain under the city opened its throat.
