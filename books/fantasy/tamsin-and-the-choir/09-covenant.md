## 9. Covenant

The Vault's alarms did not sound like bells. They sounded like the opposite of bells — a silence that spread outward through the stone, swallowing every small honest noise, so that when Tamsin drew breath to sing, she heard her own heartbeat first and was grateful for it. Something alive. Something still hers.

The wax cylinders of carved bone lay broken around her feet where she had struck them from their racks, and the voices were already loose in the air — not escaping, she realized, but *arriving*, the way weather arrives, the way dawn arrives, without asking anyone's permission. The old gods' voices moved through the undercroft of the Cathedral of the Quiet Bell like a tide coming home after a long and unforgivable absence.

Aldous Vane stood between her and the stair.

He had not drawn a weapon. He never would; men like Vane let other hands do the bleeding. He stood with his arms open, his coat immaculate even now, his face arranged into the expression he had worn the day he'd found her singing to locked doors and called it *a gift, child, a rare and precious gift*.

"Tamsin." His voice was warm as banked coals. "You don't understand what you're unbinding. The covenant was old law. Cruel law. The gods *chose* to be silent—"

"You told me they left willingly," Tamsin said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You told me the Guild kept their leavings. That you were custodians."

"I am a custodian," Vane said, and took one step closer, and his shadow on the wall did something shadows should not do — it stayed behind him while he moved forward, clinging to the dark like a man refusing a door. "Of order. Of the world as it must be. Without the Guild, girl, there is no ledger between realms, no terms, no *law*. There is only them, and us, and nothing written down. Is that what you want? A world with no record?"

Behind her, at the vault door, Lior braced his shoulder against the iron and set the point of his carving knife against the stone frame. She could hear him working — a slow, deliberate rhythm, *tick, tick, tick*, each cut a beat, each beat a bar. He was keeping time for her. He had been keeping time since the harvesters broke into the cathedral above, since the dead choirboy's warning had come down through the floorboards like water through a cracked ceiling: *they're coming, they're coming, sing now or never.*

"Every lie you sold," Tamsin said, "had a price. You taught me that yourself. First lesson of the counting house."

Vane smiled. It was the kindest thing about him, and it was the worst.

"Then count, Tamsin. Count what your song will cost. Your mentor is already in a harvest wagon. Your cathedral will be a counting house by morning. And you—" his voice dropped, gentle, terrible, "—you are one girl with borrowed breath. When the voices pass through you, do you know what remains? I've seen it. I've *sold* it. Singers come out of covenant-songs hollow as gourds."

Above them, boots on marble. Many boots. The enforcers had found the stair.

Tamsin closed her eyes.

She thought of the Quiet Bell, which never rang, which everyone said had fallen silent out of grief. She thought of Sister Maerin, taken in her place, who had once told her: *your voice isn't yours alone, child. It never was. That's what makes it holy.* She thought of nine names carved into a single femur, in Lior's careful hand, names she had read aloud only once in whispers because reading them felt like stepping off a cliff.

She opened her eyes.

"Lior," she said. "Keep the door. Keep the time."

"Always," he said, and the knife bit stone — *tick* — and she began.

* * *

The first name was the oldest, and it did not sound so much as *arrive*: a word like the pressure before a storm, like the weight of the sea deciding to move. Tamsin sang it and felt it leave her body the way heat leaves an open hand, and somewhere far above, in the nave of the Quiet Bell, the great bell — silent for sixty years — shivered in its yoke without ringing, remembering how.

The second name tasted of iron and rain. The third was low, almost a lullaby, and she understood suddenly that it had been sung over cradles when the world was younger, that mothers had known it without knowing its name, and that the Guild had bottled it and priced it by the verse.

The freed voices rose to meet her.

They came up through the broken cylinders and out through the vault walls and into the cathedral like weather — like a warm front rolling over cold ground, like the smell of rain arriving before the rain. Tamsin felt them gather behind her song the way a congregation gathers behind a single candle, and each voice that joined hers made the next name easier, the way a chorus makes a soloist brave.

The fourth name. The fifth. Her throat burned. Her knees wanted to fold. The harvesters were hammering at the doors above and below now, and Lior's knife kept its rhythm — *tick, tick, tick* — steady as a metronome, steady as a heart, and she fixed herself to that rhythm the way a climber fixes to a rope.

The sixth name she sang for Sister Maerin. The seventh for the dead choirboy beneath the counting-house floor, whose humming had started all of this, who had waited decades for someone to hear him. She felt him rise past her on his way up, light as ash, and she would have sworn — then and always — that something brushed her cheek like a hand saying *thank you.*

The eighth name cracked two of the vault's bone shelves clean in half.

And Vane —

Vane was still talking. That was the horror of him; he talked through everything, warmth poured over wound after wound. "This is theft, Tamsin. This is *embezzlement* from the lawful heirs of the covenant, and when the ledger settles, you will be the debtor—" but the ninth name was in her mouth now, and the ninth name was the clause itself, the living-voice clause, the term that said *the covenant renews only if spoken by a voice both realms trust*, and as she sang it, both realms turned to look at her.

She felt them looking. The dead looked up from under floors and inside ledgers and out of the small dark places where the Guild had filed them away. The gods looked down — or sideways, or inward; their attention had no direction she could name, only weight, like the whole sky leaning close.

Both realms trusted her voice. Not because she was good. Because she was *theirs* — orphan-born, choir-raised, a girl who had sung to locked doors because locked doors deserved songs too.

The covenant reforged around her like a ring being hammered shut.

She felt it close — a circle of terms older than the Guild, older than the city, passing through her chest and out into the world with a sound like a bell finally allowed to ring. The Quiet Bell rang. Once. The note went through stone and flesh and ledger-page alike, and everywhere it touched, lies lost their footing.

Which is why Aldous Vane fell.

He was mid-lie — she saw it happen, saw the sentence dying on his lips — *"and when the ledger settles, you will be"* — and the renewed covenant reached him and asked him, gently, terribly, to make it true. To make *all* of it true. Every warm reassurance. Every custodian's promise. *The gods chose silence. The Guild kept only leavings. No harm will come to you under my protection.* He had sold those words in installments for forty years, and now the covenant called in the debt, and the truth he had never owned came home to collect.

He came apart the way a ledger comes apart when you burn it — not violently, but completely, page by page, claim by claim, until what was standing in the undercroft was only a coat, folding down empty onto the bone-dust floor, and a last small sound like a ledger closing.

Tamsin kept singing anyway. Someone had to hold the note until the circle sealed. But tears ran down her face, and she could not have said, even then, whether they were for the monster or for the man who had almost, almost been kind enough to be real.

* * *

The enforcers broke the lower door on the twelfth blow, and Lior did not run.

She saw him through the smoke of the alarms' ending — saw him plant himself in the doorway with his back to her, knife in one fist and a length of broken shelf-bracket in the other, a thin line of red already running from his hairline down past his jaw. Three harvesters came through. Then four. He fought the way he carved: precisely, economically, each strike placed exactly where the material was weakest, and all the while — she could not believe it, and yet she could believe nothing else — he kept tapping the knife against the doorframe. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Keeping her time. Holding her tempo while the world ended around them both.

"Almost done!" he shouted, and took a baton across the shoulder, and did not stop tapping.

The ninth name resolved. The circle closed. And the voices of the old gods — free now, bound now, *covenanted* now — poured upward through the cathedral like a flood finding its old riverbed, and the harvesters stopped mid-blow and stood swaying, listening, because you cannot fight a sound that knows your true name. Their weapons clattered to the marble. One of them sat down on the steps and wept. Another pulled off his Guild badge as though it had begun to burn him, and perhaps it had; the covenant was new-minted, and new covenants are hot to the touch.

Lior slid down the doorframe, leaving a smear of red along the iron, and looked up at her with an expression she had never seen on him — not the careful ledger-keeper's neutrality, not the haunted arithmetic of a man pricing his own memories. Wonder. Plain, unbooked, unwritten wonder.

"It held," he said. "It actually held."

Tamsin came down the last steps on legs that barely answered, and the nine voices hummed in her chest like a chord that refused to resolve, and above them the Quiet Bell rang a second time — joyous now, unmistakable — and the sound went out over the rooftops of the city, over the Guild quarter, over the counting houses full of entries that were even now rewriting themselves in ink no clerk controlled.

She reached Lior and pulled his arm over her shoulders. He was lighter than she expected, or she was stronger; the nine voices carried some of the load, she suspected. They always would now.

"What happens tomorrow?" she asked.

Lior looked at the empty coat on the floor, at the shattered cylinders, at the harvesters sitting dazed among their dropped weapons, and his face did the thing it did when he was calculating — except this time the sum came quickly, and it frightened him.

"Now?" he said quietly. "Now every contract the Guild ever wrote is void, and half the city holds paper that just became worthless, and the other half holds debts the gods remember perfectly." He laughed — a short, disbelieving sound. "There's going to be a reckoning, Tamsin. And no magistrate alive has a law for any of it."

Far above, the Quiet Bell rang a third time, and beneath its note, faint but rising, Tamsin heard something else: the sound of many feet, moving toward the cathedral through the waking streets — not soldiers, not clerks.

Everyone else.
