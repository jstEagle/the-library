## 10. The Empty Ledger

The fires started at dawn, and Lior lit the first one himself.

He had chosen the counting house's back courtyard for it — a square of cracked flagstones where the Guild's clerks had once smoked pipeweed on their breaks, arguing interest rates the way other men argued theology. The theology had turned out to be the same argument all along. That was the joke of it, if Lior had been in a mood to laugh.

The ledgers filled three carts. He had counted them the night before, because counting was what he did, because it was the last night he would ever be allowed to be only that. Two hundred and eleven volumes. Sixty years of trades in impossible things. Every god-voice sold by the ounce. Every covenant clause priced, discounted, amortized. His own handwriting marching through them like a column of patient ants, recording ruin in a fair and legible hand.

*The gods are awake,* the first entry had said, a lifetime ago — eleven days ago. *Payment due.*

"Payment made," he said now, to no one, and struck the flint.

The vellum caught slowly. It always had; that was the point of vellum, that was why the Guild loved it, skin prepared to outlast the people who wrote on it. Lior fed page after page into the small fire and watched the ink curl and blacken. Names went first. Then prices. Then, at last, the terms themselves — the nine names of the old gods, carved and copied and cross-referenced until the living words of heaven had been reduced to inventory.

Somewhere behind him, Tamsin hummed.

She didn't do it on purpose anymore. He'd noticed it on the walk over: nine voices braided under her own, so faint you'd swear it was wind in the eaves, except the wind here hadn't blown in sixty years and the eaves were full of gods. When she hummed, the fire burned brighter. When she stopped, it sulked like a child denied its supper.

"You don't have to watch," she said.

"Yes, I do." Lior pressed another ledger flat against the coals with a poker, holding it there until the spine split and the pages fanned open like something surrendering. "I wrote these. Half of them. I balanced these columns while they were selling the sky by the pound. If I look away while they burn, I'm just a man destroying evidence. If I watch—" He searched for the word and found it, as he found most words, in the ledger-sense of things. "If I watch, I'm paying."

"The dead say debt is a story too." She came to stand beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm. Up close, in the firelight, he could see the change in her eyes — a depth there now, a weather. Nine storms learning to share one small harbor. "They're very philosophical since they got their voices back. One of them won't stop composing. The storm-god. He says grief should have a meter."

"Grief has always had a meter," Lior said. "It's called a ledger."

Tamsin laughed — and under her laugh, nine voices laughed with her, low and vast and ancient, and the flames leapt a foot high, and somewhere across the city a bell that had been silent since the founding rang once, experimentally, like an old man clearing his throat.

* * *

By noon, the whole quarter knew the Guild was dead.

Not dissolved — dead. There was a difference, and every soul in Vellum Quarter could feel it. Charters were paper, and paper could be argued with, amended, bought back. But the covenant beneath the charter had renewed itself around a choirgirl's voice three nights ago, and the new terms were written in no ink at all. They were written in the fact that prayers worked again. That the sick recovered when their mothers sang to them. That the rain came when called and the wheat stood up straight and the dead, God help them all, answered politely when addressed.

No magistrate had done any of it. No magistrate could have. When the Provost's enforcers had come for Tamsin in the cathedral, the law had been on their side — warrants, writs, the Provost's own seal — and the law had simply stopped mattering, the way a fence stops mattering when the river rises. Aldous Vane had been unmade between one lie and the next, caught mid-sentence with his charter in his hand, and the truths he had sold came home to him all at once, and there had not been enough of Aldous Vane left afterward to bury. What remained was a fine grey ash shaped briefly like a man, and then not even that.

His people had scattered the way rats scatter from a burning ship — except the ship wasn't burning, exactly. It was being *forgiven*, which was worse. The harvesters who had stormed the vespers service found their hands wouldn't close on anything holy anymore. The clerks who had priced psalms by the verse woke to find their ledgers blank, every entry wiped clean by a covenant that no longer recognized their authority to keep accounts. Some fled the city. Some knelt in the streets and wept and asked the air for forgiveness, and got it, which somehow shamed them more than punishment would have.

And some, Lior knew, were already telling a new story — that they had always served the gods, that the harvesting had been preservation, that they deserved to be the custodians of whatever came next. He had watched one of them, a senior factor named Corvess, bowing and scraping to Tamsin in the cathedral square that morning, offering the Guild's "remaining assets" to her stewardship.

Tamsin had looked at him with nine voices' worth of patience and said, "You kept our songs in wax and bone and sold them back to us by the measure. There are no remaining assets. There is only what you return, and what you never touch again."

Corvess had gone white and scurried. And Lior had understood, watching him go, that the ledgers in his courtyard could become weapons in men like that's hands — proof, precedent, a map of every covenant term ever traded, ready for the next clever clerk to rebuild the whole machine from. Truth, kept in the wrong hands, was just inventory.

So: the fire.

* * *

It took until dusk. Two hundred and eleven ledgers, and the last one fought him hardest — the founding volume, the first bargain, its pages soft as skin because they were skin, calf-vellum from a beast slaughtered before the city had walls. As it burned, Lior heard something rise out of the smoke. Not screaming. Singing. A fragment of melody, thin and glad, like a voice remembering itself after sixty years of silence, and then it thinned into the evening air and was gone, and Lior felt something in his chest come loose that he hadn't known was tied.

"There," Tamsin said softly. "That was the last of them. The first voice they ever took. It's been waiting in that book the whole time."

"Waiting for a fire?"

"Waiting for someone to stop reading and start listening." She nudged him with her elbow. "You're getting philosophical, bone-carver. It doesn't suit your ledger face."

"My ledger face is my best feature."

"It really isn't." But she was smiling, tiredly, the way people smile at the end of something long.

When the last cart was ash, Lior reached into his coat and brought out the one book he had saved. Blank. Plain calfskin, unruled, its spine uncreased. He turned it over in his hands and felt how light it was — lighter than any ledger he had ever kept, because it held nothing yet, because nothing in it had happened, because everything in it was still possible.

"One empty book," he said. "Out of two hundred and twelve."

"What will you put in it?"

"I genuinely don't know." The admission felt enormous, like setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for a spine. "I've recorded other people's bargains since I was fourteen years old. Trades in impossible things. I know what a memory costs, what a voice costs, what a god's name goes for on a bad market day. I don't know what anything costs when nobody's buying."

"Then write down what things are worth instead," Tamsin said. "There's a difference. My mentor used to say it. Worth is what you'd pay to keep a thing. Price is what someone else pays to take it." Her voice caught, just slightly, on *mentor* — on the man taken in her place, still not returned, still somewhere in the Guild's scattered custody or beyond it, a debt the new covenant had not yet collected. Lior saw her steady herself the way she always did: by humming, barely audible, nine harmonies settling around the wound like splints.

"We'll find him," Lior said. "The dead haven't stopped talking to me. If he's anywhere within earshot of the world, I'll hear him."

"And if he's not?"

Lior thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and had paid for it in blood and song. "Then we'll sing anyway. Loud enough that wherever he is, he'll hear us coming."

* * *

They left at first light, before the city woke enough to make leaving complicated. There would be questions — there already were, magistrates and clerics and the merely curious all wanting the story told properly, wanting it witnessed, filed, owned. Let them want. The truth was loose in the world now, walking around in a choirgirl's throat, and it belonged to no archive.

The north road climbed out of the city through terraces of fog and waking birdsong. Lior walked with the empty book slung across his back beside his carving tools, and Tamsin walked beside him humming, and the humming was doing things to the road — wildflowers opening out of season, a milestone stone straightening itself after centuries of lean, a dead crow by the wayside twitching once, puzzled, and deciding against it.

"You're going to be a menace on this journey," Lior said.

"I'm going to be *weather* on this journey. There's a difference." She grinned at him, and under the grin the nine voices rose together, brief and joyful, a chord that had not sounded in the world for sixty years and now would never fully stop.

They passed the last shrine before the open country — a little roadside niche, moss-eaten, its god long presumed departed. As Tamsin's humming washed over it, the moss receded, and the worn face of some small forgotten deity emerged smiling, and a trickle of water began, from nowhere, to run bright and cold into the trough below.

Lior stopped and looked at it for a long moment.

He still could not recall his mother's face. He had checked, quietly, every night since the payment — closed his eyes and reached for her and found only warmth without features, love without a shape to hang it on. It was a loss that did not dull, exactly. But it had changed texture. Somewhere in the smoke of the burning ledgers, or in the renewed covenant, or simply in the company of a girl who carried nine gods like breath, the loss had stopped being a wound and become a kind of room — empty, yes, but swept clean, with a window in it, and light coming through.

The dead sang to him kindly now. That was the strangest mercy of all. Where before they had whispered prices, they now hummed melodies, wordless and patient, keeping him company the way family keeps company: without demanding anything, without needing to be useful.

"Come on," Tamsin said, tugging his sleeve. "The world's awake. We're missing it."

Lior Thackeray, keeper of no ledgers, carver of bones, hearer of the kindly dead, took one last look back at the city smoking gently on its hill — the Guild quarter dark, the cathedral bells ringing themselves for the sheer joy of ringing — and then turned north, opened the empty book to its first blank page, and began, at last, to live inside the record instead of outside it.

The first thing he wrote was not a price.

It was a tune.
