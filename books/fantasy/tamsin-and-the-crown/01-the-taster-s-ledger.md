## 1. The Taster's Ledger

There were three rules to surviving a poison-taster's life in the usurper's court, and Rosalind Brightwater had written all of them down, because a rule you don't write down is just a wish.

One: never taste anything that smells of almonds and ambition at once.

Two: never let them see you enjoy the food.

Three: never, under any circumstances, be interesting.

Tonight the third rule was in danger, and it was entirely the fault of the soup.

It was a white soup, almond-thickened, served in bowls chased with silver, and it smelled faintly of both almonds *and* ambition, which by rule one made it suspect on principle. Rosalind lifted the spoon with the bored grace they had practiced for six years—the grace of a person for whom fine dining is merely a workplace hazard—and let the broth sit on their tongue while the great hall of Vanehold counted their heartbeats for them.

Nothing. Bitterness of almond, sweetness of cream, and beneath it, so faint most tongues would miss it, the cold mineral tang of *gallowsroot*—diluted to harmlessness, but present. Someone had seasoned the king's soup with poison the way a careless cook seasons with nutmeg: habitually, carelessly, or as a message.

Rosalind swallowed, set down the spoon, and gave the table the small nod that meant *edible*, which was true, and the smaller tightening of the eyes that meant *we will speak later*, which was also true, though only one person at the table would read it.

That person was not the king.

Usurper was such an ugly word. Lord-Protector Aldric Vane preferred *Sovereign-by-Necessity*, which had the advantage of being embroidered on banners and the disadvantage of being a lie long enough to trip over. He sat at the head of his long table eating the soup Rosalind had approved, thin and precise as a paper knife, and he did not look at Rosalind at all, which was the correct amount.

The person who looked—too much, too steadily—was the spymaster, Lady Maren Quill, seated three places down, wearing grey like other people wore knives.

Rosalind smiled at her the way one smiles at weather.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor behind the tapestry of the Drowned King, Corvane was waiting, which was itself against the rules, because Guildmaster Corvane of the Veiled Guild was never supposed to be anywhere at all. He was a round, comfortable man with the face of someone's favorite uncle and hands that had signed away more kingdoms than most kings had inherited.

"You tasted gallowsroot," he said, without greeting.

"I tasted *residue*. Somebody's been dosing the kitchens in homeopathic sips. Either they're building tolerance for something larger, or they're seasoning for flavor, and I genuinely don't know which worries me more."

"The second one. Cooks are the worst clients. They think everything is improved by a pinch." He fell into step beside Rosalind, and his voice dropped into the register he used for ledger-talk, low and even. "We need to talk about your name."

"My name is Rosalind Brightwater. I've had it for eleven years. I invented it myself; I'm rather proud of the 'Brightwater.' It sounds like someone who pays taxes."

"Your *other* name."

Rosalind stopped walking.

The corridor was warm with candlelight and smelled of tallow and old wool, and none of those ordinary things could explain why the air suddenly felt thin. "I don't have another name," they said. "I buried it. You buried it. It cost me two years' wages and a favor I still haven't finished repaying, and the whole point of burying a name is that nobody digs it up, least of all the gravedigger."

Corvane didn't answer. Instead he drew from inside his coat a book.

Rosalind knew books the way soldiers know weapons, and this one made their palms itch. It was small, bound in green leather gone black at the corners, its spine cracked into a ladder of use. A century old, if it was a day. The kind of book the Guild kept locked in the deep vaults, the kind that traded in futures instead of goods.

"Where did you get this?"

"It came back to us tonight. From a dead man's estate in the Marches. Dead men's estates are excellent sources of things we sold and shouldn't have." He opened it with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping animal. "Prophecy ledger. Written 103 years ago by a seer named Ivellune, who had the Guild's bad habit of writing down what she should have left alone."

He turned it toward Rosalind. The page was dense with faded ink, and down the margin ran dates, one after another, each with a line beside it. Corvane's blunt finger stopped on a date near the bottom.

Tonight's date.

Beside it, in a hand like bird tracks:

*A taster at a false king's board. She will swallow death nightly and be unharmed, for death does not know her name—but I do.*

And beneath that, in slightly larger letters, as if the seer had wanted to be sure:

*Tamsin of the Thornbury line. Last heir of the covenant.*

The corridor tilted.

Not dramatically—corridors are famously stable things—but Rosalind felt the floor take on the qualities of a ship's deck, felt the candle flames lean, heard their own breath go strange and shallow. *Tamsin.* A name they hadn't spoken aloud in eleven years. A name buried so deep they'd nearly convinced themselves it belonged to someone else, some child who'd died in the purges along with everyone else who remembered it.

"That's not my name," Rosalind said, and heard how unconvincing it sounded.

"No," Corvane agreed mildly. "It isn't. Not anymore. But names are like coins, Tamsin—they circulate." He closed the book before Rosalind could snatch it, which was probably wise. "Ivellune wrote four hundred entries. Most came true. The ones that didn't came true *wrong*, which is worse."

"What does the rest of the entry say?"

Corvane's comfortable uncle-face did something complicated. "The next line's been cut out. Razor work, old, careful. Someone removed one sentence from a prophecy a century ago, and I would very much like to know who and why, but more pressingly—" he glanced up the corridor, toward the hall where the Sovereign-by-Necessity was finishing his soup, "—Lady Quill has requested access to our ledgers."

The vertigo sharpened into something colder. "Our ledgers. The Guild's?"

"All of them. Officially she wants to audit tolls on the river trade. Unofficially, she asked my clerk—by name—for records pertaining to 'identity transactions.'" He said the Guild's stock-in-trade the way a priest says a blasphemy. "Years. Names. Second chances. Someone at court has started wondering what else can be bought besides silence and soup."

"Does she have a warrant?"

"She has a smile. With Maren Quill, that's worse."

Rosalind pressed their back against the cold stone between the tapestry's woven kings and laughed—a short, unsteady sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a person whose carefully invisible life had just developed a crack straight down the middle. Eleven years of being no one. Eleven years of tasting other people's deaths and keeping their own name in the ground. And now a dead seer had reached across a century, tapped them on the shoulder, and said *I know who you are*—and the spymaster was pulling threads from the other end.

"If Quill finds the name," Rosalind said slowly, working it through, "she doesn't find a taster. She finds an heir. Last heir of the covenant the Protector broke to take his crown. Do you understand what that means? He didn't just steal a throne—he broke a *pact*, and the pact had heirs, and I'm apparently the last one standing between him and every army, courtier, and vengeful fae lord with a grievance."

"I did say we needed to talk."

"You said we needed to talk about my *name*. You failed to mention it came with a target painted on it."

"In fairness," Corvane said, "the target was always there. It's just been underground. Like the name." He tucked the green book away and, for the first time in eleven years of knowing him, Rosalind saw the Guildmaster look genuinely afraid—not of the spymaster, but of the book, or what was missing from it. "There's one more thing. The cut line. Before the razor took it, someone copied it out. We found the copy folded into the binding."

He held out a slip of paper, yellowed, brittle, its ink brown as dried blood.

Rosalind read it once. Then again, because the first time couldn't possibly be right.

*She will flee to the queen who owes her blood, and the queen will pay her debt in truths that burn.*

"The exiled queen," Rosalind said flatly. "Ysolde. The one who abandoned my grandmother's line to the purges. The one your own guild calls *the Debtor* because she's been dodging her obligations for sixty years."

"The same."

"So the prophecy wants me to walk to the one creature in creation with the strongest motive to see me dead, and ask her for help."

"Yes," said Corvane cheerfully. "Or we stay here, wait for Lady Quill to finish her audit, and let the Protector learn he's been serving soup to the last heir of the covenant. I'd give that plan a week. Ten days if you keep tasting well."

Somewhere beyond the tapestry, the hall erupted in laughter at some joke of the Protector's, bright and hollow as struck glass. Rosalind closed their eyes and did the arithmetic they were best at: the price of staying, the price of running, and the terrible suspicion that both columns ended in the same sum.

"Get me passage," they said. "And Corvane—bring the ledger. If I'm going to be a prophecy, I intend to read the terms first."

* * *

They were halfway to the river gate when Rosalind realized, with a lurch worse than any poison, that they had never once wondered what the fourth line of the entry said—the one after the cut, the one still legible in the green book, the one Corvane had turned his face away from too quickly.

He had read it. She was almost certain he had.

And whatever it said, he had decided she wasn't ready to hear it yet.
