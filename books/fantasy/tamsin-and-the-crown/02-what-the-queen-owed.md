## 2. What the Queen Owed

The Veiled Guild kept its doors in the cellar of a candlemaker's shop, which Rosalind had always found fitting. Everything the guild sold was light that hadn't been lit yet.

They came down the stairs at midnight with soot on their sleeves and the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and Corvane was already waiting, which meant either he'd known they were coming or he'd simply never left. With Corvane it was impossible to say. He sat behind a desk made of a ship's door laid across two barrels, and he poured tea for three without asking.

"You look," he said, "like a person who has recently read their own name in an old book."

"I look like a person who is about to be arrested, Corvane. The spymaster asked me today whether the guild keeps ledgers. *Ledgers.* He used the word like a knife."

"He would. It's his favorite word." Corvane slid a cup across. "Drink. It's not poisoned. I had you taste it myself last week, if you recall."

Rosalind did not sit. "I need passage out of the capital. Tonight. Before the spymaster finishes whatever arithmetic he's doing."

"Passage we can arrange." Corvane turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Passage costs."

"Name it."

"The guild doesn't set prices, Tamsin." The old name dropped between them like a coin into a well. Rosalind flinched; Corvane watched them flinch with something that was almost kindness. "You know how this works. The buyer names the price. And your buyer—" he tapped the oilcloth bundle, "—has already named it. That book wants to go where it's going. It wants *you* to take it there. So the price is that you carry it, and you don't stop reading."

"That's not a price. That's a chore."

"Everything worth buying looks like a chore at first." Corvane stood, joints cracking like green wood. "There's a barge leaving from the tannery dock. Salt-cured hides going downriver to Greyharbor. You'll ride among the hides. Try not to smell memorable." He paused at the curtain. "And Rosalind—when you get where you're going, ask her what she owed. Not what she paid. What she *owed*. There's a difference, and it's the whole ledger."

"What are you talking about?"

But Corvane only smiled the way accountants smile when the numbers finally balance, and held the curtain aside, and let the night in.

* * *

Ysolde, once queen of the Seelie Court, once mistress of the covenant-moor, once a great many other things that no longer applied, was dying in a garden she had stolen.

It had been a duke's garden once, before the duke. Now it grew wild on the roof of a ruined watchtower above Greyharbor, roses gone feral and climbing over the battlements as though trying to escape themselves. Ysolde sat among them in a chair that had been grand two hundred years ago and was now mostly lichen, and she fed the gulls because they were rude to her and she respected that.

She had been dying for eleven years. Fae died slowly, out of spite if nothing else. Her glamour had thinned until she could see her own hands through it, translucent as sea-glass, and she had stopped pretending otherwise around the time the third rosebush died under her touch. Things that loved her wilted. She had learned to keep company with things that didn't: gulls, nettles, one extremely judgmental crow.

So when the barge arrived and a figure climbed the tower stairs smelling of salt hides and bad decisions, Ysolde knew before she saw the face. Blood remembers blood. It was the one inheritance she had never managed to sell.

"Well," she said, without turning. "The taster. Come all this way. Did you bring poison? Bring poison. I've developed opinions about it."

* * *

Rosalind had imagined this meeting perhaps forty times since the prophecy book cracked open under their fingers, and in none of those imaginings had the last heir of the fae covenant been feeding bread crumbs to seagulls while complaining about the weather.

"You're smaller than the stories," Rosalind said.

"You're taller than the ledgers suggested. Sit down. No—not there, that chair bites. It bit a bishop once; he deserved it." Ysolde gestured with the heel of the loaf. Up close her glamour flickered like a candle in a draft—beautiful, then old, then beautiful again, then something older than either. "You have questions. Everyone who climbs these stairs has questions. Ask the cheap ones first."

"Why did the covenant-stones disappear?"

"Ah. Straight to the expensive ones." The queen sighed, and the roses nearest her leaned away. "Because wars cost money, child, even for queens. Especially for queens. When the usurper's grandfather marched on the moor, I needed soldiers, and soldiers need *reasons*, and reasons need gold, and gold—" she spread her sea-glass hands, "—is heavy. So I pawned them. Three stones. One to the Merchants' Concord of Vell, one to a river-god with poor impulse control, and one to—" she paused, and for the first time looked genuinely uncomfortable, which Rosalind would learn was rare enough to be historic, "—one to a party whose name I am contractually unable to pronounce. The guild will know. The guild knows everything; it's insufferable."

"You pawned the covenant. You pawned the *realm*."

"I pawned the *pact*, which is different, and I intended to redeem them. Queens always intend to redeem them." Her laugh was dry as moth wings. "Then I lost the war, and the pawn tickets burned with my court, and here I am. Dying on a roof. Feeding birds who don't respect me."

"And my family? My bloodline? Where do we fit in your war?"

Something moved behind Ysolde's face then—something old and unlovely—and Rosalind understood, with the particular vertigo of a taster recognizing a flavor, that they had just swallowed something bitter.

"Ah," said Rosalind quietly. "That's the real question, isn't it. What did you owe?"

* * *

Ysolde had rehearsed this confession for a century and still botched the opening.

"When the covenant was young," she said, "it required an anchor. A mortal line, bound into the pact, so that neither court could break faith without breaking something living. Your ancestors volunteered. Brave, foolish people. Farmers, mostly. They thought they were guarding a border." She turned the loaf in her hands. "And when the war came, and I needed to survive, there was a power that offered me passage out of the burning moor. Its price was an anchor. A living one. A bloodline, surrendered, severed from protection, cast loose into the mortal world to fend for itself."

The gull screamed. Somewhere below, Greyharbor rang its evening bells.

"You sold us," Rosalind said. "You sold my whole line to buy your own escape."

"I traded you," Ysolde said, with the terrible precision of the very old. "Sold implies I got a good price. I got *survival*, which is the cheapest thing in any market and the most expensive. Every year since, I have told myself the anchor-line would fare better unbound—free, unnamed, invisible. And every year I believed it a little less." She looked up, and her eyes were the color of deep water over drowned things. "You survived, didn't you? Here you stand. Tasting poison in a tyrant's hall, keeping books for thieves, alive. That was the inheritance I left you. Survival. I'm told it's the only heirloom that appreciates."

Rosalind laughed, and hated the sound of it—wet and sharp. "You want credit. For abandoning us *thoroughly*."

"I want nothing. That's what dying is good for." Ysolde rose, unsteady, and pressed something into Rosalind's hand: a pawn ticket, ancient, wax-sealed, the ink still dark. "The third stone. The party whose name I cannot speak—the guild holds its debt, and the guild honors its tickets. Take it. Redeem what I couldn't." Her fingers were cool as riverbed stones. "And when you meet the fae courts again, tell them Ysolde's debt is *not* paid. Tell them the anchor chooses now. Let them choke on it."

Rosalind closed their hand around the ticket and felt the weight of everything it implied—the free cities ahead, the armies behind, three stones scattered like dropped coins, and a crown waiting at the end of it all like a trap baited with a throne.

"One more question," Rosalind said. "The prophecy. Tonight's date. My name. Who wrote it?"

Ysolde smiled then, and it was the first honest thing she'd worn all evening.

"I did, child. A hundred years ago. I wrote it the night I traded you away—because even then, some part of me knew the anchor would come back. Prophecies aren't predictions, Tamsin Brightwater. They're *invoices*." She settled back among her hostile roses. "Now go. The tide turns at dawn, and so does everything else. And do give Corvane my regards—he'll pretend he never received them, but he'll be pleased."

Rosalind stood at the top of the tower with a pawn ticket in one fist and fury and grief sitting in their chest like two passengers sharing a bench, and far to the south, faint as a scar on the horizon, the usurper's signal-fires had begun to burn—one for each city his armies meant to take.

Three fires. Three stones. And the tide turning at dawn.
