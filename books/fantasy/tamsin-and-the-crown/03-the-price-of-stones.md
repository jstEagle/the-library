## 3. The Price of Stones

The first stone was in Saltmere, and it cost a year.

Not Rosalind's year—Corvane's, though he offered it before Rosalind could stop him, which was the problem with Corvane. He had the terrible habit of loving people in ways that were logistically inconvenient.

"It's a bad year anyway," he said, counting out the coin of his own life into the pawnbroker's brass scale. "Forty-one. My knees have opinions now. I'd rather spend the year on you than keep it."

"That's not how years work," Rosalind said. "You can't just—give one away like it's a coat."

"Guildmaster," said the pawnbroker, a small woman with the patient eyes of someone who had watched a thousand people talk themselves out of bargains and a thousand more talk themselves in, "do you accept the terms? A year of Master Corvane's life, held against the covenant-stone of Saltmere, redeemable never."

Rosalind's hand was on the stone before they could think better of it. It was warm. All the stones were warm, like hands held too long, like a fire just gone out. This one hummed faintly against their palm, a note they felt in their teeth.

"I accept," Rosalind said, because the usurper's outriders were two days behind them and closing, and because Corvane was already smiling, already lighter, already a year less himself.

The stone came free of its wrappings—oilcloth, then silk, then a child's blanket, embroidered with a name that wasn't Rosalind's. The pawnbroker watched them take it with an expression Rosalind would spend the rest of the road trying to name.

"Guild business," she said, "is grief business. We just keep better ledgers than grief does."

* * *

The second stone was in Marrowfield, and it was not for sale.

It was for trade, which was worse. The Marrowfield branch of the Veiled Guild kept its stones in a cellar beneath the tanneries, and the keeper there was a soft-spoken man named Ellery who had once, in another life, been Rosalind's mother's friend.

"The stone of Marrowfield," Ellery said, "trades in memories. You'll want to give me something you won't miss. Most people do. A first kiss. The name of a childhood dog. Everyone has a drawer of things they think are worthless."

"I want to offer something specific," Rosalind said. Their mouth had gone dry. "I want to offer the memory of my mother."

Ellery went very still. "Rosalind."

"I never think of her anyway. That's not true. I think of her constantly, and it's all—fog. I was four. I remember a kitchen and a song and nothing else, and the fog has been sitting on my chest my whole life, and I am *tired* of carrying a ghost I can't even see." They heard their own voice climbing and made it stop. "Take the fog. Take all of it. I'll finally be light enough to run."

Ellery looked at them for a long time. Then he brought the stone out, and it was cold, this one, cold as a cellar, and it sang a different note, lower, like a door closing.

"Here is what I can offer, as a friend of your mother, and not as a keeper," Ellery said. "I can take the fog. I cannot take the grief. The grief isn't a memory, Rosalind. It's a shape the memory left. You'll lose the fog and keep the shape, and you won't know what the shape is of, and that is a cruelty I'd rather not sell you."

"Sold," Rosalind said. "Please."

He took it. He was gentle about it, the way you're gentle with a splinter. And afterward Rosalind stood in the tannery yard feeling—light. Genuinely light. The fog was gone. The kitchen, the song, the smell of bread, all of it, gone, and in its place a shape, an absence with arms, something that had held them once and never would again.

They wept for a full minute without knowing why, and then they stopped, because the usurper's army was one day behind them now, and the road to the third stone ran through country that burned.

* * *

The third stone was in the free city of Vael, and it cost Corvane everything.

That was not the bargain. The bargain was supposed to be simple. The Vael guild-keeper wanted loyalty—not a year, not a memory, but the living thing itself, Corvane's loyalty, held in perpetuity, which in guild terms meant: Corvane would serve whoever held the stone, forever, and the stone would be Rosalind's, and so Corvane would be Rosalind's, not out of love but out of binding.

"It's the same thing," Corvane said, when Rosalind refused. "It's the same thing, Rosalind, I promise you. I've been loyal to you for years and never once had to be *made* to be."

"That's exactly the difference," Rosalind said. "That's the whole difference. If I take this bargain, you're a lock and I'm the key, and I will never again know if you'd have stayed."

Corvane smiled at them, that patient, maddening smile. "You already don't know. Nobody ever knows. That's what love is, Rosalind—it's loyalty without a ledger. I'm offering you the ledger version because it's what I have to give. Take it. Take it and win, and then put the stone down and let me be a man again."

So Rosalind took it. And the stone was warm, and Corvane's eyes went briefly, terribly smooth, like a pond freezing over, and when he looked at Rosalind afterward there was devotion in his face and nothing behind it, no history, no joke he was waiting to tell, no year he'd given away in Saltmere. Just devotion, obedient and bottomless.

Rosalind had traded a memory and gotten grief with no name. They had traded a friend's year and gotten his gray temples. And now they had traded his *self*, and gotten a soldier who would follow them into any fire and never once ask why.

Three stones. Three people they loved, each one lighter, each one less.

* * *

They made the moor by night, ahead of the armies by hours, and Rosalind stood in the ruined circle with the stones in their arms and felt what the old kings must have felt.

It came up through the stones like heat from a hearth. *You could,* the stones said, in the wordless way stones say things. *You could hold all three and speak the old words and the covenant would answer to you. Crown, realm, courts, all of it. Ysolde abandoned her claim and it nearly killed her. You wouldn't have to abandon anything. You'd just have to pick it up.*

And the terrible thing, the thing Rosalind would never say aloud, was how *easy* it felt. How the weight of the stones fit their arms like the stones had been measured for them. They thought of the usurper's court, the poison and the ledgers, a lifetime of surviving other people's power—and here was power of their own, warm and singing, asking only that they stop trying to give everything away.

One word. The old words. And no one would ever make them taste anything again.

"Rosalind." Corvane's voice, smooth and devoted. "The armies. They're coming."

Rosalind looked down at the stones. Three warm hearts in their arms, bought with a year and a mother and a man.

*Hold on,* they thought. *One more night. Then I put you all down, and everyone goes free—including me.*

On the dark edge of the moor, the first watchfires of the usurper's army bloomed like a rash across the hills—and among them, moving against the tide of soldiers, a single figure walking toward the circle, wearing the ragged remnants of a queen.
