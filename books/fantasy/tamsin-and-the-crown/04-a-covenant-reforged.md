## 4. A Covenant Reforged

Ysolde reached the circle of standing stones as the first horns sounded across the moor, and she did not walk so much as arrive by increments—a queen's procession compressed into a ruin of a body, each step a negotiation with gravity.

"You're late," Rosalind said.

"I'm *dying*," Ysolde said. "Dying is very time-consuming. All that unfinished business." She lowered herself onto the fallen stone at the circle's edge with the dignity of someone sitting upon a throne, which, given the state of the stone, required imagination. "You have them. The stones."

"Three of them." Rosalind's arms ached from carrying them. They were warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and they had opinions; Rosalind could feel them wanting things. "A year of my life. My mother's face. Corvane."

"I'm standing right here," Corvane said, "and I resent being described in the past tense."

"You were pawned," Rosalind told him. "Your loyalty. To the Guild, during the war. I bought it back yesterday and you've been insufferably devoted ever since."

"I prefer 'steadfast.'"

"You tried to taste my soup for poison this morning."

"A gesture of esteem!"

Rosalind looked at him—this man who had run the Veiled Guild's ledgers for thirty years, who had taught a half-starved taster to read numbers like scripture—and felt the grief arrive early, ahead of schedule, the way grief does when it has been waiting politely in the corridor.

"It doesn't count," Rosalind said quietly. "What I bought. It isn't yours if it was purchased. When this is done, I'm giving it back."

Corvane opened his mouth. Closed it. For once in his long, transactional life, he had nothing to enter into any ledger.

* * *

The armies met at moonrise, which was traditional, and immediately began arguing about where exactly to meet, which was also traditional.

From the covenant-moor Rosalind could see both fires: the usurper's host on the southern hills, ten thousand strong and glittering with stolen regalia, and the free companies of the cities on the north ridge—smaller, scrappier, flying banners that had clearly been sewn in a hurry by people better at arithmetic than embroidery. Between them lay the old stones, black against the frost, waiting a hundred years for someone to say the word.

The word was not, as it happened, one word. It was a contract.

"The original covenant was simple," Ysolde said, watching the lines form below. "A crown for a bridge. One bloodline bound to both courts, mortal and fae, holding the door open between worlds. In exchange: peace. Trade. The long seasons kept honest." She coughed, and the cough had thorns in it. "Then your usurper broke the bridge to keep the tolls for himself, and my court let him, because we were tired, and proud, and it wasn't our door anymore. Wasn't it."

"And now?"

"Now you hold three stones and a prophecy with your name in it, and every army down there thinks you're going to put a crown on your own head and make everything simple again." Her eyes, still beautiful, still cold as well-water, found Rosalind's. "They will follow you if you do. That is the trap of inheritances, little taster. Everyone hands you the poison cup and calls it a birthright."

Rosalind looked down at the stones. A year. A mother. A friend. Everything they'd paid, everything everyone had ever paid, stacked up warm in their arms.

"I don't want the crown," Rosalind said.

"I know," said Ysolde. "I've known since the night I traded your bloodline away and went home and couldn't sleep. That's why I'm here. That's the debt, if you're wondering. Not the stones. Not the throne. The debt is that I get to watch you refuse it, and know the world gets one person who would."

"That's a terrible debt."

"It's a fae debt," Ysolde said. "We don't do wholesome."

* * *

The battle began the way battles do when both sides are mostly tired people following ambitious men: badly, and then worse.

Rosalind stood in the circle with the stones and did the thing no heir had ever been asked to do, which was read the fine print aloud.

The old covenant wanted a crowned keeper. Rosalind struck that clause. In its place, they wrote—speaking the words into the hungry air while Corvane scratched the terms into the Guild's master ledger, because even miracles require documentation—that the door between realms would be held open not by one bloodline but by accord: the free cities, the guilds, the courts, each a hand on the hinge, none a hand on the throat. No crown. No claim-name above any other name. Every signatory equal before the door, and the door answerable to all of them.

"It needs a price," Ysolde warned. "Covenants always cost. What are you offering?"

Rosalind had thought about this for three days and one very long night, and the answer had been sitting in their chest the whole time, the way answers do.

"The stones," they said. "All three. And everything they hold. My year. My mother's memory. Corvane's freely-given loyalty, returned unspent. Paid into the covenant as its founding fund." They swallowed. "And one more thing. The usurper's claim-name."

Below them, the battle paused—not ended, but *paused*, armies and fae alike turning toward the circle as the old stones began to drink the offered years and memories and devotions, glowing like coals under ash.

The usurper's claim-name was the true one, the old one, the one his grandmother had whispered over his cradle, the one that made soldiers believe in him and courtiers fear him and historians reach for their pens. Rosalind held it the way they held all the Guild's impossible inventory: lightly, and with an eye to resale.

They did not sell it. They gave it away.

The effect was quiet, which surprised everyone. Ten thousand soldiers simply stopped knowing why they were standing in a frozen field at midnight. Some sat down. Some started home. A sergeant near the front lines was heard to remark that he'd only joined for the boots, and honestly the boots weren't that good. The usurper himself stood in the middle of his dissolving host shouting a name that no longer meant anything to anyone, including, after a moment, himself—and then he too sat down in the frost, a man abruptly retired, and asked the nearest soldier whether anyone had thought to bring supper.

The free companies cheered. Then, being sensible people, they also asked about supper.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and clean over the moor.

Ysolde was fading with the stars, her long-deferred death finally collecting, and she seemed—Rosalind searched for the word—*relieved*. Like a ledger finally balanced. Like a taster who had tasted everything and found, at the last, nothing poisoned.

"The debt," Rosalind said. "Is it paid? Truly?"

"You refused a crown in front of two armies and a fae court," Ysolde said. "Child, it was paid the moment you said *no*. Everything since has been epilogue." She lifted a hand—thin as winter branch—and touched Rosalind's cheek, the first kind touch either of them could remember exchanging. "Your mother had your stubbornness. I made sure of it, before I sold her out of your memory. Consider that my one act of parenting."

Then she was gone, the way morning goes: gradually, and then completely, leaving the light behind.

Corvane appeared at Rosalind's elbow with the master ledger under his arm and his loyalty restored to its natural, unpurchased state—which was to say, he was looking at Rosalind with mild professional skepticism, as befitted a man auditing a miracle.

"The accounts," he said. "If we're finished playing at prophecy."

"We're finished." Rosalind took the pen. At the top of the final page, in their own careful hand, they wrote:

*Rosalind Brightwater — taster, keeper, heir of nothing and no one. Debts: paid. Stones: spent. Crown: declined. Balance: zero.*

They signed it. Then, after a moment, they crossed out their name entirely, because the covenant had taken it as surety—the old name, the buried one, the one in the prophecy—and what remained was a person with no title, no claim, and no entry anywhere in anyone's book.

"Well," Corvane said. "That's the first balanced ledger in Guild history. People will say it's a forgery."

"People say that about everything worth doing."

He hesitated, then, awkwardly, offered his arm—an old man's courtesy to a colleague walking home. Rosalind took it.

They came down off the moor together as the sun cleared the hills, past soldiers sharing breakfast with former enemies, past the free companies arguing cheerfully about the wording of the new accord (the millers' guild had already found a loophole; the millers always did), past the standing stones going quiet and ordinary, which is the finest thing old magic can become.

No one looked twice at the two of them. That was the whole point.

At the crossroads where the road bent toward the city, toward the Guild house, toward a small room above the counting-floor with a kettle and a window, Rosalind stopped and breathed in the cold morning like someone tasting something for the very first time.

"What now?" Corvane asked.

Rosalind smiled—no name attached to it, no title, no prophecy, just a person at the start of an unwritten page.

"Now," they said, "we go home, and for once, nobody owes anybody anything."

Somewhere behind them, faint as a bell across water, the new-covenant stones hummed once—as if agreeing—and then, satisfied, fell silent.
