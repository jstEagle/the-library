## 1. The Charm Seller

There are three ways to survive in Correnvale, and I have tried all of them. The first is to be useful. The second is to be invisible. The third is to be very, very lucky, and I burned through my allotment of luck seven years ago at a gate I will not name, sealing a door I will not describe, on a night I mostly do not think about before noon.

So I settled for the first two, in that order, and I became a charm-seller.

Not a *mage*. Gods, no. Not a *witch*, not a *worker*, not any of the words that get you a grey cloak and a short walk to the Warden cells on Candlemaker's Row. I sold charms the way a fishmonger sells fish: honestly, cheaply, and with a great deal of noise about how fresh everything was.

"Love knots," I called to the morning crowd on Tallow Street, holding one up so the light caught the braided cord. "Woven by my own hands, no magic in them at all, which is why they're only two coppers. If you want magic, friend, you want the temple, and the temple charges."

That got the laugh it always got. Correnvale loves a joke about the temple, because the temple is rich and the crowd on Tallow Street is not, and because joking about the temple is still allowed. Joking about the Wardens is not, but I've never needed to. The Wardens joke about themselves, in the way that a boot jokes about a face.

Here is the trick of the charm trade, and I will tell it to you because you've bought the book and I owe you some honesty: the charms work. Not the way people think. A love knot is two coppers of braided cord and a minute of my attention, and when I braid it, I think — carefully, lightly, the way you'd touch a soap bubble — *may they be kind to each other.* A luck coin gets *may the road rise to meet her*, and a birthing charm gets *may the hands that catch be steady.* Nothing anyone could name as magic. Nothing that would light a single candle in a Warden's detection glass. Just a whisper, a breath, a thought shaped and sent, so small that even I sometimes pretend it isn't there.

Seven years of pretending. I've gotten very good at it. I'm so good at it that some mornings I wake up and genuinely believe I'm just a tired man with chapped hands who braids cord for a living, and those are the best mornings.

This was not going to be one of the best mornings. I want that on the record. When they write the ballad — and someone will write the ballad, they write ballads about everything, half of them wrong — I want a verse establishing that I knew. Not what was coming. Just that something had shifted, the way a dog knows a storm before the clouds do.

It started with the milk.

* * *

Mira the milkwife sets up two stalls down from mine, and every morning she sends her boy, Pip, over with a cup of fresh buttermilk, because seven years ago I sold her a birthing charm for free when her husband was between jobs, and she has decided this makes us family. I have stopped arguing. In Correnvale, being family to a milkwife is the safest kind of family to be.

This morning the buttermilk tasted wrong. Not sour — wrong. Like the cup had been standing near something cold. I set it down and looked at Pip, who was already running back, and I looked at the sky, which was doing nothing remarkable, and I looked at the crowd on Tallow Street, which was doing everything remarkable, because Tallow Street always does.

And then I noticed the quiet.

Not silence. Correnvale is never silent. But the *quality* of the noise had changed, the way a river changes when a dam goes in upstream. The market was still roaring, but underneath it, something had stopped.

The Wardens' bell on the customs house rang twice.

Twice means inspection. Once means a thief. Three times means run, and I have heard three bells exactly once, seven years ago, and I do not expect to hear them again and survive it.

I did what any sensible man does. I kept braiding cord.

That's not cowardice, whatever you're thinking. That's the whole art of survival in this city. When the grey cloaks come through the market, the guilty run and the innocent gawk, and both get noticed. The man who keeps working, who mutters about prices and complains about the damp and scratches his head over his ledger — that man is furniture. I have been furniture for seven years. I am very fine furniture.

"Charms!" I called, a little too loudly. "Love knots, luck coins, protection against the evil eye, which I'll remind you is not a real thing, which is why I can sell it."

A woman bought three knots for her daughters. A dockhand haggled me down to a copper and a half on a luck coin and walked away feeling like he'd robbed me, which is the secret second product I sell: the feeling of having won. The market noise closed back over me like water, and for a quarter hour I almost believed the bell had been about someone else.

Then the grey cloaks came up Tallow Street, and the market learned what the river knows when the dam opens.

* * *

There were four of them, which was two more than usual. Two was routine: a Warden and a scribe, walking the stalls, checking licenses, occasionally weighing a charm on a little brass scale that didn't weigh anything at all but made everyone nervous, which was its actual function. Four meant they were looking for something. Four meant the scribe had a list.

I watched them work their way down the row and I did the arithmetic I always do. Mira: clean, she sells milk, even the Wardens drink. Old Hesk with his carved spoons: clean, unless spoons are illegal this season. The spice-seller from the Salt Road caravans: not clean, never clean, but foreign enough that the Wardens preferred to fine him rather than look too closely, because looking closely at Salt Road cargo meant paperwork, and Wardens hate paperwork the way cats hate rain — not on principle, but because it's wet and undignified.

They reached the spice-seller. There was a short, sharp conversation. The scribe made a note. Two of the grey cloaks stayed with the spice-seller, and two kept walking.

Toward me.

"Master Ashgrove." The taller of the two had a voice like a door closing. I knew him by sight — Warden-Captain Dree, who had inspected my stall eleven times in seven years and never found anything, because there was nothing to find, because I had made very sure there was nothing to find, and because I had also, in the early years, spent a little of my gift making sure that looking at my stall felt like looking at a room you'd already checked. A small thing. A nothing. The kind of working that leaves no more trace than a sigh.

I had stopped doing that three years ago. Too much use, even that much, leaves a residue, and residue is what the detection glasses drink. For three years my stall had been exactly what it appeared, and I had aged seven years' worth of nervous in three.

"Captain Dree." I smiled the smile. You know the smile. The one that says *I am a simple man and you are an important one and we both understand the arrangement.* "Cold morning for it. Can I interest you in a luck coin? Half price for the city's protectors, and I mean no flattery by that, only arithmetic."

Dree did not smile. Dree had never smiled, and I had come to believe he'd been assembled rather than born, some civic process involving ledgers and cold water.

"New license," he said.

"It's on the hook there, Captain, right where the inspector put it last month—"

"The new license." He held out a hand like a man asking for a toll. "Posted yesterday. All charm-sellers re-register. New schedule of permitted goods."

I took down the license on its hook and handed it over, and while Dree read it with the scribe peering over his shoulder, I did the thing I do, the thing I hate, the thing that is the whole of my life now: I made myself small. Shoulders in. Eyes down and to the left, the posture of a man whose entire ambition is to be permitted to keep braiding cord until he dies. Inside, where no glass could reach, I was very still, the way you're still when you hear something moving in a wall.

"Permitted goods," Dree said at last. "Read them to me."

I read them. Cord charms. Knotwork. Coins, blessed, non-liturgical. Amulets of a protective character, decorative. And then the new line, the one that hadn't been there last month, printed in that cramped official hand that always looks like it's apologizing for itself:

*No charm may be represented as efficacious. Sellers must display notice disclaiming all effect.*

"They want me to tell people my charms don't work," I said.

"They want you to tell the truth," Dree said.

And there it was, the little joke the city had been building toward for seven years: the law finally catching up to my lie. Because my charms *didn't* work — officially, provably, detectably didn't work — and the notice would be true, and I would post it, and every morning I would stand under a sign swearing my gift didn't exist, and that was the safest place I'd ever be. A man hiding in the open. I almost laughed. I want it noted that I did not.

"I'll paint the notice today, Captain."

"Paint it large." He handed the license back, and then — and this is the part I keep returning to, the splinter in the day — he paused. He looked at my hands. Not my face, not my stall, my hands, the ones holding the braided cord, and he looked at them the way a man looks at a lock he hasn't decided whether to pick.

"You were at the gate," he said. "Ashgrove. Seven years ago. Before it was sealed."

The market went on around us. A woman haggled over milk. Somewhere a cart complained about its axle. And I stood in the middle of all that ordinary noise and felt the day tilt, very slightly, the way a floor tilts when the house has decided, privately, to fall down.

"Everyone was at the gate, Captain," I said. "Half the city turned out. It was quite the spectacle. Very sad. Terrible business."

"You're named for it."

"Ashgrove's a common name in the river parishes."

"It isn't." He said it without heat, without accusation, the way you'd correct a child's sums. "I checked. There were four Ashgroves in the census. Two are dead. One is a woman in Vell's Cross. And you." He let that sit. "You came to Correnvale the month after the sealing. No trade history. No family. You registered as a charm-seller, which was, I'll note, an unusual choice for a man with no history. Most men with no history choose something quieter."

"Like what?"

"Gravedigging," Dree said. "It's quiet."

I held his eyes, and I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the gate, harder than the sealing, harder than the night that's coming and that I can't tell you about yet because we haven't got there: I let him see me be afraid. Not the fear of a guilty man — the fear of a small one. The fear of a man who has just understood that he has never once been invisible, only unexamined, and that the examination has begun.

"Captain," I said, "I braid cord. I cheat dockhands out of half coppers. I drink too much on saints' days. If I were hiding something, do you think I'd hide it in a market stall with my name on the license?"

"I think," Dree said, taking his scribe's list and rolling it closed with two precise movements, "that men who hide things hide them where they can watch them." He put the list away. "Paint your notice. Large letters. I'll be back within the month."

He walked on down the row, grey cloak moving through the crowd like a fish through reeds, and the crowd closed behind him, and the market noise came back, and everything was fine, and nothing was fine at all.

* * *

I painted the notice that afternoon. LARGE LETTERS, as instructed, in the good white paint, and I stood back and read it: *THESE CHARMS HAVE NO EFFECT AND ARE SOLD AS ORNAMENT ONLY.* Under it, because I am who I am, I added in smaller letters: *the management regrets this deeply and suggests you buy two.*

Mira laughed when she saw it. Pip laughed. A dockhand bought a luck coin *because* of the sign, he said, on the principle that anything the Wardens hated that much had to be worth something, and I took his copper and thought: there it is, the whole city in one transaction. We will buy our chains if the blacksmith mocks us while fitting them.

I closed the stall at dusk, as always. Carried my crate of cord and coins up the three stairs to my rooms above the shop, as always. Ate bread and hard cheese standing at the window, as always, watching Tallow Street empty out under the lamps, the lamps that a lamplighter lit with a taper on a pole, no magic in it at all, none anywhere, the whole city scrubbed clean of it seven years ago, and look how well we all live. Look how safe we are. Look how quiet.

I was washing the cup — Mira's cup, the buttermilk one, the one that had tasted wrong that morning, and I'd been turning that over all day like a stone in the pocket — when I heard the sound from the street below.

Not a word. Not a cry. A *weight*. The sound a body makes when it meets a door, which is a sound I hope you never learn to recognize, and which I recognized instantly, the way you recognize a bell.

I stood in my dark kitchen with the wet cup in my hands, and I did the arithmetic one more time. Stay inside. Stay small. Stay furniture. In the morning there would be a mess in the street, and the Wardens would come, and I would paint nothing and say nothing and be a charm-seller again by noon.

The knock, when it came, was barely a knock at all. More of a sliding. A hand that had meant to knock and had run out of the strength for it halfway through.

I want to tell you I hesitated. I did hesitate — a heartbeat, maybe two, the length of time it takes a man to choose between the life he has built out of smallness and the door in front of him. But here is what seven years had taught me, what the gate had taught me, what I had never once said out loud:

Doors don't stay shut. Not the ones that matter. You can seal them, you can walk away, you can build a whole quiet life with your back turned to them, and all the while they're waiting on the other side with their hand raised to knock.

I opened the door.

The man in my doorway was bleeding from a wound I couldn't yet see, in a place I couldn't yet name, and he was holding something out to me with both hands the way you'd hold a candle in wind — carefully, desperately, as if it were the only light left anywhere.

"Take it," he said. And then, as the bells of Correnvale began to ring — not twice, not three times, but all of them, every bell in the city at once, which had never happened, which was not supposed to be possible — he said the words that ended my life as a charm-seller:

"The Ember burns where the crown was made."
