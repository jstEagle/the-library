## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The bells did not stop.

That was the thing I remember most clearly afterward — not the blood, not the map, not even the words — but the bells, all of them, every tower in Correnvale ringing at once like a city screaming into its own cupped hands. The Wardens controlled the bell towers. The Wardens controlled everything that could be controlled. So either the bells had gone mad on their own, or something had happened that the Wardens hadn't planned for, and both of those possibilities were so far outside my carefully constructed life that my mind simply refused to file them.

The man in my doorway swayed.

"Take it," he said again, and pressed the thing into my hands before I'd agreed to anything, which is how the best and worst transactions in my life have always worked.

It was a map. Folded small, soft as cloth from handling, stained dark across one corner where his fingers had been wet. I knew maps. I sold little ones — charm-maps, really, squares of vellum with a lover's initials inked at the center so that whoever carried them would never quite get lost on the way home. This was not that. This was heavy in a way paper shouldn't be, the way a stone is heavy, or a promise.

"You've made a mistake," I told him. "I sell luck-charms. Anti-lice charms. A very popular one for keeping milk sweet through a hot week. Whatever you think I am—"

"I know what you are." His voice was going. I could hear it going, the way you hear a candle drowning in its own wax. "Ashgrove. You're Ashgrove."

Nobody called me that. Nobody *should* call me that. Seven years ago I had walked away from a gate and a name and a version of myself who thought duty was a kind of armor, and I had built Rafael the charm-seller out of the rubble: a mild man with ink-stained fingers and a stall near Tanner's Row, a man so aggressively unremarkable that even the Wardens' inspectors, when they came through twice a year, looked at him and saw furniture.

"Inside," I heard myself say.

Which was stupid. Which was possibly the stupidest thing I have ever done, and I once tried to bluff a Warden-captain about a shipment of singing stones by claiming they were "decorative cheese." But the bells were still ringing, and the man was bleeding onto my threshold in long slow pulses, and there is a limit to what even a coward can pretend not to see.

I got him inside. It took longer than it should have; he was tall, or he had been tall before whatever was killing him bent it out of him, and the door of my shop opens inward because I am paranoid in small practical ways. He collapsed against my counter among the milk-charms and the anti-lice sachets and the little glass bottles of *Definitely Not Magic* that I labeled myself, in my own careful hand, for the benefit of any inspector who might someday read them.

"Lie down," I said. "Don't — don't talk."

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and thin. "Charm-seller," he said, "giving orders," and then he coughed, and what came up was dark enough that I stopped pretending to myself about the arithmetic of his remaining time.

I will be honest, since honesty costs me nothing now: my first instinct was the door. My second instinct was also the door. Somewhere beneath those two instincts, under seven years of sediment, was a third one — an older one, wearing a uniform I'd burned — and it was that third instinct that knelt beside him and started tearing strips off my own shirt to pack the wound.

His side. Below the ribs. A cut, clean and deep, the kind made by a Warden's blade, which are blessed against healing the way other armies bless theirs against mercy.

"They followed me," he said. "From Vael. From—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Doesn't matter. Listen. The map. It only shows itself to — to certain hands. If it showed you the road, then it chose, and if it chose, then you're in this whether you take it or not."

"I'm not in anything," I said. "I'm a shopkeeper."

"Ashgrove sealed the gate." His eyes found mine and held them, and there was fever in them but there was something else too, something steady and awful, the look of a man who has seen a thing and cannot put it back where he saw it. "Seven years ago. Everyone says the gate closed itself. Everyone says the last warden died. But the Ember went quiet that same night, and quiet isn't dead, and I have spent six years learning the difference."

The bells stopped. All at once, mid-peal, like a breath cut off. The silence afterward was worse.

"What is the Ember?" I asked, because apparently I had decided, without consulting myself, to know things now.

He smiled. It was the worst smile I have ever seen — grief and relief holding hands over a grave. "You don't know. You really don't. Oh, that's — that's almost funny." He caught my wrist with surprising strength. "Listen to me. There isn't time for the whole of it, so here is the shape of it: the crown rules the king, and the king doesn't rule anything, and the woman on the throne isn't the woman she says she is, and the proof of all of it burns in the place where crowns were made, under Vael, and this—" he pushed the map against my chest, hard, like he was trying to push it through me into some safer man behind me, "—this is the only road there. And they know I have it. And they will search every house on this street by morning."

"Then give it back," I said. "Take it and go, I'll help you to the river, there are boats—"

"There are no boats for me." He said it simply, without self-pity, which somehow made it infinitely worse. "I've been dying since Dunmarrow. I ran this far because running was the only job left to me." His grip tightened. "There's a smuggler. Salt Road. Tamsin. If the map won't open for her alone — and it wouldn't, I tried, I tried everything — find her anyway. She has debts to me. She'll hate that. She'll come anyway."

"I'm not finding anyone. I'm not going anywhere. I sell charms."

"Yes," he said gently. "You do. That's the tragedy of it, isn't it? All that power folded up small, selling sweet milk." He let go of my wrist and lay back against my counter, and for a moment I thought he was done, and part of me — God forgive the arithmetic of fear — part of me calculated whether I could reach the door before he finished dying.

Then his eyes went past me, to the window, and went wide.

* * *

Here is what I should have done: blown out the lamp, gone still, waited. Here is what I actually did: I turned around to look, which presented the window with a perfect silhouette of a man standing over a bleeding stranger, and outside the window, moving down the street with the unhurried confidence of people who own the night, came four figures in grey.

Wardens. Not inspectors — inspectors knock. These were hunters, hooded, blades already drawn, and they moved the way water moves, filling the low places of the street without hurry because they knew, the way water knows, that everything flows toward them eventually.

They had not seen my window yet. They were gathered around something further down, near the corner by the cooper's yard, and for one bright moment I thought they'd lost him, thought he'd doubled back, thought—

Then I understood. They weren't looking for him anymore. They were looking at a trail. His trail. The blood, drop by drop, leading up the street to my door like a signature on a confession.

"He can't find it," the stranger whispered behind me. "If they take the map—"

"Then we burn it," I hissed. "There's a stove—"

"It doesn't burn." He laughed again, that drowned-candle laugh. "Nothing about it burns. It's the one fire they can't put out. That's rather the point." He tried to rise and failed, slid, and I caught him, and his weight told me the truth his voice was still lying about. "Door," he said. "Bolt it. Then the back way, through your storeroom — every shop on this street has a back way, I counted them coming in. Go south. Find the smuggler. Don't stop in Bray, whatever you do, don't stop in Bray."

"Why are you helping me?" I asked, and hated how small my voice came out. "You don't know me. You're spending your last hour on a stranger who sells anti-lice charms."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the grey shapes had reached the middle of the street. One of them crouched, touched the cobblestones, and rose again with its face turned toward my shop like a compass needle finding north.

"Because someone sealed a gate to save a city," he said, "and everyone forgot his name, and I thought — I thought if the map goes to him, maybe it means the story isn't finished. Maybe forgotten things come back." He pressed the map into my hands one final time and folded my fingers over it with both of his. "The Ember burns where the crown was made. Say it back."

"The Ember burns where the crown was made."

"Good. Now bolt the door and don't open it for anyone, and when they ask — and they will ask — you never saw me. You were asleep. Charm-sellers sleep early. Be furniture, Ashgrove. Be furniture and live."

And then, before I could say anything else — before I could tell him I didn't want his mission or his map or his dying faith in a man I hadn't been in seven years — he moved. It was the last strong thing in him and he spent it all at once: he hauled himself up, threw open my front door, and stepped out into the street with his arms spread wide, shouting, in a voice that cracked across the rooftops:

"HERE! It's HERE! Come and take it from a dead man!"

* * *

I will not describe all of it. Some things I keep on purpose, the way you keep a splinter, because removing it would hurt more than carrying it.

Four Wardens. One old man with a knife he must have taken from somewhere, because he had arrived at my door empty-handed. He fought the way I imagine he had done everything in his life — badly, bravely, and entirely on purpose. He dropped two of them. I didn't know a man could drop two Wardens; nobody drops Wardens; Wardens are the thing that happens to *you*. But he dropped them, and then the third one's blade went through him below the shoulder blade and came out the front, and the fourth one put a boot on his chest to hold him down while the light went out of him by degrees, the way a fire dies when you cover it — slowly, stubbornly, refusing right up until the end.

I stood behind my bolted door with my hand over my mouth and the map crushed against my chest, and I watched through the gap in the shutters, because some animal part of me insisted that witnessing was a debt, and I have always paid my debts.

The last thing he did — the very last, after the blade, while the Warden's boot was still on him — was turn his head toward my window. Toward me. He couldn't have seen me through the shutter-gap. I have told myself that four hundred times. He turned his head, and he smiled that terrible smile, and he mouthed two words.

*Be furniture.*

Then nothing. The bells stayed silent. The Wardens stood over him in the lamplight, breathing hard, and one of them said something I couldn't hear, and another laughed, briefly, horribly, the laugh of tired men, and I understood that to them this was Tuesday. This was paperwork. This was a body to drag to the ash-yard and a report to write in triplicate.

Then the tall one raised his head and looked directly at my window.

I have faced down a lot of frightening things in my life. I once stared down a river-god's minor avatar over a disputed toll. None of it was like those three seconds, when a hooded man looked at a gap in my shutters and I looked back and we both pretended, together, in perfect concert, that there was no one home.

He held up one finger. *One moment.* Then he crouched by the body, and did something I couldn't see, and when he stood, he was holding the stranger's knife, and he said something to the others, and they began dragging the corpse toward the cooper's yard — away from my door, away from the trail of blood that had led them there.

Away. Why away?

Because the knife was evidence, and the blood was evidence, and they had what they'd come for, and the man in the window was furniture. Furniture doesn't open doors for dying strangers. Furniture doesn't get visited by men who shout challenges at Wardens in the street. Furniture sleeps early.

Or because he wanted me to watch him decide. Because terror is a tool, and he was leaving it behind like a calling card, and the message was: *we know exactly which house, and we are choosing, tonight, to be merciful, and mercy can be revoked.*

Both, probably. With Wardens it's always both.

* * *

They took the body and the two fallen hunters — dragged their own wounded too, I noticed, because even wolves carry their wolves — and the street was empty, and the lamps burned, and Correnvale settled back into its sleep as though nothing had happened, because that is what cities do; that is their whole genius.

I sat down on my floor among the milk-charms and I held the map and I shook for a long time. I'm not ashamed of it. Shaking is honest work.

When I finally opened my hands, the map sat there, ordinary, brown, bloodstained, blank. I turned it over. Blank. I held it to the lamp. Blank, blank, blank — vellum with the ghost of sizing on it and nothing else, as if it had never been written on at all.

But it was warm. Warm as a held hand. And when I pressed my thumb flat against its center — I don't know why I did that; I have a list of things I don't know why I did that night and it grows every year — something moved under the surface. Not ink. Light. A thread of it, fine as a vein, waking up, wandering, and then — finding me. Settling into the pad of my thumb with a sensation like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was installed.

And for half a heartbeat I saw it. Roads. A road, one road, running south out of Correnvale like a spark down a fuse, through towns whose names I knew and one whose name I felt rather than read — *Bray, don't stop in Bray* — and onward, and downward, toward mountains, toward a broken crown of hills around a fallen citadel, toward a red glow deep under the stone like a heart seen through a ribcage.

Then it was gone. Blank vellum. Warm hands. A dead man in the cooper's yard whose name I never learned.

I should have burned it. I should have walked to the river and dropped it in. I should have done the smart thing, the survival thing, the seven-years-carefully-constructed thing.

Instead I sat on the floor of my shop until the sky went grey, doing sums. Because the tall Warden had spared me, and Wardens don't spare, which meant I was already in a ledger somewhere. Because the stranger had known my name, my real one, which meant someone was talking about gatekeepers in the dark places of the world. Because he'd said *find the smuggler, Tamsin, Salt Road,* and given me a debt to deliver to a woman I'd never met. And because the map had shown itself to me — to *me*, specifically, out of every hand in Correnvale — and the stranger had said the word *chose*, and I was beginning to suspect, with the particular dread of a man reading his own horoscope, that it had not chosen randomly.

By dawn I had packed a bag. I don't remember deciding to. That's the truth of it — I was shaking on the floor, and then I was packing a bag, and the decision happened somewhere in between, in the place where a man stops being furniture whether he consents to or not.

I left the lamp burning, the way shops look when their owner has just stepped out. I left the little sign that said *CHARMS — HONEST PRICES.* I locked the front door of the life I had built, put the key under the loose brick where I'd told no one it lived, and went out through the storeroom, through the back way, into the grey morning, south.

Behind me, Correnvale's bells rang the hour. Just the one bell. Just the normal number of times.

Ahead of me, at the mouth of Tanner's Row, leaning against a wall with her arms crossed and her eyes narrowed like I was a problem she'd been sent to solve, stood a woman with salt-road dust on her boots and a knife at her hip, watching my shop door as if she'd been waiting for it to open.

She looked at me. She looked at the bag on my shoulder. She smiled, and it was not a friendly smile, and I would spend many miles learning exactly how unfriendly it wasn't.

"You'll be the charm-seller," she said.
