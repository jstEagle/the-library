## 3. Tamsin of the Salt Road

There are three ways out of Correnvale, and I've used all of them, and none of them are good.

The river gate is for barges and bribes. The Salt Road runs south through the marsh causeway, which is fine if you like walking single file with your ankles in black water and every footstep audible to anyone with a grudge and a crossbow. And the Ashgrove gate — well. The Ashgrove gate has been sealed for seven years, warded shut by the last gatekeeper before he vanished into whatever hole cowards crawl into when the world gets expensive.

I was watching the Salt Road because it was the only one that made sense, and the man on it made no sense at all.

He came out of the city's shadow half an hour past midnight, moving like a man who had learned to walk quietly late in life and still didn't trust it. No lantern. No horse. A satchel clutched to his chest the way you'd hold a child, or a wound. He stopped at the edge of the causeway and looked back at Correnvale — at the smoke and the torchlight and the bells that had started ringing wrong an hour ago, fast and arrhythmic, the way they ring when the Wardens are hunting something.

Then he stepped onto the causeway, and I stepped out of the reeds, and he made a sound I'll be hearing in my dreams for a while. Something between a gasp and a prayer cut off mid-word.

"Evening," I said.

"It's—" He blinked at the sky, at the reeds, at me. "It's past midnight."

"So it is. Evening, then. Or morning. Greetings generally." I let him see the knife, casually, the way you'd show someone a coin trick. Not threatening. Just honest. "You're Rafael Ashgrove."

That landed harder than the knife would have. He went pale in the torchless dark, which is a particular kind of pale, the kind that means *how does this stranger know my name and should I start running now or after she stops talking.*

"I'm not—" he started.

"You're the charm-seller from Tanner's Row. The one whose stall got raided yesterday. The one who wasn't home when the Wardens came back tonight with questions." I tilted my head. "You're also the gatekeeper's son, but we can save that for the second course."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I make it my business to know things," I said, "and because right now, friend, you are the most interesting thing leaving this city, and I have had a very boring month."

He looked back toward Correnvale. The bells were still going. Somewhere behind us, down the causeway, a nightbird called — or something pretending to be a nightbird, which out here usually meant a toll-collector of the unofficial variety, meaning me, on other nights.

"They killed him," he said suddenly, quietly, to the marsh more than to me. "The man at my door. They killed him in the street and nobody even — people just closed their shutters."

Ah. So that was the shape of it. I'd heard the story already, secondhand, from a fishwife who heard it from a drunk who'd seen the whole thing: a wounded man staggering through Tanner's Row, collapsing against a charm-seller's door, whispering something before the grey cloaks caught up and put him down in the gutter. The Wardens had gone asking afterward. Doors. Questions. The usual choreography.

And now the charm-seller was running south at midnight with a satchel he held like a wound.

"The map," I said.

His hand moved toward the satchel before his brain could stop it. I watched him catch himself. Watched him decide to lie anyway, because people always do, because lying is the first instinct of the frightened and the guilty and, if I'm being honest, of everyone I've ever liked.

"What map?" he said.

I sighed. "Rafael. Sweet, doomed Rafael. Let me save us both some time. You are standing on a causeway in the middle of a marsh, alone, at night, with no horse, no plan, and no idea what you're carrying. I am standing between you and the only dry road south. There are three ways this conversation goes, and two of them end with me taking the satchel off your body. Let's aim for the third."

* * *

Here's the thing about my brother.

Davan was the clever one. Everyone said so, including Davan, frequently. Where I learned smuggling as a trade — routes, tariffs, which guards drink, which guards sleep — Davan learned it as a game. He liked the puzzles. He liked finding the path nobody else saw. Two years ago he took a job I told him not to take, from a client I told him not to trust, carrying something he wouldn't describe to somewhere he wouldn't name, and he came back wrong. Quiet. Drawing in that journal of his constantly, little diagrams, lines that bent where lines shouldn't bend. Then he left again, and he didn't come back at all.

They found his body in the spring. River take. His pack had been picked clean except for one thing wedged under the false bottom, stiff with river-rot: a folded square of leather that looked like nothing at all until you touched it, and then it looked like less than nothing, because it showed you nothing, blank as a licked plate.

I've carried it for two years. I've shown it to three fence-mages and a very expensive charlatan. Blank, blank, blank. I kept it anyway, because it was Davan's, and because I don't leave debts unpaid, and because some stubborn animal part of me believed the thing would open eventually, the way a fist opens, when the right hand asked it.

So when the fishwife described the dying man's words — *the Ember burns where the crown was made* — and when I heard the charm-seller from Tanner's Row had bolted the same night the Wardens started hunting, I did the arithmetic. It wasn't hard arithmetic. It was the kind Davan would have done in his sleep.

And when I saw the satchel clutched to Rafael Ashgrove's chest, I felt something in my chest go tight and hot, and I recognized it, because I'm not a sentimental woman, but I know hunger when it bites me.

* * *

We stood there a while, the two of us, negotiating without saying anything. Marsh wind. Distant bells. His breathing doing its best to steady itself and failing.

Finally he said, "If I give it to you, what do I get?"

Progress. "Passage south. My boat's moored at the far end of the causeway. Provisions, knowledge of the roads, and my considerable charm."

"Why would you help me?"

"Because you're paying me." I smiled. "In map."

"That's not helping. That's commerce."

"All helping is commerce. Anyone who tells you otherwise is about to ask you for something." I stepped closer, slow, hands visible. "Look. You can't read that thing. Whatever it is, it showed itself to a dying man and it's been silent for you ever since — I can see it in your face, you've tried. You need someone who knows the free cities, the border crossings, which lords Veyra's bought and which ones just pretend to be bought. I need—" I paused, and chose my lie with care, the way you choose footing on wet stone. "I need to get out of Correnvale's reach myself. The Wardens asked me questions too. We're both running. Only one of us knows the way."

It was a good lie. Ninety percent true, which is the correct ratio. The Wardens *had* spoken to me — I'd made sure to be seen asking them questions right back, loud enough that they'd remember my face as curious rather than involved. But the tenth part, the part about why I really wanted the map, stayed in my mouth where it belonged.

He studied me. He had honest eyes, this man, which is the worst kind. Eyes that want to believe. Eyes that will believe, given half a chance, and then hand you the knife handle-first because they think you're cold.

"The man at my door," he said slowly. "He said the Ember burns where the crown was made. Do you know what that means?"

"No," I said, which was true, and "do you?" which was the question I actually wanted answered.

"I think it's a place." He swallowed. "Crownforge. I think — there are stories. Under the citadel at Vael, before the fall. Where Aldous's crown was forged, back when forging crowns meant more than goldsmiths." He laughed, short and unhappy. "I'm a charm-seller. I sell love-lures that smell of lavender and luck-knots that unravel in the rain. Six months ago my biggest problem was a customer who wanted a refund. Now I'm standing in a marsh discussing dead kings with a smuggler who knows my name."

"Your reputation precedes you. Lavender love-lures, specifically. My brother bought one once." I hadn't planned to say that. It came out sideways, the way true things do when you're tired. "Didn't work. He was unlovable regardless."

Something flickered across his face. "Your brother?"

"Davan." I kept my voice flat as the marsh water. "He carried a map like yours once. Died for it, near as I can tell. So understand me, Rafael: I'm not offering to help you out of the goodness of my heart. My heart is a small organ with modest holdings. I'm offering because that map owes me, and I collect."

He was quiet for a long moment. The bells in Correnvale finally stopped, and the silence after them was worse, the way silence always is after a hunt ends — the question of *caught or escaped* hanging in the air unanswered.

"If I give you the map," he said carefully, "you take me south. All the way? Or just to the first crossing?"

"All the way. Crownforge, if that's truly where it points, though I'll want hazard pay for saying that sentence out loud."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take it anyway, and you can walk south behind me feeling righteous." I shrugged. "But you won't refuse. Because you looked back at the city just now, and you weren't looking at your shop. You were looking at where the man died. You're carrying something you think you owe him, and men like you don't set debts down in ditches."

I had him there and we both knew it. That's the trouble with honest eyes; they come attached to honest faces, and honest faces telegraph everything.

He opened the satchel.

And there it was — Davan's map, or its twin, and my heart did something embarrassing in my chest. Same leather. Same blankness, even now, even in his hands. He held it out and I reached for it and—

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Blank as ever, light as river-rot, warm in a way paper shouldn't be. I turned it over twice, felt the familiar nothing, and hated it a little, and loved it more.

"Well?" he said.

"It's a map," I said. "Technically."

"You can't read it either."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't say anything. Your face said it for you." He almost smiled — the first time, and it changed his whole face, made him look less like a rabbit and more like a man who'd once been somebody. "Great. Wonderful. Two of us, one unreadable map, and a legend about a dead king's forge. This is how every disaster I've ever heard of begins."

"Disasters begin with confidence," I said, tucking the map into my coat, where Davan's ghost could keep it company. "This is just uncertainty. Much safer. Boat's at the end of the causeway. Walk behind me, step where I step, and if anything in the water sings to you, ignore it. It lies."

"That was a joke, presumably."

"Half. The other half you'll learn on the road."

* * *

We walked. The marsh gave way to the long shingle spit where my boat — the *Kestrel*, thirty feet of bad decisions and good timber — sat at her mooring like a patient dog. I got us underway while Rafael stood in the stern looking back at Correnvale until the smoke of it was just a smudge, and then a memory, and then nothing.

He didn't talk much. Good. Talking on the water wastes breath needed for hauling. But somewhere past the second bend of the channel, with the salt coming in off the flats, he said, "Tamsin. That's your name? Tamsin of the Salt Road?"

"It's what they call me. Mostly with affection. Occasionally with warrants."

"Why are you really doing this?"

There it was again. Honest eyes, aimed at me like a lamp. I checked my rigging that didn't need checking and gave him the ninety-percent truth. "Because the Wardens burned a friend's barge last spring over three pounds of untaxed tea, and because Veyra's border taxes are strangling every route I run, and because a man died outside your door carrying my brother's map, and I want to know what killed him. Pick whichever answer you like best. People usually do."

He nodded slowly, accepting it, or appearing to, and I felt the familiar small shame of the successful lie — the one that works so smoothly you barely feel it go in.

What I didn't tell him, what I kept below the waterline where I keep everything that matters: the map had warmed in my coat. Not much. A degree, maybe two. The same faint warmth it had given off exactly once before — the day I pulled it from Davan's drowned pack, the day I swore on his grave I'd finish whatever he'd started.

It was warming now, as we sailed south, as if it knew where it was going.

As if it had chosen.

I looked at Rafael Ashgrove — exhausted, terrified, trusting — and I thought: *you poor fool. You have no idea what you've signed on for.*

Neither, I suspect, do I.

But the tide was with us, and the map was warm, and Crownforge waited at the end of whatever story Davan had died halfway through telling.

I meant to find out how it ended.

I just hadn't yet decided how much truth I'd spend along the way.
