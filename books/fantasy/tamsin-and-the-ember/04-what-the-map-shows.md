## 4. What the Map Shows

### Rafael

The map did nothing for three days.

I know because I checked. Every time Tamsin stopped to water the mule — she'd named it Duchess, which told me a great deal about her sense of humor and none of it reassuring — I'd take the map out of my coat and unroll it on my knee and look at it the way you look at a locked door when you've lost the key. Plain vellum. Faded ink. A few roads sketched south out of Correnvale, a river, some mountains drawn by someone who clearly loved mountains more than accuracy. Nothing that whispered *the Ember burns where the crown was made*. Nothing that justified a man dying in the street with his blood cooling between the cobbles while I stood in my own doorway like a coward holding a parcel.

Three days of nothing, and then, on the fourth morning, it woke up.

We'd camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border forts that nobody had bothered to knock down after the wars because knocking things down costs money and leaving them standing costs nothing but bad dreams. Tamsin was asleep. I was supposed to be asleep too, but sleep and I had been on poor terms since the night the stranger died, so instead I sat with the map across my knees, tracing the road south with one finger, thinking useless thoughts.

And the ink moved.

Not dramatically. I want to be clear about that, because later — when everything got worse — people would ask me what it was like, and they always expected fire, thunder, the map leaping off my lap and singing. It wasn't like that. It was like watching frost form on a window: slow, quiet, inevitable, as if the lines had always been there and were only now deciding to let me see them. The faded roads darkened. New roads surfaced between them, thin as veins under skin. And through the whole sheet ran a single thread of color I can only describe as warm — a line of ember-orange that started at the southern edge and wound up and up into the mountains, toward a mark I hadn't seen before, a small circle with a hammer struck through it.

Crownforge. Though the map didn't say so. The map said nothing at all. It just glowed faintly against my knees like a coal remembering it used to be fire, and my hand — my traitor, seven-years-buried hand — tingled all the way up to the elbow.

I should have put it away. I should have woken Tamsin. I did neither. Instead I sat alone in the cold with a forbidden thing unfolding itself for me, and I felt — this is the shameful part — I felt *chosen*. Seven years of making myself small, of selling love-charms that were just rose oil and suggestion, of flinching every time a grey cloak came down the street, and here was proof that whatever lived in me hadn't died of neglect after all. It had been waiting. The map had been waiting. For me, apparently, specifically, which raised questions I very much did not want to ask, starting with: how does a dead stranger's map know what I am?

"You're doing it wrong," Tamsin said.

I nearly went over backward into the watchtower rubble. She was sitting up, hair flat on one side, watching me with the expression of someone who has caught a child stealing from the biscuit tin and is more tired than angry.

"Doing what wrong?"

"Whatever you're doing to it." She crawled over, pulled her cloak around her shoulders, and held out her hand. "Give it here."

Every instinct I owned said no. But she'd gotten me out of Correnvale past two checkpoints and a toll bridge, and she hadn't yet asked me the question I most dreaded — *what are you?* — so I handed it over, mostly because refusing felt like answering anyway.

She unrolled it and stared at it, and I watched her face do something complicated. Not surprise. Recognition, maybe, or its cousin. Her finger hovered over the vellum without touching, the way you hover your hand over a stove to check if it's hot.

"Nothing," she said, after a long moment. "It's just a map."

"It isn't."

"No," she agreed, too quickly, "it isn't," and gave it back, and lay down again with her back to me, and that was the end of the conversation. Which is how I learned that Tamsin of the Salt Road could lie with her whole body and not just her mouth. I didn't know yet what she was lying about. I want that on the record. Whatever came later, whatever she kept folded up inside her along with that journal I never saw until it was far too late — that morning, all I knew was that she'd looked at a living map and called it dead, and that she'd done it smoothly enough that she must have practiced.

I traced the ember-line with my fingertip until dawn. South, then east, then up. Beneath a mountain marked with a broken tower. Toward Vael.

Toward the place where crowns were made, if the stranger's last words meant what they seemed to mean.

### Tamsin

Here's what Rafael doesn't know, and what I intend to keep him not knowing for as long as the road allows: I've seen that map before. Held it before. Watched it do its little frost-and-embers trick before, in a ditch outside Harrow Fen, with my brother's blood still tacky on the corners.

Doran carried it south for six weeks. He wrote to me twice from the road — short letters, coded the way we used to code manifests, because Doran never met a sentence he couldn't make safer by making it duller. The second letter said: *Found a buyer who says the goods are worth more than coin. Says they choose their carrier. Says I'll understand when I touch them.* Three days later a fisherman found him in the reeds with his pockets turned out and his boots gone, which is the standard etiquette of murder on the Salt Road, and the map was nowhere on him, and I spent four months and most of my savings trying to find out where it had gone.

Turns out it went to a charm-seller in Correnvale. Turns out the world is small and cruel and has a sense of humor.

So no, I didn't tell him. You don't hand a man a second grief when he's still carrying the first one badly. Besides — and this is the part I keep circling back to, the part that keeps me up when Duchess snores — if the map *chooses* its carrier, the way Doran's buyer claimed, then it chose Doran first. And it let him die. Or it led him to dying; on the Salt Road those are often the same thing. And now it's chosen Rafael, who is possibly the worst-armed, softest-handed, most obviously guilty-looking man ever to walk out of a city gate, and it's chosen me too, somehow, because when I touched it in that ditch outside Harrow Fen — before I knew what it was, when it was just my dead brother's mysterious cargo — the lines moved for me too.

Two carriers. One map. Either the thing is greedy, or it needs us both, and I have met enough cursed cargo in my life to know that *needs us both* is almost never good news.

What I told Rafael was that I wanted passage money and a guide who wouldn't get us killed. What I actually wanted was to finish what Doran started, find out what was worth his life, and put it in the ground over his grave if it came to that. What I said out loud, the morning after I watched him pet the map like it was a cat, was:

"We follow the bright line or we sell it. Those are the options. I've been generous letting you pick, but Dunmarrow's three days south and after that I stop being patient."

Rafael looked up from the map with the expression of a scholar who's been asked to summarize scripture and knows he'll be struck down either way. "You've seen it. The line."

"Obviously."

"And you know where it goes."

"I know where it *ends*," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because knowing the name Vael and knowing what waited under Vael were separated by rather a lot of country. "Broken citadel in the Grayreach Mountains. Nobody goes there. That's usually an excellent reason not to go there."

"Then why—"

"Because my brother died carrying it, and I don't leave family debts on the road." There. A piece of the truth, offered up like a coin to a beggar — small enough to satisfy him, real enough to pass inspection. He took it. They always take it. People are desperate to believe the first honest thing you tell them, because it means they can stop checking the rest.

He rolled the map up with hands that weren't quite steady. "It showed me Crownforge," he said quietly. "There's a forge-mark under the mountain. And the line — Tamsin, the line runs *through* things. Through towns that aren't on any other map. Through a gate in the mountains that I don't think exists anymore. It's not showing us a road. It's showing us a route that somebody planned, a long time ago, and hid."

"Hid from whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He looked south, toward the gray smudge of the Grayreaches, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Correnvale: the Wardens behind us, the hunters that had killed the stranger somewhere ahead of us, and between them and us one mule, one smuggler, and a man whose entire combat experience consisted of haggling. "Whoever made this map wanted someone to reach the Ember. Eventually. When the time was right."

"And you think the time is right now?"

"I think," Rafael said, "that the map woke up four days after a man was murdered for carrying it. So either the time is right, or the map has a very grim sense of humor." He paused. "It's choosing, isn't it. Choosing who sees it."

I didn't answer that. I swung up onto Duchess and pointed her south, and let the silence do my lying for me, which is the oldest trick in the trade and still the best.

But here's the thing I didn't say, the thing I'm writing down now because paper doesn't interrupt: when Rafael touched the map that night, the ember-line didn't just glow. It *leaned* toward him. I saw it. The way a plant leans toward light, the way a dog leans toward the one person at the table who'll feed it under the cloth. And when I touched it in the ditch outside Harrow Fen, four months ago, it had leaned toward me the same way.

One map. Two leanings. Either it's broken, or it's hungry, or —

Or it's not choosing a carrier at all. Maybe it's choosing a pair. Maybe whatever waits under Vael needs a hand that can read magic and a hand that can read roads, and the map is just the leash, and we're both already caught at the end of it.

That afternoon we crossed the first ridge and saw the Grayreaches proper for the first time — white peaks going pink in the late sun, and beneath them, so far off it was barely a suggestion, a black notch in the mountainside that might have been a pass or might have been a wound. Rafael stopped walking. He had the map half-unrolled against his chest, and the ember-line was burning brighter than I'd ever seen it, bright enough to show through the vellum like a vein of ore.

"It knows we're close," he said.

"No," I said, and nudged Duchess forward. "It knows *you're* close. Let's hope that's enough."

Behind us, far back along the road we'd come, something glinted — grey, brief, gone. Could have been sun on a stream. Could have been a cloak.

I didn't mention that either.
