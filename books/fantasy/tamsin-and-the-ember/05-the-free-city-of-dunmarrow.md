## 5. The Free City of Dunmarrow

Dunmarrow smelled the way it always had — salt, tar, frying onions, and the particular desperation of people pretending everything was fine. Free cities always smelled like that. Freedom, in my experience, was mostly a smell.

I'd been coming here since I was sixteen, when my brother Corwin first walked me through the Salt Gate and told me the three rules of Dunmarrow: never drink anything a priest pours you, never owe money to anyone with a boat named after a woman, and never, ever tell the truth in the Fishmarket unless you're selling something. He'd said it laughing. He'd been dead four years now, and I still heard him say it every time I came through those gates.

Rafael came through behind me, hunched into his coat like a man trying to fold himself small enough to post through a letter slot. Seven years of hiding will do that to a person. He kept his eyes down and his hands in his pockets, which I'd learned meant he was touching the map through the lining of his sleeve. He did it constantly. Like a man checking whether his heart was still beating.

"Stay close," I told him. "And if anyone asks, you're my cousin from upriver. Dull as ditchwater. You've never seen magic in your life."

"I haven't," he said. "Not since Ashgrove."

"That's the spirit. Lie like you mean it."

The city had changed since my last run, two years back. The lower quarter, where the smugglers' stalls used to sprawl along the canal like barnacles on a hull, was half-empty now, shuttered fronts and chalked notices: *CONSCRIPTED. REPORT TO THE GREY MUSTER.* And spilling out of the warehouses along the eastern wall were people who hadn't been here before — families, mostly, sitting on bundles they'd carried too far, cooking over little fires they'd built too carefully, in the way of people who'd learned that fires get confiscated.

Refugees. From the border war. From Veyra's war, though nobody in Dunmarrow called it hers yet. They called it "the trouble east." The trouble east had taken their farms, their sons, their daughters, and now it sat at the edge of their fields wearing grey and waiting for spring.

A girl no older than ten watched us pass, holding a wooden bird she'd whittled herself. She held it out. Not selling it. Just showing me, the way children do, as if to say: *look, I made this, the world is still a place where things can be made.*

I gave her a copper I couldn't afford and hated myself for the arithmetic.

* * *

We found supplies where supplies could always be found in Dunmarrow: Marda's chandlery, which sold candles in front and moved everything else out back. Marda herself was behind the counter, older and wider and exactly as sharp, and when she saw me her face did the thing all old smugglers' faces do — nothing at all, while the eyes counted exits.

"Tamsin Vale," she said. "Thought you were dead."

"You're the third person this year. It's becoming a reputation."

"Reputations keep you alive longer than swords." She leaned on the counter. "Word is you went quiet after Corwin. Word is you took it badly."

"Word is right."

"And now you're walking the Salt Road with a soft-handed gatekeeper who looks at my candles like they might bite him." She flicked her eyes past me to Rafael, who was examining a rack of tallow dips with the focused intensity of a man who wanted very much not to be part of this conversation. "Trouble follows you, girl. Or you follow it. Hard to say which."

"I need rope, oil, hardbread, and whatever passes for meat this season. And a room that doesn't have ears."

Marda named a price that would have made a bishop blush. I paid it without blinking, because blinking is for amateurs, and because the coin purse was Rafael's anyway, and there is a special pleasure in spending a nervous man's money slowly.

It was in the back room, over crates of lamp oil, that the real business found me. Because of course it did. In Dunmarrow, business always finds you; you just pick which kind.

* * *

His name was Ferro Callas, and he'd been one of Corwin's runners back when Corwin ran the salt road like it was his own vein. I'd known him since I was nineteen. He'd carried me out of a burning warehouse in Bray once, and I'd never quite forgiven him for how smug he'd been about it afterward.

He was waiting for me in the yard behind Marda's, leaning against the wall with his arms crossed, wearing a new coat that was too good for him. That coat told me more than any greeting could.

"Tamsin." He smiled. "Corwin's sister. Walking around like the dead don't gossip."

"Ferro. Still ugly?"

"Still honest, which is worse." He pushed off the wall. "You've got something. I can smell it on you. Same smell Corwin had, the last run. The map smell."

My stomach did what stomachs do when they know what's coming. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No? Then you won't mind hearing that there are people asking after a map. Serious people. People with coin." He let that sit. "Grey-cloaked people, even, though they're paying through intermediaries, because apparently even usurpers don't trust their own laundry. Three hundred crowns, Tamsin. For paper. For *one piece of paper*."

Three hundred crowns. I'd run cargo for two years for less than that. I thought about the girl with the wooden bird, and the refugees along the wall, and the price of rope these days, and the fact that Rafael Ashgrove was a stranger I'd known eleven days, whose name I only knew because he'd finally told it to me drunk on plum brandy somewhere past the second ford.

"Who's asking?" I said, which was already a betrayal, and we both knew it, because a smart woman says *no thank you* and walks away.

Ferro shrugged elaborately. "Intermediaries. But the coin's real. And here's the thing, Tamsin — they're not just offering. They're *asking*. Politely. Which means next week they stop being polite." He touched my arm, gentle as an old friend, which he was, which made it worse. "You've got debts. I know you've got debts. The Bray crowd has been asking after you too, and they're not polite on their best day. Three hundred crowns clears everything. You could walk away clean. Start over. Isn't that what you've wanted since Corwin died? To be done?"

To be done. Gods, yes. To be done was the whole dream, wasn't it? Every smuggler's prayer. One last job, one clean exit, a cottage somewhere the maps forgot.

"I'll think about it," I said.

Ferro nodded like a man who'd already won. "Think fast. The intermediaries drink at the Gilded Eel. I'll be there tomorrow night. Bring the paper or bring your regrets." He squeezed my arm once and walked out through the yard gate, his too-good coat swinging, and I stood there among the lamp oil and hated him, and hated myself more, because the worst part — the truly unforgivable part — was that some cold practical corner of my brain had already started doing sums.

* * *

Rafael was waiting at the room Marda had rented us, sitting on the narrow bed with the map spread across his knees, which was either very brave or very stupid, and having known him eleven days I'd stopped betting against stupid.

"We need to talk about money," I said.

"We do?" He looked up. He had that open face, that terrible open face, the kind that gets men like him killed in cities like this. "How bad is it?"

"Not the money we have. The money other people want to give us." I sat down across from him, because lying to a man's face is easier standing, and I needed the difficulty. "Someone offered me three hundred crowns today. For the map."

I watched it land. Watched him go still, the way he did — not frightened, exactly. Bracing. A man who'd spent seven years learning that the world took things from him and getting ahead of the taking.

"And you're telling me because—"

"Because you'd find out anyway. Because Ferro Callas has eyes everywhere in this city. And because—" I stopped. Here was the moment where a better woman would have lied completely, smoothly, and gone to the Gilded Eel tomorrow with the map rolled up her sleeve. Here was the moment where the smarter version of me — the Corwin-trained version, the survivor — would have already been halfway to rich.

"Because?" he prompted softly.

"Because you paid my debt at Bray without asking what it was," I said, which wasn't the whole truth but was at least true-shaped. "Because you gave a charm to a child in the pass who'll never know what it cost you. Because you're the most trusting idiot I've ever traveled with and someone has to look out for you, and apparently the gods, in their infinite humor, decided it's me."

He almost smiled. "So you're protecting me."

"I'm protecting an investment. Don't flatter yourself."

But he was looking at me with those open eyes, and I swear to every god I don't believe in, he believed me. Completely. Without reservation. And that belief sat on my chest like a stone, because I had walked into this room carrying three hundred crowns' worth of temptation and I'd told him maybe a tenth of it, and he trusted the tenth like it was the whole.

"The offer," he said carefully. "Is it still open?"

"It's always open. That's what makes it dangerous."

"And what do you want to do?"

What did I want to do. What a question. What a cruel, stupid, wonderful question, because nobody had asked me what I wanted in four years — they'd asked what I owed, what I carried, what I'd take, what I'd risk. Corwin had asked me what I wanted once. Right before the last run. *Come away with me after this one, Tam. South, past the free cities, where nobody's ever heard of a Vale.* And then he'd died carrying a map I now had folded inside another man's coat, and I'd spent four years not wanting anything so that wanting couldn't kill me too.

"I want to sleep," I said. "Go sell your charms or whatever it is you do in the evening. I'm going out."

* * *

I went to the Gilded Eel. Of course I went. Not to deal — I told myself — just to listen. To learn who the intermediaries were, how serious the coin was, whether Ferro was playing me or playing them. Listening isn't betraying. Listening is due diligence. I could hear myself building the scaffolding of excuses as I walked, plank by plank, and the horrible thing was that each plank was load-bearing.

The Eel was loud and low-ceilinged and smelled of spilled beer and false laughter. Ferro was at a corner table with two people I didn't know: a fat man with merchant's rings and a thin woman with grey eyes that didn't blink enough. Grey eyes. I clocked that and filed it under *bad*.

"Tamsin!" Ferro waved me over like we were family. Maybe we were. Families and smuggling crews have roughly the same survival rate. "Sit. These are friends of friends. No names tonight."

"No names," agreed the fat man pleasantly. "Just business. You have access to a certain document. We have access to a certain sum. The sum grows daily, incidentally. Our principals are eager."

"Eager people make me nervous," I said. "Nervous people pay extra."

The grey-eyed woman smiled without warmth. "Then you'll love our final figure. Five hundred. Tonight. The document stays with us, the debt collectors in Bray mysteriously lose your name, and you walk out of this city richer than you've ever been." She turned her grey eyes fully on me, and there was something underneath them, something patient and cold, like water under ice. "Or you walk out poorer than you've ever been. Your choice, Mistress Vale. But choose knowing that we already know about your brother. We know about the last run. We know what he carried, and we know what you carry now. You were never going to be able to put it down. The only question left is who picks it up."

Five hundred crowns. My debts were two hundred and forty. I could burn the rest, sail south, buy the cottage Corwin dreamed of, plant something, grow something, become a woman who had never heard of kings or embers or soft-handed gatekeepers with terrible open faces.

I could see it so clearly. That was the trap of wanting — it renders in perfect detail.

And underneath the detail, faint as a watermark, I saw Rafael on the bed with the map across his knees, saying *what do you want to do*, and meaning it, meaning it the way nobody had meant anything to me in years.

"I need until tomorrow night," I heard myself say. "Same terms. Same table."

The grey-eyed woman studied me for a long moment, and I understood that she was deciding whether I was a seller or a problem, and that both answers ended with her remembering my face forever.

"Tomorrow night," she said. "Bring the map, or bring a very good reason."

I walked back through Dunmarrow's dark streets with the coins I didn't take jingling in my head, past the refugee fires burning low and careful, past the chalked doors and the conscription notices, past a sentry who looked at me twice and remembered my face too.

The room was dark when I got back. Rafael was asleep, or pretending to be, curled toward the wall with his coat over him like a boy. On the table beside him, laid out neat and deliberate, were the remains of his supper — and mine. He'd saved me half of everything. Bread, cheese, two plums, arranged with the fussy precision of a man who worried about people and had no idea how to hide it.

I stood in the doorway a long time.

Then I crossed the room, took the map from his coat — gently, gently, the way you take a knife from a sleeping friend — and stepped back out into the night, telling myself I was only going to look at it one more time. Only to hold it. Only to know, before tomorrow, what five hundred crowns actually felt like in the hand.

The streets were empty. The refugee fires had burned to embers. Somewhere east, beyond the walls and the hills and the border, Veyra's armies were winning, and somewhere south, under a fallen citadel, something burned that kings had bled for, and I stood alone in a dark street in a free city holding the most dangerous piece of paper in the world, and I could not make my feet move in either direction.

Behind me, a door opened. Footsteps. Soft ones.

"Tamsin?" Rafael's voice, rough with sleep. "Where are you going?"

I closed my hand around the map and turned around.
