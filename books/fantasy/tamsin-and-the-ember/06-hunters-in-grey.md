## 6. Hunters in Grey

There is a particular quality to Dunmarrow's lower quarter that I can only describe as *honest rot*. Correnvale's slums pretend at respectability — they keep their shutters painted, their gutters swept, their misery dressed in its Sunday clothes. Dunmarrow doesn't bother. The lower quarter sprawls along the river like something the city coughed up and decided to live with, all leaning timber and wet stone, lanterns hung on hooks that had long since given up holding anything heavier than hope.

I noticed the grey cloaks before I understood what they were.

That is the thing about fear — it arrives ahead of its own explanation. We had come down from the Salt Road gate at midday, Tamsin walking half a step ahead of me the way she always did, as though the road were a debt she intended to collect personally. Oren was behind us. He'd joined us two days out of the pass without ever quite explaining why, and I had learned not to ask questions whose answers I couldn't afford.

The market square of the lower quarter smelled of fish and tar and frying onions, and it was full, and fullness should have meant safety. Crowds are cover. That was smuggler logic, and I'd absorbed enough of it by osmosis to trust it. But the grey cloaks moved wrong for a crowd. They didn't jostle. They didn't stop to haggle. They flowed, four of them, the way water flows into a low place, and every person they passed seemed to lean away without knowing why.

"Tamsin," I said quietly.

"I see them." She didn't turn her head. She'd shifted her grip on the strap of her pack, casual as a woman adjusting a burden, but her knuckles had gone white around the leather. "Don't run. Running tells them everything."

"Then what do we do?"

"We shop." And she actually smiled, thin and sharp as a fish knife, and steered us toward a stall piled with river eels. "Rafael. Look at the eels."

I looked at the eels. They were excellent eels, silver-grey and writhing, and I have never been less interested in anything in my life. Behind the stall, a broad woman with forearms like mooring ropes watched Tamsin with an expression I slowly recognized as recognition.

"Marta," Tamsin said.

"Salt Road Tamsin." The woman's eyes flicked to me, then past my shoulder to Oren, then back. Her voice dropped. "You've got trouble walking behind you prettier than you deserve."

"How many?"

"Four I've counted. Maybe six. They came off the *Warden's Mercy* this morning, asking after travelers off the mountain passes. Asking after a man who sells charms." Marta's gaze settled on me with the gentle pity one reserves for livestock. "Girl, whatever you're carrying, it's worth more dead than alive to somebody."

"That's reassuring," I said.

"It wasn't meant to be." Marta wrapped three eels in waxed paper and pushed them across the stall. "Back way's through the smokehouse. Go left at the tanning vats, right at the well nobody drinks from. You'll come out by the ferry stairs."

Tamsin paid her — overpaid her, I suspected; there was a whole silent economy happening between those two women that I wasn't party to — and we walked away from the stall at the pace of people with nowhere urgent to be. My heart was hammering so hard I could feel it in my teeth.

*The Ember burns where the crown was made.* The dying stranger's whisper had followed me out of Correnvale, across the Ashgrove hills, over the pass. Now it seemed to follow me into a fish market, wearing grey.

We turned down the alley beside the smokehouse. It was narrow enough that our shoulders nearly brushed the walls, dark enough that the lantern-light from the square barely reached its middle. Somewhere above, gulls argued over something dead.

And then the light behind us went out.

Not dimmed. *Out*, all at once, as if someone had dropped a shutter over the mouth of the alley, and with it went the noise of the market — swallowed, muffled, gone. The silence that replaced it had weight. It pressed against my ears like deep water.

I knew that silence. I had spent seven years learning every texture of hidden magic, and this one I knew the way you know a scar: a silencing ward, laid quick and expert, cutting the alley off from the world.

"They're here," I breathed.

"No," said Oren, behind us. His voice was very calm, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "They were here. Now they're sorry about it."

He stepped past me, and I saw him properly for the first time in that bad light — saw him roll his shoulders once, loose, like a man settling into an old chair, and draw his sword. Not fast. Unhurried. The blade came out of its sheath with a sound like a sigh.

Grey shapes detached themselves from the shadows ahead of us. Four of them, as advertised. Their hoods were back now, and their faces were the worst part — not cruel, not eager. *Attentive*. Faces like clerks preparing to balance a ledger.

"The map," said the foremost hunter. A woman, tall, with a voice like dry paper. "And the charm-seller. The other two may walk away."

"Generous," said Tamsin.

"The Crown is merciful."

"The Crown," Tamsin said, "is a hat."

What happened next happened quickly, and I confess I missed most of it, because most of me was busy being terrified and the rest was busy watching Oren do something I still don't have the right words for.

The hunters moved first — all four at once, no signal I could see, spreading across the width of the alley like fingers closing. And Oren met them the way a door meets a storm: not by fighting the wind but by simply being where it could not go. He didn't seem to move so much as to already be wherever their blades arrived a half-heartbeat too late. One hunter lunged and found Oren's pommel against his jaw instead of steel in Oren's chest. Another swung and hit nothing, and then hit the wall, hard, guided there by a hand on her shoulder that redirected her entire momentum into the stones.

It wasn't flashy. That was the unsettling thing. There was no flourish, no roar, none of the theater soldiers use to frighten each other. It was quiet and economical and utterly certain, like watching a man who had done this so many times that the doing had worn smooth, the way a stair wears hollow under ten thousand feet.

The third hunter got a hand inside his guard — I'll give her that much — and put a knife into his side before he put her into the ground. He grunted, more annoyed than hurt, and she folded around his knee-strike and did not get up.

The fourth ran. I will always remember that: the last hunter, seeing three companions fall in the space of perhaps twenty heartbeats, simply chose survival and fled up the alley into the dark, and Oren let her go. Let her. He stood there breathing lightly, sword dripping, and watched her go as if crossing a name off a list.

The silencing ward broke like a soap bubble. Sound rushed back in — gulls, market cries, the river, a dog barking somewhere — absurdly ordinary, as if the world hadn't just tried to end in a fish alley.

"You're bleeding," Tamsin said to Oren.

"He nicked me." Oren cleaned his blade on a dead man's cloak with the absent efficiency of long habit. "It'll keep."

"*It'll keep?*" My voice came out higher than I intended. "There were four of them. Four trained — those were Wardens' hunters, or close enough, and you killed three of them in the time it took me to remember how to breathe. Who *are* you?"

Oren looked at me. In the gloom his eyes were pale, almost colorless, and very old in a young face.

"Not here," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting until we're behind a locked door." He bent, and with two efficient motions searched the nearest body, coming up with a purse, a knife, and — this he held up to the lantern-light spilling weakly from a smokehouse window — a small disc of grey metal, stamped with a crown. "Token. Hunters carry them to prove a kill. Or—" he turned it over, and his jaw tightened at something on the reverse, "—to report one."

"Report to whom?"

"To whoever holds the other half." He pocketed it. "Move. The fourth one will have friends, and friends of hunters don't knock."

* * *

The locked door turned out to belong to a ferryman named Col, a squat, leathery man who owed Tamsin either money or his life — the distinction seemed not to matter to either of them — and who rented us his back room above the boathouse without asking a single question, which in my experience is the most expensive kind of hospitality.

Oren sat on the floor with his back to the wall, the knife wound in his side bound with a strip of shirt, and refused the bed, the chair, and the wine. Tamsin paced. I sat on the bed and held my own hands still because if I didn't give them a job they were going to shake.

"Now," I said. "Who are you?"

"My name is Oren."

"I know your *name*. I want to know how a man with a sword arm like that happens to be walking the Salt Road alone, and how he happens to find us in a fish market in a city he has no reason to be in, at the exact moment Veyra's hunters find us too."

"Coincidence," said Tamsin, from her pacing.

"Do you believe that?"

She stopped pacing. She looked at Oren for a long moment, and I watched her weigh something — I've seen her do it before, weighing whether a lie will hold a person's weight or crack under it — and then she looked away and said, "No. But I believe he saved our lives, and I believe he's bleeding on Col's floor, which buys a man some patience."

"Patience." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Do you understand what those hunters said? *The charm-seller.* Not the travelers. Not the smuggler. Me. Someone told them what I look like and what I do, and someone told them we'd come through Dunmarrow, and someone—" my voice cracked, and I hated it, "—someone knows about the map. Seven years I kept my head down. Seven years I made myself so small even the Wardens forgot the Ashgrove gate had a keeper. And within a month of touching that cursed thing, hunters find me in another kingdom."

Nobody spoke. Downstairs, Col's ferry knocked against its mooring, patient as a heartbeat.

"Someone is leaking our route," I said. "That's the plain truth of it. The stranger who gave me the map died in the street. The Wardens in Correnvale knew to raid my stall the same week I took it in. The hunters in the pass — you told me yourself, Tamsin, they were waiting at the only ford we needed. And now this. Four hunters, in the lower quarter, before we'd been in the city half a day." I looked from her to Oren and back. "So either the map itself is calling to them—"

"It might be," Oren said quietly.

"—or one of the three people who know where we're going is telling them."

The silence that followed had teeth. Tamsin's face had gone carefully blank, which I had learned, in our weeks together, was the face she wore when she was thinking fastest. Oren simply watched me, unblinking, pale-eyed, and I realized with a small cold shock that I could not read him at all. With Tamsin I could at least see the lies moving underneath, like fish under ice. Oren gave me nothing. He was ice all the way down, or he was the best liar I had ever met, and I genuinely could not tell which possibility frightened me more.

"If I were the traitor," Oren said at last, mildly, "would I have killed three hunters to protect you?"

"If you were clever, yes. Nothing buys trust like spilled blood. And you knew they were coming. You said so in the alley — *they were here, now they're sorry about it.* How did you know, Oren? Before the ward went up, before the first cloak showed, you were already rolling your shoulders. You were ready. Men aren't ready by accident."

For the first time, something moved behind those pale eyes. Not guilt. Something closer to regret, or maybe to tiredness — the tiredness of a man carrying a story too heavy to set down and too dangerous to open.

"I knew," he said, "because I've been hunting these hunters longer than they've been hunting you. I know their methods. I know they seal an alley before they spring it, and I know the smell of a silencing ward if you're standing downwind of it, and I know that when four hunters work a market, they always leave one pair watching the exits — which means there are two more out there right now, wondering why their friends stopped answering." He flexed his bandaged side and winced, the first honest human motion I'd seen from him. "That's how I knew. Not treachery. Arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I repeated.

"War is mostly arithmetic, charm-seller. The rest is waiting." He closed his eyes, apparently deciding the conversation was over. "Sleep if you can. We leave before dawn, downriver. And Rafael—"

"Yes?"

"When the time comes that you have to decide who to trust, decide fast, and don't look back. Doubt is a luxury for people the hunters haven't found yet."

He said it like a kindness. It landed like a threat. I lay back on Col's narrow bed and stared at the ceiling beams, and listened to Tamsin settle herself against the door — she always took the door; I'd stopped asking why — and I turned the day over in my mind like a coin I suspected was clipped.

Three hunters dead. A swordsman who fought like memory rather than skill. A token stamped with a crown, reporting to *whoever holds the other half*. And somewhere in Dunmarrow's honest rot, two more grey cloaks wondering where we'd gone, and somewhere beyond them, in the occupied capital, a usurper queen who had known my face before I'd ever crossed her border.

*The charm-seller.*

I closed my eyes and saw the map in my mind's eye, the way it had shown itself to me on the road — ink rising like veins under skin, the path threading south toward the fallen citadel of Vael, toward Crownforge, toward the words burned into my memory by a dying man's breath.

*Tamsin*, I thought, *where were you really, the night your brother died?*

But I didn't ask. I was afraid of the answer, and more afraid of what asking would cost me, because the truth was I had started to trust her — truly trust her, the way you trust a rope you're hanging from — and a man hanging from a rope does not go looking for frays.

Outside, the river kept talking to itself, and somewhere in the lower quarter a bell rang the hour, and beneath the bell, faint and far off, I thought I heard footsteps on the boathouse stairs.

Two sets. Pausing at each landing.

Oren opened his eyes.
