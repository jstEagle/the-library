## 7. Oren's Bargain

I have killed men for less than what I was about to do, and I have lied to better people than either of them. So when I say that following Rafael Ashgrove and the smuggler woman through Dunmarrow's lower quarter felt like walking into my own funeral with my boots on the wrong feet, understand that I say it as a man with considerable experience of bad decisions.

The grey-cloaks had them cornered against a tanner's wall by the time I reached the alley. Three hunters, efficient, quiet — Veyra trains them to kill the way other folk train horses, patiently and without sentiment. The Ashgrove man had a knife he clearly didn't know how to use and the smuggler had a knife she very much did, and neither of those facts was going to save them.

I put my sword through the first hunter's back before he knew the alley had grown a fourth body. The second turned fast — good instincts, poor odds — and the third ran, which was the smartest thing anyone in that alley did all evening, myself included.

Then came the part I'd been dreading: introductions.

"You knew," the smuggler said. She hadn't lowered her knife. It was pointed at my throat, which I took as a compliment; most people aim lower when they're frightened. "You were *waiting*. Nobody stumbles into an ambush they weren't told about."

"Nobody survives one, either," I said. "Yet here we are."

"That's not an answer."

"It rarely is." I cleaned my blade on the dead hunter's cloak, mostly for effect. A little theatre goes a long way with suspicious people. "My name is Oren. And you're right — I knew. Which means you have exactly two choices: hear me out over a drink, or kill me and spend the rest of your short lives wondering what I would have said."

Rafael Ashgrove made a small sound, somewhere between a laugh and a prayer. "He talks like a man selling something."

"I *was* a man selling something," I said. "For eleven years. It ruins you."

* * *

The tavern I chose was called the Drowned Bell, which in Dunmarrow meant it was too mean for Wardens and too honest for spies. The wine tasted of vinegar and apology. The smuggler — Tamsin, though she didn't offer the name until I'd already paid for the wine, which told me something about how she ranked courtesy against coin — sat with her back to the wall and her eyes on both doors.

Ashgrove sat like a man waiting to be arrested. Hands folded. Shoulders apologetic.

"So," Tamsin said. "Talk."

I had rehearsed this speech for three days, in four versions, and every version died on my tongue now that the moment arrived. What came out instead was the truth, stripped of the polish I'd planned.

"I served King Aldous. Before the crowning. Before Veyra. I was his sword-arm in the border wars, back when the realm still called him *the Quiet King* because he hated speeches and loved his people more than his own comfort." I turned my cup. Vinegar swirled. "Seven years ago he changed. Overnight, by every account. The man who wept at harvest festivals began signing orders that burned villages. The man who refused to execute a single prisoner after Correnvale fell now feeds dissenters to his own armies as an example. Everyone says power changed him. Power doesn't change a man that fast or that completely. I've seen power change men. It makes them crueler versions of themselves. It does not make them *strangers*."

"And you think?" Ashgrove prompted, gently, the way you speak to madmen and grieving fathers.

"I think Aldous is still in there." I looked up. "Inside his own body. Behind his own eyes. Watching whatever wears him give the orders. Seven years in a cell made of his own skin."

Silence. Somewhere behind the bar, a pot clattered. Tamsin's face gave away nothing, which is its own kind of eloquence — I've interrogated enough liars to know that blankness is just fear wearing armour.

"That's a story," she said finally.

"It's the only one that fits. You've seen the king, have you? At the processions?"

"Once. From a distance."

"Then tell me what you saw."

She frowned, working at it. "A tall man. Grey. He didn't — " She stopped.

"He didn't blink," I finished. "Or he blinked wrong, slow, like a man remembering how. Or he stood too still between movements, like a puppet resting between pulls of the string. Everyone sees it. Nobody wants to name it, because naming it means admitting the last seven years happened to a kingdom with no king at all."

Ashgrove had gone pale. Not the theatrical pallor of a man being sold a tale — the grey, sick colour of a man recognizing something. I watched him carefully. This was the moment I'd gambled everything on: not their belief, but their *recognition*.

"The map," he said quietly. "It leads to Crownforge. Where the crown was made."

"Where the crown was *forged*, yes." I let that sit. "And you're carrying a map to it through a country full of hunters who want it badly enough to die in alleys for it. So either you already believe me, Ashgrove, or you believe something worse."

"What could be worse?"

"That the Ember is real, and someone means to use it rather than free the king with it." I smiled, and I'll admit it wasn't a warm smile. "But we can save the accusations for the road. Do you want my sword or not?"

Tamsin leaned forward. "Why should we? Everything you've told us explains why you'd want to come along. Nothing explains why we'd want you. You appear out of nowhere, save us from hunters who somehow knew our route, and expect gratitude. In my trade, that's called a setup."

"In your trade," I said, "what happens to people who travel toward Crownforge without a swordsman?"

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. We both knew the arithmetic: two travellers, one of them a charm-seller with a hidden gift and no training, the other a smuggler with quick hands and debts — against Serah's hunters, against the roads, against whatever waited under the fallen citadel of Vael.

"I don't ask for trust," I said. "Trust is earned and I haven't earned any. I ask for tolerance. Watch me. Suspect me. Count my footsteps if it helps you sleep. But let me walk with you, because I have spent seven years looking for a way to reach Crownforge, and you two are the first door I've found that isn't locked."

"Why seven years?" Ashgrove asked. "If you believed all along — why wait?"

Because I'm a coward, I almost said. Because I watched the thing in the king's body sign the order that sent my brother's regiment into the Saltmarshes and I told myself a sword alone couldn't fix it. Because hope is heavier than armour and I set mine down by the roadside like a pack I couldn't carry.

"Because I needed proof," I said instead. "And then I needed a path. Your map is both."

Tamsin's eyes narrowed at the word *map*. Filed away, I could see — another coin in the purse of her suspicions. Good. Suspicion keeps people alive longer than faith does.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"You walk behind us. You eat last. And if anything goes wrong — anything — you're the first person we look at."

"Reasonable," I said. "I'd have suggested the same."

* * *

We left before dawn, through the salt-gate, south along the old drover's road. The mountains took two days to stop pretending they weren't there.

I learned things about my companions the way you learn about weather — slowly, and mostly by what they withheld. Ashgrove talked when he was nervous, which was often, about harmless things: the price of copper wire, the best wax for sealing charms, the particular misery of Warden inspections. He never once mentioned the gate he'd sealed, though I knew about it — everyone in the old service knew about the Ashgrove gate, the last working door in the northern wards, closed by a gatekeeper's apprentice the night the purges began. Seven years keeping his head down, and the first time he lifts it, he lifts it all the way to Crownforge. There was more spine in that soft-handed charm-seller than he knew. I intended to keep it that way as long as possible.

Tamsin I understood immediately, because she was my own kind of animal: watchful, transactional, generous only by accident. She walked point without being asked. She counted supplies twice a day. And twice — twice — I caught her looking at the map case in Ashgrove's coat with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

Not curiosity. *Recognition.*

That was the first splinter of doubt I let myself feel about my own story. Because here is the thing I did not tell them at the Drowned Bell, the thing I have carried since the night the Ember was torn out of the world: I did not merely serve Aldous before the crowning.

I was in Crownforge the day they forged it.

I stood guard at the forge-door while the smiths worked metal and older, worse materials into a circlet, and I heard what the high priestess said when it was done — words meant for the king alone, words I was never supposed to hear, words I have spent seven years pretending I misheard. Words about what the crown would take, and what it would leave burning underneath the citadel, and why the binding needed a living flame to hold.

I know what the Ember is. I know what it costs. And I know — though I have told no one, though I will tell no one, not yet, maybe not ever — that freeing the king and destroying the Ember may be two different errands entirely.

I tell myself I stayed silent because the truth would break their resolve. That's half of it. The other half is that the truth would break mine.

On the third night, camped in the lee of a fallen waystone, Ashgrove sat across the fire from me and asked, in his careful, apologetic way:

"Oren. When we get there. What do you think we'll find?"

"A king," I said. "Imprisoned. Waiting."

"And the Ember?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Something that burns," I said, which was true, and left unsaid everything else, which was also true.

Across the flames, Tamsin watched me with those flat grey eyes, and I had the uncomfortable certainty that she'd heard the pause — that she was already counting the shape of what I hadn't said, the way she counted supplies, twice a day, without fail.

Let her count. By the time she finished the sum, we'd be too deep in the mountains to turn around.

That, at least, was one lie I told myself without flinching.
