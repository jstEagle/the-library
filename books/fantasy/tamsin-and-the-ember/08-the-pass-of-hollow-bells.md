## 8. The Pass of Hollow Bells

The mountains don't care who wears the crown. That's what I told myself on the third day out of Bray, when the road turned to switchbacks and the air got thin enough to chew. Mountains don't care, and neither do I. I've crossed worse passes with worse company and come out the other side with my purse heavier and my conscience untouched.

Except my purse wasn't heavier. My conscience was doing something complicated in my chest that I refused to name. And my company was Rafael Ashgrove, who walked like a man apologizing to the ground for stepping on it.

"You're sulking," I said.

"I'm thinking."

"That's what sulking looks like on you. Like a cat that's been asked to explain itself."

He gave me the look he gives me — the one that says *I was a gatekeeper of Correnvale, I sealed the Ashgrove gate, I am owed respect* — and then ruined it by nearly walking into a low branch. Seven years of hiding will do that to a man. Make him small. Make him careful. Make him flinch at branches and Wardens and kindness in roughly equal measure.

Oren ranged ahead somewhere, being mysterious at his own pace. The man could make silence feel like an argument. He'd walk twenty paces ahead, then drift back without sound, then be gone entirely, and every time I checked my pack — where my brother's journal sat wrapped in oilcloth like a wound I kept re-dressing — I wondered if Oren had already gone through it. If he knew. If everyone knew except Rafael, who trusted me with the easy, open trust of a man who had spent seven years trusting no one and had apparently decided to spend it all in one place.

On me. Of all people.

The first hollow village announced itself by its bells.

You heard them before you saw anything: a bell tolling in the wind, but wrong. Not cracked — hollowed. Someone had taken the clapper out, or stuffed the bell's throat with pitch, so that what came down the mountainside was less a sound than a memory of one, a shape of ringing with nothing inside it.

"Decree," Oren said, materializing beside us the way he did, which never stopped being unsettling. "Veyra's order after the border towns rebelled. No village may ring true bells while her armies march. A bell that rings full is a bell calling men to arms."

"So they took the voices out of them," Rafael said quietly.

"They took the voices out of everything," I said. "Welcome to the realm under the new management."

The village sat in a fold of the valley like something dropped there and forgotten. Doors stood open. Cooking pots on hearths gone cold days ago. A loom with cloth half-woven, threads still taut, waiting for hands that had been marched south. Conscription notices nailed to the well-post, official script over older script: *By order of Her Radiance Veyra, Protector of the Realm, all able-bodied persons between sixteen and fifty years are called to serve.* Below it, someone had scratched, in a child's uneven hand: *come home.*

Rafael read it twice. I watched his jaw do the thing it does when he's swallowing words instead of saying them.

"We should keep moving," Oren said.

"We should bury whoever's in the churchyard properly, is what we should—" Rafael started.

"There's no one to bury. There's no one here at all." Oren's voice had that flatness that wasn't cruelty, exactly. Efficiency wearing cruelty's coat. "Which means we move before whatever emptied this place comes back to check its work."

We moved. But Rafael looked back at the village until the pass swallowed it, and I found myself looking at him instead of the road, which was becoming a habit I couldn't afford.

* * *

The second village was not empty.

We heard it from a mile off — or rather, we heard the absence, the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue. No dogs. No chickens. But smoke from one chimney, thin and stubborn, and when we came around the bend there was a girl standing in the middle of the road.

She couldn't have been more than eight. Barefoot on mountain stone in late autumn, which told us most of what we needed to know. She didn't run. She just stood there, arms at her sides, watching us come with the flat patience of a creature that has learned adults arrive and adults leave and neither means anything.

"Hello," Rafael said, crouching to her level, which I noticed he did without thinking, the way some men do — the ones who were somebody's little brother once. "Are your parents near?"

"Gone." She said it the way you'd say *Tuesday*. "Soldiers came. Da went because they said if he didn't they'd burn the mill. Ma went after because she said she'd rather follow him than wait. Gran stayed. Gran's sick."

"Where's Gran now?"

The girl pointed at the house with the smoking chimney, and then her legs folded under her, neat as a puppet with cut strings, and Rafael caught her before she hit the ground.

She was burning up. Fever like a forge-door, sweat despite the cold, lips cracked and grey. Rafael gathered her up and I saw his face go through three expressions in a heartbeat — alarm, helplessness, and then something else. Something older. Something he'd been keeping in a locked room for seven years.

"Oren," I said carefully, "how far to the next settlement with a physicker?"

"Two days. She has hours."

"Then we need—"

"No." Rafael said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than shouting. He was looking at the girl's face, at the fever-bright eyes half-focused on nothing. "No, we don't need two days."

"Rafael." I kept my voice low, kept it level, the voice you use on spooked animals and armed men. "What are you saying?"

He didn't answer me. He carried the girl toward the house, and Oren and I exchanged a look over his shoulder — his unreadable, mine, I suspect, saying very clearly: *this is about to become a problem.*

* * *

Gran was dead. Had been for a day or more, laid out on the bed with someone's hands folded over her chest — the girl's work, judging by how badly it was done. Rafael set the child down in the chair by the cold hearth and knelt in front of her, and I watched him wrestle with himself in real time, a man standing at his own gate again, hand on the bar, deciding whether to open it.

"Rafael." I crouched next to him. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"She's eight."

"I know how old she is."

"If I do nothing, she dies tonight. Alone. In a house with her grandmother's corpse." His voice didn't shake. That frightened me more than shaking would have. "Seven years, Tamsin. Seven years I have made myself small. I sold charms that did nothing. I let a Warden's inspector take my stock and I apologized to him. I watched a man die outside my door because moving would have cost me everything, and I told myself survival was the price of the gift, and I believed it, right up until a dying stranger bled a map into my hands and I understood that the smallness was never going to save anyone. Including me."

"This isn't Correnvale. There are no Wardens here. There's something worse. Serah is two days behind us, maybe less, and magic leaves a mark, Rafael. You know it does. It's a flare lit on a dark hillside, and she's been following a flare since Dunmarrow."

"And if I don't," he said, "then I'm the man who let a child die to keep his own skin, and Serah gets me anyway, sooner or later, and I'll have traded everything for nothing." He looked at me then, and his eyes were wet and steady. "You crossed the Salt Road for eleven years running contraband past cutthroats. Tell me the arithmetic doesn't favor the risk."

"The arithmetic always favors the risk," I said. "That's why smugglers die young. We're bad at fear."

Something moved through his face — almost a smile. Then he turned back to the girl, and I watched him take her small burning hand in both of his, and I watched him close his eyes, and I thought, absurdly, *don't* — and then the light came.

It wasn't dramatic. That's what I'll tell the council someday, if it ever comes to that, though I doubt anyone will believe me. It wasn't fire or thunder. It was warmth. The hearth that had been cold for days bloomed orange without wood. The frost on the window crawled backward. The girl's breathing eased from ragged to slow, and color came into her lips like dawn coming into a valley, and Rafael made a sound low in his throat — relief and pain in the same breath — because it costs, giving magic. I'd seen him pay for charms before. This was different. This was him opening a vein instead of selling a cupful.

The light guttered. Went out. Rafael swayed, and I caught his arm, and for a moment he leaned into me, heavy, human, mortal, and I held him up and hated how much I wanted to keep holding him.

"It's done," he whispered. "She'll sleep. The fever breaks by morning."

"You absolute fool," I said, and my voice cracked on *fool*, which ruined the effect entirely.

"Tamsin—"

"Serah will see it. Whatever sense she uses, she'll see it, and she'll come faster now, and it's my job to get us to Crownforge alive, and you just—" I stopped. Breathed. "You just lit a bonfire on a dark hillside for a child you met ten minutes ago."

"Yes," he said simply. And then, because he was Rafael: "I'd do it again."

"I know," I said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

We buried Gran in the morning, because Rafael insisted and because I've learned which fights to lose. He dug; Oren stood watch and pretended not to approve; the girl — Wren, her name was, Wren like the bird, small and brown and stubborn — sat on the step and watched us with those flat patient eyes that were starting to unnerve me more than any hunter.

"You can't stay here," I told her, while Rafael filled in the earth. "There's a farmstead over the ridge, half a day. Old couple, Hesta and Bren. They took in strays during the last war. Tell them Tamsin sent you."

"They won't know you."

"They will. I owe them money." She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Will the soldiers come back?"

"Not for you. Not now." I crouched to her level, the way Rafael had. "But listen to me, Wren. What happened here — the empty villages, the hollow bells, your da marching south — none of it was right. None of it was necessary. And there are people trying to prove that. To end it. Do you understand?"

"You're going to fight the Radiance?"

"We're going to tell the truth about her," I said. "Which is worse, for people like her."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached into her pocket and pressed something into my hand: a small tin whistle, dented, the kind children buy at fairs.

"Gran said if the bells ever ring true again, I should play this loud enough for her to hear." Her chin trembled, just once, and stilled. "So you make them ring true. And I'll find a high place."

I closed my fist around the whistle and could not speak. Rafael saved me by lifting Wren onto his shoulders and carrying her to the gate, pointing out the ridge path, making her laugh once — actually laugh — with some nonsense about goats. When he came back his face was composed, but his hands weren't quite steady, and I pretended not to notice, the way he pretends not to notice things about me. We were building quite a collection, the two of us. A museum of mercies we each pretended not to see.

We climbed. The pass narrowed. Behind us, far below, I swear I heard — or imagined, which amounted to the same thing — a thin bright note, a child's whistle playing over a valley of silent bells, and I walked faster so no one would see my face.

* * *

That night, in the shelter of a shepherd's ruin, Rafael couldn't sleep. I found him sitting against the wall with the map across his knees, watching the faint gold tracery pulse under his fingers like a heartbeat.

"It's stronger," he said, without looking up. "Since yesterday. Since I—" He flexed his hand. "The map responds to magic. Mine woke it. Now it's... louder."

"Louder how?"

"Like it knows we're close. Or like it knows I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "Tamsin, when I healed her, I felt something I haven't felt in seven years. Not just the power. A... pull. Southward. Toward Vael. Like a compass needle finally finding north." He looked up at me, and the firelight caught the gold still faintly clinging to his fingertips. "What if the map isn't leading me? What if it's *calling* me? What if I'm not carrying it — what if it's carrying me somewhere it needs to go?"

Outside, far down the valley, one of the hollow bells shifted in the wind and gave its voiceless toll — a shape of sound with nothing inside it, mourning nothing, warning no one.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the pass behind us: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast.

Moving toward us.
