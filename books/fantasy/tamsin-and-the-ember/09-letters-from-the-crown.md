## 9. Letters from the Crown

There is a particular kind of letter that arrives at midnight, and I have learned to dread it more than any declaration of war. War announces itself with trumpets and burning fields. The midnight letter arrives on quiet feet, sealed in grey wax, and it always says the same thing in different words: *something is moving that you did not move.*

This one came while I was reading petitions. I keep the habit of reading them myself — all of them, even the ones about drainage ditches in villages whose names I cannot pronounce — because a queen who reads only what her ministers choose to show her is a queen being read like a book by other people. And I refuse, absolutely refuse, to be anyone's book.

The petition was from a miller complaining that his neighbor's geese kept fouling his wheelrace. I had just reached the part where he described the geese as "agents of deliberate malice" when the candle guttered without wind, and every flame in the room leaned east.

Toward Vael. Toward Crownforge. Toward the thing I buried under seven years of silence and one very good lie.

I set down the miller's grievances and sat very still, the way one does when an old wound decides to remember itself. The flames righted themselves. Somewhere below, in the palace cellars where my Wardens keep their instruments, I knew — the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop — that the resonance bowls were singing.

"Damn," I said, to the empty room, because there is no one left in this palace to whom I can say anything stronger. That is the trouble with winning. You win until everyone who might have told you the truth is either dead, promoted, or too frightened of you to be useful. I built this silence brick by brick, and now I must live in the house.

* * *

Let me tell you how it actually happened, since history will certainly get it wrong. History always does; it prefers its villains simple and its heroes breathing.

Seven years ago, Aldous was crowned in Crownforge, beneath the citadel of Vael, as kings of Correnvale had been crowned for nine hundred years. He went into the forge whole and came out — changed. Everyone saw it. His councilors called it gravity. His priests called it blessing. His wife, who had known him since they were children stealing apples from the same orchard, called it nothing at all, because she stopped speaking to him within the month, and then stopped speaking entirely, and then died, quietly, of something the physicians could not name.

I know what the physicians could not name. I paid three of them to write it down honestly, and then I burned the honest copies and kept the flattering ones, because a realm needs its king, and a realm that learns its king has been hollowed out and refilled with something older than kingship will tear itself apart inside a season. I have seen realms do it. I grew up in one.

So yes. I killed him. Or I unmade him, which amounts to the same thing with better paperwork. The Ember — the fragment of soul the forge tears from every crowned king, the price of the crown that no chronicle mentions because no chronicler survives mentioning it — I took it out of the cradle where it burns and I bound it still. And the thing wearing Aldous's body, the residue, the echo, whatever name you prefer for the king-shaped absence that walked out of Crownforge wearing my husband's face — I bound that too. Quietly. Gently. A leash so fine he does not feel it, and through the leash, I hold the crown, the armies, the seal.

They call me usurper. They are not wrong. They are simply incomplete. A usurper steals power for herself. I stole a kingdom from a monster to keep it from being eaten, and if that makes me a thief, then let the record show I am a thief with excellent administrative skills. The roads are safe. The granaries are full. The border war we inherited from Aldous's last mad year is being won instead of lost. Ask any farmer in the eastern provinces whether they would trade my rule for the return of the true king. They will look at you as though you suggested trading a roof for rain.

And yet.

The Ember stirs. Which means someone has touched the map, or the map has touched someone, or the old forge has remembered it once had a purpose and grown restless in its grave. Seven years of stillness, and now the candles lean east and the bowls sing, and I am awake at midnight doing arithmetic on fear.

* * *

In the morning I summoned Serah.

She arrived the way she always does — already in the room, or nearly, announced by nothing except the sudden awareness that the door had opened at some point and you had failed to notice. She stood before my desk in her grey cloak with her hands folded, waiting, patient as frost.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question. Serah does not waste questions on things she can observe.

"The Ember moved." I turned the midnight letter toward her — the Warden report from Correnvale, the one about a charm-seller named Ashgrove whose stall had been raided, whose records showed nothing, whose name meant nothing, except that Ashgrove was the name of a gate in the western hills that had been sealed for seven years by a gatekeeper of the same name who then vanished from every register I checked at dawn. Coincidence is a currency, and I have learned not to accept it at face value. "And there is a man in Correnvale selling charms who used to keep a gate."

"Charm-sellers are common."

"Gatekeepers who abandon their gates are not." I rose and went to the window. From the high tower of the occupied capital you can see the whole grey sprawl of the city, the river, the smoke, the tiny ant-like procession of carts coming in through the south gate with grain and iron and the ordinary business of an ordinary day that I intend to continue indefinitely. "Someone gave him something, Serah. Or he found something. Either way, the map is moving again, and maps do not walk on their own legs."

"Tamsin," Serah said.

I looked back at her. "You have a name already?"

"I have been listening." She said it without pride, the way a carpenter might say *I have been measuring*. "A smuggler of the Salt Road. Her brother carried a map south two years ago and died carrying it. She has been asking questions in river towns ever since — quietly, badly, the way amateurs ask questions. If the map resurfaced, it surfaced near her."

"And you waited to tell me until the Ember itself confirmed your homework."

"I wanted to be certain." A pause, and then, because Serah permits herself exactly one honesty per conversation: "And I wanted to be sure you would send me, rather than send the Wardens."

That was the real conversation, of course, folded inside the smaller one. The Wardens are thorough. Thoroughness, in my experience, produces corpses and ash and a great many confident reports explaining why the corpses and ash were necessary. What it does not produce is understanding. I needed to understand what had woken the Ember — who, and why, and whether they knew what they carried — before I decided how much of the world around them needed to stop existing.

"You will go yourself," I said. "Take your hunters. Find the map. Find whoever carries it. And bring me the carrier alive, Serah — the map I can replace, but a person who can wake the Ember is either a threat or an instrument, and I do not destroy instruments until I have learned what they play."

"And if they cannot be taken alive?"

"Then take them dead and bring me their reasons. I want to know what they think they are doing. People die all the time; misunderstanding is the only true loss."

Serah inclined her head. At the door she paused — she always pauses, it is her one theatrical indulgence — and said, without turning: "You believe the king can still be saved."

It was not a question either, but it deserved one, so I gave it one.

"I believe," I said, "that Aldous went into that forge as a man I loved, and that somewhere inside the thing that walks around in his body, the man persists, the way a voice persists in a stopped bell. I believe the Ember is not merely his torn-out soul but the proof of him — the part that laughed, and grieved, and stole apples. And I believe that if I can bind the Ember properly, wholly, forever, then whatever wears his face will finally go quiet, and I can give him a funeral, and this kingdom can stop pretending its king is alive."

I heard my own voice and recognized it: the reasonable tone, the merciful tone, the tone I have practiced for seven years until it fits my mouth like a key fits a lock. Power justified as mercy. It is the most seductive sentence in any language, and I speak it fluently in six.

Serah considered this. "And if binding it requires killing the man who carries the map? Or the smuggler? Or a city?"

"Then I will grieve efficiently," I said, "and afterward I will read the petitions, and the drainage ditches will be cleared, and the geese will continue their campaign of malice against the millers, and no one will ever need to know what mercy cost. That is the entire duty of a crown, Serah. To know the price and pay it privately."

She left. Frost leaves rooms that way.

* * *

Alone, I did what I always do when the fear gets loud: I went down to the vaults.

The resonance bowls were singing softly, a thin silver note like a wet finger on crystal rim. I stood among them with my hands clasped behind my back and listened, and beneath the singing I felt it — faint, far, furious — the Ember turning over in its long sleep. Not waking fully. Stirring. Dreaming, perhaps, of the forge that made it, or the man it was cut from.

"Aldous," I said quietly. It is the only place I say his name aloud anymore. In the council chamber he is *the king*; in the army dispatches, *His Majesty*; in my own head, most days, *the problem*, because grief is a luxury a regent cannot afford during working hours. But down here, among the singing bowls, I allow myself the name. "If this is you, reaching for the world — stop. I am keeping you safe. I am keeping everything safe. It is the only promise I have ever managed to keep."

The bowls sang on, indifferent. The dead are poor listeners. I have often thought that if they listened better, fewer living people would talk to them.

On my way up, I passed the portrait gallery — the long hall of painted kings, each one crowned in Crownforge, each one painted after the crowning with that same subtle wrongness around the eyes that I never noticed until I knew what to look for. Nine hundred years of hollowed men, smiling in oil paint, and not one chronicler brave enough to ask what the forge takes and where it goes. My predecessors bound their Embers carelessly, or not at all, and their kingdoms drifted, kingless in all but name, ruled by councils and favorites and whoever held the leash that week. I at least had the decency to seize the leash deliberately.

History will call me a usurper. Fine. Let it also record that under the usurper, the granaries were full.

* * *

By evening the orders were drafted and sealed. Serah south, with six hunters and a purse of grey gold. Warden reinforcements to Correnvale, discreetly — a tax audit, officially, because nothing empties a quarter faster than the words *tax audit*. And a letter to the border commanders tightening the passes around Vael, in case the map-carriers got further than I expected.

I wrote the last order myself, at my desk, by candlelight, and as I pressed the seal into the wax I noticed that the flame had begun leaning east again. Gently. Persistently. Like a dog pulling at a lead.

"Not yet," I told it. "Whatever you are, whoever woke you — not yet. I have held this kingdom together with my hands for seven years, and I have not come this far to lose it to a candle flame and a smuggler's map."

The flame straightened, obedient or indifferent, I could not tell which. That is the thing about power, in the end. You can never quite determine whether the world obeys you or merely tolerates you, and the uncertainty keeps you humble, or at least keeps you awake.

I slept poorly, which was usual, and dreamed of an orchard, which was not. In the dream, a young man with apple juice on his chin was laughing at something I had said, and I was laughing too, and neither of us yet knew what crowns cost or who would pay.

I woke before dawn with the taste of apples in my mouth and the certainty, cold and complete as a drawn blade, that the Ember had found its carriers at last.

Somewhere south, someone was carrying my husband's soul toward the place it was torn from. And I intended to meet them at the door.

* * *

One final matter, and I set it down here because these private pages are the only place I am honest, and honesty hoarded is nearly as good as honesty spent:

When Serah returns — and she will return; she is the one instrument I own that has never broken — she will bring me the map-carrier, and I will look into his face, and I will decide. That is the whole of sovereignty, really. Everyone else deliberates. Someone must decide.

But I confess that some small traitorous part of me — the part that still remembers orchards — hopes the carrier is angry. Hopes he comes through my doors full of righteous fury, ready to tell me I murdered a good man and stole a kingdom. Because then, at least, someone will have said it to my face at last.

And I will smile, and offer him wine, and explain — patiently, reasonably, mercifully — why everything I did was necessary.

And if he does not believe me, well.

I have seven years of practice making people believe me.
