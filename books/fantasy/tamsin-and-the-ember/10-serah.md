## 10. Serah

There is a particular smell to a cellar that has held prisoners for more than a week. It is not the smell of blood — blood washes out easily enough, and I have never been squeamish about it. It is the smell of hope going stale. Fear has a sharpness to it, almost like vinegar, but despair is flat and damp, like bread left in the dark. By the time I descended into the holding cells beneath the customs house at Braywater Crossing, the man in the third cell had been marinating in it for nine days, and I could have told you his state of mind from the top of the stairs.

I went down anyway. It is considered polite.

My name is Serah Vael-Adjunct, which is a title rather than a name, though I have worn it so long it has grown into me like a splinter. To the soldiers of the Crown I am the Queen's Hand, or sometimes, when they think I cannot hear, other things less flattering and more accurate. To the people of the river towns I am simply *the grey woman*, and mothers use my name to frighten children who will not sleep. I have made peace with this. A reputation is a tool, and tools should be kept sharp even if they cut the hand that holds them.

The prisoner was called Merrick Dole, though he had given four other names in four days, each one delivered with the earnest conviction of a man who had lied so long that lying had become a form of breathing. He was a smuggler of the Salt Road school — soft hands gone calloused too recently, a knife scar on the forearm put there by a friend rather than an enemy, if I read it right. Smugglers always scar their friends. It is a profession with poor boundaries.

He looked up when I entered, and I watched him do the arithmetic every prisoner does: *she is here for someone else, therefore I may yet live.* Prisoners are wrong about this more often than right, but hope is cheap, and the Crown provides it free of charge.

"Master Dole," I said, settling onto the stool across from him. I had brought my own stool. The cell's furniture consisted of a bench, a bucket, and a rat who had established squatter's rights some time ago and defended them fiercely. "You've been telling my colleagues some very interesting stories."

"I don't know what you mean, lady." His voice cracked on *lady*, which was either fear or flattery. With men like Dole it is usually both.

"No," I agreed. "I don't imagine you do."

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew before I sat down, because knowing is the whole of my trade, and I resent being thought a torturer when I am in fact something far worse: a listener.

Nine days earlier, a patrol on the northern road had taken Dole's caravan — three wagons of untaxed saltwine and one wagon of something the manifest delicately described as *relics*. Among the relics: a courier's satchel, empty, stained with old blood, bearing the mark of a free-city guild that had been dissolved two years past. And stitched inside the satchel's lining, where a frightened man might hide a thing he could not bear to destroy, a scrap of vellum with a fragment of writing in a cipher our archivists had spent four days failing to crack.

The satchel belonged to a dead man. That much we knew. The border garrisons keep records of corpses the way farmers keep records of rain — impersonal, thorough, and useful for predicting what comes next. A smuggler named Corren Dole, no relation to Merrick or so he claimed, found dead on the Salt Road eight months ago with his throat opened and his cargo missing. Cargo unspecified. The report had gathered dust in a drawer until my agents pulled it two days ago, because dust is only unsorted information, and sorting information is what I am *for*.

The Queen wanted the map. I will come to the Queen shortly, because everyone always does, eventually, and it saves time if I explain her now.

But first: the brother of a dead man, sitting in my cellar, lying to me with the dedication of a craftsman.

"Merrick," I said pleasantly, "your brother Corren carried something south along the Salt Road last autumn. Something small enough to fit in a satchel and important enough that someone killed him for it. You've been asking after that something ever since — my people have watched you ask in six towns. You never found it. But you know what it was, and you know who else wants it, and this morning I woke up feeling generous, which frightens even me."

He wet his lips. "I told your people—"

"You told my people four names, none of them yours, and a story about a widow in Harrowgate that I have since confirmed is borrowed from a ballad." I let the silence stretch. Silence is the cheapest instrument in the orchestra and the one most often played badly; done properly, it does the work of a hundred questions. "Here is what generosity looks like, Master Dole. You give me the truth, and you walk out of this cellar with coin in your pocket and my protection on the roads. You lie to me again, and you stay here until the rat files a complaint about overcrowding. There is no third door. I checked."

He laughed — a single bark, surprised out of him — and then caught himself, because laughing at the grey woman is generally fatal, and we both knew it, and the shared knowledge of his imminent death sat between us like a third guest.

"It's a map," he said.

I had known it was a map for eleven days. I said nothing. Let him believe he was surprising me. People guard their surprises jealously and spend them all at once.

"A map to—" He stopped. Looked at the door. Looked at me. "They say the Queen's own hunters are looking for it. Grey cloaks. They say those ones don't take prisoners."

"They say a great many things," I said. "Continue."

"There was a buyer. Corren wouldn't say who. Big coin — bigger than anything on the Salt Road, big enough that he turned down two standing contracts to take it. He told me—" Dole's voice did something complicated here, and I recognized grief wearing the mask of anger, a costume I have seen on ten thousand faces and wear myself on occasion, though I would deny it under oath. "He told me it would set us up for life. Set up *Mara's boy*. His sister's son. He was doing it for family, whatever else he was."

"And?"

"And he's dead in a ditch and I'm in a hole, so much for family." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he took them away, he had decided something. Men always decide something at that moment; it is why I wait. "There was a name. He said if anything went wrong, find Tamsin. Tamsin of the Salt Road. She'd know the routes, she'd know the buyers. She was — Corren said she was the best of us. Best of all of us."

"Tamsin," I repeated.

"His sister."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above us, water dripped through the customs house roof, patient as arithmetic.

Corren Dole had a sister. The sister ran the same roads. The sister was, by her brother's dying instruction, the keeper of his knowledge — the routes, the buyers, the *map*. And eight months later, a gatekeeper from Correnvale with magic leaking off him like heat off a forge-stone had fled the city carrying a bloodstained map, and had been met on the southern road by a smuggler whose description matched the sister to the freckle.

I want to be clear that I felt nothing at that moment except satisfaction, and I want to be clear about it because later, when certain events had transpired, people claimed I must have known, must have hesitated, must have felt *something*. I did not. I was a key turning in a lock, and the lock was opening, and keys do not apologize.

"Tamsin Dole," I said. "Travels with a man called Rafael Ashgrove. Formerly gatekeeper at the Ashgrove pass. Currently in violation of the Edict of Unmaking, which carries penalties I don't need to recite to you."

Dole stared at me. "How do you—"

"Master Dole. I asked you questions for nine days' worth of reasons. Now I'm answering them, because you've earned a glimpse of how far over your head you've been swimming." I stood, and his eyes followed me up the way small animals follow hawks. "One more thing, and then you may go and drink yourself stupid on my coin. Your brother's debts. Did he owe anyone?"

"Everyone," Dole said bitterly. "That was Corren. Borrowed against tomorrow like tomorrow was a crop that always came in."

"In Bray. Specifically. Who holds paper on the Dole name in Bray?"

"The Ferrymen. Old crew, gone legit-ish. They hold his marker. Why—"

"Because markers survive the man," I said. "Debt is the one ghost that always finds its heir. Thank you for your honesty. It has been noted, and noted things are rewarded."

I left him sitting on his bench with his mouth open, and I climbed the stairs into the grey afternoon light, and I gave the order that I had already composed in my head before the interrogation began, because the best orders are written before the information arrives; the information merely chooses which drawer to open.

*Send word to the Ferrymen of Bray. Inform them that the Dole marker is callable, that the holder of the map their debtor carried is traveling south, and that the Crown will look favorably upon any creditor who calls in what is owed. Do not mention me. Do not mention the Queen. Let it look like what it is: money.*

* * *

Now. The Queen.

People imagine that serving Veyra means cowering before her, that her presence is a storm you endure. Her courtiers encourage this impression; it flatters them to serve something terrifying. The truth is worse and stranger. Serving Veyra is like serving a very calm physician who has diagnosed the entire realm with a disease only she can see, and who prescribes amputation with genuine sorrow, and who will weep real tears at your bedside while she takes the arm.

I have seen her weep. That is the part no one believes. In the seventh year of her regency — she does not call herself queen, another courtesy the court maintains on her behalf — she stood over a village in the Marches that had harbored a hedge-witch, and the witch had been burned according to law, and the village had been fined unto ruin according to law, and a farmer had thrown a stone that caught her cheek, and her guards had killed him according to law. And she knelt beside the farmer's body, in the mud, in front of everyone, and wept, and said, "I am saving you, and you will never forgive me for it, and that is the price I pay so you don't have to."

Then she stood up and ordered the village's remaining sons conscripted, because the border war required them, and rode on.

I believed her then. I believe her now, mostly. This is what makes her dangerous — not the armies, not the grey cloaks, not me. Belief. Half the realm believes her, and the half that doesn't believes in nothing, and belief in something terrible beats belief in nothing every time the dice are cast. I learned that watching the free cities argue. They are still arguing. They will argue until the armies arrive, and then they will argue about whose fault the arriving was.

She had summoned me the night before I came to Bray, through the token — a sliver of grey glass that warms against the palm when she speaks through it, and cold as a grave-marker when she does not. Her voice comes thin and distant, like a singer heard through a wall.

*"Serah. The Ember stirs."*

Four words. She had said the same four words once before, three years ago, and I had ridden north and burned a monastery and found nothing but monks, and I had told myself the Queen was chasing ghosts. This time she said it differently. This time there was something underneath the words, and I have served her long enough to know that what frightens Veyra frightens me more than anything I could frighten myself with.

*"A gatekeeper," she said. "From Ashgrove. He carries what my brother stole from me, or he knows who does. Bring me the map, Serah. Bring me the man if he's living, bring me his ashes if he isn't, but bring me the map whole. Everything else is negotiable. That is not."*

Her brother. King Aldous, who had been a robust and cheerful man at his crowning and was now a pale figure behind curtains, who signed what was put before him and spoke perhaps forty words a day and had not been seen to eat in public in two years. The court called it a wasting illness. The court called it many things. The court was very good at calling things.

I did not know — do not know, entirely — what the Ember is. I know it is not a jewel and not a weapon, despite the names. I know the Queen fears it the way a surgeon fears the thing growing inside a beloved patient. And I know that when she said *my brother stole from me*, her voice did something I had never heard it do in eleven years of service.

It shook.

So: I ride for the company of a smuggler and a renegade gatekeeper, following a trail of dead brothers and called-in debts, in service of a grieving tyrant who may be the only honest person in the realm. If there is a joke in that, I have not yet found the shape of it. Perhaps it will come to me on the road. Jokes usually arrive too late to be funny.

* * *

The Ferrymen's answer came back within the day, which told me everything about how hungry they were. Old creditors are like old dogs: they remember every bone ever buried, and they come running the moment they scent one being dug up by someone else.

Their message, relayed through a fishmonger with a talent for memorization and no talent at all for seeming innocent: *The marker stands. The debt is called. Payment due in full, in Bray, by month's end.*

No mention of the map. No mention of the Crown. Just clean commercial language, the kind that leaves no fingerprints, exactly as instructed. The Ferrymen believed they were squeezing an old debt on their own initiative. That is the art of it — the trap must close by its own weight, or the prey smells the snare. By the time Tamsin Dole understood what was happening in Bray, she would be standing in the room where it happened, and the room would be full of men she had known for years, and the choice offered her would be the oldest choice in the world: the map, or her life.

And if she gave up the map — well. Then I would collect it from the Ferrymen, pay them handsomely, and pay them again, differently, for having talked, because loose ends are only acceptable in tapestries.

And if she did not — if she chose loyalty over survival, which smugglers occasionally do, to everyone's surprise including their own — then she would die in a room in Bray believing she had died for friendship, and I would learn from the manner of her death exactly how much the map meant to her, and to whoever traveled with her, and I would adjust accordingly.

Either way, I won. I had built the board so that either way, I won. This is the job. I am good at the job.

There was one wrinkle. There is always one wrinkle; a plan without a wrinkle is a plan nobody has examined honestly. The wrinkle was the swordsman.

Oren. My agents had flagged him twice on the company's trail — once at Dunmarrow, where he had appeared out of nowhere to break a hunter's ambush with a style of swordwork that my instructors would have charged extra to teach, and once on the mountain road, where he had bought supplies with old coin. Not counterfeit coin. *Old* coin. Aldous-era minting, pre-crowning, the kind that circulates among collectors and dead men's estates.

I had sent his description south and received back a match to a name I had hoped never to see again: a captain of the old king's household guard, discharged — or vanished — the week of the coronation. A man who had sworn his oath to Aldous's face and then evaporated like morning frost, taking his sword and his secrets with him.

Which meant the smuggler and the gatekeeper were traveling with a ghost from the old court, and the ghost had chosen them, or they had chosen the ghost, and neither possibility comforted me. A man like that does not attach himself to random fugitives out of charity. He wants what the Queen wants. He is simply planning to want it somewhere else.

Three players circling one map, then. Four, counting the dead brother, whose ghost sat in every decision like a guest who refuses to leave. And somewhere ahead of all of us, the thing itself — the Ember, burning wherever crowns are made, stirring in its sleep after seven quiet years, waking, if the Queen was right, because two liars and a ghost were walking toward it with a piece of paper that loved them.

I stood on the customs house steps and watched the river run south, brown and patient, carrying everything toward Bray.

"Ready the horses," I told the sergeant. "We ride tonight."

He saluted and hurried off, and I stayed a moment longer, because the river deserved a moment; it was the only thing in this business moving honestly.

Month's end, the debt came due. Month's end, so did I.

I wondered, idly, which of us the smugglers would fear more — and then stopped wondering, because the answer was obvious, and because I had work to do, and because the grey woman does not stand on steps feeling things when there is a trap to finish closing.
