## 11. The Debt Called In

Bray smelled the way river towns always smell at the end of summer: fish, tar, and the particular rot of things people had decided to stop caring about. I'd been coming here since I was sixteen years old, back when my brother Dain was still alive and still pretending he wasn't going to get himself killed for somebody else's secrets.

I knew every alley in Bray. That was the problem. The alleys knew me back, and some of them owed money to the same people I did.

"Cheerful place," Rafael said behind me, adjusting the strap of his pack like a man who thought luggage was the real enemy.

"It's homey," I said. "You get used to the smell. Or you drown it in ale. Those are your options."

We came down off the ferry road with Oren a half-step behind us — he'd taken to walking there, close enough to be company, far enough to deny it if anyone asked. He hadn't explained himself yet and neither of us had pressed, which told you everything about how much trust we were all pretending to have. Three travelers and a map that only showed itself to magic, heading south toward a citadel that had fallen before any of us were born. Nothing suspicious about that at all.

The trouble with Bray started before we'd even reached the market square.

I saw them first because I'd spent my whole life learning to see them: two men leaning against the fishmonger's awning, not buying anything, not talking, watching the road from Dunmarrow with the patience of men being paid by the hour. One was fat and soft-handed, which meant he didn't do the collecting himself anymore. The other was thin as a whip handle and had a scar through his eyebrow I remembered giving him.

"Raff," I said quietly. "Don't look left."

He looked left immediately. For a man who could read an enchanted map with his bare hands, he had the instincts of a startled chicken.

"The fat one's called Mudge," I said. "He works for the Corvo brothers. The other one is Ferro. I broke his nose in Bray seven years ago over a dispute about whose cargo was whose."

"You're popular here."

"I'm *known* here. There's a difference, and the difference is about forty crowns of debt." I kept walking, casual as Sunday. "Keep moving. We'll take the back way to the ferry office and be on the water before they—"

"Tamsin of the Salt Road," Mudge called out, pleasant as a knife going in. "As I live and breathe. And here I was told you were dead."

*So was I,* I thought. *Twice, actually. Once by Wardens and once by my own better judgment.*

I turned around and gave him my best smile, the one that had talked customs officers out of searching crates full of nothing but river stones. "Mudge. You've gotten fat."

"I've gotten *prosperous*," he said, pushing off the awning. "There's a difference, and the difference is about forty crowns. Yours, as it happens. Plus interest. Plus the handling fee for having to come find you, since you seem to have developed a habit of not being where debts can find you."

Behind him, Ferro smiled at me with the expression of a man who'd been waiting seven years to collect something other than coin.

* * *

They took us to the back room of a counting house near the docks, which is where all the worst conversations in Bray happen. Counting houses are built like strongboxes — thick walls, one door, no windows worth mentioning — and the Corvos liked their negotiations private. Private meaning nobody could hear you scream, or nobody could hear you beg, depending on how the negotiation went.

Oren came along without being asked, which should have worried me more than it did. Rafael sat beside me with his hands folded like a man at a lecture, radiating the kind of calm that comes from genuinely not understanding the danger. Maybe that was it. Maybe he really didn't know what these rooms were for.

"So," Mudge said, settling into the chair behind the desk. It groaned under him. "Forty crowns. Seven years' interest. Call it ninety-five even, and I'll throw in forgiving Ferro's grudge as a courtesy."

"That's generous," I said.

"It is. Because the Corvos like you, Tamsin. You were good business. Reliable. Until you weren't." He laced his fingers over his belly. "But times change. There's a war on the border, coin's tight, and word from Dunmarrow is that you've been seen traveling with something valuable. Something a lot of important people would pay a great deal to see."

The room went very quiet. Even Ferro stopped looking at me like a meal and started looking at me like a professional.

"I don't know what you've heard," I said.

"I think you do." Mudge smiled. "Here's the arrangement, then, since we're old friends. Either you pay the ninety-five crowns — and I know you haven't got them, so let's skip past the part where you pretend — or you hand over whatever it is you're carrying south, and we call it square. Or—" he spread his hands, apologetic, "—Ferro takes you down to the river and we sell your debt to someone less patient than us. There are buyers. There are always buyers. Especially now."

I felt Rafael shift beside me. "What's she carrying?" he asked, mild as milk.

Mudge looked at him with the fond contempt of a man who'd been young once himself. "That's between her and us, friend."

"She doesn't carry anything of yours," Rafael said. "She carries my things. My pack, my supplies. If there's a debt, it's mine to settle, and I'll settle it however she—"

"*Raff.*" I put a hand on his arm. His forearm was warm through the sleeve, and steady, and I hated how much steadiness I wanted to lean on. "This isn't your fight."

"Everything that's happened to you since Dunmarrow has been my fault," he said. "The hunters followed the map. The map's mine. So this is my fight whether you like it or not."

And there it was, laid out plain in front of the Corvos' collector: confirmation that I was traveling with exactly the thing Mudge had heard rumors about. I could have kissed him or strangled him, and I honestly couldn't tell you which impulse was stronger.

Mudge's smile widened by a single, terrible degree. "Well," he said. "Now we're getting somewhere."

* * *

Here's the thing about Rafael Ashgrove that I figured out somewhere on the Salt Road, somewhere between the hollow bells and the empty villages: he trusted people the way most men breathed. Automatically. Without thinking about it. He'd known me three weeks and he trusted me with his life, his secret, and apparently his finances, and he never once asked what I'd done to earn it.

Nobody trusts like that unless they've never been taught better. Nobody survives trusting like that, either. Except he had survived — seven years in a city crawling with Wardens, selling charms out of a stall, keeping his head down and his gift hidden — and I couldn't reconcile those two facts. The man who'd sealed a gate against Veyra's whole regime and the man who'd hand his purse to a stranger because she looked hungry were the same person, and one of them had to be a lie, and I'd checked, and neither one was.

It made me want to shake him. It made me want to warn him. It made me feel, in a low persistent way I refused to examine, like a thief standing next to a man who'd just shown me where he kept his keys.

Because here's my confession, the one I keep in the same pocket as Dain's journal: I had lied to him. Repeatedly. About the map, about the route, about why I really wanted to go south. He thought I was helping him out of decency, or debt to the dead stranger, or some smuggler's code of honor. He didn't know I'd seen that map before. He didn't know my brother had died carrying it. He didn't know that every mile we walked south was a mile I'd already walked in my head, planning this, needing this, long before Rafael Ashgrove ever stumbled into my life with his bloodstained inheritance and his terrible trusting eyes.

And now his trust was about to cost him, and I couldn't even warn him properly, because warning him meant explaining myself, and explaining myself meant losing him, and I'd apparently decided somewhere along the line that I'd rather owe him a betrayal than lose him.

Which is its own kind of debt, isn't it? The kind you can't pay with coin.

"Ninety-five crowns," Mudge repeated. "Or the goods. Or Ferro collects you instead. Choose quick; the tide won't wait and neither will I."

"We need a moment," I said.

"Take two," Mudge said magnanimously. "Ferro, give the lady air."

Ferro stepped aside just enough to make the point that stepping aside was a favor, not a right. I pulled Rafael into the corner by the strongroom door, and I dropped my voice to almost nothing.

"Give them the map," I said.

His head came up. "No."

"Raff. Listen to me. It's paper. It's a piece of hide with marks on it. We can get another—"

"There isn't another. It chose us. You said so yourself." His eyes were doing that thing they did when he was frightened and refusing to be frightened, the thing that made him look about nineteen years old. "Tamsin, if I give them the map, they sell it, and whoever buys it leads Serah straight to Crownforge. Everything the stranger died for ends up in Veyra's hands because I didn't want to be inconvenienced in a counting house."

"And if you don't, Ferro takes me down to the river, and I don't come back up."

Something moved behind his face — fear, yes, but underneath it something harder, something that surprised me. "No," he said again. "They won't. Not while I'm standing here."

"What are you going to do? Charm them?" I meant it as a joke. It came out bitter, because I knew him well enough by then to be afraid of the answer.

He didn't answer. He just reached into his coat, and I saw the little wooden disc in his palm — one of his charms, the harmless kind, the kind he sold in Correnvale for coppers. Luck tokens, love tokens, tokens against bad weather. I'd watched him make them a hundred times on the road, quick and quiet, whispering into the wood like a man apologizing to it.

Except this time he didn't whisper. This time he closed his eyes and *pulled*, and I felt it happen — felt the air in the room go taut as a bowstring, felt my teeth ache the way they do before lightning. The charm in his palm darkened from pale pine to something like iron, like a hole cut in the shape of a coin.

"Raff," I whispered. "What does that one do?"

"It makes people forget," he said. "A conversation. A few minutes. Whatever's inside the circle when it breaks." He looked at me, and his eyes were clear and sorry and absolutely certain. "It costs the maker. That's the rule. Every working takes something, and forgetting-magic takes the most."

"Costs what? Coin? Blood?"

"A year," he said. "Of memory. Give or take. I don't get to choose which year."

The floor tilted under me. Not literally — though for a second I'd have sworn the whole counting house leaned. "No. No, absolutely not, we'll find another way, we'll—"

"There isn't another way. You said it yourself on the Pass: the map chooses, and we follow. Well, this is the toll on the road." He almost smiled. "Besides, I've got thirty-one years in here. Losing one is arithmetic, not tragedy."

"It might be *me*," I said, and heard my own voice crack on it. "The year it takes. Raff, what if it takes Dunmarrow? What if it takes the Pass, the shrine, the child in the snow? What if you wake up on a ferry tomorrow and don't know who I am?"

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, gently, the way you talk to someone you're about to leave: "Then I suppose you'll have to introduce yourself again. You're good at talking your way into places. You talked your way onto my ferry, after all."

"That was *your* ferry and I paid for passage—"

"Tamsin." He took my hand. His grip was warm and completely unafraid. "I trust you. Whatever happens in the next five minutes, whatever I forget — I trust you. I need you to know that before I do this, so that when I look at you like a stranger, some part of you knows it isn't true. Do you understand?"

And that — that was the worst part. Worse than Mudge, worse than Ferro's smile, worse than the debt itself. Because he trusted me completely, openly, with his memory and his life and the whole mad quest, and I stood there holding all of it knowing exactly how much I didn't deserve it, and I couldn't say why. I couldn't say *I've seen the map before*. I couldn't say *my brother died for this and I'm using you to finish what he started*. I couldn't say any of it, because saying it would break the one thing in my life that had ever been given to me clean.

"Yes," I lied. "I understand."

* * *

He turned around and walked back to the desk like a man paying a bill.

"Mudge," he said pleasantly. "New offer. Ninety-five crowns, paid in full, right now. In exchange, you and your associate forget the last hour entirely. You'll remember seeing Tamsin on the dock, exchanging pleasantries, and nothing else. She'll still owe you — I'm not erasing the ledger, just the afternoon. But you'll have no reason to connect her to anything she's carrying, and no reason to go looking."

Mudge laughed. It was a big, easy laugh, the laugh of a man who owned the room. "And what's in it for me, friend?"

"The alternative," Rafael said, "is that I break this on the floor, and you forget the last hour anyway, and I keep the ninety-five crowns I'm about to conjure out of your own strongbox."

Silence. Ferro's hand drifted toward his belt. Mudge's laugh died by degrees, the way laughter does when the body understands danger before the mind does. He was a fat, comfortable man, but he hadn't gotten comfortable in Bray by being stupid, and something in the air had changed — the same tautness, the same pre-storm ache, the sense of a held breath.

"You're a charm-seller," Mudge said slowly. "From Correnvale. They said there was one on the road south. Selling luck tokens and—"

"Forgetting is just luck," Rafael said, "for people who'd be happier without the memory."

I'll give Mudge this: he did the sums fast. Whatever he saw in Rafael's face — and I've seen men stare down Wardens with less composure — convinced him that the arithmetic had changed. "Strongbox," he said finally, jerking his chin at Ferro. "Ninety-five. Counted."

Ferro counted it out onto the desk with the sour face of a man whose seven-year grudge was being dissolved by a wooden coin. Rafael swept the crowns into his pack, set the darkened charm on the desktop, and said, "On three. One. Two."

He stamped it under his heel.

The world *folded*. There's no better word. The room bent inward like light through water, and for half a heartbeat I felt the edge of the working brush past me — cold, impersonal, enormous — and then it snapped shut around Mudge and Ferro like a trapdoor, and both men blinked, and Mudge looked down at the coins on his desk with genuine puzzlement.

"Ferro," he said. "Why am I counting out ninety-five crowns?"

"You aren't," Ferro said, frowning at his own hands. "Are you?"

They stared at each other. They stared at the money. Somewhere in the building a clerk coughed, and the ordinary sounds of Bray came flooding back in — gulls, rigging, the fishmonger shouting prices — and Mudge shook his head like a man waking from a nap and said, "Get out, both of you, before I decide you're trouble."

We went. Fast. Out the back, down the alley I'd broken Ferro's nose in, along the quay to the ferry office, where Rafael paid our passage south with the Corvos' own coin and didn't say a word about it.

* * *

He was quiet on the boat. I told myself it was tiredness. Working magic like that would tire anyone, and he'd pulled something enormous through a body that hadn't practiced in seven years.

But when I brought him the tea the ferryman's wife sold at a scandalous markup, he took it and looked at me for a long moment — polite, distant, the look you give a fellow passenger — and said:

"Thank you. Have we... met? You seem familiar."

The river slid by. Oren, at the rail, watched me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't like.

"No," I said. "Not yet. My name's Tamsin."

"Tamsin," he repeated, tasting it. "I'm Rafael."

"I know," I said, and smiled, and it was the hardest smile of my life. "I know lots of things about you, Rafael Ashgrove. By the time this journey's done, you'll wish I didn't."

He laughed — easy, trusting, already gone — and turned to watch the water, and I sat there across from a man who'd paid a year of himself to save me, holding the truth in my lap like a stone I couldn't put down and couldn't lift.

And that was when Oren crossed the deck, sat down across from me, and said, very quietly:

"He'll want to know what you're hiding. And when he asks — and he will ask, Tamsin, men like him always ask eventually — I'd think hard about whether you tell him before Serah does. Because she's coming. I've seen her sign on the road behind us. And she knows your name."
