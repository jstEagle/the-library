## 12. The Traitor's Mark

I want to say I found the token by accident. That's the version I told Tamsin, later, when she asked — and it's even mostly true, which is more than I can say for most of what came out of my mouth that week.

Here's the truth of it: I couldn't sleep. We'd made camp in the ruins of an old waystation above the Bray road, three days out from the river town and my year of memory still sitting in me like a swallowed stone. Rafael Ashgrove, who used to be able to tell you the name of every charm he'd ever sold, now had a hole where a season should be. I kept reaching for it the way your tongue finds a missing tooth. *What did I pay? What did it cost?* The creditors had taken something, and I didn't know what, and not knowing was its own kind of wound.

So I sat up by the fire while Tamsin slept with her back to a wall and her knife loose in its sheath — habits of a woman who expected betrayal as routinely as weather — and Oren stood the last watch, because Oren always volunteered for the last watch.

That should have been my first clue. A man who wants to be awake when everyone else is asleep is either very loyal or very busy.

* * *

The token was small. That's what unsettled me most, afterward. Not some grand artifact of the usurper's court, not a thing that hummed with wrongness or bled shadow at the edges. It was a coin. A grey coin, older than any mint I knew, worn smooth as a river pebble, and Oren held it cupped in his palm like a man warming his hands over a fire that gave no heat.

I saw it because I have spent seven years learning to see magic the way other men learn to see in the dark. You don't look straight at it. You look at what it touches. And the firelight wasn't touching that coin. It fell on Oren's knuckles, on the dirt, on everything else in that ruined hall — but around the coin there was a stillness, a little circle where the light went to die.

I knew that stillness. Bound things are quiet. My gate at Ashgrove had been quiet for seven years, and I had been the one who bound it, and I remembered how the silence felt from the inside: patient, obedient, waiting for instructions.

The coin was waiting too.

Oren murmured something — so low I caught only the shape of words, not their substance — and the stillness shifted. Breathe in, breathe out. A conversation. One-sided, from what I could see, but conversations with bound tokens were never truly two-way. You spoke into them. Whatever listened on the far end heard everything.

And I knew exactly who listened on the far end, because I had seen grey cloaks in Dunmarrow's lower quarter, and I had seen Serah's face once across a burning street, calm as a woman reading a ledger while the world went up around her.

I did the cowardly thing first. I went back to my bedroll and lay down and stared at the rafters and thought: *maybe it's nothing. Maybe old soldiers talk to old coins. Maybe he's praying.*

Then I thought about the ambush in Dunmarrow. How the hunters had come to exactly the right quarter, at exactly the right hour, as if someone had drawn them a map. How Oren had appeared out of nowhere with a sword already drawn, as if he'd known the moment before the blades did.

How he'd refused, ever since, to explain how.

A man who saves your life buys a strange kind of credit. He can dodge questions for weeks on the strength of it, because every time you press him, some traitorous part of you whispers: *he took a blade meant for you. Be fair.* And I am, God help me, a fair man. Fairness has nearly killed me twice this year.

But fairness also demands you look at evidence, and the evidence was cupped in a dead man's palm forty feet away, drinking firelight.

* * *

I waited until dawn. Not out of strategy — out of fear, plain and simple. Confronting him at night, alone, seemed like an excellent way to become a footnote in someone else's story. But when Tamsin woke and started breaking camp with her usual economy — no wasted motion, no wasted words — I made myself stand up and walk over to Oren and hold out my hand.

"Give it to me."

He looked at my hand. Then at my face. He had a soldier's face, lined and sun-cured, with eyes that did arithmetic instead of feeling. "Give you what, charm-seller?"

"The coin. The one that isn't reflecting light. The one you've been whispering to on watch for — I don't know, how long? Since Dunmarrow? Since before?"

Something moved behind the arithmetic. Not guilt. Calculation. I've learned there's a difference, and the difference is that guilt slows a man down and calculation doesn't.

"You were watching," he said.

"I was sleepless. There's a difference." His own words, turned back on him. "The coin, Oren."

Tamsin had gone very still by the cold fire pit. She hadn't asked what we were talking about. She'd read the scene the way she read everything — fast, whole, suspicious — and her hand had already found her knife. "Rafael," she said quietly, "what is it?"

"He's talking to Serah."

The word landed in our little camp like a dropped lantern. Tamsin crossed the distance between us in four strides and put herself between me and Oren, which I would have found touching if I weren't so busy being terrified.

"Show her," I said. "Or I will. And I'll do it loudly, and then we'll all find out together whether bound tokens explode when their owners get angry."

For a long moment Oren just looked at us. Then, unhurried, almost bored, he opened his palm.

The coin sat there, grey and smooth and silent. Now that it was in the open, I could feel it properly — a faint pull, like a hook set behind my breastbone. Magic recognizes magic, even outlawed magic, even magic that hates itself. The binding on that thing was old work. Careful work. Whoever had made it had done it with love, or something wearing love's clothes.

"Say something to it," Tamsin said. Her voice was flat. Flat the way ice is flat right before it cracks.

"No," Oren said.

"Say something to it or I'll take it and feed it to the Bray river, and we'll see how your friends in grey track us after that."

"That," Oren said, "would be a mistake."

"Most of what's happened to me this year has been a mistake," Tamsin said. "One more won't break the pattern. Talk."

And Oren Vael — because that was when I finally learned his full name, and yes, *that* Vael, and yes, I know, I should have asked sooner; seven years of making myself small teaches you never to ask questions you don't want answered — Oren Vael closed his fingers over the coin and looked at each of us in turn, and said:

"Serah thinks I'm hers. Every route I've given her has been false. The ford we're avoiding? I told her we'd cross at Miller's Bend. The pass I steered us away from? I reported we'd take it tomorrow. She has been chasing our shadows for three weeks, and every shadow I've handed her has led her hunters somewhere we aren't." He let that sit. "You're welcome."

* * *

You could have heard a moth land.

Tamsin broke the silence first, because of course she did. "Prove it."

"How?"

"Tell me something Serah said. Something you couldn't have invented. Something specific."

Oren considered. "She called you 'the Salt Road girl.' Said your brother owed the same debt you do, and that debts outlive debtors." His voice didn't change at all. "She said to remind you that Correnvale remembers its smugglers, and that the Wardens keep excellent records."

I watched Tamsin take that. Watched it go into her like a blade going into water — no splash, no sound, just disappearance. Her brother. Her brother's name, in Serah's mouth, delivered through a traitor's coin, and Oren reciting it like a supply inventory.

"And you believed her?" I asked. "About the records?"

"I believe Serah always tells the truth," Oren said. "It's what makes her dangerous. Liars you can predict. Honest people rearrange themselves around the truth like water around a stone." He pocketed the coin. "Now. Are we finished with the theatricals? Because we have a pass to cross and I'd like to reach it before her hunters figure out Miller's Bend was empty."

"Not finished," Tamsin said. "Not even close. Why should we believe a word of this? You could be feeding us false routes just as easily as her. You could be playing both ends against the middle and keeping the middle for yourself."

"Yes," Oren said. "I could."

"That's your defense?"

"It's my honesty. You wanted specific truths; there's one. I *could* be betraying you. You cannot prove I'm not. Neither can I prove I am. All either of you can do is decide whether the man who pulled you out of a knife-fight in Dunmarrow is the same man who whispers to grey coins in the dark." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "I served King Aldous. I was at Crownforge the day he was crowned. I have watched a good man hollow out for seven years and I know who holds the spoon. If that makes me untrustworthy in your eyes, charm-seller, smuggler, then trust away. Walk without me. See how far the map takes you when Serah's hunters stop chasing shadows and start chasing certainty."

Nobody moved.

That's the thing about moments like that — the ones where trust cracks and nobody knows yet whether it's broken or just bent. They don't feel dramatic from the inside. They feel like standing in a doorway deciding whether to step out into rain. You weigh what you know against what you'll lose, and you choose, and you live inside the choosing.

Tamsin chose first. She always does. "We keep moving," she said. "And you walk in front, where I can see your hands."

"Reasonable," Oren said.

"And the coin stays in your pack, wrapped in cloth, and if I see it in your palm again I take it and we find out about the exploding."

"Also reasonable."

"And Rafael—" she turned to me, and for a second something flickered under the flatness, something that might have been appeal or might have been warning, "—you don't get to be the only honest one here. It's exhausting."

* * *

We walked. The pass rose ahead of us, grey and ringing — the locals called it the Pass of Hollow Bells, and I'd learn why soon enough, and wish I hadn't. Oren took point as ordered. Tamsin took rear guard, which put me in the middle, which is where I'd been all year: between people who knew more than they said, holding a map that chose its readers, carrying a debt I couldn't remember paying.

Half of me believed Oren. That half pointed at Dunmarrow, at the sword that had been drawn before the ambush sprang, at the arithmetic of a man who'd had a hundred chances to kill us and taken none. Half of me believed him completely, and that half was tired, and wanted to stop doing the work of suspicion, and wanted very badly to believe that loyalty could be real in a world where kings got hollowed out and smugglers paid their brothers' debts.

The other half kept seeing the coin. Kept hearing the stillness around it. Kept remembering that the best lies are built on true load-bearing walls — and Oren had given us true walls. Aldous. Crownforge. Serah's honest tongue. All true, probably. And all of it arranged, maybe, around a hollow center where a man named Vael fed our route to the woman hunting us, one careful falsehood at a time.

I couldn't tell which half was right. That was the worst of it. Not doubt — doubt you can act on. This was suspension, hanging between belief and disbelief like a man between ropes over a gorge, arms burning, no ledge in sight.

At midday we stopped at a shrine — one of the small roadside ones, pre-Outlawing, its saint's face worn to a suggestion. Tamsin went ahead to scout. Oren knelt, ostensibly to adjust his bootlace, and I watched his hand drift toward his pack.

I didn't stop him. That's the confession I keep circling. I saw it, and I let it happen, because I needed to know what he'd do when he thought no one was watching — and because some cold practical corner of me understood that a double agent is only useful while both sides believe him.

His fingers touched the pack. Stopped. Came away empty.

He knew I was watching. Of course he knew. He'd known since the fire.

He stood, and without looking at me, said: "Miller's Bend was empty. By tonight she'll know I lied. Which means by tomorrow, charm-seller, she stops trusting my routes — and starts trusting her hunters." He settled the pack on his shoulders. "Whatever you've decided about me, decide it fast. The luxury of suspicion runs out at the top of that pass."

He walked on. I stood in the shrine's cold shade, under the worn face of a saint whose name nobody remembered, and realized I had made my choice after all, without meaning to.

I believed him.

God help me, I believed him — the way you believe a rope bridge: not because you trust the rope, but because the gorge is deep and the far side is the only place left to go.

Behind us, far down the valley, thin and wrong, a bell began to ring.
