## 13. Ambush at the Ford

The river at Bray's crossing was the color of old pewter and about as welcoming, and I'd have bet my last coin that we'd die within sight of it.

Not a cheerful thought, I know. But when you've spent as many years on the Salt Road as I have, you learn to let the pessimism arrive early so it doesn't ambush you later. There was plenty else waiting to do that job.

We came down through the alder scrub in the grey hour before dawn, Rafael limping — he'd been limping since Bray, since the charm he'd sold to my creditors that cost him something he wouldn't name and I couldn't bear to ask about — and Oren walking like a man who'd never once been tired in his life, which should have been my first warning. Nobody is never tired. People who claim otherwise are selling something.

"The ford's shallow here," Oren said, crouching at the bank and studying the water like it owed him money. "Stone bottom. We can cross in twenty minutes if we don't dawdle."

"And if we dawdle?"

"Then we cross wetter."

I liked him better when he was mysterious. Mysterious men don't make jokes, and jokes from Oren felt like a dog standing on its hind legs — impressive, and wrong.

Rafael said nothing. He'd been saying very little for two days, ever since the confrontation with Oren and the bound token, ever since half our company had decided to believe a man who'd been caught whispering to Veyra's enforcer and the other half had decided to believe him anyway because the alternative was being two people alone in the mountains with no swordsman. I fell into neither camp. I fell into a third camp, one with a population of one, where I sat up nights wondering what I'd do if Oren turned on us, and what I'd do if he didn't, and why both answers made me feel sick.

The truth was — and I'm getting ahead of myself, but the truth has a way of shoving to the front of the line — that I'd stopped worrying about Oren somewhere outside Dunmarrow. Not because I trusted him. Because I'd realized, with the particular clarity that comes at three in the morning around a bad fire, that I had no moral high ground from which to judge anyone. I was carrying a dead man's journal in my pack that said more than I'd ever said out loud, and every mile south it got heavier, and every mile south I found another reason not to open it, and every reason was a lie wearing honesty's coat.

But that's later. First, the ford.

* * *

We crossed in single file, water to mid-shin, cold enough to make your teeth file formal complaints. The stone underfoot was slick with river-moss, and Rafael slipped twice, and both times I caught his arm without thinking about it, and both times he looked at me with an expression I didn't have a name for. Gratitude, maybe. Or the look of a man doing arithmetic about how much of himself he had left to give away.

"Almost there," Oren called back. He was already on the far bank, scanning the treeline, and there was something in the set of his shoulders —

I want to be fair to him here. Whatever Oren was, whatever he'd done or meant to do, I believe — I still believe — that when the arrows came, he moved before he thought, and you can't fake that. You can fake a lot. You can fake loyalty for months, fake grief, fake a laugh at the right moment. But you cannot fake the half-second where a body chooses between itself and someone else. That half-second is the only honest thing most of us ever do.

The first arrow took the alder trunk beside Rafael's head and buried itself six inches deep with a sound like a door slamming.

The second would have taken Rafael himself if Oren hadn't already been moving — off the bank, down the stones, one arm sweeping Rafael behind him while his sword cleared its sheath in the same motion, all of it so fast and so smooth it looked rehearsed, and maybe it was, but not by him.

"Ambush!" I shouted, uselessly, because everyone including the archers already knew. "Upstream! Rocks!"

Grey cloaks. Four of them on the near bank melting out of the alders, and more across the river — Serah's hunters, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had done this many times and never once lost. They'd let us get halfway across. Of course they had. A ford is a trap you walk into voluntarily; the river splits your company and slows your feet and gives every archer on the far bank a shooting gallery.

"Back!" Oren roared. "Back to the trees — go!"

Rafael went. I went. And then the world went to pieces in the specific way the world does when steel comes out.

* * *

I've fought before. Let's be clear about that — I wasn't some trembling merchant's daughter dragged onto the road. I'd knifed a customs man in Dunmarrow harbor at nineteen and slept fine afterward. But fighting is different when it's four against three, when it's arrows out of mist, when the man beside you might be a traitor and the man behind you has spent seven years pretending he isn't magic and is about to stop.

Because he did stop. Pretending, I mean.

It happened fast. One of the hunters broke from the treeline toward Rafael — big man, axe, the kind of confident stride that says *this one's mine* — and Rafael turned and did something with his hand and the air between them went white. Not fire. Not lightning. Something older and quieter, a pressure like the moment before thunder, and the hunter folded backward into the alders and didn't get up.

Seven years of silence, broken in half a heartbeat, on a riverbank, for me.

There was no time to feel anything about it. There was barely time to fight. I got my knife into the shoulder of a grey-cloak who'd come at me from the shallows — she was quick, quicker than me, and I only won because she hesitated, one flicker of a second, looking past me at Rafael with an expression I'd understand later and hate understanding: recognition. She knew what he was. She'd been sent for him specifically.

Oren was everywhere. That's the only honest description. He fought the way water fights — no wasted motion, no flourish, just an economy of violence that made the other hunters' swordwork look like festival dancing. He killed the archer on the far bank with a thrown knife I didn't see him throw. He put down the woman coming at my blind side without appearing to look at her. And through all of it he kept positioning himself between Rafael and the river, between Rafael and the trees, between Rafael and everything, and some cold practical corner of my mind kept whispering: *he's guarding the asset. That's what this looks like too.*

That's the trouble with paranoia. Once it moves in, it pays rent in every direction.

The fight lasted maybe ninety seconds. It felt like a season. When it ended, the ford was quiet except for the river and Rafael's breathing, ragged, and one hunter crawling away through the reeds with an arrow in his leg — Oren's work, deliberately placed, I'd swear — and Oren standing over him.

"Who sent you?" Oren asked.

The hunter spat blood into the water. "You know who sent us." And then he looked at Oren, really looked, and smiled with red teeth, and said the sentence that unraveled everything:

"She said you'd ask. She said to tell you the route held true this time."

* * *

Oren killed him. Quickly, cleanly, almost gently — a mercy, or a silencing, and I genuinely could not tell which, and that frightened me more than the whole fight had.

For a long moment none of us spoke. Mist off the river. Blood thinning pink in the current. Somewhere upstream a bird started singing, the absolute idiot.

"The route held true," Rafael repeated quietly. He was pale — paler than the fight explained, drained in a way I recognized now, the cost of the working still settling into him. "Meaning what? Meaning they knew where we'd cross?"

"They knew," I said.

Oren turned. His face did something complicated. "Tamsin—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't defend yourself yet. Let me think."

Because here's what I was thinking: the route *had* held true. We'd crossed exactly where Oren suggested, exactly when he suggested, and the hunters had been waiting. That was the case against him, and it was damning. But the hunter's words cut the other way too — *she said you'd ask* — which meant Serah expected Oren to interrogate prisoners, which meant Serah expected Oren to be questioned, which meant either he was hers and sloppy about hiding it, or he was playing a game deep enough that even her own soldiers were props in it. Double agency, he'd claimed. Feeding false routes to protect us. And if that was true, then this route — the one that led us into an ambush — was the false one, and the real question was why it had failed to be false.

Or why it had succeeded perfectly.

"I didn't bring them here," Oren said. "On my life."

"That's the problem," I said. "Your life is the one thing you can spend without anyone noticing the cost."

"Tamsin." Rafael's voice, low. "He pulled me out of the arrow's path before I saw the bowman. Before *anyone* saw the bowman."

"Or he knew where the bowman was."

"You think he'd take an arrow risk on a performance?"

"I think," I said, and my voice came out harder than I intended, harder than I wanted, "that I am done deciding who to trust based on who bleeds for me. I've done that arithmetic before. It always comes out wrong."

And that's when it happened. That's when the morning stopped being about Oren at all.

* * *

The strap of my pack had been cut. I noticed it when I shrugged the pack off my shoulders to check the damage — a clean slice through the leather, one of the hunters' blades, a miss that should have been a hit. The pack sagged. Things shifted inside it. And out of the torn gap, wedged between a coil of rope and my spare shirt, slid a book.

Small. Water-stained. Leather cover gone soft as cloth from years of handling. My brother's journal.

I had kept it hidden. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere is listening: I had *kept it hidden*, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the pack, and I had told no one — not Rafael, not Oren — that I carried it. And now it lay in the mud of the ford in plain view, fallen open to a page I knew by heart, because I had read it so many times the ink was going soft.

*The map shows Crownforge,* my brother's handwriting said. *Under Vael. The Ember burns where the crown was made. If I don't come back, find Yara Pemberton's contact in Dunmarrow — no. Find no one. Burn this page. Tam, if you're reading this, I'm sorry, and the map chose me, and I don't know what that means yet, but I know where it goes.*

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Rafael looked at the journal. Then at me. And I watched him arrive at it — watched the understanding move across his face like weather, slow and total, drowning everything in its path. The map. The stranger's dying gift. The thing I'd offered to guide him toward, negotiated for, bled beside him over.

*I've seen it before.* Chapter and verse. I'd known where the Ember burned since before I ever met him. Since my brother died carrying the same secret, on the same road, running from the same hunters. And I had let Rafael believe — had let him trust, had taken his year of memory spent buying my debts, had watched him break seven years of silence to save my life — all while sitting on the one fact that reframed everything.

"You knew," Rafael said. Not loud. That was the worst part. He didn't shout. He said it the way you'd note a change in the wind.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Since before Bray. Since—" I stopped. Lying was easy; it had always been easy; it was the one trade I'd mastered. And I found, standing in the shallows with my brother's handwriting bleeding in the mud between us, that I couldn't do it anymore. Maybe it was the fight. Maybe it was the white flash of his working, seven years of fear spent in a heartbeat for a woman who'd been lying to him since the day they met. "Since the beginning. Since the Salt Road. The map — I recognized it. It's the same map Wren carried. The same route. He died carrying it, and I took it off his body, and I memorized it, and I burned it, and then a stranger pressed the same map into your hands and I thought — I thought if I stayed close to it, close to you, I'd finally learn what killed him."

"And instead you learned what I'd pay for you," Rafael said. "A year of my life. In Bray. For a debt you let stand rather than tell me the truth."

"It wasn't like that."

"Tell me how it was, then."

And I opened my mouth, and nothing came out, because the honest answer was: *it was exactly like that.* I had let him pay. I had stood in that room in Bray and watched him hand over a year of himself — watched him come out of it not knowing what he'd lost, reaching instinctively for memories that weren't there anymore, asking me what he'd forgotten, and I had said *nothing important* — and I had done it because telling the truth would have cost me my place beside the map, and I had chosen the map. I had chosen my brother's ghost over the living man in front of me, over and over, every day, in small ways and large ones, and I had dressed the choice up as loyalty until I almost believed it.

Oren picked the journal up out of the mud. Gently, damn him. He read the open page, and his face gave away nothing, and he held it out to Rafael handle-first like evidence at a trial.

"The Ember burns where the crown was made," Oren read aloud. "Crownforge. Under Vael." He looked at me. "She's known the destination since Dunmarrow. Possibly since the Salt Road."

"I know what it says."

"Do you know what it *means*?" Oren's voice had an edge I hadn't heard before, and I realized — with the delayed horror of a trap springing — that I'd handed him something. All my suspicion of Oren, all my careful triangulation, and in the end I'd wounded Rafael and armed Oren in the same motion. "Because I'll tell you what it means. It means every choice she's made on this road served the map first and us second. It means when she argued for the southern route, when she pushed us through Hollow Bells, when she—"

"Enough." Rafael took the journal from Oren's hands. Held it. Looked at me across the ruined ford with the river running between us like a border neither of us could cross anymore.

Here is the thing I will carry to my grave: he didn't look angry. He looked like a man reading a letter from someone he loved and finding out it was written by someone else entirely.

"I would have helped you," he said. "Find out what happened to him. I would have helped. You never had to lie."

"I know."

"That's what makes it—"

He stopped. Closed the journal. And the silence stretched, and the mist came up the river, and somewhere downstream I could hear horses — more hunters, or the same ones regrouping, it hardly mattered — and Rafael Ashgrove, gatekeeper, charm-seller, the man who'd paid a year of his life for my name, tucked my brother's journal into his own coat and turned away from me.

"We move," he said to Oren. "South. While they're regrouping."

"And her?" Oren asked. Softly. Helpfully. Like a knife offered grip-first.

Rafael didn't turn around. "She knows where we're going. She's always known. She can keep up or she can't."

They started up the far bank. And I stood in the shallows of the ford with the water pulling at my legs and my brother's secrets gone from my keeping, and I understood that I had finally, truly, run out of road behind me. Everything I'd hoarded — the lies, the half-truths, the comfortable distances — was spent. All that was left was the truth, ugly and unarmored, and the two men walking away from me, and the choice I should have made in Dunmarrow, in Bray, on the Salt Road, every single day since Wren died.

I climbed the bank after them. Because the Ember was real, and Veyra's hunters were real, and my brother's death deserved better than my pride.

And because — gods help me — Rafael was limping again, and nobody was watching his blind side, and I had not yet finished paying for what I'd done.

Behind us, downstream, the horses reached the ford.
