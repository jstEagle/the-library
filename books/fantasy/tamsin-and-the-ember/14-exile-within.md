## 14. Exile Within

Oren left at dawn without a word, which was the most honest thing he'd done since we met him. He took the north road with his sword wrapped in sacking and his face turned away from us, as if looking back might cost him something. Maybe it would have. I watched him go from the treeline while Tamsin checked the straps on her pack for the fourth time, pretending she wasn't watching too.

"He'll come back," she said.

"Probably."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

So it went: the company of three became a company of two, and the two of us had nothing to say to each other that wouldn't become an argument. Tamsin had known where the Ember burned before I ever touched that map. Her brother had died carrying it. She'd let me believe — no, worse, she'd let me *assume* — that we were strangers walking into this together, when all along she'd been walking home to something. And I had paid a year of my memory to her creditors without blinking, because I trusted her, and trust, it turns out, is just a longer word for the thing you do right before you get hurt.

We walked south through country that got poorer the further we went from Dunmarrow. Burned crofts. Fields gone to thistle. A milestone with Veyra's seal chiseled off it, someone's small defiance, though the stone didn't seem grateful.

By the third day the silence between us had developed architecture. There were rooms in it. The room where I replayed the ambush at the ford, Oren fighting on our side like a man proving a point. The room where Tamsin sat with her brother's journal open on her knees by firelight, reading pages she must have read a hundred times, not knowing I could see her face. The largest room was simply empty, and we both lived there.

"You could ask," she said on the fourth morning, apropos of nothing. We were crossing a stubble field, frost crunching underfoot.

"I could."

"You're not going to."

"I'm deciding what order to be angry in," I said. "It's taking some time. There are several categories."

That earned me half a breath of a laugh, quickly strangled. Progress, of a kind. In Correnvale I'd spent seven years learning to make myself small — smaller than suspicion, smaller than notice — and I'd thought those skills were behind me. But I made myself small again now, out of habit or self-protection, and Tamsin did the same, and two small people can walk a very long way without touching.

The mountains came up to meet us on the fifth day, grey shoulders rising out of pine dark, and somewhere among them, according to the map, lay the shrine.

* * *

I say "according to the map" the way you might say "according to a cat." The map had opinions. It showed itself only when I touched it — skin to vellum, no gloves, no intermediaries — and even then it showed what it pleased. Roads appeared and unappeared. On one memorable evening it had displayed a detailed diagram of a fish trap, which Tamsin insisted was a message and I maintained was the map being bored.

But the shrine it marked clearly: a curl of ink off the mountain road, a symbol like a flame inside a cupped hand. Old road-shrine, pre-Veyra, maybe pre-Aldous. Pilgrim country once. Now just stone and weather.

We found it at dusk, tucked into a fold of the hillside where the pines opened like a held breath. It had been a proper building once — a round chamber, domed, its roof fallen inward so the sky pooled in the middle of the floor like cold blue water. Carvings covered every wall, worn soft by centuries of rain: figures with raised hands, flames passing palm to palm down a line of them, generation to generation, until the last figure set the flame into — I crouched, traced it — a shape I recognized. A crown.

"Well," Tamsin said behind me. "That's cheerful."

There was a dry corner beneath the broken lintel where we could shelter, and we built a small fire because the alternative was freezing, and we ate hard bread and harder cheese and said perhaps forty words between us, most of them mine, none of them useful. Tamsin took first watch. I lay down with my pack for a pillow and the map against my chest because I'd taken to sleeping with it there, which Tamsin had noticed and said nothing about, another room in the silence.

I woke in the deep hours to light.

Not firelight. Firelight flickers; this didn't. It came from my own chest, from the map pressed against me, a gold glow leaking through my shirt seams like the sun through shutter slats. I sat up too fast and the world tilted, and the map slid from my shirt into my lap and opened itself — I want to be precise here, because later I told this story many times and people always assumed I opened it. I did not. It opened the way a flower opens, or an eye.

The vellum was warm. Under my fingers the ink moved, roads crawling like veins, and then the roads drained away entirely and the map went blank except for one mark: the flame-in-the-cupped-hand. The shrine's own sign. And then the mark rose off the page.

I should describe what happened next honestly, which is difficult, because vision doesn't file itself neatly into prose. The chamber around me stayed — the fallen roof, the dead fire, Tamsin a dark shape by the door with her head snapping up, hand already on her knife — but over it, through it, the shrine rebuilt itself. Walls whole. Roof whole. Torches burning with that same steady gold. And figures everywhere, hundreds of them, kneeling in ranks on a floor that had been bare stone, and every one of them holding a small flame cupped in their palms, and every flame singing.

Yes. Singing. A note too low to hear properly, felt more than heard, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth.

In the center stood a young man in plain clothes, and I knew him instantly, the way you know a face from a coin. Aldous. Not the king on the stamps and the banners — younger, unarmored, frightened. He knelt before a forge that filled the whole far wall of the vision, a forge big enough to walk into, its mouth glowing white. Around him stood figures in robes, their faces smooth as eggs, and they were speaking words I couldn't catch but whose meaning arrived anyway, the way meaning does in dreams:

*A king must be more than a man. A man can be bought, frightened, killed. Take the part of him that can be taken. Keep it safe. Let what remains be steady.*

And Aldous — gods help him, he was so young — nodded. He leaned forward toward the forge-light himself. Nobody forced him. That was the detail that broke something in me: he chose it. Whatever they told him the price was, he chose it, and the robed figures reached into his chest — not with hands, exactly, with something like hands — and drew out a thread of white-gold fire, and the sound he made was not a scream. It was quieter than a scream. It was the sound of a man realizing, mid-moment, that he has agreed to something he does not understand, and that it is too late to ask.

They set the thread of fire in a cradle of iron and stone, and the cradle cooled, and the singing stopped, and the young man who remained stood up straighter than before, calmer, and his eyes had gone the flat grey of a winter river.

*This is mercy,* the smooth faces said, to him or to themselves, I couldn't tell. *A king who cannot be corrupted. A king who cannot despair. We have saved him from himself.*

The vision folded. The walls fell back into ruin, the torches back into darkness, and I was on my knees on cold stone with the map closed in my fists and my heart going like a trapped bird.

Tamsin was beside me. I don't know how long she'd been there. Her hand hovered near my shoulder without landing, which was very much the state of us.

"Rafael." My name, at least. First time in days. "What did it show you?"

I told her. All of it, in order, because if I stopped anywhere I wasn't sure I could start again. The forge. The choice. The thread of fire drawn out of a living chest. When I finished, the silence between us had changed shape — the empty rooms were gone, and something else stood in their place, raw and unfinished.

"The Ember isn't a weapon," I said. My voice sounded scraped. "It's not a treasure. It's not even — everyone's been hunting it like it's a thing Veyra keeps locked up. It's *him*. Part of him. They cut it out of him at his crowning and everything since — the binding, the armies that never break, the way his soldiers don't desert even starving — it all runs through that piece of his soul like a wire."

Tamsin had gone very still. "Then the king we're trying to save—"

"Is missing a piece. Has been since the day they crowned him. Everything he's suffered, everything Veyra's done wearing his authority — it started with men who thought they were being kind." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Seven years I hid my magic from the Wardens because they said it was dangerous. Dangerous. Those smooth-faced bastards cut a hole in a living man and called it salvation."

"And destroying the Ember?" Tamsin asked quietly. She always asked the question I was circling. "If it's part of him—"

"Then destroying it might kill him. Or worse." I looked at the dead fire, the ruined carvings, the line of figures handing flame down the generations to set it in a crown. "Or it might free him. I don't know. The map knows. The map has known this whole time — it's been *herding* us, Tamsin. Choosing us. Showing me fish traps one night and this the next."

She was quiet a long moment. Then, slowly, she reached into her pack and brought out her brother's journal, and set it on the stones between us, open to a page dense with her brother's cramped handwriting. One line was underlined twice, the ink gone brown:

*The flame remembers whose it was.*

"He wrote that," she said. "Before Bray. Before—" She stopped. Started again, and I heard the cost of it. "Doran figured out what the Ember was. Not where it was — what. He thought that was why they killed him. Not for carrying the map. For understanding it." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and honest, finally, finally honest. "I've known since Dunmarrow that I wasn't guiding you. I was finishing his errand. I should have told you. I told myself I was protecting you, which is what liars say when the truth is they're protecting themselves."

The old anger was there, ready, familiar. I reached for it and found it had gone soft. Seven years of making myself small, and here was a woman making herself large at last, at cost, in a ruined shrine at midnight, and what was I supposed to do — punish her for arriving late to honesty? I'd hidden my gift from the entire world for seven years. We were both smugglers of a kind. She'd smuggled the truth; I'd smuggled myself.

"Doran sounds like he was smarter than both of us," I said.

"He was insufferable about it, too." A wet laugh. "You'd have hated him."

"I'd have liked him enormously."

Outside, far down the valley, a bell rang. Once. Then again — wrong, hollow, the cracked note of bells rung by decree rather than devotion, the sound we'd heard in the emptied villages of the pass. Except we were past the pass now. There shouldn't have been anyone left to ring anything.

Tamsin's head came up like a deer's. "That's not a village bell."

"No," I said. The map was warm against my chest again, warming further, and beneath my ribs something answered it — my buried, forbidden, seven-years-silent gift stirring like a sleeper hearing its name called. "It's a summons. Or a warning."

"From whom?"

I looked at the carving on the wall: the last figure setting the flame into the crown, and above it, newly visible in the map's faint glow, a second line of inscription I hadn't seen before, in a script older than any kingdom, that I somehow read anyway:

*What was taken will be returned, or all things bound shall burn.*

"Tamsin," I said, "I think Crownforge is expecting us."
