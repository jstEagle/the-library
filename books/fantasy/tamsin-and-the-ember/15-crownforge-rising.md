## 15. Crownforge Rising

The citadel of Vael did not fall so much as it gave up.

That was my first thought, anyway, standing at the tree line with Rafael beside me and the mountain wind trying to peel the skin off my face. You expect ruins to look broken — walls cracked, towers toppled, the whole sad arithmetic of war written in stone. Vael wasn't like that. The walls stood. The towers stood, all nine of them, black against a sky the color of old pewter. What was missing was everything else. No banners. No birds. Not so much as ivy on the masonry, as if the citadel had decided, some forty years ago, that it was finished being part of the world and had simply stopped answering when anyone knocked.

"It's not ruined," Rafael said quietly. He'd been quiet for two days, which for Rafael was less a mood than a weather system. "It's abandoned."

"There's a difference?"

"Ruins are what happens to things people loved." He pulled his cloak tighter. "This is what happens to things people were afraid of."

I wanted to make a joke. That's usually my job — I'm the one who talks when the silence gets expensive, who fills the road with complaints about the food and the mud and Rafael's habit of apologizing to horses. But there was nothing in me that wanted to be funny just then. My brother's journal sat against my ribs where I'd carried it since the ford, since Rafael had read those pages in front of everyone and looked at me like I'd driven the knife myself. We hadn't spoken about it. We hadn't spoken about much of anything. Two people walking toward the same fire, each carrying our own reason to burn.

"The map," I said instead.

He already had it out. He always had it out now; he touched it the way other men touch a wound to check if it still hurts. In the grey light the parchment looked ordinary, travel-stained, unremarkable — until his fingers spread across it, and the ink woke.

It did that thing it did, the thing that had stopped frightening me only because I'd run out of room to be frightened of new things. Lines surfaced beneath the surface, silver-pale, moving like something swimming under ice. And there, at the center, the mark that had appeared three nights ago and hadn't left: a small burning point below the citadel's foundations, pulsing slow and patient, like a heart that had been waiting a very long time for someone to take its pulse.

"Crownforge," Rafael said. "Directly beneath us. Under the undercrofts, under whatever's under those." He looked up at the silent towers. "Tamsin. Your brother came here."

"I know."

"He came here and he didn't come back."

"I know that too, Rafael. I was there for the not-coming-back. It's the one part of this story I can recite from memory."

He flinched. Good, said a small ugly voice in me. Then, immediately after, worse: *he's been nothing but kind to you since you lied to him for the entire length of a country.* I sighed and did the thing I'd been avoiding doing, which was being honest on purpose.

"I'm sorry. That was — I'm frightened, and when I'm frightened I get sharp, and when I get sharp people bleed. Ask anyone I've ever smuggled anything for." I nodded at the citadel. "Yes. Bren came here. His journal has three pages about the approach and then it stops mid-sentence, which is either very bad or very dramatic, and knowing Bren it was both. He'd have wanted it that way. My brother never met an exit he didn't want to make an entrance through."

Rafael studied me for a moment. Seven years of hiding teaches a man to read faces, and he read mine the way he read the map — patiently, looking for the lines underneath.

"We don't have to go in," he said.

"Yes we do."

"We could turn south. Dunmarrow. The council—"

"And tell them what? That we found a magic map that leads to a magic fire under a haunted castle, and we'd like them to please start a war about it?" I shook my head. "Nobody follows a story they can't see the end of, Rafael. Not councils, not kings, not me. We go down. We see what's actually burning. Then we come back with something true enough to frighten people into listening."

"That's not how truth usually works."

"No," I agreed. "But it's how wars do."

* * *

We went in through the water gate, because the front gate was the sort of invitation you decline.

Bren's journal had been specific about this much: *the river remembers its old bed.* A stream ran down from the mountains and vanished beneath the citadel's western wall through an arch built for exactly that purpose, back when whoever built Vael assumed their masonry would outlast the river's loyalty. It had. The arch stood open, half-drowned, dripping moss, and the water running through it was cold enough to make my hands ache within ten steps.

"You know," I said, wading, "in the free cities they tell children that Vael was struck by the gods for pride."

"In Correnvale they tell children it burned."

"Both could be true. Gods are rarely original."

The passage beyond the arch climbed gently, the water shallowing until we could walk beside it rather than in it. And here was the thing I hadn't expected, the thing that made the skin on my arms prickle even through soaked sleeves: the passage was lit. Faintly. The walls themselves gave off a glow like the last coals of a banked fire, deep red veined through black stone, so that we walked inside what felt like the interior of some enormous sleeping animal. Rafael's hand kept drifting toward his knife and drifting away again, because there was nothing to fight. There was only the light, and the silence, and the sense — growing with every step — that we were being *noticed.*

"It knows we're here," Rafael whispered.

"What does?"

"The map." He held it up. The silver lines had gone strange, all flowing toward the burning point at the center, converging, eager. "No. Not the map. Whatever the map is *of.* It's been waiting so long that—" He stopped. Swallowed. "When I touch it, it feels glad. Does that make sense? It feels like a door opening for someone it recognizes."

I didn't say what I was thinking, which was that the map had never glowed like that for me. I'd touched it once, early on, before I understood what it was, and it had lain dead as any scrap of parchment. Only Rafael's hands woke it. Only Rafael's hands, and once — though I'd told no one, least of all him — mine, for half a heartbeat, on the night we crossed the pass, when I'd picked it up thinking it was kindling. For half a heartbeat it had shown me a corridor of black stone and a red light at the end, and then it had gone cold and blank, like a dog that had barked at the wrong stranger and was embarrassed about it.

I'd decided since that I'd imagined it. People decide that sort of thing all the time. It's practically a civic duty.

The passage opened without warning into a hall so vast that my eyes refused, at first, to give it dimensions. Pillars like the trunks of petrified trees marched away into red-lit distance. Between them lay the wreckage of a civilization's confidence: overturned thrones — *thrones*, plural, someone had once thought Vael needed more than one — shattered armor, coins fused to the floor by heat so old the metal had grown into the stone like roots. And everywhere that same ember-glow, breathing. Slowly. Rhythmically.

"Tamsin." Rafael's voice had gone thin. "Look at the dust."

I looked. The dust on the floor wasn't scattered. It lay in long, swept curves, hundreds of them, overlapping, radiating outward from the hall's far end like the rings of a felled tree. As if something enormous had turned, over and over, in place. Or as if something enormous had *circled*, over and over, keeping watch.

"How long has this place been empty?" I asked, and my voice came out smaller than I intended.

"Forty years," Rafael said. "Give or take."

"Then who's been sweeping?"

Neither of us answered that. Neither of us needed to. Some questions you ask just to hear how loud the silence is afterward.

We crossed the hall along the wall, close as we could press ourselves to the pillars, and found the descent at its far side: a stair cut into the living rock, wide enough for six abreast, going down into deeper red. Steps worn hollow in their centers. Worn by feet, over centuries, or worn by something else entirely. Bren's journal had ended somewhere near here. I took it out — I don't know why, except that carrying it had started to feel like leaving him behind if I didn't — and read the last line again by the light of the walls themselves.

*It isn't a weapon. Whatever they told you, whatever the crown told itself, it isn't a weapon, it's a*

And nothing. Forty years of nothing after that sentence.

"He knew," I said. "Before he died, he knew what it was. He just didn't have time to write it down." I closed the journal. "Or someone didn't want him to."

Rafael put his hand briefly over mine on the book's cover. He didn't say anything. He'd gotten good, these past weeks, at saying things without words — probably because every word between us had gotten so heavy we'd both quietly agreed to stop lifting them.

We went down.

* * *

The stair descended farther than any stair has a right to. I counted four hundred steps and then stopped counting, partly because the number stopped mattering and partly because counting was the only thing keeping the fear in a box, and around step five hundred the box started to leak.

Because the walls changed.

Above, the red glow had been ambient, diffuse, the light of coals. Down here it gathered. It pooled in veins along the rock like molten gold poured into dark bread, thickening as we descended, until by the last hundred steps the walls weren't stone with light in them anymore but light with stone holding it in — barely. The heat rose to meet us, dry and enormous, the heat of a forge seen from across a valley. My lips cracked. Sweat ran into my eyes and stung. And underneath everything, faint but unmistakable now, a sound: a low, vast resonance, too deep to be called humming, rising and falling so slowly that you could mistake it for your own pulse if you weren't careful. If you weren't careful, you could start believing the mountain was alive and that it had been waiting for you specifically, and that it was pleased.

"Rafael," I said. "Tell me you feel that."

"I feel it." His face, in the vein-light, looked years older and years younger at once. "It's not hostile. That's the strange part. Everything down here feels—" he searched, "—*attentive.* Like a crowd before a play starts."

"Wonderful. I've always wanted to perform for geology."

He almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in two days, and I hoarded it.

Then the stair ended, and we stepped out into Crownforge, and I stopped making jokes altogether, which those who know me will tell you is the surest sign of catastrophe.

I cannot describe it properly. I'm a smuggler, not a poet; my trade is in what fits in a false-bottomed crate. But I'll try, because someone should try, because if we die down here then the only account of this place will be whatever Serah's hunters write, and theirs will be all chains and obedience.

Crownforge was a cavern, but that word is an insult to it. It was a cathedral that had been inverted — carved downward instead of upward, its vaults sunk into the earth, its nave descending in terraces of black stone toward a central floor where the heat lived. Channels ran through those terraces, rivers of slow golden light, converging. Everything converged. Every vein in every wall, every channel in every terrace, every thread of that patient red glow, all of it flowed inward and down to the center of the center, where —

Where it burned.

Not fire. Fire is hungry and quick and dies when you stop feeding it. This was older than hunger. It rose from a cradle of black iron no bigger than a bier, a flame perhaps the height of a man, and it burned without consuming anything, without flickering, without needing. It simply *was*, the way a mountain is, the way grief is. The light of it filled the cavern and moved in the channels and breathed in the walls, and standing there at the top of the last stair, I understood — suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've known all along — why kingdoms had been built on lies to keep this place secret, and why my brother had spent his last breath trying to finish a sentence about it.

"It's beautiful," Rafael said. His voice broke on the second word.

"Yeah," I managed. "That's usually how the bad ones get you."

And then I saw Oren.

He stood on the lowest terrace, twenty paces from the cradle, sword sheathed, hands loose at his sides — a man deliberately arranged to look harmless, which from Oren was its own kind of threat. He'd changed since the ford. Harder to say how. The same grey travel clothes, the same scarred jaw, the same way of standing like a drawn blade pretending to be a walking stick. But something in his face had settled, the way a man settles when he's finally arrived where he's been going for a very long time.

He watched us come down the last steps. He didn't reach for his sword. He didn't smile.

"You're late," he said. "But then, you would be. The map doesn't hurry for anyone it doesn't trust."

"Oren." Rafael's hand had found his knife hilt, and I noticed — filed away, in the cold little ledger-keeping corner of my mind that never stops working — that Oren's eyes tracked the movement and dismissed it. "You were supposed to be a day behind us. On the southern pass."

"I was. Then I wasn't." He gestured, unhurried, at the terraces above and behind us. "There were hunters in the upper galleries. Six of them. Serah's grey-cloaks, advance party. They reached the water gate yesterday morning." He paused. "They won't be reaching anything again. I cleared the way."

"Cleared it," I repeated. "Six armed hunters. Alone."

"Five," Oren said. "The sixth ran. I let him. Someone has to carry the news that the way is open." He turned, finally, to look full at the Ember, and the light of it moved across his face like water, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a swordsman than like a pilgrim at the end of a road that had cost him everything he owned. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone rough. "Forty years I've known about this place. Forty years I've walked toward it. Do you know what that does to a man? It makes him efficient."

Rafael came down another step, putting himself slightly ahead of me, which was absurd — I was the one with the knives — and also, annoyingly, made my chest do something complicated that I chose not to examine. "You said you served the king," Rafael said. "You said you wanted to free him."

"I did say that." Oren's gaze came back to us, level and calm and utterly unreadable. "Every word of it was true."

"And the token. The messages to Serah."

"Also true. Both things, Rafael. Both at once. I know how that sounds." He spread his hands. "I know exactly how that sounds. But look where you're standing. Look at what's in front of you. Nothing down here is only one thing — not the light, not the mountain, not the story. Why should we be?"

The Ember burned. The channels flowed. Somewhere high above, faint as a memory, I thought I heard the water gate drip, and thought: *that's the sound of the door closing behind us,* and thought: *it was always going to close. Doors like this exist to close.*

"Bren came here," I said.

Oren looked at me. Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, or regret, or the particular tiredness of a man who has been waiting for a question he hoped wouldn't come.

"Yes," he said. "He did."

"You met him."

"I did."

"And?"

Oren drew a slow breath, and the Ember flared — just slightly, just once, like a sleeper stirring — and in its light his face was impossible to read, and the whole cavern seemed to lean in to hear what he would say next.

"And," he said, "you should sit down for the rest."
