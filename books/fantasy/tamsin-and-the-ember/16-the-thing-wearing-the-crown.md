## 16. The Thing Wearing the Crown

I have killed forty-one people in the Queen's service. I keep the count because the Queen keeps the count — she asks for it every season, in that soft voice she uses when she is being merciful, and I have learned that mercy in Veyra's court is a ledger with your name on it.

Forty-two, if you count the smuggler in Bray, which I don't, because he's still breathing. Mostly.

The truce banner was Tamsin's idea, which surprised me, because nothing about Tamsin has surprised me since I read her brother's journal. The white cloth flew from a broken spear shaft above the ruins of Vael's outer gate like an apology nobody meant. Beneath it stood three people: the charm-seller with his haunted eyes, the smuggler with her brother's ghost riding her shoulders, and Oren Vale, who was doing a very poor job of looking like he hadn't been writing to me for two hundred miles.

And me. Serah of the Grey Cloak, enforcer to the woman who wears a dead man's crown, standing under a truce flag with my sword peace-knotted at my hip, about to commit treason or save the realm, depending entirely on how the next hour went.

I'd rehearsed this speech the whole way up the mountain. It had sounded very noble in rehearsal.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew before I climbed those stairs, because Serah does not walk into rooms unprepared. That is the whole of my craft: I am never the person who doesn't know.

I knew Rafael Ashgrove had sealed the Ashgrove gate seven years ago, the night of the Crowning, and that the gate had been the last working road into Crownforge. I knew the Ember was real because I had seen the Queen touch the scar over her heart on the anniversary of her coronation, once, thinking herself alone, and I had watched her face do something no usurper's face should do. I knew King Aldous sat his throne every day, ate every meal, signed every decree placed before him — and that in eleven years he had never once looked anyone in the eye.

I knew all of this the way you know a house is haunted. You can live in it. You can even love it. But some nights the floorboards speak, and you cannot unhear them.

What I did not know until four days ago, when the Queen sent me south with a sealed letter and a look I will be unpacking for the rest of my life, was why.

* * *

The throne room of Vael had been a forge once. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the great vented ceiling, the channels cut into the black stone floor where molten metal had run, now dry as old tears. We made camp in its shadow because there was no shelter better, and because the map wanted us here, and by then we had all stopped pretending the map didn't want things.

Rafael built a fire small enough to hide from God. Tamsin checked the perimeter twice and then a third time, which was her way of checking on him without checking on him. Oren sharpened a blade that did not need sharpening.

I walked out of the dark with my hands open and the truce token held high, and I said, "Don't. Any of you. I'm tired and I've walked sixty miles and if someone puts a knife in me before I've said my piece I will be genuinely annoyed."

Tamsin's knife was already out. Of course it was. "Serah."

"Tamsin." I let the token catch the firelight. "Truce. Old law, older than Veyra, older than Aldous. You know it. Your brother knew it — it's in his journal, page thirty, he complains about how much it costs to buy one."

Her jaw tightened. "You read his journal."

"I wrote half of it." I said it flatly, because there was no kind way to say it. "He sold information to the crown for two years before he ran. I was his handler. When he died on the Salt Road with a king's ransom sewn into his coat, I was the one who reported the loss."

The silence after that had weight. Rafael had gone very still, the way small animals go still. Oren, notably, said nothing at all, which told me everything about how much he'd already confessed.

"You're lying," Tamsin said, but her voice had gone thin.

"I'm many things, but I've never needed to lie to people who were going to find out anyway. Ask your friend Oren. He'll confirm the part where I'm a monster. He's been confirming it in letters for weeks."

Oren finally looked up. "You said—"

"I said a lot of things, Oren. You believed the ones you wanted. That's not my fault; that's just human nature wearing armor." I turned back to Tamsin. "Your brother didn't die carrying a treasure. He died running from one. And I'm here under truce to tell you why, and then you can kill me or believe me, and honestly either way I get to sit down."

* * *

Here is what the Queen gave me, in a room with no witnesses, four days before I rode south:

She gave me a letter, sealed twice, and she said, "Read it only when you are past Dunmarrow, and burn it after."

Then she took off her gloves.

Veyra's hands are famous — elegant, ringed, always gloved in public, the hands of a queen in every portrait. Without the gloves they are burned. Not scarred. Burned, old and deep, the way flesh looks when it has held something hotter than itself for a long time and then been forced to let go.

"The Ember was never a weapon," she said. "It was a piece of a soul. Mine, if you want the romantic version. His, if you want the true one."

I will give it to you as she gave it to me, because I have carried it four days and I cannot carry it any other way than whole.

Eleven years ago, Aldous came to the throne of a kingdom eating itself. Three claimants, two civil wars, a famine — you remember the histories, everyone remembers the histories, they're taught wrong. What isn't taught is that Aldous was not crowned at Crownforge. He was *made* there. The old rite, the one the founders used: the forging of a king. A ruler strong enough to hold a broken realm had to be more than a man, so the forge-mages cut away the part of him that could fail — doubt, grief, fear, the whole trembling human ledger — and bound it into living flame. The Ember. What sat on the throne afterward was a king with nothing left to lose, which sounds like strength until you understand that a man with nothing left to lose also has nothing left to love.

It worked. The wars ended. The realm healed. And the thing in the flame — the cut-away pieces, alive and screaming and *knowing* — waited in its cradle beneath Vael and grew clever.

Veyra was Aldous's general. More than that, if the old songs are right, though she neither confirmed nor denied it and I have learned not to ask queens questions I don't want answered. She went down into Crownforge eleven years ago with a company of fifty and came out alone, carrying the Ember in her bare hands, meaning to destroy it.

You cannot destroy a soul. You can only move it.

She couldn't put it back — the rite runs one direction, that's the whole horror of it, the forge-mages built a door with no handle on the inside. So she did the next thing. She bound it into herself. Into the burned places. She has been carrying the king's severed humanity against her own heart for eleven years, and it whispers, and it grieves, and it hates her, and some nights — her words, not mine — *some nights it is the only part of this kingdom that tells me the truth.*

"And Aldous?" I asked her.

"Aldous sits on his throne," she said, "and signs what I put in front of him, and does not object, because the parts of him that would object are in my chest. I did not steal his crown, Serah. I stole his pain, and the crown came with it, and I have spent eleven years telling myself those are different crimes."

She put her gloves back on. "The Ember is stirring because the binding weakens as I do. If these children destroy it, everything in it dies — and with it, whatever is left in Aldous that could ever wake. He'll live thirty more years, a warm body signing papers. If they leave it, it breaks free within the year, and it is *furious*, and it knows every road and every name in this kingdom. There is no version where the good guys win. There is only the version where someone pays, and I have been paying alone for eleven years, and I am so tired, Serah. Go and tell them the truth. Let them decide what truth is worth."

That was the mission. Not to kill them. To *tell* them.

I have served Veyra for nine years and I have never once doubted her, and I want you to understand what it cost her to send her enforcer out with orders that could end her, and what it cost me to realize that the monster I'd been serving was the only person in the palace telling the truth.

* * *

So I told them. All of it, there in the ruined forge-hall of Vael, with the fire burning low and the map glowing faintly in Rafael's pack like something listening.

I told them about the rite. About the Ember as the king's cut-away soul. About Veyra's hands. About the whispering. About the arithmetic: destroy it and Aldous never wakes; free it and it comes for the world; leave it and it eats through a dying woman's binding.

Rafael listened the way he seemed to listen to everything — completely, terribly, like each word was a debt he intended to pay. When I finished, he was quiet a long moment, and then he asked the question I'd been dreading.

"If the binding weakens as she weakens," he said, "then the answer isn't destroying it or freeing it. It's *rebinding*. Someone else takes the burden."

"Yes."

"Someone willing."

"Rafael—" Tamsin started.

"There's always been someone willing," he said, and he wasn't looking at any of us, he was looking at the dark mouth of the stairwell leading down into Crownforge, and I recognized the expression because I'd seen it on Veyra's face eleven years ago in a report I wasn't supposed to read. The expression of a person pricing their own life and finding the number acceptable.

"That's not why I came," I said, and heard how weak it sounded.

"No," Rafael said gently. "You came because she sent you. Because she's tired. Because she wants someone else to carry it and she's too honest to order it, so she sent the truth instead, dressed as treason." He smiled at me, and it was a kind smile, and kindness from that man frightened me more than any blade I'd faced. "Thank you, Serah. It was brave. Both of you."

Tamsin hadn't spoken since I named her brother. Now she stood, slow, and drew her knife — and set it on the stone between us, hilt toward me, which among Salt Road people is either surrender or a challenge, and I genuinely could not tell which.

"My brother ran from the crown," she said. "Everyone told me he was a traitor. Everyone told me he died rich." Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "He died running from the same thing you're describing. So either you're lying beautifully, or my whole life I've cursed a dead man who was the only honest person in the story."

"He was honest," I said. "Page thirty. The truce. He believed in rules that protected people. It's why he ran — the crown stopped having any."

She sat back down. It was not forgiveness. It was something smaller and harder: a decision to keep listening.

* * *

Oren found me at the edge of the camp, past midnight, while the others slept badly around the coals.

"You told them everything except the last part," he said.

"There's a last part?"

"Don't play with me, Serah. The letter. She sealed it twice. You burned it, like she ordered?" His eyes were steady and cold and I remembered, suddenly and unpleasantly, that this man had served Aldous before the crowning — before the cutting — and had loved the whole king, not the remainder.

"I burned it," I lied.

"Good," he said. "Because if Veyra's binding fails, and the Ember wakes, there is one more option she didn't write down. One she doesn't know about. The old rite had a second door, Serah. The forge-mages weren't fools — they built a handle on the inside. They just never told the kings, because a king who knows he can take his soul back is a king who might, and the rite only works if the sacrifice believes it's forever."

My mouth had gone dry. "Who told you this?"

"Someone who was in the room when they built it." He glanced back at the sleeping fire, at Rafael's shape curled against his pack, at the faint glow seeping between the seams. "The boy down there can open it. He's already half-chosen. But understand what it means, Serah. If the king takes himself back — all of himself, grief and fear and doubt — then Veyra's eleven years mean nothing. Her burns mean nothing. Everything she carried, everything she *is*, gets handed back to a man who won't remember giving it away. She becomes what they'll write in the histories. Just the usurper."

"And if he doesn't," I said, "she stays the usurper anyway, and dies carrying a stranger's soul."

"Yes," Oren said. "Now you see why I've been writing to everyone. There are no clean hands in this story. There's only the question of whose hands you're willing to dirty, and for whom."

He walked back toward the fire, and I stayed at the edge of the ruin with the wind coming down off the mountain, doing the arithmetic I had been avoiding for four days.

Forty-one kills, and every one of them certain. Certain of the Queen, certain of the cause, certain that certainty was the same as righteousness. And now I stood above a buried forge holding a truth with no good side to stand on: a queen who was a prison, a king who was a wound, a boy downstairs pricing his life at less than a stranger's grief, and a swordsman selling a secret that might be salvation or might be the oldest trap in the kingdom.

I had come under truce to collapse their easy answers. Nobody warned me I'd be collapsing my own.

Below me, deep under the ruin, something flickered — a pulse of light through the cracks in the black stone, brief as a heartbeat, and I felt it in the burned-place memory of Veyra's hands, a pull like a hook behind my ribs.

The Ember knew we were here.

And it was done waiting.
