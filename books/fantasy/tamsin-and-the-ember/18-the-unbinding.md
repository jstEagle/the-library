## 18. The Unbinding

There is a moment — I want to be honest about this, because honesty is about to become the only currency I have left — when you understand that you are going to die, and the understanding arrives not as terror but as a kind of administrative clarity. A ledger closing. *Ah,* you think. *So that's what all of this was for.*

I was having that moment in the heart of Crownforge, with a dead king's soul burning in a cradle of black iron, a traitor's sword falling toward my throat, and Tamsin screaming my name from somewhere behind me. The forge around us groaned like an old ship in a storm, shedding centuries of stone.

I would like to report that my final thought was noble. It was not. My final thought before everything changed was: *Tamsin is going to be so angry with me.*

Which, if you knew her, you'd agree was a reasonable thing to think.

* * *

Let me back up. Back up to the heat, because the heat matters.

Crownforge had been built to make something that should never have been made, and the room where they'd made it still remembered. The walls were veined with channels where molten metal had once run — channels now dry, but warm, always warm, seven hundred years of warmth leaking out of them like blood through a bandage. The Ember hung at the center of it all in its cradle: a flame no bigger than a cupped hand, gold-white, utterly silent.

That was the thing nobody tells you about the Ember. You expect it to roar. It doesn't. It burned the way a held breath burns — quietly, patiently, with the terrible composure of something that has been waiting a very long time for someone foolish enough to come and find it.

And around it, faint as breath on cold glass, stood the echo of Aldous.

I say *stood*, though he wasn't standing exactly. He was more like the memory of standing — a man-shaped absence in the air, translucent, his crown melted down his face like wax, his mouth moving without sound. He had been trying to speak since we entered. Seven years of trying, maybe. Maybe longer.

Oren lay against the far wall where Tamsin had put him, her knife through his sword arm and her boot on his chest, blood pooling under him in a way that suggested he wouldn't be getting up to betray anyone else today. His face was strange. Not defeated. *Relieved*, almost, which frightened me more than the sword had.

"You don't know what you're doing," he said, and then coughed, and laughed, and the laugh was worse than the cough. "None of us ever did."

"Rafael." Tamsin's voice came tight across the chamber. She hadn't taken her eyes off the Ember. Or off me. "Rafael, look at me. Whatever you're thinking —"

"I'm thinking," I said, "that the map didn't bring us here to destroy it."

The vision in the ruined shrine had shown me the truth, or enough of it: the Ember was not a weapon. It was not even a thing, not really. It was a *piece*. A fragment of a soul, torn out at a crowning, because some clever smiths seven centuries ago had decided that a king who could not grieve, could not doubt, could not *hesitate*, would be a better king. They had cut the mercy out of Aldous the First and bottled it, and every king since had been crowned over that bottle, bound to it, fed by it — and hollowed by it.

Including the current one. Including, God help us all, the woman who wore his crown while he sat imprisoned behind his own eyes.

Destroying the Ember would kill him. Keeping it kept Veyra's binding intact. There was only one door left, and I had known it since the shrine, and I had been walking toward it with the cheerful determination of a man who has decided not to read the contract before signing.

"Look at me," Tamsin said again, and this time I did.

She was bleeding — a long slice along her forearm from Oren's first swing, already darkening at the edges. Her hair had come loose and stuck to her face with sweat and forge-heat. She looked, in that moment, exactly like what she was: a smuggler who had lost a brother to this same road, who had lied to me about knowing it, who had carried the truth in her pack across half the world because she couldn't bear to set it down and couldn't bear to open it.

She was the best liar I have ever known, and she was looking at me with nothing on her face at all, which meant everything was on her face.

"Don't," she said.

"Tamsin —"

"There'll be another way. There's always another way. We'll take it to the council, we'll —"

"The council can't hold a soul fragment, Tam. The free cities can't even hold a border." I smiled, because if I didn't smile I was going to do something embarrassing like cry, and I had a reputation to maintain as the least dramatic charm-seller in Correnvale. "Besides. Think of it as a sale. Final item. No refunds."

"Rafael —"

"It's fine," I said. "I'm very good at letting go of things. Ask anyone. Ask the gate. Ask seven years of my life."

The ghost-echo of Aldous had drifted closer while we spoke. Close enough now that I could see his eyes, and his eyes were the worst part, because they weren't desperate. They were *sorry*. A king, apologizing to a gatekeeper. Somewhere in the last few weeks I had apparently been promoted.

He raised a hand — or the idea of a hand — and pointed at the cradle.

*I'm sorry,* his mouth shaped. *It must be freely given. That is the only lock on it.*

Freely given. Of course. The smiths had built in one safeguard, the only one that mattered: the Ember could not be seized, only accepted. Which is why Veyra needed hunters and maps and murdered strangers carrying bloodstained secrets south. Which is why Serah hadn't simply taken it. Which is why, I suppose, it had ended up in the hands of a man whose entire adult life had been an exercise in giving things away — his magic, his name, his history, his gate.

I stepped toward the cradle.

Behind me, Tamsin made a sound I will describe as small only because I lack a word for the size of it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about touching a piece of someone's soul: it is warm. That's the first thing. After all of it — the fire, the collapse, the seven stolen years — the first thing was warmth, like a hand on the back of your neck, like sun through a window you'd forgotten existed.

The second thing was the falling.

Not me falling. *Him.* Aldous, seven years ago, standing in this same chamber in his coronation robes, watching the smiths lift the Ember out of him and feeling — this is the part I cannot make smaller, so I won't try — feeling *nothing*, because the nothing was being lifted out of him even as he felt it. Grief leaving a body looks like light. Did you know that? It looks like light pouring upward out of the chest, and the face above it goes smooth and calm and wrong, wrong, wrong, like a house with all the portraits taken off the walls.

I fell into his seven years the way you fall into deep water.

I was crowned in a hall full of people I loved and could no longer quite see the point of. I signed orders that burned villages and felt the paper weigh nothing, because the weight had been poured into a bottle under a mountain. I watched Veyra — Veyra, who had been my friend, who had held my daughter when she was born, who had argued politics with me until dawn a hundred nights — watch *her* begin to notice the hollowness and be afraid of it, and I could not reassure her, because reassurance requires a heart and mine was in a cradle beneath Vael.

I felt her grief. That's the thing I need you to understand, whoever finds these words, whatever's left of me to find them. Veyra did not usurp the throne out of ambition. She took it because the thing wearing the king's body was going to burn the world, and someone had to hold the reins, and holding the reins meant wearing the crown, and wearing the crown meant binding herself to the same hollow machinery, and she did it anyway, weeping, alone, in a room full of courtiers who thought her ambition was triumph rather than sacrifice gone septic.

Power justified as mercy. Mercy curdled into power. The circle closes; the wheel turns; the forge stays warm.

Seven years. I lived them in the time it takes to draw one breath, and by the end of that breath I understood two things absolutely:

First: the king could be freed. The binding ran through the Ember like a rope through a ring — pull the ring out, and the rope goes slack. Every soldier bound by the crown, every oath enforced by it, every grey cloak marching on the free cities — slack. All of it.

Second: the ring has to go somewhere. Souls do not tolerate vacancy. The Ember would not return to Aldous — seven years of emptiness had scarred the channel shut. It needed a living vessel, willing, whole enough to hold it.

Whole enough. I laughed inside the vision, inside the falling, inside the warmth. A gatekeeper. A man who had spent seven years making himself small, selling charms that did nothing, hiding a gift he'd sealed behind a door in the Ashgrove hills. Small enough to hide. And therefore, perhaps, empty enough to fill.

*Rafael.*

Tamsin's voice, arriving from very far away, from the surface of the water, from the world.

*Rafael, your hands are burning —*

They were. Good. That meant it was working.

* * *

I opened my eyes inside the collapsing forge.

Oren was on his feet somehow — I will never understand that man, and now I never will — dragging himself upright with the knife still in his arm, shouting something. Not at me. At the ceiling, at the walls, at the ancient smiths in their graves: *"You don't have to! There was another way, there was always supposed to be another way!"* And maybe there was. Maybe in some other story, some kinder telling, a swordsman who served kings and a smuggler who kept her brother's journal and a charm-seller who talked too much found the third option. In this one, the ceiling was coming down in slabs the size of houses, and the Ember was rising out of its cradle toward my open hands like a child toward a parent, and there was no time left for other ways.

"I've got it," I said, and I don't know if anyone heard me over the roar. "I've got him. Tell the council — tell everyone — the king didn't abandon you. He was *cut out*. Cut out and bottled, and the woman you're calling a usurper has been holding the leash of a corpse for seven years, and —"

The first slab hit the floor of the forge and the sound was the sound the world makes when it decides to stop pretending otherwise.

I caught the Ember.

Warmth. Light. Falling. Aldous's seven years pouring through me and out through me and *staying* in me, settling into the spaces I had spent a lifetime hollowing out, filling me the way flood fills a dry riverbed — violently, gratefully, without asking permission, because I had given it. Freely. The only lock, and I had opened it.

And underneath the king's stolen years, deeper, older, I felt the original grief — the smiths' grief, the first grief, the sorrow of people who loved their kingdom so much they amputated its conscience and called the wound a crown. It moved into me too. All of it. Every grief the Ember had ever siphoned out of every crowned head for seven hundred years, waiting in the dark for a vessel.

My hands were burning. My arms were burning. Somewhere beyond the light, Tamsin was pulling at me, screaming words I couldn't separate from the roar of the forge coming down, and I wanted to tell her — I tried to tell her — that it was all right, that the price was fair, that I had read the contract after all, every clause, and signed anyway.

But the price was collecting itself as I named it. I could feel it going. Not flesh — the Ember wasn't killing me, it was *spending* me. Magic first: the sealed gift behind the Ashgrove door, the little warmth I'd hoarded for seven years, gone like breath off a mirror. Then memory, peeling away in strips —

— the salt road, Tamsin laughing on the salt road, gone —

— Dunmarrow, the refugees, the coin she almost took, gone —

— the pass, the child, the bell rung hollow, gone —

— Bray, the debt, the year I paid for her life, gone —

No. Wait. Not those. Please, not those, I had just gotten used to —

— her name. Her name going. *Tam*. What a strange small word. Why did it feel like a door I'd walked through a thousand times? Why did the shape of it ache like an old scar?

The forge collapsed around us and I held the light and I held the king and I held, for as long as I could, the knowledge of her face — brown eyes, knife-scarred brow, the particular way she lied with her whole body except her hands — and then the forge finished falling, and the light took everything, and the last coherent thought I had in the person I had been was simple, and stupid, and true:

*Tamsin is going to be so angry with me.*

* * *

Then: quiet.

Dust, settling. A shaft of grey daylight falling through a hole where a roof had been, illuminating nothing but ash and the wreckage of a seven-hundred-year-old lie.

A man sat in the ash at the center of it, alive, unhurt, his hands folded in his lap like a child at lessons. Gold-white light moved beneath the skin of his forearms, slow as tide. He looked at his own hands with polite curiosity, the way you look at a stranger's luggage.

Somewhere nearby, a woman was digging through rubble with her bare hands, calling a name over and over — a name that meant nothing to him, though the sound of it made his chest hurt in a way he had no history to explain.

He watched her for a while. Then, because it seemed the polite thing to do, and because he had always been, as far as he knew, a polite man, he cleared his throat and said:

"Hello. Can I help you?"

She turned. Her face was wrecked with dust and grief and something else — hope, arriving late and furious, like a smuggler crossing a border at dawn.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she wiped her face with her wrist, straightened her spine, and began, deliberately, the way you begin anything you intend to see through to the end:

"My name is Tamsin."
