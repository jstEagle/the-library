## 19. Ash and Memory

I have carried a lot of things out of bad places. Stolen ledgers, a live goose, once a man's entire dentistry in a leather pouch, which is a story I will never tell for free. But I have never carried anyone the way I carried Rafael Ashgrove down out of Vael — half over my shoulder, half dragged through the ash-fall, his weight wrong in a way I couldn't name until much later, when I understood that what had made him heavy was everything he'd left behind.

The mountain was coming apart behind us. Crownforge had been dying for seven years, and Rafael finished the job.

The last thing he said to me — the last thing *he* said, the man who remembered me — was my name. Just that. "Tamsin." Like he was setting it down somewhere safe before the dark took it. Then his eyes went white as forge-light and he said words in no language I knew, and the Ember came into him like a river accepting rain, and when it was done he looked at me with a stranger's polite confusion and asked if I was the one who'd found him.

I said yes. It was even true.

* * *

We made camp that first night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, because Serah insisted we put distance between ourselves and the citadel before the mountain finished its argument with gravity. She'd stopped calling herself Veyra's enforcer somewhere around the moment she'd watched her mistress's great secret burn itself into a charm-seller's skin. Now she just called herself Serah, which I gathered was a novelty for her.

Oren hadn't come down at all.

I want to write that plainly so I don't have to dress it up later: Oren stayed at the gate. The old gate at the heart of Crownforge, the one that groaned and split when the Ember unbound, the one that would have come down on all of us if someone hadn't stood under it and held it — not with rope, not with timber, but with his back and his arms and whatever oath had been living in him since before Aldous ever wore the crown.

Serah told me about it afterward, in the flat voice of a soldier delivering a report, because that was the only voice she had left. How he'd shouted at us to run. How the stone had taken him the way the sea takes a swimmer who's decided to stop swimming. How his last word wasn't a king's name or a god's, but something small — she thought maybe a place. A village, perhaps. Somewhere he'd been young.

"He served the king," she said. "And in the end he served the only thing left of him."

"And himself," I said, because Oren had wanted the Ember, had turned his blade for it, and I wasn't going to sand that off to make a nicer grave.

"Yes," Serah said. "Both things can be true of a man. That's usually how you know he's real."

* * *

Rafael slept for two days.

When he woke, he woke gently, the way you do in your own bed, and looked around our miserable camp — the wet firewood, the tin cup, my brother's journal sitting on my pack where I'd stopped being able to bear touching it — and smiled at me the way you smile at a stranger who has been kind.

"You're Tamsin," he said. "You told me. On the mountain."

"I did."

"Tamsin," he repeated, tasting it. "It suits you. You look like someone who'd be called that."

Gods help me, I laughed. It came out of me sideways, ugly and surprised, halfway to a sob, and I turned away and busied myself with the fire because there are some sounds you don't let a man hear you make when he doesn't know why you're making them.

He didn't know me. He didn't know the road, or Dunmarrow, or the child in the pass whose life he'd bought with seven years of silence broken. He didn't know Bray, or the year of memory he'd already paid there, or the map, or the stranger at his door, or the gate at Ashgrove he'd sealed shut when he was a younger and more frightened man. He knew Correnvale. He knew charms. He knew how to be small. Everything since the night a bleeding man pressed a map into his hands had been paid out of him like coin from a purse, and the purse was empty, and he sat there in the ashes of a war he couldn't remember starting, asking me politely to tell him again where we were going.

"North," I said. "Dunmarrow. There's a council."

"A council of what?"

"People who might listen."

He nodded, satisfied, and I thought: this is what I deserve. This is the shape of my lie come home. I spent the whole journey keeping truths from him — my brother, the journal, the fact that I'd known where the map led before he ever touched it — and now the ledger had balanced itself by taking every truth from him instead. The gods, whoever they are, have a bookkeeper's sense of humor and no mercy whatsoever in their hearts.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it wants a body. It wants someone to bring soup to. And so I brought Rafael soup, and bandaged the burns that laced his arms in patterns like script — the Ember's signature, written on him permanent as a brand — and answered his questions with the edited version of events, the flattering one, the one where I was a guide he hired and nothing more.

He caught me out on the third day.

We were walking the river road north, and he stopped at a bend where the water ran slow over flat stones, and he stood there a long time, frowning at nothing.

"I keep almost-remembering," he said. "Not things. Feelings. Like rooms in a house I've been told I lived in." He looked at me. "There's a feeling that goes with you. When you walk ahead of me on the road, there's a — an ease. Like something settling. Was I afraid of you, at first?"

"Everyone's afraid of me at first."

"That's not it." He shook his head slowly. "It's more like... I trusted you before I had reasons to. Is that something I did? Or something you did?"

I could have lied. Lying was the family trade; my brother had been better at it than me, and look where it got him, face-down in a ditch with a map in his coat. But I'd buried one man already on this road because of secrets, and I'd watched another die holding up a gate, and somewhere between those two graves I'd made a decision I hadn't told myself about yet.

"You trusted me against your own good judgment," I said. "And I hadn't earned it. Not then. By the end—" My voice did something traitorous. "By the end I'd have walked into that forge alone rather than let anything happen to you. So. There's your answer, or most of it."

He considered this with the patience of a man relearning the world.

"Then I'll trust you again," he said simply. "On credit. Until I remember why."

I had to walk ahead after that, for a while, where he couldn't see my face.

* * *

Serah left us at the crossroads above Millbrook.

She had the proof — pages pulled from Veyra's own archive, writs signed in the king's hand that the king had never written, the binding-ceremony records that showed exactly what had been torn out of Aldous at his crowning and what had grown in the hollow where it used to be. Enough to hang a usurper in any court in the free cities, if any court dared hold one.

"The council sits in Dunmarrow within the month," she said. "Word travels faster than armies, if the word is true enough. I'll carry it."

"They'll want to know where you got it," I said. "They'll want to know what you were."

"I know what I was." She said it without flinching, which is more than most people can manage. Then she looked down at Rafael, who was asleep against his pack in the noon light, burned hands folded on his chest like a carved effigy. "Does he remember any of it?"

"No."

"Good," she said, and when I stared at her: "What he did in there — what it cost him — men who remember their sacrifices wear them like armor, and armored men are easy to aim. Let him be light a while longer. The fighting ahead will need him unbent."

"You're defending him. You hunted us across half the map."

"I did." She swung onto her horse. "That's rather the point of proof, isn't it? That people can change. If I'm the only exhibit the council gets, they'll never believe it."

She rode south toward Dunmarrow with a dead king's secrets in her saddlebag and a traitor's brand she'd chosen for herself, and I watched her go and thought, not for the first time, that the world was going to be remade by the strangest collection of people ever assembled, none of whom had planned on being heroes and several of whom had actively worked against it.

* * *

At night, Rafael talked in his sleep.

Not words. Light. Faint gold, seeping from between his lips, from the corners of his eyes, pooling in the hollow of his throat and sinking back in. The Ember dreaming in him. Some nights it was warm as a banked fire and I slept near it without shame, because the road was cold and I was tired of being cold in every sense. Some nights it flared, and he cried out names he shouldn't have known — Aldous, mostly, and once, terribly, *Veyra*, in a voice full of such old grief that I sat up all the rest of the night with my knife in my hand and my heart going like a drum.

Because here's what I saw in that forge, in the last moments before the light took him: the king's ghost-echo begging. And here's what Rafael had told me after, in the minutes he still had himself, breathless and burning and certain: *the Ember isn't a weapon, Tamsin. It's a piece of a soul. Someone tore it out and crowned themselves with the absence.*

Veyra. The woman who'd bound a king and called it salvation. And now her whole stolen architecture lived inside a charm-seller from Correnvale who couldn't remember my name unless I reminded him.

Some nights I lay awake doing arithmetic I hated: what if the Ember changes him? What if the man who wakes up at the end of this isn't Rafael at all, but Aldous wearing his body like a borrowed coat? What if I've delivered one king into the world only to lose the one ordinary stubborn brave fool I—

Well. There it is. I might as well write it, since I've apparently become a woman who tells the truth.

I loved him. Somewhere between the pass and the ford and the debt in Bray, between his terrible jokes and his infuriating honesty and the year of memory he spent without blinking to save my worthless life, I had gone and loved him, quietly, without permission, the way you fall into a river — gradually and then all at once and then drowning.

And he looked at me every morning with a stranger's courteous eyes, and called me *the one who found him*, and I smiled and said *that's right*, and carried it.

* * *

We came down out of the hills on the ninth day and saw Dunmarrow in the valley, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, banners on the walls — not Veyra's grey, but the patched heraldry of the free cities, mustering. War was coming. Everyone could smell it, the way animals smell weather.

Rafael stood beside me on the ridge, and the wind moved through his hair, and under his collar I could see the faint pulse of gold at his throat, steady as a second heartbeat.

"I don't know what I am anymore," he said quietly. "But I know the city's going to need whatever it is. And I know you'll get me to the right people, because you got me this far." He glanced sideways at me, and there — just for a heartbeat — something flickered behind his eyes that wasn't a stranger at all. Something that knew me. "Tamsin. When this is over. Will you tell me everything? All of it? Even the parts where I was an idiot?"

"You were rarely an idiot," I said. "Mostly a coward. It's different."

He laughed — my laugh, the laugh I knew, there and gone like a fish surfacing — and then it was gone and he was a polite stranger again, and we started down toward the gates together.

Inside the walls, a crier was shouting the news: the council would convene at dawn. Witnesses were summoned. Evidence would be heard.

Serah was already there, waiting in the shadow of the gatehouse, and when she saw us she straightened like a soldier called to attention and said, "They'll want to hear it from both of you. And Tamsin — they'll ask what you knew, and when. All of it. Every lie."

I looked at Rafael, standing in the morning light with a king's soul under his skin and no memory of how it got there, trusting me on credit.

"All of it," I agreed. "Every lie."

Dawn was coming. So was the truth. Gods help us both, I meant to say mine out loud.
