## 20. The Council of Free Cities

They gave me a room with a bed in it, which should have been my first warning.

In eleven years of smuggling I had slept in haylofts, river barges, a wine cellar that turned out to belong to a tax collector, and once — memorably — inside a hollowed-out statue of Saint Vessia, patron of travelers, who I can only assume was laughing. Nobody gives you a soft bed for free. A bed is a down payment on something.

The chamberlain who showed me to it was very kind about it, too. "Rest well," he said, with the particular gentleness people use on the condemned and the mothers of newborns. "The council convenes at the ninth bell."

I lay on the mattress for maybe an hour, staring at the ceiling beams, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Bray. Not coin arithmetic. The other kind. The kind where you count up everything you've lied about and try to guess what the total costs.

Here is what I had, going into that chamber: Serah's testimony, sworn and sealed, about the binding on King Aldous. The journal pages we'd pulled from the ford. A scrap of crown-forged iron Oren had died holding, still humming faintly, like a struck bell nobody could hear properly. And me. Which was either the least valuable item on the list or the most, depending entirely on whether I opened my mouth all the way or just partway.

Partway had been the plan. Partway is always the plan. Tell them about Veyra, tell them about the Ember, tell them about Rafael's sacrifice — leave out the parts where I'd known where Crownforge was before Rafael ever touched the map, where I'd nearly sold them out in Dunmarrow for two hundred crowns and a clean slate, where my brother had died carrying the same map because I'd sent him, because I'd been too much of a coward to go myself and too proud to say so.

Then I thought about Rafael, sitting somewhere in this city right now with seven months of his life gone, looking at me like I was a stranger. Because to him, I was. He'd paid a year of memory for my debt in Bray without hesitating — hadn't even asked what the debt *was* — and then he'd paid the rest of himself for a king he'd never met, and the version of him that woke up in Ashgrove didn't remember a single reason to trust me.

The old Rafael had trusted me completely. That was the worst part. Not that he'd forgotten me. That the man who remembered me had trusted me more than I'd ever deserved, and now there was no one left alive who knew how much I didn't deserve it except me.

You can't build anything on a debt you're hiding. My mother taught me that, in her way, which was mostly by example and mostly bad. But she'd said it once, drunk, on the Salt Road: "Tamsin, girl, a lie kept is just a debt with worse interest." She'd been talking about money. It travels.

* * *

The council chamber in Dunmarrow had been a granary before the Free Cities declared themselves free, and it hadn't entirely lost the habit. Grain chutes still ran along the walls, capped with bronze. The nine seats were arranged in a horseshoe on a dais of pale stone, and the delegates sat behind them looking exactly like what they were: merchants, guildmasters, one retired admiral, and a priestess of the Twin Rivers who kept glancing at the exits.

Nine cities. Nine votes needed to do anything, which meant nothing had been done in Dunmarrow's council hall for six years except argue about tariffs.

Serah stood to one side in plain grey — her hunters' grey, but she'd surrendered her blades at the door, and watching her do it had been its own small spectacle. She'd insisted on testifying first. "Let them hear it from the enemy," she'd told me on the walk over. "It lands harder." Then, quieter: "And let them hear it from you after. You're the one who has to make it land."

I still didn't trust her. I want that on the record, wherever records go when stories end. She'd hunted us across half the map. She'd put a knife against Rafael's throat at the ford. But she'd also knelt in the ash of Crownforge and wept — actually wept, ugly and unguarded — when she understood what her queen had done, and there are only so many performances a person can sustain while crying into their own hands.

"Delegate Pemberton." The speaker was Master Corvel of Dunmarrow, a guildmaster with a voice like wet gravel and eyes that had counted a thousand ledgers. "You've asked for the floor under unusual circumstances. The council has heard testimony regarding the usurper Veyra, the binding upon King Aldous, and the events at Crownforge. What remains?"

"What remains," I said, "is the parts everyone else would find embarrassing."

A rustle went through the horseshoe. Good. Embarrassment gets attention faster than heroism; every merchant in that room knew it in their bones.

"I'm not here as the woman who helped save the realm," I said. "I'm here as a smuggler, and I'd like to start with my credentials."

* * *

So I told them.

Not the ballad version. The real one, start to finish, and I watched their faces change as I went.

I told them about the Salt Road runs, how I'd moved charm-goods and fugitives out of Correnvale for years, how the Wardens' price lists worked — a smuggler caught with cargo walks free if she names three names, and everyone in the trade knows it, and everyone does it eventually, and that's why the trade runs on distrust the way mills run on water.

I told them about my brother Wren. His name came out of my mouth and it cost more than I expected, the way certain words do when you haven't spent them in a long time. Wren, who was nineteen and quick and believed in causes the way other people believe in weather — constantly, cheerfully, without any plan for what to do when it rained. How a courier had approached our crew with a job: carry a sealed packet south, no questions, triple pay. How I'd looked at the seal and recognized the work of a Crownforge smith because our father had been one, before the citadel fell, before magic became contraband instead of craft.

How I'd wanted to know what was in the packet. How Wren had volunteered to go instead, partly because he was brave and partly because I'd made the wanting obvious enough that he'd wanted to give it to me.

"And I let him," I said. "That's the part I need this council to hear. I let my nineteen-year-old brother carry a thing I suspected was dangerous because I was afraid of it and flattered that he'd go in my place. Veyra's hunters caught him at the border of the Ashwood. He burned the packet rather than give it up. They killed him for the ashes."

The priestess of the Twin Rivers had stopped looking at the exits.

"The map that started all of this — the one Rafael Ashgrove carried to Crownforge — was Wren's map. Or a copy of it. The original died with him. I recognized it the night I intercepted Rafael on the road, and I did not tell him. For four hundred miles I traveled beside a man who trusted me, knowing I'd seen that path before, knowing my brother had bled out twenty feet from it, and I said nothing, because saying something meant admitting I'd sent Wren to die."

Corvel's gravel voice cut in. "Why confess this now? The council has what it needs. The binding, the proof, the enforcer's testimony—"

"Because you're about to decide whether to fight," I said, "and I know exactly how you'll make the decision, because I've watched nine city councils the way I've watched nine city guard rotations. Someone will stand up and offer the flattering version. The brave smuggler, the noble gatekeeper, the loyal swordsman, evil defeated through courage and friendship. And you'll vote for resistance because the story makes you feel like heroes, and feeling like heroes lasts until the first grey cloak burns your granaries, and then you'll look for someone to blame, and you'll blame whoever sold you the flattering version."

I held up the scrap of crown-iron. It hummed against my palm, faint and stubborn, the way it had since Crownforge.

"This is what loyalty bought us. Oren Vale served Aldous faithfully for thirty years. When the binding took the king, Oren couldn't accept it, so he decided the Ember could restore the old dynasty — through himself. He turned his blade on us in the forge heart. He died holding the gate open so we could live, and I will swear on anything you like that some part of him believed, to his last breath, that he was being loyal. Loyalty is what Veyra's whole army runs on. Every soldier bound by that crown loves their queen sincerely. Sincerity isn't worth anything. Truth is worth something, and truth is expensive, and I'm paying for mine right now in front of nine cities."

Silence. The good kind, the kind where you can hear people deciding things.

* * *

It was the admiral who broke it, naturally. Military people always break silence first; waiting feels like losing to them.

"You nearly sold your companions out in this city," Admiral Kess said. "For two hundred crowns."

"For two hundred crowns and forgiveness of a debt that would have gotten me killed," I said. "Yes. I got as far as naming the meeting place. I sat in the back room of the Drowned Bell with a broker named Hesk and I gave him the street corner where Rafael would be standing at moonrise. Then I walked out and warned them instead, and Hesk's people have wanted my throat since. Ask Serah. Her hunters used my debts to set the trap at Bray."

Kess looked at Serah. Serah nodded once, precise as a signature.

"Why?" Kess asked me. "What changed?"

"Nothing changed," I said. "That's the point. I walked out of that room exactly as guilty as I walked in. I chose differently anyway. People think character is what you are. Character is what you do while you're still whatever you were five minutes ago. If this council waits until it feels pure enough to resist Veyra, it will wait forever, because none of us are pure, and she is counting on that. Her whole rule is built on the idea that everyone has a price and everyone has a shame and both can be managed. I'm telling you my price and my shame so you can check her math."

The priestess spoke then, softly. "And the gatekeeper? The one who took the Ember into himself. Where is he?"

"He's in Ashgrove," I said. "He doesn't remember me. He doesn't remember any of it. He remembers his shop and his charms and seven years of hiding, and he's carrying a fire in his chest that he has no explanation for, and he went back to the gate he sealed because it felt like home and he couldn't say why." My voice did something on the last part that I didn't authorize. I let it. "He paid everything, and the payment was that he'll never know what he paid for. So when you vote today, don't vote for him. He's past needing it. Vote for the version of yourselves that won't have to lie to your grandchildren about what you did when the grey cloaks came."

* * *

They argued for four hours. Of course they did. Nine cities, nine interests, and a war proposal on the table. I sat through tariff objections, a theological dispute about whether bound soldiers counted as murdered or merely imprisoned, and one truly remarkable fifteen-minute digression from the delegate of Marrowford about grain futures during wartime, which I endured with the professional patience of a woman who had once spent three days in a wine cellar listening to a tax collector describe his stamp collection.

But something had shifted in the room, and I could feel it, and I knew what it was, because I'd felt the same shift in myself walking out of the Drowned Bell. They'd come in expecting a hero and gotten a witness instead, and witnesses are harder to dismiss. Heroes inspire you for an evening. Witnesses follow you home.

When the vote finally came, it wasn't unanimous. Six cities for resistance, two abstaining, one — little salt-poor Quillon's Reach, terrified, sensible Quillon's Reach — against. Six of nine. In that chamber, it might as well have been thunder.

Master Corvel read the resolution aloud: the Free Cities would refuse Veyra's grain levies, open their armories, and send word to every garrison town that the binding on the king's army could be broken — that the soldiers wearing the crown's obedience were victims, not enemies, and that surrender would be met with shelter, not ropes. Undermining an army by telling its soldiers the truth. It was, when you thought about it, the most smuggling thing the council had ever done, and I said so, and for one moment the granary-chamber laughed, all nine cities at once, brief and startled, like sun through cloud.

Afterward, Serah found me on the steps outside. The evening was coming down gold and cold, and Dunmarrow's bells were ringing the hour, and for the first time in weeks they rang full-bodied, unhollowed, as if the metal itself had been waiting for permission.

"They'll march within the month," she said. "Veyra, I mean. She won't negotiate now. The council's defiance will read to her army as rebellion, and the binding will hold long enough to bring them here." She paused. "Unless the binding breaks first."

"Ashgrove," I said.

"Ashgrove," she agreed. "Whatever he's become, whatever the Ember is making of him — if he seals that gate with the fire still in him, it may shatter her hold on every soldier under the crown at once. Or it may kill him. Or both. We won't know from here."

"No," I said. "We won't."

She looked at me for a moment with those grey hunter's eyes, and then she did something I hadn't expected: she bowed. Small, but real. "Your brother," she said. "Wren. He burned the map rather than let it be taken. I was at the Ashwood border that day. I've carried the memory of it for two years and told myself it was justice." Her jaw tightened. "It wasn't. He was braver than anyone I've served under. I wanted you to know that someone from the other side saw it."

She walked away before I could answer, which was a mercy, because I don't think I could have answered.

I stood on the council steps a long time, watching the light go out of the market square, doing one last piece of arithmetic. Wren, avenged not by a sword but by a vote. Rafael, owed a debt he'd never collect because he didn't remember lending. A war coming north with the winter, and one man at a gate in the mountains holding a fire he didn't understand.

There was a horse saddled in the yard below. Mine. Paid for, I learned later, by anonymous resolution of the council, though I suspected the priestess.

Six days' hard ride to Ashgrove. Maybe less, if I rode the way I intended to, which was like a woman chasing something she'd already lost once and refused to lose twice.

I swung up into the saddle and pointed her south, toward the mountains, toward the gate that should stay shut and the man who didn't know my name — and who was, at that very moment, I'd learn too late, watching Veyra's armies gather on the far side of it like stormwater behind a dam.
