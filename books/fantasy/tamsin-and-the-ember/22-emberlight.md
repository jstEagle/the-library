## 22. Emberlight

### Rafael

I woke at the Ashgrove gate with ash on my lips and a name in my mouth that wasn't mine.

That's not poetry. I mean it literally. There was a name sitting behind my teeth like a stone I'd swallowed, warm and heavy, and when I opened my mouth to test it — *Aldous* — the gate stones answered. A low hum ran through the wall, through the ground, up through my boots into my knees, and every hair on my arms stood up like soldiers called to attention.

I didn't know why I was there.

Let me be clear about that, because everyone since has wanted to make it romantic. *He returned to the gate of his childhood, drawn by destiny.* No. I woke in the dirt with no memory of how I'd come, no memory of anything past a certain morning seven years ago when I'd sealed a gate and walked away whistling. Everything between was a blank page. The journey, the road, the people — gone. I had charms in my pockets I didn't remember making. I had a burn scar across my palm shaped like a crown. And I had this name, this warmth, this terrible sense of *home* in a place I had spent seven years making sure I never looked at twice.

The gate itself was shut. Of course it was. I'd shut it. But it glowed now, faintly, along its seams — ember-colored light leaking out like heat from an oven door — and when I pressed my scarred palm against the stone, it sighed.

"You came back," said a voice inside my own skull, and it was not my voice, though it was tired the way I was tired.

"I don't know who you are," I said, "but I have a feeling you're going to tell me whether I want you to or not."

*Seven years,* the voice said, and it sounded almost amused. *Seven years I have been a passenger in a man who sells luck-charms and flinches from his own shadow. You have no idea how dull it's been.*

"Try having no idea at all," I said. "Apparently I did something important. Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a funeral and a festival at once."

*You unbound me,* the king said — because by then I'd worked out it was the king, Aldous, or what remained of him, the remnant that had ridden in my chest since Crownforge since the Ember passed into me since — well. Since whatever I couldn't remember. *And now Veyra comes, and her armies come with her, and you are going to have to do the hardest thing you have ever done.*

"Which is?"

*Stand still,* he said, *and let them see what you are.*

I want it noted for the record that standing still is the thing I am worst at. Seven years of practice at being small, at being nothing, at being a charm-seller whose wares were harmless because harmlessness was survival — and here was a dead king in my ribs telling me the whole war would turn on my willingness to be seen.

"They're coming today?" I asked.

*They are already marching. She is already bound to them, all ten thousand, threads of the crown running from her will into their spines. When she learned the Ember was loose, she gathered everything. She cannot risk a soldier waking up.*

"Waking up to what?"

There was a pause. Kings are not used to being asked questions; I could feel him deciding whether to be offended.

*To themselves,* he said finally. *She did not conquer Correnvale, boy. She purchased it. Every oath sworn on the crown is a thread, and every thread is a leash, and every leash can be cut. That is what the Ember was for. That is what you are for, now.*

I sat down in the dirt, because it seemed wise, and looked at the gate glowing like a banked fire, and thought about the blank pages in my head. Somewhere in those missing years there had been people. I could feel their shapes the way you feel the shape of furniture in a dark room — a smuggler with a laugh like a knife being sharpened, a swordsman whose name tasted of regret, a woman in grey who had chosen a side too late. Names wouldn't come. Just the shapes, and one shape more than all the others, one presence in the dark room that made the whole space feel warmer and more dangerous.

"There's someone," I said. "Someone I knew. Someone who—" I stopped, embarrassed, talking to a ghost about feelings I couldn't source. "It doesn't matter."

*It matters more than the armies,* Aldous said quietly. *But it will keep. Armies rarely do.*

* * *

They came at dusk.

I heard them before I saw them — the particular sound of ten thousand boots that do not want to march but march anyway, a sound like rain on a roof, steady and joyless. They crested the western hills in grey ranks under a sky going the color of old pewter, and at their center rode a figure in white and gold, and even at that distance I felt her attention land on me like a hand on the back of my neck.

Veyra. The usurper. The grieving woman, if the king's memories — the few fragments that had survived the transfer — were true. She reined in a hundred yards off, close enough that I could see her face was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and she looked at me standing alone before a glowing gate, and she laughed.

"The charm-seller," she called. Her voice carried impossibly, the crown doing its work. "Correnvale's most modest man. Do you know what you're standing in front of?"

"A door," I said. My voice cracked on the first word and steadied after, which I chose to consider growth. "Do you know what's behind it?"

"Nothing. Ash. A forge that ate my husband and gave me—" She stopped herself. For one moment the winter cracked and something raw showed through, something that had been carrying a corpse of a marriage for seven years and calling it a kingdom. Then it sealed over. "Give me the Ember, boy, and I'll let you walk away. I've burned enough. I only ever wanted—"

"To save us," I said. "Yes. I'm told you say that. I'm told you believe it." I put my scarred palm flat on the gate. It was hot as a heartbeat. "Here's what I believe. I believe you bound ten thousand soldiers to your grief and called it peace. And I think it's time they got their own minds back."

*I cannot hold them all,* Aldous warned inside me. *Not alone. Not even together. Unless—*

"Unless what?"

*Unless you open the gate. All of it. Let the Emberlight through the crown-threads like light through glass. It will cost you everything that remains of me, and possibly everything that remains of you.*

"Define 'possibly.'"

*Boy—*

"I'm joking," I said. "Badly. It's a coping mechanism. Open the door."

I turned my palm and pushed, and the Ashgrove gate — the gate I had sealed with seven years of my life once already, the gate that should stay shut, the gate that had been shut for good reasons I could no longer remember — opened.

Light came out.

I don't have words for it that aren't stolen from better writers. It was the color of the first fire anyone ever lit, and it went through me and out of me and through the king's remnant and out of him, and it crossed the field between the gate and the army faster than fear, and where it touched the grey ranks, the grey ranks *stopped*.

Ten thousand soldiers stood very still in the emberlight. And then, all down the line, like wind moving through wheat, they began to weep.

Because the truth was in the light. That was the trick of it, the mercy of it, the horror of it: the Ember carried memory, and the memory it carried was of every oath freely given and then stolen, every man and woman remembering in the same instant exactly when their will had been taken from them and by whom. They remembered signing up as volunteers and waking as puppets. They remembered loving their queen and being unable to refuse her. Some of them fell to their knees. Some dropped their weapons as if the steel had turned to snakes. And Veyra — Veyra screamed, one short terrible note, as ten thousand threads snapped at once and the crown on her head went dark and became, in a single breath, just metal.

Her horse turned. She let it. I watched the most powerful woman in the world ride away from her own army with her hands empty, and I felt no triumph at all. Only the strange, exhausted tenderness you feel watching someone finally set down something far too heavy.

Then the light finished passing through me, and I sat down again in the dirt, and the last of King Aldous went out of my chest like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

*Thank you,* he said, from nowhere, from everywhere, from the place where paid prices go. And then nothing, and the nothing was peaceful, and I was so tired.

I might have slept. I honestly don't know. What I know is that at some point there were boots in front of me — worn boots, salt-stained, the boots of someone who has walked a very long way — and a voice above me saying:

"Well. You look terrible."

I looked up.

She had a face like weather and eyes that had decided, at some point, to stop pretending they weren't kind. She had a sword she held like an afterthought and a pack that had clearly been through several wars, and when she saw that I didn't recognize her, something moved across her expression — quick, controlled, gone — like a fish under ice.

"Do I know you?" I asked.

"No," she said. Too fast. Then, deliberately, slower, holding out a hand: "No. You don't. Tamsin. Tamsin of the Salt Road. Smuggler, occasional hero, terrible liar." A crooked half-smile. "You're Rafael. I know that much, anyway. Everyone does, now. The man who broke the crown."

I took her hand. Her grip was strong and calloused and it fit mine like — like a key fits a lock it hasn't seen in years but cut itself for.

"Have we met?" I asked. "You seem... I don't know. Familiar. Like a song I've forgotten the words to but could hum."

Something in her face did the fish-under-ice thing again, worse this time.

"No," she said. "We haven't met." And then, because she was apparently a terrible liar and knew it: "But I have a feeling we're going to. Come on. Dunmarrow's two days east, the council's waiting to hear how you did what you did, and you're going to need someone to do the talking, because I've heard you try and you ramble."

"I ramble?"

"Like a river," she said, and hauled me to my feet, and did not let go of my hand quite as quickly as a stranger would have.

### Tamsin

Here's the thing nobody tells you about telling the truth: after the first time, it gets easier, and then it gets harder, because easy truths are cheap and the expensive ones keep coming due.

I told the council everything. My brother's death, the map, the debt, the journal in my pack, the months I'd known where we were going while letting Rafael believe he was leading. I stood in the Dunmarrow council hall with emberlight still in the air of the world and I laid out every lie like contraband on a customs table, and I watched their faces, and I did not flinch, and when I finished, an old shipwright from the coast said, "Well. At least she's honest," and voted to resist Veyra's rump forces on the spot. Truth, it turns out, is worth ten heroes. Heroes can be bought. Confession can't.

Serah testified too. Grey cloak folded over her arm like a skin she'd shed. She gave them Veyra's binding ledgers, the oath-threads, the names of every soldier conscripted by crown-magic, and when she was done she looked at me across the hall and nodded once, and I nodded back, and that was the whole of our reconciliation, and it was enough. Oren got a cairn on the pass, which he'd have hated, and a song already, which he'd have hated more, and both were deserved.

Rafael remembers none of it.

The healers say the Ember took its price in memory — that's what it had been doing to Aldous for seven years, eating him from the inside, and when Rafael pulled it into himself he fed it the freshest thing he had: us. The road. Bray and the debt and the charm that cost him a year. The pass, the bells, the child in the snow. Me.

He looks at me sometimes like I'm a room he knows is important but can't find the door to. And I've decided — gods help me, I've actually decided — not to tell him. Not yet. Not because I'm protecting myself, though I am, obviously, I'm a smuggler, self-protection is the family trade. But because I watched him pay for other people's salvation with pieces of himself until there was almost nothing left, and I will not be one more person taking something from Rafael Ashgrove before he's ready to give it.

So I introduced myself on the field at Ashgrove like we were strangers, and it was the hardest lie I've ever told, and I'm getting better at them, which should worry me more than it does.

We walk east together. He talks too much — genuinely, catastrophically, he narrates everything, the weather, his theories about the gate, a suspiciously detailed opinion about bread — and I let him, because every word is a brick and he's building himself a new house to live in, and I want to be around when he hangs the first picture. Sometimes he stops mid-sentence and frowns at nothing, chasing a shape in the dark furniture of his mind, and sometimes he says, "Did we—? No. Never mind." And I say, "Never mind," and take his hand, and he lets me, and neither of us asks why it feels like coming home.

Yesterday, on the Salt Road, with the hills going gold and the war behind us and whatever comes next ahead, he stopped dead in the middle of the track and said, "Tamsin. Your brother. Was his name Wren?"

My heart did something complicated.

"Why?" I managed.

"Because I dreamed about him," Rafael said slowly, frowning at the middle distance. "A man with ink on his fingers, laughing, saying *tell my sister the map was never the treasure.*" He looked at me. "Tamsin, why would I dream that? Who is your sister? Do you have a sister?"

I looked at this man — this brave, rambling, hollowed-out, rebuilt man — holding a piece of my brother that the Ember had kept safe for me without either of us knowing, and I understood that some doors, once opened, don't shut again, and that I was done being the one who kept them shut.

"Yes," I said. "I have a brother. Sit down, Rafael. This is going to take a while."

And because the world, for once, was kind, the sun stayed out while I told him everything.
