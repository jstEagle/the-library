## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mules hated the high road, and Tamsin Almeida had learned long ago to trust their judgment more than her own.

They picked their way along the shelf of stone above the Vale of Sighs, hooves striking sparks from frost-slicked granite, while below them the drop fell away into a darkness so complete it seemed to have weight. Thornfell in late autumn was a country of teeth and silence—the peaks like the jaws of some sleeping beast, the passes like the spaces between its ribs. Tamsin had lived in those spaces for eleven years, and she had made a religion of being unremarkable. A woman with a weathered face and a mule train, hauling moon-iron ore down from the abandoned mines at Crag's Hollow. Nothing more. No one looked twice at a smuggler, and that was precisely the point.

Behind her, old Bren hummed off-key, as he always did when he was nervous, which was always.

"You're going to wake the wardens with that racket," Tamsin said, not turning around.

"Wardens don't listen for songs, girl. They listen for storms." Bren spat over the edge of the trail, a habit that had once cost him a good coat when the wind threw it back in his face. "And there's not a cloud in the sky. Cleanest night we've had all month."

He was right about that. The sky above Thornfell was swept clean as a polished blade, and the stars were out in their thousands—hard, cold, brilliant, the way they only got this high in the mountains, where the air was thin enough to see the truth of things. Tamsin had always hated them, a little. Stars had a way of looking back.

She flexed her gloved hands on the lead rope and said nothing. She had not spoken of storms in eleven years, not even to Bren, and especially not to Bren, who had carried her out of the burning lowlands when she was nineteen and never once asked what she'd done to earn the fire.

But her storm-sense was awake. It was always awake, a little—a low hum behind her sternum, like a tuning fork someone had struck and never let die. She'd learned to live with it the way one lives with a bad tooth. But tonight the hum had changed pitch. It had climbed, slowly, over the last hour, the way the air climbs before a summer squall, and there was not a cloud within fifty miles, and there should not have been anything in the world to make it climb at all.

The wards, she thought. The border wards.

Thornfell's passes were threaded with them—old magic, older than the kingdom, older than the usurper who sat the throne now, humming along the mountain spines like a held breath. Every smuggler knew them by feel. You crossed a ward-line and your teeth ached and the mules shied and for a heartbeat you could taste copper and rain. The wards were the reason Veylan's wardens couldn't ride where they pleased, the reason the free cities still breathed, the reason a woman with a burned name could vanish into the high country and stay vanished.

They were also, Tamsin realized with a slow, cold certainty, the reason the hum behind her sternum had been climbing all night. The wards were straining. All of them, everywhere, all at once.

"Bren," she said quietly. "Stop the train."

"Now? We're not at the relay yet. The Hollow's still two miles—"

"Stop the train."

He heard it in her voice. Eleven years of partnership had taught him that Tamsin Almeida did not use that voice twice. He whistled the mules down, and the little caravan ground to a halt on the shelf of stone, breath steaming, bells silent because smugglers did not use bells.

Tamsin stood in the middle of the trail and closed her eyes.

The hum was climbing still. Higher. Higher. It felt like standing in a field as the pressure drops and the light goes strange and green, like the whole sky drawing back its fist. She could feel the ward-lines themselves now, faint and vast, running under the mountains like veins under skin—and she could feel them *thinning*. Stretched. Pulled taut by something on the far side of the country, something pulling with a patience and a strength that made her palms sweat inside her gloves.

"Bren," she said. "When did you last feel the wards?"

He frowned at her. In the starlight his face was all creases and shadow, and his hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, to the knife at his belt. "This morning. Crossing under the Widow's Tooth. Same as always. Why?"

"And since then?"

"Since then it's been a quiet night, Tamsin, and I'd like to keep it—"

The wards snapped.

There was no other word for it. It was not an explosion, not a shattering—it was *severance*, clean and total, like a hundred harp strings cut with a hundred blades in the same instant. The sound of it was not a sound at all; it came through the stone and through her bones and through the secret organ behind her sternum that had been her gift and her ruin, and Tamsin cried out and dropped to one knee on the frozen trail with her hands pressed flat against the rock, because the mountain itself seemed to flinch.

Above her, the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*—all of them, all at once, from horizon to horizon, as if a hand had passed over the sky and closed it like a lid. One heartbeat the peaks were stitched with cold silver fire; the next, the darkness above Thornfell was absolute, a blackness so total that the mountains lost their edges and the world became the inside of a closed mouth.

The mules screamed. All of them, at once, a sound Tamsin had never heard a mule make, high and human and terrified, and they surged against their leads in a clashing, kicking knot of panic. Bren was shouting, hauling on the lead rope of the nearest animal, his voice cracked and strange: "Easy, easy, easy, what in the deep—Tamsin, Tamsin, what *was* that—"

She didn't answer. She was on her knees in the dark with her heart slamming against her ribs, and every instinct she had spent eleven years burying was screaming at her in a voice she had not heard since the exile courts.

*Storm's coming*, it screamed. *Storm of storms. Run.*

But there was no storm. There was nothing. The air was still and cold and utterly, horribly silent, and the stars were gone, and the wards were gone, and the border of the kingdom was now nothing but a line on a map.

"Tamsin." Bren's hand found her shoulder in the dark, gripped hard. "Girl. Look at me. It's weather. It has to be weather—some trick of the high air, a pressure change, I've seen the sky do strange things at altitude—"

"It's not weather." Her voice came out flat and calm, which surprised her, because nothing in her was calm. "Bren. The wards are gone."

"Don't say that."

"Feel it. Feel the stone."

He was silent a long moment. She felt him go still beside her, felt the moment the truth reached him through his boots, through his knees, through whatever old smuggler's sense had kept him alive through thirty years of Thornfell winters. When he spoke again, his voice had aged ten years.

"That's not possible. Those wards are older than the kingdom. Older than the *war*. Nothing man-made can—"

"Nothing man-made," Tamsin said. She got to her feet. Her legs held. She was almost proud of them. "Get the mules calmed. We're not stopping at the Hollow. We're going straight down to the tollhouse tonight, and in the morning you're taking the ore on to Vessa without me."

"Like hell I am. Tamsin, whatever you think is happening—"

"I don't *think* anything, Bren. I know what a storm feels like before it comes, and this is that, a hundred times over, and it isn't weather." She was re-packing the lead rope as she spoke, hands moving on their own, because hands that had work to do did not shake. "The border is open. Do you understand what that means? Every warden, every rider, every paid knife Veylan has—they can come up the passes now. Nothing is holding them back but distance and weather, and the weather just stopped mattering."

The mules had quieted somewhat, huddled together in a shivering mass, and Bren moved among them murmuring the low nonsense he used on frightened animals, but she could feel his eyes on her back, and she could feel the question in them, the question he had never asked in eleven years and was now, she could see, on the very edge of asking.

*What are you, Tamsin Almeida? What did they burn your name for?*

She was spared the answer. She was not sure she would have survived giving it.

Because that was when she saw the light.

It was faint—a smear of pale gold down the trail, around the shoulder of the mountain where the high road bent toward the Widow's Tooth. Not a lantern; lanterns swung. Not a ward-light; the wards were dead. It flickered, guttered, held. A fire, then. A small one, built fast and low, the way a hunted man builds a fire.

"Bren," she said. "Someone's on the road."

"At this hour? Nobody travels the high road at night, not even us, and we're idiots." But he had seen it too, and he had unslung his crossbow, and they went forward together, leading the mules, because leaving the mules was leaving their livelihood and leaving their livelihood was a slower death than whatever waited around the bend.

The trail curved. The fire grew. And Tamsin smelled the blood before she saw its source—copper and wet iron on the cold air, a lot of it, the smell of a wound that had been opened hours ago and badly tied.

He was crumpled against the rock face at the trail's edge, half in the light of his own dying fire. A man, young-ish, though it was hard to tell; his face was gray with pain and blood loss, his clothes cut to ribbons, and his left hand was cradled against his chest as though it were the wound—but Tamsin's eyes, trained by years of assessing cargo for damage, went instead to the tears in his coat, and the dark stains on them, and the way he was propped with his back to the stone and his feet drawn under him, ready to roll or run. A soldier's posture. Or a courier's.

He heard them. Of course he did—the mules' shoes on stone were not subtle. His head came up, and his eyes found her in the darkness, and Tamsin saw him take stock of her the same way she had taken stock of him: threat, distance, weapon, escape.

"Please," he said. His voice was ruined, scraped down to the wood. "The road. Is the road open?"

Tamsin glanced up, out of old habit, at the place where the stars should have been.

"No," she said. "The stars are gone."

The man made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sob. "Then I'm not too late," he whispered, and his eyes rolled back, and he slid down the rock face into the dark.
