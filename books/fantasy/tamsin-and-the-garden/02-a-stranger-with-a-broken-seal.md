## 2. A Stranger with a Broken Seal

He weighed nothing. That was the first thing Tamsin noticed, hauling him up from the road and over her shoulder like a sack of wet wool—a grown man, broad through the chest, and yet so light it frightened her. Men who weighed too little had bled too much, and men who had bled too much were dead men walking, and dead men walking brought wardens down on your head like crows on a gut pile.

Bren would have told her to leave him. Bren *was* telling her to leave him, in his way, standing at the edge of the firelight with his arms crossed and his face folded into its usual expression of a man smelling something sour.

"You found him where?"

"High road, past the third marker." Tamsin knelt in the mouth of the smuggler's cave, easing the stranger down onto her bedroll. The cave was one of Maren's old caches, dug into the shoulder of Raven's Tooth, dry and deep enough to hide a caravan if you knew the false wall. She'd slept here a hundred nights across ten years of exile. It was the closest thing she had to a home, which was to say it was a hole in a mountain.

"Past the third marker," Bren repeated. "That's warden country since the wards snapped. You walked into warden country for a corpse."

"He wasn't a corpse."

"He will be by morning, and then he'll be a problem. Corpses are honest. Problems lie." He spat into the dark. "Leave him outside. Wolves have to eat same as us."

Tamsin didn't answer. She was busy with the wound.

It was a blade cut, low on the left side, long and ugly but not—she probed carefully, and he groaned without waking—not deep. Whoever had cut him had wanted him stopped, not finished. That interested her more than she liked. Wardens killed. Wardens did not leave witnesses bleeding on roads unless they were very sure the mountains would finish their work for them.

She cleaned the cut with spirits from her kit, packed it with moss-poultice bitter as regret, and bound it tight. The stranger never woke, but his breathing steadied, going from torn rags to something with a rhythm to it. Outside, the sky stayed black. Not clouded—*black*, a clean and starless black, the way the sky looked only in the deepest caves. Ten years in the Thornfells and Tamsin had learned every mood of that sky, and this was no mood she knew. It was an absence. A theft.

Her storm-sense kept reaching up toward it, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth, and finding nothing, and screaming about it in a register below sound.

"Sleep," Bren said. "I'll take first watch. And Tamsin—" he paused at the cave's inner passage, where the mules were tethered among the moon-iron crates, "—whatever he is, he isn't ours. We deliver iron to Vessa, we come back, we get paid. That's the whole of the world. Don't go making it bigger."

"It's not big," she said. "It's a man with a hole in him."

Bren grunted, unconvinced, and went to sit with the mules.

* * *

He woke near dawn—or what should have been dawn, though the light came grey and sourceless, as if the sun itself weren't sure whether to bother.

Tamsin heard him before she saw him: the small, controlled sounds of a man testing his own body, cataloguing injuries, deciding whether to run. She'd made those sounds herself, once, in a cell beneath the exile court, the night they took her gift's measure and put a price on her head.

"Don't," she said, without turning from the fire. "The stitches hold if you don't tear them. Tear them and I'll let you bleed out on principle."

He froze. Then, slowly, he lowered himself back against the cave wall, and she turned and looked at him properly for the first time.

He was younger than she'd guessed in the dark—thirty, perhaps, or thirty-five, with the soft hands and hollow cheeks of a man who lived by ink rather than iron. A scholar's face. But the eyes were wrong for a scholar: too quick, too cornered, always moving to the cave mouth and back again, measuring. His clothes had been fine once, a courier's travelling coat, now torn and stiff with old blood that wasn't all his own.

"You stitched me," he said. His voice was still ruined, but working.

"I stitched you. Don't mistake it for kindness. Dead men stink up caves."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. "Then I'll try not to die. My name is Corin."

"I didn't ask."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm going to keep saying things until you do, because I've been alone on that road for two days and I think silence has started eating me alive." He shifted, winced, pressed a hand flat against the bandage. "You're a smuggler. Moon-iron, by the smell of the crates. Running the high passes while the border wards are down, which means either you're very brave or you haven't yet understood what happened last night."

"The stars went out," Tamsin said flatly. "I understood."

"Then you understand more than most. Most people will call it weather. Most people will call it anything at all except what it is." He watched her across the fire, and there it was again—that measuring look, but aimed differently now. Not at her weapons. At her. "What did it feel like? To you. When they went."

The question landed strangely, precisely, like a key finding a lock she hadn't known was exposed. Tamsin kept her face still. Ten years of practice.

"Like weather," she said.

Corin held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something. "Of course. Like weather." He flexed his right hand, grimacing, and Tamsin saw it then—the thing she would spend the rest of this story unable to unsee.

Across his palm, burned into the flesh itself, was a sigil. Old work, ritual work, the kind of brand that wasn't punishment but *sealing*. And it had been shattered—struck through, broken into seven jagged pieces, each piece still faintly glowing with a heat that had no business surviving days on an open road.

Tamsin knew that symbol.

She had seen it exactly once, ten years ago, in the great hall of the exile court, carved into the lintel above the judges' bench where no one was meant to look at it. An old sigil, older than the kingdom, older than Veylan's line. The archivists called it the mark of the Stormheart. The guards called it bad luck and wouldn't walk under it. And a girl of seventeen, kneeling on cold stone waiting to hear her sentence, had stared at it because looking anywhere else meant crying.

Every legend agreed on one thing: the Stormheart was broken. Broken in the first war, broken beyond mending, its pieces lost or destroyed. That was the whole point of the stories. *Gone*, so that no storm-caller could ever be tempted to seek it, so that no king could ever claim it.

And here it was, branded living into a dying scholar's hand—and *shattered anew*, as if something had broken it a second time, recently, violently, from somewhere very far away.

The night the stars went out.

Tamsin's skin prickled. Her storm-sense, that traitor sense, reached upward again into the starless black and screamed its toothless scream.

"Where did you get that?" she asked. Her voice came out lower than she intended.

Corin looked down at his palm as if he'd forgotten it was there. When he spoke, all the levity had gone out of him. "In the royal archive, under the palace of King Veylan, four nights ago. There's a vault beneath it that doesn't appear on any map. I was the junior archivist assigned to catalogue its contents. I was supposed to spend my life pretending it was empty." He closed his fist, and the glow of the broken seal leaked between his fingers like embers. "It isn't empty."

"You stole something."

"I stole proof." He leaned forward, and the movement cost him—she saw the pain cross his face and watched him refuse to show it, and something in her, some old buried thing, recognized the gesture. "Proof of what extinguished the stars. Proof of what Veylan wears on his head and calls a crown. Proof that the first war—the war they taught you was fought against monsters—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening. "I need to reach Vessa. The free city. There are people there who can carry this further than I can. People whose hearts aren't bought."

"And the wardens chasing you?"

"Wardens, and worse." He met her eyes. "They know what I carry. They don't know I've already sent word ahead, or they'd have let me die on that road instead of hunting me across three passes. Which brings me to my request, and I want you to hear all of it before you say no." He drew a breath. "Take me to Vessa. You know these mountains. You're running moon-iron down to the free cities anyway—I saw the manifests in your lead crate, don't look surprised, I was an archivist, we read everything. I can pay. I can pay in ways that will matter when the truth comes out, and the truth *is* coming out, whether I live to see it or not."

Tamsin sat very still.

Everything in her said *no*. It said it loudly, in Bren's voice and Maren's voice and her own voice worn hoarse over ten years of survival: *invisible, Tamsin. Small. A woman who carries crates and says nothing and owes nothing.* Helping him meant becoming visible. It meant putting her name—if not her name, then her face, her route, her cave—into a story that already had wardens in it. She had spent a decade learning that the mountains kept you alive only as long as you asked nothing of them and gave them nothing to want.

And yet.

She looked at his hand. At the broken seal, guttering like a dying coal. At the man attached to it, who had crawled three passes on a sliced-open side to carry a truth to strangers who might burn him for it, and who had smiled—almost—when she threatened to let him bleed.

She thought of the exile court. Of the lintel. Of a girl on her knees who had refused, absolutely refused, to turn her storms on a village full of civilians, and who had been exiled for it by men sitting beneath a symbol they didn't understand. She had told herself for ten years that refusal had been the end of her life. Sitting here, watching the sigil breathe its faint heat, she felt the vertiginous suspicion that it had been the beginning of someone else's plan.

"I should say no," she said aloud.

"Yes," Corin agreed quietly. "You should."

"The smart thing—the only thing—is to feed you another dose of poultice, point you down the south trail, and forget your face."

"That's what I'd do," he said. "If I were you."

"You're not me." Tamsin rose, went to the cache shelf, and pulled down the second bedroll, the good one, the one she'd been saving against a winter worse than this one. She threw it at him. It hit him in the chest, and he caught it with his branded hand, and the broken seal flared briefly against the wool like a heartbeat.

"We leave tomorrow, before first light," she said. "There's a route down the Serpent Stair that avoids the tollhouses. Mostly. If you slow me down, I tie you to a mule. If you get me killed, I will find some way to haunt you, and I promise you I was trained in haunting by professionals."

Corin stared at her. Then he laughed—a real laugh this time, cracked and disbelieving, the laugh of a man who had expected to die alone in the dark and instead been handed a blanket and a threat.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Thank whatever god broke your seal badly enough that you fell on *my* road." But even as she said it, Tamsin felt the words land wrong, heavy, prophetic. Because nothing about last night had been accident. The wards snapping, the stars vanishing, a wounded carrier collapsing at her feet on the exact stretch of stone she rode twice a season—

Her storm-sense screamed again, and this time, far below the mountain, beneath the sleeping rock, something answered.

A single pulse. Faint. Familiar. Like a heart remembering how to beat.

Tamsin stood frozen, one hand braced against the cave wall, and Corin saw her face change.

"What is it?" he whispered. "What did you feel?"

She looked at him—at the broken seal, at the ruined scholar's eyes—and lied for the second time in ten years.

"Nothing," she said. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we ride."
