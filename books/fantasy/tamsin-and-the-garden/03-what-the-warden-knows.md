## 3. What the Warden Knows

Corin woke to the smell of pine smoke and the sound of someone sharpening a knife.

For a moment he did not know where he was. The ceiling above him was stone, veined with pale mineral that caught the firelight like frost. His shoulder throbbed in time with his heartbeat, a deep, wet ache that told him the wound had been bound and rebound at least twice. He lay still and took inventory, as he had learned to do in the archive's lower vaults, where a man survived by knowing exactly what he had and who might want it.

One satchel. Still beneath his coat. The oilskin wrapping unbroken.

A woman sat across the small cave, turning a whetstone along a blade with the unhurried rhythm of long habit. She was younger than he had expected—the storm-caller of Thornfell, the exile whose name the wardens whispered like a curse. Tamsin Almeida. In Veylan's court she had been a lesson taught to children: *this is what happens to those who refuse the crown.* He had imagined her older, harder, bent by bitterness. Instead she looked like a woman who had simply decided, one day, to stop being what other people wanted, and had paid for it every day since.

"You're awake," she said without looking up. "Good. You can answer questions now."

"I can," Corin said. His voice came out scraped raw. "Though I suspect you've already searched my things."

"Your coat, your boots, and the lining of both." The whetstone paused. "No papers. No letters. Nothing but a broken seal branded into your hand and a man too stubborn to die quietly. Either you're nobody—which no one bleeds that much to be—or you're very good at hiding."

He pushed himself up against the cave wall, and the motion pulled at the wound hard enough to whiten his vision. When it cleared, she was watching him. Not with suspicion exactly. With the particular patience of someone who had learned that most people lied, and that waiting cost nothing.

"My name is Corin Vaile," he said. "I was second archivist to the Royal Repository in Carreth. Until nine days ago."

"And now?"

"Now I'm a thief." He smiled, and found it hurt more than the shoulder. "The first honest work I've ever done."

* * *

She made him eat first—hard bread, dried meat, tea brewed from something bitter that numbed the edge of the pain—and only when he'd finished did she sit back down across from him, knife across her knees, fire between them.

"Start with the seal," she said. "I've seen sigils my whole life. Wardens' marks, tollhouse brands, smuggler signs. That one on your palm isn't any of them. But I've *seen* it before, somewhere I can't name, and it's making me itch."

Corin turned his left hand over. The brand had been pressed into his skin with a heated matrix—old magic, old iron—and then shattered, cracked through its center like river ice underfoot. Even damaged, the design was unmistakable to anyone who had spent years among the oldest documents in the kingdom: a circle pierced by a descending line, ringed by seven points.

"The Stormheart," he said.

Tamsin went very still. It was not the stillness of disbelief, he thought. It was the stillness of recognition arriving somewhere unwelcome.

"That's a story," she said finally. "An exile-court story. The artifact that broke the sky. My grandmother used to scare us with it. The Stormheart fell in the first war, humans and sky-born tore each other apart over it, and the pieces were lost or destroyed or never existed at all. Children's tales."

"Yes," Corin said. "That is precisely what the crown has spent three hundred years ensuring everyone believed. I know because I catalogued the lie. It was my job. Document 4471-C, *Suppression Order, Reign of Aldric II*: all cartographic references to the Vault of Sundering to be struck from public record; all genealogies tracing the Veylan line to the shard-bearers to be sealed; all surviving sky-born accounts of the sundering to be burned." He recited it from memory because he could not help himself; the order lived behind his eyes now, day and night. "I filed that document myself, four years ago, thinking it was routine housekeeping. Thinking history was just old paper."

Outside the cave, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant surf. Tamsin did not move.

"Tell me about the stars," she said quietly.

And there it was—the question underneath everything, the one the whole mountain was asking, the one that had emptied the night sky nine days ago and left the world under a lid of black. Corin set down his cup. His hands were not steady. They had not been steady since the night the last star went out, and he had stood in the archive tower watching the dark arrive from the horizon inward, like ink dropped into water, and understood that whatever he had found in the sealed vaults was not a scandal but an apocalypse.

* * *

"It began with a ledger," he said. "Eleven months ago. A requisition record from the reign of Veylan's grandfather—a payment to a mining guild in the Greyreach, for extraction of 'heart-iron, one piece, unsized.' Heart-iron doesn't exist. It's a term from the oldest strata of the archive, pre-war. I cross-referenced. There were four such payments across two centuries, each buried in mundane columns, each signed with the same private cipher. The cipher belongs to the crown's inner household. Not the king publicly. The king *privately*. The part of the crown no one audits."

"You're saying they've been mining it," Tamsin said slowly. "Mining pieces of the Stormheart. For generations."

"Pieces, or the pieces finding their way to them—I don't know which. What I know is what I found in the sealed vault beneath the Repository, three weeks ago, behind a door that opens to royal blood alone." He closed his eyes; the memory came anyway, complete and merciless. "There is a shard, Tamsin. A large one. It sits in the base of the coronation regalia—the Scepter of Accord, they call it, the stone that 'binds the realm.' And it does bind. The border wards, the mountain wards, the wards around every garrison and prison in Veylan's reach—they aren't fed by ley-lines or priest-song anymore. They're fed by the shard. Every ward in the kingdom is a leash, and the shard holds all the leashes in one fist."

"Then why would the wards fail? Why did they snap two nights ago?"

"Because the shard is dying." He said it plainly, because there was no kind way to say it. "It isn't stone. It never was. The Stormheart was the sky-born's own heart—the shared heart of their whole kind, the thing they poured themselves into so that mortals and sky-born could speak to each other without fear. When it was stolen and broken, the sky-born didn't die at once. They began to die *slowly*. Each generation fewer. Each generation dimmer. The old records call them the Fading Years. And the shard, cut from the living heart, burns through what remains of it. Veylan's power is literally consuming the last life of the sky-born, star by star."

Tamsin's face had gone the color of ash. "The stars going out—"

"Are the sky-born dying." Corin heard his own voice crack and did not care. "Each extinguished star is one of them, ending. Nine days ago the last of the great ones failed, and the shard drew so hard on what remained that the wards overloaded and snapped. What you felt on the road—that snapping—was the sound of a kingdom's chains breaking and a people's death rattle happening at once." He leaned forward, firelight carving his face into hollows. "Veylan knows. Of course he knows. The fading accelerates whenever the shard is drawn upon. Every war he wages, every city his wards strangle, spends the sky-born faster. He is burning down heaven to keep his throne warm, and he has forty years of ledgers proving he always intended to."

* * *

Silence held the cave for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, water dripped with the patience of stone.

Then Tamsin said, in a voice carefully flattened, "Ilse Varn. The warden-captain hunting you. Tell me about her."

Corin almost laughed. "You know her?"

"I know her name. Everyone in Thornfell knows her name. She doesn't chase smugglers. Smugglers are beneath her. So tell me why a woman like that crossed the border into the high passes in winter for one bleeding archivist."

"Because of what I carry." He touched the satchel beneath his coat. "Not copies. Copies can be dismissed, forged, explained away. In the vault I also took the *keystone*—the master attestation, written in Veylan's grandfather's own hand, describing the shard's placement in the regalia and authorizing the ward-binding in perpetuity. Sealed with the private cipher. Signed. Dated. It is the single document that cannot survive daylight, and Ilse Varn knows it exists because she was in the tower the night I ran. She saw me take it." He met Tamsin's eyes. "She isn't hunting a thief. She's hunting the end of her king's reign, and she'll burn every village between here and Vessa to stop it reaching the free cities' council."

Tamsin rose and walked to the cave mouth. Beyond her shoulder, Corin could see the black sky pressing down on the peaks—not clouded, not overcast, but *empty*, a darkness with no depth to it, no distant fires of other suns. He had grown up under that sky and never once thought about what held the stars up. Now he thought about it constantly.

"There's something else," Tamsin said, without turning around. "Something you're not saying. You've been watching me since you woke up. Not the way a man watches his rescuer. The way a man watches a piece on a board."

Corin exhaled slowly. There was no gentle way to do this either. He had rehearsed it on the road, in the snow, half-delirious, and every version of it sounded monstrous.

"When I fled Carreth," he said, "I didn't run blind. I needed a route to Vessa that avoided the wards, and I needed someone who could open paths no warden would expect. So I read the files. Exile files. There's a thick one on a girl who refused a conscription order nineteen years ago—refused to call storms against the river-villages during the Harrow Rebellion. The court let her live. Did you ever wonder why?" He watched her shoulders go rigid. "Because letting you go wasn't mercy. It was investment. The order came down from the inner household—the same cipher, the same hand. *Let the storm-caller live in exile. Unaligned. Unwatched. A gift unspent may yet be spent.* They wrote you into their contingency plans, Tamsin. Your whole life out here—Bren, the caravans, the passes you learned, the reputation you built—they have a file on all of it. Because a day might come when they'd need a storm-caller no one knew was theirs to call."

She turned around. Her face was terrible and calm, the calm of deep water over something drowning.

"They exiled me," she said, "and then they kept me. Like a coin in a drawer."

"Like a chess piece," Corin said softly. "Nineteen years ago, someone in Carreth looked at a girl who refused to be a weapon, and decided that refusing made her more valuable later. Everything you built to be free of them—they catalogued it. I'm sorry. I am so deeply sorry. But you should know that the moment I crossed into Thornfell carrying this satchel, their plan for you started moving whether you consent to it or not. Which means we have a choice to make, and not much time to make it. Because Ilse Varn isn't following my trail anymore."

Tamsin went still again. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that yesterday morning, while you were gathering wood, I heard falcon-call from the eastern ridge. Three notes, repeated. Warden signal-relay." He held her gaze. "They're not tracking me, Tamsin. They already know where I am. They're tracking *you*—waiting to see what you do with me. And the next move on this board is yours."

Far off across the black peaks, where no star had shone for nine nights, something answered the wind—a low rolling murmur, like thunder remembering its name.
