## 4. The Tollhouse Bargain

The tollhouse crouched at the throat of Greywater Pass like an old toad that had learned to swallow lightning and call it digestion. Tamsin had known it all her life—first as a child chasing cats among its barrel-stacked cellars, later as a courier signing Maren's ledger with a false name. It was built of black granite quarried from the pass itself, so that in the grey light of the overcast morning it seemed less a building than a wound in the mountain that had decided, out of stubbornness, to have windows.

Smoke curled from its chimney. Lanterns burned behind horn panes even though it was barely past dawn. And beneath the ordinary signs of habitation, Tamsin felt the other thing—the hum of wards woven into the very stones, old work, pre-Imperial work, paid for in favors no ledger recorded. The tollhouse had stood through three wars and a plague because no army had ever been entirely sure what would happen if they knocked on its door uninvited.

She reined in her mule at the edge of the yard and looked back down the trail.

Bren sat his donkey like a sack of turnips that had learned resentment, his grey beard crusted with two days of trail frost. Behind him, Corin rode slung across a pack-mule's back, half-tied there for his own safety. He had faded in and out of fever since the cave—sometimes lucid enough to argue about the route, sometimes murmuring names Tamsin didn't recognize, archivists' names, dead men's names. His wounded arm had been bound with moss and linen and a paste of yarrow that Bren swore by and Tamsin privately distrusted.

"He's worse," she said quietly, when Bren drew level.

"He's alive. That's more than the wardens can say for whoever they caught up with last night." Bren spat to the side. "You sure about this, girl? There are other ways down."

"There aren't. Not with him fevered. The eastern switchbacks take six days in good weather; we'd be carrying him and he'd be dead by the fourth. The Serpent Stair is one night, maybe two, straight down to the low road." She kept her eyes on the tollhouse door. "And Maren controls the Stair."

"Maren." Bren said the name the way some men said *storm*. "You haven't seen her in four years."

"Three."

"You left without saying goodbye."

"I left without saying goodbye because staying meant becoming the thing she wanted me to be." Tamsin gathered the reins. "It'll be fine."

It was a lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said anything else, because that was how things were done between smugglers: lies were permitted, but only if everyone agreed not to notice them.

* * *

Inside, the tollhouse smelled of tallow, pipe-smoke, wet wool, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang of the mountain itself. The common room was half-full at this hour—carriers drinking small beer before the day's haul, a pair of fur-trappers arguing over dice, a boy asleep under a table with his arms around a cheese wheel like a lover. Every face turned when Tamsin entered. Every face pretended, a heartbeat later, that it hadn't.

Maren received them in the back room, where the ledgers lived and the real business happened.

She hadn't changed. That was the first cruelty of it. Tamsin had spent three years building Maren into something manageable in memory—a hard old woman, a means to an end, a chapter closed. But there she was: broad-shouldered, iron-haired, her hands resting flat on the table before her like two well-fed spiders, her eyes the pale grey of glacier ice and about as warm. She wore a man's coat cut down to fit her and a ring of keys at her belt that opened doors in seven cities.

"Tamsin Almeida," Maren said. "Come back from the dead."

"Just from the far side of the pass."

"So you say." Maren's gaze moved past her, cataloguing: Bren, dripping in the doorway; the fever-bright stranger being propped against the wall by two of Maren's people who had taken his weight without being asked. "Sit down. Both of you. You look like something the crows gave up on."

They sat. Wine appeared—real wine, southern wine, worth more than the mules outside—and Maren poured it herself, which was either courtesy or a message. With Maren it was always both.

"Now," she said, settling back. "Tell me why you've come crawling to my door with a dying man tied to a pack animal, two days after every star in the sky went out, in the same week that Veylan's wardens tripled their patrols on every road I own. Take your time. Lie to me slowly. I enjoy watching people try."

Tamsin took a breath and told her most of it.

Not all of it. She gave her the shape of the truth: a stranger found on the high road, wounded, carrying something valuable, needing passage to Vessa. She did not mention the Stormheart. She did not mention the seal branded into his palm. She did not mention that the thing he carried might explain the darkened sky, or that wardens were hunting him specifically rather than generally. Those truths were coins she wasn't ready to spend—not because she distrusted Maren, but because she understood exactly what Maren was, and what Maren would do with them.

Maren listened with her chin propped on her knuckles, and when Tamsin finished, she was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice in the hearth.

"Vessa," she said at last. "Through the Serpent Stair."

"Yes."

"The Stair is closed."

"It's not closed. You closed it."

"I own it. Same thing." Maren smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "Here is my problem, girl. Wardens on my roads asking questions about a fugitive. A fugitive who turns up in my tollhouse carried by my runaway daughter. You see how a woman of business might find this interesting."

"I'm not your daughter."

"No," Maren agreed, and something flickered behind the ice of her eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish under frost. "You made certain of that."

* * *

They negotiated for two hours.

It was, in its way, almost tender. They argued over tariffs and escorts, over which of Maren's riders would take them down and at what hour, over whether Corin could be moved tonight or must wait for morning. Maren tried to charge them double and Tamsin talked her down to time-and-a-half plus a favor to be named later, which was a terrible term and Tamsin took it anyway, because the alternative was the eastern switchbacks and a grave dug in the snow.

But underneath the haggling, Tamsin watched Maren watch Corin.

It was subtle. Maren was too good for it to be anything else. But twice—twice—her glance lingered on the stranger's bandaged arm a moment too long. Once she asked, casually, what trade he followed, and Tamsin saw her file the non-answer away. And once, when Corin shivered in his fever and muttered something in his sleep, Maren's head tilted, just slightly, like a hawk adjusting for wind.

Tamsin had grown up learning to read that tilt. It meant *I know more than you think I know.*

When the terms were finally struck, Maren dismissed Bren with a task—inventory of the cellar stores, a job designed to take hours—and poured a second cup of wine for Tamsin alone. That was the signal. That was always the signal. Twenty years ago it had meant a lesson in cipher-work by candlelight. Now it meant something else.

"Alone, then," Maren said, when the door had shut behind Bren. "Let's speak plainly, since you've insulted us both with the other kind of talk."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" Maren leaned forward. "The man carries a royal seal. Broken, but a seal—I saw the wax scrap in his coat pocket while your friend was busy bleeding on my floor. Do you know how rare it is, these days, to see the old sigil? The nine-pointed star?" Her voice dropped. "The Stormheart."

The word went through Tamsin like cold water through cloth. She kept her face still. She had learned stillness in exile the way other people learned prayers.

"I don't know what that is."

"You do. Your mother sang you the court legends same as mine sang me. Guardians of the sky. The breaking of the world's oldest promise." Maren turned her cup slowly on the table. "And now here's a man with that sigil in his pocket, hunted by wardens, and the stars go dark the same week. I'm an old woman, Tamsin. I don't believe in coincidence. I believe in price."

"What are you offering?"

"Passage down the Stair. My best riders. Clean papers at the bottom." Maren paused, and Tamsin felt the trap assembling itself around her, joint by joint, elegant as a lock. "In exchange—you leave him with me."

The room seemed to tilt. "Leave him—"

"At the tollhouse. Or at the bottom of the Stair, delivered to my people, whichever serves. He goes no further toward Vessa." Maren's voice was gentle, which was how Tamsin knew it was serious. "There are buyers south of the mountains who will pay a fortune for whatever he's carrying, and more who will pay simply to ask him questions. I've already sent word. By tomorrow there will be three parties bidding. By the day after, wardens too, probably, and then the price gets ugly. So we move fast, you and I. You get your clean papers and your quiet life back. I get paid. Everyone wins."

"And him?"

Maren shrugged, vast and unbothered, a mountain range in a man's coat. "Him is merchandise."

* * *

Later—much later, after the candles had burned down and the common room had emptied and the feverish murmur of Corin's voice came faintly through the floorboards from the room below—Tamsin stood at the window of the loft where they'd been given beds, and looked out at a sky with nothing in it.

No stars. No moon. Just a smooth black nothing from ridge to ridge, as if the night itself had been skinned. She had lived under that sky for five nights now and it still made her chest hurt, some deep animal part of her insisting that the world had been robbed.

*Merenchandise.* She turned Maren's word over. This woman had pulled her out of a snowdrift at eight years old, fed her, taught her letters with a smuggler's alphabet, held her when she woke screaming after the first time she'd called lightning. *Mother* was too soft a word for what Maren had been—but it was the only word that fit, and Tamsin had spent three years trying to amputate it and failed.

Which was precisely why Maren has asked for this. Not the money—Maren had money. She wanted to see whether Tamsin would hand over a helpless man for the sake of belonging again. She wanted the daughter back, and she wanted the daughter proven hers.

Or perhaps that was vanity. Perhaps it really was just coin. Perhaps it didn't matter, because either way the answer was the same.

Because Tamsin had seen the seal on Corin's palm. She had heard him, in his fever, swear that the proof he carried would bring down a king. And she remembered—she would always remember—the exact texture of her own exile: the trumped-up charges, the trial that had already been decided, the way the whole machine of the court had ground her down for the crime of refusing to burn civilians. If Corin was right, that machine had been running on stolen starlight all along. Handing him to Maren wouldn't save her quiet life. It would just make her complicit in keeping the lights off forever.

So she went downstairs, past the sleeping boy and the cheese wheel, to the room where Corin lay sweating in the dark, and she checked his bandages and gave him water, and when he surfaced enough to whisper "Are we still going to Vessa?" she said, "Yes. I promised."

Then she climbed back up, and found Maren waiting for her in the back room, alone, the second cup of wine still untouched on the table between them like a question.

"Well?" Maren said.

Tamsin sat down. She picked up the cup. She met those glacier-grey eyes, and she lied.

"Yes," she said. "You'll have him. At the bottom of the Stair, delivered to your people. On one condition—my riders, my choice of escort, and Bren never knows. He's old. He'd only argue."

Something moved through Maren's face—satisfaction, and beneath it, briefly, something almost like grief, there and gone. She reached across the table and gripped Tamsin's wrist, hard, the old grip, the training grip.

"Good girl," she said. "We leave at moonset."

"There is no moon," Tamsin said.

"No," Maren agreed, releasing her. "There isn't. Isn't that something."

Tamsin drank the wine. It tasted of the south, of sunlight, of everything she was walking away from. She smiled, and thanked her, and went up to the loft, and lay awake in the dark listening to Corin breathe.

Below her, she heard Maren's boots cross the yard, and voices gathering—riders being summoned, more than the bargain had named. And one voice, low and clipped, that did not belong to any smuggler Tamsin had ever known, asking questions in the accent of the capital.

She lay still in the darkness and began, silently, to count the hours until moonset.
