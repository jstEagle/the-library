## 5. Wardens on the Stair

The Serpent Stair had earned its name honestly. Tamsin had climbed it a dozen times in her years with Maren's network, and every climb felt like being swallowed: a switchbacking shelf of road cut into the mountain's eastern face, narrow enough that two mules could not pass, dropping away into mist so deep the bottom might have been the world's end. The stone was slick with snowmelt even in high summer, and the wind came up the gorge in long, moaning breaths, as though the mountain itself were sleeping badly.

She went first, testing each foothold, with Corin three paces behind and Bren bringing up the rear with the mule. Corin's fever had broken in the night, but he moved like a man made of glass and wire, one hand pressed to his bandaged side, his breathing carefully controlled. He hadn't complained once. Tamsin found that she liked him better for it—and trusted him more warily for it too.

"Storm's coming," Bren said behind her, cheerful as ever. The old man said it the way other men commented on breakfast. "Feel that in my knee. Big one."

Tamsin did not answer. She was feeling something else entirely.

It had started an hour ago, at the top of the Stair, when they'd passed beneath the last of the wayshrines—crude cairns the smugglers kept stacked with offerings to keep the mountain roads quiet. Her storm-sense, that second skin of pressure and charge she'd carried since childhood, had prickled like the moment before lightning strikes. Not weather. Weather rolled in from the west, gradual, honest. This was a wrongness in the air itself, a taste of hot copper at the back of her throat, and it was getting stronger with every switchback down.

Someone was using warded iron on her mountain.

"Corin." She stopped, braced against the rock face, and looked back up the road. "How many wardens does Varn command?"

He went still. "A company. Forty, maybe fifty. Why?"

"Because I can smell them." She turned fully, scanning the heights above—the grey shelves of scree, the stunted pines clinging like lice to the cliff, the white smear of a hanging glacier far overhead. Nothing moved. That was the problem. In the Thornfells there was always something moving: choughs, marmots, the flicker of a hawk. The silence above them was the silence of held breath.

"They've been following us since the tollhouse," she said quietly. "Or since before. They let us walk onto the Stair because the Stair is where you corner things."

Bren spat over the edge. "Girl talks like the end of the world's always due Tuesday. It's weather. Look—" he pointed east, where a bruise-colored cloud bank was shouldering up over the lower peaks, "—that's a proper storm front. We'll be wet, is all."

But Tamsin watched the cloud bank and felt nothing from it. No pull, no pressure, no whisper of charge along her nerves. A dead sky, rolling toward them like scenery painted on canvas. And beneath it, growing sharper by the minute, that copper taste—warded iron, dozens of pieces of it, converging.

*They know,* she thought. *Not just that we're here. They know what I am.*

* * *

It happened at the third bend from the bottom, where the road widened around a shoulder of black granite and the drop fell away sheer for six hundred feet.

The first crossbow bolt took the mule through the neck.

The animal screamed—a horrible, human sound—and pitched sideways into the rock wall, and Bren was shouting, hauling at its harness, and then the mountainside simply *opened*. Wardens rose out of the scree above them like grey ghosts shedding greyer cloaks, a dozen of them, twenty, crossbows leveled, and from the bend below came the ring of boots on stone as more climbed toward them, cutting off the descent.

And down the gorge, walking unhurried along the narrow road with her grey cloak snapping in the wind, came Ilse Varn.

Tamsin had seen her once before, years ago, across a burning checkpoint she'd been lucky to escape. She remembered thinking the warden-captain had eyes like frozen puddles—you could see your own reflection in them, and nothing else. Up close now, that hadn't changed. Varn stopped thirty feet down the road, folded her hands atop her sword hilt, and looked past Tamsin entirely.

"Archivist Corin Vale," she called, and her voice carried flat and clear over the wind. "By order of King Veylan, you are remanded to the crown's custody. Your companions may live if they step aside." Her gaze slid to Tamsin at last, and something almost like amusement touched her mouth. "Step aside, smuggler. You're standing on a king's road."

"There are no king's roads in Thornfell," Tamsin said.

"No," Varn agreed pleasantly. "There won't be much longer."

Corin had gone white. He pressed himself against the granite wall, one hand fumbling inside his coat—not for a weapon; Tamsin knew now what he carried, the sealed proofs, the archive-leathers that named Veylan's crown for what it was—and Bren stood over the dying mule with his old skinning knife drawn, absurd and brave and doomed.

"Tamsin," Corin whispered. "Run. You can make the scree—"

"Shut up," she said.

Her heart was slamming. Below, the wardens were climbing; above, the crossbows tracked them with the patience of men who had done this a hundred times. There was no way out. There was no clever smuggler's trick, no hidden path, no bargain left to strike. There was only the wall, the drop, and forty blades.

And there was the sky.

The dead, painted cloud bank had reached the peaks above them now, and beyond it—beyond everything—Tamsin felt the true storm waiting. The real one. The great slow mass of pressure and lightning that had been building all day in the honest west, dragging its skirts over the high country, full of all the wild electricity of a summer breaking. It hung there like a held note. Like a bow drawn back.

Like an invitation.

*Never again.* She had sworn it on the ash of Hallowmere, on the memory of what her gift had been meant for before she refused—sworn it kneeling in the ruins while the exile-writs were read aloud. *Never call. Never be their weapon. Never let them see what you are.*

Varn raised one gloved hand, and twenty crossbows drew as one.

"I'm sorry," Tamsin said—to Corin, to Bren, to the girl she had been, to the vow itself—and reached into the sky.

* * *

Calling a storm, when you had spent seven years refusing to do it, was like opening a door you'd nailed shut from the inside. The nails tore. Something in her chest ripped wide, and the whole roaring weight of the western front came pouring through the wound in her soul, and Tamsin Almeida threw back her head and *laughed*, because it hurt so much and felt so much like home.

Lightning answered her.

It came down the gorge in a white flood—not one bolt but a lattice of them, striking the scree slopes above and below, detonating the rock, hurling shards and thunder across the pass. The crossbow line above shattered into screaming, tumbling shapes. The wardens climbing from below flattened against the rock face, hands over their heads. Even Varn was driven back a step, cloak whipping, her frozen-puddle eyes fixed on Tamsin with an expression that was no longer amusement.

"*Storm-caller,*" the warden-captain breathed. And then, louder, in a voice pitched to carry to every ear on the mountain: "SEIZE HER! The Almeida girl is worth ten thousand crowns—TAKE HER ALIVE!"

The name rang off the cliffs and echoed down the gorge and out into the wide listening dark of the Thornfells, and Tamsin knew, even as the next bolt leapt from her fingertips and split the road ahead of Varn's boots, that it would keep ringing. Every smuggler, every herder, every paid informer between here and Vessa had just heard it. The invisible life was over. Seven years of careful nothing, burned away in a single breath of lightning.

"MOVE!" She grabbed Corin's collar and hauled him past the smoking crater where the road had been. Above them, the scree slope had come loose in a hissing grey avalanche, burying the upper crossbow line, opening a ragged scar of loose stone up the cliff face—a way, not a good one, but a way.

"The caves!" Bren bellowed. He'd cut the mule free—or the mule had broken free; it was gone, crashing down-trail, bolt-stub jutting from its neck—and the old man was pointing with his knife toward a black seam in the rock wall fifty feet up the scree. "Smuggler's door! I marked it years back—go, both of you, GO!"

"You first!" Tamsin shouted back.

"I'm the rear guard, girl, that's how rearguards work!" And then the wardens from below were swarming up the road again, and Bren Hartwell—seventy-one years old, bad knee, skinning knife—turned to meet them with a grin on his weathered face. "Go on! Old men die slower than they think anyway!"

What happened next happened fast and would stay slow in her memory forever. Corin scrambling up the scree, gasping, hand pressed to his wound. Tamsin calling another bolt down to blast the road behind them, buying seconds, watching wardens scatter like startled crows. Reaching the cave mouth. Turning.

Seeing Bren go down under three of them—not killed, *pinned*, his knife skittering away across the stone, Varn's boot on his chest, the old man still laughing, still swearing cheerfully even as they bound his wrists.

"TAMSIN!" he roared after her, one last time, as she hesitated on the threshold of the dark. His voice cracked with fury and fear and something that sounded horribly like pride. "DON'T YOU DARE COME BACK FOR ME! RUN, GIRL!"

She ran.

* * *

The deep caves swallowed them by degrees—first the smuggler's tunnel, dry and soot-marked with old route-signs, then wider galleries where water dripped in the darkness and their single lantern threw shadows like drowning things. They climbed for hours, or it felt like hours, until the sounds of pursuit faded and only the mountain's own slow breathing remained.

Finally, in a chamber where pale mineral veins glittered in the lantern light like frozen lightning, Tamsin sat down hard against the wall and discovered she was shaking.

Not from fear. From the other thing. The power still hummed in her blood like a struck bell that wouldn't fall silent, singing through her teeth, her fingertips, the hollows of her bones. It had felt *magnificent*. That was the horror of it. Seven years of discipline, and the moment the choice came, her body had remembered joy.

Corin slid down beside her, grey-faced, breathing in shallow stitches. For a long time neither of them spoke.

"Bren taught me to read route-signs," she said at last, to no one, to the dark. "I was eleven. I couldn't reach the marks, so he used to lift me up on his shoulders." Her voice came out strange and flat. "He's in Varn's hands because of me. Because I lit up half the sky like a festival."

"You saved my life," Corin said quietly.

"And told every ear in the mountains who I am." She stared at the mineral veins, at the trapped light in the stone. "Varn will work him over for our route. And when she's done, she'll turn him loose or hang him, whichever amuses her, and either way—" Her throat closed. Either way, the man who had lifted her onto his shoulders was spending his last days in a warden's cellar, and it was her lightning that put him there.

Somewhere far above, muffled by a thousand feet of rock, thunder rolled—the real storm now, arriving late and honest, raining on a mountain that already knew her name.

Corin touched her arm, gentle. "Tamsin. There's something you need to hear. About why Varn wants you alive—and about what your storm-gift actually is." His fever-bright eyes caught the lantern flame. "It's the reason they exiled you. All of it. It starts with the Stormheart—and I think... I think the vault beneath this very mountain is where it began."

Outside, the thunder rolled again, closer now, like something ancient turning over in its sleep.
