## 6. The Price of Old Loyalties

Corin heard the horses before he saw them—a soft iron ringing against stone, carried up the switchbacks on the cold morning air. His fever had burned through the night, leaving him wrung out and strange, the world at the edges of his vision gone soft as wet vellum. He had dozed against a granite shoulder of the pass with Tamsin's oilskin draped over him, and in his half-sleep he dreamed again of the archive: the sealed case under his arm, the sigil burning itself into his palm, Veylan's archivists turning toward him with faces like closed doors.

The hoofbeats were not a dream.

"Tamsin." His voice came out cracked. "Tamsin, we have company."

She was already awake—she was always already awake, he was learning that about her—and standing at the lip of the hollow where they'd sheltered, her body angled into the wind as though she could read it the way other people read letters. He watched her go very still. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of recognition.

"Six riders," she said quietly. "Thornfell tack. Grey cloaks."

"Yours?"

"They aren't mine anymore." But something moved across her face when she said it, something old and tender and wounded, and Corin understood before she spoke another word. These were not strangers riding up the Serpent Stair. These were family.

* * *

They came around the bend of the pass in a loose crescent, six riders on shaggy mountain ponies bred for footing rather than speed, and at their head rode a woman built like the mountain itself—broad through the shoulders, silver-streaked hair braided back from a face that might once have been beautiful and had decided long ago it had better uses. She wore no crown and needed none. The riders flanked her the way water flanks a stone it has shaped for centuries.

Maren. Even Corin, who had spent eleven years among dusty scrolls and had never set foot in Thornfell until this month, knew the name. The smuggler queen of the high passes. Half the contraband in three kingdoms moved through her tollhouses, and the other half paid her to look away.

He expected Tamsin to run. Everything he knew of her—the hard-won invisibility, the vow of silence, the way she flinched from her own name—said she would bolt for the scree slopes and let the mountain hide her. Instead she stepped out of the hollow and stood in the open road, arms loose at her sides, and waited.

"Maren," she said.

"Tamsin Almeida." Maren swung down from her pony with a grunt, and up close Corin saw that age sat on her like weather on a cliff face—not gentle, but honest. Her eyes found Tamsin's and held them, and there was so much history in that look that Corin felt like an intruder reading someone else's letters. "Three years you've been gone from my table. Three years, and the first I hear of you is wardens asking questions in my low camps and lightning splitting the sky over the Serpent Stair."

"The lightning wasn't—"

"Don't." Maren raised one hand, not unkindly. "Don't lie to me, girl. I taught you better lies than that."

Corin shifted in the hollow, and every rider's hand went to a weapon. Maren didn't look at him, but her voice changed, cooled, became the voice of a woman who ran an empire of shadows. "And there he is. The archivist. The one Veylan's court is paying a small kingdom's ransom to recover alive." Now she turned, and her gaze weighed Corin like a merchant weighing ore. "You've cost me two good riders already, boy, shot full of arrows on the Stair. I'd like to know what makes you worth it."

"He's worth nothing to me," Tamsin said quickly. Too quickly. "I found him on the road. I was taking him to Vessa for the fee, nothing more."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then she sighed, and it was the sigh of a mother, and somehow that was worse than anything else she might have done.

"Sit down, both of you," she said. "We'll eat first. What comes after should be said over bread, not over blades."

* * *

They ate at a flat place above the treeline where the riders had made a fire of dwarf-pine, and the bread was dark and dense and tasted of the same woodsmoke Corin imagined filled Maren's tollhouse kitchens. Tamsin sat close enough to touch him and far enough away to deny him, and she did not eat. She turned the bread over and over in her hands like something she was trying to remember how to hold.

Maren talked while they ate—of the passes, of the price of moon-iron since the stars went out, of a warden patrol burned out of the low camps by "some fool calling storms they'd sworn off." She talked the way she must always have talked at her own table, drawing everyone in, and Corin watched Tamsin's shoulders slowly come down from around her ears, watched her almost smile at some old joke about Bren's drinking, and understood with a scholar's precision exactly what was being done to her. This was not cruelty. It was something far more effective. It was home, offered back.

Then the plates were cleared, and Maren folded her hands, and the temperature of the morning dropped.

"I'm going to tell you a thing now, Tamsin, and I want you to hear all of it before you speak." She nodded to one of her riders, a lean man with a ledger-case slung at his hip, and he brought her a document heavy with wax seals. "Three days ago, a courier came up from the lowlands wearing court colors under a trader's coat. He carried this." She let it lie on her knee, unopened. "A contract. Renewal of an arrangement that's been in place since before you were born."

Corin's blood went cold. "An arrangement."

"My tollhouses stay open. My people stay free of the king's gallows. My caravans cross borders no honest caravan crosses." Maren's eyes never left Tamsin. "In exchange, certain cargoes move when I say move, and certain people are found when the crown asks me to find them. You think a woman holds these mountains on charm and moon-iron? Veylan's court has fed me for twenty years, girl. Every road you ever smuggled down, every camp that sheltered you after your exile—I bought them all with their coin."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow-load on a roof. Corin watched Tamsin's face and saw the architecture of her whole life quietly rearranging itself behind her eyes. Every kindness. Every refuge. Every door that had opened just wide enough.

"You knew," Tamsin said at last, and her voice was terribly level. "When I came to you after the exile trial. When I wept at your table. You knew what the court had done to me."

"I suspected." For the first time, something flickered behind Maren's composure—regret, maybe, or its well-worn disguise. "The order for your exile came through channels that don't exist for a girl who refused one order. Someone powerful wanted you broken and scattered, and I wanted to know why. So I kept you close and I kept you ignorant, because ignorance kept you alive." She leaned forward. "Until now. Until you called lightning off the Serpent Stair and put your name back in every ear from here to the capital. And now the court asks me for one thing, Tamsin. One thing to keep twenty years of bargains intact."

"No," Tamsin said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it." But Maren held up her hand, and Tamsin stopped, because some lessons outlast love.

"The archivist," Maren said gently. "Delivered to the warden-captain Ilse Varn at the Grayford tollhouse, alive and whole. That's the price. That's the only price. Hand him over, walk back down to my table, and everything goes on as it was. Your name is mud in the lowlands for a season, and then it isn't. You have a family, Tamsin. You have a bed and a roof and forty people who would die for you. All you have to lose is a stranger you've known five days."

Corin said nothing. There was nothing he could say that wouldn't tip the scale, and he had spent his life believing that scales should be tipped by evidence, not pleading. But his hand found the sealed case at his side, and beneath it his branded palm throbbed in time with his heart, and he thought: *the stars are dying, and I am the last proof, and this woman is being asked to trade the sky for a roof.*

Tamsin looked at him then. Just looked. And whatever passed between them in that moment, it was not gratitude—he hadn't earned gratitude—but recognition. Two people standing in the same wind.

"You're wrong about one thing, Maren," Tamsin said quietly. "You said all I have to lose is a stranger."

"Tamsin—"

"Five days ago I was invisible. I was safe. I was nothing, and nothing is what I told myself I wanted." She stood, slow, like a woman surfacing from deep water. "But I've seen what's under his seal. I've seen what Veylan is doing to the sky, and I've done the arithmetic of what happens to everyone at your table—to Bren, to the children in the low camps—when the last star goes out and the wards fail and whatever the sky-born were holding back comes through. You didn't buy my safety, Maren. You rented it. And the bill just came due for all of us."

Maren rose too, and up close the difference in their heights vanished; the older woman seemed suddenly carved from something less forgiving than stone. "So that's your answer."

"That's my answer." Tamsin's voice broke on it, just barely, just enough that Corin alone, sitting closest, heard the fracture. "You were the only family I had left. You fed me when I was fourteen and wanted to die. I will love you until the day I die for that." She swallowed. "And I am going to walk past you right now, and take him to Vessa, and if your riders stop me I will do things on this mountainside that will make the lightning on the Stair look like a candle."

Nobody moved. The fire popped. Somewhere below, a hawk cried once and fell silent, as if embarrassed.

Maren studied her for a long, long moment—the way, Corin thought, a woman studies a daughter she is deciding whether to mourn. Then she reached into her coat and drew out a knife, and every rider tensed, and Corin's breath stopped—

—and she used it to cut the contract on her knee in half, and then in half again, and let the pieces fall into the fire.

"Varn will be at Grayford by tomorrow dusk with sixty men," Maren said flatly. "You can't take the low road. Take the under-mountain. The old vault roads through the Sleeping Mountain—they're sealed, but seals are just doors that haven't met the right key." She jerked her chin at Tamsin. "You were always the right key, girl. You were born resonating with the deep places. Why do you think I never let you near the vault roads?"

Tamsin stared at her. "You knew about that too."

"I know about everything. It's my curse." Maren turned away, and her voice, when it came, was rougher than the mountain gravel. "Go. Before I change my mind and decide twenty years of bargains are worth more than one stubborn child." She paused, her back to them both. "And Tamsin. If you make it to Vessa—if you actually pull this mad thing off—don't come back and thank me. Just send word that the stars are lit again. That's payment enough."

Tamsin crossed the distance between them in three strides and embraced her, brief and fierce, and Maren endured it the way cliffs endure rain. Then Tamsin pulled Corin to his feet, took up the sealed case, and walked west without looking back.

Corin looked back once. Maren stood alone by the dead fire, watching them go, and her riders had turned their faces away to give her privacy, and Corin—who had spent his life among documents—understood that he had just witnessed the destruction of a record more valuable than anything in the royal archive.

Behind them, far to the east, a horn sounded from the direction of Grayford. One note. Then another. Then a third, closer, from a ridge they could not see.

Ilse Varn had found the trail after all.
