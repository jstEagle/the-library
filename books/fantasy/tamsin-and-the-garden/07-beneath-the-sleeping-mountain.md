## 7. Beneath the Sleeping Mountain

The under-mountain roads had a smell all their own—cold stone and old water, iron in the dark, and beneath it something older still, something that made Tamsin's skin prickle before she could name it. She had smuggled through these deep ways twice before, years ago, hauling moon-iron down from the high diggings while Maren's tolls were watched too closely to risk the surface passes. She remembered them as empty. Dead veins of rock, abandoned by whatever people had first cut them, useful only because no one else wanted them.

They did not feel empty now.

Three days since Maren's riders had let them go—or rather, since Tamsin had chosen Corin over the only family she had left, and Maren had kept her word about one thing only: that she would not stop them herself. The queen of the Thornfell smugglers had simply turned her back, and let the mountain decide what it wanted with them. What the mountain wanted, apparently, was to swallow them whole.

Corin walked badly now. His wound had gone from fever to something worse; he leaned on Tamsin's shoulder for half of every hour, then on the walls, then on nothing at all when his legs refused him and she had to half-carry him along the slick flagstones. He had stopped talking much. When he did talk, it was fragments—the archivist's habit of reaching for precision even as his mind fogged. *The proof is sewn into my coat lining,* he'd said once, staring at nothing. *The seal impressions. The tally of grain shipments to the wardens' garrisons. If I die, burn nothing.* And then he'd slept where he stood, and Tamsin had learned to walk with a sleeping man draped against her side, one hand always free for the knife.

The dark pressed close. Their single lamp threw a circle of amber maybe ten feet wide, and beyond it the tunnels went on in every direction, branching like the roots of some drowned tree. Tamsin navigated by feel and by memory and by the small, stubborn instinct that had kept her alive in the mountains since she was fourteen: which way the air moved, which stone rang hollow, which passage smelled of dead ends and which of distance. The air here moved. Faintly, coldly, steadily—from somewhere ahead, somewhere deeper, as though the mountain itself were breathing in.

Her storm-sense had been quiet since the Serpent Stair. Quiet the way a bruised thing is quiet. But tonight, three days underground, it began to wake—not screaming this time, not warning, but humming. A low resonance, felt more than heard, rising through the soles of her boots like the first tremor of a quake that never quite arrives. She stopped walking. Corin swayed against her.

"Do you feel that?" she asked.

He lifted his head slowly. His eyes were glassy with fever, but they sharpened at the question, sharpened the way a scholar's eyes do when the thing he has spent years studying steps out of the page and stands in front of him.

"Yes," he said. "Oh—" His voice cracked. "Tamsin. Yes. It's stronger here."

*Here.* Which meant there was a *here*. Not just tunnels. A destination. Something down in the dark that answered storms.

She should have turned back. Every survival instinct she owned told her so: wounded man, single lamp, unknown depths, and somewhere above them Ilse Varn's wardens combing the passes with Bren's confession in their hands. The smart move was to find a side gallery, hole up, wait out the hunt, creep back toward Vessa by stages. That was what the old Tamsin—the invisible woman, the careful one, the girl who had spent nine years making herself unfindable—would have done.

But the hum was pulling at something in her blood, and her blood was tired of being prudent.

"Which way?" she asked Corin.

He closed his eyes, listening with whatever part of him still worked properly. "Left," he said finally. "And down. The resonance comes from below. From very far below." A pause. "From the oldest rock."

They went left, and down, and down again, through galleries that grew wider and stranger. The cut stone changed. The smugglers' roads had been rough-hewn, practical, recent by the measure of mountains. These passages were different—walls dressed in blocks fitted so precisely she couldn't have slid a knife-blade between them, carved with lines and spirals that caught the lamplight and seemed to move. And everywhere, everywhere, the same symbol, repeated in friezes worn shallow by uncountable centuries: a circle broken by a jagged line, lightning held inside a wheel.

She knew it. Of course she knew it. She had seen it branded into Corin's ruined palm the night she pulled him off the high road, and she had known it even before that, from the exile-court legends her grandmother used to tell—stories of the Stormheart, the thing that broke the world's first peace. But seeing it here, cut into living rock by hands ten centuries dead, was different from hearing it whispered over a fire. Here it wasn't legend. Here it was architecture. Here it was *everywhere*, the way a god's name is everywhere in a temple.

"The sky-born built this," Corin whispered. He had stopped leaning on her long enough to run his fingers along one of the spiral carvings, reverent, the way other men touched relics. "This isn't human work. Humans don't build like this. Humans don't *think* like this."

"You said they died," Tamsin said. "When the stars went out."

"I said they're dying." He coughed, wet and deep. "There's a difference. There's a difference, and I think—I think we may be standing in the middle of it."

* * *

The passage ended at a door.

Not a door like any door Tamsin had ever seen—a slab of black stone, twenty feet high, seamless except for the great broken-wheel sigil carved at its center, its jagged crack inlaid with something that still glimmered faintly after all this time. Silver, maybe. Or moon-iron, the metal she'd spent years smuggling, the metal that drank light and gave back nothing. Around the doorframe ran an inscription in a script neither of them could read, rows of characters like falling rain given form.

But the door was ajar.

Not broken. Not forced. *Ajar*—swung inward by the width of a hand, as if someone had left it open deliberately, recently, or as if the mountain itself had shifted and unsealed it. Cold air poured through the gap, air that tasted of deep time and, faintly, electrically, of coming weather. Tamsin put her shoulder against the slab and pushed. It moved easier than it had any right to, groaning once on hinges of some mechanism she couldn't see, and the darkness beyond opened up around them like a mouth.

They stepped through into a vault.

The lamp was almost useless against it. The chamber was vast—vast past sense, its ceiling lost in blackness overhead, its floor a polished sweep of black stone that reflected their little flame back at them doubled and tripled until they seemed to stand on a lake of fireflies. Pillars rose around them in ranks, dozens of them, each carved top to bottom with figures. And along the far wall, running the whole length of the chamber, stretched a mural.

Tamsin walked toward it because there was nothing else to do. Her boots echoed. The hum in her bones deepened with every step, and by the time she stood before the painted stone, it had become a kind of pressure, a hand laid flat against her sternum from the inside.

The mural told a story. She could read it without reading it, the way you can read a picture that wants to be understood.

It began with stars. Hundreds of them, silver-white against a deep blue field, and among the stars flew figures—winged, luminous, tall, their bodies trailing light like comets' tails. They descended toward a green world, toward small brown human figures who looked up at them without fear. In the second panel, the winged ones knelt among the humans, teaching: hands extended over fields, over rivers, over children. In the third, they raised something together—a great heart of storm and light, a sphere banded in silver, cradled between human hands and winged hands alike. The Stormheart. Made together. Held together.

Then the fourth panel, and Tamsin's breath went out of her.

In the fourth panel, a human figure—crowned, robed, rendered larger than life in the manner of kings—reached up alone and tore a piece from the Stormheart. The sphere cracked. Light spilled from the wound like blood. And around the crowned thief, in a ring, the winged figures recoiled, their faces—faces, real faces, grief-struck and furious and *afraid*—turned toward the viewer across a thousand years.

They had faces. That was the thing. Ten years of exile, a childhood of border stories, a war's worth of propaganda, and every tale she'd ever heard had made the sky-born into shapes without souls—monsters, tempests, the wrath of heaven given claws. Court painters drew them as storms with teeth. Mothers in the Thornfell valleys warned children against them the way they warned against wolves.

But here, in the oldest place, in the deepest stone, painted by hands that had actually looked upon them, the sky-born had faces like her own. Sorrow like her own. The fifth panel showed them leaving—one last procession rising from the green world back toward the stars, looking back, always looking back—and the sixth showed the world dimming, the fields browning, the rivers thinning, the stars themselves beginning to go out one by one, each extinguished star painted with a small winged figure folded around it like a mourner.

Like a coffin. Each star was a coffin.

"They weren't monsters," Tamsin said aloud, to no one, to the dark, to herself. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the vault's immensity. "They were guardians. They were *guardians*, and we stole from them, and we've been telling our children they were monsters ever since so no one would ask what we took."

Corin was laughing, or crying, or both—it was hard to tell, with him, hard to tell with scholars. "The wards," he managed. "Do you understand? The border wards, the mountain wards, the wards around every city—they were never ours. We never built them. The sky-born wove them as a covenant, anchored to the Stormheart, a promise: *we will hold the borders of your world safe, if you hold the heart unbroken.* And when Veylan's ancestors tore the shard loose—" he pressed his palm, the shattered seal, flat against the mural's fourth panel, against the crowned thief, "—they didn't just wound the sky-born. They took the keystone. They made the wards *theirs*. Every ward in every kingdom runs on stolen power. Veylan's crown is the lock, and this—" he gestured at everything, the vault, the murals, the black door, the hum, "—this is what was locked away. This is what they've spent ten generations making sure no one ever saw."

Tamsin stood very still.

Nine years ago, she had refused to call a storm down on a village of farmers, and they had called it treason, and she had run, and she had told herself for nine years that she understood why. Ambition. Fear. A king who wanted obedience. Simple things, explicable things, the ordinary machinery of tyranny.

It had never once occurred to her that her refusal had been *dangerous*—not politically dangerous, personally dangerous, dangerous to a specific plan. That a storm-caller who wouldn't kill civilians was a loose thread in a tapestry woven from stolen weather. That the court hadn't exiled her despite her gift. They had exiled her *because* of it, because a storm-gift that answered to conscience instead of crown was a flaw in the keystone's architecture, a reminder written in lightning that the power Veylan wielded had originally answered to something better.

Her whole life. Her trial, her flight, her years of moon-iron and false names and learning to be no one—all of it, someone else's chess move. Someone in a palace she'd never seen had looked at a girl who could call storms and thought: *that one must be removed, gently, quietly, before she becomes a question.*

The vertigo of it nearly put her on her knees.

* * *

It was Corin who noticed the last thing. He was lying on the cold floor by then, fever dragging him down, but his eyes were still working, still cataloguing, still the archivist even now. He pointed upward, at the vault's ceiling, at the darkness above the pillars where the lamp couldn't reach.

"The dome," he said. "Look at the dome."

She looked. She had to climb onto a fallen pillar-shard to see it clearly, and when she did, she found the ceiling painted as a night sky—stars in silver leaf, hundreds of them, arranged in constellations she half-recognized from the skies of her childhood. And at the dome's center, directly above the chamber's heart, one star burned larger than all the rest, set with a socket of empty stone.

A star was missing from the ceiling. One star, pried out, its socket empty, its absence framed by a ring of soot-black scorching—as if something had been torn from that spot with heat and violence, long ago, and the vault had recorded the theft the way a body records a wound.

"That's it," Corin whispered. "That's the shard's place. That's where it lived before it was taken. The heart of the ceiling, the heart of the Stormheart, the heart of the wards—all one point. Everything turns on that one missing piece." He coughed again, longer this time, and when the cough passed he was shaking. "Veylan wears it. In the crown. They say no blade can touch him while he wears it, that the wards themselves defend him. Because they do. They're *his* now. Stolen things defending the thief."

Tamsin climbed down from the pillar-shard and stood in the center of the chamber, directly beneath the empty socket, and the hum in her bones became something else entirely.

It became recognition.

Her storm-gift had always been a mystery to her—an accident of birth, a quirk of blood, the reason for her ruin. The court mages had tested her as a child and pronounced her a natural conductor, a rare vessel, and she had believed them, because what else does a child believe? But standing in this place, beneath the wound in the sky-dome, she felt the truth of it settle over her like cold water: her gift was not an accident. It was a *resonance*. The storm-callers were not a family or a bloodline or a curse. They were echoes. Living remnants of the covenant, human vessels tuned to the same frequency as the Stormheart, the way a harp string hums when its note is played across the room. The sky-born had made them—or left them, seeded through the human population like sparks banked in ash—so that someone, someday, might be able to reach the heart again.

Someone like her.

The vault knew it. The vault was answering her. As she stood there, the hum climbing through her ribs, the air around her began to taste of ozone, of the charged hush before lightning, and fine white light gathered along the carved spirals of the nearest pillars, tracing the ancient lines like dawn creeping along a mountain ridge. The dead carvings were waking. The room was responding to her presence the way a bell responds to the right voice.

Corin watched from the floor, feverish and gray, and made a sound that was half wonder and half terror. "It knows you," he said. "Tamsin—it *knows* you."

And that was when the lamp flickered.

Not the oil. Not the wind. The flame bent sideways, all at once, and guttered, and in the half-dark the white light along the pillars brightened, and Tamsin felt her gift rise in her chest like a tide—unasked, uncontrollable, the first time in nine years that the storm had come to *her* instead of being summoned. Static crawled over her skin. Her hair lifted from her braids. Somewhere in the blackness overhead, in the lost reaches of the vault's dome, thunder rolled—a deep, impossible, indoor thunder, the sound of weather where no weather could be.

The vault was breathing with her. The vault was calling the storm *with* her.

And in that moment, with lightning gathering in a room a thousand feet underground, Tamsin understood the terrible shape of the choice waiting for her. The shard in Veylan's crown could be reached. The wards could be unwoven. The stars could be relit. But the price of touching the Stormheart's broken heart was written into the murals themselves, into every grieving face and every coffined star: the covenant demanded everything of whoever came to mend it. The heart did not lend its power. It *spent* its vessels.

The thunder rolled again, closer, and the white light on the pillars flared—

—and at the far end of the chamber, faint as a candle seen through a keyhole, the great black door they had entered through swung another inch wider on its unseen hinges.

Something else had heard the storm.

Something was coming down the under-mountain roads behind them, following the resonance the way wolves follow blood, and Tamsin's storm-sense—the sense that had screamed warnings across nine years of exile—now sang one note, clear and cold and certain:

They were no longer alone in the dark.
