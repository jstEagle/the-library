## 8. The Traitor's Road

Corin woke to the taste of copper and the smell of wet stone.

For a long moment he did not know where he was—only that the dark around him was not the dark of the archive vaults in Veylan's citadel, where he had spent eleven years among sealed ledgers and the dust of inconvenient truths. This dark breathed. It dripped somewhere far off, patient as a metronome, and it was cold in the way the under-mountain was cold: a cold that had never once been interrupted by the sun.

Then the pain arrived, and with it, memory.

He was lying against the wall of a side gallery off the great vault road, wrapped in Tamsin's oilcloth cloak, and his left side—the side where Ilse Varn's warden-blade had opened him from hip to ribs on the night the stars went out—was on fire. Not the clean fire of a fresh wound, either. This was the deep, banked burn of rot setting in, the heat of blood going wrong inside him. He knew this fire. He had catalogued its symptoms once, in a medical treatise he'd copied as an apprentice archivist, back when such knowledge had been abstract and other people's.

*Fever. The flush of the skin. The sweetish smell beneath the sweat.*

*Blood-poisoning. Four days, perhaps five, untreated.*

He had been walking for three.

"Tamsin." His voice came out a ruin, barely more than breath. He tried again. "Tamsin."

She was there before the second attempt finished—a shadow resolving out of shadows, kneeling beside him, her hand cool on his forehead and then, with a small sound she tried to swallow, on his cheek. Even in the blackness he could feel her stillness, the particular quality of attention she gave things: the storm-caller's habit of reading pressure changes in everything, including people.

"You're burning," she said.

"I know what I am." He managed something that was meant to be a smile. "I wrote the chapter on it."

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Don't do that. Don't make jokes about dying in a hole in the ground while I'm trying to save your life."

"Is anyone trying?" He coughed, and the cough hurt so badly that for a moment the world went white at the edges. When it cleared, she had pressed a waterskin to his lips, and he drank greedily, shamelessly, because whatever dignity remained to him was going to be spent on honesty tonight. "Tamsin. Listen to me. We need to talk about what happens if I don't make it to Vessa."

"We're making it to Vessa."

"The proof has to reach the heartstone chamber." He said it slowly, because fever made words slippery and this above all could not slip. "The shard-proof. Sealed in my blood-signature, keyed to the heartstone itself. That was the whole design—I built it that way when I stole it, because anything less could be forged, dismissed, burned. But the seal only opens to a direct transfer. Skin to stone. And if I die on the road—" He stopped. Swallowed. "If I die on the road, the proof dies with me. Unless someone else can carry it in."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, water dripped its patient count.

"Someone else," Tamsin repeated.

"A storm-caller." He found her wrist in the dark and held it, feeling the pulse there, quick and strong, and hated how much comfort he took from it. "The heartstone recognizes resonance. It always did—that's why Veylan's family kept the shard hidden behind wards only their bloodline could pass. But you've felt it already. In the vault. Don't lie to me, Tamsin Almeida; I watched those pillars light up when you were angry. The Stormheart knows you. If I can't walk through Vessa's gates, *you* have to be the one who carries it into the heartstone chamber."

"I broke my vow to call one storm," she said quietly. "One. To save your life on the Stair. You want me to walk into the heart of the thing that ruined mine and use what's left of it."

"I want you to live in a world where the stars come back," Corin said. "I want the sky-born to stop dying alone in the dark while a usurper wears their stolen heart as a crown. And yes—I want you to be free of it afterward, all of it, every chain. But freedom isn't won by hiding, Tamsin. Believe me. I hid for eleven years in the archive stacks, and all my hiding bought was the certainty that no one would ever come looking until it was too late."

Her pulse jumped under his fingers. She didn't answer. Above them, faint through fathoms of rock, thunder rolled—and Corin understood, with the strange clarity that fever grants, that it wasn't natural weather. It was her gift answering her grief, the way a dog answers a whistle.

* * *

They moved at first light—or what passed for it down here, the grey seepage through ventilation shafts that told them the sun existed somewhere above. Tamsin half-carried him along the vault road, his arm over her shoulders, her body warm and stubborn against his failing side. She had found willow bark and something bitter she called crowsbane growing in the damp near a spring, chewed it into a paste, and packed it into the wound with hands that did not shake even though her jaw did.

"It's not enough," he told her, because he owed her truth. "It buys time. A day, maybe two."

"Then we have two days. Vessa is four."

"Three, if we take the Shepherd's Cut instead of the low road."

She stopped walking. In the grey gloom he saw her face turn toward him, and there was something in it he hadn't seen before—not fear exactly. Recognition. "The Shepherd's Cut is a goat track above the tree line. In winter. With wardens hunting us."

"And the low road," Corin said gently, "is where they'll be waiting."

She stared at him. "How could you possibly—"

"Bren."

The name dropped between them like a stone into a well. They both heard it fall, and neither spoke for a long moment, because saying it aloud made it real: old Bren, who had shared their fire for nine years of smuggling runs, who had taught Tamsin which passes froze first and which tollhouses could be trusted, who had stood on the Serpent Stair and held the wardens off with nothing but a knife and his own broad body so that they could escape into the caves.

Bren, who had been captured covering their retreat.

"They took him to the tollhouse at Greywater," Corin said. His voice was steadier than he felt; fever had burned away everything in him but the facts, and the facts were all he had left to give. "Ilse Varn doesn't interrogate prisoners in field camps. She moves them somewhere with walls and hot irons and time. Greywater is the nearest place with walls. And Bren—" He hesitated, hating himself for it. "Bren is brave, Tamsin. Brave men break fastest, because they break trying to protect other people. He'll have held out just long enough to learn that silence gets you killed slower than speech, and then he'll have given her the route. Not because he wanted to. Because she asked him a question he couldn't survive not answering."

"You don't know that."

"I was an archivist for the crown for eleven years." He said it flatly. "I read the interrogation ledgers. I filed them. I know exactly what Ilse Varn's questions look like when they're finished with a man."

Tamsin said nothing. But he felt the change in her, the way you feel a storm front arrive—not a sound or a sight but a pressure, a tightening, the whole air of her going taut. Then she adjusted his weight across her shoulders and turned them off the vault road onto a narrow ascending passage that smelled of frost and old sheep dung, and Corin understood that she had chosen the Shepherd's Cut without another word, choosing to believe him, choosing the harder road because the easy one might be a trap.

That, more than anything, was when he understood he would follow this woman anywhere, even if it killed him.

*Especially* if it killed him.

* * *

They came up out of the mountain near dusk, and the world above was wrong.

Corin had known, intellectually, that the stars were gone—he had helped steal the proof of why, after all—but knowing and seeing were different countries. The sky over Thornfell was a smooth black lid, starless and moonless, and beneath it the peaks stood pale as bone. Snowfields glowed without any light to glow with. The whole landscape looked like a painting of itself, painted by someone who had forgotten the most important detail.

And below—far below, spread through the valley passes like spilled ink—

"Gods," Tamsin whispered.

Torches. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A river of fire winding down the western valleys, camp-fires multiplying in the dark like a rash, and beyond them, where the foothills flattened toward the plains, the vast dim sprawl of a mustering army. Siege engines sat under canvas like sleeping beasts. Supply trains crawled the roads south. Banners he could not read at this distance but did not need to: the black-and-gold of Veylan's crown, massed for the assault on the free cities.

Vessa lay three days' ride beyond that army. Everything between here and there belonged to the king now.

"He's not chasing us anymore," Corin said. The fever made his voice dreamlike; he heard himself as if from a distance. "He doesn't need to. He knows what we carry and where we're going, so he simply puts everything he has between us and the gates. We're not fugitives now, Tamsin. We're a message trying to cross a battlefield."

"Then we cross it." She crouched beside him, and in the lightless dark he heard rather than saw her resolve settle over her like armor. "There are smuggler paths through the army's flank. Old ones. Paths the king's maps don't show because my people made sure they never got drawn. If we move at night—"

"If we move at all, with my leg like this, we move at a crawl."

"Then we crawl."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough folded him nearly double against her shoulder. When the spasm passed, he was shaking, and the shaking wasn't entirely the cold. There was something else he had been carrying since the vault, since the pillars lit at her anger, and he found, with the last of his hoarded strength, that he could not carry it into battle unspoken.

"Tamsin. There's something you should know. About why Veylan wants us so badly. Why it was *me* they sent Ilse Varn for, personally, the night the stars went out."

She went very still beside him.

"The proof I stole—it isn't just documents. Documents can be denied. What I carry is a fragment of the original seal-work, taken from the vault where the Stormheart was first broken. It resonates, Tamsin. It sings, if you have the ears for it. And when I touched it, the first time, in the deep archive—" His breath caught. "It showed me things. The war. The theft. And a prophecy the old archivists sealed away and called superstition: *when the shard burns brightest, a child of the storm will stand before the heartstone, and the choice of the world will rest in her two hands.*"

The wind moved along the ridge above them, carrying snow-smell and the distant woodsmoke of ten thousand enemy fires.

"I used to think the prophecy was about some future storm-caller," Corin whispered. "Some hero not yet born. But I've watched you, Tamsin. In the caves, on the Stair, in the vault. I don't think it's about someone yet to come." He lifted his eyes to hers, burning with fever and certainty in equal measure. "I think the reason Veylan extinguished the stars the very night you refused to kill for him—the reason your exile was engineered nine years ago, the reason every road of your life bends toward Vessa—is that the prophecy was always about *you*. And he has known it longer than you have."

Far below, a horn sounded from the army's edge—one long note, then another, then a third—and along the valley floor, torches began to move.

Not south, toward Vessa.

Up. Toward the pass. Toward them.

Tamsin rose slowly to her feet beside him, and Corin felt the air change the way it always changed now when her gift woke—the pressure dropping, the fine hairs on his arms lifting, the world holding its breath.

"They found us," she said.

"No," Corin answered, and reached for her hand. "They found *us*, Tamsin. Which means we finally get to choose the ground."
