## 9. Stormheart

The gates of Vessa rose out of the last grey mile like the ribs of some drowned god—white stone veined with heartlight, older than any kingdom, older, Corin had sworn, than the war that had broken the sky. Tamsin had seen them once before, from a distance, in the years when she was still someone's daughter and not yet someone's exile. Up close they were worse. Up close she could feel them humming, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has died, and beneath the hum something vast and wounded turning over in its sleep.

"They know we're coming," Bren said.

He was limping badly. Three days out of Ilse Varn's cells, three days since he'd appeared at the mouth of the under-mountain road half-starved and grey-faced, saying only *I got loose* and nothing else, and Tamsin had been too relieved to ask what loosening had cost him. Now his left hand wouldn't close around his staff properly, and he kept touching his ribs as though checking that they were still where he'd left them.

"Of course they know," Tamsin said. "Ilse Varn has known our route since before we did."

"Then why are we walking toward her?"

"Because there's nowhere else." She said it flatly, because anything softer would have cracked. "The proof dies with Corin if it doesn't reach the heartstone. And the heartstone is behind those gates."

Corin said nothing. He hadn't said much for two days. The fever had him by the throat now; she could see it in the shine of his skin, the way his eyes took too long to find things. He walked between them with the satchel strapped across his chest like a second heartbeat, and every few steps his hand went to it—not guarding it anymore, she thought, just reassuring himself it was still real.

The road to the gates was empty. That was the worst part. No farmers, no carts, no pilgrims. Veylan's armies were massed somewhere below the foothills, a darkness on the land you could smell—woodsmoke and iron and the sour stink of too many men camped in one place—but here there was only wind moving through the high grass, and the three of them, and the gates.

And then the wardens came out of the grass.

They rose the way wraiths rise in bad stories, a dozen of them, then two dozen, grey-cloaked, ward-lanterns swinging at their belts, and at their center, unhurried as a woman crossing her own courtyard, walked Ilse Varn.

"Tamsin Almeida." Her voice carried without effort, the way a blade carries light. "You've made this very easy. I want you to know I appreciate that. I've chased you across half the Thornfells, and in all that time you never once did the sensible thing."

"Ilse." Tamsin stopped walking. Beside her, Bren's breath went ragged. Behind her, she could hear Corin sway and catch himself. "Let us pass."

"Pass?" Ilse smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind that had opened doors for her for twenty years. "You're carrying stolen property of the crown into the last free city in the world. You're carrying a dead man and a dead man's papers. What I'm going to do is take all three of you back down the mountain, and His Majesty is going to be so pleased with me that perhaps he'll let me sleep indoors again." She tilted her head. "Or—and I confess this is my preference—you can put down the satchel, walk away, and live. You've spent nine years being no one, Tamsin. It suits you. Why stop now?"

Tamsin felt the old fear rise in her, the fear that had kept her alive through nine years of small roads and smaller lies: *be invisible, be nothing, be weather.* And beneath it, for the first time since the night the stars went out, she felt something else answer it.

Her storm-sense had come back on the road that morning. Not the full gift—she wasn't sure the full gift existed anymore, not with the sky strangled the way it was—but a low pressure behind her eyes, a taste of iron on the air, the mountain's blood moving. The wards on the gate were starving. She could feel that too. Whatever fed Vessa's heartstone was running thin, and thin things break.

"You're right," she said. "It does suit me."

She heard Bren shift beside her. Heard the small, wrong sound of steel leaving cloth.

"Bren—" she started, and stopped, because old Bren, sixty-year-old Bren, smuggler Bren, who had taught her which mushrooms lied and which passes breathed, had drawn his short blade and stepped past her, out onto the open road, between her and the wardens.

"Boy," Bren said—to Corin, not looking back—"run when she runs. Don't think. Run."

"Bren, what are you doing?"

"What I should've done in the tollhouse." He rolled his shoulders, and Tamsin saw, with a clarity that would stay with her the rest of her life, how old he looked. How tired. "They had me three days, girl. Three days in a room with a man whose job is questions. And in three days I learned everything I needed to know about King Veylan's men." He spat, neatly, to the side. "I raised thieves. We steal from the rich and lie to everyone, but we don't—we don't *hollow people out*. Whatever's wearing that crown isn't a king. It's a hole where a king used to be."

"Ilse Varn," Tamsin said quickly, stepping forward, hands open, buying seconds, "you don't need him. He's an old man, he's worth nothing to—"

"He's worth a lesson," Ilse said, and flicked two fingers.

The wardens moved. And Bren—old, limping, half-broken Bren—met them like a landslide.

He fought the way he'd done everything in his life: economically, viciously, without wasted motion or wasted breath. He took the first warden's legs out and put his knife through the gap in the man's gorget before the body finished falling. He caught a ward-lantern on his forearm and didn't even grunt, just turned the burn into a hook that dragged its owner off balance. For ten seconds—ten whole seconds—he was the terror of the Thornfell passes, the man whose name mothers used to hush their children with, and Tamsin stood frozen because she had never, not once, seen him fight like this, had never guessed what all that dry humor and mushroom-lore had been built on top of.

"GO," he roared, and his voice broke in the middle of it, and that was when Tamsin understood that he knew. He knew exactly how this ended. He had known since he drew the blade.

Corin grabbed her arm. "Tamsin—"

She ran.

She hated herself for it with every stride, and she ran anyway, because Bren had bought her those strides with the only coin he had left, and wasting them would be the second betrayal. Behind her she heard steel, heard shouting, heard Ilse Varn's voice crack out of its pleasant register for the first time—*"Stop them, STOP THEM"*—and then she heard the deep, grinding groan of the gates of Vessa answering a knock they had waited nine years to hear.

Because Corin had fallen against them, both palms flat to the white stone, and the shattered seal on his hand—the sigil of the Stormheart, branded into his flesh—was burning.

The gates remembered him. Or they remembered what he carried. Either way, the stone parted along a seam no eye could find, groaning like a waking thing, and Tamsin shoved Corin through into the dark beyond and turned for one last look back down the road.

Bren was down on one knee. There were four wardens around him and he was still moving, still swinging, and Ilse Varn was walking toward him with her sword already clear, and Tamsin met the old man's eyes across forty yards of empty road.

He grinned at her. Gap-toothed, unrepentant, the same grin he'd given her the day she'd botched her first fence deal at fourteen.

"Everywhere, girl," he called. "I told you. Everywhere."

Then the wardens closed, and Tamsin turned away, and the gates ground shut behind her like a held breath finally let go.

* * *

The heartstone chamber was at the center of everything.

They descended through the guts of the city—corridors of white stone lit by veins of failing blue light, past vaults and stairwells and halls where the architecture stopped making sense, rooms built at angles no human hand would choose—and Corin guided her by fever and memory together, mumbling directions he shouldn't have known, until the corridor opened and they were standing inside the mountain's heart.

It was a sphere of open air, vast as a cathedral turned inside out, and at its center hung the heartstone: a shard of crystal the size of a grown man, suspended in nothing, drinking light. Even from the far side of the chamber Tamsin could feel it pulling at her—the way it pulled at everything, at the wards, at the sky, at the dying stars themselves—a great greedy wound in the world, wearing a king's crown of gold filigree at its peak.

Veylan's work. Veylan's ancestors' work. Nine generations of theft, nailed shut with gold.

"It has to be broken from inside the resonance," Corin whispered. He was sliding down the wall; she caught him, lowered him gently, propped him against the stone. His eyes were bright and terrible with fever. "A storm-caller. It was always a storm-caller. The vault murals—the guardians chose one of us, every generation, to keep the balance. Your gift isn't a weapon, Tamsin. It never was. It's a *key*." His hand found hers and pressed the satchel into it. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I looked for another way for six years, and there wasn't one."

"There never is," Tamsin said. "That's the joke."

She knelt beside him a moment longer. Then she stood, and opened the satchel, and took out the proof that had cost an archivist his life and an old smuggler his and was about to cost her everything else: a fragment of black crystal wrapped in oilcloth, cold as a dead star, humming with the same hungry note as the heartstone above it.

The shard. A splinter of the original Stormheart, pried loose and carried out of the royal vaults in a dead archivist's coat.

She walked out onto the narrow bridge of stone that reached toward the hanging crystal, and the pull hit her fully for the first time, and it *knew* her. The shard flared against her palms. The storm-gift—the gift she had refused to use on civilians, the gift she had starved for nine years, the gift that had gotten her exiled and hunted and hollowed—rose in her like a tide answering the moon, and for one vertiginous moment she understood everything: the wards, the sky-born, the extinguished stars, the shape of the cage the world had been living in.

The sky-born weren't monsters. They were the *weather itself*, the living storm, and Veylan's line had caged them in crystal and called the cage a crown.

"Come home," Tamsin whispered—to the shard, to the caged sky, to all of it. "I'll pay the toll."

She poured everything in.

There are no words for what it cost, because words belong to the person who comes out the other side, and the woman who walked onto that bridge did not entirely come out. She gave it the lightning she'd sworn off and the thunder she'd hoarded and the nine years of small careful nothing, gave it the child who'd first felt the storm-sense at seven years old and the exile who'd buried it at twenty-two, gave it Bren's grin and Maren's lullabies and every secret she'd ever kept to survive. The shard screamed. The heartstone screamed louder. Cracks raced across the crystal like white rivers, and the gold filigree at its peak burned away like frost in sunlight, and the last thing Tamsin felt of her own gift was a hand—the first kind hand the storm had ever extended—closing gently around hers before letting go.

Then the chamber filled with light, and wings, and a sound like the whole sky inhaling after nine years underwater.

* * *

She woke on the bridge with Corin's coat over her and stars coming back outside.

She felt them before she saw them—no, that was wrong. That was the point. She felt *nothing*. The pressure behind her eyes was gone. The iron taste, the mountain's blood, the low constant conversation she'd had with the weather since childhood—all of it, silent. She lay still a long moment, listening to the absence the way an amputee listens to a missing limb.

Then she crawled to the chamber's edge, where the wall opened onto the night, and looked up.

Stars. Hundreds. Thousands. Pouring back into the sky like water into a dry riverbed, and among them—moving, wheeling, *descending*—shapes of light and storm, vast and gentle, the sky-born coming home to a world that had finally stopped stealing from them.

Corin was asleep against the wall, breathing, fever-broken, alive. Somewhere above, the free cities would be spilling into their streets. Somewhere below, Veylan's armies would be looking up at a sky that no longer belonged to their king.

Somewhere on the road to Vessa, an old man was lying in the grass who would never see any of it.

Tamsin sat in the doorway between the mountain and the returned night, an ordinary woman with ordinary hands, and wept for all of it at once—the victory, the cost, and the strange, terrifying freedom waiting for her in the morning, when she would have to decide, for the first time in her life, exactly who she wanted to be.
