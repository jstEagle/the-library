## 10. An Ordinary Woman Under Open Stars

The council hall of Vessa had been built to hold a thousand voices, and on the morning of the third testimony it held every one of them in silence.

Tamsin stood at the back, beneath the gallery's stone ribs, where the light from the great oculus fell in a pale column and the dust turned slowly like snow that had forgotten which way was down. She had been offered a seat—three seats, actually, each more prominent than the last—but she had declined them all with a politeness that had begun to worry the heralds. So she stood among the clerks and the couriers and the fishwives who had come up from the harbor to see for themselves, and she watched the sky-born give their testimony.

There were seven of them. When the stars had come back—when Tamsin had shattered the stolen shard in the heartstone chamber and felt her storm-gift tear out of her like a splinter drawn from living bone—the sky-born had returned with them. Not all. The chamber of the heartstone kept a ledger of names now, read aloud each dawn by archivists who wept as they wrote: *returned, returned, not returned.* But seven had crossed the veil between starlight and stone, and they came to the council hall wrapped in light the color of high-altitude ice, and when they spoke, their voices did not echo so much as resonate, the way thunder resonates in the chest before the ears hear anything at all.

The eldest of them stood at the center of the floor. Her name was Aelir, and she was old beyond any human counting, though she wore the shape of a woman no older than Tamsin herself. She spoke of the first war—not the way the histories told it, all banners and heroism, but the way it had actually been: guardians betrayed, wards enslaved, a shard torn from the Stormheart and hammered into a crown so that one bloodline could hold the sky hostage.

"Your king," Aelir said, and her voice made the oculus hum, "did not steal power. He inherited theft. There is a difference only in the telling."

Below, King Veylan's advocates sat very still. Veylan himself had fled the city nine days after the stars returned, when three of his wardens' garrisons opened their gates without a fight and the fourth burned its own warrant-rolls in the courtyard. They said he had gone east, toward the Ashen Reaches, where even the restored sky-born could not yet see. They said other things too, less useful and more satisfying. Tamsin did not listen to those.

She watched instead the faces of the ordinary people—the fishwife two rows ahead of her, who had lost a son to the wardens; the young clerk whose quill shook as he recorded; Bren's cousin, who had traveled down from Thornfell for this and sat with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral she was determined not to cry through.

Bren. His name had been read into the record on the first day, along with the manner of his death: he had turned his blade against the men he had sold them to, once he saw what they truly were, and bought Tamsin eleven minutes at the Vessa gates with his life. Eleven minutes. She had counted them afterward, obsessively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Eleven minutes to carry Corin through the gate, down the heart-stair, into the chamber where everything ended and everything began.

Some nights she still woke reaching for the lightning that wasn't there anymore. Her hand would rise on its own, fingers spread, waiting for the familiar pressure behind her sternum—that coiled, singing readiness—and find nothing but an ordinary heartbeat, steady and dumb and mortal. The first time it happened she had lain awake until dawn, grieving. Now, weeks later, she mostly just noticed it, the way you notice an amputated limb aching in cold weather, and let the feeling pass through her and out.

Aelir finished speaking. The council chamber erupted—not chaos, but something more frightening: consensus. Motion after motion passed. The wardens' order dissolved. The border wards would be rebuilt *with* the sky-born rather than against them. And then the Speaker rose, a broad woman named Odile Ferran with grey in her braid and a reputation for never wasting words, and said:

"There is one more matter. The one called Tamsin Almeida, exile of the Thornfell courts, breaker of the shard. Let her be brought forward."

Every head in the gallery turned. Tamsin felt the attention settle over her like a weight of wet wool.

*Here it is,* she thought. *The new cage.*

They meant it kindly. That was what made it dangerous. Odile Ferran spoke of honors—a seat on the council itself, or if she preferred action, command of the new Wardens' Accord, the rebuilt order that would stand between the free cities and whatever Veylan might become in the east. The sky-born offered something stranger: Aelir descended from the floor and stood before Tamsin in the column of dusty light, and said, quietly, so that only the front rows heard:

"The Stormheart remembers what it lost. What was taken from you can be regrown, given time. Given willingness. You could call storms again, Tamsin Almeida. Real ones. Ours."

The hall held its breath.

And Tamsin—who had spent her whole life being someone else's instrument, first the exile-courts' weapon, then Veylan's scapegoat, then Maren's courier, then the shard-proof's unwilling vessel—looked at the offer hanging in the air like a held note, and found that she wanted none of it.

Not because the gift had been cruel. It hadn't, always. There had been mornings on the high passes when calling lightning had felt like laughing. But every gift she had ever been given had come with hands attached—hands that guided, hands that gripped, hands that spent her like coin. For the first time since she was a child standing in a court that had already decided what she was for, nobody was deciding anything for her. The emptiness behind her sternum wasn't a wound. It was a door, and it was open, and outside it was just—weather. Ordinary weather. Rain that belonged to no one.

"I'm honored," she said, and her voice carried further than she expected in the hush. "But no."

A ripple went through the chamber. Aelir studied her with eyes like frozen lakes, and then—Tamsin would remember this all her life—the sky-born *inclined her head*, the way one warrior salutes another.

"Then take this instead," Aelir said. "When the sky-born look down and see a woman walking alone on the mountain roads, we will know you. And we will keep the weather gentle where you sleep."

It wasn't a title. It was better. It was permission to be nobody, witnessed by the oldest powers alive.

* * *

Corin found her that evening on the wall above the harbor, where the stones still held the day's warmth and the fishing boats were coming home with lamps lit at their prows. He walked better now. The fever had broken a fortnight past, and though the wound in his side had left him thin and pale as winter paper, the archivist's stoop was gone from his shoulders. He had started carrying himself like a man instead of a message.

He lowered himself onto the wall beside her with a grunt, careful of the scar, and followed her gaze out to the water. Above the harbor, the stars were coming out one by one—real stars, steady and ancient, and among them seven points that burned a little brighter than the rest, a little warmer, like windows lit for someone expected home.

"They voted you a statue today," he said. "Behind your back. Odile Ferran argued it down to a plaque. I think you should be flattered."

"I'll deface the plaque."

"That's the spirit." He was quiet a moment. The tide breathed below them. "You know they'll ask again. The council. Aelir. Offers don't die politely in cities like this—they just wait."

"Let them wait." Tamsin pulled her cloak closer. The evening wind off the water was cool and smelled of salt and tar and, faintly, of the mountains far to the north, though that part was surely her imagination. "I've spent my whole life being asked to be something. Weapon. Smuggler. Martyr. Hero, apparently, now. I'd like to try being nothing for a while and see how it fits."

"It fits you," Corin said simply. "Better than the armor ever did."

She glanced at him. In the lamplight coming up from the harbor road his face was half shadow, and there was something in it she had learned to read on the long road from Thornfell—the careful way he looked at her when he was about to say something true. He had nearly died twice carrying her secret down a mountain. He had confessed, feverish and grey-lipped in a cave beneath the Sleeping Mountain, that the proof in his blood might kill him faster than the wardens would. He had lived anyway. People kept doing that around her, against all odds, and she was learning—slowly, suspiciously, like a stray learning to trust a hand—to be glad.

"And you?" she asked. "Back to the archives? They'll build you a wing. *Corin Vale, Restorer of Records.*"

"Gods, no." He laughed, and coughed, and laughed again. "I spent six years in the under-vaults of a usurper's palace reading lies in beautiful handwriting. I want air. Distance. Roads I haven't memorized." He paused, and the careful look came back. "Actually. There's a commission. The new accord wants the old guardian-shrines surveyed—all of them, every pass and valley where the sky-born kept watch before the war. Someone has to walk those roads and write down what's true. It pays badly. It's months of nothing but walking."

"Sounds terrible," Tamsin said.

"Doesn't it."

The tide turned below them. One of the brighter stars—the one the archivists had tentatively assigned to Aelir—stood directly overhead now, and Tamsin felt its light on her face like a hand resting briefly on her hair, gentle as promised weather.

"So where will you go?" Corin asked. "First. When you leave."

Tamsin considered it properly, because it deserved consideration. North, to Thornfell, to sit awhile with Bren's cousin and tell her stories about a grumpy old man who complained about the weather right up until the moment he died defending two fools he loved. West, to the tollhouse, to stand at Maren's door—not to forgive, not yet, maybe not ever, but to say *I survived what you taught me to survive, and I am more than it*. East, eventually, toward the Ashen Reaches, because somewhere out there Veylan was learning to live in the dark, and the world would need guides who knew how storms used to feel. Everywhere the shrines waited, and the roads between them, and the long slow work of writing down what was true.

"Everywhere," she said.

Corin smiled. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." She stood, and stretched, and felt her body move the way it always had—no storm in it, no song, just muscle and breath and an ordinary woman's strength, which turned out to be enough. She held out her hand. "Well? The accord's paying badly, you said. Bad pay means bad horses. We'll walk."

Corin took her hand and rose beside her, wincing, grinning, alive. Below them the lamps of Vessa kindled street by street, and above them the stars burned on, unextinguished, unowned, keeping their gentle watch over the small figure of a woman who had given away the sky and gotten back herself.

"Everywhere," Corin repeated, testing the word. He shook his head, but he was still smiling. "All right. But I'm choosing the first shrine."

"You're choosing nothing," Tamsin said, turning toward the stair that led down into the lamplit streets, into the morning that was coming, into all of it. "We're flipping a coin at the north gate. Loser carries the maps."

And laughing—ordinary, mortal, free—they went down together into the city of the saved, while overhead the sky-born watched, and the weather, wherever Tamsin Almeida slept, stayed gentle all the night through.
