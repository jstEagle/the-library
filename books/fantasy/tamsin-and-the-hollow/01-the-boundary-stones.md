## 1. The Boundary Stones

There are forty-one boundary stones in the Hollow, and I have memorized every one of them the way other people memorize prayers: not because I believe in them, but because I was raised by someone who did, and rebellion is exhausting when you're also carrying the bread.

"Third stone's leaning again," Aldous said from behind me. He always knew. He could be forty paces back, wheezing like a bellows with a hole in it, and he'd still know which stone had shifted before I did.

"It's been leaning since spring," I said. "It likes it there."

"Stones don't like things, Tamsin."

"Then it's comfortable."

He made the sound he makes when he wants to scold me but can't spare the breath for it—a sort of wet click, followed by dignity. I heard him set his walking stick down against something, which meant he meant to stand a while and watch me work, which meant I had at least ten minutes of being supervised by a dying man who thought ritual was a substitute for medicine.

I knelt in the frost-browned grass and put my palms flat against the third stone. It was taller than me by half a head, granite gone soft-edged with centuries of weather, and carved into its face were the Warden's marks—circles within circles, worn nearly smooth. The old script beneath them nobody living could read. Aldous claimed he could, but Aldous claimed a lot of things after his second cup of cider.

The rite itself was simple enough to insult both of us. You laid your hands on the stone. You spoke the words—the same eleven words, in the same order, that Wardens had spoken since before the valley had a name. And you felt, if you were honest about it, absolutely nothing.

I felt nothing. I want to be clear about that, because later—well. Later is later. On that morning, with the first snow coming down fine as sifted flour, I felt nothing at all except cold seeping through my knees and the particular resentment of a twenty-three-year-old woman performing liturgy for an audience of sheep.

"*The line holds. The line is kept. What is without stays without; what is within stays within.*"

Eleven words. I'd counted them once out of spite, during a sermon-length lecture from Aldous on the sanctity of repetition. Eleven words, four times a year per stone, forty-one stones, one hundred and sixty-four recitations annually, and in all my life the sum total of what the line had held back was a very determined badger and Master Corwin's pigs, back when there had been a Master Corwin.

That was the thing about the Wardens of the Hollow that nobody outside the valley knew, mostly because nobody outside the valley thought about us at all: we used to be an order. There had been lodges, Aldous said. Chapter houses in three kingdoms. Wardens by the hundred, sworn and robed and insufferable. Now there was Aldous Vane, seventy-one years old and coughing up pieces of himself into a handkerchief he pretended I didn't see, and there was me, Tamsin Blackwood, last apprentice, keeper of forty-one stones and exactly zero answers.

"The snow's early," Aldous said.

"It's past harvest."

"It's *early*." He said it the way he said things that mattered, which was quietly, looking at the middle distance instead of at me. "Snow doesn't come until the feast of Saint Ellery. My mother told me, and her mother told her, and neither of them was ever wrong about weather, only about men."

I stood and brushed off my knees. "You're in a mood."

"I'm always in a mood. You've only just gotten old enough to notice." He took my arm—not for support, though he needed it, but in the way he did when he wanted me to walk slower than I wanted to walk. "Humor me. Take the long way back along the wall."

The long way back along the wall added twenty minutes and passed the gate, which was not really a gate anymore, just two posts and a memory of a fence where the drovers' road came down out of the hills into the valley. Nothing came through it. Drovers hadn't come this way in thirty years; the road beyond the stones had gone to bracken and rumor. That was the arrangement of our lives, though I'd never thought of it as strange until I was old enough to think at all: the valley inside, the world outside, and between them a ring of old stones that everyone in the Hollow touched with two fingers when they passed, the way you'd touch iron, or a saint's foot.

We walked. Snow gathered on Aldous's shoulders and he didn't brush it off, which frightened me more than any cough had. He was watching the gate. He had been watching the gate since the third stone, I realized—watching it the way you watch a door you've locked twice and still don't trust.

"Aldous," I said. "What's out there?"

"Out where?"

"Don't do that."

He smiled, which was worse. My master had a smile for every occasion and I'd catalogued most of them: the cider smile, the you're-being-foolish smile, the rare and terrible smile he wore when a fevered child recovered against his expectations. This one I didn't know. This one looked like a man remembering something he'd spent a lifetime forgetting.

"When I was your age," he said, "I asked my master the same question. What's out there. And she told me: winter, wolves, and the world. In that order of danger."

"And what did you say?"

"I said that seemed backwards." He laughed, and it turned into the cough, and I held his elbow through it and hated how thin his arm had gotten under my hands. When it passed he leaned on me and we stood there in the falling snow like a couple of crooked fenceposts. "I've had sixty years to decide whether she was lying," he said, when he could speak. "I still don't know which part."

* * *

We got back to the chapel-house as the light went the color of weak tea. I built up the fire while Aldous sat in his chair doing his evening accounting—that was what he called it, his accounting, though I'd never seen a ledger. He just sat with his eyes shut and his lips moving, going over some list only he had, and some nights he'd open his eyes at the end of it looking like a man who'd found an error he couldn't correct.

I put barley and salt pork in the pot. I fed the hens. I did all the small domestic machinery of our lives, and while I did it I let myself have the thought I'd been not-having all day, the way you let yourself look directly at a sore tooth:

When Aldous died, there would be no one left to teach me the words.

Oh, I knew the words. Eleven of them, and the longer litany for the founding-day rite, and the burial verses. But knowing words isn't the same as knowing what they mean, and Aldous had spent twenty years deflecting my questions with the skill of a man who'd had a great deal of practice. *Why these stones? Why here? Who founded us? Where did the other lodges go?* — *In time, Tamsin. When you're older. Some doors open from the other side.*

I was older. The doors stayed shut. And now his handkerchief had spots of red on it that he thought I didn't notice, and the accounting at night went longer, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that whatever door he was keeping shut was going to close forever with him on the wrong side of it, taking the key down into the dark with him.

So yes. Resentment. That morning I had resented the stones, the ritual, the guild that was two people and a chapel full of moth-eaten robes. I had resented the eleven words most of all, those smug little sentences about what stays without and what stays within, recited to a valley that had forgotten why anyone bothered.

I want it noted that I got my wish for something to happen. I have tried, in the years since, to be gentler with myself about that. But I did wish it, and the world was listening, and the world has never once given me what I actually wanted instead of what I asked for.

* * *

The knock came at dusk.

Not at the door—at the gate, down the path, a small sound, more scrape than knock, the kind of sound a thing makes when it hasn't the strength to announce itself properly. I was mending a shirt by the fire. Aldous was asleep in his chair, or near enough to it that I almost didn't wake him. Almost.

But I'd grown up on stories, the ones mothers in the Hollow tell their children: *never answer the gate after dark, never answer a knock you can't see over the wall, the stones keep what's within and what's without, and you'd best know which side of the line you're standing on.* Superstition, I'd always called it. We were the Wardens. If anything crossed, we'd know.

I took the poker anyway. Sentiment only goes so far.

The snow had thickened after dark, coming down in fat slow flakes that the wind couldn't make up its mind about. Our yard was already a white page with no writing on it—and then, at the gate, writing. Small bare feet had come up the drovers' road through fresh-fallen snow, leaving prints sharp and unhurried, and at the end of the trail stood a child.

A girl, six years old perhaps, seven. Thin as a winter branch, in a dress that had been fine once—good wool, good stitching, now gray with road-dirt and torn at the hem. No coat. No shoes. Bare feet in the snow, and here is the detail I would give anything to unsee: the snow around her feet was not melted. It lay undisturbed, pristine, as though the cold had decided to make an exception of her rather than the other way around.

She looked at me. She looked at the poker. She looked at the gateposts between which she stood, and she did not cross them.

And then she spoke.

I have been asked, since, what it sounded like, and I have never managed an honest answer, because the honest answer is that it sounded like water sounds if water had something to say. Liquid, layered, nothing like the valley tongue or the lowland speech the peddlers used. The vowels went places vowels shouldn't. It was beautiful, and it was wrong, the way a flower blooming out of season is wrong—lovely, and telling you the season itself has broken.

She said maybe six words. She said them clearly, carefully, the way you repeat something you've been told to repeat, something entrusted to you. Then she looked at me with eyes too old for her face and waited, as if I might simply understand.

"Child—" I said, and stopped, because what was I going to offer her? I knew four languages' worth of greetings between them and none of them hers. "Come inside. It's cold."

She didn't move. She said the six words again, and this time I caught something in them—urgency, or grief, or both braided together—and behind me, inside the chapel-house, I heard a chair go over.

Aldous stood in the doorway with the firelight behind him, and I have seen my master afraid exactly twice in my life. Once was the night his heart stuttered and he gripped my wrist and made me promise things I didn't understand. This was the second time.

He was looking at the girl the way you look at a ghost. Worse—ghosts you can tell yourself stories about. He was looking at her the way a man looks at a debt come due.

"No," he said. Not to me. To her. To the six words, maybe, or whatever they meant. "*No.*"

The girl answered him—in his own tongue, this time, or trying to, shaping the valley speech clumsily around those liquid vowels. One word. Just one, and even I knew it wasn't valley speech at all, wasn't any speech, just a name, offered up like a key offered to a lock:

"Caelmere."

Behind me, Aldous Vane, last master of the Wardens of the Hollow, sat down in the snow.

The snow fell on us all, early and gentle, like something beginning.
