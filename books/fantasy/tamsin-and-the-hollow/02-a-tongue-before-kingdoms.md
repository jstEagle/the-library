## 2. A Tongue Before Kingdoms

The child slept in my bed that first night, and I slept in the chair by the hearth with a poker across my knees, which tells you everything about how much good I thought a poker would do.

She didn't stir once. She lay small and straight as a folded letter, hands tucked under her cheek, breathing the slow, even breath of someone who has finally stopped running. Frost had gotten into her braid somehow—little white beads at the ends of it that didn't melt by the fire, not even when I picked them apart with my fingers. They came away like grains of salt. I kept one in my pocket for three days before it vanished, and I never told Aldous I'd taken it, because I already knew what he'd say: *Don't keep things from the stones, Tamsin.*

Well. The stones had sent her, hadn't they? Or something had. And I was keeping her.

Morning came gray and thin, the kind of light that makes the valley look like a sketch of itself. I fed her porridge with honey, because honey is the universal diplomat, and she ate it with the grave concentration of a scholar at work. When she finished she looked up at me and said a word—two syllables, round and soft, like water going over a smooth stone—and pointed at the bowl.

"That's *thank you*," I said. "Or *more*. Honestly, either works."

She said it again, patient, as though I were the one failing to learn.

I tried every language I had, which is to say every language the guild had bothered to teach its last apprentice: the Common Tongue, obviously; High Vellish for reading old warrants; the trade-pidgin of the river towns; and a smattering of the lowland dialects Aldous called "the languages of people who owe us money." Nothing landed. She watched my mouth move with polite, exhausted attention, the way you watch an old dog try to fetch a stick it clearly cannot find.

So we invented our own language instead. She pointed at things; I named them. She corrected my pronunciation of *her* names for things with a firm little frown, and I let her, because a girl who has walked out of a snowfall into a valley that shouldn't be reachable deserves to win some arguments.

By midmorning I had settled on Wren. It wasn't her name—I could tell that much, because when I tapped my chest and said "*Tamsin*," and then tapped hers and waited, she made a sound like wind through winter branches, four syllables long, with a break in the middle where a breath should go. I tried it twice and mangled it twice, and she laughed—the first sound out of her that needed no translation—and shook her head, and pointed at herself again, and made the wind-sound again, softer this time, almost apologetic.

"Wren," I said firmly. "Small bird. Fierce. Sings in cold weather." I flapped my elbows. She considered this performance with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once, accepting the appointment on behalf of the crown.

Aldous came down while we were playing this game. He stood in the doorway of the kitchen with his hand on the frame, and I want to be fair to him here, I do—but fairness is easier in hindsight than it was that morning, watching the color leave his face like water out of a cracked jug.

Because she was talking. She talked all the time, my Wren, a running commentary on porridge and firelight and the crooked shutter, in that language of hers, and I had spent half a day finding it beautiful without understanding a word. But Aldous understood.

He went white. Not pale—white, the particular white of a man hearing a voice he buried years ago come up through the floorboards. He gripped the doorframe. The wood creaked.

"Say that again," he said—not to me, not really to her either. To the room. To the past.

Wren looked at him, tilted her head, and repeated herself: a question about the shutter, as far as I ever learned. Something about it being crooked. Children are children in every tongue.

Aldous sat down across from her very slowly, the way you sit down when your knees have made the decision without consulting you. And then he answered her.

In her language.

I have heard Aldous speak perhaps six words of ceremony-Old-Vellish in nine years, mumbled over boundary stones like a man reciting a shopping list. I had never heard him *converse*. But there he was, voice rusty and halting, shaping those same round syllables, and Wren's whole face changed—went from wary to blazing, from blazing to wet, and she leaned across the table and grabbed his wrist with both hands and said something urgent and trembling, and he closed his eyes and said, "Slowly, little one. Slowly. It's been a long time."

It's been a long time. In her language. Said fluently.

I stood in my own kitchen feeling like a stranger at a funeral. "You're going to tell me what she's saying," I said, "and you're going to tell me what that language is, and you're going to do both now."

He didn't let go of her wrist. "Her name isn't Wren," he said quietly. "But Wren will do. Names are doors, Tamsin. Some doors are better left painted shut."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He looked at me over the girl's head, and there was something in his face I'd never seen there before, and it took me a moment to name it because I'd only ever seen it aimed outward, at floods and blights and the rare stranger who climbed too high on the ridge. Fear. My master, who had faced down the Hollow's worst winters with nothing but ritual and stubbornness, was afraid of a child asking about a crooked shutter.

"It's Old Caelmerish," he said at last, as if the words cost toll. "The tongue of Caelmere."

"Caelmere," I repeated. The word felt strange in my mouth, like a coin from a country I couldn't place—which, it turned out, was exactly what it was. "Where's that? North of the Vellish marches? I don't think we ever covered it in—"

"There is no north of the Vellish marches," Aldous said. "There is no Caelmere. There hasn't been, in any map or memory or ledger, for longer than you've been alive. Longer than *I've* been alive, if the stories are true, and I used to think the stories were drunk men talking." He drew a breath. "The country was struck out. Not conquered—struck out. Unmade in the records of the world. And its language died with it, because a language can't live without a country to stand on."

"Then how does she—"

"I know how she speaks it," he said, sharper than I'd ever heard him, and Wren flinched at his tone, and immediately his voice gentled down to nothing. "That's the one thing you must not ask me. Do you hear me, Tamsin? Not how she crossed the stones. Not how she got here. Not now. Maybe not ever. Swear it."

The fire popped. Wren looked between us, reading the argument perfectly well without a single word of it, the way children read weather.

"You're asking me to swear ignorance," I said. "That's not a thing. You taught me better. *A Warden who doesn't ask is just a wall with a title,*" I quoted at him, his own words, from my second year, when I'd asked why the eastern stone needed re-cutting and everyone else said leave it alone.

"And I was younger then," Aldous said, "and hadn't yet learned that some questions are load-bearing. Pull them out and the whole house comes down on whoever's standing inside." His hand found Wren's hair, awkward, gentle. "This house has two people in it, girl. One of them is dying. Think about what you'd be pulling on."

I hated him a little, right then. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I hated him for the forbidding, and more for the tenderness underneath the forbidding, because tenderness meant he loved her already, which meant whatever he knew, he'd known it for less than a day and had still decided it was worth dying with.

"I'll not swear," I said. "I'll wait. That's different, and you know it's different."

Something moved behind his eyes—relief, maybe, or its cousin. "Waiting," he said, "is the oldest Warden virtue. We invented it. We've been practicing it so long we forgot what we were waiting for." He pushed himself up from the table, joints cracking like green wood, and paused at the door with his back to me.

"She stays away from the boundary stones," he said. "And tomorrow I'll need the archive key. There are things I should have burned thirty years ago, and it seems the past has decided to deliver itself to our gate instead."

He left. Wren watched the empty doorway for a long moment, then turned to me and said something in that round, water-over-stone language, and patted the table beside her, and offered me the last of the honey.

Later, after she'd gone up to bed, I stood in the kitchen and did arithmetic I didn't like. A country erased from every map. A master who spoke its dead tongue like a man remembering his mother's voice. A charter locked in the archive that no apprentice had been allowed to read past the third page. And a child walking through snowfall that fell upward, carrying frost that wouldn't melt, speaking a language that should have had no speakers left anywhere under any sky.

Aldous had told me not to ask how she crossed the stones.

He had not told me not to wonder. And wondering, I was learning, is a tide—you can forbid it the shore, but it comes back every night, a little higher.

In the morning, the archive key was gone from its hook, and Aldous was gone with it, down into the chapel crypt where the guild kept everything it had decided to forget. I gave him until noon.

Then I followed him down.
