## 3. What the Charter Keeps

The archive of the Wardens of the Hollow occupied the cellar beneath the guildhall, and it smelled like every cellar I had ever known: stone, dust, tallow, and the particular sweetness of old paper slowly giving up. Four shelves. That was the whole of it. When I was small, Aldous had told me the Wardens once kept archives in three countries, and I had imagined endless vaults, candlelit corridors, librarians with ink-stained fingers. Then I grew tall enough to count.

Four shelves. One of them held jam.

Still, there were books. Ledgers going back two centuries, each hand growing more cramped as the guild shrank from dozens to a handful to, eventually, my master and me. Ritual manuals. Boundary surveys. A tax record from 1743 that someone had annotated *paid under protest* in a furious hand. And at the center of the room, on a lectern under glass, the founding charter itself—the oldest thing we owned, older than the guildhall, older than the chapel, older, Aldous claimed, than the valley's name.

I had looked at that charter maybe fifty times in my life. It hung behind glass the way relics hang in churches: to be venerated, not read. But last night I had heard Aldous in here after midnight, moving along the shelves, and this morning he had burned letters in the hearth upstairs—letters I'd never seen him receive—and so now I stood in the cold cellar with a lamp and a key I had taken from his coat while he slept, telling myself that this was not betrayal. This was due diligence. Warden business. The kind of thing a proper apprentice would do without needing to steal anything, if her master ever told her anything at all.

I lifted the glass case. The charter lay open to its first page, the ink gone brown as dried blood, the script looping and formal:

*Be it known to all who come after: the Wardens keep the Hollow, and the Hollow keeps what must be kept.*

Fine words. Below them, the articles began. Article the First: the boundary stones shall stand. Article the Second: no magic shall cross. Article the Third—

Article the Third was a stub. A half-inch of paper where a page should have been, its edge clean as a surgical wound. Someone had taken a blade to the founding charter of my family's order, and they had done it carefully, close against the binding, so that unless you knew to look you would see only an odd gap in the numbering, a Third that leapt straight to a Fourth.

I turned pages with hands that had begun to shake. There were four excisions in total. Articles Three through Five, gone. A whole signature near the middle, razored out. And on the final page, beneath the signatures of eleven founders whose names I had memorized as a child the way other children memorized kings, one name had been scraped away entirely—parchment thinned to translucency, the ghost of letters still visible if I tilted the lamp just so.

I tilted the lamp just so.

The ghost-letters would not resolve. But the scrape-marks were fresh. Not centuries fresh. The fibers still stood up pale and raw where the blade had lifted the ink, the way wood stands up when you plane it. I have mended enough books to know what old damage looks like—softened, darkened, healed into the page. This was new damage. This was weeks old. Maybe days.

Someone had cut Caelmere out of our charter recently. Someone who was still alive. Someone with a key to this room, of which there were exactly two.

I sat down on the jam shelf—I mean the shelf that held the jam, though at that moment I could not have told you which of us was being preserved better—and I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since the child arrived speaking a dead tongue. Aldous had gone white at the sound of her voice. He had forbidden me, in a tone I had never heard from him, to ask how she crossed the stones. And now our founding document was bleeding freshly cut pages, and the only other key hung warm in my pocket.

*The Wardens keep the Hollow,* said the first page.

*And the Hollow keeps what must be kept,* I finished, alone, in a cellar that suddenly felt less like an archive and more like a grave someone had tidied.

* * *

I might have confronted him that evening. I rehearsed it, actually, over supper—*Master, about the charter*—in several tones ranging from wounded daughter to prosecuting attorney. But when I came upstairs the hearth was roaring hotter than any winter night required, and Aldous stood before it feeding papers into the flames one at a time, unhurried, the way he fed the stove or the chickens, and something in the set of his shoulders made me hold my tongue and simply watch.

He was burning correspondence. Bundles of it, tied in the faded ribbon we used for official Warden letters. He read each one briefly before it went in—not skimming; reading, the way you read something you wrote yourself long ago and are checking for typos. Some he paused over. One made him close his eyes for a moment before he let it fall.

"Aldous," I said finally. "What are you doing?"

"Housekeeping." He didn't turn around. "The archive wants culling. It always has."

"The archive is downstairs."

"This correspondence never reached the archive." Another letter into the fire. "It came to me directly. Branch business."

"We don't have branches."

He stopped then. His hand hovered over the next bundle, and for a moment I thought he would lie to me—he had been lying to me, I understood that now, in the low continuous way a roof lies about the weather—but instead he sighed, a long rattling sigh that ended in the cough I hated, the wet deep one, and lowered himself into his chair by the fire.

"No," he agreed. "We don't. Not anymore." He looked at me across the firelight, and his face was very old, and very tired, and for one strange moment almost fond. "Sit down, Tamsin."

I sat. I did not give back the key. I kept my hand in my pocket around it like a stone.

"There was a lodge," he said, "in the north. And one over the western mountains, and one on the coast, and others besides. Eleven lodges once. We were the smallest and the least important, and we are the only ones left, because the others were closed." He fed another letter to the fire. "Closed is the word we used. You'll notice how much of our work comes down to choosing gentle words for hard things. Contained. Closed. Kept."

"Kept," I said. "Like the charter says."

"Just so." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I had ever seen on him, because it was genuine. "You've been downstairs."

It wasn't a question. My hand tightened on the key until the teeth bit my palm. "There are pages missing," I said. "Cut out. Recently. And a name scraped off the signatures. If you're burning the evidence of whatever those pages said—"

"I'm burning nothing of consequence." He said it flatly, and I believed him, which frightened me more than a confession would have. "The charter cannot be uncut, girl. What was removed was removed because keeping it invited questions, and questions, in the wrong season, invite worse. I am burning letters that say nothing you will not learn anyway, and learning them later rather than sooner is a mercy I am trying to extend to you while I still can."

"You're dying," I said. "That's the mercy you should be extending. The truth. While you still can."

The fire popped. Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, I heard the small sounds of Wren settling in the loft—she slept badly, murmuring in that language like water running backward—and Aldous's eyes went up toward the sound, and everything in his face changed. Grief, I thought. Or guilt. With him I was beginning to learn they wore the same coat.

"When she speaks," he said quietly, "do you understand what you're hearing?"

"Old Caelmerish. You said."

"And do you know what Caelmere was?"

"A country," I said. "Before the maps. You told me once it was a story."

"It was never a story." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, an old man gathering himself to lift something heavy. "Tamsin. Listen to me, because I will only say this much tonight, and perhaps not even this much tomorrow. Whatever you find in that cellar—and you will find it, you have your mother's inability to leave a locked box alone—whatever you find, remember one thing before you judge us. The war was ending. Children were dying on both sides, winters were eating whole provinces, and the men who signed that charter believed—with reason, Tamsin, with *reason*—that they were choosing the smaller grief."

"Choosing it for whom?" I asked.

He didn't answer. He picked up the last bundle of letters, weighed it in his hand like a stone he meant to throw into deep water, and then—instead of burning it—set it on the table between us, deliberately, within my reach, as if some part of him had decided that mercy was a debt he no longer had the strength to service.

"That's for the morning," he said. "Or for never. Your choice. You seem determined, these days, to make all your own choices." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and paused at the doorway with his back to me. "One thing tonight, since you'll only worry otherwise. The excisions weren't mine."

My blood went cold. "Then whose—"

"They were my predecessor's. Done the winter before I took the chair, on instruction from the northern lodge. Which means—" he glanced back at me, firelight carving his face into something ancient and ashamed, "—whoever gave that instruction has successors. And successors, Tamsin, tend to check their work."

He climbed the stairs. I sat alone with the fire and the bundle of letters, and I did not touch them, because my hands had started shaking again and I wanted to read them steady.

But as I banked the coals, one loose sheet slipped free of the ribbon and lay curled on the flagstones, glowing faintly at its edge where an ember had kissed it. I snatched it up and beat the flame out, and by that light, in that scorch-marked margin, I read a single line in a stranger's careful hand:

*The remnant must not reach the valley. If it does, send word to the third lodge — the seal still holds there.*

The third lodge. The one Aldous swore had been closed.

Outside, the snow had begun again, falling soft and straight past the window—and somewhere far off, or perhaps not far off at all, I heard the boundary stones humming.
