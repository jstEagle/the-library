## 4. The Winter That Follows

The snow began falling upward on the third morning, and Wren was the only one who thought this was worth mentioning.

She tried, in her way. She stood at the kitchen window with her breath fogging the glass and tugged Tamsin's sleeve twice, then pointed at the boundary stones, where the flakes were rising off the ground like a flock of small white birds deciding they'd made a mistake.

Tamsin looked. Tamsin squinted. Tamsin said, "Huh," in the tone of someone who had expected nothing stranger than weather, and went back to chopping onions for Aldous's broth.

That was the thing about the valley people, Wren had decided. They were kind the way a hearth is kind—warm, steady, entirely unaware of what burned to make them so. Tamsin fed her. Tamsin had named her, *Wren*, pressing a finger to her own chest and then to the bird carved into the chapel door until Wren understood it was hers now, a name like a coat handed over in cold weather. But when the world did something impossible, the valley people said *huh* and chopped their onions, because the stones kept everything out, and what the stones kept out could not matter.

The stones were wrong about that. The stones had always been wrong about that.

* * *

At night she dreamed in pieces, and the pieces were of home.

Not this cottage with its low beams and its smell of ash and rosemary. Home: the city with no name anyone living remembered, though Wren's body remembered it the way a shell remembers the sea. In the dreams there were bridges made of glass, arched high between towers, and you could walk them at dusk and see the whole city glowing beneath your feet like a lamp seen through frosted panes. There had been lamps everywhere. Caelmere had loved light—not the fierce gold of southern countries but a pale, patient silver, light that seemed to be waiting for something rather than looking for it.

In the dreams her mother's hands smelled of lamp-oil and cedar. Her mother sang while she worked, songs with no beginning, the kind of song that assumed it would simply continue forever. Nothing in Caelmere ended. That had been the whole point of the country, if a child understood such things: bridges that had stood nine hundred years, libraries where the oldest books were still being read aloud every year so the words wouldn't feel abandoned, doors left unlocked for travelers who might come in a decade or never. A country built on the assumption of continuance.

And then one night in autumn—the last autumn, though no one knew it yet—the singing stopped mid-verse.

Wren did not dream the rest. She woke instead, every time, at exactly the place where the memory went white, her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs, her hands cold as coins. Whatever came after the white was not gone. It was only folded up inside her somewhere, the way a letter is folded, and some nights she could feel it pressing at the creases.

Tonight the folding felt thinner than usual.

* * *

She woke before dawn to find frost writing on the window.

Not the ordinary fern-patterns frost makes when it is only minding its own business. This was script—tall, slanted letters, drawn from the outside of the glass, which should have been impossible, since frost does not write from outside, and certainly not in Old Caelmerish, and certainly not in her grandmother's hand.

Wren sat up slowly. The cottage was dark and warm around her; Tamsin slept in the loft above, Aldous in his room down the hall, his breathing audible these days even through walls, a sound like wind moving through a hollow reed. Neither of them stirred. The frost waited.

She knew the words before she was close enough to read them, the way you know a knock on the door before you know who's knocking.

*THE SNOW REMEMBERS YOU.*

She pressed her palm flat against the glass. It was bitter cold, colder than any window in a heated house had a right to be, and beneath her hand the letters rearranged themselves, unhurried, like a spider reweaving.

*COME OUT AND BE COUNTED.*

"No," Wren whispered. It was the first word she'd spoken aloud in this valley, and it came out cracked and small, and the frost did not care. More letters bloomed, delicate and terrible, each one edged in tiny crystals that caught the last starlight.

*WE CLOSED THE DOOR. WE KEPT ONE.*

She snatched her hand back. Down the hall, Aldous coughed—a long, wet, awful sound—and muttered something in his sleep, and the frost on the window, all of it, the letters and the innocent ferning at the corners alike, slid away at once, melting as though the glass had been breathed on from within.

Wren stayed by the window until the sky went grey, watching the boundary stones. The snow was still falling upward there, patiently, flake after flake rising off the fields and climbing into the dark, as if the winter on the other side were gathering itself back in, taking inventory, making sure nothing had been missed.

Nothing had been missed. That was the problem. Something had gotten out.

Her.

* * *

After breakfast—porridge, honey, Tamsin chattering gently at her in English Wren mostly read from her face rather than her words—Wren went out to the chapel yard alone. Tamsin let her now; the girl had learned that Wren needed hours of silence the way other children needed games, and she'd stopped hovering, mostly, though Wren could always feel her attention at the edge of things like sun through a curtain.

The yard was white and still. The boundary stones stood in their old circle beyond the fence, five of them visible from here, mossy shoulders hunched against the sky. Ordinary stones, the valley said. Just stones.

Wren walked to the nearest one and put her hand on it, and the stone hummed.

Not loudly. Not the way a bell hums. It was more like standing near a great beast in its sleep—you didn't hear it so much as feel it in your teeth, in the soles of your feet, in the small bones behind your ears. The humming had layers. Underneath the surface hum, deep down, there was a second sound, and Wren recognized it with a lurch, because she had heard it once before, through a closing door, on the last night:

Singing. Many voices, far away, holding one long note.

The Wardens' work. The guild's work. Whoever had built these stones had tuned them like instrument strings, and what they had chosen to tune them to was the sound of an entire nation being shut away. Every hour of every day, for longer than Wren had been alive, the stones had been quietly singing the closing of the door, over and over, a lullaby sung to keep a country asleep.

She took her hand off the stone. Her fingers had gone numb, but the cold in them wasn't the stone's cold. It was coming from farther away. From beyond the hills. From the direction the upward snow was pointing, like ten thousand small white arrows all indicating the same thing.

Something was walking toward the valley.

She couldn't have said how she knew. Knowing lived in her body below language, below even the fragments of memory. But she knew it the way she knew the weight of her own hands: the winter that had eaten Caelmere had not finished eating. It had been told to stop—it had been *sealed*, that was the word the frost used, *closed, counted, kept*—and seals, in Wren's short and instructive experience, existed to be broken. Out past the hills, in the white country that was no longer on any map, something vast had turned its face toward this valley, toward the stones, toward the one small warm irregularity in a world it had been promised would be smooth and silent forever.

It was coming to collect her.

Or worse—she understood this only dimly, the way you understand thunder means lightning without understanding either—to collect what she carried. Because she was not only a girl who had slipped through a door. She was the last page of a book someone had tried to burn. Everything Caelmere had been—its bridges, its lamps, its songs with no endings, its nine hundred years of patient light—all of it had been folded into the country when the country was closed, and folded into the country's people, and the country's people were gone, and she was what remained. A remnant. A living archive with a heartbeat.

The frost had said *kept*. Things that are kept are kept for reasons. And things that escape being kept are pursued.

* * *

That evening Tamsin found her sitting on the chapel steps, and did the thing Wren had begun, secretly, to love her for: she didn't ask questions. She just sat down, close enough that their shoulders touched, and pulled her own cloak half over Wren's, and watched the boundary stones with her.

The snow was falling upward again. Harder now. Faster. The flakes rose off the fields in long silver ribbons, streaming toward the hills like rivers running backward, and the light coming off them was wrong—not moonlight, not starlight, but the pale patient silver of Caelmere's lamps, the color of a country showing itself one last time in the only mirror it had left.

Tamsin stared at it for a long moment. Then, quietly, in her soft valley voice, she said something Wren didn't understand—but she said it while looking at the stones, and her hand found Wren's and held it, and her grip said the rest plainly enough: *I see it too. I don't know what it is. But I see it, and I'm not leaving you alone with it.*

Wren leaned into her shoulder and watched the winter gather itself beyond the hills, and in her chest, under her ribs, the folded letter of her memory pressed hard at its creases, and somewhere very far away, something vast took another step, and the frost on the chapel window behind them, unseen, began quietly to write:

*SHE IS FOUND.*
