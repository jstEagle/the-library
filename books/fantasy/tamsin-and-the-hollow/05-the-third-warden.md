## 5. The Third Warden

The stranger arrived on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophes ought to have the decency to arrive at midnight, in storms, with thunder doing the overture. This one came at mid-morning while I was mending a fence post, and he came walking up the valley road like a tax collector.

I saw him before he saw me—or thought I did—and had time to take his measure. Tall, but stooped, as if he'd spent years ducking under doorframes built by shorter men. Grey traveling cloak, good boots gone soft at the seams, a staff of pale wood that I recognized down to my bones because it was boundary-stone oak, cut and cured the way Wardens cure it. He walked the road's center line exactly, never drifting, and there was only one kind of man who did that: a man who had spent his life measuring distances against wards.

Aldous was on the porch shelling beans into a bowl that caught more on the boards than in it. His hands hadn't been trustworthy since midsummer.

"Company," I said.

He looked up, and I watched something cross his face—something quick and cold, a fish under ice—and then it was gone, replaced by the mild blankness he wore for strangers. That should have been my first warning. Aldous didn't do mild blankness for strangers. Aldous did hospitality like a siege defense: overwhelming, suffocating, impossible to breach.

The man stopped at the gate. Not through it. At it. And I noticed—he waited, hands folded on his staff, until Aldous gave him a nod so small you'd miss it if you weren't watching for exactly that.

Then he stepped across, and the stones did nothing to him at all.

Every hair on my arms stood up. The boundary stones kept out magic; they did not keep out men. But they *sang* when anything crossed them—I'd heard it all my life, a hum in the teeth like a struck tuning fork, faint as breath. I heard nothing now. Either the man carried no magic whatsoever, or the stones knew him and chose silence.

Both possibilities were worse than the alternative.

"Warden Corvane," he said, inclining his head. "Of the Marrowfield lodge." He said it the way you say a thing you've said ten thousand times, smooth as river stone, and I waited for the name to mean something to me.

It didn't. In twenty-six years, in a guild that had once counted forty lodges across three kingdoms, I had never once heard *Marrowfield*. But the guild had been old when Aldous was young, and Aldous was older than the chapel roof, and there were things even he had stopped mentioning—lodges gone quiet, brothers who "went east" and were never spoken of again. So I filed the name away in the drawer where I kept everything Aldous declined to explain, and I smiled.

"Tamsin Blackwood," I said. "You'll want tea. Everyone wants tea after that road."

* * *

He sat in Aldous's chair, which I noted, and Aldous let him, which I noted harder. Wren sat on the floor by the hearth, playing with a wooden horse I'd carved her, and I watched Corvane watch her with an attention so total it made my skin prickle. It wasn't the way you look at a child. It was the way you look at a lock you intend to pick.

"Marrowfield," I said, setting down the tea. "I don't know it."

"You wouldn't." He smiled. He had a good smile, warm at the edges, and it reached his eyes about as far as winter reaches into a cellar. "We keep to ourselves. Low-country work. Containment, mostly."

"Containment," Aldous repeated, in a voice I couldn't read.

"The Guild has many functions." Corvane turned the teacup in his long fingers without drinking from it. Another mark against him. Nobody refuses Aldous's tea; it's practically heresy. "Guardianship. Record-keeping. And containment, when something is found that shouldn't be moving freely in the world."

The fire popped. Wren looked up from her horse.

"And what brings containment work up our road?" I asked, keeping my voice pleasant the way you keep a hand pleasant on a spooked animal.

"A report." He set the cup down with a click too precise to be accidental. "Of a crossing. An irregular crossing, at these stones, some weeks past. First snowfall."

The room got very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the bare apple trees, and somewhere a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

"There's been no crossing," Aldous said. "Nothing crosses here. That's rather the point of us."

"Of course." Corvane's gaze slid back to Wren, gentle as a snare. "And yet reports travel strange roads. A child, the report said. Small. Dark-haired. Speaking a tongue no living mouth should hold." He paused, delicately, like a man laying a card face-up. "*Caelmerish.*"

Wren went still. Not frightened-still. Something older than frightened—the stillness of a deer that has scented smoke on a wind too far off for anyone else to smell. Her hand closed around the wooden horse until the carved legs pressed white lines into her palm.

"I don't know the word," I said.

My voice did something interesting on the way out. It stayed level. It stayed courteous. And underneath both of those things, for the first time in my life, it lied—not in what it said, but in what it refused to say, in the smooth blank wall it put where the truth was. Because I *did* know the word. I'd found it razored out of our own charter not two months past, and I'd watched Aldous burn letters about it, and I had asked no questions because asking questions felt like pulling threads on a sweater your dead mother knitted you.

Corvane looked at me. Just looked, for a long moment, with those cellar-winter eyes, and I understood that he was weighing me, and that I was coming up lighter than I wanted to.

"No," he agreed mildly. "I don't suppose you would."

* * *

They sent me out of the room.

That was the second insult of the day. Aldous said, "Tamsin, the stores need inventorying before hard frost," in the tone he used for lessons, and Corvane smiled his warm-edged smile, and I understood that whatever was going to be said would be said over my head, in the old way, the way the guild had apparently always done things—decided above, endured below.

So I inventoried the stores. Barley, salt, onions, the last of the honey. My hands did the counting and the rest of me stood in the cold larder listening to my own blood and thinking: *he knows. He knows she's here. Aldous knows he knows.*

And here is the thing I could not get around, the knot in the rope: Aldous had told me to hide nothing from the guild. Aldous had raised me on the catechism—*a Warden's word is a ward itself; speak true or speak nothing.* Twenty-six years of it, ground into me like flour into bread. And now a stranger with boundary-oak under his hand sat in my master's chair asking after my child—because she was mine, whatever anyone said, I'd named her, fed her, taught her to whistle through a grass blade—and my master, my dying, beloved, infuriating master, had let him in past the stones without a whisper of resistance.

Either Aldous was betraying Wren.

Or Aldous was playing a game whose board I'd never seen, and I was a piece on it.

Neither option improved my temper.

* * *

I came back in near dusk and found them standing, the talk evidently finished, the air in the room carrying that scraped-out feeling of a conversation that has decided things. Corvane had his cloak on. Aldous stood by the hearth looking ten years older than he had at breakfast, which for Aldous was saying something.

"We'll return tomorrow," Corvane said to me pleasantly, buckling his cloak. "With proper writ. The Guild requires the child surrendered for containment. Standard procedure." He said *standard procedure* the way priests say *blessed assurance*, and I hated him completely and calmly, the way you hate weather.

"She'll be surrendered if the Guild says so," Aldous said, and my heart dropped through the floorboards and kept going.

But Corvane's eyes flicked to Aldous—one heartbeat, two—and something passed between them that I couldn't read, some signal in a code I wasn't taught, and then the stranger bowed to us both and walked out the gate, center-line straight, and the stones still didn't sing.

I waited until he was a grey smudge on the darkening road. Then I turned to Aldous.

"You're giving her to him."

"I'm buying us a night," Aldous said. His voice was gravel and ash. "One night, girl. Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit *down*, Tamsin." And there was something in it—not command, something rawer—that put me in the chair despite myself. He lowered himself opposite me, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and took both my hands in his ruined ones, and his grip was still strong enough to hurt.

"Listen to me. Corvane is no more from Marrowfield than I am from the moon. There is no Marrowfield lodge. There hasn't been since—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Since Caelmere."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"But he *is* Guild," Aldous went on. "Or he was. Or he serves what the Guild became after. And he will come back tomorrow with men, writ or no writ, and they will take her, and what they do with children who carry a dead country in their mouths is not written down anywhere I can show you." His thumbs pressed hard into my wrists. "So tonight you will lie, Tamsin. You, who have never lied in your life, God help you, because it will be agony and you'll be bad at it. Tonight the crypt. Tomorrow morning, when they ask, you tell them she ran in the night—you wept, you searched, you failed. Can you do that?"

"Why?" My voice cracked clean down the middle. "Why are you helping me lie? Two days ago you wouldn't tell me how she crossed the stones. You burned letters. You—" I pulled my hands free. "What aren't you telling me?"

Aldous looked at me for a long time. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind the firelight there was something else, something old and buried and struggling upward like a body under ice.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "When they've come and gone. Tomorrow night I'll tell you everything, and you'll wish to God I hadn't. Now go. Take the lamp, the blanket from my chest, and the girl. The crypt stair sticks—lift, don't push. And Tamsin—" I was already at the door. "Whatever you hear up there tomorrow, however it sounds—remember that I am buying you a night, and spend it well."

* * *

The crypt beneath the chapel smelled of cold stone and candle grease and, faintly, of apples, because the valley's dead lay under its oldest orchard and the roots didn't respect walls. I got Wren down the sticking stair with the lamp held high, wrapped her in Aldous's blanket on the flat top of a tomb some forgotten Blackwood, and told her a story about a fox who outsmarted a whole village of hunters.

She listened with her chin on her knees. When I finished she didn't smile. She reached out and touched my cheek, once, very carefully, the way you touch a thing you're afraid might break, and said something soft in the tongue of a country that isn't there anymore.

I know maybe six words of Old Caelmerish. One of them, I'm fairly sure, means *stay*.

I stayed until she slept. Then I climbed the sticking stair, shuttered the lamp, and sat in the dark chapel with my back against the door, listening to the wind worry the eaves, rehearsing my tears.

It was going to be the longest night of my life, and the shortest day would follow it, and somewhere down the valley road a grey smudge of a man was walking toward us with a writ and a smile and whatever else he carried in that long coat.

Above me, in the rafters, something shifted—a soft scrape, like a boot adjusting its weight.

I did not move. I did not breathe.

Because I had locked the chapel door behind me, and I was sitting against it, and it had just opened inward an inch.
