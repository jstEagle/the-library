## 6. Confession at the Hearth

There is a particular art to dying slowly, and I have mastered it the way I once mastered warding-script: reluctantly, and with a great deal of complaining.

The cough came first, three winters ago. Then the weight slid off me like snow off a roof. Now I measure my days in trips between chair and hearth, and my victories in whether I can split kindling without sitting down halfway through. Tamsin thinks I don't notice her watching me across the kitchen. She watches the way I used to watch the boundary stones—waiting for the crack, dreading it, unable to look away.

Tonight she is mending a sock by firelight, badly, and pretending not to count my breaths. The girl—Wren, Tamsin calls her—is asleep upstairs in the narrow bed that was mine when I was an apprentice with two good lungs and no secrets worth mentioning. Or so I believed, back then. Secrets are like mice. You think you've sealed the house, and then you find the droppings behind the flour bin.

I have put this off for eleven days. I know because I have been counting them the way a man counts the steps of his own gallows.

"Tamsin," I say.

"Hm." She doesn't look up from the sock. She has learned my tones. This one is the tone of *pass me the willow bark*, and she is rationing her attention accordingly.

"Put down the sock."

That gets her. She looks up, and there it is—the thing I have dreaded more than the coughing blood: her eyes going sharp and wary all at once, the wariness aimed at *me*. Eleven days ago she found the razored pages in the charter. Eleven days ago she started watching me the way one watches a door that might be load-bearing. I could have lied. Gods know the guild gave me practice enough. But I am done lying to her, partly because it's wrong, mostly because I haven't the breath for long sentences anymore.

"Bring the black bottle down from my room," I said. "The one under the floorboard. And then sit, because what I have to tell you wants telling drunk on my end and sober on yours."

* * *

She brought the bottle. She did not sit so much as perch, coiled, on the far side of the hearth, arms crossed, a magistrate of nineteen winters. I poured myself two fingers of plum brandy that has been waiting under that floorboard since before she was born—for this exact evening, if I'm honest, though I always imagined I'd have more hair.

"You found the charter," I said.

"I found where the charter used to be whole."

"Same thing, in this house." I drank. The brandy tasted of plums and of every year I'd hoarded it. "Ask me your question, then. The real one. Not the polite one you've been swallowing at breakfast."

Her jaw worked. "Who cut the pages out?"

"I did."

I had expected anger. What I got was worse—a stillness, a kind of careful settling, like a bridge deciding whether to hold. "When?"

"Nine days before you found them."

"Why?"

"Because you were ready to read them." I turned the glass in my fingers. "And because I am a coward, and cutting them out felt, in the moment, like mercy. Mine, not yours."

"Aldous." My name in her mouth had gone strange—no longer *master*, not quite anything else. "What's in those pages?"

So. There it was. The hearth popped; a log shifted its burning weight. Outside, the first true cold of the season pressed its face against the shutters, and somewhere up on the ridge the boundary stones stood their silent watch, doing the only job I ever told her they did.

I told her the rest.

* * *

"You know the catechism," I began. "Every Warden learns it kneeling. *We keep the hollow, and the hollow keeps us. The stones turn aside wild magic, as a wall turns weather.* Say it often enough and you stop hearing the words. I said it for fifty years. I taught it to your mother, and she taught it to you, and neither of us heard what was missing from it."

"Which is?"

"The other side of the wall." I set down the glass because my hand had begun to embarrass me. "Tamsin, we never guarded magic *out*. Magic isn't a wolf at the sheepfold. The stones were raised to keep something *in*—or rather, to keep the world from remembering it. We are not shepherds, child. We are the lid on a pot."

"That's nonsense," she said, but softly, the way one says it when it isn't.

"Is it? Then explain the girl."

She couldn't, and I watched her try, watched the explanations queue up and fail one after another behind her eyes. A lost child. A feral child. A trick. None of them fit, and she knew none of them fit, because she had already heard the girl speak in her sleep—that language old enough to predate the valley, old enough to predate nearly everything.

"There was a country called Caelmere," I said.

"No, there wasn't."

"There was. That's rather the point." I laughed, and it turned into the cough, and I rode it out with my fist against my chest while she half-rose and I waved her down. When I had my breath again I went on, quieter. "It lay north and east of here, past the salt marshes. A country of glass bridges, they say—I never saw it whole. Rivers that ran warm in winter. A language with no word for *orphan*, because the whole nation considered itself kin. It had been there longer than our maps, longer than our guild, longer than most of our gods' names. And eighty years ago, the Wardens helped unmake it. Not destroy. Unmake. There's a difference, and the difference is the crime."

Tamsin had gone very pale. "Unmake it how?"

"With an oath. The founding charter—the part I cut out—isn't rules, girl. It's a sentence. A binding, signed by the first nine Wardens, sealing Caelmere behind a ring of standing stones exactly like ours. Every map that showed it forgot it. Every book that named it lost the name. Every person who ever traded there or loved someone from there woke one morning with a soft blank place where the memory had been. The country didn't fall. It was *edited*."

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why would anyone—"

"War." I said it flatly, because there was no honest way to dress it. "Caelmere and the eastern kingdoms went to war over the marsh trade, and the war went on fourteen years and ate three generations, and it would not end, because Caelmere could not be beaten—they had craft of their own, older than ours—and the kingdoms could not retreat without shame. So certain men, tired men, important men, came to the first Wardens with a proposal. End the war. All wars, that war and any future one over the same ground. Take the whole country out of the argument. Out of the world. And the first Wardens—nine of them, sworn keepers of balance—sat in a stone hall and weighed a nation against a peace, and decided the arithmetic favored the peace."

"And signed it."

"And signed it. And then spent the rest of their lives calling themselves guardians, because men can bear almost any crime if they're allowed to rename it first." I looked into the fire. "Our order was founded on that signature, Tamsin. Everything since—the rituals, the rounds, the tending of the stones—it's all maintenance. Custodial work. We aren't keeping magic from the valley. We're keeping the valley from noticing the shape of the hole where a country used to be."

The silence went on long enough that I heard the wind change outside. When she spoke again her voice was very controlled, which in Tamsin means the opposite of calm.

"The stones around *this* valley."

"Are the seal's anchor-stones. The strongest of them. This hollow was chosen because it sits at the knot of the working. Every Warden who ever lived here was a lock-keeper and didn't know it—or knew it and chose the comfortable word for it. *Warden.*" I spat the brandy's last taste into the fire. "Jailer, if you want the plain one."

"And the girl."

"Wren." I nodded. "Eighty years the seal held, and the country inside it didn't die, Tamsin. That's the part the signers never asked about. They assumed erasure was ending. But inside the stones, Caelmere kept on—cut off, unmapped, unwitnessed, winter closing in year by year as the working drained the land to feed itself. The people endured. They endured until there were too few to endure with, and then—" I stopped. My throat had closed, and not from the coughing.

"Then what?" she whispered.

"Then someone inside found the flaw. Because there's always a flaw; even the best oath is a made thing, and made things have seams. Someone sent something through. A child. The last speaker of the last living generation, carrying the language out through the stones the way water finds a crack. She is not a refugee, Tamsin. She is a message. She is Caelmere's whole remaining case, delivered to the doorstep of the court that condemned it."

"She's eight years old."

"Yes."

"And the winter that follows her—the frost-writing, the snow falling wrong—"

"That's the seal, failing. Or hunting. Depending on which verb you prefer, and I have spent fifty years preferring the gentler one." I finally met her eyes. "Something is coming for her, girl. Something the working sends to close breaches. And when it arrives, it will not knock."

* * *

She stood up then. I expected shouting—I had earned shouting—but Tamsin's anger has always run quiet and deep, a river under ice. She walked to the window and looked out at the dark where the ridge would be, and her shoulders were rigid.

"My mother," she said. "Did she know?"

"No. Your grandmother neither. After the signing, the founders agreed the truth should die with each generation. Kindness, they called it. Each Warden got a clean conscience and a full catechism, and passed both on." I rubbed my face. "I found out at forty-one, from my own master, in this same chair, over this same brandy. He cried. I swore I'd do it differently—tell my apprentice young, let her choose with open eyes. And then you arrived, all elbows and grief, thirteen years old and orphaned, and I thought: *not yet. Let her have a few years of believing her life's work is decent.* And *not yet* became fifty years, apparently, compressed. Cowardice keeps excellent books, Tamsin. It always knows exactly how much time it has borrowed."

"What happens now?" Still facing the window. "To her. To us. If the seal breaks—if Caelmere comes back—"

"Then every debt comes due at once. The world remembers a theft it committed, or permitted, or profited from. The dead tongue returns to living mouths. And the guild—" I smiled, and it hurt. "The guild ends, girl. Honestly, cleanly, the way a bad law ends. There is no version of this where the Wardens survive with our name intact. I've checked. I've checked for fifty years."

Now she turned. Her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than I had seen them since her mother died.

"You let me kneel at those stones," she said. "You let me learn the catechism. You let me love this work."

"Yes."

"Say it plainly, Aldous. Did you let me become a jailer's daughter?"

I took my time, because she deserved a plain answer and plain answers cost the most.

"I let you become a Warden," I said. "Which is the same thing. I'm sorry doesn't cover it. Nothing covers it. That's rather what I'm trying to teach you tonight: some wrongs don't get covered. They get *told*. That's all that's left to us."

She sat back down across from me, slowly, and put her face in her hands, and for a while the only sound was the fire and the wind and my ruined breathing. I let her grieve it—grieve the guild, the catechism, the version of me she'd carried since childhood. Grief is the tuition on truth. I'd have paid hers for her if I could.

When she looked up, her face had settled into something I recognized with a jolt: her mother's face, deciding.

"The stranger," she said. "The one who came asking for the girl. The third Warden. He knew. Didn't he? Containment—he was using the old words."

"He knew enough." I reached for the bottle and my hand shook too badly, so she poured for me, which felt like a changing of some guard. "There are others, Tamsin. Off the rolls, out past the stones—men who kept the old knowledge the way a body keeps a splinter. They've waited eighty years for a breach, and now one has walked up to our gate wearing small boots. They won't ask twice. And I—" the cough took me again, harder, and I rode it down into the chair, and when I surfaced she was holding my wrist with two fingers on the pulse like a little physician, and her expression told me she had already counted what the pulse had left.

"How long?" she asked.

"Weeks. Days, if I'm stubborn, and I'm always stubborn." I gripped her hand. "Listen to me. When I'm gone, they'll come, and they'll come with papers and titles and a logic you'll want to agree with, because their logic is tidy and the truth is a mess. Don't you dare find it persuasive. The girl goes free, or nothing I've said tonight meant anything."

"And if I can't save her alone?"

I looked at her—at this fierce, unbribeable creature the guild had accidentally produced despite itself—and I found, buried under the terror, a small coal of pride that no confession could smother.

"Then you'll need to go where I never could," I said. "Over the stones, Tamsin. Out of the valley. Find the door back into the country that isn't there—and God help you, open it."

Outside, the wind rose along the ridge, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, patient—came a sound I had heard only once before, sixty years ago, on the worst night of my master's life.

Footsteps in the snow. Too slow. Too heavy. Coming down from the boundary stones toward the chapel, unhurried as winter itself.
