## 7. Over the Stones

I buried Aldous on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult to him. He'd have wanted something more dramatic—a storm, at least, or a gathering of mourners. What he got was me, a shovel, and the frozen ground of the chapel yard arguing with every stroke.

The false Warden gave me three days to grieve. I know because he told me so, standing in our doorway with snow melting on his grey cloak and his face arranged into something he probably thought was sympathy.

"Take the time you need," he said. "Then bring the child to the lodge at Harrow's Ford. For containment."

Containment. He said it the way Aldous said *ritual*—a word worn smooth from use until nothing underneath it showed anymore.

I said yes. I nodded and thanked him and looked suitably bereaved, and I have never in my life been more grateful for anything than I was for the fact that lying, it turns out, comes naturally if you practice on yourself first. Because I had already decided. I'd decided somewhere between the third shovelful of dirt and the moment I found Aldous's letter tucked inside his copy of the *Rites*, the one that named the road over the stones and the door at the end of it, and finished with: *Don't let them finish what we started.*

So while the false Warden—Corvan, he called himself, Corvan of the Ashford Lodge, a lodge that did not exist before this winter and would not exist after—rode back down-valley to wait out my grief, I packed.

You'd be surprised how little a life weighs when you carry it in your arms. A loaf of bread. A skin of water. Aldous's knife, which was really my knife now, though I hadn't earned it and never would the way he'd meant me to. The razored pages I'd rescued from the hearth, curled and browned at their edges but legible still. And Wren, who weighed almost nothing, who watched me move through the chapel with those dark eyes that held an entire erased country and asked me nothing, because she couldn't ask, and I couldn't answer anyway.

*We're leaving,* I told her, in the gestures we'd built between us over the long weeks—hand to chest, then outward, then down toward the gate. She understood. Of course she understood. She'd been waiting for me to understand first.

* * *

The boundary stones stood where they had always stood, nine of them in a broken arc across the valley mouth, older than the guild, older than the chapel, older than any story anyone had ever told me about them. All my life they had been simply *there*, the way weather is there. I had oiled them. I had sung to them on the solstices. I had pressed my palm to the warm granite each spring and felt the faint hum of the warding, and thought: *this is what keeps us safe.*

Nobody had ever told me what it kept us safe *from*. Now I knew, and knowing made the stones look different—smaller, somehow. A fence is only impressive until you learn what the farmer buried underneath it.

Snow was falling upward as we approached. I want to be precise about that, because precision was Aldous's gift to me even when everything else he gave me turned out to be a lie: the flakes rose. They lifted off the ground in slow spirals and climbed past the stones into a sky that had no business accepting them, and where they crossed the line of the warding they came apart—not melting, *unmaking*, dissolving into a glitter that wasn't quite light.

Wren stopped walking. Her small hand closed on my sleeve with surprising strength.

"I see it," I said, which was stupid, because she couldn't hear me either. But I said it anyway. "I see it. We're going anyway."

Here is what nobody tells you about crossing a boundary that has defined your entire existence: it takes four steps. Four. I had spent twenty-three years inside a circle I could have walked out of in the time it took to sneeze, and I had never once tried, because the guild taught us that outside the stones the world was thin and hungry and full of magic that would unmake a person who hadn't been trained to hold it.

Aldous had also taught me that the Wardens were guardians. So.

Step one: nothing happened. The hum under my palm grew louder, a beeswarm sound, a sound like a word being spoken very slowly by something enormous.

Step two: the cold changed. It stopped being weather-cold and became *attention*-cold, the whole valley noticing me, the stones singing their one long note directly into my bones.

Step three: Wren's hand slipped from my sleeve and found my fingers instead, and I realized she was shaking, and that she had crossed this line once before, alone, mute, hunted, and here she was doing it again because she trusted me, which was either the most beautiful or the most terrible thing that had ever happened to anyone.

Step four:

Step four was the magic.

I don't have words for it and I am, whatever else is true of me, a woman of words. It didn't enter me so much as *recognize* me—the way water recognizes a channel, the way fire recognizes a wick. It came up through the frozen ground and down through the rising snow and sideways through the air itself, and it poured into every part of me that had ever been empty, and it was cold, God, it was cold, cold as the well-water in January, cold as the space between stars, and it was *alive*, terrifyingly alive, alive the way a river is alive when it has decided to go somewhere and does not care what stands in its way.

I heard myself make a sound. I don't know what sound. Somewhere in it I think I was screaming, and somewhere in it I think I was laughing, and both were true.

And then I was through, and on my knees in the snow beyond the stones, and the valley was behind me, and everything was different.

Not the world—I want to be clear about that too. The world looked exactly like the world. Pines, drifts, the grey shoulder of the mountain going up into cloud. But I could *see* it now, all of it, the way you can suddenly hear a note you've been deaf to all your life. Every tree had a voice. Every stone had a memory. The snow itself was language, falling in sentences, and I could read maybe one word in a hundred and even that was almost too much.

Wren knelt beside me. She put her hand flat on the ground, the way she did sometimes, listening to things through her palms, and then she looked at me and smiled—the first real smile I'd seen on her, wide and crooked and missing a tooth—and pointed at my chest.

I looked down. Light was coming off me. Faintly, softly, the way a banked fire breathes, but unmistakably: I glowed.

"Well," I said. My voice came out strange, resonant, doubled. "That's new."

* * *

We walked north and east, following Wren's certainty, because I had none of my own. Outside the valley the world was mid-winter in a way the valley's tidy seasons had never permitted—deep, old winter, the kind that gets into a place's bones, with drifts higher than my head and a silence so complete I could hear my own heartbeat answering the light in my chest.

And the world was wrong. Not broken—wrong the way a room is wrong when the furniture has been moved. Twice that first day we came upon roads: honest roads, graded and stone-laid, running straight through the forest and then simply ending. Not fading. Ending, cut clean, the last paving stone giving way to untouched snow, as though the road had been walking somewhere and had forgotten where.

The second time it happened, Wren wouldn't walk on the road at all. She detoured around it through the trees, and when I followed her look back at it, I saw what she saw—or began to. Under the snow where the road ended, the land had a shape. Ridges too regular for hills. Lines too straight for streams. Foundations. A field that remembered being a street, a woodlot that remembered being a market square, and underneath the remembering, deeper, a grief so old and so large that my new-flooded senses flinched away from it like a hand from a stove.

"That was a town," I said aloud, and my doubled voice cracked on it. "There was a town here."

Wren touched the nearest tree, gently, the way you touch a mourner's arm. Then she looked at me and made a sound—not a word, not in any tongue, just a small raw noise in her throat, and pressed her fist against her sternum, and I understood her perfectly.

It hurt here. It hurt everywhere. We were walking through the wound where a country used to be.

We camped that night in the lee of a fallen pine, and I built a fire with a snap of my fingers because apparently I could do that now, and I lay awake watching the snow come down without rising, and I missed the valley. That's the shameful truth of it. I stood inside the crime scene of an entire nation and I ached for home—for the chapel's crooked roofline, for Aldous's grumbling, for the boundary stones humming their one safe note. Homesickness doesn't check your conscience before it moves in. It just moves in.

But under the homesickness, quieter, something else was settling into place. The magic in me wasn't fighting me. It was waiting. All those years the guild told us the world outside was hungry and thin, and the truth was the opposite: the world was *full*, and we were the ones who had been starved, rationed, kept behind a fence and told the fence was a gift.

Near dawn, Wren shook me awake. She was standing, pointing east, rigid as a fencepost.

Through the trees, far off across a white field, something was glowing. Not my kind of glow—banked, accidental. This was deliberate. A lantern-signal, blinking in a pattern. And below it, at the field's edge, a line of figures in grey cloaks stood motionless in the snow, spaced evenly, like fence posts themselves.

Waiting along the only path forward.

Wren grabbed my hand. Her fingers spelled against mine, fast and frantic, the gesture we'd invented for one word only, the word she'd learned first because it was the word she needed most:

*Run.*
