## 8. The Country That Isn't There

Three days out from the valley, I had learned two things about the world beyond the stones: it was enormous, and it was trying to pretend otherwise.

The roads were the worst of it. They'd start confidently enough—a proper road, gravelled, hedged—and then simply stop, mid-stride, as if the builders had looked at what lay ahead and lost their nerve. We'd walk around the place where the road ended and find it beginning again a mile on, going in the same direction, having apparently decided not to speak of the gap.

"They don't like coming here," Wren signed to me, when I asked. She had taught me perhaps forty words of her hand-speech by then, enough for weather and hunger and *don't touch that*, which was proving the most useful vocabulary of my life. "Roads remember being useful."

I didn't ask how a road remembers. I was past asking that kind of question. The snow, for instance, fell upward whenever we crossed a stream, hanging in the air like a held breath before drifting back down on the far bank. I had stopped remarking on it. A person can only marvel so many times a day before marveling becomes a chore.

Wren led. That was the arrangement, and it wasn't really an arrangement at all—it was simply that she knew where we were going and I did not, and she carried her knowledge the way you carry a broken thing: carefully, in fragments, wincing when a piece shifted. Some mornings she would stop dead in the middle of a field and stare at nothing, and I learned not to interrupt, because she was reading something. Frost wrote on fence posts and gate rails in letters I couldn't see, and she read them the way Aldous used to read the boundary stones—quickly, expertly, with a face that got worse each time.

*Closer*, she signed once. Then, after a long pause: *It knows my name.*

I told her names could be changed. She gave me a look that said, better than any word in either language, that some things are knitted into you too deep for alteration, and walked on.

* * *

The city came back to Wren in pieces, and the pieces came in dreams, and the dreams came whether she wanted them or not.

She dreamed of bridges first. Bridges of glass, arched high over water that glowed green-gold beneath them, so that walking across the city at night had been like walking across a sky full of drowned stars. She remembered the sound her school shoes made on the glass—a particular ringing note, different on every bridge, so that the whole city was an instrument and the morning commute its music. She remembered her mother's hands, quick and brown, folding paper boats that actually sailed, because in Caelmere even the paper had been a little alive.

She remembered the last night mostly as sounds. Not shouting—she had expected shouting, later, when she understood what had happened, and the absence of it frightened her more than noise would have. What she remembered was the bells. Every bell in the city ringing at once, not in alarm but in something slower, sadder, a rhythm like a lullaby played at a funeral. And under the bells, a hiss, like snow falling on a fire, except the fire was everything—the lamps, the hearths, the glass bridges themselves, all of it going grey and quiet from the edges inward.

Her mother had put her in a cupboard. That was the memory that hurt most, because it was so small. Not a heroic flight through burning streets. A cupboard, with a blanket, and a kiss on the forehead, and a word Wren had spent six years unable to say aloud and now couldn't say at all.

*Wait*, the word meant. *Wait, and be small, and live.*

Then the door of the world had opened—not the cupboard door, the other one, the one underneath everything—and someone's hands, cold and official, had lifted her out, and the bells stopped mid-note, and there was white, and silence, and a valley where magic pooled at the edges of stones like rain at the lip of a gutter.

And behind her, following her out through whatever crack she'd slipped through, patient as arithmetic, came the winter.

* * *

On the fourth day the land began apologizing.

That's the only way I can describe it. We crested a hill and the country below us seemed embarrassed—fields that lay flat and featureless where hills should have rolled, hedgerows that ran straight through where a village square clearly ought to have been, a river that bent around the ghost of a harbor though the sea was fifty miles away. Farmers' lanes detoured politely around empty air. A church stood at the bottom of the valley facing, with great devotion, absolutely nothing.

"Here," Wren signed, and her hands were shaking. "Here was the Ambergate. Here was the Street of Bells." Her fingers moved faster than my lessons could follow, and I caught only fragments: *my school was there, the fountain, the bridge with the low note.* She pointed at a bare field and named a district of eighty thousand people.

We walked down into it. And that was when I noticed the shadows—or rather, stood in the middle of Caelmere's capital at noon and realized I hadn't seen mine since morning.

Nothing cast a shadow. Not me, not Wren, not the dead church or the crooked fences or the crows sitting silent in the hedges, watching us with what I swear was pity. The light fell on everything equally, without preference, the way light falls in a place that has decided no single thing in it deserves emphasis. It should have been beautiful. It was like standing inside a held breath inside another held breath.

At the center of the city—where the palace must have stood, or the great temple, something with weight—the land dipped. Not dramatically. Just a shallow bowl in the earth, a hollow maybe half a mile across, smooth as the bottom of a cup, with no grass growing in it and no snow lying on it either, though snow lay everywhere else in drifts up to our knees.

Wren wouldn't go near the edge. She stood at the rim with her fists clenched and signed one word, over and over: *closed, closed, closed.*

"It's down there," I said. "Isn't it. The door."

She nodded. Then she grabbed my sleeve and pointed north, and I turned, and my stomach performed a maneuver I felt obliged to forgive it for.

Down the long white avenue that had once been the Street of Bells, walking toward us without hurry, without footprints, came the false Warden.

* * *

He had shed the pretense of travel somewhere along the way. No pack, no staff, no breath steaming in the cold. His grey coat was greyer than the sky, and his face had settled into the calm of a man who has done a very tedious sum and arrived, at last, at the correct answer.

He stopped twenty paces off. He didn't need to come closer, and we both knew he could have.

"You've brought her home," he said. "Good. That saves everyone a great deal of walking."

"She's not going in that hole," I said, "and neither am I."

"The hollow isn't a punishment, Miss Blackwood. It's a mercy." He gestured at the city around us with something almost like tenderness. "Do you know what the guild found, at the end of the war? Not an enemy. An engine. Caelmere's magicians had built something that would have ended every kingdom on the map, theirs included, within a generation. Your founders saw it. They were given a choice: let the world burn slowly, or close one country quickly. They chose. It was the hardest thing anyone has ever chosen, and they carried it, and their children carried it, and the charter exists so that the carrying has a shape."

"So they erased a nation."

"They *sealed* a nation," he said, "and then they erased the crime, because a wound doesn't heal while it's being looked at. And now the last of it has leaked out"—he inclined his head toward Wren, who had gone very still—"and the winter has followed her, and here is the truth your master never had the stomach to tell you: the winter always comes. It has been falling on this place for sixty years, patient as debt. All we have ever done is decide where it falls. Let it finish here, in the hollow, over the sealed door, and the country closes forever—cleanly, finally, and no child ever again grows up speaking a dead tongue with death walking behind her. Or—" and here his voice softened, and I hated him for how reasonable it sounded, "—you open the door, and everything Caelmere was comes pouring back into a world that has finished grieving it, and the winter follows the people, and this time there is no valley, no stones, no charter, nowhere left to shut it in. You will not save her, Miss Blackwood. You will merely choose who else freezes."

The wind moved through the shadowless streets. Somewhere a crow made a sound like a hinge.

"And the people inside?" I asked. "The ones who didn't die. The ones still waiting behind the door?"

His composure flickered—the first true expression I'd seen on him. "There is no *inside*. That is what sealing means. There is only the door, and the winter, and the choice."

But Wren had gone pale in a new way, and she was signing fast, urgently, tugging my arm: *he's wrong, he's wrong, I can hear them, I've always heard them, under the frost, under everything—they're singing the bell-song, they're still there—*

And beneath our feet, faint as a pulse, faint as a memory of music, something in the hollow answered her.

The false Warden heard it too. For the first time, he looked afraid.

"That's impossible," he said. "The oath was absolute."

Wren stepped forward to the edge of the hollow, lifted her chin, and spoke her first words in six years—Old Caelmerish, clear as a struck glass, a name.

The ground began, very quietly, to sing back.
