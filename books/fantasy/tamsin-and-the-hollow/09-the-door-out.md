## 9. The Door Out

The hollow had no bottom, as far as Wren could tell, but it had a middle, and the middle had a door.

She had known it was there the way you know a word is on the tip of your tongue—not the shape of it, not the sound, only the certainty that it existed and that you would be ashamed when it finally came to you. She had walked through snow that fell upward, past fences made of frost, through streets where the houses had forgotten to be houses and stood instead as suggestions, outlines drawn in cold air by someone who remembered them badly. Tamsin walked beside her the whole way, one hand fisted in the back of Wren's coat, as if the country might try to take her back.

It might have. Wren wouldn't have blamed it.

The door stood alone at the center of everything, which was the first strange thing, because doors are supposed to belong to walls. This one rose out of bare ground like a tooth from a gum: tall, dark wood banded in iron gone green, with no frame and no building behind it. When you looked at its edges, your eyes slid off, the way they slide off a star. Snow refused to land on it. It simply stopped falling an arm's length away, hung there, and drifted back up into the grey.

Words were carved across it in Old Caelmerish, and Wren read them without trying, the way she breathed.

*Who comes home must come whole.*

"I can't read it," Tamsin said. Her breath came out in white ribbons. She'd been saying things like that all day—*I can't feel my toes, I can't believe this used to be a harbor, I can't stop shaking*—and Wren had begun to suspect the sentence *I can't* was simply how Tamsin's people prayed.

Wren touched the carving. Under her fingers the letters were warm. Everything else in Caelmere was cold enough to bite, but the door held the temperature of a hand pressed flat against a window from the inside.

And under the warmth, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something knocked.

Not once. Not twice. A rhythm, patient, endless—the sound of many hands, on the far side of the door, knocking to be let out. Or perhaps it wasn't hands. Perhaps it was the country itself, tapping its fingers, waiting for someone to remember the password.

"You hear it too," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question. Nothing about Wren had been a question since the first snowfall, since the gate, since Aldous's face had gone the color of old candle wax. "What is it?"

Wren didn't answer. She never answered. But she thought, in the language she couldn't give away: *It's everyone. It's everyone we closed the door on.*

Behind them, out in the white, something crunched.

They both turned. The false Warden came walking down the slope of nothing, unhurried, his grey coat dusted with snow that respected him even less than it respected the door. He had followed them for two days, and he had stopped pretending to hurry, the way a cat stops pretending when it knows the mouse is tired.

"There it is," he said, and his voice had something in it Wren hadn't heard before. Not triumph. Relief. "Nine hundred years I've walked winters looking for it. Do you know what that's like, little ghost? Every lodge, every ledger, every dying master whispering half a map?" He stopped ten paces off and spread his empty hands. "And two children find it in a week."

"We're not children," Tamsin said. "I'm nineteen."

"You're a novice with a dead master and a stolen charter page," he said. "You're a rounding error." His eyes went to Wren, and softened, which was worse. "But you. You're the last of it. The remnant. Do you know why they sealed the door behind you?"

Wren knew. She knew the way she knew the knocking—fragments, splinters, a city of glass bridges shattering upward into a sky that drank them. She had been small. There had been a woman with flour on her sleeves pushing her through a crowd, through a gate, through a stone arch that sang when it closed. There had been a word spoken over her head like a blessing, or a burial.

"She doesn't have to know anything," Tamsin said, stepping between them. "Whatever you're going to say—"

"Someone should say it." The false Warden's voice dropped, gentle as snowfall. "The oath that shut this door is the same oath that erased the country. It required a truth to lock it. It will require a truth to open it. And here is mine, freely given, so you understand what you're standing in front of." He took a breath. "The Wardens ended a war no one could win. Caelmere's magic had gone ravenous—it ate its own fields, its own rivers, its own children's shadows. The guild offered mercy: seal the nation behind the stones, let the long winter fall, and in nine hundred years the world heals and forgets, and no one has to grieve a country that officially never existed."

"Liar," Tamsin whispered.

"Am I?" He tilted his head. "Ask your ghost. Ask her whether the bridges were already cracking before the Wardens ever came. Ask her what the winter was doing to Caelmere *before* it was set loose to follow her." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "We did not make the storm, girl. We only signed the paperwork that let it finish."

Wren felt Tamsin's hand tighten on her shoulder, trembling.

Because the terrible thing—the thing Wren would carry longer than any scar—was that some of it was true. She remembered glass bridges singing in the wind, and she remembered them groaning. She remembered rivers that ran silver and then ran backward. The hunger had been in Caelmere before the stones. The Wardens had found a dying country and, rather than mourn it, had locked the corpse in a cellar and thrown away the map.

But the false Warden left out the knocking. He left out the hands on the other side of the door, still asking, still waiting, still *whole*. Mercy that forgets you exist is just a slower kind of killing.

"The long winter finishes," the false Warden said, "and the crime is buried so deep even guilt can't find it. That is containment. That is peace. Open this door, and every grief comes back through it, and every debt with it. Is that what you want for her?" He nodded at Wren. "A country of ghosts pouring into a world that will hate them for existing?"

Wren stepped forward, out from under Tamsin's hand.

The snow hushed. Even the false Warden went still.

She looked at the door, at the words carved warm beneath the frost of nine centuries, and she understood them now—not the grammar, which she had always had, but the meaning, which she had needed to walk a dead country to earn.

*Who comes home must come whole.* Whole meant unbroken. Whole meant *nothing left out*. The oath had locked the door with a lie—the lie that Caelmere was finished, that its people were a rounding error, that forgetting was healing. And a lie, however large, however official, however carved into charter parchment with razored patience, could not hold a door forever. Truth was the key. It always had been. That was the joke the founders had built into their own prison and been too proud to notice.

One name in the old tongue. One truth in the new.

Wren turned to Tamsin and pointed at the door, then at herself, then pressed her palm flat over her own heart.

"You want me to speak," Tamsin said. "In—in Caelmerish? I don't—"

Wren shook her head. She pointed at Tamsin's mouth. Then at the door. Then she drew in the snow, quick and clumsy, the only word she could make Tamsin's alphabet produce: a shape like a small bird, like a girl, like a country.

"Wren," Tamsin said slowly. "Your name. You need your name spoken—" and then she stopped, because she understood, and Wren watched the understanding arrive in her face like dawn coming up over a battlefield. "No. Both halves. A name in the old tongue and a truth in the new. And the truth can't be mine, can it. It has to be—" Her voice cracked. "It has to be the guild's. It has to be *ours*."

The false Warden moved. Fast, suddenly, all that patience spent. "Don't," he said, crossing the snow. "Girl, listen to me. Nine hundred years of order—"

Tamsin didn't look at him. She knelt in the snow in front of the door, in front of Wren, and put her hands on Wren's shoulders, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain.

"Tell me your name," she said. "However you can. I'll carry it the rest of the way."

Wren opened her mouth. For the first time since the gate, since the valley, since the night of flour-dusted sleeves and shattering glass, sound came out of her—and it was not her voice alone. It was thin, and rusty, and underneath it ran a chorus, hundreds deep, the knocking given words at last. The name she spoke was long. It held a city and a river and a grandmother's kitchen and a bridge of glass. It held everyone.

Tamsin repeated it. Badly. Wonderfully. She mangled two syllables and got three exactly right, and the door's iron bands glowed green-gold, and the frost on the ground melted outward from her knees in a perfect ring.

Then Tamsin lifted her face to the listening dark—to the door, to the hollow, to whatever remained of Caelmere to hear it—and spoke in her own plain tongue, the tongue of the valley, the tongue of the guild that had done the deed.

"My name is Tamsin Blackwood, apprentice of the Wardens of the Hollow, and I am the last of them, so the confession is mine to make. My guild helped erase your country. We razored you out of the charter. We burned your maps and salted your memory and called it mercy, and we raised our children on the lie, and I believed it, and I am sorry. You were not a war to be ended. You were people. You are people. You knocked, and we did not answer, and that was the crime—not the sealing, but the silence after." Her voice broke and she let it. "Come home whole. All of you. Nothing left out."

The lock turned.

There was no click. There was a sound like a held breath released by an entire nation, and the door swung inward onto light—not summer, not yet, but the pale gold promise of it, a road going down through thawing fields toward rooftops and smoke and figures standing very still, faces turned toward the opening door, nine hundred years of waiting ending in a single step.

The false Warden shouted something. Tamsin shoved Wren through the doorway first—

and the last thing Wren saw, looking back, was the snow around them beginning, everywhere, all at once, to fall upward.
