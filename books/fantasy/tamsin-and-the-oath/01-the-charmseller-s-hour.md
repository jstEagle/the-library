## 1. The Charmseller's Hour

The trouble with wallpaper, according to the dead man, was that it had been his wife's idea.

"It's the *geese*," he said, in the voice of a man who had been complaining about geese for thirty years and intended to complain about them for thirty more. "Little green geese, marching around the border of every room like they own the place. I told her, Odette, I said, why would we want birds on our walls when we have birds at the window? But she'd already bought four rolls. Four. You don't return four rolls of geese."

Zephyrine Iversen kept carving and did not answer him, because answering was the first rule she broke and the last one she could never afford to.

The shop was called The Quiet Edge, which was either a joke or a prayer depending on how you looked at it. It sat on Tallow Lane where Vaelhollow went soft and crooked, wedged between a chandler who sold candles that smelled of nothing so much as other candles, and a cobbler whose sign promised HONEST BOOTS in letters that had faded to a kind of apologetic gray. Her license hung behind the counter in a frame of blackened silver: ZEPHYRINE IVERSEN, LICENSED BONE-CARVER, WARDEN CHARTER 4-118. It permitted her to carve buttons, combs, knife-handles, dice, mourning charms of the *uninscribed* variety — smooth shapes, no marks, no meaning, nothing that could be mistaken for working.

It did not permit what she was doing right now, which was inscribing a mourning charm with a dead man's name while he stood at her elbow critiquing the wallpaper of a house he had not lived in for two years.

"Third room down," he said. "The guest room. She papered it the week before I died, you know. Never got to see it finished. I'd have liked to see it finished." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quieter, the complaint worn through to something underneath. "It's a good pattern, really. The geese. She always liked geese."

Zephyrine's knife moved through the calf-bone in small, patient curls. The charm was coming along well — a teardrop shape, palm-sized, the kind a widow could wear against her breastbone and touch without anyone thinking twice about it. Unlicensed charms were confiscated. Inscribed ones were burned, and their makers were burned shortly after, usually in a courtyard with a very efficient queue. So hers looked like nothing: a smooth stone of bone, warm from her hands, and hidden inside its curve, cut fine as a hair, the letters *O-R-I-N*, and beneath them a spiral that meant — in the old tongue the Wardens had spent a century stamping out — *I am still here.*

"Tell her the geese were a good choice," the dead man said. "She'll argue. She'll say I hated them. Let her argue. Arguing was half the marriage."

Zephyrine almost smiled. That was the cruelty and the kindness of her gift, braided together so tightly she could no longer tell which strand was which: the dead did not come to her with wisdom or warnings or the great unfinished business of ballads. They came with the small things. The wallpaper. The unreturned library book. The way their daughter chewed. The joke they'd never gotten to finish. Two years dead and Orin Fenwick's whole soul, as far as she could hear it, was a man standing in a hallway being fondly annoyed by waterfowl.

She thought that if she ever died, she hoped someone would hear her complaining about something equally stupid. It seemed the only honest way to be remembered.

The bell over the door rang.

Zephyrine's knife stopped mid-stroke. Old reflex, older than the shop: stillness, blankness, the face of a woman thinking about nothing but bone. The dead man's voice thinned to a whisper, the way it always did when the living crowded close, as though they took up all the air in the room and left none for ghosts.

The widow Odette Fenwick came in out of the rain like a folded letter — small, gray, precise, her umbrella shaking off droplets with little efficient snaps. She was the sort of woman who had run a household for forty years and now ran her grief the same way: on a schedule, with a list, allowing herself exactly one cry per day and preferably after supper.

"Madam Iversen." She set a wrapped parcel on the counter. "You said it would be ready today."

"It is." Zephyrine wiped her hands on her apron and lifted the charm into the light, turning it slowly so the widow could see the whole unremarkable surface of it. "Calf-bone, as requested. No inscription, per the charter. A plain piece, madam. Nothing in it but polish."

That was the liturgy and they both knew their parts. Odette nodded gravely, the nod of a woman confirming that yes, this was merely an object, and paid the coins across the counter one at a time, each with a small click, as if counting were itself a comfort.

Then, with the parcel wrapped again and tucked into her coat, she paused. Her eyes — red-rimmed, dry, tired past weeping — flicked up.

"He liked the geese," she said abruptly. "Everyone said he hated them. He complained for a month. But the night before—" Her jaw tightened. "He stood in the hall looking at that border and he said, *you know, Odette, I think they're growing on me.* And I've thought about it every day since. Whether he meant it. Whether he was just being kind. Two years, and I can't stop wondering whether my husband liked my geese."

The dead man, at Zephyrine's shoulder, made a sound like a man struck through the heart.

"I meant it," he said. "Gods' teeth, woman, I *meant* it. Tell her. Tell her I meant it, tell her I used to count them when I couldn't sleep, tell her—"

Zephyrine kept her face still and her voice level, and committed, in the space of one held breath, the crime she had committed every day for seven years.

"A guess, madam," she said softly. "But men who complain that long are usually hiding affection. My father complained about my mother's cooking for thirty years and ate three helpings of everything. If he stood in that hall and said they were growing on him — I'd believe he counted them after."

Odette stared at her. For a moment Zephyrine thought she had overplayed it, had let too much warmth leak through the crack between licensed and unlicensed — and then the widow's face crumpled, quietly, the way a folded letter comes open, and she pressed her hand flat over the parcel at her breast and said, "Thank you," in a voice scraped nearly bare.

She left. The bell rang. The rain took her.

Zephyrine stood at the counter with her head bowed until her hands stopped trembling.

"You did well," Orin Fenwick said, gentle now, the complaint gone out of him entirely. "You do this well. Better than the priests ever did. They gave Odette a candle and a hymn. You gave her back a Tuesday evening in a hallway."

"Don't," Zephyrine said, very quietly, to the empty shop.

It was the second rule she broke daily: she answered.

* * *

She closed early. She always closed early on charm-days; the work drained her in a way carving never did, some slow hemorrhage of self that left her wanting soup and silence and, ideally, someone else's company for an hour — though that last want she had learned to file under *impractical*.

The city outside was doing its evening arithmetic: lamplighters making their rounds, gulls arguing over the fish-market leavings, the great bells of the Hollow Crown district ringing the hour in a pattern everyone knew and nobody listened to anymore. Vaelhollow stacked itself upward and downward from the harbor in tiers of slate and salt-stain, and everywhere — on corners, on bridges, painted on the underside of arches where children would see it — the same broadsheet, reprinted monthly at Warden expense:

**MAGIC IS A DEBT. THE CROWN COLLECTS.**

Below it, smaller: *Report unregistered practitioners. Reward offered. The King watches with love.*

*With love.* Zephyrine had lived here seven years and had never once heard the phrase spoken without a small involuntary flinch, the way you'd flinch at a dog that had bitten before. The crown watched with love. The Wardens served with love. Love, love, love, laid over the city like the geese over Odette's walls, and about as easy to take down.

She walked home the long way, because the long way passed the reliquary wall on Chandler's Stair, and she allowed herself one indulgence a day and this was it.

The wall was old work, pre-Warden, a stretch of masonry carved with a procession of figures whose faces had been chiseled away during the Purging a century past. Defaced saints, the Wardens called them. Zephyrine knew better. She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone as she passed, briefly, casually, the way you might steady yourself on a railing — and heard, faint as a song through a wall, the murmur of the old carvings. Not words, exactly. Not yet. Just presence. A hundred small voices sleeping in the stone, dreaming whatever stone dreams, and beneath them something older and slower that she had never been able to wake and was not sure she wanted to.

"Evening, ladies," she murmured, barely moving her lips.

Nothing answered. They never did, on the street, in daylight, with the broadsheets watching. But she felt the usual shift in the murmur, the sense of attention turning toward her like sunflowers toward a dim and distant lamp, and that was enough. That was the whole of her social life, some days, and she knew exactly how pathetic that sounded and had made her peace with it the way you make peace with weather.

Her rooms were above the shop, two of them and a kitchenette, kept ruthlessly tidy because mess invited questions and questions invited Wardens. She made the soup. She ate the soup. She sat by the window with her mending and listened to Orin Fenwick, who had followed her home because he always did on charm-nights, unwilling yet to go wherever it was the dead went when they weren't leaning on her hearing.

"She'll wear it tomorrow," he said. "To the market. She'll touch it when she buys the herring, because I always bought the herring, and she'll pretend it's nothing."

"Yes," Zephyrine said, to her darning needle.

"And in a year or two I'll thin out. They always do. The voice goes quiet, then it goes quieter, then one day she touches the charm and there's nothing there but bone, and she'll think, *well, that's that,* and she'll buy new curtains." He sighed, a sound like wind finding a gap. "It's a good arrangement, yours. Cruel and kind at once. Very like the world."

"You're philosophical tonight."

"I'm dead. It's practically required." A pause. When he spoke again, the humor had gone careful, the way people's voices go careful when they're about to say the thing they actually came to say. "There's something else, carver. I've been circling it all evening like a gull round a fish-head, and I'll lose my nerve if I don't say it."

Zephyrine set down the mending. In seven years, the dead had brought her warnings twice — a cellar fire, a Warden sweep — and both times the warning had arrived in exactly this tone.

"Say it, then."

"There's a new silence in the city. You won't have noticed it, because you're young and your ears are full of the likes of me. But us dead, we notice. There are voices that used to be here that aren't. Not gone-on, mind — not faded natural, the way I will. *Removed.* Erased. Whole stretches of the old town where there used to be murmuring and now there's nothing, clean as a scraped plate." His voice dropped further. "And it's spreading inward. Toward the Crown district. Toward the palace. Something up there is eating, carver. Something is eating the dead, and it's getting bolder, and whatever it is—" the ghost's voice frayed, and for a moment he sounded less like a fond widower than like a man standing at the edge of a very deep dark, "—whatever it is, it noticed *you.*"

The fire ticked. Down in the lane, a cart rattled past, and the bells rang the half-hour, and Vaelhollow went on being Vaelhollow, vast and indifferent and lit with love.

"Noticed me," Zephyrine repeated. "Orin. I carve buttons. I'm nobody."

"Mm," said the dead man, in the unconvinced tone of the dead everywhere. "That's what the candle said to the moth."

She laughed despite herself — a short, tired sound — and told him he was being dramatic, and he told her that death entitled a man to drama, and they argued comfortably about it while the rain started again against the glass.

But she did not sleep well. And near midnight, lying awake in the dark above her tidy, secret, carefully small life, she heard it herself — just once, just at the edge of hearing, so faint she might have dreamed it.

A silence. Moving through the city below her window. Slow, deliberate, tasting.

Like a tongue run along the edge of a blade.

She lay very still until dawn, and in the morning there was a note under her door, written in a hand she didn't know, inviting her to dinner.
