## 2. A Date with Prophecy

The candle between them had burned down to a stub shaped like a small weeping tower, and Zephyrine was beginning to suspect that Corin Vael had never actually been on a date before.

Not that he was bad at it, exactly. He was *formal* at it, the way a man is formal at a funeral he's rehearsed for years but never attended. He had arrived at The Gilded Carp twelve minutes early — she'd watched him through the window from across the street, working up her own courage and pretending to examine a display of salted eels — and when she'd finally walked in, he'd stood up so quickly his chair barked against the floorboards, and then he'd bowed.

Bowed. To her. In a restaurant where the specialty was fish stew served in bread.

"You must be Zephyrine," he'd said, as though there were any other woman in Vaelhollow who would be meeting a stranger at a corner table on Thawday evening because a matchmaker named Odette with three dead husbands and excellent instincts had insisted upon it.

"And you're the one who paid Odette an obscene amount to arrange this," Zephyrine had said, sliding into her chair. "Which makes me either very flattered or slightly worried about what's wrong with you."

"Both, I hope." And he'd smiled, and the smile had been warm and crooked and entirely disarming, and she'd thought: *oh no.*

That had been an hour ago. Since then he had asked her questions — real ones, not the usual Vaelhollow dinner interrogation of *what do you do, where do you live, do your parents have money.* He'd asked why bone, of all materials. He'd asked whether she liked rain or merely tolerated it. He'd asked if she believed a person could be homesick for a place they'd never been, and when she said yes without thinking, he'd looked at her for a long moment like she'd answered something much larger than the question.

Now he was apologizing to the serving girl for the third time because he kept forgetting that in Vaelhollow one did not thank people by touching their elbow, and Zephyrine was carving a tiny practice rose out of a chicken bone under the table, which was what her hands did when her mind needed somewhere to hide.

"You're doing it again," Corin said.

"Doing what?"

"The thing with your hands. Under the table." He nodded at her lap with the serene confidence of a man who could not possibly know what she was doing under the table and yet somehow did. "You've been doing it since the soup course. You go somewhere else when you work. I can see the door close."

Zephyrine set down the half-formed rose and wiped her fingers on her napkin with great dignity. "It's a nervous habit."

"It's not nervous. Nervous is fast. Yours is slow. Deliberate." He turned his wine glass by its stem, watching her over the rim. "My grandmother used to shell peas like that. Like each one was being asked a question only it could answer."

"That's absurdly specific for a man who claims to be a cartographer."

"I'm very specific. It's my worst quality." He smiled again, and this time the smile didn't quite reach whatever was going on behind his eyes. "Odette told me you carve charms. Mourning pieces, mostly."

"Mourning pieces, luck pieces, the occasional fertility charm for couples who should honestly just talk to each other instead." She shrugged. "Licensed work. Nothing interesting."

"I didn't say anything about interesting."

"You were about to. People always are. They hear 'bone-carver' and they want to know if I can tell their fortune, or curse their landlord, or speak to their dead mother, and I have to explain — patiently, pleasantly — that bone-carving is a licensed trade, regulated by the Wardens' Guild, and that anyone claiming otherwise is either a fraud or about to be arrested." She delivered the speech lightly, the way she always did, the well-worn lid sliding over the well-worn pot. "So. Charms. That's all."

"Of course," Corin said, and there was something in the way he said it — two small words, perfectly agreeable, agreeing with absolutely nothing — that made the back of her neck prickle.

The serving girl brought the stew then, mercifully, and for a few minutes there was nothing in the world but bread bowls and the small comedy of Corin discovering that he'd been given the spiciest batch in the kitchen and was too proud to admit it. His eyes watered. He drank water like a man crossing a desert. He maintained, throughout, a expression of grave composure that made Zephyrine laugh harder than she had in months — a real laugh, ugly and loud enough that the couple at the next table glanced over, and she found she didn't mind.

*This,* she thought, and immediately hated herself for thinking it, because thoughts like that were how you got careless. Thoughts like that were how seven careful years developed a crack right down the middle.

*He's kind,* she thought anyway. *He's strange and formal and he bows at waitresses and he's kind, and you are going to finish this dinner and go home and never see him again, because that is what safe people do.*

"So," Corin said, when he'd recovered enough of his dignity to speak. "I'm going to tell you something now, and I'd like you to promise not to think I'm mad until at least the end of the story."

"That's a terrible opening."

"It's the best one I have." He set down his spoon. He'd gone still in a new way — not the stillness of shyness but the stillness of a man standing at the edge of a high place, checking the wind. "I've had the same dream since I was six years old. Every few months. Sometimes more. Twenty-nine years of it, give or take."

"People have recurring dreams. Teeth falling out. Showing up to work undressed."

"Not like this one." He rubbed his thumb along the table's edge, back and forth, and Zephyrine realized with a small cold start that he was doing it the way she did things with her hands — asking a question of the wood, waiting for an answer. "It starts in a city. Not this one exactly, but near enough — the same bones of it, if that makes sense. The same streets wearing different skin. There's a bell tower with no bell in it, and everyone in the streets is looking up at the palace, and nobody is speaking. Not frightened-silent. *Listening*-silent. Like the whole city is one ear pressed to a door."

The prickle at the back of Zephyrine's neck became a slow crawl down her spine.

"In the dream I'm walking toward the palace," he went on, quieter now. "I don't want to. My feet won't stop. And there's a woman ahead of me in the crowd — I've never seen her face clearly, but I know her. The way you know someone in a dream. And she's carrying something. Small. Pale. Carved." He looked up. "And above the palace, above the crown of the highest tower, there's a shape in the air. Like smoke that's decided to be patient. And every silent person in that crowd is leaning toward it, all together, like grass bending under wind, and the woman with the carved thing is the only one standing straight."

The Gilded Carp went on around them — clatter, laughter, the serving girl singing under her breath as she stacked bowls — but Zephyrine heard none of it. She was somewhere else. She was twenty-three years old and newly apprenticed, cataloguing confiscated volumes in the Warden archive's restricted stacks, wearing white gloves and trying not to breathe too deeply around books that smelled of mildew and prohibition. She was holding a slim ledger bound in grey leather, unmarked except for a number stenciled on the spine, and opening it to a page of drawings done in faded iron-gall ink:

*A city seen from above, its streets drawn as veins. A bell tower, empty-crowned. A crowd of small ink figures, every head tilted upward. And in the foreground, two figures rendered larger than the rest, in careful detail: a man walking, and a woman standing straight, holding something pale in her raised hand. Above them both, sketched in strokes so light they might have been an afterthought — or a warning — a shape like smoke that has decided to be patient.*

She had catalogued it as *Anonymous devotional verse, late period, value negligible* and shelved it in the deep stacks where unregistered prophecy went to be forgotten, and she had gone home and drunk half a bottle of plum wine and told herself the resemblance meant nothing, because the woman in the drawing had no face, and neither did the man, and faces were everything and nothing.

But she remembered the caption. Gods, she remembered the caption, in that cramped antique script she'd spent weeks learning to read:

*The Hearer and the Walker shall stand beneath the Patient Smoke, and the city shall hold its breath to learn which of them it belongs to.*

"—rine? Zephyrine?"

She surfaced. Corin was watching her with an expression she couldn't read — concern braided with something sharper, something that looked almost like fear, though whether for her or of her she couldn't have said.

"You went away," he said gently. "Door closed."

"Sorry. Sorry, I—" She picked up her wine, found it empty, and set it down again. Her hands were not steady. She flattened them on the table. "Your dream. The woman in it. What does she look like?"

"I told you. I've never seen her face."

"What does she *look* like, Corin. Not her face. Everything else."

He frowned, and she watched him reach inward for something he'd carried so long he'd stopped examining it. "Small," he said slowly. "Dark hair, always coming loose from its pins. She stands like — like someone listening to music no one else can hear. And she holds the carved thing up like a lantern." His frown deepened. "Why do you ask like that? Like you already—"

"Describe the object."

"A charm. A pendant, maybe. Bone, I think. Pale, with a hole through the middle, strung on cord." He paused. "Actually, that's odd. I've seen that exact piece before. Recently. Hanging in a shop window on Cordwainer Lane, in among the luck-charms. I nearly went in. Odette's letter came the next day, and I took it as—" He stopped.

Zephyrine's hands had gone cold.

Because she knew the piece. Of course she knew the piece. She'd carved it eight months ago, a mourning pendant for a fisherman's widow, hollow-boned gull wing with a pierced center, and she'd made a second one from the offcut because the bone had sung strangely under her knife — had *hummed*, the way bone only hummed when it remembered being part of something that mattered — and she'd hung the second in her window because it was pretty and because she was proud of it and because she had never once, in seven years, imagined that anyone would recognize it out of a dream.

Out of *the* dream.

"Corin," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her, because underneath the levelness was a roar like the sea. "When you wrote to Odette. When you arranged this dinner. What did you tell her you were looking for?"

He met her eyes. And there it was again, that stillness, that edge-of-the-roof stillness, and she understood suddenly that the charming awkwardness, the bowing, the chili-sweat comedy — some of it had been real, but some of it had been a door held open, a warmth extended by a man who had spent twenty-nine years walking toward something and wanted, desperately, for the walking to be over.

"Honestly?" he said. "I told her I was looking for a woman who stands straight in a crowd."

The candle guttered. Somewhere behind the counter, the serving girl dropped a stack of plates, and the crash rang through the room like a struck bell — a bell tower with no bell in it — and every head in the Gilded Carp turned toward the sound, every face tilted up and away, and for one vertiginous heartbeat Zephyrine saw the dream laid over the room like a transparency: the crowd, the silence, the listening.

Then the moment passed. Laughter, apologies, the ordinary noise of the living swallowing everything whole.

Zephyrine stood up so fast her chair scraped. "I need some air."

"Zephyrine—"

"Two minutes. Don't follow me." She was already moving, weaving between tables, pushing through into the alley behind the Carp where the evening air was cold and smelled of river and rotting cabbage, and she pressed her back against the wet brick and breathed and tried to be a woman who sold charms and nothing else, a woman whose life was small on purpose, a woman no book had ever drawn.

But the ledger page was open behind her eyes, and the caption sat under it in its patient antique script, and beneath the caption, smaller, almost lost in the margin, she could now remember one more line — a line she'd skipped past at twenty-three because it had seemed like scribble, like ornament:

*The Walker follows the Hearer. The Hearer runs. She always runs.*

Zephyrine looked up the alley toward the street, toward the glow of the Carp's windows, and saw Corin's silhouette appear in the doorway — not following, exactly. Just standing there. Waiting. Walking, at the speed of patience.

Her shop keys were in her pocket. The archive was four districts away. The deep stacks closed at midnight.

She had until midnight to find out whether her whole life had been hers, or whether it had been drafted a century ago by a dead hand holding a pen of iron-gall ink.

Zephyrine pushed off the wall and started walking — fast, alone, running in everything but name — and behind her, unhurried, footsteps followed.
