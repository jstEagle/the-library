## 3. What the Ledger Remembers

The Warden archive did not believe in windows, which Zephyrine took as a professional courtesy. Neither did she believe in breaking into it, and yet here she was at half past midnight, standing in the rain across the street from a building that looked like a tomb designed by someone who hated tombs for being too cheerful.

"You're stalling," said Halvard.

Halvard was dead. He had been dead for four years, and he spent most of them complaining about it. He lived now in the little whale-ivory pendant Zephyrine wore under her collarbone—a relic she'd carved from his finger bone, with his permission, three days before he died, because Halvard had never once let propriety interfere with an opportunity to be useful.

"I'm assessing," she said.

"You're counting reasons not to go in. I can hear you counting. You're on eleven."

"Twelve: I am a licensed artisan with a clean record."

"Thirteen: you are a licensed artisan with a clean record who is about to commit burglary against the institution that outlaws everything you do after sundown." A dry rasp of laughter in her skull. "Fourteen: the lock on the east door is a joke. I installed half those locks myself when I still had hands. Go around back."

She went around back.

The east door's lock was, indeed, a joke—three tumblers and a warding sigil that had been painted over during some long-ago renovation, because the painters hadn't recognized it as anything but decoration. Zephyrine picked it with two bone pins from her sleeve while the rain tried its best to drown her, and Halvard narrated like a man watching his grandson take his first steps off a cliff.

"Left. Left again. No—the *other* left. Honestly, girl, how did you survive seven years in this city?"

"Cautiously. Until tonight."

"Tonight isn't survival anymore. Tonight is the ledger." His voice lost its teasing edge. In life, Halvard had been her master in the carving trade and, secretly, her teacher in everything else—the listening, the hearing, the quiet crime of being what she was. He had also been, before that, before she knew him, something else entirely: a cataloguer for the Wardens themselves, until he'd seen one page too many and walked out with his principles and nothing else. "You remember what I told you about the restricted stacks?"

"That they're behind the iron door, third sublevel, and that if anyone asks, I'm delivering a repair order for a cracked reliquary."

"And?"

"And that the Wardens catalogue magic the way misers catalogue gold—they count it, they fear it, and they never, ever spend it."

"Good girl. Now stop reciting and start walking."

* * *

The archive interior smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of paper that had been forbidden so long it had forgotten how to be read. Zephyrine moved through the dark by feel and by Halvard's voice, past shelves of confiscated charms in numbered boxes, past jars of things she decided firmly not to look at, down the stair to the third sublevel where the iron door stood waiting.

The sigil on the iron door was live. She felt it the way you feel a hand hovering near your cheek—not touching, but aware of you.

"It'll taste you," Halvard warned. "Warden wards don't test for keys. They test for *nature*. Anything magical touches that door, it screams."

"So we knock politely?"

"We go through the wall instead. Two feet left of the doorframe there's a seam where the masons cut corners in the old days. The stone's soft as bread. And—" here his voice did something complicated, pride and grief tangled together, "—and I know, because I put a weakness there on purpose, forty years ago, for exactly this kind of night. I just never thought it would be you using it."

Zephyrine pressed her palm flat against the stone and carved. It was the work she was born for, the work beneath the charms and trinkets: bone and stone were cousins, both of them memory given structure, and her knife sang through the soft seam in slow, patient strokes while sweat ran cold down her spine. The ward tasted her gift through the wall and shivered, uncertain, like a dog hearing a whistle pitched too high to locate.

Then the block came free, and she was through.

The restricted stacks were smaller than she'd imagined. That somehow made it worse—all the erased history of Vaelhollow, shelved in a room the size of a generous pantry. Ledgers bound in grey leather. Boxes labeled in a clerk's careful hand: *PROPHECY, SUPPRESSED. ORACLE FRAGMENTS, MISC. DREAM-RECORDS, UNVERIFIED.*

"Dream-records," she murmured. "Corin's dream."

"Third shelf. Grey spine, brass corners. The Aldric accession ledgers—I told you, the year the crown changed hands, the Wardens recorded everything and then buried it. Find the year. Find the page."

She found it. The ledger was heavier than it had any right to be, as though the paper itself were reluctant. She set it on the reading table, lit the small hooded lamp she'd brought, and opened it to the ribbon marker some frightened clerk had left there a hundred years ago and no one had dared move since.

The handwriting was cramped, urgent, the script of a person writing fast because they believed they would be stopped:

*The oracle of the Hollow Gate speaks true or dies trying. She names the crown's return and the unmaking of it. She names two figures.*

There were sketches.

Zephyrine had seen her own face every morning for thirty-one years. She knew the stubborn set of her own jaw, the way her hair fell wrong no matter what she did to it, the small scar above her brow from the time she'd fallen out of Halvard's apple tree at nine years old and lied about it for a week.

All of these things looked up at her from a page drawn before her grandmother was born.

The sketch was rough charcoal, hurried, but unmistakable: a woman with her jaw, her scar, her wrong-falling hair, bent over a carving knife. Beside her, a second figure—a man, tall, formal in his bearing even in charcoal, with a face she had looked across a dinner table at four nights ago while he'd charmed her and she'd pretended not to enjoy it.

Beneath the drawings, the same clerk's hand had written:

*The hearer and the hundred-year man. Between them, the crown stands or falls. God forgive us for what we do with this.*

Zephyrine sat down without meaning to. Her knees simply resigned.

"Halvard," she whispered. "Halvard, this is me."

"I see it." For once, the dead man had nothing clever to say. Then, quieter: "I saw it once, girl. Forty years ago. I read this page and I walked out of the Wardens the same day. I told myself I'd imagined the likeness. I told myself a great many things." A pause. "And then a bone-carver's apprentice walked into my shop with her grandmother's knife and your exact stubborn chin, and I never slept properly again."

"You *knew*? All this time—you knew and you didn't tell me?"

"If I'd told you, you'd have spent your whole life waiting for a man with a charcoal face instead of living one. I wanted you to arrive at this page *yourself*. Free. Not—" he searched for the word, and the dead are rarely at a loss for words, which frightened her more than the drawing did, "—not *assigned* to it."

Zephyrine stared at the ledger. *The hearer and the hundred-year man.* Corin. Charming, oddly formal Corin, who ate dinner with the posture of a portrait and spoke of a childhood dream with the weight of a man reciting scripture. *A tower of bells with no sound coming out. A woman at the base of it, carving. And a crown hanging in the sky like a second moon.* He'd laughed when he said it, embarrassed. She'd laughed too.

Neither of them had been laughing now, if either of them had known.

Her carefully built life—seven years of harmless charms, of careful invisibility, of being nobody in a city that punished somebody—lay open on the table like a dissected thing. Every step she'd taken to Vaelhollow. Her apprenticeship with Halvard. Even, possibly, the blind date her friend Odile had insisted on arranging, *he's perfect for you, Zeph, you'll love him*—had any of it been hers? Or had she been a marble rolling down a groove someone else had cut a century before she was born?

Vertigo, pure and simple. The room tilted. She gripped the table edge.

"I need a copy," she said, because work was the only antidote she knew. "I need—"

The lamp went out.

Not guttered. *Went out*, all at once, like a held breath released, and in the sudden black Zephyrine heard—felt—the archive change around her. Dust settling differently. The particular attention of a building becoming aware of an intruder.

"Someone's coming," Halvard breathed. "Upstairs. Lamp-light. Two of them, maybe three. Girl, *move*."

She shoved the ledger's loose pages—the prophecy leaf itself, torn free along its old crease—into her shirt, closed the ledger, and was reaching for the wall-seam when the iron door at the top of the stairs swung open, and lamplight spilled yellow down the stairwell like something poured.

* * *

Corin had followed her.

He wasn't proud of it. He had, in fact, composed a short speech about how he wasn't proud of it, to be delivered later, ideally after she'd stopped wanting to kill him. But when he'd watched her leave the charm-shop that evening with a hooded lantern and the walk of a woman going to do something unforgivable, he had discovered that a hundred years of being maneuvered by other people's designs had left him exactly one rebellion available: he could refuse to wait in the dark to be told what happened next.

So he waited in the dark inside the archive instead, having slipped in ahead of her through means he preferred not to examine, and now he stood pressed into the shadow of the restricted stacks, heart hammering, watching lamplight descend the stairs toward the woman whose face had hung in his dreams since he was six years old.

He knew the Wardens' rounds. He'd helped design them—or rather, the thing wearing the king's crown had used his hands to design them, decades ago, in another of the lives it had spent him on. The night patrol checked the archive at the half hour. Which meant he had perhaps four minutes before the interruption became a catastrophe, and Zephyrine—brilliant, furious, terrified Zephyrine, who had just found her own face in a book older than her grandmother—was frozen between the stacks like a rabbit deciding whether the fox had seen it.

He stepped out of the shadow and put his hand gently over her mouth.

She bit him. Of course she bit him. He'd have been disappointed otherwise.

"*It's me,*" he breathed, when she'd loosened her teeth enough to permit speech. "It's Corin. Don't scream. Patrol, ninety seconds, maybe less. There's a service corridor behind the dream-record shelf—push the third box sideways, there's a gap in the mortar. I've known about it since before your parents were born, which sounds mad, and I will explain everything, I swear it, but *not here*."

Her eyes in the dark were wide and bright and full of a question shaped exactly like the ledger she clutched to her chest. He saw her glance at it, then at him, doing arithmetic he couldn't follow—and then, worse, arithmetic he *could*: the dates, the drawing, the dream he'd described over dinner, the way he'd gone pale when she'd mentioned the archive earlier that day.

"You knew," she whispered. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"Not all of it. Not—" The lamplight on the stairs brightened. Boots. "Zephyrine. Please. Ninety seconds."

Something in her face closed, the way a shop closes—shutters down, sign turned, warmth withdrawn behind glass. But she pushed the third box sideways, and the gap in the mortar was there, and she went through it first without looking back, which Corin understood to be both forgiveness withheld and permission granted, provisionally, under protest, terms and conditions unspecified.

They came out into a rain-slick alley behind the archive, and the moment the night air touched them she rounded on him, ledger-page crumpled in her fist, rain plastering her hair down in the way he privately found magnificent and knew better than to say so.

"A century," she said. "That drawing is a hundred years old. *I'm* a hundred years old, apparently. We both are. So you're going to stand there in the rain and tell me what you are, Corin, and why a dead oracle drew your face next to mine before my mother's mother learned to walk—and you're going to tell me now, or I swear by every dead soul I've ever listened to, I will carve your name into a charm and make your ghost regret ever meeting me."

Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled the hour, and Corin—who had heard that same bell toll ten thousand times, who had been pulled forward through the years by a prophecy he'd never asked to serve—felt the last of his disguises fall away like wet cloth.

"My name is Corin Vale," he said. "And I am not what you think I am. What I am is older. What I am is *bait*."

Lightning stitched the sky above Vaelhollow, and in its white flash Zephyrine saw his face clearly for the first time—not the charming dinner companion, but something wearier and far more frightened, a man holding a door shut against a century.

"And the thing that sent me to find you," Corin said quietly, "is sitting on the throne."
