## 4. The Thing in the Crown

The relic arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth and delivered by a boy who smelled of fish and had clearly been paid not to ask questions.

Zephyrine paid him anyway. She tipped well; it was one of the few luxuries her double life allowed, and besides, the boy's grandmother had died in the spring and he carried a lock of her hair in his pocket like a stone he couldn't put down. Zephyrine heard the old woman humming as the boy left — some fishing song about a girl named Bettine who was worth three of any man — and she let herself smile at it before the door closed.

Then she unwrapped the oilcloth, and stopped smiling.

It was a brooch. Silver, tarnished nearly black, shaped like a swan with its wings folded wrong — folded *inward*, as if the bird were trying to hold itself together. A single garnet sat where an eye should be, and even before Zephyrine touched it, even before her fingertips brushed the cold metal, she could feel something on the other side of it. Not the usual murmur. Not the chatty, domestic dead she'd spent seven years listening to through bone and ivory and whale-tooth.

This was older. Heavier. Like pressing your ear to a door and hearing the sea.

"Where did you come from?" she murmured.

The brooch didn't answer, which was normal. Relics never answered questions directly. They answered everything *else* — the weather the day they were made, the hands that held them, the arguments conducted over them. You had to carve to listen properly, open a channel through the material the way you'd drill a tap into a maple. And so Zephyrine set the brooch in her clamps, took up her finest burin, and began to work a hairline groove along the swan's folded wing.

The dead woman came through all at once, the way deep water comes through a cracked dam.

*— they made me wear it to my own wedding, can you imagine, silver at a spring wedding, my mother wept, though not for the fashion reasons —*

"Madam," Zephyrine said carefully, "who are you?"

*— Queen Isolde, if you please, though I was Maren-of-the-Harbor-Quarter long before anyone pinned a crown question on me, and I will thank you not to shout, I am dead but I am not deaf —*

Zephyrine's burin slipped. She stared at the brooch. At the swan. At the garnet eye that suddenly seemed very much like an eye that was looking back.

"You're the old queen," she whispered. "You're Aldric's wife."

*I am his widow,* said Isolde of Vaelhollow, with a precision that suggested the distinction mattered enormously to her. *Which is more than most people remember, and considerably more than the thing wearing his face would like anyone to remember.*

The workshop went very quiet. Outside, a cart rattled past; someone was arguing cheerfully about the price of onions. The ordinary sounds of Vaelhollow, the city Zephyrine had spent seven years learning to be invisible in.

"The thing," Zephyrine repeated. "Wearing his face."

*Oh, good,* said the dead queen. *You're quick. The last carver I spoke to took four hours to get there, and by then the Wardens had come for him, so do forgive me if I hurry.* A pause, and when Isolde spoke again her voice had changed — lost its tartness, gained something underneath it that made the hairs on Zephyrine's arms stand up. *Girl. Bone-carver. Listen to me, because I have been waiting thirty years for someone with ears to listen.*

"Aldric died," Zephyrine said slowly. It wasn't a guess anymore. It was falling down a staircase and naming each step as it hit. "Aldric died years ago."

*Nineteen years after the coronation. A fever. Ordinary, undramatic, the kind of death that takes kings and fishermen alike and doesn't care which.* Isolde's voice tightened. *And in the hour between his last breath and the breath the court expected next, the crown opened its eye. What stood up from the deathbed wore Aldric's body because the body was still warm and the devotion was still flowing. Thirty years now it has fed. Every parade. Every feast of Saint Aldric's Day. Every mother telling her child the king loves you, the king watches over you, be good for the king. It drinks belonging, carver. Love is its wine and loyalty is its bread, and this city has set a table for it every single day for thirty years without knowing what sits at the head of it.*

Zephyrine discovered she was gripping the edge of her workbench hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Why are you telling me?"

*Because you have ears,* Isolde said simply. *And because the crown silences us. Do you know why the Wardens outlawed magic, girl? Why hearing was the first gift forbidden? It isn't fear of fire-throwers or storm-callers. Fire burns out. No — the crown cannot abide witnesses. Every soul it erases, every voice it muffles, every memory it lets rot in the archive dust — that is its harvest. The dead of Vaelhollow are its larder. And you—*

The brooch shuddered in the clamp. The garnet eye flickered — or seemed to.

*— and you, it has not noticed yet. That is the only mercy either of us has. There is a young man coming to you. Has he come already?*

Zephyrine's mouth went dry. "Corin."

*Corin.* The name came out flat, like a coin dropped on stone. *Yes. Ask him what he dreams. Ask him what he was sent to find. And when he tells you — and he will tell you, girl, because whatever else he is, he is not good enough at lying to survive you — decide quickly whether you hate him. We have very little time, and hatred is a luxury we cannot afford to carry far.*

"Isolde—" Zephyrine leaned close to the brooch. "How do I stop it? How does anyone stop a thing like that?"

For a long moment the dead queen said nothing, and Zephyrine had the terrible sense of a woman on the other side of a door, deciding how much truth the door could bear.

*You don't stop it,* Isolde said at last. *Not alone. Not with charms and hiding. You need the fire that binds, and the ones the crown has buried deepest, and a vessel willing to refuse it. But that is a conversation for when you believe me. Go and look at your king tomorrow, carver. Look closely. Look at how the crowd looks at him. Then come back and tell me what you saw.*

"I don't understand—"

*No one ever does, the first time.* And Isolde's voice was already receding, folding back into the silver like a swan drawing its wings in, holding itself together. *Ask Corin about the dream. Ask him tonight. And carver — whatever he says, however it lands — remember that bait does not choose the hook.*

The brooch went silent. The garnet eye dimmed to ordinary stone.

Zephyrine sat in her workshop for a long time, listening to the onion-cart and the gulls and the ordinary breathing city, and understood that nothing about her life was going to be ordinary again.

She also understood, with the particular clarity of a woman whose evening had just been ruined by a dead queen, that she had a dinner date in three hours, and that she was going to keep it, because Corin knew something, and because — she admitted it to the empty room with some irritation — she liked him, and liking people had always been her most expensive habit.

* * *

Corin was early.

He was standing outside the Gilded Perch — a restaurant slightly too nice for either of their incomes, chosen by Zephyrine precisely because the noise would cover conversation — and when he saw her coming he did the thing he always did, straightening up like a soldier called to attention and then immediately trying to disguise it as a casual lean against a lamppost. He was bad at casual. He was bad at a great many things that came naturally to other people, and Zephyrine had found this oddly endearing over the course of exactly one and a half dates.

"You're staring," she said, arriving beside him.

"I'm greeting," Corin said. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Greeting is anticipatory."

"Greeting is anticipatory," Zephyrine repeated. "Who taught you to talk? A treaty?"

"My mother, mostly. And a great deal of etiquette instruction." He offered his arm with a formality that belonged to another century, and she took it, and they went in.

Dinner proceeded the way their first dinner had: pleasantly, strangely. Corin ate with excellent manners and no evident pleasure, as though food were a duty he performed beautifully. He asked questions about bone-carving that were too precise for a curious layman — *do you find the material remembers differently depending on the age of the animal? does worked bone hold sound better than raw?* — and when Zephyrine gave careful, licensed-charm-seller answers, he accepted them with a nod that said he knew they were careful and loved her for offering them anyway.

It was over the second course that he finally set down his fork.

"I have to tell you something," he said, "and I've been rehearsing it since Tuesday, and every version sounded insane, so I'm going to say the unrehearsed version and beg your patience."

Zephyrine's heart, which had been braced since the brooch went silent, braced further. "Go on."

"I've had a dream," Corin said. "Since I was a child. The same dream, every night, or near enough. There's a tower with no door. There's a bell that rings downward instead of upward. And there's a woman at a workbench, carving, and the light comes from below her hands, and she's making something that will either save the city or end it, and I wake up before I see which." He looked at her across the candle flame, and his voice dropped. "In the dream, I know her. Not recognize — *know*. The way you know a word you've said ten thousand times. And three weeks ago I walked into a bookshop on Cordwainer Lane and there she was, arguing with a bookseller about the price of goose quills, and I forgot every single thing I was sent here to do except sit down across from her."

The restaurant roared on around them — laughter, cutlery, a waiter announcing the specials — and Zephyrine sat inside a small, ringing silence.

"Sent here," she said.

Corin winced. "That's the part I rehearsed badly."

"By whom, Corin?" She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice of a woman discussing the wine. Under the table, her hand found the little whale-tooth charm she carried in her pocket — her habit, her comfort, her ear pressed to the world. "Sent by whom, to do what?"

He told her. All of it, in a low, rapid voice, the words tumbling like men escaping a burning building. A circle of the old families, keepers of a ledger a century old. A prophecy in a dead dialect that named a bone-carver who would hear what the crown had silenced. His own strange history — the dream that had never let him age quite right, the tutors who taught him things no living tutor remembered, the years of preparation for a single task: *find her, earn her trust, bring her to the ledger.*

"They call it the Vigil," he finished. "I've called it that my whole life. It sounds noble when you say it fast." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "I'm a lure, Zephyrine. I'm the pretty hook on the end of somebody else's line. And the worst part — the part I can't —" He stopped. Started again. "I stopped being their instrument somewhere around the second date. I just didn't know how to say it. Bait doesn't get a lot of practice at honesty."

*Bait does not choose the hook,* Isolde had said. Zephyrine closed her eyes briefly.

When she opened them, Corin was watching her with the expression of a man awaiting sentencing, and despite everything — despite the vertigo, despite the dead queen's warnings, despite the fact that her carefully constructed life had apparently been designed a century before she was born — despite all of it, she found herself almost laughing.

"You argued with a bookseller about quills," she said. "That's your opening move. That's what a hundred years of prophecy prepared you for."

"It seemed important at the time," Corin said with dignity. "You were being robbed."

"I was being *quoted fairly*."

"You were being robbed gently. There's a difference. Staring is rude; greeting is anticipatory."

And then she did laugh, helplessly, into her napkin, while the waiter hovered uncertainly, and something in Corin's shoulders came down an inch — the inch a man's shoulders come down when he realizes the sentence might not be execution after all.

"Here's what I know that you don't," Zephyrine said, when she'd recovered. She glanced around, lowered her voice until it was barely above the candle-flame. "I talked to a relic today. A brooch. It belonged to Queen Isolde."

Corin went very still. "Isolde died four hundred years ago."

"No," Zephyrine said. "She died thirty-one years ago, in a bed, of a fever, three days after her husband — and nobody remembers her at all, Corin. Nobody. There's no portrait, no tomb, no feast day. I checked. I've been checking since sundown. A queen of Vaelhollow, erased so thoroughly that even the archives have a hole where she should be." She watched his face change as the implication landed. "Your Vigil thinks the prophecy is about unbinding a king. Your Vigil is reading half a ledger. Aldric isn't imprisoned by the crown, Corin. Aldric is *gone*. What's sitting on that throne wears his shape the way a glove wears a hand, and it eats what the city feels for him, and it has spent thirty years eating the dead to keep anyone from noticing."

The candle guttered between them. Somewhere across the restaurant, a string quartet sawed away at something cheerful.

Corin sat with it. To his credit — and Zephyrine was beginning to suspect his credit ran deeper than any of his handlers had guessed — he didn't argue, didn't deflect, didn't reach for the comfortable version. He just sat with the horror the way you sit with a wounded bird, carefully, both hands.

"The Vigil sent me to find a weapon," he said quietly. "They didn't tell me what it was aimed at."

"They may not know. Or they may know exactly, and want their own glove on the throne when it's empty." She turned her wineglass slowly by its stem. "Either way, we can't go back to them. And we can't stay here. If the crown notices me — Isolde says it hasn't yet, but *yet* is doing a lot of work in that sentence —"

"Then we need somewhere it can't hear," Corin said. And then, hesitantly, like a man testing ice: "I know a way down. Under the city. Places the Wardens stopped mapping a hundred years ago. I was given the knowledge in case you — in case the plan required extraction." His mouth twisted. "I was given a great deal in case the plan required things. None of it included instructions for what to do if I fell in love with the target."

Zephyrine looked at him for a long moment.

She thought about seven years of small safe lies. About a widow's mourning charm and a fisher-boy's grandmother humming about Bettine. About a dead queen folded inside a swan, waiting thirty years for ears. About the word *belonging*, which the crown ate, and which Zephyrine had never once been permitted to taste herself.

"We'll need supplies," she said at last. "And I'm bringing the brooch. And Corin — if this is another layer, if there's a third reveal under the second one, I will carve you into a hat rack and sell you to a sailor."

"That's fair," Corin said solemnly. "I'd wear poorly as a hat rack, but I'd accept the principle."

They rose. He paid — of course he paid, with the strange old coins of a family that measured time in centuries — and they stepped out into the Vaelhollow night, where the palace glowed on its hill above the city like a lamp full of something that was not light.

Zephyrine felt it then, for the first time. Faint, at the edge of her gift, at the very limit of what bone could carry: a vast and patient attention, turning over the city beneath it like a tongue tasting the inside of a cheek.

It paused, briefly, on the Gilded Perch.

On her.

Then moved on — sated, unhurried, certain of its meal.

"Walk faster," Zephyrine said, and took Corin's arm, and did not look back at the hill.
