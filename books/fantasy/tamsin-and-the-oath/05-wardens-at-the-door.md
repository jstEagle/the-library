## 5. Wardens at the Door

The trouble with a small safe life, Zephyrine thought as she swept bone dust from her workbench, is that it teaches you to love the floor you're standing on. And floors, she had learned, could be pulled out from under a person with very little ceremony.

It was the last mild evening of autumn, and she was doing what she did most evenings: carving. The shop smelled of beeswax and marrow-oil, and the little lamp on her bench threw warm circles across trays of half-finished work — buttons, hairpins, a set of chess pieces for the cooper's son, and one delicate mourning charm shaped like a sleeping wren, commissioned by Widow Ansel three days past.

The dead man inside the wren was complaining about wallpaper.

"It was *green*, Zephyrine," Aldous Ansel said, his voice arriving through the charm the way voices arrive through water, slightly blurred but unmistakably aggrieved. "Fifty years I told her. Fifty years. 'Marta,' I said, 'that green will be the death of me.' And now look."

"You died of a bad heart, Aldous, not a color scheme."

"A heart weakened by *green*. There's a difference." He sighed, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "She's keeping the curtains, though. The yellow ones. Tell her — when she comes for the charm — tell her the yellow ones were always my favorites."

Zephyrine's knife paused. "You told me you hated them."

"I lied. She bought them for our anniversary. A man can hate a curtain and love why it hangs." He was quiet a moment, and then, softer: "That's the whole trick of marriage, girl. Nobody tells you that."

She smiled down at the wren and carved the last feather into its wing. This was the part of her life no Warden statute covered: not magic, exactly. Just listening. Just carrying messages between people who loved each other and had run out of time to say so. If that was a crime, then Vaelhollow had outlawed tenderness along with everything else, which — knowing the Wardens — it probably had.

Seven years she'd kept this shop. Seven years of licensed carvings sold honestly over the counter, and unlicensed listenings done after dark with the shutters drawn. Seven years of learning which customers whispered and which ones wept, of keeping a ledger with two columns, one for coin and one for secrets. Seven years of being nobody in particular, which in Vaelhollow was the safest thing a person could be.

And for seven years, she had been careful.

But careful, she was beginning to suspect, only worked until it didn't.

* * *

Corin arrived at dusk with bread and a bottle of plum wine, because he had apparently decided that courtship meant feeding her, and she hadn't yet found the strength of character to object.

"You know," he said, settling onto the stool across from her bench like he'd been sitting there all his life, "most shops in this city smell like tallow and despair. Yours smells like—" he sniffed "—soup made of trees."

"Beeswax."

"I knew it was one of those." He tore the bread and handed her the larger piece without appearing to notice he'd done it, which was, she was learning, simply how Corin was built. "Widow Ansel came by while I waited outside. She looked — lighter. Like someone had finally told her something true."

Zephyrine said nothing. She was still deciding how much truth Corin got, this man out of a century-old prophecy book who had appeared in her life with suspiciously good timing and an even more suspicious dream. But he'd helped her into the Warden archive, and he'd stood watch in the rain without complaint, and there was a steadiness in him that felt less like strategy and more like furniture. You could lean on it whether or not you trusted where it came from.

"The ledger," she said instead. "I've been thinking about the dates."

"Dreadful habit. Thinking."

"The sketch of us is dated ninety-eight years before the archive copy was made. Which means whoever drew it saw something. Or believed they did." She turned the wren charm over in her palm. "Prophecies don't just happen, Corin. Someone makes them happen, or wants to."

He was quiet long enough that she looked up. In the lamplight his face did something complicated — guilt trying to pass itself off as concern and not quite managing it.

"Zeph—"

The knock came at the front door.

Three raps. Measured. Official. The kind of knocking that didn't expect an answer so much as grant one a formality.

Zephyrine went cold from the scalp down. She knew that knock. Every unregistered soul in Vaelhollow knew that knock; it featured in nightmares the way wolves featured in shepherd's tales.

"Wardens," she breathed.

"How do you know? Could be a customer."

"At this hour? With *that* knock?" She was already moving, snuffing the lamp, sweeping the day's commissions into a drawer. "Back door sticks. Cellar's faster. Go —"

The second knock came harder, and with it a voice, flat as slate: "By authority of the Warden Conclave. Open in the name of the Crown."

*The crown.* Something in Zephyrine's chest flinched at the word, some animal instinct older than thought.

"They're not here for charms," Corin said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"No." Her hands were shaking as she pried up the loose flagstone behind the counter — the one that had taken her two months of evenings to loosen properly, the one that led down into the old bone-cellars beneath the Carvers' Quarter, tunnels her trade had dug into the city's foundations centuries before anyone thought to fear them. "They're here for me. And someone told them where to look."

The trapdoor opened onto darkness and the smell of deep earth. Below, her cellar — her real cellar — held everything she couldn't bear to burn: her mother's carving knives, her mentor Edvard's letters, twenty years of sketches.

Twenty seconds, maybe, before they broke the door.

"Take the stairs," she said. "I'm right behind you."

She wasn't. Because even as Corin dropped into the dark, even as she swung one leg over the edge, her eyes caught the lamp she'd snuffed — and beside it, the shelf above her bench. The wren charm. Widow Ansel's wren, with Aldous inside it, waiting to tell Marta about the curtains.

If the Wardens found it, they'd find a relic. They'd find proof. And Marta Ansel would spend the rest of her grief answering questions in a stone room.

Zephyrine swore, snatched the wren off the shelf, closed her fist around it — and heard, absurdly, distantly, Aldous say: *"Girl, RUN."*

The front door splintered inward just as she dropped through the floor and hauled the trapdoor shut above her head.

* * *

The bone-cellars were black as the inside of a closed mouth.

Zephyrine descended by touch and memory, one hand on the wall, the wren clutched to her chest, Corin's breathing somewhere ahead of her in the dark. Behind and above, muffled through stone and timber, came the sounds of the raid: boots, orders barked in that particular Warden cadence that treated every sentence like a sentencing, and then —

Smoke. The smell of it threaded down through the floorboards, acrid and wrong.

"They're burning it," she whispered. "Why would they *burn* it?"

"Licenses, stock, records," Corin said grimly. "If they can't prove the crime, they destroy the evidence either way. It's cheaper than trials." He caught her elbow in the dark, steadied her on a slick step. "Zephyrine. We have to keep moving."

But she had stopped. Not because of the smoke. Because of the sound.

Through the wren in her fist, faint as a memory, Aldous was singing. Some old harvest song, tuneless and sweet, the kind of thing a man hums to keep someone company in the dark. And around him — she strained, she *listened* — other voices were rising through the bones of the city. The cellars themselves were full of them. Old carvers' bones packed the walls of these tunnels, and the dead of the Carvers' Quarter had never entirely left their work.

*"Down and left,"* murmured a woman's voice, dry as chalk, from the wall itself. *"The third arch. The Wardens never learned the underways — too proud to ask the dead trades anything."*

"There's a guide," Zephyrine said wonderingly. "Corin — the walls are full of guides."

"That's either wonderful or horrifying."

"Historically it's been both."

They went down and left, through the third arch, the dead woman's directions coming soft and patient as a grandmother teaching stitches. The smoke-smell faded behind them. Somewhere above, seven years of Zephyrine's careful, small, safe life was going up in ash — the counter where she'd taught herself patience, the window display she changed with the seasons, the second chair she'd bought six months ago without letting herself think about why, and which Corin now sat in most evenings as if it had always been his.

She grieved it in real time, walking away from it in the dark. Grieved it the way you grieve a limb: not with drama, but with the constant astonished wrongness of its absence.

"I had a system," she said, and heard her own voice crack, and hated it. "I had a *life*. I paid my taxes. I filed my license. I was *boring*, Corin. I was aggressively, defensively boring for seven years, and it took them one evening to —"

"To find you." His voice was low. "Because someone told them where to look. Zephyrine, who knew about the shop? Who knew about *you*?"

The question landed like a stone in still water, and the ripples spread outward through everyone she'd ever trusted. Her supplier, Tolla, who weighed bone by candlelight and never asked questions. The widow. The cooper's son. Edvard's old colleagues. Corin himself, a voice added helpfully, treacherously, in her own head — *the man from the prophecy book, the man who appeared from nowhere, the man who followed you into an archive.*

"I don't know," she said. And then, because the dark made honesty cheap: "I don't know, and that's the worst part. That's what they do, isn't it? They don't just take people. They make you unable to trust anyone ever again. They turn every friendship into a suspect list."

Ahead, the tunnel opened into a wider chamber where groundwater dripped somewhere unseen, and the air moved — moving air meant a way out. Zephyrine pressed her palm against the wall as they passed, an instinctive gesture, thanks given to the dead woman who had steered them.

*"Mind the fourth step,"* the chalk-dry voice said. *"It's been rotten since the flood year. And girl —"* a pause, the dead gathering words the way the living gather breath — *"they burned your shop, but they didn't burn you. There's a difference. Learn it fast."*

Zephyrine stopped.

Something shifted in her chest — not the grief, which sat in her like swallowed river-stone, but underneath the grief. Something smaller and hotter. A coal, newly lit.

For seven years she had aimed her fear downward and inward. Fear of discovery, fear of the Wardens, fear of her own gift — she had folded it all small and kept it in the cellar of herself, next to the trapdoor, checked nightly. She had spent seven years being afraid *correctly*, politely, in approved directions.

But standing in the dark with ash falling through the cracks of her old life, Zephyrine discovered that fear had a ceiling. And above that ceiling lived the people who had knocked.

Not the wardens at her door — they were hounds, and hounds only follow. Above them: the captains who signed the burn orders. Above them: the Conclave that wrote the statutes making tenderness contraband. And above *them*, crowned and distant and worshipped, the king in his palace, whose devotion-devouring shape had stared at her from a century-old page beside her own face.

The fury arrived quietly, which was how she'd know it was real. Not a firework. A forge-fire. Slow, and hot, and *aimed upward* for the first time in her life.

"Zephyrine?" Corin's hand found her shoulder. "We should move. They'll check the registered cellar entrances by morning."

"They knew about the shop," she said. "They knew about the back room. They knew *when* to come." She turned toward the moving air, toward whatever came next, and heard her own voice come out level and strange. "Someone I trusted fed me to them, Corin. And I intend to find out who."

"And then what?"

She touched the wren in her pocket, where Aldous had gone quiet, keeping vigil.

"And then," she said, "I stop being boring."

They climbed toward the distant gray suggestion of an exit — an old coal-chute, the dead woman said, opening behind the tanneries on Mire Street — and Zephyrine took one last look back down the tunnel, back toward the smoke and the heat and the small safe life guttering out above.

Behind them, far off, muffled by stone, the bells of the Warden tower began to ring.

Not the alarm pattern. Not the fire pattern.

The summons pattern. Three-and-one, three-and-one — the rhythm reserved, Edvard had once told her, for exactly one purpose in all of Vaelhollow:

A royal decree.

The crown, it seemed, had noticed her too.
