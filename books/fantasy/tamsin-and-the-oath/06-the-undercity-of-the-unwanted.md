## 6. The Undercity of the Unwanted

The bone-cellars went down farther than Zephyrine had ever imagined, and she had spent the last two days imagining very hard.

Corin walked ahead with a lantern he'd stolen from a Warden post—"borrowed," he insisted, "we'll return it when the government is reconstituted"—and she followed, one hand trailing the wall of packed earth and old foundation stone. Above them, her shop was ash. Seven years of charms, carvings, careful small work, all of it smoke. She was trying not to think about it. She was failing at a professional level.

"Mind the step," Corin said.

"Which step?"

"The one that isn't there."

She found it with her shin. "You're a wonderful guide."

"I'm told I have a face for it." He held the lantern lower so she could see the gap where stairs had once been, now replaced by iron rungs driven into the stone. "There's a ladder. Then a tunnel. Then—well. You'll see."

"Corin." She gripped the first rung. "Where are we going?"

He looked back at her, lantern-light carving his face into gold and shadow, and for a moment the charming formality dropped away entirely. What was underneath looked tired. Old, even, though that made no sense.

"Down," he said. "We're going down."

* * *

Zephyrine had always known Vaelhollow had underlevels. Everyone knew. The city was built like a liar's story—one layer stacked on another until nobody could remember what the truth had been. The Wardens sealed the deep ways a century ago and put it in all the schoolbooks: *Nothing lives below. Nothing wants to.*

The schoolbooks were wrong, which surprised her not at all. Schoolbooks usually were.

The ladder ended in a tunnel that smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, faintly, of bread. Actual bread. Her stomach made an undignified noise, and Corin had the grace not to laugh, mostly.

Then the tunnel opened, and she stopped walking.

It was a street. An actual street, underground, cobbled with river stones and lit by lamps that burned without flame—a soft amber glow that came from the glass itself. Houses leaned against the cavern walls, built from ship timber and cathedral scraps and, in one case, what appeared to be an entire retired ferryboat turned on its side. Laundry lines strung between lamp posts. A cat regarded her from a windowsill with the particular contempt of a cat who has seen many newcomers and outlived most of them.

"Oh," Zephyrine said, because eloquence had abandoned her.

"People live here," she said, when eloquence partially returned.

"People have always lived here," Corin said. "That's rather the point. The Wardens say magic is gone from Vaelhollow. The Wardens also say the deep levels are empty. The Wardens are men who write reports about the world instead of looking at it."

A door opened ahead of them—a real door, painted a defiant cheerful blue—and a woman stepped out carrying a basket of onions. She saw them, saw the lantern, saw the ash still smudged on Zephyrine's sleeves, and did exactly what people in hiding learn to do: nothing. Her face closed like a shop shutter. She turned and walked briskly the other way.

"They'll know you're here within the hour," Corin said. "Word travels faster than fire down here. That's not a metaphor. There's an actual woman named Doss who runs messages, and she is genuinely faster than fire, which is a long story involving a wager and a chimney."

"Corin." Zephyrine caught his sleeve. "You've been here before."

It wasn't a question. He didn't pretend otherwise. "Yes."

"How many times?"

"That depends," he said carefully, "on how you count time. Which is also a long story. One I owe you. But not here, and not yet, because if I tell it to you in a tunnel you'll have nowhere to walk away to, and I'd rather give you the option."

She stared at him. He stared back, and there it was again—that flicker of something ancient behind the young face, like a coin worn smooth showing the ghost of an older king's profile.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm keeping a list."

"A list?"

"Of stories you owe me. It's getting long. There's the dream. The prophecy book. Why you knew about the archive's east entrance. And now this."

"Add 'the ferryboat,'" Corin said solemnly. "Everyone asks about the ferryboat."

* * *

They were fed before they were questioned, which Zephyrine decided was the mark of a civilized society. The meal happened in a long low room beneath the blue door, crowded with perhaps thirty people and smelling gloriously of onion soup. Nobody asked their names. Everybody watched them eat. Zephyrine understood the etiquette immediately: *eat first, prove you're not a Warden plant, then talk.* Wardens, she'd heard, always refused food on principle. It was apparently the only principle they shared with saints.

An old man across the table kept glancing at her hands.

"You're a carver," he said finally. Not accusingly. Wonderingly.

"Licensed," she said automatically, and then felt foolish, because her license had presumably burned along with everything else. "I was licensed."

"What kind of work?"

"Mourning charms, mostly. Keepsakes. Small things." She turned her spoon in the soup. "Harmless things."

The old man smiled with only half his mouth. "Down here, girl, we don't much believe in harmless magic. We believe in *kept* magic. Magic you hold close and feed honestly." He tapped his own chest. "Mine's finding. Lost things, lost people. Haven't lost a child in this level in eleven years."

"That's remarkable," Zephyrine said, and meant it.

"It's a living," he said, which was somehow more remarkable.

And then the room changed—not in any way she could name at first, and then she realized: everyone had gone quiet at once, the way a household goes quiet when the head of it walks in. Except the person walking in was small, gray-haired, and carrying a covered lantern like a woman transporting something extremely precious and mildly annoyed.

"That's Maren," Corin murmured. "Be normal."

"I'm always normal."

"You're currently gripping your spoon like a dagger."

She loosened her grip. Maren crossed the room, set her lantern on the table with exaggerated care, and looked at Zephyrine for a long moment. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they had the unsettling quality of eyes that had seen too much fire and found it wanting.

"So," Maren said. "You're the one from the ledger."

The soup went cold in Zephyrine's mouth. "You know about the ledger."

"Child, we've known about the ledger since before your grandmother was born. We just didn't know your *face* belonged to it." Maren sat down across from her, uninvited, unhurried. "Corin sent word ahead. Says you hear the dead through bone. Says the Wardens burned you out. Says you're either the answer to a hundred-year-old riddle or the bait in someone else's trap, and possibly both."

"He's thorough," Zephyrine said weakly.

"He's a liar with excellent penmanship," Maren said, without heat, as though stating the weather. "Now. Show me your hands."

Zephyrine showed her. Maren took them—her grip dry and strong as tree roots—and turned them over, studying the calluses, the fine scars from chisel slips, the ink stains that never quite washed out.

"Hm," said Maren, which was somehow both a diagnosis and a verdict.

"Hm?" said Zephyrine.

"You carve dead things and listen to them talk. That's the oldest trade there is, girl. Older than crowns. Older than lies." Maren released her hands. "Come with me. There's something you should hear. And I mean that literally, which will interest you."

* * *

Corin tried to come. Maren stopped him at the door with one raised finger, a gesture of such absolute authority that Zephyrine half expected the finger to be legally binding.

"She comes alone," Maren said.

"She doesn't know you," Corin protested. "For all she knows—"

"For all she knows, I'm going to murder her in a dragon vault. Yes. She's clever; she'll risk it." Maren glanced at Zephyrine. "You'll risk it. Because you're the sort who can't stand a locked door, and because whatever I'm about to show you, Corin can't show you himself, because he's part of the problem and hasn't admitted it yet."

Zephyrine looked at Corin. He looked pained, which she was beginning to understand was his resting expression underneath the charm.

"Go," he said quietly. "She's insufferable but she's safe. Safer than me, certainly."

"That's a low bar," Zephyrine said, and followed Maren into the dark.

* * *

They walked for twenty minutes through passages that grew warmer as they went, and stranger. The unlit lamps gave way to veins of something glowing in the rock itself—a dull red shimmer, like embers buried in the world's bones. Zephyrine's fingers itched. Her whole life, bone had spoken to her: the relics she carved, the keepsakes, the little pieces of the dead that remembered being alive. But this wasn't bone. This was older. This was the mountain itself, and it was murmuring.

"What is that glow?" she asked.

"Wakefulness," Maren said shortly. "Keep up."

The passage opened into a vault.

Zephyrine had expected—she didn't know what. A furnace. A forge. Something industrial. Instead the space was vast and round and quiet as a cathedral, its ceiling lost in darkness, and at its center, sunk into a basin of black stone, lay a coiled shape the size of a fishing boat.

It was a dragon. Or it had been. Its scales were the gray of long-dead coals, its wings folded like a ship's furled sails, and its chest—the only part of it that moved—rose and fell with the slow, labored rhythm of a bellows nearly empty. Beneath it, in the basin, pooled a shallow lake of light: dim orange, guttering, the last fire in the world that had never been lit by human hands.

Zephyrine stopped breathing.

Because she could *hear* it.

Not words. Not the way the dead spoke through her carvings, in fragments and complaints and sudden piercing griefs. This was deeper—a sound like a hymn played on a cracked bell, like wind through a canyon, like something enormous dreaming. And threaded through the grandeur, unmistakably, was exhaustion. A tiredness so old it had become geology.

"She's dying," Zephyrine whispered.

"She's been dying for ninety years," Maren said. "Slowly. Dragons don't do anything quickly, including leave." She walked to the basin's edge and knelt, and her voice changed—softened into something Zephyrine hadn't heard in it before. Tenderness, worn smooth by decades of repetition. "I sing to her. Every day. It doesn't heal her. But it keeps the fire company while it goes out. Everything deserves company at the end. Even this. *Especially* this."

Zephyrine approached slowly. Up close, the dragon's eye was open a sliver—a slit of ember-gold, watching her without surprise, as though it had been expecting her for a century and had simply gotten comfortable waiting.

"She knows what I am," Zephyrine breathed.

"Of course she does. She knew before you were born. Knew before the crown was forged, probably, and she's got opinions about that, let me tell you." Maren snorted. "Dragons invented holding grudges. Everything else is imitation."

"Why show me this? Why now?"

Maren rose. In the firelight her plain face looked suddenly fierce, a flint struck against the dark.

"Because tomorrow I'm going to teach you how to really listen—to the dead, to her, to everything the crown has been smothering in this city for thirty years. And you'll want to know why." She nodded at the guttering lake of light. "Because that fire is the only thing left that can break what the crown made. And because it's going out, Zephyrine Iversen. Weeks, maybe days. Whatever you're going to do about the thing wearing King Aldric's face—you'll do it on a deadline."

Above them, far above, through a hundred feet of stone, Zephyrine thought she heard a bell begin to ring.

Maren heard it too. Her jaw tightened.

"Well," she said. "That'll be the Wardens finding the cellar door. They always find it eventually." She picked up her lantern. "Question is, girl—when they come down here hunting you, are you the woman who ran and hid? Or the one who listened?"

In the basin, the dragon's ember eye slid shut, and in the deep place where Zephyrine's gift lived, something vast and tired murmured a single word she would carry for the rest of her life:

*Finally.*
