## 7. Lessons in Listening

Maren's kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and scorched rosemary, which Zephyrine learned was not a cooking failure but a deliberate choice. "The smoke remembers," Maren said, dropping another sprig into the brazier until it crackled and spat. "Fire's the oldest witness there is. Everything that ever burned left its story in the flame, if you know how to ask."

"I don't ask," Zephyrine said. "I mostly wish they'd stop talking."

The fire-singer laughed—a big, unguarded sound that filled the low-ceilinged room of the undercity safehouse and rattled the copper pots hanging from hooks overhead. She was a broad woman with burn-scarred forearms and gray threading through her black braid, and she moved through her kitchen with the easy authority of someone who had fed a hundred hungry fugitives and expected to feed a hundred more.

"Sit," she said, pointing at a stool by the brazier. "Eat first. Grief-work on an empty stomach makes people weepy and dramatic, and I've had enough drama this decade."

Zephyrine sat. The bread was dense and dark and better than anything she'd baked in seven years above ground, where she had measured out her life in careful, harmless portions: one charm sold here, one smile offered there, never too much of anything, never enough to be noticed.

She had been noticed anyway. That was how she'd come to be sitting in a cellar beneath the city she'd spent seven years being invisible in, eating bread with a wanted fire-singer while Corin—her blind date, her prophecy-portrait companion, her whatever-he-was—slept off his watch shift in the next room.

"The dead you hear," Maren said, settling across from her. "What are they like?"

Zephyrine considered the question properly, because no one had ever asked it before. Her clients asked *can you reach him*. The Wardens, if they'd caught her, would have asked *how long have you been doing it*. But what are they *like*—

"Thin," she said at last. "Like voices heard through a wall. They repeat themselves. They circle back to the same grievances, the same loves. The husband whose widow I carved for last week—he'd been dead four years and he was still furious about the wallpaper in the front room. Mustard yellow. He hated mustard yellow." She smiled despite herself. "He'd gotten very articulate about it over the years."

"And did you tell the widow?"

"I told her he loved her and he was at peace."

Maren snorted. "Liar."

"Liar," Zephyrine agreed. "But kind. There's a difference between lying and mercy."

"There is," Maren said, "and the crown has made a fortune confusing the two." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the ridges of old scars. "Now listen to me, bone-carver, because this is the lesson, and I'll only say it once, though knowing you, you'll make me repeat it anyway because you won't believe it the first time."

"That sounds accurate."

"The dead aren't echoes."

Zephyrine opened her mouth.

"You were going to argue."

"They *are* echoes," Zephyrine said. "I've listened to them for twenty years. They loop. They fade. A strong one might hold its shape for a century, but it's just—impressions. What they felt hardest at the end. The wallpaper. The unfinished letter. The name they called when they fell."

"That's what you hear *through your carvings*," Maren said. "That's what slips through a keyhole. You've been listening to the dead through a cracked door your whole life and concluding the house is empty." She held up a hand before Zephyrine could object. "Your gift is real. It's also starving. You carve a relic, you catch a fragment, a splinter, a single note—and you've built an entire understanding of music out of one note. Tonight we open the door."

The brazier popped. Somewhere deeper in the undercity, water dripped in slow rhythm against stone, patient as a metronome.

"How?" Zephyrine asked. And then, because honesty had become a habit somewhere between the archive break-in and the burning of her shop: "And why do you care? You don't know me. You're risking Warden attention on a stranger."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—the humor banked down to coals, the fierceness underneath showing through.

"Because thirty years ago I stood in the throne room of Vaelhollow and swore an oath to a king who was already dead, though none of us knew it. Because everything I loved got folded into that oath and used against me. Because I have spent my whole life since learning exactly how that thing in the crown works, and every person who learns it is one more person it can't eat quietly." She stood, joints cracking, and fetched a bundle from a chest in the corner—wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord worn soft from handling. "And because you heard the queen's ghost through a relic and came out the other side still willing to fight. Most people who learn what wears our king run. You got angry instead. Angry people can be taught."

* * *

The lesson began with a bowl of water.

"Just water?" Zephyrine said, skeptical. "No relic? No carving?"

"Water remembers being rain," Maren said. "Rain remembers falling on everyone. It's the most democratic substance in the world." She set the bowl on the floor between them, beside the brazier, so that firelight shivered across its surface. "Now. Your method. Walk me through it."

Zephyrine wrapped her hands around her knees. "I carve. Bone takes memory best—something about the density, the way it held a life inside it already. As I work, I listen. Whatever's attached to the bone comes through. Faintly. In pieces."

"And when you're done?"

"When I'm done, it fades. Days, sometimes weeks. Like a voice going around a corner."

Maren nodded slowly. "Because you're listening with your craft. The carving does the hearing; you just stand nearby. Fine for charmselling. Useless for war." She gestured at the bowl. "Tonight you listen with yourself. No tools. No distance. Nothing between you and them but your own nerve."

"What happens if I lose my nerve?"

"Then you pull back, and nothing happens except you'll hate yourself a little, and I'll feed you more bread." Maren's voice gentled. "I'm not going to pretend there's no risk. Opening wide to the dead, all of them, at once—that's a tide, girl, not a stream. But you've been standing in shallows your whole life pretending the ocean isn't there. It's time you saw it."

Zephyrine looked at the water. Ordinary water. She thought of her shop, burning. Of the ledger with her own face sketched a century ago. Of the queen's ghost, whispering truths through a relic that had cost Zephyrine three days of nosebleeds to hear clearly.

*Through a cracked door,* Maren had said.

"Tell me what to do," she said.

"Breathe. Put your hands flat on the floor—not the bowl, the floor. Stone holds everything; this city is built on its own bones. Let your gift out of the carving. All of it. Don't aim it anywhere. Just... unlatch the door and stand back."

Zephyrine breathed. Unlatched.

For a moment, nothing. The drip of water. The pop of rosemary. Corin turning over in the next room, the creak of a cot.

Then the floor *opened*.

Not literally—she would spend years failing to explain to anyone without the gift that it was not literal—but the sensation was of a trapdoor swinging wide beneath her, and instead of falling she was *flooded*. Sound poured up through the stone of Vaelhollow the way light pours through a cracked dam, and it was not thin, it was not looping, it was not echoes.

It was a city.

A fishwife from two hundred years gone, arguing cheerfully with a customer about the freshness of eels, her voice rich with the particular joy of winning an argument she'd had a thousand times. A child counting stairs in a tenement that had burned down before Zephyrine's grandmother was born, counting and counting, giggling on the landing. A stonemason humming while he worked, the same eight bars over and over, contented as a cat. A woman reciting poetry to her lover in a language Zephyrine didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand warmth. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Not fragments, not loops—*lives*, ongoing, layered, the whole deep sediment of everyone who had ever lived and died within these walls, still here, still themselves, still *present*.

Zephyrine made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from her own throat.

"They're—" Her hands pressed harder against the stone. Tears slid down her face and she didn't bother wiping them. "They're *people*. They're all still *here*. Twenty years I thought—I was hearing ghosts, leftovers, and they're just—"

"Witnesses," Maren said quietly. "The dead see everything. They remember everything. They're not stuck repeating their deaths—they're stuck repeating their deaths *because someone made the doors too small to walk through otherwise*. Now keep listening. There's a second lesson underneath the first, and I need you to find it yourself, because you won't believe it from me."

Zephyrine listened.

Beneath the fishwife and the child and the mason, beneath the vast warm sediment of the ordinary dead, she found... gaps. Holes in the fabric, like missing teeth. Whole districts of the undercity where the chorus went suddenly, horribly quiet—not silent the way empty rooms are silent, but silent the way a mouth is silent after a tooth is pulled. Places where voices should have been and weren't. And at the edges of each gap, the remaining dead spoke differently. Softer. Watchful. Afraid.

She followed the largest gap upward. Through stone, through foundations, through the roots of the palace itself, to the throne room she had visited only days ago in borrowed silks, playing the humble charm-seller while something ancient tasted her gift like a tongue against the roof of its mouth.

There, at the crown of the city, she found the shape of the silence.

It wasn't absence. Absence was passive. This was *work*. Something was drinking the dead—one by one, year by year, decade by decade—the way a leech feeds, slowly enough that no single loss alarms anyone. Every ghost that grew too clear, too coherent, too *witnessing*, got drawn up through the stones and into that crowned dark, and was unmade into fuel. Into devotion. Into the warm, mindless love that the people of Vaelhollow poured toward their king every festival day, never asking why their grief felt lighter than it should, why their dead seemed fainter than their grandparents' dead had been, why the old songs complained of crowds of ancestors and the new songs sang of quiet.

"He's not just wearing Aldric," Zephyrine whispered. "He's *eating* them. All of them. Everyone the city loves, he eats, and the city thanks him for it."

"Now you see it," Maren said. Her voice was rough. "Thirty years. Do you understand now why the Wardens outlaw hearing? It was never about chaos. Chaos doesn't frighten a thing like that. *Witnesses* frighten it. Every bone-carver arrested, every hedge-witch driven underground, every relic smashed—it was never law. It was a creature tidying its table."

The chorus surged around Zephyrine, and now that she knew what to listen for, she could hear them straining. The fishwife had stopped arguing about eels. The child had stopped counting. Thousands of voices, all turning, all leaning toward her the way flowers lean toward a window—all of them trying, with everything they had, to be *heard*.

And one voice, older than the rest, cut through the flood like a blade through silk.

*Bone-carver.* A woman's voice, iron and velvet. *You found the door. Now listen well, because we have waited thirty years for someone to open it, and we do not know how long you'll stay standing.*

Zephyrine gripped the stone. "I hear you. All of you. I—gods, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I didn't know—"

*Sorry later. Work now.* The voice drew closer, or focused sharper. *We are many, but we cannot touch the world. You can. Listen, and we will tell you what the living have forgotten.*

And they told her.

They told her about the vault beneath the Warden citadel where dragon-fire guttered in its cage, and how many days it had left—they could count its heartbeat through the stone, and the count was small. They told her about the wards on the palace, and which ones were old and true and which ones the crown-thing had let rot because it trusted no one would ever come. They told her about Aldric—not the monster, the man—buried alive beneath decades of stolen worship, still fighting, still himself, a candle of defiance in the belly of the thing that wore him.

And they told her, in a hundred voices braided into one, the thing that turned Zephyrine's awe to ash and her ash to iron:

*He knows you opened the door, bone-carver. He felt you the moment you did. He is deciding, even now, whether to kill you or keep you—and either way, he is sending for you.*

The connection snapped shut like a book closing. Zephyrine gasped, rocked back on her heels, the kitchen rushing in around her—smoke and rosemary and Maren's steady eyes and the ordinary dripping dark.

"Well?" Maren asked.

Zephyrine wiped her face with both hands. When she lowered them, she discovered that her fear was still there—of course it was—but it had been rearranged, shoved to the edges, crowded out at the center by something hotter and far more durable.

"They're witnesses," she said. "All of them. And he's been eating them for thirty years while the whole city applauds." She stood, legs unsteady, voice not. "Maren—he's sending for me. The dead say he's already decided."

From the doorway behind them, barefoot and sleep-rumpled, Corin said quietly:

"Then we'd better decide faster."
