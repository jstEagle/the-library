## 8. Corin's Hundred Years

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like patience.

I have been practicing it for a hundred years.

Corin Vael — no, that wasn't right either. Corin Ashvale. No. He sat on the edge of the narrow bed in the undercity guest room, which was really a storage alcove with a mattress and a curtain, and tried to remember his own name the way you'd try to remember a house you'd lived in as a child. The shape was there. The details kept sliding.

*Corin.* Just Corin, lately. The surname had worn away like a coin passed through too many hands, and he supposed that was fitting, because that was more or less what had happened to him.

Above him, through the floorboards, he could hear Zephyrine moving around in Maren's workshop. Not literally hear — he had no gift, that was the whole obscene joke of him — but the undercity carried sound strangely, and her footsteps had a rhythm he'd already learned without meaning to. Quick, then a pause. Quick, then a pause. She stopped whenever she was listening to something he couldn't hear. Which was often. Which was always.

He was supposed to be sleeping. They were all supposed to be sleeping, because tomorrow Maren wanted to show them the vault where the last dragon-fire guttered in its Warden cage, and Maren had opinions about punctuality that she expressed the way other people expressed religious conviction.

Instead Corin sat with his elbows on his knees and rehearsed.

That was what he did instead of courage. He rehearsed. He had done it before every meeting with his handlers, before every dinner, before the night he'd walked into a charm shop in the Lantern Quarter and said, with a smile he'd practiced in four different mirrors, *You must be Zephyrine Iversen. I'm told we were set up by mutual friends.*

There were no mutual friends. There never were. There was only the ledger, and the men who kept it, and the instruction they had given him so long ago that it had fossilized into something like scripture: *She will come to you. Be worth coming to.*

He had been worth coming to for a very long time. It was the only thing he'd ever been good at, and he was beginning to suspect it wasn't a skill at all. It was a wound wearing a skill's clothes.

* * *

The first thing you should understand about me — he thought, addressing her across the floorboards, across the dark, across everything he hadn't said at dinner or in the archive or in the bone-cellars while her life burned behind them — is that I didn't lie to you. Not exactly. Not mostly. I just let you believe a true-shaped thing that was false all the way down, and if you think that's better than lying, I promise you, I've had a century to consider the question and it isn't.

He'd been twenty-six when they found him. That was the age his face still wore, give or take a lie. Twenty-six, a junior clerk in the Warden archive — because of course it was the archive, the story had always been circular, a snake eating its own first page — cataloguing confiscated texts in a basement that smelled of mildew and lamp oil. He'd been good at it. Careful. The sort of young man who alphabetized things that didn't need alphabetizing because the alternative was thinking about how much he hated the work and everyone who made him do it.

The ledger had been brought down to him in a crate of items slated for destruction. Black covers, silver clasps, pages that felt warm when you weren't looking directly at them. His supervisor told him to burn it unread.

He read it.

He would spend the rest of his unnatural life trying to explain to himself why. Curiosity, he'd said at first. Then duty — someone should know what they're burning. Then, in the honest hours, the truth: because the book opened itself to him. Because the moment his fingers touched the clasp, the prophecy turned its own pages until it found the one with two faces sketched in faded ink, and one of the faces was his.

Not similar. *His.* The same stubborn jaw, the same crooked nose he'd gotten from falling off a hay cart at nine years old. Dated a hundred years before his birth.

He'd stood in that basement holding his own face in a dead man's handwriting, and he had not screamed, and he had not run, and he had not burned the book. He had shown it to his supervisor. That was the decision. Everything else — the century, the loneliness, the woman upstairs whose footsteps he knew by heart — had grown out of that one small, obedient, catastrophic choice.

Because his supervisor hadn't been horrified. His supervisor had smiled. And taken the ledger upstairs. And three days later, men in plain coats had come for Corin, and they had not arrested him. They had *thanked* him.

"You've been chosen," one of them said, and Corin, twenty-six years old and flattered by the worst people in the city, had felt his heart lift before his mind caught up.

* * *

They called it the Long Commission. He learned later that there had been others before him — a woman named Sable who'd lasted forty years, a man whose name even the records had eaten — but he was the one the prophecy had marked twice, face beside hers, and so he was the one they kept.

The keeping was the part he could never make anyone understand. They hadn't imprisoned him. Imprisonment would have required walls, and walls rust, and prisoners escape. What they'd done was simpler and far crueler: they had made him comfortable, and then they had made him *necessary*, and between those two conditions a man can be held for a hundred years without ever once seeing a lock.

The aging stopped the year after he signed his commission papers. He woke up one morning and the grey in his beard was gone, and the ache in his back from hunching over ledgers had vanished, and his reflection looked back at him with the same face it had worn at twenty-six. The Warden physicians examined him with instruments he wasn't allowed to ask about and pronounced him "stable." Stable. As though he were a bridge. As though the point of him were to bear weight indefinitely and never complain.

"Prophecy needs its instrument fresh," his handler had said, a soft-spoken man named Grieve who smelled of ink and peppermint and had once, Corin was almost sure, been human. "You'll thank us when the hour comes."

He had never thanked them. But he also, in a hundred years, had never once refused them, and he had learned that these were not the same failure but they weighed nearly the same on the scale.

His job was education. That was the word they used. *Education.* He studied her — the woman in the sketch, Zephyrine Iversen, bone-carver, unlicensed hearer — from a distance, through reports and informants and, in the early decades, through actual surveillance that made him sick to participate in. He learned her routines. Her prices. The way she undercharged widows and overcharged merchants who could afford it, and how she pretended this was simple business sense rather than a moral position. He learned that she talked to her carvings while she worked, low and constant, like a woman gossiping with a friend, and that whatever she heard back made her smile sometimes, and that she always, always hid the smile before a customer came in.

He fell in love with her a little, in those years, the way you fall in love with a lighthouse: from far off, grateful, certain you'll never be permitted to touch it.

Then the crown-thing — though he didn't know yet what wore the king, none of them below the thirteenth rank knew — gave the order that rearranged his soul: *Close the distance. Become the mutual friend. Be there when she needs a reason to hope.*

And so the blind date. And so the practiced smile. And so seven months of dinners and walks along the canal and one disastrous attempt at dancing that she still teased him about, during which he held her hand with hands that had been briefed on holding it, and laughed at her jokes with laughter that was real, God help him, that was the one thing that was real, and told her about a dream he'd had since childhood —

— except it wasn't his dream. That was the poison at the center of it. The dream of the tower of white bones and the woman standing at its foot with light in her hands, the dream he'd described so sincerely over the fish course: the Wardens had given it to him. Planted it in his sleep when he was young, watered it for decades, until it grew roots deep enough to feel like memory. A lure doesn't know it's shiny. A lure just hangs there, believing the shine is its own.

*You recognized the imagery,* he thought at the ceiling of the storage alcove. *Of course you did. You'd catalogued the book. You were meant to. Every step of your life since that crate arrived in my basement has been a corridor with doors that only open one way, and I am the doorman, and I have hated myself with such industry for so long that hatred has become a kind of home.*

* * *

A knock at the curtain. Not Maren's knock — Maren knocked like she was serving legal papers. This was softer. Two knuckles, apologetic.

"It's me," Zephyrine said. "You're awake. Your breathing does a thing when you're awake."

"That's alarming."

"I'm a professional listener. Everything's alarming." She pushed the curtain aside and stood there in the undercity's perpetual lamplight, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of dragon-leather, holding two chipped cups. "Maren brews something she calls tea. I use the word loosely. It's technically a broth. Of what, she won't say."

"Is it safe?"

"She says everything down here is safe, which is either reassuring or the most frightening sentence in the world." She handed him a cup and sat on the edge of the bed without asking, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked into her broth with the expression of a woman assembling a difficult sentence.

He knew that expression. He'd spent seven months learning it, and a hundred years learning to dread it.

"Zeph—"

"Don't." Quiet. Not sharp. "Let me go first, because if you go first I'll lose my nerve, and I've been building up nerve all evening like a savings account." She took a breath. "I heard something today. In a relic. One of Maren's. An old woman's ring — the dead woman who owned it, she's been gone sixty years, and she remembers the Warden recruitment houses from when she was alive. And she remembers *you*, Corin. Or she remembers a face like yours, in a place recruits don't come out of, and she remembers people calling it the Long House." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were steady and terrible. "So. Before I ask you anything. I want you to tell me. All of it. And I want you to start further back than you think I need, because I've noticed you do that. You start stories late. Like a man paying with exact change so nobody sees how much is in his purse."

The broth steamed between them. Somewhere deeper in the undercity, the captive flame breathed, and the whole level seemed to inhale with it.

So he started early. Earlier than he'd planned, earlier than the rehearsal, back to the basement and the crate and the warm black book. He told her about reading his own face. About the men in plain coats and the word *chosen*. About the stopping of his aging, described flatly because any other tone would have cracked him open. About Sable, who'd lasted forty years and what had finally happened to her — they'd retired her, Grieve had said, and Corin had never learned what retirement meant, and some nights he suspected he didn't want to. About the surveillance years, and he watched her face while he confessed that part, watched for disgust, and found instead a terrible attentive stillness, the stillness of a woman listening to the dead, and he understood suddenly that to her this was familiar country. Betrayal was just another haunting. She knew how to sit with ghosts.

"And the dream," she said, when he reached it. "The tower. The woman with light in her hands."

"Planted. When I was nineteen. They came to my lodgings and told me it was a prophetic gift, that I'd been blessed, and I believed them because believing them meant my life had a shape, and a shaped life felt like a gift when yours has been ledgers and mildew." His voice did something humiliating on the last words. He let it. "Every time I dreamed it, it got clearer. By the time I met you, I couldn't tell anymore which parts of me were mine and which parts were bait. That's the honest answer, Zephyrine. That's the whole ugly inventory. I was sent to catch you. I have known what you are longer than you've known what you are. I have been the hook, and the line, and half the time I think I was also the fisherman, because I reeled you toward the archive myself, didn't I? I followed you there. I let you find the ledger. I stood in that doorway and watched you see your own face and I did nothing, because doing nothing was the mission."

Silence. The flame-breath of the deep vault. Her cup, set down carefully on the floorboards, a small deliberate click.

"Do you love me?" she asked.

It was such a direct question that every rehearsed answer died instantly, which he suspected was the point. Rehearsal was useless against her. She listened for a living; she could hear the seams in a sentence.

"Yes," he said. "But you can't trust that answer, and I know you can't, and I'm going to say it anyway because withholding it would be one more manipulation, and I am finished — I am *trying* to be finished — with managing you. Yes. Since before I met you, which is its own horror, because how much of that was theirs? I don't know. I will never know. That's my punishment, I think. Not the century. The not-knowing."

She was quiet for a long time. Then: "The wallpaper."

"What?"

"The widow's husband. First week you visited my shop. I told you about the ghost complaining about his widow's wallpaper, and you laughed — really laughed, mid-sentence, before you could stop yourself." Her mouth twitched despite everything. "Bait doesn't laugh like that. Bait is smooth. You were terrible at being smooth. You knocked over a display of teeth charms and apologized to the teeth."

"They seemed offended."

"They were. Old fellow named Brannock. Very proud of his enamel." She picked up her cup again, and he watched her turn it slowly, and he understood that she was deciding something, and that he had never in a hundred years felt so completely outside a decision that mattered to him.

"I'm not forgiving you tonight," she said at last. "I want that clear. Forgiveness is work, and I'm tired, and tomorrow we're going to look at a dying dragon because apparently that's my life now. But—" she exhaled, "—you told me early. You started the story where I asked you to start it. Nobody in my life has ever done that. The living are worse liars than the dead, Corin. The dead at least have the decency to be finished with their secrets." She stood, and paused at the curtain, and did not quite look back at him. "One more thing. If you were sent to catch me — and you were, and we both know it — then somebody above you knows exactly what I am. Knows what I'm *for*. And I want to know why a crown that eats devotion would spend a hundred years preparing a welcome for a bone-carver."

The curtain swung shut behind her.

Corin sat alone with his broth going cold, and the question she'd left behind settled over him like frost, because he knew the answer — had known it since the archive, since the page where two faces shared one fate — and the knowing was a stone he'd swallowed so long ago he'd mistaken it for his own heart.

The prophecy had never promised a hero.

It had promised a vessel. And he had been the delivery boy.
