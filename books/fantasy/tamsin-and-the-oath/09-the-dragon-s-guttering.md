## 9. The Dragon's Guttering

The Warden vault beneath the Hall of Registers had a door made of a single slab of black iron, and the iron had been spelled so thoroughly that even the dead refused to touch it.

I knew this because I'd asked.

"You could knock," said Edvard, my dead mentor, drifting at my shoulder in the grey way he did when he was trying to look casual and failing. "Knocking is traditional."

"The Wardens don't answer knocks. The Wardens answer warrants."

"Then perhaps we should get a warrant."

"I'm a hearer of the dead operating without a license in a city where that's punishable by the removal of several interesting parts," I said. "Warrants are not really my genre."

Edvard sighed the sigh of a man who had spent forty years teaching archive etiquette to someone who now committed regular felonies with his stolen lockpicks. He'd been dead three years and had never once let me forget whose picks those were.

The problem with the vault door wasn't the lock. Locks were honest; locks wanted something specific from you and told you what it was. The problem was the binding laid over the whole doorway like frost on glass — old work, deep work, the kind of binding that didn't come from any Warden's handbook. It came from somewhere older. It came, if Maren was right, from *fire*.

Which was why I'd come down here with a lantern, a bone stylus, and the last relic of Queen Isolde warming in my pocket like a small disapproving heart.

And why I'd come down here, mostly, because the dragon was dying.

Maren had told me about it two nights ago, in the undercity, while she fed pinches of ash to the little stove that kept her workshop warm. She'd said it the way people say things they've carried too long — flatly, quickly, hoping the words would fall somewhere other than on her own shoulders.

"There's one left," she'd said. "One true fire. They keep it under the Hall of Registers. Everything the Wardens bind — every spell-lock, every suppression chain, every collar they put on people like me — it all draws from that fire. Dragon-fire is the only thing that binds true. And it's guttering." She had stared into her stove as though it were the dragon itself. "When it goes out, either everything the Wardens ever bound comes loose at once... or they find another fire. And there isn't another fire."

"And nobody thought to mention this to anyone?"

"To *whom*, Zephyrine?" She'd laughed, and there hadn't been much laugh in it. "To the crown? The crown would sooner let it die. A dying fire suits them fine. It means everything they've built holds just long enough for them to finish building it."

So here I was. Committing burglary against the most secure room in Vaelhollow, because a dying dragon had information I needed, and because — though I'd have bitten my tongue bloody before admitting it aloud — because something in me couldn't stand the idea of the last of its kind going out alone in the dark, unheard.

Hearing things was, after all, rather my whole affliction.

* * *

Getting past the outer wards took an hour and cost me a nosebleed. Getting past the inner wards took another hour and cost me a memory — a small one, a Tuesday, gone like a coin through a torn pocket — which I resented deeply, because Tuesdays were already thin on the ground.

Edvard guided me through the last stretch by feel, since the wards ate sound the way sand eats water and even he grew faint near them.

"Left," he murmured, barely a breath against my ear. "No — your other left, dear. Honestly, seven years and you still navigate like a drunk pigeon."

"My other left is a philosophical impossibility."

"So is what you're about to do. Left anyway."

The corridor ended at the black iron door, and the binding lay over it exactly as Maren had described: not a spell, not a seal, but a *hunger*. Old binding-work has a texture when you can read it — a wovenness, a patience. This had none of that. This was a knot tied around something that did not wish to be held, and the knot was fraying, and the fraying had a smell I could almost taste through the iron.

Burning.

I pressed my palm flat against the door and reached out with the sense I'd spent seven years pretending I didn't have.

And the dragon heard me first.

* * *

What came up through the iron was not a voice. Voices are shaped by throats, and the dragon had no throat anymore — only a flame the size of a cathedral candle, cupped in a brazier of warded stone, tended by machines that dripped oil on a schedule and had never once asked how it felt about being dripped on.

What came up through the iron was *heat with meaning in it*. An attention. Something vast turning over in its sleep, or in whatever lies under sleep, and finding a small warm mind pressed against its prison wall.

*Little bone-scraper,* it said, and the words arrived the way sunrise arrives — too large for the container. *You are not Warden.*

"No," I said aloud, foolishly, to a door. Then, more quietly, along the thread between us: "No. I'm not."

*A hearer.* The attention sharpened, and I felt it the way you feel a magnifying glass find your hand on a hot day. *They are rare. They were rarer still, once, before the quieting. Why do you come?*

"I need to know about King Aldric. About the thing he bound. The crown-thing." I swallowed. "And I need to know how he bound it. Because we think—" I stopped. Started again. "Because I think the binding is failing. Both bindings. Yours and his. And I think they might be the same kind of failing."

Silence. Or the dragon's version of silence, which was a heat so total it became a kind of hush.

Then: *Come in, then, little scraper. You have paid the tolls. Your hands smell of blood and bone-dust and stubbornness. Come and see what they keep of me.*

The lock, when I touched it, opened like a flower. Whatever the dragon was, it still had pull over the bindings drawn from its own fire. The black iron swung inward on hinges that hadn't moved in years, and the air that rolled out was dry and mineral and old, old, old.

* * *

The vault was smaller than I'd expected. That was the first cruelty of it. You imagine a dragon's prison as a mountain hall, a cavern, something scaled to the prisoner. Instead there was a round chamber, maybe thirty feet across, ringed with suppression chains anchored into the walls, and in the center, a brazier.

In the brazier burned a flame about the size of a swan.

It should have filled this room. It should have filled this *city*. What remained of the last dragon-fire of Vaelhollow fit inside a bird-sized shape, and it guttered as I watched — flaring, sinking, flaring — like a candle in a draft that never quite went out and never quite stopped trying to.

"Oh," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Oh, you poor thing."

*I am not a thing,* said the fire, mildly. *I am Ysolvarrathanegeth. But the Wardens found the name too long for their ledgers, so 'the asset' will do.*

"That's obscene."

*It is efficient. They value efficiency. It is their whole religion — efficiency wearing a uniform.* The flame leaned toward me, and the heat of it was gentle, deliberately gentled, the way you'd soften your grip for a child. *You wanted Aldric. Sit, then. Sitting helps. Memory is heavy, and I am tired, and I would rather pour it than drop it.*

There was nothing to sit on but the floor, so I sat on the floor, cross-legged, close enough to feel the warmth on my face. Edvard settled beside me — he couldn't enter the chamber proper, the chains barred the dead, but he pressed himself against the threshold like a cat at a closed door, watching with an expression I'd never seen him wear. Reverence, maybe. Grief. Both.

*Before the crown,* the dragon began, *there was a king who was only a man. Remember that. Everyone forgets it now, and the forgetting is the thing's favorite meal.*

The memory poured into me.

* * *

I saw a young man in a plain grey coat standing in a smithy that was also a throne room, because in those days the throne room had been given over to honest work while the palace was rebuilt after the Sundering War. I saw Aldric — and he was young, younger than Corin, with ink on his fingers and a burn scar on his forearm — arguing with a creature made of smoke and gold.

Not fighting it. Arguing. That was the horror of the memory: how reasonable everyone was being.

The crown-thing had come down from somewhere older than the city, riding a comet or a famine or a bad winter — the memory blurred there, the way memories blur when the witness didn't understand what they were seeing. It had worn three kings before Aldric. It wore them slowly. It fed on belonging — on the warmth of a city loving its king — and it twisted that love as it drank, until loyalty became leash and devotion became chain and the people of Vaelhollow loved their ruler the way a fist loves a glove.

Aldric had stood in the smithy-throne-room with the thing coiled above the empty crown-stand, and he had not tried to destroy it, because you cannot destroy it; it simply pours itself into whoever strikes at it. He had understood that the way you understand a load-bearing wall.

So he had bargained.

*He offered it a feast,* the dragon said, and the memory shuddered. *Thirty years of a kingdom's love, freely given, in exchange for a promise: that it would take only what was offered. Never more. Never the children. Never the unwilling. He wrote the terms in binding-fire — my fire — and sealed them with his own name, and the thing accepted, because the thing believes all love can be bought eventually, and it thought it had bought a king.*

*And then Aldric did the clever, terrible thing. He died.*

I went cold in front of a furnace.

*Not murdered. Not escaped. He chose it. He drank poison the night the binding was sealed, quietly, in his rooms, with a letter to his sister on the desk. Because the bargain bound the crown to the wearer — and a dead man cannot be worn. The thing got its thirty years of a kingdom's love, but it got them secondhand, filtered through a corpse it had to animate and puppet, spending its stolen strength just to make the body smile and wave and sign decrees. Every year it has grown weaker in some ways and crueler in others, because hunger does that. Thirty years it has kept its promise — never the children, never the unwilling — because the binding holds, because MY fire holds it—*

The flame guttered hard, sank to almost nothing, and for one terrible moment the vault went dark except for my lantern.

Then it steadied. Smaller than before.

*My fire holds it,* the dragon repeated, quieter. *For now. Do you see, little scraper? Do you see the arithmetic?*

I did. I wished I didn't. When the dragon-fire failed, the binding on the crown-thing failed with it — and the thing would be free of its own bargain, free to take the children, the unwilling, everything, with nothing left to stop it but a city full of people who had forgotten how to govern themselves because a puppet king had done it for them for thirty years.

"How long?" I whispered. "How long does your fire have?"

The dragon considered. Fire is honest; it doesn't perform hope.

*Seasons. Perhaps fewer. The Wardens draw from me daily and give back nothing. Binding spends; nothing refills.* A pause, and then, with something that in a person would have been dry humor: *Though I confess your visit has been the first interesting thing in forty years, and interest burns a surprising amount of fuel.*

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it so hard it hurt. "I'm sorry we're letting you go out like this. Alone, in a box, feeding their locks."

*Alone.* The word turned over in the heat like a stone in a stream. *Do you know what I remember most, little scraper? Not the war. Not the binding. I remember the old queen — Isolde, your pocket-rat's mistress — coming down here when she was very old, when she could barely walk, and sitting where you're sitting, and talking to me for six hours about her garden. Not asking anything. Not wanting anything. Just talking, because she believed a captive creature ought to have company.*

The flame flickered, and I could have sworn — I would swear it — that the shape of it bent briefly into something like a bow.

*She asked me, at the end, whether dragons grieve. I told her we do not grieve the way you do. We grieve forward. We look at what is coming and mourn it in advance, thoroughly, so that when it arrives there is nothing left to feel but the work. It is a good system. I recommend it.*

"What work?" I asked. "What's left to do?"

*Yours, not mine. Listen to the rest. There is a piece of the memory I have saved for last, because it is the piece the Wardens would burn me out entirely to keep you from having.*

The memory rose again, and this time it showed me the binding itself — the moment of sealing, Aldric's living hand tracing the terms in lines of dragon-fire, and I saw, with a lurch, the structure of it. The knot. The same knot that was fraying on the vault door upstairs. And I saw what the binding required to hold:

A keeper. A willing witness, bound by choice, who remembered the terms. Someone who heard the truth and carried it, so the bargain could never quietly be rewritten by the thing that wanted it rewritten. In Aldric's day that keeper had been the dragon itself.

The dragon was dying.

And the crown-thing had spent thirty years silencing every hearer in Vaelhollow, one by one, because a hearer was the only other soul who could serve.

*Now you see why it noticed you,* the dragon said gently. *Now you see why it sent you a boy out of a prophecy book. It does not merely want to wear you, little bone-scraper. It wants to make you its new keeper — willing, bound by choice, remembering the terms it prefers. You would hold the bargain shut forever, and hold it shut wrong.*

The vault was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere above us, muffled by yards of stone, the bells of Vaelhollow rang the third hour, and thirty years of a lie rang with them.

"Then the fire has to be freed," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, steady and far away. "If the binding fails either way, better it fails loose than fails into their hands. Maren was right. We get you out of this box before you gutter, and we spend what's left of you on our terms — on breaking the crown, not feeding it."

*Spending me,* the dragon repeated. There was a long pause, and in it I felt something enormous and tired consider the offer, the way an old soldier considers a last order. *Isolde would have liked you. She also proposed crimes to captive creatures within an hour of meeting them.*

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me all year."

*The vault cannot be opened from outside — the wards drink magic, and your fire-singer's gifts would feed them, not break them. But the anchor-chains are drawn from my own fire, and what my fire binds, my fire may unbind. From within. Once. There will not be enough of me afterward to do anything but one great final act. Choose it wisely, and choose it soon, because—*

The flame guttered again. Harder this time. When it recovered, it was visibly smaller, the way a hand goes smaller with age, and the machines overhead clicked and dripped their indifferent oil.

*—because I do not have many evenings left, and neither, I think, does your city.*

I got to my feet on legs that felt borrowed. Edvard was staring at me from the threshold, and behind his ghost-grey face his expression had finally resolved into something I recognized: the look he used to give me across the archive desk when I'd found the book that changed everything, the look that said *well, now there's no putting it back.*

"We'll come back," I told the dragon. "With help. With a plan. With—" I gestured vaguely upward, toward the whole weight of Vaelhollow lying on top of us like a lid. "With more than a lantern and a nosebleed."

*Bring the boy,* said the dragon, and something in the heat turned sly. *The hundred-year one. I remember prophecies. Tedious things, prophecies — always written by someone who knows how the sentence ends. But this one interests me. Bring him, and I will tell you what the ledger's fine print actually says. I was alive when it was written, little scraper. I know what the vessel clause means.*

The word *vessel* went through me like cold water through cloth.

But before I could ask — before I could even form the question — the vault door boomed shut behind Edvard on its own weight, the wards reknitting, and from far above us, faint through the stone, came a sound I knew too well and had hoped never to hear aimed at anything I loved:

Warden horns. Three notes, rising. Not a patrol signal.

A summons.

Addressed, if the shiver down my spine was any guide, to me.
