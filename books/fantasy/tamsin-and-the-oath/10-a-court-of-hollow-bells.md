## 10. A Court of Hollow Bells

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which Zephyrine felt was deeply unfair. Tuesdays were for inventory. Tuesdays were safe.

It came in the form of a Warden courier who did not knock so much as materialize in her doorway like a bad memory, holding a letter sealed with grey wax and the crowned sigil of Vaelhollow. The wax was cold. Everything about it was cold.

"Zephyrine Iversen," the courier read aloud, though she was standing right there and could read perfectly well, "licensed bone-carver, is summoned to attend His Majesty's court on the feast of Saint Verity, to present works of craft befitting the crown's magnificence."

"That's very flattering," Zephyrine said, "but I mostly make charms against earaches."

The courier's face did not change. Couriers, she had long suspected, were grown in vats somewhere beneath the Wardenhall, fed on rules and vinegar. "Attendance is not optional."

"No," she agreed. "I didn't think it would be."

He left. She stood in her shop among the little bones of her trade—rabbit feet and river-otter teeth and the polished knuckle of a bear that had died of old age, thank you very much—and let herself shake for exactly one minute. She counted it out on the clock. Then she put the kettle on, because terror is best endured with tea, and began to think.

A summons to court was not a compliment. A summons to court was a hook, and somewhere above the waterline, something patient was holding the rod.

* * *

Corin arrived within the hour, which told her he'd heard too. He had a way of arriving exactly when the world got worse, as if bad news rang a small bell only he could hear.

"You're not going," he said from the doorway.

"I'm absolutely going."

"They summon a charm-seller to present before the king? Zeph, charm-sellers don't get summoned. Charm-sellers get *ignored*, professionally, for their entire lives. That's the whole charm of charm-selling." He caught himself. "Poor word choice."

"Wonderful word choice, actually." She poured him tea without asking; he'd stopped being a guest around the third near-death experience. "If I don't go, they come here. If they come here, they find the cellar door, and the ledger page, and the jar of grave-dirt I have never once explained to anyone's satisfaction. Going is the lesser catastrophe."

"And if the thing on the throne looks at you?"

She wrapped her hands around her cup. Outside, Vaelhollow was hanging feast-lanterns for Saint Verity, whose whole doctrine was that truth makes you free, a doctrine the city had honored mainly by hanging lanterns instead of practicing it.

"Then I curtsy," she said, "and I show them rabbit feet, and I am the dullest woman ever to stand on palace marble. I've been invisible for seven years, Corin. I'm rather good at it."

He looked at her for a long moment—the look he had, the one that made her feel catalogued, like a page in someone else's book. "I'll be there," he said. "Court opens its galleries on feast days. Anyone can come gawk at the king."

"Will you gawk?"

"I'll gawk *strategically*."

* * *

She spent three days preparing, and most of that preparation was subtraction. The mourning charm she'd carved for Widow Hessel went into the floor-cache. The queen's relic—a sliver of bone no bigger than a thumbnail, wrapped in silk, warm in a way bone should never be—she agonized over. Leaving it home meant leaving her ears behind. Bringing it meant carrying contraband into the one building in Vaelhollow built entirely out of finding things out.

She brought it. Sewn into her bodice, flat against her sternum, where it sat like a second heartbeat with opinions.

For the presentation itself, she chose her dullest work: a comb of antler, a set of buttons carved from deer horn, a little box with a hare on the lid doing nothing in particular. Harmless. Domestic. The kind of objects that made Wardens' eyes slide right off.

Maren saw her off at the mouth of the undercity stair, arms crossed, firelight catching the iron-grey streak in her hair. "You know what I did the last time I stood in front of a crown official?" Maren said.

"Burned something?"

"I *wanted* to burn something. There's a difference. It's called growth." She gripped Zephyrine's shoulder hard enough to leave a philosophy. "You're a listener. So listen past the words in there. Words are what they give you. Listen for what they're *doing* while they say them."

"And if what they're doing is eating me?"

"Then run toward the kitchens. Palaces always have terrible sightlines near the buttery."

It was, Zephyrine reflected on the barge upriver to the palace, genuinely good advice, and she hated how much comfort she took from it.

* * *

The palace of Vaelhollow was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful: all that shine, all that depth, and underneath, the certain knowledge that things had gone down through it and not come back up.

The feast of Saint Verity filled the great hall with light and noise. Chandeliers like chandeliers everywhere, but these burned a pale, steady flame that gave off more gleam than warmth. Musicians played in a gallery. Courtiers drifted in silks the color of frost and ash, laughing at intervals so regular they might have been scheduled.

And at the far end, on a dais beneath a canopy of grey velvet, sat King Aldric.

Zephyrine had seen portraits. Portraits showed a broad, ruddy man with a beard like a storm front and eyes that promised to remember your name. The thing on the throne had Aldric's shape the way a glove has a hand's shape—correct, complete, and empty. He sat very still. He smiled at intervals so regular they might have been scheduled. And the crown on his head—

The crown was old iron and black gold, twisted into thorn-shapes, and it did not sit on the king. It *grew* from him. She could see, even at forty paces, even across a crowd, that the skin at his temples met the metal without a seam.

Her stomach performed a slow, dignified revolt.

"Smile," murmured Corin at her elbow, appearing with two glasses of wine she hadn't seen him acquire. "You're doing the face you do when a customer asks if the rabbit foot is ethically sourced."

"It's a reasonable question."

"You're at court. Curtsy at things. Drink nothing."

"Why?"

"Because I helped design the wards on this place, and I can tell you the water is warded, the wine is warded, and the soup—" he sipped his own wine with visible enjoyment, "—the soup is probably fine, but I never tested the soup."

An usher with a voice like a struck bell announced, "Master carvers and craftsfolk of the city, approach and present!"

* * *

There were eleven of them. A glassblower whose work sang when the wind found it. A weaver of tapestries so fine they looked like held breath. A locksmith, a bell-founder, a maker of clocks. And Zephyrine, last in line, clutching a box of buttons.

They processed down the length of the hall one by one, each kneeling briefly before the dais while the king said some variation of *the crown is pleased*. Zephyrine watched the glassblower go first and noticed something odd: the king received each gift with the same smile, the same tilt of the head, the same pause. Exactly the same. Not a performance repeated—she knew performances; she gave one daily—but something worn smooth, like a stone the river had given up arguing with.

Then it was her turn.

She knelt on the marble, which was colder than marble had any right to be, and opened her box, and heard herself say, in her shopkeeper's voice, bright and modest: "Buttons of deer horn, Majesty, and a comb, and a little box with a hare on the lid. Simple work. Honest work. May they serve you well."

Silence.

Not the polite silence of a court waiting for the next name. A silence with texture. A silence like the moment before a trap closes, when the whole mechanism holds its breath.

The king leaned forward.

Up close, the wrongness resolved into details. His eyes were brown and kind and utterly unfocused, like painted eyes on a temple statue. His breathing was even, unhurried, and she realized with a lurch that she couldn't hear it at all—not the fabric-shift, not the small wet sounds a living body makes. Around him, the air was quiet in a way that wasn't absence. It was *consumption*. Every sound that reached the dais seemed to arrive slightly thinner than it left.

And then the crown noticed her.

She had no other word for it. The thing on the king's head turned its attention upon her the way a tongue finds the sore spot in your mouth—deliberately, thoroughly, pressing until the ache confirms the shape. It moved along her the way she moved along a relic: reading. Tasting. It pressed against the walls she'd built around her gift, seven years of careful mortar, and she felt them flex.

Against her sternum, the queen's sliver woke like a struck match.

*Oh,* said a voice, thin and furious and dead thirty years, *oh, child, it smells you. Do not run. Running is a confession. Smile.*

Zephyrine smiled.

It cost her everything she had. Her face wanted to do other things—to scream, to bolt, to weep—and she held it into deference the way you hold a door shut against wind. She kept her eyes lowered, the picture of a humble craftswoman overwhelmed by royal attention, and inside her skull the crown-thing pressed again, curious now, and she felt it find the edge of her hearing the way a burglar's fingers find the seam of a window latch.

It paused there. Interested.

*Interesting,* it did not say, because it did not need words; the pressure itself was the sentence. *What a small, clever door. What might be behind it.*

She did the only thing she could think of. She thought about wallpaper.

Specifically, she thought about the Hessel widow's wallpaper, the mustard-yellow monstrosity the late Mr. Hessel had complained about for eleven years of afterlife—*stripes, Zephyrine, vertical stripes in a north-facing room, the man had no eye, no eye at all*—and she packed her mind full of it, stripes and paste and the argument about whether the border matched, until her inner landscape was the least interesting room in Vaelhollow. A dead man's decorating grievances as a warding spell. Her mentor would have laughed himself into a second death.

The pressure withdrew. Slowly. Reluctantly. Like a tongue leaving a tooth it suspected of trouble.

"The crown is pleased," said King Aldric, in a voice like a bell with the clapper removed—all the shape of sound, none of the life. "Rise, carver. Your king sees your devotion."

*I see you,* the crown added, in the pressure-language, almost tenderly. *Later.*

She rose. She curtsied. She walked back down forty feet of marble on legs that belonged to someone else, and she did not run, because running was a confession, and because the buttery was, on inspection, on the wrong side of the hall.

* * *

She found Corin by the musicians' gallery, and her hand shook so badly getting the wineglass to her lips that he steadied it with two fingers.

"Well?" he asked quietly.

"It knows," she said. "Or—it doesn't know yet, but it's *interested*. It touched my gift, Corin. Like a tongue. Like it was tasting whether I was ripe."

His jaw tightened. "Did it take anything? Did it follow you back?"

"I don't think so. I hid behind wallpaper."

"You—"

"Long story. Dead man. Terrible taste in interior design. Saved my life." She drained the wine, which tasted faintly of wards and not at all of grapes. "Corin, it spoke to me. Not in words. In... appetite. And then it said *later*."

Around them the feast glittered on. The king raised a hand and the hall hushed, and Aldric-the-glove delivered a speech about unity and devotion and the blessed quiet of a well-ordered city, and every sentence landed on schedule, and the courtiers laughed and sighed at intervals so regular they might have been scheduled, and Zephyrine understood suddenly, horribly, that half the people in this room were not performing loyalty.

They were *leashed*. She could hear it now that she knew to listen—the faint, frayed sound of strings running up from a hundred throats to the dais, to the crown, taut as harp wire. Love, worn into leash-leather. Devotion, braided into rope.

One of the leashed ones, a young courtier with glassy, happy eyes, drifted close to her in the crowd and said, dreamily, "His Majesty admires makers. He collects them. You've been *noticed*."

It was meant as an honor. It landed like a brand.

* * *

They escaped at the first decent interval, which Corin identified with the precision of a man who had once designed this building's social choreography as well as its wards. On the barge going back downriver, with the palace shrinking into its own glow behind them, Zephyrine finally let her shoulders drop from around her ears.

"He's still in there," she said. "Aldric. Underneath. I couldn't hear him—the crown eats sound, it eats *everything*—but the queen's sliver was hot as a coal the whole time, and she was saying, over and over—" Zephyrine closed her eyes, replaying it. "*Find what he buried. Find what he buried.*"

"Aldric buried something?"

"Or someone. Or some *part* of himself." She stared at the black water. "Thirty years, Corin. Thirty years wearing that thing, and somewhere under all that stolen devotion there's a man who hid a piece of himself away from it, the way I hide my gift. A seed in a cellar. And the queen wants me to dig."

Corin was quiet a moment. "That's assuming the ghost of a dead queen isn't leading you exactly where the crown wants you. It noticed you today, Zeph. It said *later*. Things that say *later* usually mean they're choosing the moment."

"Thank you, that's very comforting."

"I contain multitudes of comforting."

But his joke didn't land, because at that moment the queen's sliver, sewn against her heart, went abruptly, pointedly warm—and shaped within the warmth, faint as breath on glass, came a single thread of sound. Not the queen's voice.

A man's. Old, tired, buried deep, speaking from somewhere far beneath the palace floors, as if from the bottom of a well:

*"...if anyone hears this. If anyone at all. The vaults. Third door past the dry cistern. I could not destroy it. I could only hide the half of me strong enough to try. Please. Before it finishes learning my face—"*

The voice cut off, snipped clean, the way a sound dies when something large closes over it.

Zephyrine and Corin looked at each other across the rocking deck of the barge, and neither of them said the obvious thing, which was that the king's hidden half had just spoken to her directly—which meant the crown's attention today had not been idle curiosity at all.

It had been confirmation. The trap had a name in it now, and the name was hers.

"We go to the vaults," she said.

"Tonight?"

"Corin. It said *later*."
