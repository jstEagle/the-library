## 11. The Informant's Face

The relic had been in Zephyrine's pocket for three days before it started talking.

That was not unusual. Bone took its time. A fresh-carved piece might sit silent for weeks, gathering whatever it gathered — she'd never been able to explain it to customers, who wanted their charms chatty by supper — and then one morning you'd reach for your keys and hear a dead woman complaining about the damp.

This relic was different. This one was a knucklebone, small and honey-colored, worn smooth at the joints in a way that said *this was held often, this was worried between fingers for years*. She hadn't carved it. She'd found it in her mentor Edvard's effects after he died, tucked into a drawer with her own name scratched on the lining in his cramped hand, and she had carried it ever since without quite knowing why. It never spoke of him. It never spoke at all.

Until the night after the barge, when she sat at her workbench — what remained of her workbench, in what remained of her shop, because the Wardens' raid had taken the front room but not the cellar, and Corin had spent two days hauling her tools up out of the dark like a man rescuing drowning kittens — and the bone warmed against her palm like a hand closing over hers.

"Zeph," it said.

She dropped it. It rolled under the bench, into the shadow where the floorboards gapped, and kept talking anyway, patient as weather.

"Zeph, don't do that. You always drop things when you're frightened. You dropped a whole tray of charms when Master Edvard sneezed once. He never let either of us forget it."

She knew that voice. She knew that voice the way you know the creak of your own stairs, the way you know rain on a particular roof — not as information but as architecture, something her whole self had been built around.

"Tobin," she whispered.

* * *

Tobin Hale had died six years ago, in autumn, in a way the city called an accident and everyone who knew him called nothing of the kind. He had been her first friend in Vaelhollow — a Warden's clerk, of all things, a thin, quick-witted young man with ink-stained cuffs and a laugh that arrived a half-second before his jokes did, as though even his laughter couldn't wait its turn. He'd come into her shop to buy a charm against headaches, paid with a coin he confessed was borrowed, and stayed three hours arguing about whether the dead could lie.

"They can't," twelve-year-old Zephyrine had insisted, before catching herself, too late, too specific.

He'd looked at her for a long moment. Then: "No," he'd said quietly. "I don't suppose they can." And he had never told a soul, and had come back every week for eleven years, until the week he didn't come back at all.

They said he fell into the Gray Canal. They said he'd been drinking. Tobin drank tea, exclusively, and was afraid of deep water, and had told her so on the very bridge they claimed he'd tumbled from.

Now his voice was coming out of a knucklebone in her cellar, saying her name like he used to say it across a crowded market — that little upward lilt, *Zeph*, half greeting, half dare.

"Say something," the bone said. "I've been in here a long time, Zeph. It's very quiet in here. Or it was. Lately there's a lot of... traffic."

Her hands were shaking. She made herself pick the bone up. It was warm. Bones were never warm.

"How are you in there?" she asked. Her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Tobin, you're — I carved nothing for you. There's no charm of yours. I never —"

"I know." A pause, and in the pause she heard something she had never heard in any dead voice before: hesitation. The dead were witnesses; they remembered; they did not, as a rule, hesitate. Hesitation belonged to the living. "That's rather the point. I'm sorry, Zeph. I need you to listen, and I need you to not interrupt, because if I stop I'm not certain I can start again. Can you do that?"

She could not speak at all, which accomplished the same thing.

"You remember the night I died. Of course you do. You lit candles for me for a year. You still light them, don't you? I can't see them, but I know. Dead men know the shape of what's owed them." The voice wavered, steadied. "Here is what happened, and I want you to understand that I have rehearsed this in the dark for six years, and every version ends the same way, which is with you hating me, and I would rather you hated me knowing the truth than loved me believing the canal story.

"I reported you."

The cellar went very still. Somewhere above, Corin's footsteps crossed the shop floor — he was reorganizing her stock by height, which was wrong, everything in her shop was organized by grief-category, and she'd have corrected him any other day — and came down the stairs, and stopped, because he could read her face even now, even in the dark.

"Zeph?" he said softly.

She held up one finger. *Wait.* In her palm, the bone went on.

"It was three years before the end. You'll remember — no, you won't, that's the cruelty of it, you won't remember the night at all, it was nothing to you. The night a Warden inspector came through the market row and stopped at your stall and picked up a mourning charm and turned it over twice, and asked you where you sourced your bone, and you gave him your licensed answer, and he smiled and moved on. Do you remember?"

She did not remember. Her stomach said she was about to wish she did.

"He wasn't inspecting. He was testing. The crown has — had — a method. They call it *tasting*. An inspector handles a hundred carvings in a day, and ninety-nine of them feel like nothing, and one feels like a door left open, and they mark the stall. Not arrest. Mark. They watch for months. They learn who comes and goes. And then they find the softest thing in that person's life, and they squeeze it."

Corin had come down another step. She couldn't look at him. She was looking at the knucklebone as if it might blink.

"I was already a clerk by then. Low rank, low pay, copying seizure manifests in a basement that smelled of mildew and despair. And a senior officer — a woman named Vess, you'd have hated her, everyone hated her, that was practically her job description — called me in and put a file on the desk. Your file. Seven years of observations. Every charm, every customer, every candle you lit on the wrong holidays. And she said, *Hale, we believe the carver Iversen is an unregistered practitioner. We believe you can confirm it. Confirm it, and your debts are cleared, your mother's pension is restored, and you keep your post. Refuse, and we reopen the question of how a clerk with your salary affords his lodgings.*

"And I said no." The voice cracked — actually cracked, a dead man's voice cracking, which should not be possible, which meant whatever held him was bending him. "I want that recorded somewhere, even if it's only a bone in a cellar. I said no. I said I'd sooner swim the Gray Canal. And Vess smiled — Zeph, she *smiled*, like I'd passed a test instead of failed one — and she said, *Good. That's the answer we needed. Now we know what to use.*

"They didn't threaten me again. That's the thing you have to understand about them. Threats are for strangers. For friends, they use love. They sent my mother's pension back — restored, generous, backdated, with a note thanking me for my continued service. I never asked for it. Accepting it felt like nothing. That's how it works, Zeph, I swear to you on whatever's left of me, that's how it *works*. It doesn't kick the door in. It pays the rent. It remembers your birthday. It makes itself so gently useful that one day you look around and every kindness in your life has a Warden seal on the bottom of it, and you cannot tell anymore which parts of you are yours.

"And then one evening Vess invited me to dinner. Just dinner. She poured wine — I told you I only drink tea, but you make exceptions, everyone makes exceptions, that's the whole design of it — and she talked about her garden, her late husband, the terrible state of the river district. And near the end, casually, peeling an apple, she said: *Your friend the carver. She's been seen lighting death-candles at the Unsanctioned Moon. Curious habit for a licensed woman. If you happened to confirm it, no one would ever trouble your mother again. If you happened not to, well.* She ate the apple slice. *Well.*

"And I went home, and I sat up all night, and I wrote the report."

The bone was cold now. Cold as any honest dead thing. Somehow that was worse.

"I wrote that I had observed the carver Zephyrine Iversen performing unsanctioned rites. I signed it. I sealed it. I walked it to the drop-box myself because I couldn't bear to let a courier carry it, which I told myself was honor and knew was cowardice. And eleven days later I was dead in the canal, and everyone said accident, and you lit candles, and I — "

The voice stopped. Not snipped, this time. Stopped the way a person stops when they've walked to the edge of what they can say.

"Tobin," Zephyrine managed. It came out as almost nothing.

"Don't," he said. "Don't forgive me yet. There's more, and the more is the reason I fought my way back through — I don't even have words for what I fought through, Zeph, there are *layers* down here, there are doors — the reason I spent six years learning to knock on one piece of bone. Because I didn't just report you. After I reported you, they kept me. Dead men make such loyal clerks. No debts, no family to bribe, nothing to do but serve. They bound me — there's a word for what they did, I heard it in the vault below the palace, a word I hope you never learn — and they set me to listening. To watching. To being, for lack of a kinder term, an ear.

"I am the ear they pointed at you, Zeph. I have been for six years. Everything you carved, everyone who came to your door, every candle — it came through me, and through me to them. When they raided your shop, they knew which cellar wall was thinnest because *I told them*. I stood in the dark and I listened to you survive, and I handed them the map."

Above them, faintly, the shop bell rang — a customer, or the wind, or the city breathing. Corin had gone white. He had one hand on the stair rail and one half-raised toward her, hovering, unsure whether touch would help or shatter.

"Why," Zephyrine said. It was all she had. "Why tell me now? Why come back at all?"

"Because they're finished using me," Tobin said simply. "A tool that's served its purpose gets melted down. Three nights ago the listening went quiet — they pulled me out of the network, they're rerouting everything, they're *concentrating*, Zeph, the crown is pulling all its ears in close, like a man clenching his fist. And a clenched fist has gaps. I slipped through one. I've been trying to slip through one for six years." A sound that might have been a laugh. "You always said I was stubborn."

"I said you were *pedantic*."

"That too. Listen — the last thing they had me do, before they cut me loose. The last report I carried. It concerned you. It named you. There's a document moving up through the Warden ranks tonight with your name on it, and beneath your name a single word, and the word isn't *arrest* and it isn't *execute*. I saw it. I carried it. I am so sorry, Zeph, I carried it upstairs myself, like a good little ghost."

"What word?"

"*Vessel.*"

The word landed in the cellar like a stone down a well. Corin made a small noise. Zephyrine discovered she was standing, though she had no memory of standing, and that her free hand had closed around the edge of the workbench hard enough to hurt.

"The prophecy," she said. "The ledger. It never named a hero."

"No," Tobin said. "It named a vessel. And they've known longer than you have. Longer than the boy behind you has, I'd wager, though he looks like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. Zeph — I have maybe minutes. Maybe less. Whatever holds me is fraying, and fraying fast, and part of me wants to spend my last breath apologizing properly, at length, with footnotes, because you know how I — "

"Tobin."

"But the useful part of me says: *the bargain is coming.* Tomorrow, next week, some night soon, something will speak to you with your mentor's voice, or mine, or your mother's, and it will offer you exactly what you most want, wrapped exactly how you'd want it wrapped. That is not generosity. That is bait shaped like love. It is what they did to me, scaled up to fit a kingdom. When it comes — and it will come — remember that the kindest voice in the room may be the deepest hook."

The bone was cooling in her hand, rapidly now, like a body losing heat.

"Zeph. One more thing. The last thing, I can feel it going." His voice was thinner, stretched, a thread drawn to its end. "Six years I listened to you through walls. Six years. And do you know what I learned? What I held onto down in the dark, when they worked me like a lamp? I learned that you light candles for people who wronged you. I watched you do it. For clients' enemies. For a Warden, once, whose name you never even told me. You forgive like other people breathe — thoughtlessly, constantly, without seeming to notice the effort.

"So I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking you to do it the way you do everything. Without noticing. Later. When I'm gone and it costs you nothing and means everything." A pause, and the old upward lilt, one last time: "*Zeph.*"

And then the knucklebone was only a knucklebone, cool and smooth and silent, and the warmth that had been in it was nowhere in the world at all.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Corin crossed the cellar and, very carefully, sat down beside her on the floor, which was dusty and cold and entirely beneath the dignity of a century-old prophesied man, and said nothing, which was the correct amount.

"He was my best friend," Zephyrine said finally. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Eleven years. He bought a headache charm with a borrowed coin and argued with me about whether the dead could lie, and I thought — I built seven years of my life on the belief that the worst thing this city could do to me was fear me. I never once considered it might *hire* the people I loved."

"He didn't choose it."

"He chose the first report. Everything after was chosen *for* him." She turned the knucklebone over in her palm. Honey-colored. Worried smooth by living fingers, years ago, in a life neither of them had survived intact. "Do you know what the worst part is? It's not the betrayal. It's that he's right. Something *is* going to offer me everything. I can feel it circling. And it will use his voice, or Edvard's, and I will *want* to say yes, and the only weapon I'll have is knowing exactly how the trap feels from inside."

Corin was quiet a moment. "My grandmother used to say," he offered, "that the difference between a leash and a ribbon is only whether you can take it off."

"Your grandmother sounds invented."

"She may be. I'm a hundred and four; my childhood memories have been edited several times for clarity." He bumped his shoulder against hers, gently, an anchor thrown in shallow water. "What do we do?"

Zephyrine looked at the bone. At the cold little face of it, smooth and blank, holding nothing now but what she chose to remember.

"We light a candle," she said. "And then we get ready. Because it's coming, Corin. And when it does, I intend to be rude to it."

She was reaching for the matches when every bell in Vaelhollow began to ring at once — not the hour, not the alarm, but something older, deeper, a sound like the city clearing its throat — and in the ringing, faint and unmistakable, she heard her own name.

Spoken in Edvard's voice.

Coming from everywhere.
