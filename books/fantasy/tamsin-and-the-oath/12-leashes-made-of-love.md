## 12. Leashes Made of Love

The dream came to her in the bone-cellars, which should have been impossible, because Zephyrine had not slept.

She had meant to sleep. Maren had pressed a tin cup of something bitter and steaming into her hands three hours ago — "dragonroot tea, it tastes like punishment but it works" — and Corin had rolled himself into a blanket near the fire-singer's hearth with the untroubled ease of a man who had, apparently, been twenty-five for a hundred years and had gotten very good at napping anywhere. Zephyrine had sat down on a crate of old femurs, drunk the tea, and closed her eyes for one moment.

One moment was all it took.

She stood in her own shop. Not the burned ruin of it — the shop as it had been, whole and warm, sawdust gold in the lamplight, the little brass bell over the door giving its cheerful *ting*. Everything exactly where she'd left it. The widow's mourning charm, half-carved, on the bench. The kettle she'd never get back. Seven years of careful, quiet life, restored down to the water stains on the ceiling she'd never gotten around to fixing.

"You kept it tidy," said a voice behind her. "I admire that. Tidiness is a form of devotion, you know."

Zephyrine turned very slowly, the way you turn when you already know what's there and are hoping to be wrong.

The shop's back wall was gone. In its place stood the throne room of Vaelhollow palace — or something shaped like it, stretched wrong at the edges, the pillars too tall and the shadows pooling like spilled ink. And on the throne, or wearing it rather, the way a hand wears a glove: King Aldric. His face was kind. That was the worst part. It was the face from every coin and every proclamation and every painted banner over the Warden garrisons, a face designed by committee to be trusted, and it smiled at her with the patience of something that had been smiling for thirty years.

"You're not the king," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was a small mercy. "Aldric died. I heard him. I heard what's left of him, under you."

"Aldric." The thing in the crown turned the name over like a coin it found mildly interesting. "He does go on, doesn't he? Buried under all that love and still talking. You'd be amazed how loud a dead man can be when he's determined to be disapproved of." It rose from the throne — no, it didn't rise; the throne simply rearranged itself around it, the way furniture does in dreams — and crossed the impossible distance between them in a single unhurried step. "But let's not spend our evening on him. Let's talk about you."

"I'd rather not."

"I know," it said warmly. "That's what makes this fun."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrives looking like your own front door.

The crown-thing did not threaten her. It didn't need to. Instead it gestured, almost shyly, toward the workbench, and the half-carved mourning charm finished itself — grain by grain, line by line, the way she would have done it, better than she could have done it, because the thing had watched her work through a hundred pairs of eyes across thirty years and had learned her hands the way she'd learned the dead's voices.

"You're lonely," it said, conversational, settling onto her customer's stool like it belonged there. "Don't argue, it's rude and also I have receipts. Seven years in Vaelhollow and how many people know what you actually are? One. One and a half, if we count the dragon woman, who strikes me as more of a colleague than a confidante. You carve charms for widows and you listen to their husbands complain about wallpaper, and you are *kind* about it, and then they leave, and you sweep up the bone dust, and you eat dinner alone above a shop that isn't yours, in a city that would burn you if it knew you."

"That's a bit dramatic."

"It's Tuesday," said the crown-thing. "I've seen Tuesdays. I've eaten Tuesdays."

Zephyrine folded her arms. "What do you want?"

"What I've always wanted. What I'm *made* of." It leaned forward, and its shadow leaned with it, and for just a moment the kind face flickered and she saw underneath — not a monster's face, nothing so useful as that, but an absence, a hunger wearing the shape of a face the way a glove wears a hand. "Belonging. Devotion. The feeling a city gets when it loves its king. I feed on being needed, Zephyrine Iversen, and you — you are the loneliest woman in Vaelhollow, and you don't even know how much company you could have."

And then it offered her everything.

* * *

It started small, the way all good cons start. A sound.

Her mentor's voice.

Tobias Fenwick had been dead four years, and she still caught herself reaching for the relic of his knucklebone on cold mornings, wanting his grumbling commentary on her technique — *left-handed again, girl, you carve like a man signing a confession* — and getting only silence, because he had been cremated against his wishes and there was almost nothing of him left to hold. She had mourned him twice: once when he died, and once when she realized the burning had taken even his ghost.

Now his voice filled her shop, warm and irritable and alive: *"Zeph, you've got the grain running the wrong way, honestly, were you raised in a barn—"*

"Don't," she whispered.

*"—and another thing, if you're going to keep secrets from the Wardens, at least keep them tidily—"*

"*Don't.*"

The crown-thing watched her with gentle, terrible patience. "I can give him back. Not an echo. Him. He's not scattered, whatever you've feared — the fire took his bones but not his binding, and I hold every binding in this city. I can put Tobias Fenwick back together like a dropped teacup. He'll grumble. He'll drink too much plum wine and criticize your posture. He'll be *yours*, fully, forever, because I will make sure nothing ever takes him from you again."

Zephyrine's hands were shaking. She looked at them like they belonged to someone else.

"And that's not all," the thing continued, softer now, the way you speak to a spooked animal. "Your hearing. Everyone in this city treats your gift as a curse to hide, a crime to confess. I would honor it. Publicly. Court Bone-Carver. A workshop with windows. Apprentices who come to *you*. You would never again have to pretend to be less than you are. You would belong, Zephyrine — openly, legally, lavishly. All I ask—"

"Don't say it."

"—is so small. Deliver me the man Corin."

* * *

She made herself laugh. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, but she made herself do it. "You want me to hand over the man from your own prophecy book. The one your whole machine has been steering toward you for a century. And you can't just — take him? You, with your Wardens and your bells and your thirty years of eating a kingdom's love?"

Something moved behind the king's face. Not anger. Something worse: *embarrassment.*

"The prophecy binds as tightly as any oath," it said, and now the warmth had a hairline fracture in it. "He was pulled out of his own time to be delivered to me, and delivered he must be — freely, willingly, by a hand that chooses. That is the shape of the lock. I cannot pick it. But you—" the kind face reassembled itself, smile first, "—you, Zephyrine, are the key it was cut for. Isn't that flattering? Out of everyone in Vaelhollow, the prophecy chose the lonely bone-carver. Destiny has excellent taste."

"It chose a vessel," she said flatly. "We read the fine print. Chapter seventeen, as it were. You don't want me to hand him over. You want me close enough to wear you."

"And wouldn't that be splendid?" It spread its hands, and for a moment the throne room shimmered and she saw the city as the crown saw it: ten thousand points of warm light, each one a heart, each one a little hearth-fire of belonging, and the crown drinking from all of them. "You think I'm offering you scraps. I'm offering you the *kingdom*. Every widow's grief soothed. Every ghost given voice instead of silence. You hate what I do to the dead — mute them, erase them, tidy them away — but under *your* hand, crowned, none of that. You could let them all speak. You could be the mercy this city has been starving for. Tobias back at your elbow. The chorus of the dead singing in your ears like a choir that finally got a decent conductor. All you have to lose is—" it waved a hand, dismissive, delicate, "—Corin. Who lied to you. Who was *built* to lie to you. A lure, Zephyrine. A hundred-year-old piece of bait with a nice smile. Are you really going to wreck eternity over bait?"

And that — that was the knife, and it knew it, and it slid it in slow.

Because here was the truth she'd been carrying since the archive, since the ledger, since his confession in the dark of the undercity: Corin *had* lied. Every charming, oddly formal word over that first dinner had been a hook. He had been sent to find her the way a fisherman sends a fly to find a trout, and he had known, and she hadn't, and some bruised practical part of her — the part that had survived seven years by trusting no one — whispered that the crown-thing was right. Bait was bait. Kindness with a purpose behind it was just a leash with better stitching.

The crown-thing felt her waver. She knew it did, because its smile deepened by one degree, patient as rot.

"There she is," it murmured. "There's my sensible girl."

* * *

In the real world — in the cold cellar that smelled of stone and old marrow and dragonroot tea — Zephyrine's body sat on a crate of femurs with her eyes closed, and somewhere nearby Corin slept under a blanket, breathing slow, utterly unaware that two ancient powers were currently arguing over him like a disputed inheritance.

Inside the dream, she walked to the workbench. The crown-thing let her, watchful, certain.

She picked up the finished mourning charm. It was perfect. It was the finest thing she had ever carved, and she hadn't carved it, and that was the whole sermon in miniature, wasn't it — everything the crown offered was beautiful precisely because it wasn't hers. Tobias restored would be a Tobias assembled by something that ate cities. The honored gift would be a gift with a collar sewn into the lining. Even the chorus of the dead, freed to sing, would sing only what the crown permitted, and she knew — she had heard it in the queen's relic, in the dragon's memories, in Aldric's buried ghost — that the crown's idea of mercy was a cage with the bars filed smooth.

"You're quiet," said the crown-thing. "Quiet is usually agreement."

"I'm thinking about wallpaper," Zephyrine said.

"...I beg your pardon?"

"The widow's husband. First charm I ever listened to, seven years ago. Dead eleven years and the first thing he wanted to discuss was the wallpaper his wife put up after the funeral. Hated it. *Dreadful green stripes.* Went on about it for forty minutes." She turned the perfect charm over in her fingers. "And you know what she said when I told her — I told her it was a feeling I had, couldn't admit the truth — she laughed and said, 'Well, he always was a fussy man,' and she changed the wallpaper the next week, and she *thanked* me. Because it was hers to change. Nobody handed her that. Nobody arranged it. It cost her something and it was *hers*."

She set the charm down.

"That's the difference between us," she said. "You offer belonging the way a leash offers a walk. Everything I'd ever want, pre-chewed, pre-arranged, pre-owned by you. My mentor back — but *your* version of him. My gift honored — but *your* version of honoring. You don't want to give me a home. You want to be my home, walls and roof and lock." She looked up at the kind face, and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't afraid anymore. Furious, yes. Grieving, certainly. But fury and grief together, she was learning, made a surprisingly sturdy floor to stand on. "So here's my counteroffer. No."

The smile stayed. The warmth did not. For one naked instant the throne room showed its true architecture — a throat, vast and lined with thirty years of swallowed devotion — and the voice that came out of it was the sound a city makes when it stops loving something.

"You will deliver him," it said. "Freely or otherwise. The prophecy is patient. So am I. Ask the dead how patient I am."

"They can't answer," Zephyrine said. "You made sure of that."

"Yes," it agreed pleasantly. "I did."

* * *

She woke with a gasp, tea-cold, hands pressed flat against the femur crate, and the first thing she saw was Corin sitting up across the cellar, blanket pooled around his waist, watching her with eyes far too awake for a sleeping man.

"You were talking in your dream," he said quietly. "You said 'no' four times. Then you said something about wallpaper."

Despite everything — despite the throat and the leash and the ache where Tobias's voice had been — a laugh escaped her, ragged and real. "It was a very persuasive wallpaper."

He crossed to her, and she didn't flinch, which was new, and he sat down beside her on the crate of femurs without asking, which was newer. "It spoke to you. The crown."

"It offered me Tobias. My hearing. A kingdom that belongs to me." She stared at the dying glow of Maren's hearth. "All I had to do was walk you to the palace door like a good dog delivering a stick."

Corin was silent a moment. When he spoke, his voice was carefully level, the levelness of a man holding something down. "And you said no."

"I said no."

"But it's still standing there in your head, isn't it. The offer. It doesn't leave just because you refused." He said it with the weary authority of someone who knew — someone who had been pulled across a century by that same patient voice and knew exactly how long its echoes lasted.

"No," Zephyrine admitted. "It's not leaving. It tucked itself into a corner of my skull like a cat that's decided the house is its now." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Corin — it knew things. About Tobias. About the binding. It said the fire didn't take him, that he's *held*, that it could put him back whenever it likes, which means everything I've mourned for four years has been sitting in that thing's pocket like loose change—"

"Zeph."

"—and the worst part, the genuinely humiliating worst part, is that for about nine seconds I *considered* it. Nine seconds where handing you over seemed like a reasonable price for getting my teacher back. Nine seconds." She dropped her hands and looked at him. "I'm telling you because you deserve to know that the woman the prophecy picked is the kind of woman who can be bought for nine seconds."

Corin considered this with the grave formality she'd once found suspicious and now found, infuriatingly, endearing. "Nine seconds," he said. "Out of seven years of staying hidden, staying safe, staying good. And you spent those nine seconds *arguing with it about wallpaper.*" He shook his head slowly. "If that's what betrayal looks like in you, I've met saints who'd trade."

Before she could decide whether to be comforted or annoyed, the cellar door banged open and Maren came down the stairs three at a time, firelight dancing on her face and something wild in her expression that Zephyrine had learned, in the short terrible weeks since the raid, to recognize as either very good news or a great deal of bad news arriving quickly.

"Up," Maren said. "Both of you. Now." She was already throwing Corin his coat. "The Wardens just posted a city-wide summons — your description, Zeph, and yours, pretty-boy, and a reward that could buy the undercity twice over. But that's not why we're moving tonight." Her grin was all teeth and old guilt transmuted into purpose. "The dragon-vault rotation changes at dawn. Fewest guards it will ever have. We take the fire tomorrow — or we never take it at all."

Above them, faint through the cellar stones, Vaelhollow's bells began to ring — not the hour, not the alarm, but the long rolling peal the crown ordered when it wanted the city's love refreshed.

The crown was calling its city home.

And Zephyrine, deaf in her dreams to everything but the offer still curled in the corner of her skull, rose to answer it with fire instead.
