## 13. Riot of the Quiet

Maren had always said that if you were going to burn something down, you ought to bring bread.

"Riots run on empty stomachs," she told the undercity, standing on an overturned fish cart in the Lamplight Market with a torch in one hand and half a loaf in the other. "So eat first. Nobody makes good history hungry."

There was scattered laughter, the nervous kind, and then—because Maren had that gift, the same one she had for coaxing a dying flame back to life—the laughter turned into something steadier. Zephyrine stood at the edge of the crowd with Corin's shoulder against hers and watched three hundred people who had spent their whole lives being quiet decide, all at once, to be loud.

The plan was simple, which Maren insisted was the only kind of plan worth having. The Wardens believed Zephyrine was hiding somewhere in the Spindleward district—they had been tipped, again, by their informant's careful whispers. So tonight the undercity would *let* them believe it. A thousand rumors would bloom by morning: the bone-carver seen on Tallow Street, the bone-carver seen in the chapel ruins, the bone-carver buying eggs. Every Warden in Vaelhollow would come running to Spindleward with nets and iron.

And while they ran, Maren would take forty people down through the drowned stair into the vaults beneath the Warden citadel, where the last dragon-fire guttered in its cage of wards—and set it free.

"You're sure about the timing?" Corin murmured. He had his hood up, which did nothing to disguise him, because Corin had the kind of face that made hoods look like a fashion choice.

"The bells ring the ninth hour," Maren said. "When they do, every Warden in Spindleward looks up. Old habit. They think the ninth bell means shift change." She grinned, and there was firelight in her teeth. "It doesn't. It means us."

Zephyrine touched the relic at her throat—a small carved knucklebone, her mentor Odell's, worn smooth from her thumb worrying it. All night it had been whispering.

*They know,* Odell kept saying, in the dry, fussy voice he'd used for correcting her carving angles. *They know more than you think, girl. Check your angles.*

"I'm checking," she muttered.

"What?" said Corin.

"Nothing. Talking to a dead man about geometry."

"That's normal for you, isn't it."

"Deeply."

The ninth bell rang.

It went up through the city like a stone dropped into still water, and the water moved. In Spindleward, doors opened and closed. Rumors walked out wearing coats. And down in the Lamplight Market, Maren raised her torch, and forty people who had never been counted by anyone began to count.

* * *

Corin had designed the wards around the dragon-vault thirty years ago, when he still believed the Wardens were shepherds and he was a shepherd's clever dog.

He had spent two days remembering how to undo them, and the remembering had been its own small humiliation. The wards were good work. That was the worst part. He'd been proud of them. He could still feel the pride sitting in his chest like an old coin, tarnished but recognizable.

"Third seal wants a drop of blood," he said, crouched before a door of black iron that hummed faintly, like a struck tuning fork deciding whether to be a bell or a scream. "Mine will do. It remembers me."

"That's grim," said Maren.

"It's efficient."

"It's grim *and* efficient. You're a complicated man, Corin."

He cut his palm on the knife she handed him—it was a cheese knife, because Maren planned rebellions out of what she had—and pressed it to the seal. The iron sighed. Somewhere behind the door, something vast and old shifted its weight, and the temperature of the corridor rose five degrees, and everyone's breath caught at once.

"She knows we're here," whispered a boy called Tam, fifteen years old and trembling so hard his lantern rattled.

"She's known we're here since we came down the stairs," Maren said gently. "Dragons aren't surprised by much. It's one of their best qualities."

The door swung open on heat.

The vault was not what Zephyrine had imagined when Corin described it. She had imagined a furnace, a cage, something dramatic. Instead it was a round chamber, clean and dry, and in its center, coiled beneath a dome of woven ward-light, lay a dragon no bigger than an ox.

She was gray as ash. Her scales had gone dull, her wings folded tight as a shut book, and the fire that should have roared inside her showed only at the corners of her mouth—a flicker, a pilot light, the last ember of a hearth that had once warmed the world.

"Oh," Zephyrine said, and her voice cracked on it.

The dragon opened one eye. It was the color of deep coals, and it looked at Zephyrine for a long moment, and then—at the knucklebone at her throat—and something rumbled up out of her chest. Not a growl. Words, worn almost shapeless by disuse:

*"You hear."*

"I do," Zephyrine whispered.

*"Then hear this, little listener. The man in the crown took my fire to bind his leash. I have been spending it, these thirty years, drop by drop, holding the knot loose. I am nearly empty. When I am gone, the crown holds fast forever."*

Zephyrine translated, voice shaking, and watched the words land on forty faces like rain on drought ground.

Maren stepped forward. She had gone very still, the way she did only around fire. "Your flame," she said. "If we free you—if we carry you out—you can spend the rest burning the knot loose all at once?"

*"I can spend the rest burning,"* the dragon agreed, with what Zephyrine could have sworn was dry humor, *"which is what I was made for. But your Wardens built my cage well. There is a keystone. A ward-stone, in the citadel above, in the room where they keep their ledgers."*

Zephyrine's stomach dropped. "The archive."

*"The archive,"* the dragon agreed.

Which was how the diversion became, in the space of one sentence, not a diversion at all.

* * *

The bells of Vaelhollow had not all rung at once in living memory.

That was the point. Maren's people had spent three weeks—three weeks of whispered meetings in tanneries and tomb-crypts and the back room of a pie shop whose owner pretended deafness very profitably—climbing the bell towers of the city. Nine towers. Nine teams. At the ninth hour, while the Wardens of Spindleward chased rumors of a bone-carver through streets full of nothing, the bells rang.

Not the orderly peal of a festival. They rang *wrong*, each tower on its own rhythm, clashing and tangling, a sound like the city itself had woken from a long sleep and didn't know yet whether it was singing or screaming.

People came out of their houses. That was the part no one had dared hope for. They came out in nightshirts and work coats, holding candles, and they looked up at the ringing towers, and someone—no one ever learned who—began to cheer.

In the Warden citadel, the duty officer stood at his window and felt the first cold drop of a very bad evening.

"Report," he said.

"Sir, the bells are—" His junior officer paused, listening. "They're not following the canon, sir."

"I can *hear* that. Why are they not following the canon?"

"Sir, it appears no one is following the canon."

* * *

Zephyrine had broken into the archive once before, with Odell's ghost coaching her through the dark. It had taken four hours and most of her nerve.

Tonight she came in through the front.

The front doors of the Warden archive stood open because the Wardens were running—not toward the archive, but away from it, summoned by bells and fires and the sight of three hundred quiet people refusing to be quiet anymore. Zephyrine walked up the marble steps between two overturned torch-stands, Corin at her side, and thought: *seven years of hiding, and it ends with me walking in the front door.*

"There," Corin said. The ledger room. He knew the citadel the way he knew his own hands, having built half its locks, and he took the turns without hesitating, past shelves of confiscated charms, past cabinets of names—thousands of names, every unregistered magic-user ever recorded, catalogued like specimens.

Zephyrine stopped at one cabinet. Her own name was in there. She'd known it would be; she'd written it herself, years ago, in a clerk's neat hand, lying about everything except the spelling.

"Later," Corin said softly. "Burn it later. Keystone now."

The ward-stone sat in a case of glass in the center of the ledger room, a lump of gray rock about the size of a loaf of bread, utterly unremarkable except for the way the air bent slightly around it, the way sound went flat near it, the way Zephyrine's hearing—the dead hearing, the true hearing—went silent within three feet of it.

She hated it immediately.

"That's it? It looks like a potato."

"It's a very powerful potato," Corin said. "Thirty years ago I helped enchant it. Watch the seams."

He worked the case open with two hairpins and an apology, and the moment the glass lifted, the stone screamed.

Not aloud. Into the wards. Zephyrine felt it go out through the citadel like a shout through a house, felt every Warden within a mile turn their head—

And then Maren's people hit the citadel's lower gates with fire and numbers, and the shout became one voice in a chorus of chaos, and nobody came.

"They're busy," Zephyrine breathed.

"They're very busy." Corin wrapped the ward-stone in his coat like a swaddled infant. "Come on. We have a dragon to deliver."

* * *

It should have gone cleanly after that. Maren would say, later, that she should have known better than to trust *should*.

They carried the stone down through the citadel's bones, forty rebels and one stolen keystone, and the dragon-vault door stood open and breathing heat, and for one shining moment it seemed the night might cost nothing but sleep.

Then the Wardens came down the drowned stair behind them.

Not many. Twelve, maybe fifteen—the ones too slow for Spindleward, too dutiful for retreat, led by a captain with a voice like a slammed door. Iron nets. Suppression rods. The tools of a trade that had never, until tonight, met resistance.

"Hold the corridor!" Maren shouted, and the undercity held it.

Zephyrine pressed against the wall with the knucklebone in her fist, and the dead of the citadel crowded close—all the prisoners, all the accused, all the ones the Wardens had erased from their own families' memories. They couldn't fight. But they could *tell*. 

*Left side, third rank, the tall one drops his shield when he casts—*

"Duck left!" she screamed. "Tall one, when he raises the rod!"

Tam ducked. The rod's white bolt scorched the wall where his head had been. The boy stared at her, wide-eyed, and then grinned like the sun coming up, and threw the lantern.

Fire caught the net-oil. The corridor lit gold. And through the smoke came the dragon.

She moved slowly—she was dying, and she moved like something dying, each step an argument with gravity—but when her shadow crossed the Wardens, they broke. Not because she attacked. Because she *looked* at them, with eyes full of thirty years of captivity, and whatever men build cages with, it is not proof against being seen by the caged.

They fled. Most of them.

One did not. A young Warden, barely older than Tam, stood his ground with a suppression rod shaking in both hands, and Maren—Maren, who had burned records to save her family and sworn never to burn anything else that breathed—stepped between him and the dragon and said, quietly, "Go home, lad. Go home and tell them what you saw."

He lowered the rod. He ran.

And in the doorway behind him, where the smoke thinned, Zephyrine saw the ones who hadn't come to fight. Two figures in plain coats, standing very still, watching the dragon pass with expressions she recognized even across the smoke, because she had worn them herself for seven years.

Wonder. And grief. And the beginning of a choice.

* * *

The dragon reached the river at dawn.

They had carried her down through the undercity, forty people and a keystone and a legend, and the people of the forgotten levels had come out of their doors to watch her pass, and some of them had knelt, and she had told Zephyrine, grumpily, *"Tell them to stop that, I haven't earned it, I've been napping for thirty years."*

At the water's edge she stopped. The ward-stone, unwrapped, crumbled in Corin's hands like stale bread, its power spent breaking its own cage. Above them, across the waking city, the crown on King Aldric's head felt the loosening of its knot—and Zephyrine felt it feel it, a cold attention turning toward the river like an eye.

*"Little listener,"* the dragon said. *"I have perhaps a day of fire left in me. One burning. Choose well what it unmakes."*

"I will," Zephyrine promised.

*"No."* The coal-dark eye fixed on her, ancient and amused and terribly kind. *"You won't choose. That is the trap the prophecy built for you. Someone else must choose for you—or you will choose wrong, because you are exactly the sort of fool who chooses sacrifice when offered a throne."*

Behind her, Corin made a small sound. She didn't look at him. She was looking at the dragon, and the dragon was looking at her, and somewhere far above, in a palace full of hollow bells, something ancient began to laugh.

The dragon's fire caught the sunrise, and for one moment the whole river burned gold.

Then the bells of Vaelhollow rang again—every tower, in perfect unison, a single clear note—and Zephyrine knew, with the certainty of the dead, that the note was meant for her.

*Come,* said the crown, in a voice like honey poured over iron. *We should talk.*
