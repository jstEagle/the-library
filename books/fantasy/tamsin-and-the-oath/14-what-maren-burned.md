## 14. What Maren Burned

Maren had a rule about fire: it never forgot what you asked of it.

People thought fire was stupid. Hungry and stupid, all appetite and no memory. But Maren had been singing to flame since she was six years old, and she knew better. Fire remembered every kindness and every cruelty. It remembered the hearth-song her mother hummed on cold mornings. It remembered the barn that burned when she was nine, and how she'd stood in the yard with her hands out like a conductor and *begged* it to stop, and how it had stopped — mid-roar, mid-crackle, the whole burning beast of it going quiet as a snuffed candle because a child asked nicely.

That was the day the Wardens should have come for her. They didn't, because her mother threw her into the root cellar and sat on the hatch and told the neighbors the barn had caught from a lantern, nothing strange, nothing strange at all.

Maren learned two lessons that day. One: fire listens. Two: love is a thing you bury.

* * *

The village was called Emberlow, which everyone agreed later was asking for trouble. Forty households tucked into a fold of hills three days south of Vaelhollow, a place where the king's name was spoken the way you spoke of weather — distant, occasionally unpleasant, not your problem. Maren's family kept bees and a small orchard and, unofficially, a very large secret.

Her grandmother had been a hearth-witch back when hearth-witching was merely frowned upon rather than hanged. She'd taught Maren the old songs: the ones that coaxed flame low, the ones that woke it hungry, the one — only once, on her deathbed — that could call fire up out of cold ash, out of things already burned, out of the deep places under the world where the last true fires slept.

"Dragon-work," her grandmother had whispered, gripping Maren's wrist with surprising strength. "The oldest binding there is. If you ever learn it, girl, you'll be worth more to a crown than a legion. That's not a compliment."

Maren was seventeen and immortal-feeling and thought, *worth more than a legion sounds lovely, actually.*

She was twenty-two when the crown came calling.

* * *

They didn't send soldiers first. They sent a letter, written in beautiful copperplate, inviting her — by name, by title she hadn't known she had — to serve as Keeper of the Royal Flame. There was a salary. There were rooms in the palace. There was the phrase *by order of His Majesty King Aldric*, and beneath it a seal that seemed, if you looked too long, to look back.

Her father wanted her to refuse. Her mother wanted her to run. Her little brother Tam wanted to come along, because he was eleven and the palace sounded like somewhere dragons might be.

Maren went. She told herself it was to protect them — a fire-singer loose in the hills was a danger to her family; a fire-singer wearing palace livery was respectable. She told herself a great many things. That was the year she learned the third lesson, the one that took the longest to forgive: the best lies are the ones you tell yourself while looking in a mirror.

* * *

The Royal Flame lived in a vault beneath the east wing, behind seven doors, and it was dying.

No one had told her that part. The letters had spoken of tending, of stewardship, of honor. What they meant was: the last dragon-fire in Vaelhollow — perhaps in the world, though the Wardens' records were sealed even to Keepers — guttered in an iron cradle the size of a wagon, and it had been guttering for thirty years, and the crown needed someone who could sing to it.

She sang. The flame leaned toward her voice like a tired animal toward a familiar hand, and Maren felt something crack open in her chest, some door she hadn't known was there.

"You're beautiful," she told it, the first night, alone in the vault with her voice echoing off iron. "What happened to you?"

And the flame — because fire remembers — showed her.

Not words. Fire doesn't do words. It did *images*, pressed into her mind like flowers between pages: a dragon vast as a hillside, wings folded around a young king in armor; a bargain struck in smoke; a crown being forged — no, not forged, *fed* — and something pouring itself into the gold the way water pours into a cup, except the cup drank back. A king kneeling. A king rising. A king who moved wrong afterward, whose smile arrived a half-second late, whose eyes in portraits always looked slightly past the painter.

Aldric had bound the thing in the crown with dragon-fire. And the binding was eating the fire alive.

Maren sat down on the cold vault floor and wept for a creature she'd known one hour, and then she got up and went back to work, because that was what Keepers did, and because the alternative — refusing, running, becoming a fugitive with a target painted on her whole family — was unthinkable.

Unthinkable. She'd remember that word later, with bitterness. So much of what she did next had seemed unthinkable right up until she did it.

* * *

For six years she served. She learned the vault's rhythms, the Warden captains' names, which clerks could be charmed and which could only be bribed. She learned that the crown-thing — she'd started thinking of it as *the Tenant*, because it clearly considered the king's body a lodging — fed on devotion the way she fed on bread. Every festival, every oath of fealty, every child taught to love the king's face on the coin: the Tenant ate, and grew sleeker, and the dragon-fire dimmed another degree.

She also learned that the Tenant noticed people. That was the worst of it. It wasn't a blind hunger; it was *attentive*. When she sang to the flame, she sometimes felt a pressure at the edge of the vault, high above, through seven doors and thirty feet of stone — a tasting, a considering. The way you pause over a shelf, wondering whether to take down a jar.

In her sixth year, the pressure sharpened into interest. In her seventh, into appetite.

The summons came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Tuesdays were for ledger-work. Instead: the throne room, empty but for the king and four silent guards, and the king smiling that half-second-late smile, and saying, "Keeper Iversen. We understand you have family in Emberlow."

"We do, Your Majesty." Her knees were doing their best impression of loyalty.

"Devoted folk, we hear. Loyal." He said the word slowly, rolling it like a sweet. "Loyalty is such a precious thing. We do hope your family knows its duty. Records must be accurate, after all. The census comes to Emberlow in the spring." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Wardens do so enjoy accurate censuses."

There was no threat in it. That was the horror. It was a friendly reminder, a landlord mentioning the rent. *Your family exists at our pleasure. Their names are in our books. Books burn, of course — but so do villages.*

She walked out of the throne room with her spine straight and her mind already working, and that night in the vault she made the decision that would define the rest of her life.

She would burn the records herself.

* * *

It took eight months. Eight months of careful, patient, terrified work. She couldn't just torch the census office — the Tenant would know instantly, and it would know *why*, and Emberlow would pay. No. The records had to die of natural causes. A lamp knocked over. A chimney fire. An accident of the sort that happens to paper buildings full of paper.

She spent those months learning the census office the way she'd learned the vault: its rhythms, its drafts, its habits. She learned that the west annex — where the provincial rolls lived, including everything on Emberlow — had a chimney that drew badly and a clerk named Pell who smoked a pipe he was forbidden to have and fell asleep at his desk most afternoons.

She learned, too, that fire can be *asked* to wait. That was the hardest singing of her life: coaxing a coal of intention into the chimney flue, a slow seed of heat that would sleep for days and then wake hungry in exactly the wrong place. Her grandmother had called this ember-craft and forbidden it absolutely. *Fire that waits is fire that chooses,* she'd said. *And a singer who teaches fire to choose will answer for its choices.*

"I'll answer," Maren whispered to the sleeping coal. "I'm answering already."

On the night of the fire, she was in the vault, singing to the dragon-flame, visible to forty witnesses, unimpeachable. Across the palace, the west annex caught at the hour past midnight. By dawn, the provincial rolls for the southern hill country were ash, Clerk Pell was being treated for smoke inhalation and heroic pipe-related innocence, and the census of Emberlow existed nowhere in the world except in the memories of forty beekeeping households.

The Tenant knew. Of course it knew. Fire remembers, and so does the thing that eats kingdoms. But knowing and proving were different animals, and Maren had left no thread to pull — or so she believed for one golden, foolish month.

Then the letters began arriving in Emberlow. Friendly letters. Census follow-ups. A Warden survey team, terribly sorry about the inconvenience in the capital, simply *must* verify the village rolls in person, nothing to worry about, purely routine.

Routine teams don't ride with manacles in their saddlebags. Maren heard about the manacles from a laundry-maid she'd been kind to, and she understood: the Tenant couldn't prove anything, so it had decided to collect the evidence itself. Forty households, catalogued, tested, sorted. Anyone in Emberlow with a gift would go into the books, and the books would go into the archive, and her family would live leashed forever — or die inconvenient, whichever was tidier.

So Maren did the second-worst thing of her life, which was necessary, and which she has never once forgiven herself for.

She burned Emberlow's records again. All of them. The parish book, the tithe rolls, the school registers, the letters in her mother's cedar chest, the drawings Tam had made at six years old with his name carefully spelled *Tamn*. Everything with a name on it, everything that tied a person to a person, everything the Wardens could use to build a web.

And because a web needs anchors, and because anchors are people, she burned the connections too. She wrote letters — as a concerned citizen of the capital, as a tax collector's clerk, as a spurned sweetheart — that scattered her family like seeds on wind. Father to an uncle in the west who owed him money. Mother to a sister nobody had mentioned in years. Tam to a boarding apprenticeship with a cooper three provinces away, arranged through channels she still doesn't fully want to examine.

By the time the Warden team reached Emberlow, they found a village of forty households with unusually poor record-keeping and no unusual persons whatsoever. The Iversen family, they concluded, had dispersed years ago. One household listed a *M. Iversen, deceased, age 19, fever.*

Maren read her own death notice in a copied report and laughed until she cried, alone in the vault, with the dying dragon-fire flickering sympathy against the iron walls.

* * *

Here is what she does not tell people, what she has told no one, not Zephyrine, not Corin, not the smugglers who brought her down into the undercity: the fire she set in Emberlow's parish house was not supposed to spread.

Ember-craft is precise. She had sung the boundaries into the coal herself — *this room, these shelves, this paper and no more.* But fire that chooses is fire that negotiates, and the parish house was old and dry, and the wind that night came off the hills wrong, and the fire crossed the lane and took the miller's barn before it died.

No one died. She checks that sentence every day, the way you check a scar. No one died. The miller lost his barn and his winter stores and his good humor permanently. A boy named Oskar, who had helped her carry apples when she was a girl, lost his eyebrows and very nearly his arm.

But the village looked at the ashes, and the village talked, and what the village said was: *the Iversens were cursed, and thank goodness they're gone.*

Her mother's last letter to her — smuggled out through three hands, arriving months late, smelling of someone else's kitchen — contained one line Maren can recite in the dark:

*You saved us, love, and I will never say otherwise. But your grandmother warned you what fire costs, and now you know, and knowing is the heaviest thing you'll ever carry.*

Knowing is the heaviest thing. She carries it down the undercity stairs every morning. She carries it past the dying dragon-flame she stole out of the Warden vault the same night she fled the palace — yes, she took it, the last true fire in the world, smuggled out in a brazier disguised as a soup cauldron while alarms rang overhead, and if that makes her a thief then let history argue with the ashes.

She carries it, and she has decided — this is the transmutation, the alchemy her grandmother never taught because it can't be taught, only chosen — that the weight will buy something. That every ounce of guilt gets poured into guarding the people in front of her now. The bone-carver who hears the dead and pretends she doesn't. The pretty, formal, haunted man who looks at Zephyrine like a man reading his own sentence.

Maren failed her family by loving them from inside the machine. She will not fail these two. Whatever it costs. Whatever fire asks.

* * *

Presently, in the present tense, in the lamplit workshop under the forgotten levels, Maren watches Zephyrine carve.

The bone-carver works the way Maren sings: total attention, hands moving on faith. Tonight she's shaping a small round token for Corin, who sits across the table pretending to read and actually watching Zephyrine's hands with an expression he thinks is neutral and is not.

"It's a listening charm," Zephyrine says, without looking up. "Sort of. Don't get excited. It won't do much."

"What will it do?" Corin asks.

"It'll mean that if I ever stop hearing you — really hearing you — the charm goes cold. So I'll know." She blows bone-dust off the surface. "Everyone I've ever carved for, I can find again. Even deaf. It's not magic, exactly. It's more like... remembering, with a handle on it."

Maren says nothing. She is stirring the soup cauldron that is not a soup cauldron, keeping the dragon-flame company, and she is thinking: *she carves handles for remembering. She has no idea what she's making. She has no idea what she's for.*

Because Maren has read the ledger pages Corin recited from memory, and she has done arithmetic the other two haven't yet dared. The prophecy doesn't end with a hero. Maren has spent enough years around the Tenant to know how it reads fine print — and the fine print, she is nearly certain, ends with a vessel. A wearer. A new crown-shaped home for an old hungry thing, and the vessel's name rhymes with *bone-carver.*

She hasn't said it aloud. Saying it makes it real, and real things demand action, and action demands a plan, and plans require her to admit how little time they have.

Instead she ladles soup — actual soup, from an actual pot, because a woman cannot live on destiny — and sets three bowls on the table, and says, "Eat. Both of you. Prophecies keep; soup doesn't."

Zephyrine smiles at her, tired and grateful, and for one ordinary moment the workshop feels like a kitchen in a house where nobody is hiding.

Then the lamps gutter. Not the dragon-flame — that burns steady as ever — but the ordinary lamps, all three at once, and the temperature drops, and Maren's skin prickles with a sensation she knows from the throne room, from the vault, from seven years of being tasted through stone.

Pressure. Attention. Appetite, arriving.

Zephyrine goes rigid, her head tilting the way it does when she listens to the dead — and then her hand flies to her mouth.

"No," she whispers. "No, no — they're gone. They're all gone. I can't hear *anyone.*"

Maren sets down the ladle very slowly, ice walking up her spine, and understands that the Tenant has stopped tasting and started striking — and that it has begun, as it always begins, by taking away everyone who might help.
