## 15. The Dead Go Silent

The morning it happened, Zephyrine was arguing with a dead baker about sourdough.

"You're overworking it," Master Fenwick said from his corner of the shop, where he had spent every hour since his death offering unsolicited opinions on bread she could not even eat in peace. "You fold it like you're angry at it."

"I *am* angry at it," Zephyrine said, shaping the loaf on the counter between a mourning charm and a half-finished rosary of knucklebones. "It's a metaphor for my life."

"Everything's a metaphor for your life. That's what happens when you talk to ghosts instead of going to festivals."

She flicked flour at him. It passed through his shoulder and dusted the wall behind, which he took as a personal victory. That was the thing about Fenwick — eleven years dead and still insufferable, still warm, still hers. He had been her teacher in life and remained her teacher after, though now the curriculum was mostly baking, complaining about modern youth, and reminding her to eat.

The shop smelled of bone dust and yeast. Outside, Vaelhollow went about its morning: cart wheels in the cobbles, the bell of Saint Ossuary tolling eight, a fishmonger shouting that his herring were practically still swimming. The city's dead murmured beneath all of it, as they always did — a low tide of voices under the living noise. The widow Hessa humming in her grave. Three generations of dockworkers grumbling about the new harbor master. A child's laughter from a century ago, chasing a dog no one remembered.

Zephyrine had lived with that chorus so long she'd stopped hearing it as sound. It was more like weather. Like knowing rain was coming by the ache in your knees.

The loaf went into the oven. Fenwick approved. Corin was due back from the undercity before noon, and Maren had promised — *threatened* — to teach them both the fire-song's second verse, which apparently involved holding a note until your sinuses surrendered.

It was, all things considered, a good morning.

Which should have been her first warning. Good mornings, in her experience, were the crown-thing's favorite camouflage.

* * *

It began, afterward, with silence where there should have been a voice.

She noticed it the way you notice a missing stair. She was carving a charm for a customer — old Tomas, alive and impatient, wanting something for his rheumatism — and she reached, out of habit, for the dead man whose femur she was working. He always had opinions about how she held her tools. He'd been a surgeon, once, and considered every other craft a hobby.

Nothing came.

She frowned. Reached again, deeper, the way you'd cup your ear toward a distant room.

Still nothing.

"Tomas," she said aloud, keeping her voice light, "give me a moment. I want to check the grain."

He grunted and turned to examine her shelf of trinkets, and Zephyrine set down the femur and pressed her palm flat against it, the way she'd done ten thousand times, and listened with everything she had.

Empty.

Not quiet — quiet was full, quiet was the sea-sound of the near-dead, the murmur of bone remembering. This was *nothing*. This was pressing her ear against a door and finding no room behind it. She picked up another relic. Another. The widow Hessa's ring. A soldier's button. The little clay whistle she'd carved for a girl who'd died of fever forty years before Zephyrine was born.

All of them empty. All of them just — objects. Dead things being dead things, the way a chair is a chair.

"No," she said softly.

Fenwick appeared beside her, concern creasing his translucent face. "Zeph? You've gone white as your stock."

"Fenwick." Her voice cracked on his name. "Say something. Anything. Insult my bread."

"I already did. Twice." He touched her cheek; she felt only cold air. "Zeph. I'm right here. Can't you hear me?"

And she understood, then, with the particular horror of watching a flood take the ground you stand on, that he was speaking and she was not hearing. That the silence wasn't in the bones. It was in *her*.

* * *

She ran through the shop like a woman drowning, grabbing relics, shaking them, begging them. She pulled the queen's locket from its hiding place beneath the floorboards — the relic that had told her the truth about Aldric, the one thing the crown-thing would most want to muzzle — and pressed it to her ear until the metal bit her skin.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

Through the window, she saw the city, and saw it wrong for the first time in seven years. Vaelhollow had always been two cities layered together, the living walking atop the dead like ice over a lake. Now the lake had frozen solid. Every churchyard she could see from her door — and there were four — sat utterly mute. The great ossuary cathedral of Saint Ossuary, where the bones of ten thousand citizens were stacked into arches and pillars, gave off nothing at all. It should have been deafening. It should have been a choir.

It was furniture. Ten thousand people, reduced to furniture.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, you *coward*."

Because this was not an attack on her. Not really. This was the crown-thing striking at everything she loved at once, the way a petulant king burns a province because one village defied him. It couldn't find her — Maren's wards and the undercity's winding ways had kept her hidden these weeks — so it had done the next best thing. It had taken away the dead. All of them. Everywhere.

Mass erasure, performed not with fire but with silence. No graves desecrated. No bodies moved. Just every voice in Vaelhollow switched off like lamps at curfew, and no one living would ever know anything had changed.

Except her. Except the ones who knew to listen.

She thought of the child with the clay whistle, laughing somewhere in the deep layers of the city's memory, and wondered if she was still laughing, or if she had been made into nothing at all. If erasure meant death or something worse than death. If the dead could feel themselves being silenced, the way sleepers feel a hand over their mouths.

Her legs gave out. She sat down hard on the shop floor among her bones and her broken loaves and her seven years of careful, careful work, and she did not cry, because crying required a kind of energy she did not have. She simply sat, in a silence so total it had weight, pressing against her eardrums like deep water.

Fenwick knelt across from her. His mouth was moving. She watched his lips shape words she couldn't catch — her name, probably, over and over. *Zeph. Zeph. Zeph.*

"I can see you," she told him, and her voice sounded enormous in the dead air. "That's — okay. That's something. I can see you. Talk slow."

He talked slow. She read his lips like a woman learning a language in reverse, piecing meaning from shapes: *Wardens didn't do this. Too big. It's him. It's the crown. He's cutting the lines.*

"I know," she said. "I know. Why does it feel like — like losing you all over again?"

His face did something terrible and gentle. He reached out and couldn't touch her, and she watched him understand that too, watch the ghost learn he had become a picture of himself.

*Find Maren,* his lips said. *The dragon-fire. If anyone knows how to mend this—*

The shop door banged open.

* * *

Corin came through it at a run, coat flying, breathing like he'd climbed from the undercity without stopping — which, judging by the soot on his boots, he had. He took one look at her on the floor and dropped to his knees.

"What happened? Are you hurt? Maren felt something — the flame *screamed*, she said, the whole vault screamed, and then—"

"It's gone." Her voice came out flat and strange. "All of it. Every dead voice in Vaelhollow. He's cut the lines, Corin. The whole city. Ten thousand years of the dead, and he just—" She made a small gesture, a snuffing motion. "I reached for Fenwick and there was nothing. There's never been nothing. Even before I knew what I was, there was *something*. It's like being born backwards. Like someone reached into my skull and took out the part of me that—"

She stopped. Breathed. Corin's hand hovered near hers, not touching, waiting for permission the way he always did, and something about that patience nearly undid her.

"He did it because of me," she said. "To punish me. To make me come running. And the worst part — the truly obscene part — is that it worked. Because I don't know what to do if I can't hear them. Seven years, Corin. Seven years I built my whole self around listening, and now I'm just—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm just a woman sitting on the floor talking to herself."

"You're talking to me," Corin said quietly.

"That's different."

"Is it?"

She looked up at him. He was close enough that she could see the fine lines around his eyes that didn't match his face, the hundred-year weariness he wore under the charm of his youth. He had been bait once, a lure strung on prophecy's hook, and he had chosen her anyway, and she had not yet fully forgiven him but she had stopped counting the debt every day, and that was almost the same thing.

"The dead are witnesses," she recited — Maren's lesson, the first one. "Not echoes. Witnesses. They remember what the crown wants forgotten."

"And now?" Corin asked gently.

"Now they've been made to forget." She pressed her palms against her eyes. "Do you understand what he's doing? It's not punishment. Punishment is simple. This is *housekeeping*. Every person who could testify against him — every ghost who remembers the real Aldric, every ancestor who remembers what Vaelhollow was before the crown — silenced before we can call them to stand witness. He's not attacking me. He's destroying evidence."

Fenwick, kneeling beside her, mouthed something urgent. She squinted at his lips. *Maren. Vault. Go.*

"Maren wants us at the vault," she translated.

"She guessed as much," Corin said. "There's more. When the flame screamed — right before the silence fell — Maren says it burned *blue*. For maybe ten heartbeats. She's never seen it do that. She thinks—" He hesitated.

"Think fast," Zephyrine said. "We haven't got much else."

"She thinks the silence cost him something. That severing the whole city at once drew on the same well the dragon-fire draws from. Binding magic, unbinding magic — same coin, different pockets. He just spent a fortune, Zeph. Maybe most of what he has."

Hope is a dangerous thing to hand a drowning woman. She gripped it anyway.

"Then he's wounded," she said slowly, "and frightened, and he's just shown us exactly where it hurts. Cutting off the dead wasn't strength. It was panic wearing armor." She pushed herself upright. Her knees ached. Her ears rang with a silence that had texture, that had teeth. "Good. Let him be afraid. I've been afraid for seven years. It's his turn."

But even as she said it, gathering her coat, tucking the mute locket against her heart where it weighed nothing and everything, the true horror of the morning settled over her like snowfall. Not the strategy. Not the war.

The quiet.

She had spent her whole life accompanied. First the unwanted murmur of strangers' dead, then Fenwick's voice, then the vast chorus of Vaelhollow's remembered thousands. She had complained about it, resented it, hidden it, been hunted for it — and all along it had been the one constant companionship of her existence. She had never once been alone inside her own head.

Now she was. Utterly. Irrevocably, perhaps. And underneath her fury, underneath the plan already forming, a smaller, colder voice asked the question she couldn't say aloud:

If he never lifted the silence — if the dead stayed dark forever — who would she be then? A carver of charms that were only charms. A woman with a gift amputated. Alone in a way no living person was ever meant to be alone.

Corin touched her elbow. "Ready?"

No, she thought. I will never be ready again.

"Yes," she said.

They went out into the loud, bright, indifferent morning, into a city where ten thousand witnesses had been made to forget their own names — and Zephyrine carried the silence with her like a wound, and did not yet know that the wound was also a door.

Behind them, unnoticed, the queen's locket lay against her heart — and in its hollow metal, faint as breath on winter glass, something stirred that was neither dead nor silent, and had been waiting a very long time to be heard.
