## 16. Deaf to Everything But Him

The silence had been with her for three days now, and Zephyrine was learning that it had textures.

There was the silence of the shop — no, not the shop; the shop was ash. There was the silence of Maren's undercity rooms, where the dragon-fire's glow came through the vents in slow amber pulses and nobody spoke above a whisper because sound carried strangely down there. That silence was thick, felted, like wool packed into her ears.

And then there was the silence of herself. The one where she reached, out of habit, for the dead the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, and found nothing. Not quiet. Nothing. The city above them held ten thousand ghosts and she could not hear so much as a sigh from any of them.

She sat on the edge of a cot that smelled of lamp oil and old stone and did not sleep.

"You're doing the thing again," Corin said from the doorway.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you tilt your head like you're listening for something." He came in carrying two chipped mugs of chicory that Maren brewed strong enough to strip varnish. "You've done it forty times since dinner. I counted."

"You counted."

"I'm a hundred and twelve years old, Zeph. Counting is most of what I have left."

She took the mug because refusing it would have required more energy than she possessed. The warmth bit through the ceramic into her palms. "I wasn't listening for anything," she said. "That's the whole problem. There's nothing *to* listen for. I just keep reaching anyway. Like scratching at a wall where a door used to be."

Corin lowered himself onto the cot beside her, leaving the careful six inches of space he always left, as though proximity were something that had to be earned rather than assumed. She noticed, as she had been noticing for days now, how deliberate he was about everything. A man who had spent a century being aimed at people learned to move carefully, apparently. Learned to make sure every step could be explained.

"Does it hurt?" he asked. "The reaching."

"No. That's the awful part. It doesn't hurt. It's just *absent*. Like a room in my head got bricked over while I was standing in it." She wrapped both hands around the mug. "You know what the worst part is? Before all this, I would have given anything for an hour of true quiet. Seven years I hid my gift, muffled it, lied around it. And now that someone's taken it—" Her voice caught on something. She drank the terrible chicory to cover it.

He didn't say *I'm sorry*. He'd said it enough in the first day that she'd told him if he apologized once more she was going to hit him with a charm blank, and she'd meant it, and he'd known she meant it, which was its own strange intimacy.

Instead he said: "Maren asked if you wanted to try the resonance bowls tomorrow. The fire-singers use them for training. She thinks—"

"I know what she thinks. She thinks if I can't hear the dead, maybe I can learn to hear the living better. Make a virtue of my mutilation." Zephyrine heard her own bitterness and winced at it. "That's not fair. She's kind. They're all kind. It's just—" She set the mug down on the floor with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to do something less careful. "Everyone keeps treating me like a wound that needs dressing. And I let them, because it's easier than saying what I actually am, which is furious, and frightened, and so tired of being *useful* to other people's plans."

The words hung there. Corin looked at his own hands.

"That's what I was," he said quietly. "Useful. For a hundred years. You get to be angry about three days of it. I had a century of practice pretending I didn't mind."

She turned to look at him properly. In the amber light from the vent his face was all planes and patience, handsome in the way furniture is handsome — well-made, enduring, built by someone for a purpose. Except that wasn't fair either, and she was tired tonight of letting unfairness stand.

"Tell me again," she said. "All of it. From the beginning. I want to hear it when I'm not bleeding."

* * *

Corin had told the story twice before — once to Maren, once in fragments to Zephyrine during the flight from the burning shop — but never sitting still, never in the dark, never to someone who was choosing to listen rather than needing to know.

"I was born in 1211, in a fishing village up the coast that isn't on maps anymore," he began. "I was twenty-two when the Wardens came through recruiting for the palace archive. I could read. In those days that made you rare as a saint's fingerbone. They put me in the prophecy ledgers section, cataloguing, copying, indexing. Boring work. Holy work, they called it." He smiled without humor. "I believed them. That's the part I need you to understand, Zeph. I didn't fall. I *climbed*. Every rung of it felt like service."

"And then you read the ledger."

"And then I read the ledger." His hands flexed in his lap. "There was a page — you've seen it, the one with our faces — and beneath it, instructions. Not prophecy. *Procedure*. 'When the hearer of bone appears, send the keeper of the page. Let him be young. Let him be charming. Let him love her, if he can manage it; it will hold longer than duty.' Signed by the Archivist Royal." He paused. "Who, we now know, hadn't been alive for thirty years when he signed it. The crown signed it. Through him."

Zephyrine watched his profile. "They kept you young."

"There's a word for what I am. The undercity uses it. *Lodestone*. A person made magnetic, so the right people can't help walking toward him." He laughed, short and ugly. "Do you know how it works? I don't fully. Some binding in my blood, paid for with — I don't even know what they took. Years, probably. A family. I can't remember my mother's face anymore, and I remember *everything*, that's the joke of it. Everything except the things they spent."

"They sent you to find me."

"They sent me to find you and deliver you. There were orders for after, Zeph. I burned them. But I memorized them first, because I'm a good archivist, and I can recite them, and some nights I do, like penance." His voice went flat, recitation-voice: "'The vessel will come willingly if she believes she is loved. Ensure she believes it.'" He stopped. The vent-light pulsed once, twice, amber across the stone.

"In the archive," Zephyrine said slowly, "when you followed me. When you caught me in the stacks. You said you'd dreamed my face since childhood."

"I said what the crown needed you to hear. The dream was real — they seeded it, planted imagery in my sleep for decades so I'd believe destiny when it arrived. But the *telling* of it, that night, the timing — that was the lure working." He finally turned to face her. "Here's the part I have no defense for. Somewhere between the dinner and the archive and the cellars, it stopped being an assignment. I don't know when. I don't know if that makes it better or worse. Maybe the binding made me love you. Maybe loving you broke the binding. I genuinely cannot tell the difference from inside, and I have had a hundred years to become an expert on the difference, and I *cannot tell*."

The silence after that was the third kind of silence — not felted, not absent, but held, the way you hold your breath.

"So you understand," Zephyrine said, "why I can't trust anything I feel around you."

"Yes."

"And you understand why I have to decide anyway."

His breath actually stopped. She saw it happen — saw the archivist, the lodestone, the hundred-year-old boy from the unmapped village all go still at once, waiting.

"Because here's what I know," she said. "For seven years, every relationship I had was filtered through my gift. I knew what people really meant. I knew the widow loved her husband and hated his wallpaper. I knew which customers lied and which ones grieved. I never once had to take anyone on faith, and I told myself that was wisdom, but it was cowardice wearing wisdom's coat. I never chose anyone. Things were simply *true*, and I stood among truths like a woman standing in a market, sampling." She picked the mug back up just to have her hands occupied. "Now the market's gone. Now there's only you, and what you say, and what you do, and whether I believe them. No relics. No whispers. Just—" she gestured vaguely at all of him, "—a man who burns his orders and counts my head-tilts and brews nothing because Maren won't let him near the stove."

"That last one was a kindness to everyone," Corin said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and she pretended not to notice because he would have wanted her to.

"I choose you," she said. "Not the prophecy's version. Not the version the crown designed. I've met that version — he's very smooth, he mentions dreams at exactly the right moment. I'm choosing the one who leaves six inches of space on cots like a gentleman from a century that doesn't exist. The one who memorizes his sins so he can't pretend out of them. The one who's terrified right now, and hiding it badly." She reached across the six inches and took his hand. His fingers were cold and closed around hers like a man catching a rope thrown into water. "I can't verify you anymore, Corin. So I'm going to find out the ordinary way. Slowly. Badly. Like everyone else does."

"That sounds," he managed, "unbearable."

"It is. Welcome to being alive."

He laughed — really laughed, the first time since the shop burned, and the sound went through the little stone room and came back off the walls, and Zephyrine realized she was hearing it the way hearing people hear things: once, unrepeatable, worth attending to. She leaned into him, deliberately closing the six inches, and felt him exhale a hundred years.

* * *

Later — much later, the vent-light gone dim and blue — Corin lay awake watching her sleep and conducted his usual audit.

This was the ritual: every night, he reviewed the day for evidence of the lure. Had he said anything calculated? Had he touched her hand at a moment engineered for maximum effect? Was the warmth in his chest genuine or administered?

Tonight the audit kept failing, and the failure felt like a door opening.

Because here was the thing the crown had never understood about its own machinery: it had built him to be believed. It had polished him, rehearsed him, seeded his dreams and tuned his smile. But it had never imagined that the woman it aimed him at would lose the very sense that made her vulnerable — that she would arrive at him deaf to enchantment, stripped of every subtle current the binding rode on, and look at him with nothing but her ordinary human judgment and say *yes*.

The lure had been caught. By the bait's own heart, if it was his heart. He was choosing to believe it was.

She stirred against his shoulder and murmured something. He bent close.

"...ledger," she muttered. "Final page. The margins. Tamsin's oath... there was writing in the margins, under the illumination..."

Corin went very still.

He was, whatever else he was, the finest cataloguer the Warden archive had ever produced. And he suddenly remembered — with the terrible clarity of a man whose entire curse was memory — a final page he had copied himself, a hundred years ago, in a young man's careful hand. A page whose marginalia had been dismissed as scribble. A page he had indexed, filed, and delivered to the restricted vault beneath the palace.

The vault he alone, of everyone left alive, knew how to enter.

He lay back in the dark, heart hammering, and began, silently, to plan.
