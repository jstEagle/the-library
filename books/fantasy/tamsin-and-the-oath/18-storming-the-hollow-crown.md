## 18. Storming the Hollow Crown

### Zephyrine

The bells began at midnight, and they were wrong.

Zephyrine had lived in Vaelhollow long enough to know its bells the way she knew her own pulse—the cathedral's deep bronze, the harbor tower's bright triple-strike, the little cracked thing in Tanner's Row that always rang a half-beat late. Tonight every bell in the city rang at once, and none of them rang true. They sounded hollow. Struck from inside.

"That's Maren," Corin said beside her. They crouched on the roof of a tannery in the Shambles, close enough to the palace hill to see it black against the stars, its windows lit gold like a cake no one was allowed to eat. "She promised fire and bells."

"She promised *diversion*," Zephyrine said. "This is theater."

"This is Maren." He smiled, and even now—especially now—with the whole plan balanced on a knife's edge, his smile did something unfair to her chest. "You should have heard her planning speech. There were gestures."

Below them, the undercity came up into the world.

They poured out of grates and cellar doors and storm channels, hundreds of them, people Zephyrine had spent three weeks learning by name: the lamplighter twins who could walk through shadows; old Piet with his unregistered gift for making locks forget themselves; a fishwife named Odile whose voice could crack glass and, when she was angry, which was now, stone. They carried no torches. Maren had been very firm about that. *We are the light*, she'd said, and everyone had groaned, and she'd said, *Good, you're taking it seriously.*

"Second wave on my mark," Corin murmured. His hand found hers in the dark. His palm was sweating. That comforted her more than any charm ever had—even prophets, it turned out, got frightened.

"You memorized the wards?" she asked.

"I designed four of them." He said it lightly, but she heard the shame underneath, the same note it had carried since he'd confessed what he was: a man pulled forward out of his own century, kept young and useful as bait in a trap a hundred years in the setting. "The fifth one I only helped with. The fifth one is going to be a problem."

"The fifth one is always a problem," said a dry voice behind them, and there was Maren, climbing over the tannery ridge tiles with soot already on her knuckles and her braid coming loose, looking like a woman about to commit joyful arson. She dropped down between them. "Wardens' garrison just moved toward the harbor. Bells worked. Odile's working the east gate. You two—" she pointed at Zephyrine, then Corin, then the palace, in the manner of someone directing particularly slow children "—go be prophetic."

"And you?"

Maren grinned, and for a moment firelight that wasn't there yet flickered in her eyes. "I'm going to go say hello to an old friend in the vault. Someone has to carry the dragon."

She kissed Zephyrine once on each cheek—a Maren blessing—and was gone down the roof slope like water finding its way downhill, leaving a faint smell of struck matches.

Zephyrine watched her go and felt the dead stir around her, faint as breath on glass. Even here, even now, they gathered. *Go on then*, whispered her mentor Brannic's voice, worn soft as river stone. *Mind the left stair. It's rotten.* 

"Left stair's rotten," she told Corin.

"How do you—"

"Brannic."

Corin nodded solemnly at the empty air. "Thank you, Brannic."

Somewhere in the dark above them, Zephyrine would have sworn she heard a huff, the ghostly equivalent of *finally*.

* * *

### Corin

The palace wards woke as they crossed the outer wall, and Corin felt them the way he imagined a spider feels its web tremble—every thread of them his own work, laid down across decades he hadn't lived honestly.

He'd built these defenses believing he was protecting a kingdom. That had been the story they gave him, back when he still believed stories were given rather than made. *The crown must be guarded, Corin. The king must be kept safe.* He'd been twenty-six years old and flattered to be asked. By the time he understood what wore the crown, the wards knew his hands better than he knew his own face, and there was no unmaking them without unmaking himself.

Tonight he intended to do both.

"Third gate," he whispered. Ahead of them the gate stood open—Odile's work, or Piet's, or simply the garrison chasing Maren's fires into the lower city—but the arch above it shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmered off summer cobbles. Only Corin could see it. Only Corin had ever seen it. "Don't touch the stones. Walk the center line. And whatever happens, don't apologize to it."

"Don't *apologize* to it?"

"It's a ward of courtesy. I made it when I was young and earnest." He winced at the memory. "It responds to manners. If you thank it, it will hold you there politely forever."

Zephyrine stared at him. "You built a trap that runs on politeness."

"I was *twenty-six*."

They walked the center line together, and Corin felt the courtesy-ward taste them as they passed, feel their intent, find nothing in it it recognized as threat because he had never taught it to recognize this. He'd taught it bandits and assassins and foreign kings. He had never imagined the danger would come up from underneath, carrying bone charms and righteous fury and, in his case, a hundred years of guilt sharpened to a point.

Inside the wall, the palace grounds were wrong. He'd known they would be, but knowing didn't help. The gardens grew in patterns no gardener chose. The fountains ran backward. And everywhere, everywhere, that faint golden glow from the throne room windows, pulsing slow as something asleep—or digesting.

"It knows we're here," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Not yet. It knows *something*." Corin touched her elbow, steering her left, away from the grand stair. "It tastes devotion the way we taste air. Right now the whole city is ringing hollow bells and burning Warden posts, and all that fear and hope is a feast. We're a crumb in a banquet."

"Charming."

"I'm told I have a gift for metaphor."

"You have a gift for being insufferable in Latin grammar," she said, but her shoulder pressed against his as they moved through the dark, and he took the warmth of it like a ration against a long winter.

They reached the servants' door Brannic's ghost had described—a door Corin had walked through a thousand times in another life—and Piet was already there, picking the lock with his gift and his fingers both, muttering to it affectionately. *"There we are, lovely, you never saw us, no, you're just a door, doors open all the time—"* The lock sighed and forgot itself.

"After you," Piet said, bowing.

And that was when the bells stopped.

All of them. At once. Mid-peal, cut off like a throat gripped shut, and in the ringing absence Corin heard the thing he had dreaded for a century: the sound of the crown turning its attention.

From the highest tower, a light kindled. Not gold. White. Cold as a ledger's final page.

"It's awake," Corin said. His mouth had gone dry. "Zeph—it's awake, and it's not looking at the riot. It's looking for *you*."

### Maren

The vault door was Warden steel, three inches thick, warded with everything the Wardens had learned in thirty years of hoarding magic they didn't understand. Maren stood before it in the flooded dark of the under-vaults, ankle-deep in cold water, and cracked her knuckles.

"Hello, darling," she said to the door. "Remember me?"

The door did not answer. Doors rarely did. But the flame beyond it stirred—she felt it the way she'd felt it every day for eleven years, since they'd posted her to tend the last dragon-fire and she'd discovered that tending was just another word for loving something doomed. A guttering heart of amber light in a cage of iron, older than the city, older than the crown, the last living ember of the last true dragon, and the source of every binding that had ever held anything to anything.

Including, if Brannic's ghost had it right, the binding that held the crown-thing's shape together.

"You ready in there?" she asked softly.

The flame flickered. Once for yes. It had learned her language over eleven years of nightly conversation, most of which had consisted of Maren complaining about Wardens and the flame flickering in what she chose to interpret as agreement.

"Good." She pressed both palms flat to the steel. "Because I'm about to do something stupid, and I'd like you to witness it."

Fire-singing wasn't throwing fire. Anyone could throw fire; half the undercity could throw fire; it was the magical equivalent of shouting. Fire-*singing* was asking. It was standing at the edge of a flame and offering it a shape and hoping, desperately, that it liked the shape enough to wear it. Maren had sung hearth-fires small and forge-fires patient and funeral pyres gentle. She had never once tried to sing a dragon.

She drew a breath that went all the way down to the girl she'd been before the crown took her village's records and her family's names and eleven years of her life, and she sang.

The song had no words anyone would recognize. It was the shape of a key, offered. It was *open, open, you great stubborn beautiful thing, we have somewhere to be.* The steel groaned. The wards—Warden-made, crown-taught—flared white and tried to bite her, and she sang louder, and somewhere above them the whole palace shuddered as Zephyrine and Corin did whatever mad prophetic thing they were doing, and the vault door—

The vault door remembered it was iron, and iron melts.

It ran down its own hinges like candlewax, and the dragon-fire rose out of its cage for the first time in thirty years, and it was *hungry*, and it was *glad*, and Maren laughed out loud in the flooding dark because it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen and she was absolutely certain it was going to kill her.

"All right," she said, spreading her arms. "Up we go. Mind the stairs."

* * *

### Zephyrine

The throne room doors opened for them, which was the worst thing that had happened all night.

Corin had expected to fight through them. He'd planned three contingencies involving Piet, two involving brute force, and one involving Zephyrine charming the hinges loose with a mourning-bone latchkey. Instead the great doors swung inward at their approach, smooth and welcoming, and the golden light inside spilled over them like honey over flies.

"It's inviting us," Zephyrine whispered.

"Traps invite," Corin said. But his hand was shaking, and she took it, and they walked in together because the alternative was walking in separately.

The throne room was full of bells.

Not the city's bells—those lay silent below, strangled mid-peal. These hung from the vaulted ceiling in their hundreds, bronze and silver and black iron, and none of them moved, and all of them were listening. Zephyrine felt their attention the way she felt the dead: a pressure, a leaning-in. The whole ceiling was an ear.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

He looked exactly as he had on every coin and proclamation for thirty years: broad, gray-bearded, kindly, tired in the way of a good shepherd. That was the horror of him. Up close, at last, Zephyrine could see the seams—the way his eyes stayed fixed a half-second too long between blinks, the way his chest rose without quite reaching his shoulders, the way the crown on his head seemed less worn than *grown*, gold meeting skin without any boundary a knife could find.

"Zephyrine Iversen," the king said, and his voice was warm, and it was wrong, it was a hundred voices braided into one, every silenced bell and every erased name speaking in chorus through a borrowed mouth. "Bone-carver. Hearer. *Vessel.*"

"I prefer charmseller," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. Beside her, Corin had gone pale as paper. "And I'm afraid I don't take commissions after midnight."

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Amusement, maybe. Hunger, certainly. The crown drank the torchlight and gave back nothing.

"You brought the fire," it said. "I can smell it climbing my stairs. You brought the dead. I can hear them *scratching*. You brought the boy I grew in a century ago like a flower in a forced bed." Its gaze slid to Corin, and Corin made a sound low in his throat. "And still you think you came here by your own choosing. Dear child. Every road you ever walked, I paved."

Behind the throne room's far doors, muffled by stone, Maren's fire roared closer. The hanging bells trembled on their hooks. Somewhere in the walls, the dead were scratching, exactly as promised, and Zephyrine pressed her hand flat against the relic at her throat—Brannic's knucklebone, worn smooth—and listened past the crown's chorus, down, down, beneath the stolen voice, to the very bottom of the well.

And there—faint, crushed, buried under thirty years of another will's weight—she heard a second heartbeat in the king's chest.

A man's heartbeat. Alone. Still counting the years.

*Aldric*, she thought, and the buried heartbeat stuttered, and the crown-thing's smile widened by one precise degree, and every bell in the ceiling leaned closer, and the far doors burst open in a bloom of dragon-light.

"There she is," said Maren, silhouetted in fire.
