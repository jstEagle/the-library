## 19. Audience with the Wearer

They took her cloak at the door.

That was the first thing Zephyrine noticed about the palace of Vaelhollow: it stole from you in small, polite increments. The cloak first, then her satchel—"no tools, mistress carver, house rules"—then, when she hesitated over the little bone whistle at her throat, a guard's gloved hand closed around it with the gentle firmness of someone lifting a teacup from a child.

"It's just a charm," she said. "For headaches."

"Of course it is." The guard smiled the way people smile at dogs who have clearly been eating something they shouldn't. "You'll get everything back on your way out."

*If* you come out, he did not say, and she appreciated that. There was a courtesy in not saying it.

The corridor to the throne room was long and white and utterly silent, which should have been a comfort and was instead the loneliest thing Zephyrine had ever walked through. She had spent seven years learning to live with noise—the dead murmuring through every carved thing in the city, complaining about their wallpaper, their grandchildren, the indignity of being remembered wrong. Silence, for her, had always meant one thing: *nothing here is listening.*

But this silence was different. This silence was manufactured. The walls were bone-white marble, polished to a shine, and she understood with a lurch why the palace made her skin crawl: there was nothing dead in it. Nothing old. The Wardens hadn't scrubbed the palace of magic—they'd scrubbed it of *memory*. Every stone had been cut so new that no ghost could get a foothold. It was a house built specifically so that no one who had ever lived in it could stay.

Someone had been very afraid of being heard.

*Steady,* she told herself, in Maren's voice, because Maren's voice was better at steadiness than her own. *You've talked your way past customs officers and one extremely suspicious widow. You can talk your way past a king.*

Except he wasn't a king. That was rather the point.

The doors opened without anyone touching them—of course they did—and Zephyrine walked into the throne room alone, as instructed, as summoned, as prophesied, and tried very hard to look like a humble artisan overwhelmed by grandeur rather than an assassin of metaphysics sizing up the load-bearing walls.

The throne room was smaller than she'd expected. That was the second surprise. She'd imagined vastness, echoing halls built to make petitioners feel like insects. Instead it was almost intimate—a fire lit, two chairs near it, a table with wine. A room arranged like a parlor. Like someone wanted visitors comfortable.

Like someone wanted them *willing.*

And on the throne—or rather, not on it, standing beside it with his hand resting on its arm the way a man rests his hand on an old horse's neck—stood King Aldric.

He looked exactly like the coins. Silver-haired, broad-shouldered, weathered into dignity, with the kind of face that made you trust him before he'd said a word. Thirty years of portraits and statuary had worn him smooth into an idea of a king, and the man wearing that face fit it perfectly.

Which was how she knew. Nothing real fit that well.

"Zephyrine Iversen," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like a key turning. "Bone-carver. Charmseller. The woman who catalogued a forbidden ledger and then had the poor sense to remember it." He gestured to the chair by the fire. "Sit. Please. I've been looking forward to this conversation for longer than you've been alive, and I find I dislike conducting it across a room."

She sat. Her knees did it mostly without her permission.

"You know what I am," he said. Not a question.

"I know what you're *not*," Zephyrine said, and then, because terror has always made her mouth run: "Though I'll admit the beard is very convincing. Is it part of you or do you, ah. Grow it? Attach it? I only ask professionally. Bone-work adjacent."

A pause. Then the king-thing laughed—a real laugh, startled out of it, brief and human-shaped—and something in Zephyrine's chest went cold, because she recognized that laugh. She'd heard it through relics a hundred times. The dead laughed like that when they remembered being alive.

"I forget," it said, "how much I enjoy the living. They're so *nervous.*" It poured wine itself, refusing the servant who hovered at the far wall, and handed her a cup. "Drink or don't. It's not drugged. Poison would be redundant; if I wanted you dead, dear girl, you'd never have gotten past the cloakroom."

"That's oddly reassuring."

"Honesty usually is, once you stop flinching from it." It settled into the opposite chair, and for a moment the firelight moved across its face and Zephyrine saw—just saw, at the edge of things, the way you see a shape in smoke—that the face was a mask worn well. Underneath it something vast shifted its weight and pretended to be a tired old king.

"So," she said. "You know why I'm here."

"I know why you *think* you're here. You've come to destroy me. Or free me. The prophecy was always admirably vague on that point—it's why I've kept it suppressed rather than burned. Ambiguity is useful. Certainty gets people killed." It swirled its wine. "I invited you because I wanted to meet the vessel before the choosing. And because I hoped—I genuinely hoped—you might be sensible."

"Sensible," Zephyrine repeated.

"To accept."

And there it was. The offer, arriving early, dressed in comfort. She thought of chapter twelve of her life, the bargain she'd already been made once in dreams: her mentor restored, her hearing honored, a kingdom that knew her name. She set down the wine untasted.

"No."

"Not yet," it agreed pleasantly. "I haven't told you what acceptance means. Refusing blind isn't virtue, Miss Iversen. It's just ignorance with good posture."

"I've been accused of worse."

It studied her then, really studied, and she felt it—the tasting she'd felt across the throne room at court weeks ago, a tongue against the back of her mind, patient and curious and *hungry.* It was reading her gift the way she read bone. And she braced herself for revulsion, for the predator's satisfaction—

Instead, it sighed. And the sigh was so tired, so old, so genuinely grieved, that every hair on her arms stood up.

"You hear the dead," it said softly. "Do you know what that makes you, in this city? The last honest ear. Everyone else listens to me. Thirty years of bells and festivals and oaths of devotion, all of it funneling into this—" it touched its temple, where beneath the silver hair, if you knew to look, sat the crown. Thin. Dark. Almost shy. "Every prayer, every anthem, every mother telling her child the king keeps us safe. Do you know what devotion tastes like, from the inside?"

"No," Zephyrine said carefully. "What?"

"Warm," it said. "That's the horror of it. It doesn't taste like theft. It tastes like supper."

The fire crackled. Somewhere far off, a bell rang the hour, and Zephyrine watched the king-thing flinch—actually flinch—at the sound, a small involuntary motion, like a dog that's been kicked by a familiar boot.

"You hate the bells," she said.

"I ring them," it corrected. "There's a difference. There's a difference between most things, Miss Iversen, and that's the entire tragedy of my existence." It leaned forward. "You came here believing you'd found the monster. I'm going to tell you something worse. I'm going to tell you where the monster keeps its prisoner."

It reached up—and Zephyrine's whole body tensed, certain it was about to remove its face like a mask, reveal the writhing ancient thing beneath—but it only touched the crown. Two fingers. Lightly. The way you touch a scar.

"Aldric is still here," it said.

The words dropped through her like a stone through still water.

"He has been here for thirty years. Awake. Aware. Watching his hands sign laws he despises, watching his people love the thing that wears him, unable to scream because I borrowed his voice along with everything else." The king-thing's eyes—Aldric's eyes—were wet. Whether the tears belonged to the monster or the man, she couldn't tell, and suspected neither could it. "You hear ghosts. So hear this one. He's been trying to reach a listener for three decades. You're the first who's ever walked in the front door."

And Zephyrine—who had spent seven years hearing the dead complain about wallpaper and grandchildren and the price of candles—did the thing she was made for. She stopped listening to the thing that spoke and listened *beneath* it.

It wasn't hard. That was the terrible part. The crown-thing wore Aldric like a coat, but a coat doesn't fill itself, and underneath the silver voice, under the measured cadence and the weary wit, there was another rhythm. Fainter. Frayed. A heartbeat of a ghost pressed flat under thirty years of stolen worship.

She closed her eyes and let her gift go deep, deeper than she'd ever dared in open air, and found him.

*Aldric.*

Not the coin-face. The man. A young king in her mind's ear—younger than Corin, God help her—screaming without sound, screaming in the wordless way the newest dead screamed, except he wasn't dead, he was *buried*, and his scream had worn itself down over thirty years into something smaller. Into a name. Over and over, worn smooth as a pebble:

*Elira. Elira. Elira.*

The old queen. Whose relic had started all of this. Whose ghost had told Zephyrine the truth in a cellar while the city slept.

"He calls for her," Zephyrine whispered. Her eyes were stinging. "Thirty years, and all he can say is her name."

The crown-thing didn't deny it. It looked into the fire, and its borrowed face did something complicated—grief and hunger tangled together like roots.

"She bound me once, you know. With dragon-fire and her own living will. Aldric helped her. Brave couple. Foolish, loving, brave." Its voice went distant. "Binding hurts, Miss Iversen. Did your fire-singer mention that? To bind a thing like me is to hold it against your body while it starves. Elira held me for nine days. It killed her. And her husband, her sweet devoted husband, took up the binding to finish it—and I was starving, and he was *right there*, full of love and duty and grief, and devotion is food, and I—" It stopped. Started again, quieter. "I didn't mean to wear him. That's the confession no one wants from a monster. I didn't mean any of it. Hunger doesn't mean. It just eats."

"And now?" Zephyrine asked. Her voice shook. "Thirty years later. Do you mean it now?"

"Now I keep him alive," it said simply. "Because if he dies, the binding breaks, and I go back to the dark between things, and I have been in the dark between things, and I would do anything—*anything*—to avoid it again. So yes. Now I mean it. I keep my prisoner breathing because his breath is my wall, and I have convinced an entire city to love me so that no one ever looks too closely at the wall." It smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, and it was awful. "You wanted a monster. Here is a hungrier, sadder truth: I am a leash that learned to love its dog."

Zephyrine sat with that. Around them the fire settled, and the manufactured silence of the memory-scrubbed palace pressed in, and she realized her hands were shaking—not with fear, or not only fear, but with the specific grief of her trade. She had held ten thousand relics. She had never once held one this heavy.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Why summon the one person prophesied to end you and *confess?*"

"Because of the terms," the crown-thing said. "You've decoded half the ledger. You know the vessel clause. But I don't think you've found the final page's fine print, because if you had, you wouldn't be sitting here drinking my wine. You'd already be running." It set down its cup. "So let me save us both some time. Yes, the prophecy names a vessel. Yes, I intend you. But hear the rest, the part Elira wrote in code because she wanted a loophole, bless her stubborn heart:

"*The crown breaks only in the hands of one who might have worn it, and would not.*"

Zephyrine went very still.

"Anyone can swing a hammer at me," the king-thing said. "Wardens have tried. Fanatics have tried. The blow passes through, because the crown is not metal, it is *consent*—thirty years of it, compacted. To shatter consent you must be the one consent was offered to. You must stand where I stood. Be offered what I was offered. And refuse." Its eyes fixed on hers, and for the first time all evening, the thing behind the face looked *afraid.* "You are the first person in a century strong enough to hear him, kind enough to pity him, and tempted enough to matter. Which means you are the only person alive who can kill me. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Miss Iversen?"

"That I'm your executioner."

"I'm telling you that you're my *only* exit. And that every instinct I have is screaming to destroy you, and I am asking you—begging you, in the voice of the man I've imprisoned—to walk out of this room, gather your fire-singer and your hundred-year boy, and come back tomorrow and do it anyway." The king-thing bowed its head, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, the buried ghost said its wife's name one more time. "*Please.*"

Zephyrine stood. Her legs held, barely. She thought of Maren's fire, of Corin's face when she'd forgiven him—not fully, but truly—of the undercity full of people the crown had erased, singing quietly in the dark.

"One condition," she said.

The king-thing raised its head.

"When I come back," Zephyrine said, "when I shatter it—you don't fight. You hold him still, you let him go, and you go back to the dark *without taking anyone with you.* No vessel. No bargain. No last grab. Swear it on the only thing you have left."

It looked at her for a long moment. And then King Aldric—the thing wearing King Aldric—laughed again, that startled human laugh, and said:

"You drive a harder bargain than Elira did. Agreed."

She turned for the doors, and her heart was hammering, because it had worked, it had actually worked, she had an oath from the monster itself—

Behind her, soft as a knife going into silk, the crown-thing added:

"Oh—one more thing, Miss Iversen. Your informant. The friend whose voice you carry in your pocket." A pause, delicately cruel. "You should know he didn't betray you willingly. None of mine ever do. I'll expect you tomorrow. Do give my regards to the dragon."

Zephyrine walked out of the throne room with her spine straight and her world tilting, and the doors closed behind her on a sound she would carry into the next day and the rest of her life: a buried king, whispering thanks.
