## 21. Aftermath of Silence

The silence came in like a tide going out.

One moment Zephyrine stood in the shattered audience chamber with dragon-fire cooling on her palms and the crown in pieces at her feet, and the whole city was singing—the dead of Vaelhollow, ten thousand voices layered like sediment, all of them crying out at once as the thing that had eaten their names let go. And then the next moment there was nothing.

Not quiet. Quiet was a room with a door you could close. This was the absence of the door itself.

She did not remember sitting down. She was on the floor among the shards, and Corin was kneeling in front of her, and his mouth was moving, and no sound arrived. She watched him say her name—she knew the shape of it, three syllables she'd heard from him a hundred times by now—and nothing came.

*Oh,* she thought, with a strange floating calm. *So that's what I paid.*

She raised one hand. It was still trembling. She pressed it flat against the floor, against the stone, and felt the vibration of something enormous happening somewhere below—the city shifting its weight, maybe, or just her own heart hammering—and for the first time in her life, the stone said nothing back.

She had spent seven years pretending to be only a bone-carver. Now the pretense was true, and it fit like a shoe two sizes too small.

Corin caught her hand. He was saying something urgent and kind, his face doing the work his voice couldn't reach her through, and she read him the way she'd once read relics: the furrowed brow, the wet eyes, the question. *Are you hurt?*

She shook her head. Then, because lying to the living was harder than lying to anyone had ever been, she nodded.

He gathered her up off the floor, and she let him, and together they walked out of the palace into a dawn that had no king in it.

* * *

Aldric had gone quietly. That was the thing she kept returning to, in the days after, when she could think about it at all.

In the last moment before the crown broke, she had heard him—not the thing that wore him, but him, the man, buried thirty years deep under stolen devotion like a coin at the bottom of a well. He had been so tired. He had been so *grateful*. His voice had been thin as winter light, and he had said:

*I tried to refuse it. Thirty years ago, I tried. It doesn't let you refuse—it lets you be refused *for*, if you understand me. Someone has to do it from outside.* A pause, and then, almost gently, almost amused: *You understand me. That's why it chose you. That's why it always chooses the ones who listen.*

And then the fire, and the breaking, and the silence.

She hadn't heard him say thank you. She would spend a long time wishing she had. But Maren swore he'd said it—that in the last instant before the blast, every soul in the palace had heard a king's voice, clear as a bell over water, say *thank you* to a bone-carver from the Tanners' Row—and Zephyrine chose, deliberately, deliberately, to believe her.

Believing things without hearing them firsthand was going to be the work of the rest of her life. She might as well start practicing.

* * *

Vaelhollow woke badly.

That was the truth nobody wanted to say out loud in those first weeks: the city woke like a patient coming out of a long fever, sweating and confused and furious about the dreams. For thirty years, every bell in every tower had rung on the crown's schedule. Every guild oath, every marriage rite, every market-day blessing had flowed through the palace and come back wearing the crown's approval like a collar. Take that away overnight, and what remained was a city full of people who had to decide things *themselves*, many of them for the first time in their adult lives, and most of them discovering they were terrible at it.

The Guildhall argued for eleven straight days about who should ring the dawn bells now. Eleven days. Zephyrine followed the reports the way other people followed horse races, with grim delight. The Bakers' faction wanted the bells rung by seniority. The Dockers wanted them rung by lottery. One elderly chandler proposed, with complete sincerity, that the bells simply not be rung at all until a proper king could be found, and was shouted down so thoroughly that he reportedly went home and rang his own dinner bell out of spite.

"They're going to make themselves sick," Corin said, reading the broadsheet aloud at her kitchen table because he'd learned, without being asked, that she liked watching words happen even when she couldn't catch them. "There's a motion to appoint a Council of Nine. There's a counter-motion to appoint a Council of Twelve, because nine is 'a suspiciously royal number.' There's a letter here from someone called the Vaelhollow Association of Concerned Grandmothers proposing that *they* run everything, and honestly—"

He looked up. "Honestly, they might."

Zephyrine signed, with hands still clumsy at it: *Let them. Grandmothers have governed worse.*

Corin laughed, and she felt it before she saw it—a warmth in the room, a shift in the air against her skin. She was learning the world through her skin now, through her eyes, through the way bread smelled when it was done and the way floorboards announced a visitor three steps early. The world was louder, oddly, without sound in it. She noticed everything. She noticed that Corin hummed when he thought she wasn't looking—an old tune, a hundred years old, from before—and she never told him she could feel the table humming under his elbows when he did it.

Some gifts you keep. Even small ones. Even borrowed ones.

* * *

Maren came on the ninth day, smelling of smoke and river water, and let herself in the way she always did, which was to say the lock gave up before she touched it.

"You look terrible," she announced, and then, seeing Zephyrine's face, corrected course with the grace of a barge turning. "You look like a woman who broke a thousand-year-old curse and got a bargain, which is to say terrible, which is to say correct."

*I can't hear them,* Zephyrine signed. It was easier with Maren than with anyone; Maren had learned the signs fast and ugly and didn't flinch at how blunt they made everything. *I keep waiting. Like an ear pressed to a door that isn't there anymore.*

"I know." Maren sat down across from her, uninvited, which was its own kind of tenderness. "I burned my village's records, Zeph. Every name, every birth, every grave-marker entry. Do you know what I've done every year since? I go back. I stand in the ash-field. I wait for them to be angry at me." She turned her cup around and around. "They never are. Because they're not there. Because I made sure of it. And some mornings that's mercy and some mornings it's the worst thing I ever did, and both of those are true, and I've stopped trying to pick one."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the shelf where she kept her carving tools, and took down the small oak box she had not opened since the palace.

Inside were three relics. Her mentor's knucklebone, worn smooth. The widow's husband's ring, left with her the morning after the charm, with a note she'd never been able to read twice. And a sliver of palace stone, taken from under the throne, where Aldric's ghost had waited thirty years to be found.

She set them on the table between them.

"Zeph," Maren said carefully, "you don't have to—"

*I know,* she signed. *That's why I'm doing it.*

She picked up the knucklebone. Held it. Waited for the familiar brush of voice, the texture of a dead man's attention.

Nothing. Warm bone. Cool room. A cart passing outside, felt in the floorboards rather than heard.

She held it until her arm ached. Then she set it down, and laughed—one short, cracked, astonished sound—and Maren reached across the table and gripped her wrist, hard, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to hear and everything to say, and for once the silence between them was just silence, ordinary and human and shared.

Later, much later, Maren asked her what she'd do with them. The relics.

Zephyrine turned the little box in her hands. *Bury them properly,* she signed. *With names. In ground that belongs to them. Not as voices. As people.*

"That's the most Warden-respecting thing I've ever heard you say," Maren said, grinning.

*Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation.*

* * *

The grief came in waves, the way Maren had promised it would, the way grief does when it's braided with relief and doesn't know which strand to pull first.

Some days it was small: reaching for a charm to check whether the tea had gone bitter, out of pure habit, and finding her own fingers instead of a voice. Some days it was enormous: standing in the market on Tanners' Row, surrounded by four hundred living people haggling and laughing and shoving, and realizing that for seven years she had stood in this exact spot and heard *two hundred more*—the dead fishwife still arguing prices, the drowned dockhand still complaining about the cold—and she had never once, not once, let any of them know she could hear them. She had been a secret walking through a crowd of secrets, and now the crowd was half the size and entirely honest, and she missed the dishonest ones so much her ribs hurt.

Corin found her like that once, standing in the market with her eyes closed, and he didn't ask. He just stood next to her, shoulder to shoulder, and let the crowd flow around them, and after a while he said, "Tell me about them sometime. The ones you used to hear. Not as ghosts. As people. I never got to meet them."

So she did. On the walk home, in fragments, in signs and written words and once by stopping in the middle of Tanner's Bridge to act out, with full theatrical commitment, the manner in which old Widow Casselin's late husband had complained about wallpaper. Corin laughed until he had to hold the railing. And Zephyrine discovered something she would keep discovering, over and over, like a coin found in an old coat: that telling the living about the dead was its own kind of listening. That memory passed hand to hand was a relic too. That she had not lost the dead. She had only lost the shortcut.

* * *

On the twenty-third morning after, Vaelhollow rang its own bells.

No decree. No council vote—the Council of Nine (reduced to Seven after two resignations over the suspicious-number affair) had tabled the question indefinitely. But at dawn, the bell-ringer of the Old Cathedral, a woman named Petra Oduya who had rung the crown's schedule for thirty years without once choosing a note herself, climbed the tower and rang whatever she pleased.

What she pleased, it turned out, was a pattern no one recognized. Not a royal peal. Not a funeral toll. Something looser, stranger, with gaps in it—deliberate gaps, spaces where the bells *didn't* speak, and the whole city, standing in doorways and leaning out windows, fell into those gaps and held its breath.

Zephyrine felt it through the floor. Through the walls. Through the soles of her feet and the bones of her hands, which was fitting, because bones had always been her instrument and the city was mostly bones underneath, whatever else it was.

Corin appeared in the doorway with his hair uncombed and the broadsheet still in his hand. "Petra Oduya," he said. "It says here she told the Guildhall envoy, quote, 'I rang for a king for thirty years. Today I'm ringing for whoever's still asleep.'" He paused. "There's a crowd forming at the cathedral. A big one. And—" he squinted at the page, and his voice did something complicated— "and the Concerned Grandmothers have issued a statement supporting her. Unanimously. They say the bells belong to the city now, and so does everything else, and they'd like a word with the Council about the docks levy."

Zephyrine set down her tea. She looked at the window, where the light was coming up gold and ordinary over a city with no king, no crown, and no plan, and she felt the vibration of the bells move through her chest like a second heartbeat.

Then she got up, and washed her hands, and took out her carving knives, because she had a commission—a real one, a plain one, a merchant's daughter who wanted a name-plate for a new shop, no magic in it anywhere, just good honest bone and a steady hand—and for the first time in longer than she could measure, the work ahead of her was exactly what it appeared to be.

Almost exactly.

Because when she opened her shutters to let the morning in, she found, wedged in the frame where no wind could have put it, a single curl of paper. Old paper. The color of parchment that had waited a long time in the dark.

She unfolded it with fingers that remembered how to be careful.

One line, in a hand she knew—she had catalogued it a century ago, or a man who looked like her had—and beneath it, a sketch, half-finished, of a shop sign bearing a name that was not yet hers but soon would be.

*Tamsin,* the line read. *The last page is never the last word.*

Zephyrine stood in her doorway a long while, deaf to the dead, alive to the morning, holding the future lightly in both hands.

Then she went inside to fetch her knives.
