## 22. Tamsin's Oath

The sign took me three days to carve and I loved every minute of it.

Not because it was difficult—though oak is a stubborn old thing, full of knots like clenched fists—but because for the first time in my life, I was carving something that meant exactly what it said. No charms. No charms at all. Just: **IVERSEN & COMPANY, BONE AND WOOD**, in letters deep enough to survive weather, gossip, and the occasional Warden inspection.

Well. Former Warden inspections. The Wardens' Hall had been converted into something called the People's Assembly, which mostly meant the same men sat in the same chairs but now had to let everyone watch them do it. Progress, Maren called it, spitting into the gutter. Slow progress, she meant.

I ran my thumb along the finished letters one last time. The grain was good. Honest work, my mentor Halvard used to say, is the only kind that doesn't lie to you. He'd been dead seven years before I learned he'd also been right about nearly everything else.

"You're doing the thing again," Corin said from the doorway.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you touch your work like you're checking it for a pulse."

I laughed. It still surprised me sometimes, how easily laughter came now, when for so long every sound I made had been half-drowned under the murmur of the dead—the widow's husband complaining about wallpaper, the drowned boy singing, the thousand small voices of Vaelhollow's departed pressing against the inside of my skull like rain on a window.

Now there was nothing. Silence, total and permanent, since the day the crown shattered and took my hearing with it.

People expected me to grieve it more than I did. And I did grieve—I grieved Halvard's voice, which I would never hear again even as a ghost; I grieved the queen's lullaby; I grieved the dragon's last long exhale of memory. But there is a difference between silence and quiet, and I had finally learned it. Silence is absence. Quiet is peace. My head, for the first time in thirty years, belonged entirely to me.

"Come inside," I said. "Help me hang it."

"I'm not allowed to help. Maren was very specific. She said if I so much as held a nail she'd sing my eyebrows off."

"She can't sing anymore either. The dragon-fire's gone."

"She doesn't know that. And I'm not going to be the one to tell her."

* * *

We hung the sign together anyway, because Corin had never once in his hundred-odd years done what he was told, and because Maren, when she arrived an hour later with a crate of her terrible honey wine, looked at our handiwork and pronounced it "crooked but load-bearing," which we both chose to take as a compliment.

The shop was smaller than my old one. That was deliberate. The old shop had burned on the night of the raid, along with seven years of charms and carvings and careful, cowardly anonymity, and when I rebuilt, I built a place with no cellar, no back room, no hiding places at all. One door. Big windows. Anyone could see everything I did, all the time.

It turned out I liked living where anyone could see me.

Inside, the shelves were already stocked—not with mourning charms or luck-knots or the little bone bells I'd once sold to frightened people who wanted to feel protected from things they didn't believe in. Now there were spoons and combs, buttons and chess pieces, a cradle rattle for the baker's wife who was due in spring, a set of surgical needles for the surgeon on Copper Lane who had heard I worked "clean." A girl of maybe ten stood at the counter with her mother, choosing between two carved horses, and I watched her hold them up to the light the way I used to hold relics, looking for something hidden inside them.

There was nothing hidden inside them. They were just horses. Good ones, though. I'd sanded the manes until they flowed.

"That one," the girl announced, pointing at the left horse with the certainty of a queen distributing titles.

Her mother paid. I wrapped the horse in cloth and, out of pure old habit, almost whispered something over it—a blessing, a ward, some small borrowed magic—and stopped myself. Instead I said, "He likes apples. Carved ones. Don't ask me how I know."

The girl nodded gravely, as though this were perfectly reasonable information, and left believing her horse had opinions. Which, honestly, was more magic than most of my charms had ever contained.

When they were gone, Maren set the wine down hard enough to make the counter jump. "Right. Before we open properly. Before the Assembly sends someone to cut ribbons and make speeches nobody wants. We should do the thing."

"The thing," Corin said.

"You know the thing. The oath. We've been putting it off for two months, and I'm tired of pretending we're going to feel more ready later. We're never going to feel ready. Readiness is a lie told by people who want you to wait."

She poured three cups. The honey wine was, as always, roughly forty percent wax and sixty percent regret, but we drank it because refusing Maren's wine was, in her words, an act of war.

* * *

We stood in the middle of the shop, between the shelves of honest craft, and I realized I had no idea what the oath should say. Prophecies have language for this sort of thing—solemn lines, formal cadences, the whole architecture of destiny. But the prophecy had turned out to be a trap dressed in beautiful words, its last line a noose:

*And Tamsin shall wear what Aldric wore.*

I had taken the name anyway. That was the joke of it, the defiance of it—they'd written *Tamsin* as a vessel's name, a placeholder for the next crown-bearer, and instead I'd claimed it as mine, scoured of their meaning, refilled with my own. You can't haunt a person with a word they've decided to love.

"I'll start," Maren said, because Maren always started, and if she waited for someone else she'd die of impatience somewhere around age ninety, which she insisted was still decades away despite evidence to the contrary.

She raised her cup. "I swear to burn anything that needs burning, and nothing that doesn't. I've burned enough that didn't." Her voice went rough on that last part—we all knew about the village records, about the family names she'd saved by turning her own history to ash. "I swear to guard this city from any crown that whispers, any throne that feeds, any power that offers belonging at the price of a leash. And I swear—" she paused, and glared at us preemptively, daring us to find it funny, "—I swear to keep showing up. Even when it's boring. Especially when it's boring. Guarding things is mostly boring. Somebody has to be willing to be bored for the rest of you."

Corin lifted his cup. He was quiet a moment, and I watched him gather himself the way he did—this man who had spent a century being a lure, a bait, a beautiful lie pulled forward through time by a prophecy that used him up and discarded him, and who had chosen, afterward, to stay. To be true. He still flinched sometimes at his own reflection, expecting to catch it performing.

"I swear to tell the truth," he said, "even when a prettier story is available. I was a pretty story for a hundred years. I know what they cost." He looked at me then, and his eyes did the thing that still undid me slightly, the thing where a hundred years of guilt met one stubborn decision to hope. "I swear to stand between any whispering crown and whoever it's whispering to. Not because I'm strong. Because I know exactly how they talk. I was on their side of the conversation once, and I remember every word."

Then they both looked at me, and I understood that the oath wasn't finished, because mine was the one that mattered, and they were kind enough to wait for it without saying so.

I thought about what to swear. Not to guard, exactly—that was theirs, fire and vigilance, and I honored it. But my gift was gone. My dead were silent. What did a woman with two hands, a shop, and a head full of blessed quiet have to offer an oath?

Then I knew.

"I can't hear the dead anymore," I said. "So I'll listen to the living. All of them. Even the ones who are wrong, especially the ones who are wrong, because that's where crowns get started—in the space where nobody listens to somebody until somebody else promises to." I raised my cup. "I swear to carve honest things and sell them at honest prices. I swear to teach any child who asks, and half the adults, because adults need it more. I swear that no one in this city will ever again be a secret the way I was a secret—alone, afraid, useful only in hiding. And I swear—" my voice caught, and I let it, "—that if another crown ever whispers, it will find three people in a small shop who already know how the story ends, and will refuse to read it aloud."

We drank. The wine was awful. It was perfect.

Maren wiped her eyes and blamed the wax content. Corin kissed my temple and said nothing, which from him was the loudest speech he knew.

* * *

We opened the doors officially the next morning, and Vaelhollow came.

That's the thing nobody tells you about the fall of a tyrant: the city doesn't transform overnight. Kingless dawn came and went, and people woke and argued about drainage rights and bread prices, and the Assembly bickered, and half the bells in the palace towers stayed cracked because nobody could agree who should recast them. Life continued, unglamorous and self-governed, which was precisely the point.

But they came to the shop. The widow whose husband had complained about wallpaper—she'd found me after the crown fell, and wept, and told me she'd known, somehow, that someone had listened to him all those years, and that it had made her feel less alone in her grief without her ever knowing why. She bought a plain wooden box for his letters. Nothing enchanted. Just a box, well-made, that would hold what needed holding.

The girl with the horse came back and reported that it did, in fact, prefer apples. I thanked her for the intelligence update.

An old Warden came, gray-faced and trembling, and asked—not demanded, *asked*—if I would carve a memorial marker for his brother, whom the crown's purges had erased from every record. I did it free. His brother's name went into the bone in letters deep enough to survive anything, and when I finished, the old man touched it the way I used to touch my work, checking for a pulse.

"It's just a name," he said apologetically.

"It's never just a name," I said.

Some evenings Corin sat by the window whittling badly—he was improving, slowly, with the patience of a man making up for a century of being decorative—and told me stories about the Vaelhollow of a hundred years ago, before the crown-thing settled in, when the city had smelled of tallow and river mud and ambition. Maren came by most nights with wine that got marginally less terrible each batch, or possibly we were simply getting more forgiving. The dragon's vault was empty now, its last flame spent shattering the crown, and Maren tended a small ordinary hearth-fire in the undercity instead, teaching children who'd never seen a dragon that fire could be a friend if you asked politely.

Some nights I missed the dead so much it felt like a missing limb, phantom voices itching in the silence. Some nights I dreamed of the crown, whole and gleaming, offering me everything, and woke with Corin's heartbeat under my ear reminding me what I'd chosen instead.

But most nights—most nights were like this one.

* * *

This one: lamplight low, shop closed, the three of us around the little stove I'd installed because Maren refused to heat a room with magic she no longer trusted herself to use. Corin was asleep in the chair, a half-carved spoon in his lap, looking younger than his years and older than his face, both at once. Maren was telling me about a rumor from the eastern provinces—some noble house there claiming an old regalia had begun, quote, "singing to its keeper"—and I was listening with the particular attention of someone who has sworn an oath about exactly this.

"Singing," I said. "Crowns don't sing."

"This one allegedly hums. Like it's thinking."

"Crowns don't think."

"You thought that about kings, once."

I turned my cup in my hands. Outside, Vaelhollow murmured its ordinary evening sounds—carts, arguments, a dog with strong opinions, the bakery banking its ovens. A city governing itself, badly and beautifully. A city that belonged to no one and therefore to everyone.

"If it's true," I said slowly, "then we go east. That's the oath. That's the whole point of the oath—it's not a monument, it's a promise with legs."

Maren grinned, fierce and tired and ready. "I'll pack the wine."

"Don't. We'll buy wine. We'll be respectable travelers."

"We have never once been respectable travelers."

Corin stirred, opened one eye, and said, "Are we leaving Tuesday? I hate leaving on Mondays. Bad associations."

And I laughed—loud, unhidden, mine—and reached for a fresh piece of oak, because whatever came east out of those rumors, it would find us ready, and bored, and unwilling, and together.

The prophecy had named me a vessel. I signed my work *Tamsin*, and it meant: *author*.
