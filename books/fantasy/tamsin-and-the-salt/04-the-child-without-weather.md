## 4. The Child Without Weather

Noor didn't sleep.

She sat at her worktable with the lamp turned low and the prophecy book closed beneath her palm, as if it might climb off the desk and walk away. Outside, Saltmere's bells rang the third hour, then the fourth, and she counted them the way she used to count stitches in a coastline — one mark for every certainty.

*Noor Hartwell.* Her own handwriting on a page a century old. She had turned the book over four times looking for a forgery's tells: bleed-through, modern ink, the faint chemical shine of a trick. Nothing. The ink had gone brown the way old ink does, sunk into paper like a bruise into skin.

And still, when dawn finally grayed the window, her first thought was not of prophecy or theft or the court beneath the mountain.

It was of Wren.

The girl lived two doors down, above the chandler's shop, in rooms that always smelled of tallow and cinnamon. Noor had known her since she was a baby — had sold the mother, Odile, a crib-charm when Wren was born, a cheap tin thing that did nothing but look pretty, because that was all Noor ever sold. That was the law. That was the point.

Wren was seven now.

Wren cast no shadow.

Noor had told herself it was nothing. Children play games; children stand oddly in light. But she had been mapping impossible things for twenty years, and there is a particular cold that settles in a cartographer's stomach when a detail refuses to fit — when the coastline runs off the edge of the page and keeps going.

She dressed without deciding to dress. She took her coat, her satchel, and a jar of honey-candies she kept for neighborhood children, and she went out into a morning so bright and hard-frozen that the air itself seemed to have edges.

Saltmere glittered. It always glittered in winter — salt crusted on every sill and lintel, thrown down by the wardens each dusk, the city's oldest ritual and its emptiest. *Salt against sorcery,* the law said. *Salt keeps us clean.* Noor stepped over the white lines at her threshold the way she had ten thousand times before, and for the first time in twenty years she felt what they really were: a fence. A border drawn around her whole life by people who feared what lay inside it.

Two doors down, she knocked.

Odile answered with flour on her hands and worry already on her face, as though she'd been standing behind the door waiting for someone to knock. "Noor. Thank the salt. Come in, come in — I've got bread rising, mind the—"

"Is Wren here?"

Something moved through Odile's face, quick as a fish under ice. "She's sleeping."

"It's past eight," Noor said gently. "She's never asleep past eight. She's usually on my counter stealing my pens."

Odile's hands twisted in her apron. Behind her, the stair up to the loft creaked once, though no one stood on it.

"Noor." The baker's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Can I ask you something? And can you promise me — *promise* me — you won't say it loud?"

"I'm listening."

"Wren doesn't make frost."

*

They went up to the loft together, quiet as burglars in their own building.

The girl was awake after all, sitting cross-legged on her bed with her dolls arranged in a circle, and when she saw Noor she lit up the way only seven-year-olds and shopkeepers' best customers can light up. "Noor! Did you bring the honey ones?"

"Every last one." Noor sat on the floor beside the doll-circle, joints cracking, and handed over the jar. Up close, in the flat gray light from the window, she made herself look. Really look. The way she'd look at a map that had lied to her.

Wren was beautiful. Dark curls, gap-toothed grin, a scrape healing on one knee from some forbidden rooftop adventure. An ordinary child in every respect except—

Except the window.

Frost ferned across every pane in Saltmere every morning of winter; it was the city's second heartbeat. On this window, the frost grew thick and feathered and lovely everywhere — everywhere except a rough circle in the center of the lower pane, roughly child-shaped, roughly the height of a seated girl.

As if the glass itself had decided not to touch her.

"Wren," Noor said, keeping her voice light as thread. "Come stand by the window a moment. I want to see how tall you've gotten."

"You saw me Tuesday."

"Humor an old woman."

"I'm *seven*, Noor. You're not old, you're just slow on stairs."

Noor laughed, and it cost her something, and she watched the girl come stand in the morning light. Watched the sun fall full on her small shoulders. Watched the floorboards take the light and give back nothing — no shape, no dark twin, no shadow at all where a shadow should have pooled like spilled ink.

The dolls in their circle had shadows. The bedpost had a shadow. The candle stub on the sill had three shadows, one for each window's light.

Wren had none.

The girl followed Noor's gaze down to the bare boards and her grin faded by degrees, the way a fire dies when no one feeds it. "Mama says it's because I'm special," she said. "That the sun likes me too much to put a wall between us."

"Mamas say wise things," Noor said.

"Do *you* think I'm special?"

Noor looked at this child who had been in her shop a hundred times, haggling over marbles with the ferocity of a dockside merchant, and felt the prophecy book's weight in her memory like a stone sewn into her coat. *The thieves will steal back the soul of Wren.* She had read the name and thought it coincidence. There were a dozen Wrens in Saltmere.

There were not a dozen Wrens who cast no shadow.

"I think," Noor said carefully, "that you are exactly yourself, and that is the rarest thing there is."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once, satisfied, and returned to her dolls. Noor rose. In the doorway Odile caught her arm, and the two women stood in the stairwell among the smell of bread, and Odile's composure — the composure of a woman who has been carrying a fear alone for months — finally cracked down the middle.

"They came in autumn," she whispered. "Men. Not witch-finders — worse, the kind that don't wear badges. They had papers. They said Wren was... spoken for. That a debt was settled before she was born and the payment had come due. I told them they were mad. I told them I'd go to the wardens. And the tall one smiled at me, Noor, he smiled like a man being patient with a dog, and he said—" Her breath hitched. "He said, *Madam, the mountain remembers longer than the wardens do.* Then they left. And three days later she stopped casting a shadow, and the frost stopped touching her, and she sleeps less than she used to, and sometimes — sometimes she stands at the window at night and talks in a language I don't know, in a voice that isn't quite hers, and in the morning she doesn't remember any of it."

"Have you told anyone? The wardens? A physician?"

"And say what?" Odile's laugh was terrible. "*Excuse me, my daughter has no shadow, please arrest whoever did magic*? They'd take her away. Or worse — they'd decide *I'd* done something. You know what happens to mothers in this city when magic comes near their children." Her grip tightened. "You're the only one I could think of. You deal in charms. Little things, harmless things, but you know more than you sell. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it."

Noor said nothing. There was nothing honest to say that wasn't dangerous.

"Please." Odile's voice broke entirely now, flour-dusted hands clasped like a prayer. "I don't care what it costs. I don't care if it's illegal, I don't care if it's wicked — she's *seven*, Noor. Whatever they took, whatever's missing in her — get it back. You hear me? Get my daughter back."

*

Noor walked home the long way, along the frozen canal, because she needed the wind in her face and the lie of open space.

*Get my daughter back.*

As if it were a hat left on a coach. As if souls were mittens.

But Noor had read the book. *The thieves will steal back the soul of Wren, sold to the court beneath the mountain.* She had thought herself the reader of that sentence. Now she understood she was its subject — named, dated, scheduled, like a train or an execution. Some author a century dead had written her into a crime she hadn't committed yet, in her own hand, and the first victim of the crime was asleep two doors down with a circle of dolls and no shadow to keep them company.

Guilt is a strange cartography. It lays itself over a city you thought you knew and turns every street into an accusation. The chandler's shop: she had bought candles there last week, haggled cheerfully, while upstairs a child spoke in a borrowed voice. The wardhouse with its salt-barrels: she had nodded to the wardens all her life while they guarded a fence built to protect no one but the thing beneath the mountain. Even her own shopfront, her own safe little life of tin charms and harmless trinkets — it wasn't innocence. It had never been innocence. It was a hiding place, and hiding places are rented from whoever benefits from your silence.

She stopped at the canal rail. Below, the water moved black and slow under its lid of ice, and her reflection looked back at her — whole, shadowed, weathered, *hers*. Everything Wren had lost, Noor still had, and had spent twenty years treating as ordinary.

Twenty years. She had woken in a hired room at the Salt Gate inn with no name she recognized and no memories older than that morning, and she had built a life anyway, brick by brick, charm by charm. She had never asked why. Asking why felt like pressing on a bruise that went all the way down.

Now she pressed.

If her memory had been taken — and the book, the date, the handwriting said *taken*, not *lost* — then someone had paid for its removal. Debts ran both ways. If Wren's soul had been sold to settle a bargain, whose bargain? Signed by whom? *Hartwell,* the ledger would say somewhere, if ledgers existed, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome and true:

*What if the debt is mine? What if I signed something once, before I knew my own name, and this child is paying my installments?*

The wind came down the canal and cut through her coat, and Noor Hartwell — trinket-seller, coward, keeper of twenty years of small safe lies — stood at the rail and let it cut, because she deserved the cold and Wren couldn't feel it at all.

When she raised her head, her decision had already been made somewhere below thought, the way a river decides its course long before it reaches the sea.

She would find the court beneath the mountain. She would learn the terms of the sale. And if the debt was hers, she would pay it — not with the child's soul, and not with her own if she could help it, but with the only currency she had ever truly owned.

Her maps. Every realm she had ever charted that shouldn't exist. Every door she had drawn and never shown a living soul.

But first—

First she needed Corwin Ash, the blind-date stranger who quoted prophecy like scripture and read salt-lines like a priest reads sins, and who owed her an explanation about how a man she'd met four nights ago appeared in her own hand-drawn map from years before she'd ever met him.

She turned toward the shop, and stopped.

Her door stood open.

She had locked it. She always locked it — habit older than memory, the one ritual she'd never questioned until now. Yet the door swung loose on its hinge, and inside, on her worktable, amid her instruments and her innocent tin charms, sat the prophecy book.

Open.

To the final page.

And beneath her own name, in fresh ink that glistened wet as canal water, a single new line had been added since dawn:

*The thief asks the price. The price asks back.*
