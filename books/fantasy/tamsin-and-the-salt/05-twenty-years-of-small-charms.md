## 5. Twenty Years of Small Charms

The morning after the date, Noor did what she always did when the world tilted: she opened the shop early and made something small.

A luck-knot, this time. Red thread, three turns, a word whispered into the center that wasn't quite a word — more of an intention with its edges sanded off. She tied it off, held it up to the gray light coming through the shop window, and watched it glow faintly, like a coal remembering fire. Harmless. That was the whole art of it. A charm that could do nothing worse than make a nervous bride feel steadier on her feet. Saltmere had laws about magic, but it had never legislated against comfort.

She set the knot in the basket by the register with the others and tried not to think about Corwin Ash's face.

Tried, and failed, because his face was the problem. She'd drawn it before. Not him — she'd never seen him before last night — but *that face*, older, harder, lit by some light she couldn't place, rendered in her own confident hand on a map she had no memory of making. She'd gone home after the date and pulled every sketchbook off her shelf, all eleven of them, and found nothing. The drawing existed only where she'd left it: folded inside the sealed book beneath the floorboards, waiting for her like a letter from a stranger who knew her handwriting better than she did.

So instead she made a luck-knot. It was either that or scream.

* * *

Twenty years ago, Noor Hartwell had walked into Saltmere through the south gate with frost in her hair and nothing in her pockets but a compass that pointed at things that weren't north.

She remembered that day perfectly. That was the strange part — the part she'd spent two decades refusing to look at straight-on. She remembered the gate guard's chapped hands. She remembered the smell of the tanneries, the cry of the fish-gulls, the exact weight of the coin she paid for a bowl of barley soup at a stall near the customs house. She remembered everything from that morning forward with the terrible fidelity of a mind that had been scoured clean and then told to start writing.

And before that morning: nothing.

Not fog. Fog would have been kinder, fog could be argued with, fog might lift. What lay behind that first day was simply absence, smooth as the back of a mirror. She had tested it over the years the way you test a missing tooth — pressing her tongue into the gap, again and again, hoping to be wrong. She had no mother's voice. No childhood street. No name before the one she'd given the gate guard, which she'd chosen, she was almost certain, off a shipping manifest nailed to the customs house door. *Hartwell.* It had sounded like someone who owned things. She'd liked that.

The compass she kept. Even then, half-starved and hollowed out, she had understood that the compass was hers in some way deeper than ownership. Its needle didn't point north. It pointed at edges — at the seams between the world and whatever pressed against the world. In Saltmere, surrounded by salt-flat and sky, it mostly trembled and gave up. But sometimes, on certain nights, it strained toward the mountain like a dog at the end of a chain.

She had learned, very quickly, not to let anyone see it strain.

* * *

Saltmere in those days was a city performing its own innocence.

Magic had been outlawed for a century — everyone agreed on the number, though no one agreed on why. The witch-finders' charter hung in the magistrate's hall, and their gray coats moved through the markets in pairs, unhurried, the way men move when they own the ground they're walking on. And yet every third stall sold charms. Dried lavender against bad dreams. Iron nails bent at dawn for protection. Little glass bottles of "storm-water" that were, if you were honest, just rain with attitude. The city had decided that magic was fine so long as it was dead — so long as it came pre-killed, dried, bottled, labeled *trinket*, drained of anything that might answer back.

Noor had looked at those stalls on her second day in the city and felt something turn over in her chest. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite.

Her first sale had been an accident. A woman at the soup stall was crying — quietly, the practiced way, the way you cry when you've done it enough times that it doesn't interrupt your work — and Noor, without deciding to, reached across the counter and tucked a sprig of rosemary behind the woman's ear and said, "For the house. Rosemary remembers."

It wasn't even a charm. It was a kindness with a folk saying attached. But the woman stopped crying. And three days later she came back with her sister, and the sister brought a friend, and within a month Noor had a table at the market and a reputation: the girl who made small comforts that worked, or seemed to, or worked often enough that nobody wanted to examine it too closely.

She learned the craft of harmlessness the way other people learned piety. Every charm she made, she made smaller than it wanted to be. There was a moment, each time — the thread in her fingers, the word half-formed — when the thing she was making *reached* toward something larger, the way a flame leans toward a draft, and every time she leaned the other way. She pinched the wick. She made the love-charm that only helped you say what you already meant. She made the road-charm that only reminded your feet of the way home, which any grandmother's trick could do. She made weather-senses that predicted rain no better than an aching knee.

Once — only once, in the early years — she had slipped. A child had fallen through the ice on the millpond, and Noor had been there, and without thinking she had put her hand flat on the frozen water and *asked* it, in the language that wasn't quite words, to give him back.

The ice had opened. The boy had come up gasping. And Noor had walked home shaking, not from cold but from the sound the pond made as it obeyed — a low, glad note, like a hinge that had been waiting twenty years to be oiled. She hadn't slept. At dawn she'd sat at her worktable and made herself a rule, and the rule had a shape she could hold: *small*. Everything small. Everything that could be denied. A life built from trinkets so harmless that even she could almost believe she was harmless too.

* * *

Maren had found her in the second winter.

"You're doing it wrong," the woman said, appearing at Noor's market table with the confidence of someone who had been invited. She was broad and quick-eyed, with flour permanently in the creases of her knuckles — a baker, it turned out, and a good one, and something else besides, though it took Noor another year to learn what.

"Doing what wrong?"

"The rosemary." Maren picked up a sprig from Noor's basket, sniffed it, set it down. "You tell them it remembers. You don't tell them what to feed it so it keeps." She leaned in. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Honey and ash, once a season. Then it remembers properly."

Noor stared at her. "That's—"

"That's baking," Maren said pleasantly, loudly, for the benefit of the passing gray coat at the end of the row. "Come by the shop tomorrow. I'll show you."

The bakery on Candle Lane had a back room with a low ceiling and a table scarred by decades of work, and in that room Maren taught her — carefully, obliquely, in the grammar of recipes — how to be what she was without dying of it. There were others. That was the revelation that undid Noor a little, that first year: she was not alone, and never had been. The midwife who could slow blood with a hummed note. The lamplighter whose flame never guttered in wind. Old Tammas down the lane who talked to locks, and locks, improbably, listened. They called themselves nothing. They met rarely and quietly. They had survived a hundred years of witch-finders by being exactly what Noor had made herself: small, deniable, folded into the ordinary life of the city like salt into bread.

"You came from outside," Maren said to her once, kneading dough, not looking up. "Where?"

"I don't know."

"Before that. Your people. Your name."

"I don't know." Noor turned her cup in her hands. "I walked through the south gate and there was nothing behind me. Not hidden. Just — gone. Like a page torn out, cleanly, at the spine."

Maren's hands went still in the dough. Only for a breath. Then they resumed, steady, and she said, "Memory's a fragile cargo. Long roads shake it loose," and changed the subject, and Noor — hungry for belonging, grateful past the point of sense — let her.

Later, much later, she would remember how still Maren's hands had gone. Later, she would count the seconds of that pause and understand them. But that was later.

* * *

The years stacked themselves up like coins.

She moved from the market table to a proper shop — a narrow place on Thimble Row with a bow window and a cellar that flooded every spring, which she rented cheap and loved completely. She painted the sign herself: *HARTWELL'S SMALL CHARMS*, and underneath, smaller, in letters that made Maren snort: *Nothing here is dangerous.* It became a joke between them, and then a slogan, and then, in a way none of them said aloud, a covenant.

She learned the city the way she'd once, apparently, learned other cities — by drawing it. Her maps began as inventory: which streets flooded, which landlords took bribes, which corners the witch-finders watched. But her hand kept going past usefulness. She drew Saltmere's rooftops and found herself shading the spaces *between* them, and the spaces between the spaces, until the margins of her maps filled with streets that weren't there — or weren't there yet, or were there somewhere else, under elsewhere. Her compass needle would strain, and she'd follow it to some cellar wall or bricked-up archway, and stand there feeling the seam of the world like a raised scar under wallpaper.

She never mapped those places onto paper she kept. She'd learned that much caution. She drew them, and then she burned them, and watched the ink curl and go up, and told herself the burning was discipline rather than fear.

Wren was born in her ninth year in the city — Sefa's girl, red-faced and furious in the way of babies who arrive during a storm and hold the weather against everyone. Noor made her a birth-charm, the smallest thing she knew: a white bead that would stay warm as long as the child was well. She hung it over Wren's cradle and thought, with a tenderness that startled her, *this is what the hole in me is for. This is what I'm filling it with.*

Because there was a hole. She had stopped pretending otherwise around her fifth year, sitting alone in the shop on a night of hard rain, holding a finished charm and realizing she couldn't remember making it — not the making itself, but the wanting of it, the reason. She made comforts for other people's memories: keepsake knots, remembrance beads, charms to hold a mother's voice. She knew the words for every kind of longing except her own. When customers asked — kindly, carelessly, the way people ask — *where are you from, originally?* — she had a smile ready and a lie behind it, and behind the lie, nothing. A room swept bare. A mirror's back.

Some nights she stood at her worktable with the compass in her palm and let the needle strain toward the mountain, and she thought: *whatever I was, it's still in there. Whatever I lost, the mountain has it.* And then she'd close her fist around the compass and put it in the drawer, because wanting things was how you got caught, and she had a shop full of people who needed her small.

* * *

Now, twenty years on, she stood in that same shop with a luck-knot cooling in her fingers and Corwin Ash's face folded in a book beneath her feet, and the hole in her had begun, for the first time, to ache in a specific direction.

She thought about the pond. About the ice opening at her word, glad to obey. About Maren's still hands. About the sealed book that had waited under her floorboards — how long? since when? — for her to find it. About a prophecy quoting itself through the mouth of a handsome stranger, naming her, dating her, scheduling her like an appointment she'd made and forgotten.

*Small,* she told herself, setting the knot in the basket. *Stay small.*

But her hand was already moving, sliding the floorboard aside, lifting out the book — and the book was warm. Warm as a living thing. Warm as Wren's white bead on a healthy morning, and when she opened it to the last page, her own name looked back up at her, and beneath the name, in fresh ink that had not been there yesterday, a single new line had been written:

*She remembers the pond. Now ask her what she traded to forget the rest.*

Down the street, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, not the market bell, but the thin iron tongue of the witch-finders' watch, and it did not stop.
