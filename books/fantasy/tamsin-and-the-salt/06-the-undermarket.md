## 6. The Undermarket

The tunnel mouth was behind a wall of salted herring barrels in the cellar of the Gilded Eel, which was neither gilded nor, as far as Noor could tell, an eel establishment. It smelled like brine and old coin and something sweeter underneath — burnt sugar, maybe, or the memory of it.

"You're sure about this," Corwin said. He held the lantern low so the light wouldn't spill up the stairs behind them.

"No," Noor said. "But I've been unsure about everything for twenty years and it hasn't killed me yet."

She pulled the third barrel aside the way the fishmonger's boy had shown her that afternoon, after she'd paid him more than his mother earned in a month and sworn him to a silence he'd already broken twice by supper. Behind it: a hatch, iron-ringed, set into stone that was older than Saltmere's walls. Older than Saltmere's name.

Corwin crouched beside her and pressed two fingers flat against the hatch, the way he'd pressed them against the salt-lines outside Wren's house three nights ago. His eyes went distant, unfocused, reading something written in a language of mineral and pressure.

"There's debt down there," he said quietly. "A lot of it. Layered. Like — you know how ice forms on a window, one frost-fern over another?"

"I know how maps layer," Noor said. "Go on."

"It's like that. Debts stacked on debts until nobody remembers what the original loan even bought." He looked up at her. In the lantern light his face had the particular stillness of a man standing at the edge of something. "Your name isn't on any of it. I checked. Whatever they sold, whoever signed — Hartwell doesn't appear in the first six layers."

"Six layers," Noor repeated. "And below that?"

"Below that it stops being readable. The salt goes black." He stood. "Which is either very good or very bad."

"Those are usually the same thing, in my experience."

She lifted the hatch.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the geography of Saltmere allowed. Noor counted them out of habit — cartographer's reflex, the same instinct that made her measure doorways with her eyes and estimate the height of ceilings — and stopped counting at two hundred because the number had stopped meaning anything. The air changed as they descended. It warmed, then cooled, then took on a texture, almost a taste: copper and candle smoke and rain on hot stone.

"Nobody knows who dug this," Corwin said, his voice dropping into the hush the way voices do underground. "The smugglers found it. Before them, the debt-brokers. Before them—" He shrugged. "Before them is the part everyone lies about."

"And you know this how? You quote prophecies at blind dates and read salt-lines, but who taught you the Undermarket?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. The lantern swung between them, throwing their shadows huge against the wet stone — hers long and thin, his oddly doubled, as if the rock behind him held a second Corwin slightly out of step with the first.

"My grandmother brokered here," he said finally. "Debts. Small ones. A widow's roof, a fisherman's luck, that kind of thing. She used to say the Undermarket was the only honest place left in Saltmere, because at least down here people admitted what things cost." A pause. "She also said never go below the Amber Stair. So naturally that's where we're going."

Noor almost smiled. Almost. Then the stairs ended, and the Undermarket opened beneath her, and every thought she had was taken clean out of her hands.

It was a cavern — no. That word was too small. It was a *city inverted*, a vaulted dark so high that lanterns hung from chains a hundred feet long and still seemed close to the ceiling, each one burning without flame, without oil, just a soft gold insistence that light should exist here whether anything fed it or not. Stalls climbed the walls in tiers. Bridges arched overhead, crowded with figures leaning on rails, watching the floor the way theatergoers watch a stage. And everywhere — everywhere — *goods*.

Not goods. Noor corrected herself, staring. Not cloth, not grain, not iron.

*Obligations.*

She saw a stall where a woman weighed promises on brass scales: small ones, feather-light, in stoppered vials. *I'll come home Tuesday.* *I'll forgive him by spring.* *I'll stop.* Each vial glowed faintly, amber or grey depending on — Noor couldn't tell what. She saw a man selling years, laid out in rows like dried fish, each one labeled in a hand too fine to read at distance. She saw children — no, not children, she realized with a lurch; they were *shadows*, walking upright, wearing their owners' postures, browsing the stalls for the people they'd been peeled from.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, this is real. This has always been real."

"Since before Saltmere," Corwin said. "Saltmere exists because of this. Not the other way around. The city above is the lid."

They walked. Noor made herself walk slowly, made herself *look*, because twenty years of hiding had taught her that panic drew eyes and wonder drew none. Wonder was safe. Wonder was what a tourist wore. So she let herself be amazed out loud, let Corwin guide her by the elbow through the crowd of brokers and runners and hollow-eyed sellers, and all the while the cartographer in her worked furiously beneath the performance, mapping exits, sightlines, choke points, the tiered geometry of the bridges, the way the lightless lanterns cast no shadows at all —

No shadows. She stopped.

None. Herself, Corwin, the crowd — hundreds of bodies under hundreds of lanterns, and not one shadow anywhere on the floor. The stone drank them. Or something had already collected them.

"Don't look for yours," Corwin murmured, not breaking stride. "People who look for theirs get asked why."

"Asked by whom?"

"That's the wrong question too."

They passed the Amber Stair — a broad flight of white stone rising toward a gallery hung with velvet, guarded by two figures whose faces were smooth as eggs — and kept walking, past it, into the deeper warrens where the stalls grew poorer and stranger. Here the debts were older. Here the vials didn't glow; they *hummed*. Here a broker sat behind a counter of black salt — actual salt, fused into a slab, shot through with veins of darkness like the lines Corwin had read on the hatch — and watched them come with the patience of furniture.

He was old in a way that suggested he'd chosen the age rather than earned it. His coat was good wool gone soft with decades. His hands were folded. On the counter before him lay a ledger bound in something that wasn't leather, and Noor felt her gift stir toward it the way a compass needle stirs toward north — a pull, faint and sickening, in her sternum.

"Cartographer," the broker said.

Noor's blood went cold. "You have me confused with—"

"No." He unfolded his hands. Opened the ledger partway. "I don't confuse. Down here we keep accounts." He turned the book toward them, and Noor saw — with the vertigo of seeing her own handwriting in a place she'd never been — columns of entries in ink that shifted color as the light moved. Names. Dates. Terms. And running down the margin, in a script she recognized the way you recognize your own face in a dark window: *Hartwell. Hartwell. Hartwell.*

Three times. Three centuries apart.

"We want information," Corwin said, stepping half in front of her. "About a purchase. A soul. A child's. Sold four months ago through a runner named Pell Wick."

"Pell Wick sells only to quality." The broker's voice was dry as the salt he sat behind. "Quality came. Quality paid." He tapped one long finger on the ledger, on an entry near the bottom, and did not turn the book further. "You want to know who holds the paper."

"Yes," Noor said.

"The court beneath the mountain." He said it the way a man says *the sea* or *the law* — flatly, finally, a fact beyond argument. "The Gloam Court purchased the child Wren's soul outright, in perpetuity, four months past. Payment rendered: one winter, deferred." His finger moved up the column, up and up, past entries in dozens of hands, decades of them, and stopped at the top of the page where the ink was oldest and darkest. "This is not the first such transaction. It is the most recent renewal of a very old arrangement. The court does not buy souls, cartographer. It *lends winters*. And everything it lends, it collects."

Noor heard her own heartbeat. She heard the hum of the dead vials on the shelves behind the broker. She heard, distantly, the crowd above them going about its trade of promises and years, and it sounded suddenly like insects.

"Deferred," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Deferred from when?"

The broker looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his face — not kindness, nothing so warm, but recognition, the way a clerk recognizes a signature.

"From the last time someone tried to take a soul back out of that mountain," he said. "Ask yourself, Hartwell: what did you buy with the winter that ended a century ago? And ask yourself why the bill has come due now, in *your* lifetime, with *your* name still wet on the account."

"I don't understand—"

"No," he agreed, closing the ledger. "That was the price of the last bargain too. Understanding is expensive. Someone paid yours away before you were born." He folded his hands again, furniture-patient, finished. "The Gloam Court holds the girl. The Gloam Court holds the winter. And the Gloam Court, cartographer, holds a great deal of paper with your family name on it. Go home. Sell charms. Forget the girl casts no shadow."

"And if I don't?"

The broker smiled. It was the worst thing Noor had seen all night, worse than the shadow-children, worse than the doubled silhouette on the stair — because it was gentle.

"Then come back," he said. "Down here, we love a repeat customer."

* * *

They didn't speak until the stairs were behind them and the herring barrels were ahead, and even then Corwin spoke first, low and fast, in the dark of the cellar.

"He knew your name. He knew it before you gave it."

"He read it off the ledger."

"He read it off *three ledgers*, Noor. Three. A century apart." Corwin set the lantern down on a barrel head and gripped the edge of it, knuckles pale. "I told you the salt went black below six layers. Black means sealed. Sealed means somebody with authority closed those accounts — and the only authority down there is the court itself. Your name isn't *in* the debt, it's *underwriting* it. Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere in that mountain there's a contract with your signature on it, and the court has been collecting interest on it since before your grandmother was born."

Noor leaned against the cold stone wall. Above them, muffled through floors of timber and plaster, Saltmere was going about its evening — carts, bells, the smell of bread, the witch-finders' whistles on the corners. A city built on a lid. A city that outlawed magic so thoroughly that it had forgotten magic was the only reason the city existed.

"Wren," she said. "Four months ago. Right when the frost started coming early. Right when Voss started doubling her patrols." She pushed off the wall. "It's not a hunt, Corwin. It's a *collection*. They can feel the debt coming due. The whole city can feel it, and they're blaming us for it."

"Then we go to the mountain."

"We?" She looked at him. In the lantern light his doubled shadow had followed him up the stairs; she could see it now, faintly, on the cellar wall behind him — a second outline, a half-step behind, and she wondered with a chill whether he knew it was there. "Why are you doing this? You quoted a prophecy at me over oysters. You read salt-lines nobody else can see. You have a shadow that doesn't match your body. Who *are* you, Corwin Ash?"

He was silent. Somewhere above, a whistle blew twice — patrol changing shift.

"In the book," he said at last. "The prophecy. There's a verse about the thief who comes twice — once to pay, once to steal back. My grandmother underlined it. She wrote one word in the margin next to it." He met Noor's eyes. "*Ash,*" he said. "My name is in the book too, Noor. It's been in the book for a hundred years. I didn't come to that date by accident, and neither did you."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, boots began to descend the stairs of the Gilded Eel.

Many boots. Moving fast.
