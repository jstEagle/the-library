## 7. Terms of Sale

The broker's stall smelled of cloves and old paper.

Corwin had expected something grander for a debt that touched the Gloam Court — velvet, maybe, or the hush of a temple. Instead he got a crooked booth wedged between a tanner of eel-skins and a woman selling bottled coughs, three shelves of ledger books, and a man with a face like a folded map: creased in all the places a face folds when it has been opened and shut ten thousand times.

"Name," said the broker. He didn't look up from his counting.

"Ash."

"First name?"

"No."

The broker's pen paused. He looked up then, and his eyes were the color of tea gone cold in the cup. "A man with no first name walks into my market asking after a soul-deed. You understand I could sell *you* for that alone. Curiosity is a currency down here. Yours is spending itself loudly."

"I'm told you broker quiet," Corwin said. "I'm buying some."

Behind him, Noor stood very still in the shadow of the stall's awning. She'd pulled her hood low, but the lamplight kept finding her hands — ink-stained, restless, turning over and over a scrap of salt-paper she'd torn from the tunnel wall like it was a rosary. She hadn't spoken since they'd passed under the river. Corwin was beginning to learn what her silences meant. This one meant she was afraid, and that being afraid made her angry at herself for being afraid, and that the anger was easier to hold than the fear.

He understood that arithmetic better than she knew.

"The deed you're asking after," the broker said, "was filed under seal. Court paper. Do you know what that means, above ground? It means the contract isn't property. It's weather. It happens to you whether you read it or not." He set down his pen. "So before I open the book, I want to know who sent you. Not your name. Your *reason*. Reasons leave fingerprints."

Noor moved. One step forward, hood back, and the lamplight did what it always did — found her face and refused to let go of it.

"Hartwell," she said. "Noor Hartwell. I keep the charm shop on Bellwether Lane. A child on my street sold her soul, and everyone tells me it can't be gotten back, and I don't believe them."

The broker looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a dry sound, like a page turning.

"Oh," he said. "*Oh.* You came yourself. You walked into my market wearing your own name." He was already reaching for a ledger on the top shelf, one bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth. "Madam, do you know how many people come down here asking about Hartwell deeds? None. In thirty years behind this counter, none. Because anyone who knew enough to ask also knew enough to stay home."

"My name is common enough," Noor said.

"It is not," said the broker, "on this paper."

* * *

He laid the ledger flat and unlaced its clasp with the tenderness of a man undressing a wound. The pages inside were not paper. They were something thinner, translucent, and they held the lamplight the way ice holds a bruise.

"Standard form," the broker said. "Soul, one, juvenile, female. Vendor: the parents, jointly, consideration received." His finger traced a column of figures Corwin couldn't read — the numbers seemed to slide away from his eye, the way water slides off wax. "Purchaser: an intermediary, acting for a principal unnamed. And here—" the finger stopped, tapped twice, "—the interesting part. The consideration wasn't coin. It never is, at this level. Souls are bought with *outcomes*. Do you know what outcome clears a debt of this size?"

"Tell us," Corwin said.

"Winter," said the broker. "The end of one. Specifically—" he turned the ledger so they could see, though the script swam and would not be read, "—specifically the winter of ninety-nine years past. The Long White, the old books call it. Nine years of frost that killed the harbor, the orchards, the third of Saltmere that starved. It ended in a single night. Thawed so fast the river cracked like a shot. The histories call it a miracle. The histories are written by people who don't shop here."

The stall was very quiet. Somewhere down the row, the cough-seller was arguing with a customer about vintage.

"That's not possible," Noor said. Her voice had gone thin. "That was a hundred years ago. Nobody alive—"

"Nobody alive signed it," the broker agreed pleasantly. "But signatures outlive signatories. That's rather the point of writing things down." He rotated the ledger again, angling it toward the light, and Corwin saw that the translucent page showed two faces at once — the contract on the front, and ghosted through from the page behind, faint as breath on glass, a copy. A duplicate. Filed, as court papers were, in triplicate.

"Here's the vendor line," the broker said. "Names of the parents, redacted by the intermediary — standard practice, protects the family. Here's the witness line, sealed. And here—" his nail came down on the signature block, "—here is the guarantor. The party who pledged the outcome. Who promised the winter would end, and put up collateral against failure."

He waited. Corwin realized the man wanted one of them to say it, wanted to watch their faces while they did, because down here even cruelty was inventory and a reaction was a thing you could bank.

"Read it," Corwin said.

"Guarantor of record," the broker read, in the mild voice of a man reciting a laundry list, "*Hartwell. Tamsin Hartwell.*"

* * *

The world went narrow.

Later, Corwin would try to reconstruct those seconds and find only fragments: the lamplight guttering; the cough-seller's voice rising and falling like weather; Noor's hand coming down on the edge of the counter, hard, the way a person catches themselves on furniture when the floor betrays them.

"Tamsin," she said.

"That's the spelling on the deed," the broker said. "T-A-M-S-I-N. Unusual. I remember it because unusual names are good collateral too — easy to trace, hard to forge."

"That's not—" Noor stopped. Started again. "My name is Noor. My mother named me Noor. I have—" She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, as if checking that the person inside was still there. "I have twenty years of being Noor Hartwell. There is no Tamsin."

"There doesn't need to be," the broker said, with terrible gentleness. "Deeds attach to blood, madam, not to memory. If a Hartwell guaranteed a debt, every Hartwell after carries the guarantee. That's how the old contracts work. That's why they're worth more than coin — coin spends, but a name accrues." He laced the ledger closed. "You didn't sign anything. But you were signed. There's a difference, and the difference is that you don't get to renegotiate."

Corwin watched her take that in. He watched it land the way a stone lands in still water — no splash, just the rings going out and out, disturbing everything at a distance from the center first.

Because he had known. God help him, he had known there was a chance, and he had brought her down here anyway, because the verse needed proving and because — he could admit it now, in the privacy of his own cowardice — because he had wanted her to want this as badly as he needed her to. The prophecy hadn't given him a choice about finding her. It had only left him a choice about how.

"When," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved. "When was the deed executed. The date."

The broker consulted the ledger. "Ninety-nine years ago, autumn. Why?"

"Read me the day."

"Fifth of Frostmonth."

Corwin closed his eyes. *On the fifth of Frostmonth the thief comes twice,* the verse went, *once to give and once to take, and the mountain will not know which hand is which.* He had thought *twice* meant two thieves. Him and her. He had built his whole understanding of the prophecy on that reading, and now a dead woman's name sat on a century-old contract wearing Noor's face, and *twice* might mean something else entirely. Might mean the same hand, giving and taking, separated by a hundred years.

Or might mean a woman who gave her name away and took another one, and never knew why the second one fit so loosely.

"We're done," Noor said.

"Madam—"

"We're *done*." She was already moving, pushing past the eel-skin tanner's racks, and Corwin threw a coin on the counter — too much, deliberately, because generosity was cheaper than questions — and went after her.

* * *

She stopped him at the mouth of the tunnel, where the Undermarket's borrowed light gave out and the smugglers' lamps began. She stood with her back to him and her hand braced on the wet stone, and for a moment he thought she was going to be sick.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it now, while I'm too angry to fall apart."

Corwin weighed honesty against mercy and found, not for the first time, that he owned less of each than the situation required.

"The prophecy I quoted to you," he said. "The night we met. I didn't memorize it from a book I happened to own. I've been carrying it since I was nineteen. It was read over me. By someone who believed it was a map, and I was the territory."

"And the name in the ledger—"

"Is yours. Or was. Or will be. I don't know which tense applies, and neither does the mountain." He stepped closer, careful, the way you approach a bird with a broken wing — not because it will hurt you, but because it will hurt itself worse if you're clumsy. "Noor. Twenty years ago you arrived in Saltmere with no history. Everyone says it. Maren says it. You've said it yourself — no memories older than your shop, no mother anyone ever met, no grave to visit. People don't spring up out of the ground at twenty-two years old with a talent for drawing places that don't exist."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't you dare build your theory out of my missing pieces."

"Then you build it," he said. "You're the cartographer. You know better than anyone — a map with a blank space isn't a map of nothing. It's a map of something someone decided you shouldn't see."

The tunnel dripped. Far off, the market went on with its trading of other people's debts, and somewhere above them Saltmere slept under a sky that had begun, three nights early, to smell of snow.

Noor turned around. Her eyes were dry, which frightened him more than tears would have.

"If there was a Tamsin," she said slowly, "and if she sold a child's soul to buy back a summer — then either she was a monster, or she was desperate, and I have spent twenty years being kind to desperate people because some part of me knows what desperation costs." Her hand went to her sternum again, that same gesture, pressing on the hollow where a life should be. "And if she erased herself to get away from what she did—"

"Then the debt didn't die with her," Corwin finished quietly. "It waited. Debts down here don't expire, Noor. They *ripen*."

"And Wren's soul is the interest."

Neither of them said the rest of it. Neither of them had to. Because they had both seen the ledger's last line before the broker closed it — the clause written small, in the margin, in handwriting that had nothing of the court in it, something hurried and human and afraid:

*If the guarantor's line fails to discharge, the purchaser may collect in kind.*

Collect in kind. A soul for a soul. A Hartwell for a Hartwell.

Noor looked up at him, and Corwin watched her arrive at it — watched the whole shape of it close around her like a door.

"The winter's starting early," she said. "Three days early. Voss's raids doubling. Frost on the south glass in Frostmonth's first week, when it never comes before midwinter." She laughed, one short, awful note. "It's not weather, is it. It's *collection*. The mountain is sending an invoice."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and the bones of the city, a bell began to ring — the frost-bell, the one that hadn't sounded in living memory, the one whose rope the harbormaster kept sealed under wax.

The broker's voice drifted after them down the tunnel, mild as milk:

"Madam? If you mean to contest the deed — you'll want to see what the guarantor pledged as collateral. It's itemized on the reverse."

Corwin turned. So did Noor.

"Collateral," she repeated. "She put up collateral *against her own promise*?"

"She put up the only thing the court values more than souls," the broker said, and reopened the ledger to the page he had not shown them, and turned it toward the light.

The reverse side was covered in drawings. Fine-lined, precise, unmistakable — coastlines that curled off the edges of the page into nowhere, cities with streets that looped like knotted thread, mountains drawn in cross-section with doors in their roots. Maps. Dozens of them, inked straight onto the contract's skin, each one labeled in a hand Corwin had seen every day for weeks, in a shop on Bellwether Lane, on charts Noor claimed she'd invented herself.

Every realm she had ever mapped. Every place that shouldn't exist.

The court had them all. Had had them for a century. And at the bottom of the page, in the same hurried human hand, a final line that read:

*These are the roads in. Let them also be the roads out.*

Noor reached out and touched the page, and the lamplight came up through the translucent skin and lit her face from below, and Corwin saw her understand — not the debt, not yet, but the maps. That she had never invented them. That she had been *remembering* them, twenty years, stroke by stroke, without knowing whose hand she was copying.

"Take me back down," she said to the broker, in a voice he had not heard from her before — steady, level, and cold as the thing falling toward the city above. "I want to read every word she wrote."
