## 10. Frostline

The frost came to Saltmere on the ninth of Harvestmonth, three weeks before any winter had a right to it, and it came wrong.

Noor knew it was wrong before she opened her eyes. She knew it in her teeth, in the old ache that lived where her memories used to be — a hollow tooth of a feeling, as if something beneath the city had shifted its weight. By the time she reached the shop's front window with her tea still steaming, breath fogging glass that should not have been cold enough to fog, the street outside was white.

Not snow-white. Frost-white. Every cobblestone in Lantern Row wore a lace of ice so fine it looked spun, and the iron railings of the lamplighter's stair had bloomed with ferns of crystal that caught the early light and threw it back in colors salt-ice had no business making: green, violet, a bruised gold at the edges.

She set down her cup. She took out her sketchbook, because twenty years of habit dies hard, and began to draw what she saw.

Halfway through the first railing, her hand stopped.

She knew these patterns. Not from this morning. From the archive beneath her shop, from the sealed book whose final page bore her own name in her own handwriting. The prophecy's illustrations — those strange, precise plates she'd pored over until Corwin came knocking and everything got worse — showed frost exactly like this. Ferns of ice on ironwork. Lace on cobbles. A city wearing its winter like a signature.

The illustrations were dated, in the book's careful marginalia, to a winter one hundred years past.

Noor closed the sketchbook. Her tea had gone cold in the time it took to draw one railing, which was also wrong, because tea did not go cold that fast unless the cold was coming *from* somewhere, moving with intent, drinking the warmth out of things in order.

Outside, Mrs. Pell from the chandlery stood in the middle of the street in her nightclothes, staring up at the sky with an expression Noor had seen only once before — the day the plague-ships were burned in the harbor, when everyone understood at once that the world had changed shape overnight.

"It's early," Mrs. Pell said when Noor joined her. Not a question. A complaint filed with the universe.

"Three weeks," Noor agreed.

Mrs. Pell's eyes slid sideways. "My grandmother used to say there was a winter once that didn't end. That they paid it off somehow." She laughed, a small frightened sound. "Old women's talk."

"Old women are usually right about the important things," Noor said, and went back inside to lock her door, which was a thing honest charm-sellers did not need to do at seven in the morning.

* * *

By noon, the frost had opinions.

It didn't fall evenly, the way weather does. It fell in *patterns*. Gull Street froze in a spiral that tightened toward the well. The market square iced over in concentric rings, and the rings were not smooth — they were inscribed, fine lines within lines, and if you looked too long the lines seemed to rearrange themselves, the way handwriting seems to move at the edge of vision.

Noor walked the city with her collar up and her sketchbook open, mapping the frost the way she'd once mapped coastlines that appeared on no chart. And everywhere she went, the same sick recognition followed her: she had drawn these shapes before. In the margins of the prophecy. In maps she never made, in dreams she didn't keep.

At the corner of Salters' Way, a boy no older than Wren was chipping at the ice with a spoon, trying to free a cat that had wandered onto the frozen fountain and gotten its paws stuck. The ice around the cat had grown *upward*, little spires of it, like the fountain was growing teeth.

"Don't," Noor said, kneeling. "Let me."

She put her bare hands against the spires — a foolish thing, a thing that would cost her skin — and the ice relented all at once, sloughing away like a dog called off. The cat shot free. The boy stared at her.

"You're warm," he said. "You're the only warm thing on this whole street."

Noor looked at her hands. Unmarked. Unfrozen. Warm as a hearthstone, while the whole city shivered.

She thought of Wren, who cast no shadow, whom no frost would touch. She thought of the court beneath the mountain, and a contract signed a century ago by a woman with her name, and a debt that was, apparently, coming due.

The cold wasn't falling on Saltmere, she realized. It was reaching for it. Feeling along the streets like fingers reading braille, checking whether the payment was still where it had been left.

And finding, perhaps, that it had been moved.

* * *

The witch-finders came to Lantern Row at dusk, and this time they did not come politely.

Noor heard them before she saw them — boots, many boots, and the particular ringing silence that falls on a street when every shopkeeper pretends very hard to be alone. She doused her lamp and watched through the shutter-crack as six of them moved down the row in formation, gray coats, iron badges shaped like the letter V for Verity, though everyone knew it stood for something older that no one said aloud.

Sera Voss walked at their head. She always did. Tall, unhurried, her lantern held low so its light found boots and hems and doorways rather than faces — a courtesy, or a technique. Behind her, two finders carried between them a frame of black iron strung with wires, and from the wires hung slips of paper that fluttered without wind.

Charm-detectors. Noor had seen them twice in twenty years. Both times, someone had been taken.

They stopped at Maren's dye-shop. Of course they did. Maren, who mordanted cloth in colors no mordant should yield; Maren, who had known Noor since the night Noor arrived in Saltmere with nothing but a name and a hole where her life should have been.

Voss knocked. Maren opened. Their voices carried in the frozen air, which carried everything now, sound traveling strangely over the ice like it couldn't decide which surface to bounce from.

"Licensing inspection," Voss said. "New ordinance. All pigments to be declared."

"I declare my pigments every quarter," Maren said. "You have the declarations."

"The frost has changed things." Voss's voice was flat as slate. "The Council believes the early winter is unnatural. They believe it is worked. Every trade that touches color, heat, or light will be inspected until the worker is found."

"And if it isn't a worker? If it's just weather?"

"Then we will inspect until we are satisfied it is just weather." A pause. "Open your back room, Mistress Sallow."

Noor's hands curled against the shutter. She knew what was in Maren's back room. Everyone on the row knew, and no one said, because that was the covenant of Saltmere's hidden ones: you kept your small magics small, you kept each other's secrets, and the witch-finders stayed fat and bored and blind.

But the frost had changed things. Voss had said it herself, and Noor heard what lived under the words: *the rules of hiding are suspended. We are hunting now.*

Maren opened the back room. There was a long silence, the kind of silence that has a body count pending. Then Voss said, quietly enough that Noor had to press her ear to the wood:

"These dyes shift color in the dark. I've known for six years, Mistress Sallow. Why do you think you've never been inspected?"

"Mistress Voss—"

"Scrape your vats tonight. Dilute the reds. If I'm sent back here with the wire-frame, I cannot be gentle again." Another pause, longer. "There's a woman on this street who sells charms that actually work. Find her. Tell her the frost is drawing the prophecy's figures, and tell her—" the finder's voice did something Noor had never heard it do, something almost human, almost afraid— "tell her my grandmother's copy of that book is missing, and I want to know who took it."

Then the boots moved on, and Noor stood in her dark shop with her heart going like a trapped bird, understanding two things at once.

First: Voss knew about her. Had always known. Had spent years choosing not to see.

Second: the mercy was ending. Whatever patience had protected the hidden congregation of Saltmere for a generation was burning down like a lamp in a draft, and when it guttered out, the wire-frames would come for Maren, for the dyers and tinsmiths and midwives, for every soul who had ever traded warmth for safety.

And third — she counted it separately, because it deserved its own terror — Sera Voss wanted her prophecy book back. Which meant Voss knew the book existed. Which meant Voss might know what was written in it.

Which meant Voss might know *her*.

* * *

Corwin arrived after midnight, letting himself in with the key she'd given him three days ago and immediately regretted and could not bring herself to take back. He was snow-dusted despite the dry frost, breathing hard, his coat smelling of smoke and river water.

"They're burning books in Candler's Court," he said, without preamble. "Not banned books. *All* books. Anything with maps in it, anything with star-charts, anything with illustrations of weather. A printer named Osk was taken because he'd engraved a calendar with snowflakes on the border."

Noor stared at him. "Snowflakes."

"Six-pointed ones." He pulled off his gloves finger by finger; his hands were shaking. "The Council's issued a proclamation. The winter is unnatural, it is *worked*, and the working is written somewhere — in diagrams, in charts, in pictures. Anyone possessing such material is to surrender it for inspection. Anyone concealing it is an accomplice to the working." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you understand what they've done? Half the city owns an almanac. They've made criminals of everyone, so that no one dares shelter anyone else. It's the oldest trick in a hunter's book, Noor. You don't chase the fox. You burn the hedgerows."

Noor went to the shelf behind the counter, the false-backed one, and took out the prophecy book. It sat in her hands heavier than it had any right to, warm when everything else was cold, the way her own skin was warm.

"If they find this," she said, "they don't take me. They take everyone I've ever spoken to. Maren. The congregation. You."

"So we leave."

"Where?" She gestured at the window, at the white street beyond, at the spiral frost tightening on Gull Street like a noose being drawn. "Look at it, Corwin. It's not weather. It's a *diagram*. The whole city is being inked in. Something under the mountain is writing on us, and I think—" her voice caught, steadied— "I think it's checking its ledger. I think the frost is looking for what it's owed."

Corwin crossed the room and took the book from her hands, gently, and opened it to the plate she'd been avoiding for days: the illustration of a frozen city, streets in spirals and rings, a mountain rising behind it with a door in its flank. Beneath the image, in the old marginalia, ran a line neither of them had fully parsed before, because neither of them had wanted to.

He read it aloud anyway. "'When the frost returns before its season, the debt walks abroad to count what remains. What was sold must be surrendered, or the city shall stand surety.'"

"Surety," Noor repeated. "The city stands surety. Meaning if the debt isn't paid—"

"The winter finishes falling. All of it. The century of it they deferred." He closed the book. "One hundred years ago, someone bought Saltmere a spring. And the bill has come due, and the collector is drawing up the invoice in ice on our streets."

Outside, somewhere down the row, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, not the tide-bell, but the old alarm in the market tower that hadn't sounded in living memory. Then another bell, farther off. Then another, answering, until the whole frozen city rang with a sound like breaking glass stretched into music.

Noor pressed her hand flat against the window. Beyond her own reflection, down the length of Lantern Row, the frost on the cobbles had begun to glow faintly, tracing itself brighter, line by line, ring by ring — completing itself, the way a map completes itself under a cartographer's pen.

And in the newest ring, still forming at the mouth of the alley across the street, she could read the shape taking form. Not a pattern this time. Not geometry.

Letters. Ice-letters, tall as a child, spelling out a single word in a script she recognized because she had written it herself, a century ago, in a hand she did not remember learning:

**HARTWELL.**

Corwin saw it too. She felt him go still beside her.

"Noor," he said carefully. "Whoever Hartwell was — whatever Hartwell did — the mountain knows she's in this city. And it's telling everyone."

Down the street, a door banged open. Lamplight spilled yellow across the ice. Voices rose — angry, frightened, gathering — and above them all, cutting through the bells, a woman's voice that Noor knew from inspections and proclamations and six years of merciful blindness:

"Witch-finders! Clear the street!"

Sera Voss had come for Lantern Row. And this time, she was not walking slowly.
