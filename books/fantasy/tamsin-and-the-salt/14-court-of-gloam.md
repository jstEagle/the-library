## 14. Court of Gloam

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the third day.

We had climbed through the pass Noor mapped in her sleep — through the throat of it, where the rock was veined with salt like marble gone bad — and then the walls simply opened, and there it was.

The Gloam Court.

It was a cathedral, if a cathedral could be grown rather than built. Ice in columns thick as watchtowers, arches that curved like ribs, and everywhere light without source. No candles. No lamps. No windows. The glow came from nowhere and belonged to no one, a pale silver wash that made every shadow sharp as a knife laid flat. Corwin's breath hung in the air beside mine, but the cold here wasn't weather. It was architecture. It had been cold here for a hundred years, patiently, on purpose.

"Say nothing," Corwin murmured. "Let me do the talking."

"You've done so well so far," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. The cathedral took it anyway, swallowed it into all that ice.

He glanced at me — quick, worried, the look of a man who had spent three days learning to trust someone and hated how much of his safety now hung on her. "Noor."

"I know." I flexed my fingers inside my gloves. They ached from the climb, from the frostbite I'd nursed at the fire we weren't supposed to light, from something older than both. "I know what this place is. I've known since the pass. Haven't you wondered why I never once needed to check the map?"

He hadn't answered, because he knew the answer, and neither of us wanted it said aloud: some part of me had been here before. Some part of me remembered the way home even after everything else was taken.

* * *

There were no guards. There didn't need to be. The doors at the far end of the nave were tall enough for grief to walk through upright, and they stood open, waiting, the way a trap waits — not hidden, just patient.

We crossed the floor of the court and my boots rang against ice that sounded, horribly, like bells. Between the columns stood things I refused to look at directly: shapes in the gloam, tall and thin, wearing robes of frost that never melted. Servants. Or wardens. Or the court's memory of people who had once come here and not left. When I turned my head they were always slightly closer than they'd been, though their feet never moved.

"Don't count them," Corwin said quietly.

"I wasn't."

"You were. Everyone counts them. That's how they get your number."

So I looked straight ahead instead, at the dais at the end of the nave, and at what sat upon it.

I don't know what I expected. A monster, maybe. Something with teeth and too many joints, something out of the cautionary woodcuts Voss's witch-finders nailed to posts around Saltmere. But the sovereign of the Gloam Court was an old woman.

She sat on a throne of black ice with her hands folded in her lap like a grandmother at a funeral, small and neat, wrapped in a shawl the color of deep water. Her face was lined and kind and terribly familiar, the way faces in dreams are familiar — not because you've seen them, but because some part of you has been braced for them your whole life.

"Noor Hartwell," she said. Her voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was warm the way a hearth is warm when you've been walking in snow for three days, and it reached into my chest and unclenched muscles I hadn't known were tight. "You came farther than last time."

Beside me, Corwin went very still. "Last time," he repeated.

"I don't know what she's talking about," I said.

"No," the sovereign said gently. "You don't. That was rather the point." She rose — she was shorter than I'd imagined, barely taller than Wren — and descended the steps of the dais one at a time, unhurried, until she stood close enough that I could see her eyes. They were pale as frost on glass, and behind them was depth, the kind of depth you see down a well and flinch from. "Come. Sit. You've walked a long way to be offered a kindness, and kindness should be given sitting down."

"We're not staying," Corwin said.

"You are already staying," she said, without malice, without even emphasis. She said it the way one says *the sun is up*. "But sit regardless. Standing makes bargainers stubborn."

* * *

There were chairs. Of course there were chairs — two of them, set before a low table of grey ice, arranged as if someone had known we were coming, which someone had, which someone always had. I sat because my legs made the decision for me. Corwin remained standing, planted between me and the sovereign like a fence post, and she regarded him with something that was almost fondness.

"The reader of salt-lines," she said. "You found her. How industrious. Do you know, a century ago I told her the same thing I'm about to tell her now, and she also brought someone who wouldn't sit down."

"Stop," I said. "Stop doing that. Stop talking about me like you were there."

"I was there," the sovereign said. "That is the entire nature of my office. I am always here. The mountain does not keep time, child; it keeps accounts. And yours—" she folded her hands again, and the candleless light seemed to lean toward her, listening— "yours has come due."

From somewhere in her shawl she produced a ledger. Not the broker's copy from the Undermarket — this one was older, its cover scarred, its spine cracked white with frost. She set it on the table between us and did not open it. She didn't need to. We both knew what was written in it. I had seen the entry in the Undermarket with my own eyes: *One soul, child, female, aged six years. Payment rendered against the winter account. Signed: Hartwell.*

"Wren," I said. Not a question. I made it not a question by force of will alone.

"A lovely name," the sovereign said. "Her mother chose well. The girl herself is quite comfortable, if that concerns you. Souls feel very little down here. It's rather like sleep, except the sleeper doesn't dream, and doesn't wake, and doesn't age, and doesn't grieve." She tilted her head. "Some would call that mercy. I have called it mercy myself, on occasion, and meant it."

"What do you want?" Corwin said.

"Want." The sovereign smiled, and for a moment the warmth in her face cracked and showed something beneath it, vast and patient and old as the ice overhead. "Such a blunt instrument of a word. I don't *want*, reader. I hold. What I have is held, and what is owed is owed, and the only question ever put to anyone in this room is the same question: what will you give to balance the book?"

* * *

And then she told me what it would cost. And gods forgive me, but for one whole minute — one long, candleless, silver-lit minute — I wanted to say yes.

"Your memories," she said. "What remains of them. Twenty years' worth. The shop. The charm trade. Maren's laugh. The smell of salt-dough drying on your worktable. The blind date you didn't know was a summons. All of it, surrendered cleanly, and the girl goes free. Whole. Shadowed. Frost will cling to her windowpanes again by morning, and her mother will wake to a daughter who cries at cold feet like any other child." She spread her hands, an old woman offering tea. "You would walk out of here with a life to rebuild and no idea what you'd paid for it. You've done it once before, Noor. You were good at it. You built something lovely out of nothing, out of a hole where a past should be. I watched you do it. It was, I confess, rather beautiful."

The silence rang like the ice floor under my boots.

Because here was the shape of the temptation, and it was not greed, and it was not fear. It was mercy, exactly as she'd framed it, mercy wearing mercy's own face. Wren would live. Truly live — not the half-life of a child who cast no shadow, whose mother warmed hands over her and felt nothing come back. One soul restored, one family mended, one debt paid in full and honestly, and the price was only—

Only the twenty years I had fought to build out of nothing.

Only Maren, who would greet me tomorrow as a stranger and grieve for a friend who no longer existed. Only the congregation in the undercellars who had kept me safe, who would become, to me, a locked door with no key. Only Corwin, standing there not sitting down, who had quoted prophecy at me across a restaurant table and then bled for me in a witch-finder raid and taught me to read salt-lines by lantern-light — none of which I would remember. He would be a stranger quoting verses at a woman who had no reason to trust him. Worse: he would be a stranger who *did*, and I would look at him the way you look at a man who knows too much about you, and I would be afraid.

"You're hesitating," the sovereign observed, kindly.

"She's thinking," Corwin said sharply.

"Hesitating," she agreed, "is a form of thinking. The most honest kind." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something almost tender, almost grandmotherly, and that was the trap within the trap — that she made cruelty sound like care, that she offered oblivion the way you'd offer a blanket. "Child. Let me tell you what I see, since no one else in your life has had the courage. You are a woman built on a foundation she cannot see. Every kindness you've been given, you've accepted with a flinch, because some part of you knows it might be withdrawn. Every friendship, you hold at arm's length. Even the girl — even little shadowless Wren — you love her, and loving her frightens you, because you know, somewhere below knowing, that you are the reason she is hollow." She let that settle. It settled like snowfall, soft and total. "Give me the memories. Be empty, and be free. Emptiness is a mercy I can actually deliver. I have never once lied to a signer, and I will not lie to you: you will not miss what you lose. You never have."

And the horrible thing — the thing I will carry even if I carry nothing else — is that every word was true.

I thought about it. Gods help me, I sat in that chair of grey ice and I *thought about it*. I thought about waking tomorrow in a shop I didn't recognize, with clean hands and no debts and a city full of strangers, and how light that would be. How light it had been, once. I had proof. I had twenty years of proof that a person could be emptied and still make something gentle out of the remainder.

My hand moved toward the ledger.

Corwin caught it.

Not dramatically. He didn't grab me or shout or do anything the ballads would approve of. He simply set his hand over mine on the table, palm down, calloused and cold from the pass, and said, "Ask her what happens to the winter."

The sovereign's smile did not move. But something behind her eyes did — a recalculation, a ledger-line redrawn.

"The winter," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised us both. "If I pay. If Wren walks free and I walk out empty. What happens to the winter that's coming? The one your frost-patterns have been writing on Saltmere's streets all month?"

"That account," the sovereign said, "is separate."

"Separate," Corwin repeated. "Or deferred?"

For the first time, the sovereign of the Gloam Court was silent.

It lasted perhaps two heartbeats. In a human face it would have been nothing. On hers, in that candleless light, it was a chasm opening — and I understood, suddenly and completely, why she had framed the trade as mercy. Not because she was lying about the mercy. Because the truth was worse than any lie she could have told, and a truthful woman will always show you the kinder door first.

"You may ask your reader," she said at last, rising, drawing her shawl close, "what he found in the broker's copy. If he dares say it aloud in my court." She turned those well-deep eyes on me, and the warmth in them banked itself like a fire left to die. "Take tonight, Noor Hartwell. Sleep on my offer. The court is generous with time; it has so much of it. But know this much, and know it clearly—"

She leaned down, close enough that her breath stirred my hair, and it smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than brimstone.

"—the last woman who sat in that chair chose the other door. She chose to fight me. And everyone who helped her paid for it, and she paid most of all, and in the end she begged me for the very emptiness I am offering you now, freely, with love." She straightened. "History, in this place, does not repeat. It merely comes due."

* * *

They gave us a cell. Of course they did — a guest-chamber, the frost-wardens called it, carved from blue ice with a bed of packed snow that never melted and never warmed. The door closed behind us with a sound like a held breath.

Corwin sat against the wall and pulled the broker's copied page from inside his coat, the one he'd stolen in the Undermarket while I'd been staring at Wren's name, and smoothed it against his knee. His hands were shaking. I noticed, in the silver light, that frost had gotten into the creases of his knuckles during the climb and never quite left.

"You were going to take it," he said. Not accusing. Just checking, the way you check a rope before you trust it with weight.

"For about a minute," I said. "Maybe less."

"It felt longer."

"It felt like forever. That's how she builds it. Forever, made comfortable." I sat down beside him, our shoulders touching, and stared at the ceiling of ice, where the candleless light pooled like water that couldn't decide whether to fall. "Corwin. The winter. What did you find in the broker's copy?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that I turned to look at him, and saw him wrestling with it — with whether to tell me now, tonight, in a cell of ice with the sovereign's offer hanging over us like the blade of a guillotine that hadn't dropped yet.

"The original contract," he said finally. "The one signed a century ago. It doesn't say the winter *ended*, Noor. Contracts like these — debt instruments, soul-collateral — they don't end things. They defer them." He tapped the page. "Every year of warmth Saltmere got was borrowed. And the interest—" his voice caught, steadied— "the interest was paid in souls. Small ones. Quiet ones. Children whose shadows went missing and whose mothers were told it was a wasting sickness. Wren isn't the first. She's just the largest payment yet. The final installment."

"And when the installments run out?"

He looked at me, and in the candleless light his face was young and scared and certain, all at once.

"There's a clause," he said. "At the bottom of the last page. In the margin, in handwriting I'd swear I've seen somewhere before. It says: *When the account closes, the winter falls entire.*"

Outside our cell, far above, through a hundred fathoms of ice, I heard — or dreamed I heard — the sound of snow beginning to fall on a city that had outlawed the only person who could save it.

And beneath that, softer, patient as a signature drying:

the sovereign, laughing.
