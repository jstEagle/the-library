## 16. Voss at the Gate

The mountain road was a lie told in switchbacks.

Sera Voss had climbed it twice before — once at nineteen, on her certification ascent, when the witch-finders still pretended the pass mattered; once at thirty-one, carrying a body down that no one had ever claimed. Both times the weather had been merely cruel. Tonight it was *intentional*. The wind came off the peak in long, deliberate strokes, like a hand smoothing a bedsheet, and the frost formed not in patches but in lines — straight lines, branching lines, patterns she had spent her whole career pretending were coincidence.

She knew those patterns. She'd known them since she was eight years old, sitting cross-legged on her grandmother's floor while Edith Voss traced them with one cracked fingernail across the pages of a book she was never allowed to touch.

*Salt-lines,* her grandmother had called them. Not the salt-lines of debt that Corwin Ash read with his smuggler's eyes — the other kind. The kind written into the world itself. *The mountain keeps its accounts, Sera. Everything else is bookkeeping.*

Her grandmother had been dead eleven years, and Sera had spent every one of them doing exactly what Edith had begged her, on her deathbed, with her hand gripping Sera's wrist hard enough to bruise, *not* to do.

*Don't join them, girl. Don't you dare join them.*

Sera had joined them within the year. Grief makes obedient people of us all.

Now she stood at the gate of the Gloam Court with six witch-finders behind her and a writ in her coat pocket that named three fugitives: Noor Hartwell, cartographer and unregistered practitioner; Corwin Ash, smuggler and suspected accomplice; and — this was new, this had come through an hour ago by courier hawk, in handwriting that made her stomach drop — Sera Voss herself, cited for obstruction, for aiding, for *knowing*.

The writ had been signed by First Finder Aldous Crane. Beneath his signature, in smaller ink, the seal of the Office of Salt and Order: two crossed keys over a mountain. She had worn that seal on her chest for fourteen years. She had never once looked at it properly.

She looked at it now, by lantern light, at four thousand feet, with the frost writing its patient script across the gate's iron.

The keys weren't crossed. They were *turned* — both of them, turned inward, toward the mountain. Locking something in. Or locking everyone else out.

"Finder." Dannel, her second, at her shoulder. Young, eager, cold-burned at the cheeks. "Gate's not guarded."

"No," Sera said. "It isn't."

"You'd think a place like this—"

"It doesn't need guards, Dannel. It has terms."

He didn't understand. He was twenty-three and he thought magic was a thing you caught, like poachers catching birds, and that the law was a net and they were the hands that held it. She had thought so too, once. She had built a whole self out of thinking so.

The gate opened before they touched it.

Not swung — *unlatched*, the way a door unlatches when someone inside has been waiting, and the sound it made was small and domestic and utterly wrong for a cathedral of ice, and Sera felt the bottom fall out of the last certainty she owned. Beyond the threshold: light without candles, pale and sourceless, falling in shafts that did not move when the lanterns moved. A nave of blue glass ice. And far ahead, at the altar-end of all that glittering dark, voices. Her fugitives' voices, thin with distance, arguing about something — a ledger, she'd learn later; a loan; a winter deferred for a hundred years like a bill slipped under a door.

"Positions," she said, and hated how steady her voice came out. "We go in slow. We take them alive. Hartwell especially — Crane wants her breathing."

"And if the court objects?"

"The court doesn't object, Dannel. The court *invoices*."

They went in. Their boots rang on ice that had never known a boot-heel, and the sound followed them down the nave like applause from an audience that could not be seen.

* * *

She found them at the vault doors, and the sight of them stopped her hand on her sword-hilt longer than any spell ever had.

Noor Hartwell stood with both palms flat against the ice of the vault door as though listening to a heartbeat inside it. Corwin Ash was beside her, gray-faced, frost climbing his left arm in fern-patterns that Sera recognized with a lurch — the same fern-patterns from the prophecy book's margins, drawn there in faded brown ink by a hand she knew better than her own mother's. And between them, wrapped in Ash's coat, a child. Small. Shadowless. Wren, the neighbor's daughter, the one whose file Sera had closed three years ago marked *no evidence of practice* because the alternative had been opening a file she couldn't bear to open.

The child was awake. The child was looking directly at Sera with eyes like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Finder Voss." Hartwell didn't turn around. Her voice was wrecked — raw, used up, the voice of a woman who had been crying or shouting or both for hours. "You're late. Everyone's always late to this part."

"Sera Voss." Corwin Ash said her name like he was reading it off a contract. "There's a version of events where you arrest us now."

"There is," she agreed.

"In that version, the child goes back to Saltmere in a wagon, soulless, and the winter finishes falling anyway, and Crane pins it on all three of us, and the court collects, and nothing changes until the next century comes due." He lifted his frost-rimed arm slightly, an exhibit. "I've read the ledger, Finder. Have you?"

"I've read my orders."

"Yes," Ash said softly. "So did the men who built the gallows at Fenwick Hollow. Orders are very readable. That's rather their function."

Dannel moved up on her left, sword half-drawn, breath steaming. "Finder, give the word."

And here was the moment. Here was the hinge of her entire life, and it did not feel like a hinge. It felt like standing in her grandmother's parlor again, aged eight, watching Edith trace frost-patterns on a forbidden page, hearing her say *the mountain keeps its accounts* — and then, later, older, drunker, dying: *I helped carry the book to the archive under the charm shop, Sera. I helped hide it where the Hartwell woman would find it. God forgive me, I helped write her into it.*

Sera had thought that was grief talking. Senility. The morphine syrup they gave the dying.

She unslung her satchel.

Hartwell turned then, finally, and her face was terrible and ordinary at once — a shopkeeper's face, a tired woman's face, forty-some years old and carved by twenty years of small kindnesses. "What are you doing?" she asked.

"What I should have done at your shop," Sera said, "the day I first read the warrant against you. Which was four years ago, in case you're wondering how long I've been a coward."

She took out the prophecy book.

It was not the archive copy — that one had burned, or cracked, or fled, depending which rumor you believed. This was the other one. The family one. The one Edith Voss had kept wrapped in oilcloth at the back of a wardrobe for sixty years, the one Sera had inherited along with the seal and the guilt and the name. She had carried it up the mountain in her satchel the way you carry a letter you can't decide to send.

She set it on the ice floor between them and stepped back.

"That's—" Ash started.

"Mine," Sera said. "My grandmother's. Read the margins."

* * *

Here is what Sera Voss knew, and had always known, and had arranged her life into a shape that let her not know it:

Edith Voss had been a founder. Not of the witch-finders — of what came before them. The Office of Salt and Order had begun, a hundred and one years ago, as nine signatories to a document called the Accord of Deferred Winter. Nine names. Sera had found the list once, in her twenties, in a sealed drawer of the First Finder's office, and had read the names and felt nothing, because history is just names until it's yours.

The ninth name had been E. Voss.

She had told herself it was a coincidence. There must have been other Vosses. Saltmere was full of Vosses, probably, somewhere, statistically.

Now she knelt on the ice of the Gloam Court's vault antechamber and turned the pages of her grandmother's book with numb fingers, and the marginalia rose up to meet her like water rising in a locked room.

Edith's hand. Unmistakable. The same hand that had written Sera's birthday cards, cramped and slanting, pressing too hard.

*Page 12, beside the illustration of the frozen city:* We thought we were ending it. Remember that. Whatever they tell the grandchildren — we thought we were ENDING it.

*Page 19, beside the figure of the sovereign with its candleless crown:* It does not want worship. It wants punctuality. That is worse.

*Page 31, beside the drawing of a woman signing a contract, her face left blank:* We made her do it. Say it plainly, Edie. We took a desperate woman and we put her name on our debt because hers was the only hand that could hold the pen. The Hartwell woman. The mapmaker. She signed so the rest of us could sleep.

*Page 40, beside the image of a child casting no shadow:* And then we founded the finders. Not to hunt magic. To hunt HER. To make sure that if she ever remembered — if the memory ever started to grow back, the way memories do, like frost flowers on glass — there would be officers enough to stop her before she reached the mountain. We wrote the laws backward from our own crime. Every raid, every burning, every family broken in the Salt District — all of it, bookkeeping. All of it interest payments on what we stole.

And on the final page, beneath the verse Corwin Ash had quoted to Noor Hartwell across a table in a wine-shop, in letters gone brown and careful:

*Sera — if you are reading this, then either you disobeyed me and joined them, or the book found you some other way. If you joined them: look at the seal. Look at what the keys are doing. We did not build the Office to lock magic out of Saltmere. We built it to lock Saltmere's memory inside itself. You are not a hunter, my love. You are a collections agent. Go to the mountain. Undo it. — E.V.*

Sera sat back on her heels.

The nave was silent except for the child's breathing, small and steady, and the creak of frost growing on Corwin Ash's arm, and somewhere far above, the first soft detonations of the deferred winter beginning to land on the peak like artillery fired by a patient god.

"Finder," Dannel said. His voice had changed. He was reading over her shoulder — young men always read over shoulders, it was the first thing the Academy taught them and the last thing they unlearned — and his voice had changed. "Finder, is this—"

"Get out," Sera said.

"Ma'am?"

"Get out of this court, Dannel. Take the others. Ride down to Saltmere and don't stop at the garrison — go to the print-house on Candle Lane, the one with the green door, and ask for Maren. Tell her Voss sent you and the charter's a lie. Can you remember that? Green door. Maren. The charter's a lie."

He stared at her. Behind him, the five other finders stared at her, lanterns trembling, faces doing the arithmetic of mutiny.

"Dannel." She made her voice the voice that had broken up the Salt District riots, the voice that had talked down a possessed ferryman on the Winterquay. "In about an hour, everything you believe will be true and none of it will be survivable. Go be useful instead."

They went. Boots ringing back up the nave, faster than they'd come, the applause fading. Dannel paused at the threshold — she saw him look back, saw him mouth *green door* to himself, committing it like scripture — and then the gate swallowed him and it was done. Fourteen years, dismissed in a sentence.

"You knew," Hartwell said quietly. "Before tonight. How long?"

"Four years. Since your warrant crossed my desk and I recognized the address." Sera closed the book. Held it against her chest like a splint. "Your shop sits on the old archive. My grandmother hid your own past beneath your floorboards and then helped write the laws that would arrest you for finding it. When I understood that, I should have burned the Office down. Instead I sat on your file for four years and told myself I was protecting you." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Cowardice wears such convincing uniforms."

Corwin Ash lowered himself against the vault door, gray as the ice, the frost now past his elbow. "Voss. Your grandmother — the ninth signature. She tried to undo it?"

"She spent fifty years trying. Quietly. Smuggling records, hiding practitioners, feeding the congregation." Sera looked at the child, at Wren's coin-bottomed eyes, and something in her chest turned over. "And then she got old, and scared, and she hid the book under a charm shop and died asking me not to become her. And I became her anyway. The scared part. The hiding part." She stood. Drew her sword — and saw Hartwell flinch, and Ash push himself upright despite the frost, and almost smiled. "Relax. It's for the lock."

The vault door had no keyhole. It had a seam, hairline-fine, running floor to ceiling, and along the seam ran a line of salt fused into the ice — a salt-line, the oldest one, the master line, the account-book of the court itself rendered in mineral and cold. Sera had spent fourteen years learning to read salt-lines the way the Office taught it: as evidence, as accusation, as a thing to follow toward a burning.

Edith's marginalia had taught her the other way. The old way. The way the nine had known it before they lied about it.

A salt-line wasn't a chain. It was a *sentence* — grammar, not iron. And every sentence could be edited by anyone who understood what it said.

"Step back," she told them. "Both of you. And whatever happens — whatever comes out of that door when it opens — nobody signs anything. That's the whole law of this place. Nobody signs anything."

She laid the flat of the blade against the seam, and spoke the words her grandmother had written sideways in the margin of page 44, tiny, rotated ninety degrees, hidden even from the hidden: the words that were not a spell but a *reading*, a recitation of the line's own text back to itself, the way a mirror shows a liar his face.

The salt-line flickered.

The seam breathed open, exhaling air that smelled of candle smoke and old paper and, underneath it, faintly, unbearably, of a nursery — of milk and woodsmoke and a child's room a hundred years cold.

Inside, on a shelf of black ice, in a jar the size of a heartbeat, something glowed.

Wren made a sound. Not a word. A recognition — the sound a person makes finding their own reflection after years of mirrors that showed someone else.

And behind them, far above, the mountain finished clearing its throat, and the deferred winter began to fall in earnest — vast, total, a century of withheld snow descending all at once, and through the gate at the end of the nave Sera watched the sky go white as paper, and understood, with her grandmother's calm and her own rising terror, that whatever they took out of this vault, they would have to outrun the debt to keep it.

"Take your soul, child," Sera Voss said, and stood aside from the door she had spent her whole life guarding. "We're leaving. Now."
