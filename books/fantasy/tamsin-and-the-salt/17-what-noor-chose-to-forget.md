## 17. What Noor Chose to Forget

The memory did not come back all at once. It came back the way a map comes back to a cartographer — in layers, in ink and then in contour, in the slow flooding of a coastline that had been blank for twenty years.

Maren's hands were still on her shoulders. The confession was still hanging in the cold air of the congregation's underhall: *I helped erase you. I thought I was saving us.* And Noor should have been furious — some part of her was, a small hot coal banked low behind her ribs — but what she felt most of all, standing there with Maren's tears freezing on her own collarbone, was a terrible, vertiginous familiarity.

Because she had felt this before. Not remembered it. *Felt* it. For twenty years she had woken some mornings with the specific grief of a woman who has lost something enormous and agreed to lose it. She had never known why. Now she knew there was a why, and it was sitting in her skull like a door someone had bricked over from the inside.

"Take me back," Noor said.

Maren flinched. "Noor—"

"Not an apology. Not an explanation. Take me *back*. You put the wall up. You can show me where it stands."

"You don't understand what asking that costs." Maren's voice cracked down the middle. "The last time you stood at that wall, you begged us to do it. You *made* us. If I open it even a crack, the court will feel it. The debt is tied to your memory, Noor. That's how they hold the note. Your forgetting is their collateral."

"Then let them come collect," Noor said. "I'm done being a locked room."

Corwin was watching from across the hall, arms folded, salt-lines glimmering faintly along his forearms where he'd rolled his sleeves to work the ledger they'd stolen from the Undermarket broker. He didn't say anything. He'd learned, in the weeks since the blind date that had ended with witch-finders burning a district, when to be quiet around her. But he met her eyes, and he nodded once, and that nod said: *I'll catch you if this goes wrong.*

It would go wrong. She could feel it. She asked anyway.

* * *

They did it in the map room beneath the old chapel, because if memory was going to flood back into her, Maren wanted it to have somewhere to go. "You were always like water," Maren said, setting out the instruments with hands that shook only a little. "You fill whatever shape holds you. So we give you a shape."

The shape was a table. Her own table — the great scarred oak from her shop above, hauled down through the smuggler's tunnels on Corwin's back while Voss's patrols swept the frostline streets. On its surface, Noor laid out every map she owned. Not the trinket maps, not the charming fake coastlines she sold to tourists who wanted magic as decoration. The real ones. The ones she drew without knowing why, in the small hours, her hand moving with a certainty her mind had never been given.

Twenty years of them. Cities that weren't on any chart. Rivers that ran uphill. A mountain with a door drawn into its roots, over and over, dozens of times, in dozens of hands' worth of styles she'd cycled through so no one would notice the repetition.

She had never noticed the repetition either. That was the cruelest part. They'd cut the memory out and left the *habit*, like amputating a hand and leaving it twitching.

Maren lit the candles. Corwin stood at the door with his palm flat against the stone, reading the salt-lines in the walls the way other men read weather. "Nothing moving," he said. "Yet."

"That's very comforting," Maren muttered.

"Noor." Maren took both her hands. Hers were rough, a dyer's hands, a friend's hands, hands that had held Noor's through fevers and heartbreaks and the long gray years when Noor woke weeping and couldn't say why. "When it comes, it'll come like winter. All at once, and colder than you expect. Whatever you see — whoever you see — remember that she wasn't a stranger. She was you, and she was doing the best she could with a century she didn't ask for."

"A century," Noor repeated. The word still didn't fit in her mouth. "Say it plainly. How old am I?"

Maren's jaw worked. "A hundred and nineteen," she said. "Give or take the years you spent as ice."

"As *ice*?"

"I said what I said. Sit down."

Noor sat. Maren pressed her thumbs to Noor's temples and began to murmur — not the congregation's prayers, something older, something with salt in it — and the candle flames leaned toward Noor like a crowd leaning toward a stage.

Then the wall came down, and the century came through it.

* * *

She was standing in Saltmere, but it was not her Saltmere.

This city was loud. This city was *alive* — lantern-lit and singing, market stalls selling charms openly, witches walking the streets with their marks bare to the air, children learning cantrips at school desks beside their letters. Magic was not outlawed here. Magic was the *trade*. Saltmere before the laws, Saltmere before the fear, and Noor walked through it in a body that was older than her body, wearing a coat she didn't recognize, and everyone who passed her nodded. Respected her. Some of them looked afraid of her, and she understood, dimly, from inside the memory, that the fear was earned.

*Tamsin,* people called her. The name landed on her like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there. *Tamsin Hartwell. Tamsin, the surveyor. Tamsin, who maps the unmappable.*

And she — Noor, Tamsin, the woman split across a hundred years — was walking toward the mountain with a knife made of folded paper.

That was the first thing the memory gave her whole: the theft itself. She watched herself plan it with the same clarity she brought to coastlines. The Gloam Court held souls as collateral; that was its whole economy, the reason the winters came and went on schedule, the reason the court below the mountain traded in seasons the way merchants traded in grain. And among the souls in its vault, filed and shelved and accruing interest, was a child's. Wren — not this Wren, not the shadowless girl two doors down from Noor's shop, but Wren's grandmother, another child sold by another desperate family in another hard year, a hundred years ago. The court had held her soul for decades. The family had died waiting to buy her back. The debt had passed, as debts do, to strangers.

Tamsin Hartwell had decided that was obscene. Tamsin Hartwell had decided a lot of things were obscene.

Noor watched herself forge the map — because that was the trick, that had always been the trick: the court navigated by claim-lines, by the geometry of ownership, and a cartographer who could draw a realm that shouldn't exist could draw a *false* one. She drew a vault that wasn't there. She drew corridors that looped. She slipped the forgery into the court's own archives and walked in behind it like a thief walking in behind her own shadow.

For three hours, it worked. Noor lived those three hours at full speed — the candleless light, the sovereign's empty throne, the shelves of small bright flames each one a person, each one *waiting*. She found the child's flame on the ninety-first shelf. She wrapped it in paper. She turned to leave.

And the map betrayed her.

Not because it was badly drawn. Because the court had read it. Somewhere in those three hours, something in the dark had unfolded her forgery, studied her linework — her *hand*, unmistakable, the way a signature is unmistakable — and understood exactly who had come calling. The false corridors unmade themselves around her. The true ones closed like a fist.

She ran. Noor ran with herself, down halls of ice, clutching a paper-wrapped flame that guttered and screamed in a voice too small for the horror of it. She reached the door. The door was already shut. And the sovereign's voice came through the ice, gentle as frost forming:

*You cannot steal what is owed. But you can owe something greater.*

The bargain was offered in the memory exactly as the broker's contract had recorded it: the long winter deferred, the city spared, the child's soul kept as surety. And the price — the price Noor had never been able to read in anyone's account, because no one living had known it — was not the winter. The winter was the *consequence*, the loan coming due. The price was smaller and worse.

*Your memory,* the court said. *All of it. Every year. You will walk out of this mountain knowing nothing of what you are, and your forgetting will seal the note. As long as you do not remember, the debt sleeps. The moment you remember — truly remember, name yourself, know what you signed — the winter falls.*

And Tamsin Hartwell, kneeling on the ice with a stolen soul she could not carry out, had looked at the one thing the court had not counted on: the congregation. Her people. Forty witches and charm-makers and hedge-surveyors who would tear the mountain apart looking for her if she vanished, who would learn what she'd tried, who would follow her into the dark one by one until the court had a *collection* instead of a prisoner.

She could not carry the child's soul out. But she could make sure no one else came in after her.

So she chose oblivion. Not as defeat. As a *wall*.

Noor felt her former self do it — felt her set the memory down like a heavy pack at the top of a pass, felt her speak the words that handed a century of herself to the ice. *Let them forget me. Let me forget me. Let them think I died. As long as none of us remembers, none of us follows, and the winter stays buried.*

The last thing Tamsin saw, before the white took her, was her own hand drawing — reflexively, hopelessly, the way a drowning man swims — a small map on the floor of the vault. A map of a city that didn't exist yet. A shop with a cartographer's table. A life.

*If I must be no one,* she thought, *let me at least be someone kind.*

* * *

Noor came back to the map room gasping, both hands pressed flat on the oak, tears freezing in her lashes because the candles had gone blue with cold and no one had moved to warm them.

Maren was sobbing quietly, steadily, the way you cry at funerals you attended long ago. "You asked me once," she managed, "near the beginning — you asked me why you trusted me, and I couldn't answer. God forgive me, Noor, I've known you a hundred years and I let you ask me that."

"I know." Noor's voice came out scraped raw, but steady. Steadier than she expected. "I know why you did it. That's the worst part, Maren. I *watched myself order it*. I sat on that ice and I chose this. I chose the shop and the trinkets and the twenty blind years, because the alternative was all of you dying in the dark for my pride."

"And now?" Corwin, from the door. Quiet. His salt-lines were glowing brighter than she'd ever seen them, the walls themselves humming with what was coming.

"Now I know the terms better than the court does." Noor straightened. The coal of fury was still there, but it had changed shape — it was no longer aimed at Maren, or at her former self, or even at the court. It was aimed at the *bargain*. At the idea that a soul could be collateral. At the idea that forgetting was ever a fair price. "She wasn't a coward," Noor said, and meant it, and felt something in her chest finally knit closed. "She was a cartographer. She mapped the only route out she could see. But she only had one map. I've got twenty years of them, and a thief, and a witch-finder who owes us, and a girl two doors down with no shadow who has waited long enough."

Maren wiped her face. "The court will feel that you remember. The moment you named yourself —"

"I know."

"— the winter —"

"*I know.*" Noor was already rolling up the maps, fast and certain, her hands remembering movements older than her memory. "So we don't wait for it to fall on the city. We go to the mountain and we finish what she started. Properly, this time. With witnesses."

Corwin pushed off the doorframe. "The pass through the frostline closes within the day, once the cold comes down. If we're going, it's now."

Noor looked down at the topmost map on the stack — the oldest one, the one she'd drawn her first week in Saltmere without knowing why: a mountain with a door in its roots, and beneath it, in handwriting she finally recognized as her own, a single line she'd always taken for decoration.

*What is owed can be redrawn.*

"Tamsin," she said aloud, testing the name, letting it sit beside Noor like two cities on the same coast. Both hers. Both true. "All right, Tamsin. Let's go get our soul back."

Above them, through the chapel floor, through the streets, through the whole frozen spine of Saltmere, the temperature began to drop — and far away, beneath the mountain, something that had slept for a hundred years opened one eye.
