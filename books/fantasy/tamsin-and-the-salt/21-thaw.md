## 21. Thaw

**Noor**

The first thing to break was not the ice. It was the silence.

For three days after they fled the Gloam Court — three days of carrying Corwin's fading body down the pass while the deferred century fell behind them like a debt called in all at once — Saltmere had made no sound at all. No cart wheels. No gulls. No bells from the salt-cathedral, which had not rung in Noor's living memory and which she now understood had been silenced on purpose, a hundred years ago, so no one would count the hours of the winter that never ended.

Then, on the morning she laid Corwin on the table in her own shop and could not tell if he was breathing, the bells rang.

All of them. Every tower in the city, out of order, overlapping, ragged as a flock of birds startled off a wire.

Noor stood over him with Wren's soul cupped against her chest — a small warm weight wrapped in a square of her oldest map, the one of a coastline that did not exist — and listened to the city make noise for the first time in a century.

"Come back," she told Corwin. His lips were blue-gray. Frost had climbed his throat in patterns she recognized: the same whorls that decorated the prophecy's margins, the same script the court wrote its ledgers in. The frost was *writing* on him. Recording him. Entering his name into a debit column even now, even here, even with the vault emptied behind them.

She had surrendered her maps to buy every soul the court had ever claimed. She had watched the cathedral of ice go dark candleless light by candleless light. But the frost on Corwin's skin kept writing, patient as any clerk, because the court's ledger was not only in the court.

"No," she said. "You're paid for. You're *paid for*."

The frost finished its last character anyway. Then it stopped.

And the bells rang, and somewhere above the shop a roof shed its snow all at once with a sound like applause, and Corwin drew a breath — shallow, wrong, but a breath — and his eyes opened halfway, gray as thaw-water, and found her face.

He looked at her the way you look at a stranger who is holding something of yours.

"You were there," he said. His voice was gravel and water. "In the court. You were—" He frowned, reaching for it the way a man reaches for a word on the tip of his tongue. "I know your hands. I don't— I'm sorry. I know your hands."

"That's enough," Noor said, and was surprised to find it true. "That's enough for now."

**Wren**

Wren had not had a shadow since she was six years old, and so the first thing she did when her soul came back to her was stand very still in the middle of her mother's kitchen and watch it grow.

It came slowly, the way color comes back to a bruise. First a smudge under her heels, then a pale gray stain, then — as the strange warm wind came off the mountain and the snow on the windowsill began to weep — a long dark shape stretching across the floorboards toward the stove, toward the door, toward the whole bright terrifying ordinary world.

"Mama," she said. "Mama, look."

Her mother dropped a kettle. Wren would remember that always: the clang of it, the water spreading across the floor, her mother on her knees in the puddle with her arms around Wren's waist, sobbing into her coat, saying her name over and over like a word she'd forgotten how to pronounce.

Wren patted her mother's hair and kept watching the shadow. It moved when she moved. It leaned when she leaned. When she lifted her arm, it lifted an arm, thin and faithful, and Wren thought: *so that's what I look like.* She had spent two years being a girl without weather — no shadow, no breath-fog in the cold, no frost melting where she touched — and she had gotten very good at pretending not to mind, the way children get good at things adults never ask them to carry.

Now the frost on the window was melting in front of her eyes, and where her breath touched the glass it fogged, and she breathed on it deliberately, again and again, making little clouds, little ghosts, hers, hers, hers.

Outside, the whole street was doing the same thing. Doors opening. People standing in the slush in their nightclothes, faces tipped up to a sky that was finally, actually falling — rain, real rain, the first in a hundred years, drumming on the eaves and running brown down the gutters and steaming faintly where it hit the old packed ice.

Someone laughed. Then someone else. Then it caught, the way fire catches, until the whole street was laughing and crying and shouting names up at windows, and Wren stood in the doorway with her new shadow pooled around her feet like a returned dog, and thought about the woman with the kind tired eyes who had come to visit her sometimes and never once treated her like a haunted house.

*Tamsin,* someone had called that woman, down in the dark place. And the woman had said, *Noor.* And both names had fit her, the way Wren supposed two names could fit a person if a person had lived long enough.

Wren decided she would bring her something. She didn't know what yet. Something that melted.

**Sera Voss**

The charter burned badly.

Voss had expected that. Parchment written by the Gloam Court's own clerks a century ago, countersigned by men whose grandfathers' grandfathers had founded the witch-finders — such paper did not want to burn. It smoked and guttered and turned black at the edges while the letters stayed legible, and Voss stood in the brazier-lit yard behind the finding-house and held it with tongs like a stubborn knot of hair and waited.

Behind her, the finding-house stood empty. She had dismissed the order at dawn — all forty-one of them, standing in the yard in their gray coats with their salt-irons on their belts, watching her read aloud from the ledger pages Noor Hartwell had smuggled out of the mountain. The contract. The terms. The clause, buried in clerk's hand on the third page, that established *an office of enforcement within the city of Saltmere, funded by the court, empowered to suppress all magical practice save such practice as the contract itself required.*

They had not been founded to stop magic.

They had been founded to keep the winter running. To hunt down anyone strong enough to interfere with the loan, while leaving untouched the small charm-sellers and trinket-mongers whose harmless commerce made the outlawing of magic look like law instead of theft. Her grandmother's marginalia in the prophecy book — *ask who profits, Sera, ask who profits* — had been a warning aimed straight down a hundred years at her, and she had needed a cartographer and a dying man and a stolen child to finally read it.

The charter curled. The seal of the Gloam Court — a circle of nine candles that never lit — blackened and cracked.

"Who profits," Voss said quietly, to the smoke, to her grandmother, to nobody.

The parchment went up all at once then, the way things do when they finally decide, and she let the last corner fall into the brazier and watched the ash rise on the thaw-wind and drift out over the city.

Rain struck the back of her neck. She flinched — a hundred years of doctrine said weather like this was sorcery, was crime, was a finding waiting to be made — and then she made herself stand in it. Made herself feel it run down her collar. Rain. Just rain. The sky settling accounts.

Forty-one gray coats were scattered through the crowd below the finding-house steps, some weeping, some furious, some already pulling off their badges. One of them — young Pell, who'd joined at sixteen because he genuinely believed he was protecting people — stood at the bottom of the steps holding his salt-iron out flat on both palms like an offering, asking anyone who passed what he was supposed to be now.

Voss picked up her own badge from the yard where she'd dropped it, weighed it, and put it in her pocket instead of the bin.

Not yet thrown away. There were debts in that ledger besides souls, and someone with a finder's training ought to go through them line by line. But she walked out of the yard unarmed, and she took the long way to the charm-shop street, and she did not knock when she arrived. She just stood across the road in the rain, looking at the lit window, working up to something that might, eventually, with time, be called an apology.

**Corwin**

He remembered the mountain best.

That seemed wrong — the mountain was where the frost had taken him, where the ledger had begun writing him down — but memory is a strange tenant and pays its rent in whatever coin it likes. He remembered the pass in perfect detail: the way Noor had mapped it with her finger in the air before they entered, drawing doors in the rock that were only doors because she said so. He remembered the wind speaking in the court's grammar. He remembered the exact weight of Wren's soul through the map-wrapping, warm as a sleeping bird.

What he did not remember, reliably, was her.

There was a Noor-shaped hole in him with edges too precise to be natural. He knew her hands — he'd said so, the first stupid thing out of his mouth, and she'd taken it with more grace than he deserved. He knew the smell of her shop: salt, ink, beeswax. He knew that when she was frightened she drew, fast and small, in the margins of anything nearby, and that the drawings were always doors.

But the blind date — the wine, the terrible restaurant on Charm Street, the moment he'd quoted a verse he had no right to know — that was gauze. He could see shapes through it. He could not hold anything.

"It'll come back or it won't," Noor said. They were in her shop three days after the thaw began, and she was rehanging maps — new ones, drawn fresh, of streets that existed, because everything else she'd ever drawn was gone into the court's dark and she was learning what her gift felt like unburdened. "Maren says memory taken by bargain doesn't return by kindness. It returns by choice. Yours wasn't taken, though. Yours was *spent*. That's different."

"How?"

"I don't know." She hung the map. Stepped back. "Maybe it spends itself back."

He looked at her profile against the window, rain-bright, and felt the hole ache in its precise edges, and said the truest thing he had: "I'd rather meet you again than recover you. If it comes to that. I want you to know that's — I'm not grieving you. I'm looking forward to you."

Noor went very still. Then she laughed — a short, wet, disbelieving sound — and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes.

"A century ago," she said, "I apparently chose forgetting over letting people die for me. And you're sitting in my shop telling me you'd choose meeting over remembering." She shook her head. "This city. This *city*. Everyone in it keeps paying the wrong price and calling it love."

"Is it the wrong price?"

She considered that honestly, which he was learning was what she did — considered it the way she'd consider a coastline, testing it for false inlets.

"Ask me again in a year," she said. "When you've met me properly. Then we'll see whether the second version of us agrees with the first."

**Noor**

On the seventh day of the thaw, Wren came to the shop.

Noor heard the footsteps hesitate outside — a child's careful hesitation, learned from two years of being a thing people stared at — and then the bell over the door rang, and there she stood: solid, shadowed, dripping rain off a hood much too big for her, clutching something in both fists.

"I brought you a present," Wren announced. "It melts, so you have to take it now."

It was a snowball. Or what had been a snowball; it was mostly rainwater and grit by now, pooling in the child's cupped hands, and Noor took it with complete solemnity and let it run through her fingers onto the floorboards.

"Best gift I've had in twenty years," she said, and meant it, and Wren grinned with all her teeth like a girl who had just discovered her face could do that.

"The lady with the gray coat is standing across the street," Wren reported, with the tactlessness of the recently resurrected. "The one who used to arrest people. She's been there a long time. Are you going to let her in?"

Noor looked past her, through the wet glass. Sera Voss stood in the rain with her hands empty and her badge-less coat hanging open, and she did not look like a woman deciding whether to knock. She looked like a woman practicing.

"Not yet," Noor said. "But soon. Go home before your mother worries, Wren. And—" The child paused at the door, and Noor found she had to say it, had to give it to someone: "Your shadow looks very good on you."

Wren looked down at it, stretched long and dark across the threshold into the washed gold of the evening, and smiled again, softer this time.

"It remembers where I've been," she said, and went out into the rain.

Noor stood alone in her shop among the new maps, listening to the bells — still ringing, days on, as if the city couldn't stop counting hours it had lost — and beneath the bells, faint, from the direction of the mountain, she heard something else.

A knocking. Slow. Patient. Three blows, pause, three blows, coming up through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the archive beneath her shop where a sealed book had once waited a hundred years for her to open it.

The court was dark. The vault was empty. The ledger was paid.

But mountains are old things, and old things keep rooms.

Noor picked up her pen, and on the freshest map — the one of Saltmere, the one she was drawing for the first time without hiding — she began, carefully, to draw a door where the mountain used to be.
