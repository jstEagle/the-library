## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with living in a place where miracles can't happen is that you stop checking for them.

I'd been tending my grandmother's candle shop for six years — measuring beeswax, trimming wicks, arguing with the delivery boy about the price of tallow — and in all that time the strangest thing to occur on Candle Row was the winter Mrs. Aldous's cat learned to open the latch. So when I say the valley began to crack at its seams that autumn, I want it understood that I noticed the way you notice a hairline fracture in a teacup: late, reluctantly, and only after the tea had already scalded me.

It started with the candles. Which sounds like a joke I'm making at my own expense, but hear me out.

Grandmother Vale had a rule she repeated so often it wore grooves into the family vocabulary: *We sell light, not luck.* No blessing the wicks, no whispering intentions over the wax, no selling "prayer candles" the way they did downriver where people still believed the gods bothered with small transactions. The Hollow Valley had been dead to magic for as long as anyone could remember — that was its whole selling point, if a valley can be said to have one. People came here to get away from altars and omens and the particular exhaustion of being watched from above. We were, collectively, off the gods' mailing list, and proud of it.

So when old Tam Hedley shuffled into the shop on a grey Tuesday and asked, embarrassed, for seven white candles "for a vigil," I didn't think anything of it. Vigils are just grief with a schedule. I wrapped them in brown paper and charged him full price because sentiment doesn't discount.

What I didn't know — what nobody told me until three days later, when the whole row was buzzing like a struck bell — was that Tam hadn't been holding a vigil for his wife, who'd been dead eleven years and needed no further attention. He'd been praying. At the old shrine up past the mill, the moss-eaten one everyone used as a bench, he'd set those candles in a half-circle and whispered a prayer into hands too stiff to fold properly, asking — this is the part that made my skin crawl when I heard it — asking not for anything grand. Just one more autumn. One more season of apples and his wife's sister's letters and the smell of woodsmoke.

Tam Hedley had been dying since spring. The cough, the weight loss, the doctor from over the pass shaking his head with professional regret. Everyone knew. Everyone had already begun the terrible arithmetic of funerals.

And then, three days after the candles, Tam walked into the market square carrying two sacks of apples like they weighed nothing, pink-faced and loud, and announced to anyone within earshot that he felt *marvelous*.

The valley did what the valley does with anything unusual: it absorbed the event, chewed it thoroughly, and produced a consensus. Bad diagnosis, said some. Good luck, said others. The shrine, whispered a few, and then looked around quickly, as though naming it might summon something.

I kept wrapping candles. But I'll admit I started listening differently.

* * *

Here's what you have to understand about the Hollow Valley: we are not merely far from anywhere. Distance is cheap; there are plenty of far places. What we are is *closed*. The mountains don't just wall us in physically — ask any traveler who's come over the pass how strange the last mile feels, like walking through cold water, like a door easing shut behind them. Magic doesn't fail here dramatically, with sparks and screams. It fails politely. A storm-caller raises his hand and gets nothing but damp fingers. An oracle stares into her bowl and sees only soup. The gods, whatever they are, look at our little green bowl of a valley and see a blank page.

That was the covenant, or so the oldest stories go — not that I ever put much stock in stories. Grandmother Vale certainly didn't. She was the least mystical woman ever to make her living adjacent to religion-adjacent goods. She believed in hard work, exact measurements, and paying debts before they could grow teeth. When she died she left me the shop, the house above it, a recipe book written in her cramped hand, and one piece of advice delivered from her deathbed with the gravity of scripture:

"Tamsin. If anyone ever asks you about your birthmark, lie."

I was twenty-three and grief-stupid and I laughed, thinking it was a joke, thinking she was being funny the way she was sometimes funny — flat and unexpected, like a stone in your shoe. She wasn't laughing. Her hand, papery and strong, closed around my wrist right over the mark itself: the pale whorl on the inside of my left forearm that I'd had since birth, which Mother Aldous the midwife had squinted at and pronounced "a key, or maybe a fern," and which I had spent my entire life treating as an interesting flaw. Nothing more. A birthmark is just the body's handwriting, isn't it? Some people get port-wine stains. I got what looked like someone had tried to sign me and given up halfway through.

"About my birthmark," I'd repeated. "Gran, who on earth would—"

"Lie anyway." And then she'd closed her eyes and, being Gran, had done even dying efficiently, and was gone before I could ask a single follow-up question.

Six years I'd carried that instruction like a stone in my pocket, feeling its weight occasionally and never once needing it. Nobody had ever asked about my birthmark. Why would they? It was a smudge. It was nothing.

You can see where this is going. I could see where it was going, eventually, and I still walked straight into it, because that's the other thing about the valley: it teaches you that nothing ever happens, until everything does.

* * *

The second wrongness came through Widow Marsh, who is the closest thing our town has to a newspaper with legs. She came in for church candles — actual ones, for the chapel, which buys in bulk and pays late — and stayed to talk, as she always does, standing exactly where customers stand so I can't escape without rudeness.

"You've heard about Tam Hedley," she said. Not a question. In the valley, news arrives pre-digested.

"I heard he's carrying apples again."

"He planted them himself. Carried the saplings up the hill in March, coughing the whole way, poor man, saying he wanted to taste fruit from trees he'd put in the ground. Well." She let that hang there, delicately, like a hanged man. "And that's not the half of it. You know the Fenwick baby? Born blue, they said, heart all wrong, the midwife sent for the doctor over the pass and the doctor said prepare yourselves. Well, the Fenwicks went out to that old shrine — *that* old shrine, the mossy one — and sat all night doing their whispering, and I'm told the child screamed the whole sky pink at dawn and hasn't stopped screaming since, which in this case is considered a *good* sign."

I trimmed a wick. My hands were steady, which surprised me. "People recover, Mrs. Marsh. Babies especially. They're stubborn."

"Mm." She looked at me the way she looks at jam jars she suspects of hiding something. "And the Coles' well. Sweet water again, after four years of sulfur. They prayed over it. And Hesta Vane's eyesight, coming back at her age, after she lit a candle — one of yours, dear, I recognized the wax — and asked nicely."

"One of mine," I said. "Mrs. Marsh, I sell candles. I don't sell outcomes."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But isn't it funny how many outcomes have been happening lately? Almost like somebody's been reading the mail." And she paid for her candles, counted out the coins one by one to maximize my suffering, and left.

I stood in my quiet shop surrounded by several hundred sticks of wax and felt, for the first time in my life, that the silence of the Hollow Valley was less like peace and more like held breath.

Because here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I'd been not-saying to myself since Tam Hedley carried in his apples: the night before his vigil, I'd dreamed of fire. A tower burning against a black sky, and a woman's voice calling a name that wasn't Tamsin — a name that slid off my memory like water off wax, gone before I could grip it — and I'd woken with my heart hammering and my left arm itching so fiercely I'd scratched it raw. And the mark had been warm. Warm, in a valley where nothing magical has ever been warm, or anything else at all.

Coincidence. I told myself it was coincidence. I was very firm about it, the way you're firm with a dog begging at the table.

* * *

The stranger arrived on a Thursday, which I note only because Thursdays are slow and therefore I remember everything about him with humiliating clarity.

He came in out of weather that couldn't decide between rain and sleet, bringing the smell of the high pass with him — pine and cold iron and something underneath, faintly electric, like the air before lightning. He was tall in the way that suggests he's tired of ducking through doorways, with grey shot through dark hair and a face that had been handsome before life got its opinions in. His coat was good quality and badly worn, mended in places with thread that didn't match, and he moved like a man who checks exits as a matter of course.

He also stopped dead in my doorway, one boot over the threshold, and stared at me with an expression I can only describe as *recognition arriving at speed*.

I've thought about that moment a great deal since. At the time, I did what any sensible shopkeeper does with a large strange man looming: I reached for the poker by the stove and smiled with my teeth.

"Candles?" I said. "Or were you looking for the chapel?"

His gaze dropped — deliberately, I realized later, the way you'd lower a weapon — to my hands, to the left forearm where my sleeve had ridden up while I gripped the poker. To the mark. Something crossed his face that was too complicated to be surprise and too hungry to be relief.

"Just candles," he said, and his voice was low and roughened, an accent from somewhere over the mountains, somewhere magic still worked. "White ones. Seven, if you have them."

"We sell a lot of sevens lately." I let go of the poker and pulled candles from the rack, keeping the counter between us, mostly out of habit and slightly out of instinct. "Vigil?"

Something flickered in his expression. "You could say that. Though the person I'd sit vigil for isn't dead yet." He laid coins on the counter — real silver, worn smooth, older than any coinage I knew — and then, instead of taking the paper bundle, he rested one finger on it and asked, casually, terribly:

"This is the Hollow Valley, isn't it. Where prayers don't get answered."

"That's generally considered the attraction."

"And yet." He turned the bundle slowly, like a man turning a key. "Three answered prayers in one month, in a valley that's been silent for centuries. That's not weather, mistress candlemaker. That's a pattern. And patterns—" his eyes came back up to mine, grey as slate and twice as heavy, "—patterns mean something woke up."

My mouth was dry. "If you've come to sell doom, the chapel takes donations on Sundays."

A ghost of a smile. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Do you know," he said, "I've crossed two ranges and a salt flat chasing rumors, and every lead went cold until I walked through that door. Funny thing about this valley — my trade doesn't work here. Hasn't worked since I crossed the pass. Dead as a doornail, and I confess I'd forgotten how that feels." He picked up his candles at last. "But standing next to you, just now, I could almost swear I felt it twitch."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, sleet ticked against the window in counterpoint, and the whole ordinary world of the Hollow Valley — the wax and the wicks and the safe, blessed silence — seemed to lean in and listen.

"I think you should go," I said quietly.

"I think I will." He paused at the door, rain-light behind him, and looked back with an expression I'd spend weeks trying to name and finally would: it was guilt. Old, load-bearing guilt, the kind a man builds his whole posture around. "One question, and then I'm gone. Have you ever had anyone ask about your birthmark?"

Every drop of blood in my body went cold and attentive, like a deer hearing a snapped twig. Six years of a dead woman's warning rose up in my throat, and I opened my mouth to do exactly as she'd instructed—

—and found, to my lasting horror, that I hesitated. Because under the fear, under the loyalty to a grandmother six years gone, something in me — something old and buried and patient — *wanted* to answer. Wanted it the way a locked room wants a key.

The stranger saw the hesitation. Of course he did. He was a man who made his living noticing things, whatever that living was.

"That's what I thought," he said softly. "Seven candles, and I never gave you my name. It's Eriksen. Dominic Eriksen. When the wardens come asking — and they will, soon, probably tonight — remember that I bought candles and asked nothing else." He tipped his head, almost a bow. "Lock your door, mistress candlemaker. Whatever woke up knows your address."

And then he was gone into the sleet, and I stood alone in my grandmother's shop with a poker in one hand and a lie half-formed in my throat, watching the door swing, understanding with perfect, useless clarity that the valley had finished holding its breath —

— and that the exhale was going to take everything with it.
