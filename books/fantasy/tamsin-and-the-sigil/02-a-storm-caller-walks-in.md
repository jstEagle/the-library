## 2. A Storm-Caller Walks In

The rain stopped at the valley's edge like a servant dismissed.

I felt it go — the way you feel a limb fall asleep, that slow staticky withdrawal of something that had been yours. One stride I was soaked to the bone with three days of mountain weather I'd been carrying in my knuckles like coins; the next, the drops simply refused to land on me. They hung a half-inch off my skin, confused, and then fell away into the mud as if they'd never been called at all.

I stood there on the boundary stone with my hands still half-raised, fingers curled around nothing, and listened to the silence where the storm should have been.

"Right," I said to no one. "That's welcoming."

The Hollow Valley didn't answer. It never does. That's rather the point of it.

Let me back up, because I can hear you asking the obvious question: why would Dominic Eriksen, exiled storm-caller, breaker of covenants, man whose name is still spoken in certain temples the way sailors speak of reefs — why would such a man walk voluntarily into the one place in the world where his magic doesn't work?

Because of a rumor. Because of a prayer.

Three weeks ago, in a dockside tavern in Saltmere, I'd heard a pilgrim woman tell her companion that her brother's fever broke the night she whispered his name at a roadside shrine — a shrine in the Hollow Valley, where prayers are supposed to go nowhere, where the whole architecture of the place is built on the principle that the gods cannot hear. She'd said it lightly, the way people say things that don't matter, and then she'd laughed and said *and him not even sorry for what he did to me*, and gone back to her ale.

I hadn't laughed. I'd set down my cup very carefully, because my hands had started shaking, and I'd asked her — casually, I hoped — which shrine.

She'd looked at me like I was mad. Everyone looks at me like I'm mad eventually. It's one of my more consistent qualities.

Here's the thing about answered prayers: they're not supposed to happen anymore. Not since the covenant broke. Not since *I* broke it. The realms went quiet, the gods went deaf, and the world settled into a long gray convalescence where you lit your candles and said your words and understood, in your bones, that nobody was listening. That was the bargain's ugly mercy — the gods couldn't reach us, but neither could they demand anything of us. Silence, both directions.

And now prayers were being answered in a valley specifically designed to be proof against divine attention. Which meant either the valley was failing, or something inside it was waking up, or — and this was the possibility that kept me walking north through the mountains with wet boots and a bad attitude — someone had left a door open. A door I might recognize, because I'd helped break the lock.

So. The Hollow Valley. Where my lightning wouldn't come when I called it.

I want to be honest about what that feels like, because people romanticize powerlessness from a safe distance. It doesn't feel like freedom. It feels like being naked in a crowd. For eleven years, the storm has been the first thing I reach for — before words, before weapons, before sense. When a stranger walks toward me on a dark road, some part of me is already counting the heartbeats until the sky answers. Walking into the valley was like walking with my hands tied behind my back into a room full of people who might have reasons to hate me.

Which, as it turned out, was exactly what it was.

* * *

The town was called Emberlow, which struck me as optimistic naming for a place that ran on candlelight and suspicion. One street, mostly. A shrine at each end — small stone affairs, moss-eaten, their offering bowls dry as old bread. A chandlery with a painted sign of a flame that had faded to the color of weak tea. And everywhere, that silence. Not quiet — quiet is just an absence of noise, and I've slept in graveyards quieter than most cities. This was *silence*. A held breath. The feeling of a room where something has been locked away and everyone has agreed, without ever discussing it, not to look at the lock.

I'd been in the valley perhaps two hours when I saw the first wrongness.

A boy — eight, maybe nine, knees scabbed, chasing a hoop down the street — tripped over a root and went sprawling hard enough that I winced in sympathy. His mother came running, gathered him up, and I watched her lips move over his scraped palms. A prayer. The old kind, automatic, the kind mothers have said over hurt children since before the covenant, since before the gods, probably. *Mend him. Mend him.*

And the scrapes *faded*. Not healed — healed takes time, takes a glow, takes the whole theatrical apparatus of divine intervention. They faded, like ink lifted out of cloth, quick and casual, and the boy squirmed free of his mother's arms and went back to his hoop without so much as a thank-you, and the mother stood there a moment with her hands pressed over her mouth.

Then she looked around — quickly, guiltily, the way you check whether anyone saw you drop something — and hurried inside.

I stood in the shadow of the chandlery's awning and felt the bottom drop out of my stomach.

Because here's what I knew that she apparently didn't: that kind of healing isn't charity. Nothing the gods do is charity. Every answered prayer is a contract, every miracle a signature on a line, and the terms are written somewhere you can't read until it's too late. I had spent six years learning that lesson in the most expensive classroom there is. I had a sister who paid the tuition.

I needed to know who was signing contracts in this valley. And I needed to know it before whoever it was learned that Dominic Eriksen was standing in their marketplace with no lightning in his pockets.

So I did what any sensible infiltrator would do. I bought a candle.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of beeswax and tallow and something underneath both — a faint mineral sharpness, like the air after lightning, which made the hair on my arms stand up for reasons I chose not to examine. Bells over the door. Shelves of dipped tapers in ranks of white and cream, bundles of rushlights, a jar of wicks. Ordinary. Aggressively ordinary. The shop seemed to be trying very hard to be ordinary, the way a spy tries very hard to look bored.

Behind the counter stood a girl.

I'm going to describe her badly, because describing her well would require admitting what I noticed first, and what I noticed first was the way the light sat wrong on her. Candlelight loves people — it flatters, it pools, it makes conspirators of us all. But on this girl the light behaved differently. It gathered. Along her forearms, at the hollow of her throat, along the edge of her jaw, as if her skin were faintly, imperceptibly *attractive* to it. As if she were a low place where illumination collected.

She was maybe twenty, dark-haired, sleeves rolled, wax stains on her fingers, with the wary set of someone who'd spent her whole life in a town of four hundred faces and had recently started finding some of them strange. There were shadows under her eyes that spoke of dreams she hadn't enjoyed.

"You're not from here," she said. Not hostile. Just factual, the way you'd note the weather.

"Is it the boots?" I said. "People always say it's the boots."

"It's everything." Her eyes did a quick inventory of me — travel-worn coat, salt-stained hem, the particular lean look of a man who'd walked a long way from something. "We don't get many travelers. The pass is closed half the year."

"I'm told the other half isn't much better."

"Then why—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Shopkeeper's discipline. "Candles? We've got tallow, beeswax, church-pure if you're— " a small hesitation, "—if you're the praying sort."

"And if I'm not?"

"Everyone's the praying sort eventually," she said, and there was something under the flippancy, something tired and raw, that told me she'd been having a week. "It's just a question of what for."

I asked for beeswax, two tapers, and paid with coin that had crossed three borders to get here. She counted change into my palm and I watched her hands, and that's when I saw it.

On the inside of her left wrist, half-hidden by a pushed-up sleeve: a birthmark. Thin lines, fine as etching, in a pattern that was almost — *almost* — decorative. A loop, a crossbar, a descending stroke. People have birthmarks shaped like rabbits and continents and, in one memorable case I witnessed in Corvane, an extremely rude word. This wasn't that. This was precise. This was *drafted*.

I knew it.

Gods forgive me, I knew it the way you know a face from a wanted poster — instantly, horribly, with the lurch of recognition that comes right before the lurch of fear. I'd seen that mark before, but not on skin. On vellum. In the archive vaults beneath the Temple of the Sundered Oath, in the years when I still had clearance and still had a soul, in the margins of the oldest copies of the covenant text. The binding script. The clauses that held the pact between realms together, drawn in a hand older than any living language.

They weren't supposed to appear on people. They were supposed to appear on *nothing*, ever again, because the covenant was broken and its script was dead letter, and I would know, because I'm the one who killed it.

She caught me looking. Of course she did. You don't spend years keeping secrets in a small town without developing a sense for being watched.

"What?" she said, and pulled her sleeve down, too fast, too deliberate.

"Nothing," I said. "Sorry. It's a striking mark."

"It's a birthmark."

"Mm." I picked up my candles with a hand that was, I'm pleased to report, almost steady. "Does it have a shape? Some of them do. My grandmother had one shaped like a fish."

"It doesn't have a shape." A beat. Then, quieter, in the tone of someone confessing to a priest who doesn't exist: "My gran says it looks like a key."

The floor of the world tilted, very slightly, and settled.

A key. Yes. I could see it now that she'd named it — the loop the bow, the crossbar the bit, the descending stroke the shaft. A key drawn in binding-script, on the wrist of a candlemaker's granddaughter in a valley built to keep the gods out, while prayers got answered in the street outside and the silence that was supposed to be permanent developed cracks.

I am not, whatever my reputation suggests, an impulsive man. Impulsive men die young in my profession. Everything in me said: put the candles down, walk out, walk back over the pass, find a deep hole, and let whatever is happening in this valley happen to someone else. I had broken one world already. I did not need to watch it happen twice.

But I thought of my sister's name, which I haven't spoken aloud in six years because saying it feels like pressing on a wound to check if it still hurts. It does. It always does. And I thought: if there is a key in this valley, then somewhere there is a lock, and somewhere there is a hand turning it, and every prayer answered in that street was a coin changing sides in a war I started.

"Actually," I said, "could I trouble you for a third candle?"

Her eyebrows rose. "Planning a vigil?"

"Planning ahead." I paid her. Our fingers didn't touch, but the air between our hands did something odd — a faint pressure, like the moment before thunder, there and gone. She felt it too. I watched her feel it. Watched her glance down at her wrist, then at me, with an expression I recognized intimately because I wore it in mirrors for years after the Sundering: the look of someone who has just noticed a door in a wall they'd believed was solid.

"You felt that," she said.

"Felt what?"

"Don't." No anger in it. Almost pleading. "Please don't do that. Everyone in this town does that. Something strange happens and we all agree not to have seen it, and we've gotten so *good* at it, and I'm so tired of being good at it."

There it was. The crack in the silence, sitting across the counter from me, smelling of beeswax.

"Three days ago," she went on, before I could decide whether to run, "old Marta prayed at the east shrine. She's dying — was dying, everyone knows, her lungs were going like wet paper. And the next morning she was hauling water buckets like a girl of thirty." Her voice dropped. "And yesterday a stranger came through asking questions. About a girl born in this valley with a mark shaped like a key. He asked at the shrine, at the Warden's office, at the tavern. And last night he was gone, and this morning the Warden posted men at the pass, and nobody will say why, and Marta won't leave her house, and I—" She stopped. Drew breath. Looked at me with those light-gathering eyes. "You knew what my birthmark was. Before I said anything. I watched your face."

I should have lied. I want that on the record, for whatever record survives: I *should* have lied. Six years of exile had taught me exactly one survival skill worth the name, and it was lying beautifully to people who deserved better.

"The mark on your wrist," I said instead. "Has it always looked like that? Exactly that? Or has it changed lately?"

Something flickered across her face — fear, yes, but under it, terribly, hope. "Changed how?"

"Like it's being written," I said. "Rather than drawn."

The bells over the door chimed. Neither of us had touched them.

We both looked. The door stood shut. Outside, the street lay empty in the flat gray light, and at the far end of it, by the east shrine, the air had that particular shimmer I'd seen only once before — the day the covenant broke, the day the sky over the Temple of the Sundered Oath tore like wet paper and everything I loved went with it.

The girl's hand had gone to her wrist. She was staring at it. And though the sleeve covered it, I swear to you I could see the light gathering there, brightening, line by line, like ink rising through parchment —

— like something, on the far side of the world, taking up a pen.

"Tell me," she said quietly, not looking up, "exactly what you know about keys."

And I, Dominic Eriksen, who had sworn on my sister's grave to never again stand between gods and their appetites, heard myself say:

"It's going to take more than one candle."
