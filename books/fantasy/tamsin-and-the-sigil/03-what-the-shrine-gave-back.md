## 3. What the Shrine Gave Back

The trouble with living in a valley that forgets is that you learn to stop asking questions. It's not a flaw in the character of the Hollow — it's practically the civic religion. Somebody's cow wanders off? The valley forgot where it went. A road you'd swear was there last summer turns out to have always been a streambed? The valley forgot the road. My grandmother used to say the Hollow Valley was like an old man with a head full of holes: he'll tell you a story twice and mean it both times.

I had inherited her candle shop, her cottage above it, and her talent for not asking questions, which I'd always assumed was the least valuable of the three until this week, when it started to feel less like a talent and more like a symptom.

It began with Widow Marsh's bees.

I know how that sounds. Bear with me. Old Marta Marsh kept hives at the edge of her garden for forty years, and every autumn the valley took its tithe — a few hives gone quiet, a few more gone wandering, and Marta shrugging and saying the valley forgot them, same as everything else. But on Tuesday morning she came into the shop for altar candles — we sold those too, though nobody had lit one at a shrine in my lifetime — and she stood at the counter counting out coppers with hands that shook so badly the coins chattered against each other like teeth.

"Bad winter coming for me," she said, not looking up. "Doctor Bram says my heart's got a leak in it. Says I should put my affairs in order." She snorted. "My affairs fit in a biscuit tin."

"I'm sorry," I said, because what do you say?

She looked up then, and her eyes were wet and furious. "I'm not ready, Tamsin. That's the thing nobody tells you about dying. You can be ninety-one years old and still not be *ready*." She pushed the coppers across the counter. "The black ones. For the shrine."

"Marta, nobody's prayed at that shrine since before I was born."

"Somebody has to start," she said, "if anybody's going to finish."

I wrapped the candles in brown paper and told myself the chill down my spine was just a draft from the door. In the Hollow Valley, you learn to blame drafts.

* * *

She went up to the shrine that evening. I know because half the village saw her go, leaning on her stick, moving at the speed of continental drift, and everyone agreed afterward that they'd all thought the same thing: *poor woman, let her have her ritual, it does no harm.* The shrine sits on the hill behind the mill — a ring of standing stones older than any record, worn smooth as soap, with a flat altar-stone in the middle where moss grows in patterns too regular to be accidental. When I was small, Grandmother caught me stacking pebbles there once and made me take them down, and when I asked why, she said, "Because the valley forgets, Tamsin, but stones remember," and then she wouldn't explain further, which was very much her style.

Marta knelt at that stone with our black candles and prayed. We know what she prayed for because she told everyone, loudly, the way she told everyone everything: she asked whatever was listening to give her one more spring. Just one. She wanted to see the bees come back up out of the hives.

And here is where I have to stop narrating things I witnessed and start narrating things the whole village witnessed, because by Thursday morning Marta Marsh was splitting firewood.

Not metaphorically. Actually splitting firewood. Doctor Bram examined her three times, drew blood he had no way to test properly, listened to her heart with his little brass instrument until his ears must have ached, and finally pronounced, in the baffled tone of a man whose entire profession has just been politely mugged: "There's no leak. There never was a leak. Either I misdiagnosed her or—" and here he stopped, because the alternative sentence doesn't exist in the Hollow Valley. Things don't get better here. The valley forgets; it doesn't fix.

Marta, for her part, was insufferable about it. "Told you somebody had to start," she said to me over the fence, glowing like one of my own best candles. "You should try it, girl. Whatever's up there, it's *listening* now."

"It's probably just the doctor being wrong," I said.

"Probably," she agreed cheerfully, in the tone of someone who absolutely did not believe it, and handed me a jar of honey as if I were the one who'd been healed.

That night I stood in the shop after closing, turning a black candle over in my hands, and felt something I hadn't felt since childhood: the specific vertigo of a floorboard that might or might not hold. Because here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I've been circling like a moth around a flame it distrusts. Three days ago, a stranger came into this shop asking about a girl with a birthmark shaped like a key. And yesterday morning, when I was washing at the basin, I caught sight of that birthmark in the mirror — the one I've had since birth, low on my left shoulder blade, the one the midwife supposedly remarked on, the one I've stopped seeing the way you stop seeing your own nose —

— and it wasn't where I remembered it.

It had moved. Halfway up my spine now, and finer-lined, sharper, like someone had re-inked it while I slept. And I had stood there dripping, staring at my own back in the spotted mirror, and done what any sensible daughter of the Hollow Valley would do.

I decided it had always been there, and I was misremembering.

The valley forgets. I'd simply forgotten along with it. That was the story. That was a fine, load-bearing story, and I carried it around for two days, and it held my weight, right up until the night I dreamed of the burning tower.

* * *

I don't dream often, and when I do it's the usual inventory: the shop flooding with wax, wolves made of shadow, teeth. Ordinary nonsense. This was not that.

In the dream I was high up. That was the first fact, and it arrived with terrible certainty, the way facts do in dreams — not learned but *known*, the way you know water is wet. I was in a tower, a tall one, white stone banded with metal that glowed faintly like banked coals, and I knew the tower's name the way you know your own name, except I couldn't hold onto it; it slid away whenever I reached for it, like a fish under ice.

The tower was burning.

Not all at once — that was the horror of it, the slow horror. The fire climbed floor by floor with the patience of something following instructions, and below me I could see figures spilling out onto the grass, tiny as ants, and none of them were looking up. None of them were coming for me. And I understood, in the dream's merciless grammar, that this was correct. That no one was coming because no one was *allowed* to come, because I had been left there on purpose, the way you leave a seal on a letter.

A woman's voice came through the smoke. Not shouting. Worse than shouting — calm, the way you speak to someone you love when you're doing something unforgivable to them and want them to understand it's necessary.

"*Seryth*," the woman said. "Look at me, Seryth. You won't remember this. That's the mercy of it. You'll wake up somewhere green and quiet, and you'll be happy, and you'll never once wonder what you paid for it. Say you forgive me."

And dream-me — who was me, who was wearing my own hands, except my hands were covered in light, lines of it winding up my wrists and forearms like ivy made of dawn — dream-me opened my mouth to answer her, and I still don't know what I said, because that's exactly when I woke.

I woke the way you surface from deep water: gasping, ears ringing, the cottage dark around me and my heart going like a hammer on a nail. Gray pre-dawn light through the shutters. The smell of last night's fire, cold now. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly where I'd left it.

Except my wrist.

There was a mark on the inside of my left wrist, just below the palm. I lay there staring at it in the gray light for a long time, telling myself it was ink, a stain, a bruise taking shape. It was none of those things. It was a line of script — no, not script, something older than script, a sigil, a closed loop with three barbs like a fishhook drawn by someone who'd never seen a fish but had heard them described by a liar. And it *glowed*. Faintly, the way the coals in the tower's bands had glowed, a dim gold pulse beneath my skin, brightening and fading in rhythm.

It took me an embarrassing amount of time to realize the rhythm matched my heartbeat.

I sat up. I walked to the basin on legs that belonged to someone else. I turned my wrist in the gray light and watched the mark breathe with my pulse, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do for three days:

A stranger asks about a girl with a key-shaped birthmark. A dead woman's prayer gets answered for the first time in living memory. A birthmark migrates. A dream arrives wearing someone else's name — *Seryth* — and fits me like a glove. And now my own skin is writing itself without my permission.

The valley forgets. Fine. But I was starting to suspect the valley didn't forget on its own. I suspected the valley had been *made* to forget, the way you muzzle a dog, the way you seal a letter — and that whatever was sealed inside it had recently begun, very quietly, to lick through the wax.

Grandmother's voice, from years ago, from some ordinary evening shelling peas: *"Stones remember, Tamsin."* And I'd laughed and asked what stones had to remember, and she'd looked at me with an expression I'd spent my whole life filing under *old people are tired* and was only now, holding my glowing wrist up to the dark window, re-filing under something considerably worse.

Pity. She'd looked at me with pity.

* * *

I didn't open the shop that morning. First time in six years. I hung the CLOSED sign — a board Grandmother painted, the letters faded to ghosts — and I sat on the stool behind the counter with all the black candles arranged in front of me like a jury, and I waited to feel ridiculous.

I didn't feel ridiculous. That was the alarming part.

Because here's what kept surfacing, between the fear: fragments. Not memories exactly — memories have weight and context, doors and weather. These were more like the *shapes* memories leave behind. The sensation of stone under bare feet, warm from sun. The certainty that I had once known the names of seven things that are forbidden to name. A smell — ozone and crushed herbs — that hit me so hard I gripped the counter. And underneath it all, persistent as tinnitus, a word that wasn't quite a word: *covenant.*

I knew the word, obviously. Everyone knows the word. It shows up in scripture and wedding vows and the names of about four taverns in the free cities. But the way it surfaced in me wasn't knowledge. It was *recognition*. The way you recognize a face across a market crowd — instantly, totally, before you can say why.

Around noon, someone knocked on the shop door. I nearly jumped out of my skin. Through the glass I saw Marta Marsh, radiant and insufferable, holding another jar of honey, and I waved her off with a smile I hoped read as *indisposed* rather than *unhinged*, and she went, and I realized my wrist had been behind my back the whole conversation without my deciding to put it there.

Hiding. I was already hiding it. From the neighbors. From the valley.

From whoever came next asking about girls with key-shaped birthmarks.

By late afternoon I'd made a decision, or rather the decision had made itself while I wasn't looking, the way decisions do when you're too frightened to make them honestly. Tomorrow I would walk up to the shrine. I would stand on the hill behind the mill, among the stones that remember, and I would look at the altar-stone with its moss growing in patterns too regular to be accidental, and I would ask — out loud, in plain words, no candles, no kneeling — what it was that had started listening, and why it kept writing on me.

And if nothing answered, fine. If nothing answered, I'd go home, tell myself a fine load-bearing story, and carry on selling candles to people who'd forgotten what they were for.

But I didn't think nothing would answer.

Because as the light failed and I stood to light the lamp, my wrist pulsed — once, hard, unbidden, like a knock from the inside — and across the street, in the gathering dusk, every lantern in the Hollow Valley flickered in perfect unison.

All of them. At once. Like something enormous drawing breath.

Like something answering before I'd even finished asking.
