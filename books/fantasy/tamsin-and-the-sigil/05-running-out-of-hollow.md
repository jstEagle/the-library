## 5. Running Out of Hollow

*Tamsin*

The knock came at half past four in the morning, which is the hour when knocks stop being knocks and start being verdicts.

I'd been awake anyway. I'd been awake since the sigil on my wrist started itching around midnight — a fine, crawling itch, like a line of ants spelling out a word I couldn't read yet. I sat in the back room of the candle shop with my grandmother's ledger open on my knees and a cup of tea gone cold beside me, watching the mark glow faintly through my sleeve like a coal that refused to die.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. The kind of knocking that has never once been followed by good news.

I didn't answer it. Instead I went to the window and looked out through the gap in the shutters, and there they were: two figures in grey standing under the lamp outside Merrow's bakery across the lane. Wardens. I knew them the way you know a shape from childhood — the high collars, the ledger-satchels, the posture of people who have never once been surprised by anything. The valley's Warden had come to our schoolroom when I was nine to explain that magic could not enter the Hollow, that we were safe here, that questions about the outside were best kept for geography lessons.

One of the wardens turned his head toward my window. Not toward the shop generally. Toward *my* window.

I stepped back so fast I knocked the cold tea off the table. It hit the floorboards without breaking, which felt like an omen of some kind, though I couldn't have told you whether it was good or bad.

"Right," I said to the empty room, because talking to yourself is free and no one else in the Hollow Valley was going to be honest with me at this hour. "Options."

Option one: open the door, ask what they wanted, be told something soothing and bureaucratic, and spend the rest of my life in a valley where prayers were suddenly being answered by things that weren't listening before. Option two: climb out the back window over the tallow barrels, which smelled like a candle factory's armpit, and run into the mountains in the dark with no plan, no supplies, and a glowing tattoo I'd had for less than a day.

I want it noted that I considered option one seriously. I want it noted that I am, by temperament, a person who pays her taxes early and apologizes to furniture she bumps into.

Then I heard the front door's lock turn — not forced, not broken, just *opened*, with a key, which meant someone in this valley had given them a key to my grandmother's shop — and option one died quietly in the corner.

The tallow barrels it was.

* * *

*Dominic*

I had made it exactly eleven steps out of the miller's loft when the first Warden rounded the corner of the granary, and I learned something interesting: in the Hollow Valley, eleven steps is enough time to discover that your entire body has gone quiet.

Storm-calling isn't a thing you do so much as a thing you are. There's always weather in me — pressure, charge, the low hum of a sky deciding what it wants to be. For three days in this valley I'd felt like a bell with the clapper removed. Muted. Present but pointless.

Now, with a Warden twenty feet away and my back against wet stone, I felt something flicker. Faint. Distant. Like hearing thunder from a storm two counties over.

It was coming from behind me. From the direction of the candle shop.

Which confirmed what I'd suspected since the shrine, since the girl with the key-shaped birthmark had looked at me like she was trying to remember whether she'd ever known me: the valley wasn't dead. The valley was *held*. And whatever held it was starting to lose its grip, right around the edges of Tamsin Vale.

The Warden stopped. Looked at me. Looked at the satchel on her hip, then at me again, in the manner of someone consulting a list.

"You're not resident," she said.

"Astounding deduction. I'm a traveler."

"Travelers don't lodge in lofts." She took a step closer, and I saw the ledger-chain glinting at her belt — iron links, each link stamped with a seal I recognized from covenant lore, the kind of seals that used to be reserved for binding gods and were now apparently being used for inventory management. "You crossed the pass at Miller's Fork. You asked questions at the shrine. You spoke to the Vale girl."

"I complimented her candles."

"The Vale girl is to be re-contained."

There it was. That word. *Re-contained.* As if she were grain. As if she were water. As if she hadn't stood in front of me yesterday with her sleeves pushed up and her jaw set and said, *I don't know what's happening to me, but I know it's mine.*

I've done cowardly things in my life. I have a whole catalog. But there are limits, and one of them turns out to be watching a bureaucrat say *re-contained* about a person.

"Funny thing about containment," I said, and let the flicker rise in my chest, small as a struck match. "It only works while the walls hold."

The match found something to burn. Not much — a thread of static, enough to make the lamp above us stutter and the hair on the Warden's arms lift. Her eyes went wide, because storm-magic should not exist inside the Hollow, and for one glorious second I watched certainty fail a Warden of the valley.

Then I ran, because one second of glory does not win fights, and I ran toward the candle shop, toward the source of the flicker, toward the girl whose existence was apparently cracking a prison I'd helped build.

Some things you can't outrun. Might as well run toward them.

* * *

*Tamsin*

I got one leg over the windowsill and a hand full of tallow stink when someone grabbed my ankle.

I did what any reasonable person does: I screamed, kicked, and connected solidly with a face. The face swore — in a voice I recognized — and said, "It's me, you absolute—"

"Dominic?" I hung upside down out of the window, one leg in the shop, one leg in the night, staring at the exiled storm-caller crouched among the barrels with his hand over his nose. "What are you doing here?"

"Rescuing you, apparently, from being rescued incorrectly. Wardens are in your shop. Front door. Two more covering the alley on the north side. We go south, over the tannery wall, and up toward the pass."

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I've spent the last hour being hunted by the same people, and unlike you, I noticed." He straightened, still pinching his nose. "Also, you smell like a chandler's mistake."

"You climbed into a barrel of tallow."

"I *hid behind* a barrel of tallow. The distinction matters to me personally." He glanced up at me, and even upside down I could see the calculation in his face — the same wary arithmetic he'd been doing since the moment we met. *Do I trust her? Does she know what she is? Am I saving her or delivering her?*

"Get down here, Vale," he said. "Unless you'd rather explain the glowing wrist to the people with the iron ledgers."

I looked back into the shop. Lamplight moved behind the curtain to the front room. A voice, calm and terrible, said something I couldn't make out, and another voice answered, and somewhere in between those two voices my grandmother's shop stopped being mine.

I dropped out of the window. Dominic caught most of me. We ended up in an undignified heap among the barrels, and if the situation hadn't been life or death I'd have laughed, because he smelled like tallow too now, and misery loves company.

"The pass," I whispered. "That's a day's climb."

"It's six hours if we don't stop."

"And if we do stop?"

"We're contained." He said it lightly, but I heard the weight underneath. He knew that word. He flinched at it. Which meant Dominic Eriksen knew exactly what the Wardens thought I was — and he still came for me. I filed that away. I didn't know what drawer to put it in yet, but I filed it.

We went south.

* * *

*Dominic*

She climbed like she'd been born on rock, which surprised me until I remembered that everything about this girl was apparently a surprise wrapped in an erasure. She went over the tannery wall without a rope, dropped eight feet onto packed earth, rolled, and came up running. Behind us the valley lay quiet under its lamps, and ahead of us the mountain rose black against a sky with no stars in it — because the Hollow Valley doesn't get stars, did you know that? Three days I'd been here before I noticed. No stars. The silence that keeps magic out keeps starlight out too, or something does, and I'd been so busy mourning my own dead power that I hadn't looked up.

"What happens when we cross the pass?" she asked, breath ragged, keeping pace beside me on the switchback trail.

"Weather," I said. "Real weather. Stars. My magic, if I'm lucky."

"And if you're not lucky?"

"Then we find out together." I risked a look at her wrist, where the sigil pulsed faintly under torn sleeve. Every hundred yards or so it brightened, and every time it brightened, something in my chest flickered in answer. Static. Pressure. The ghost of a storm. "Tamsin. What did the dream show you?"

Her footsteps faltered — just once, just enough.

"A tower burning," she said. "A woman calling me a name that wasn't Tamsin. And—" She stopped walking. Above us the trail bent around a shoulder of granite, and below us the valley sat in its bowl of silence, and between those two facts she stood very still. "And a door. A big one. Locked. With me on the wrong side of it."

"That sounds about right."

"Don't say it like that. Don't say 'that sounds about right' like you've read a book with me in it."

I had, in a sense. Covenant lore. The binding of the realms. The seal that kept gods on their side of the sky. I'd shattered it, and the shards had gone somewhere, and I had spent seven years telling myself they'd scattered harmlessly into the world like spilled salt.

They hadn't scattered. They'd *condensed*. Into a bloodline. Into a child delivered to a valley built to forget her.

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and the sigil flared — actually flared, light spilling between her fingers, and I felt the air charge so hard my teeth ached. "Not yet. Whatever you know, whatever you did, whoever I was — not yet. Right now I just need to get over that pass before the people with keys catch up. Everything else can wait until I'm standing somewhere real."

Somewhere real. As if the valley weren't real. As if twenty years of candles and neighbors and her grandmother's hands teaching hers to dip wicks were a waiting room.

But I understood. Gods help me, I understood better than she knew. There's a particular vertigo in discovering your life has a hidden floor, and the only way through it is forward motion. So we moved.

Behind us, far below, a bell began to ring. Not the chapel bell. The Warden's bell — flat, toneless, a sound like a filing cabinet falling down stairs. And with every ring, the silence of the valley changed pitch. I can't describe it better than that. Silence has texture when you've lived inside power; this one was fraying, unraveling at the rim like old cloth, and the fraying was spreading outward from a candle shop in the middle of town.

"They're losing it," I breathed. "The containment. It's failing."

"Is that good?"

"For us? Yes." I looked up the trail, at the pass still hours away, at the dark getting darker. "For everyone else in that valley? I genuinely don't know."

* * *

*Tamsin*

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing your entire life: it's mostly climbing.

By the third hour my lungs were on fire and my thighs had filed a formal complaint and Dominic had stopped pretending the pace was sustainable and started simply dragging me upward by the hand whenever the grade steepened. His hand was warm. Warmer than it should have been, I thought, for a man whose magic was supposedly dead. Warm like wire left in the sun.

"Tell me something true," I gasped, mostly to keep my mind off the burning. "Anything. Since we're apparently doing this."

He was quiet for three switchbacks. Then: "I had a sister."

"That's true?"

"It's the truest thing I own." His voice had gone strange — flattened, like a road after frost. "Everything else about me is negotiable. Exile, oath, name. But I had a sister, and everything I did, I did because of her."

"What happened to her?"

"I don't know." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "I haven't known for seven years. That's the price of exile — you don't get letters. You don't get anything." He glanced at me, and in the pre-dawn grey his eyes looked bruised with something older than tiredness. "Your turn. Something true."

I thought about it. The honest inventory of my life, itemized while running for the border: I make candles. I liked my grandmother, who is dead. I have no memory before age five, which everyone always said was normal, *children forget*, and which I am beginning to suspect was never normal at all.

"I'm scared," I said. "Not of the Wardens. Of the sigils. Because every time one appears, it feels less like something happening *to* me and more like something happening *back*. Like my body remembering a job I never applied for."

Dominic stopped. Above us, finally, finally, the pass yawned open between two shoulders of mountain — a notch of sky gone pale at the eastern edge, the first honest dawnlight I'd seen in years, maybe ever, though I couldn't have said why *ever* felt right.

"There," he said. "Cross that line, and the valley's rules stop applying. Cross it, and I can call a storm if we need one. Cross it, and—" He hesitated.

"And what?"

"And you'll feel it too. Whatever you are. The valley's been holding it down along with everything else." He turned to face me fully, and I realized his hand — the one holding mine — was trembling, and that the tremor wasn't fear. It was resonance. Like two strings tuned to the same note. "Once we cross, there's no putting the lid back on. For either of us."

Below us, the Warden's bell rang again, closer now, angrier, and beneath its flat tolling I heard something new: voices on the wind. Not human voices. The valley itself, maybe, or the thing that had kept it silent — groaning like a hinge asked to bear too much weight.

"They're coming," I said.

"Yes."

"And the lid's already off."

"Looks that way."

"So we run." I pulled him forward, up the last stretch of scree, toward the notch of light. "You can apologize for whatever you did later. I'll add it to the list. It seems to be a long list."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped him — short, disbelieving, alive. "You really are going to be insufferable about this, aren't you?"

"I contain multitudes," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because the word *contain* sat wrong in my mouth, like a stone.

We crossed the line.

I felt it happen. There was no wall, no shimmer, no dramatic threshold — just one step in shadow and the next step in light, and between them the whole world *opened*. Sound rushed in: wind, birdsong, the distant complaint of a living mountain. Color flooded back into things I hadn't known were grey. And deep in my chest, behind my ribs, something that had been sleeping for twenty years rolled over and stretched, and every sigil on my skin lit at once like a city at dusk.

Beside me, Dominic threw back his head and laughed aloud as clouds gathered overhead out of a clear sky — his clouds, his storm, answering him like a dog hearing its name.

"Oh," he breathed, arms spread, rain beginning to speckle the dust around us. "Oh, I've missed you."

And that was when we both turned to look back down into the valley, and saw that the silence hadn't merely failed.

It had *lifted* — peeled back like a lid off a well — and far below, in the bowl of the Hollow, lights were appearing that had no business existing. Not lamplight. Not lantern-light. A slow, deliberate constellation of altars igniting one by one across the valley floor, and above each one, shapes gathering that were too large to be people and too patient to be animals.

Every god the valley had ever locked out was being let in at once.

Or — worse, much worse — they had never needed letting in. They had only been waiting for the door to stop mattering.

"Tamsin," Dominic said quietly, and the joy had drained out of his voice entirely. "Run faster."
