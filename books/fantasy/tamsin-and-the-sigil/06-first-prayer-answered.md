## 6. First Prayer Answered

The Hollow Valley ended the way most good things end: without ceremony and with a lot of walking.

One moment we were still inside its borders — I could tell because my hands were just hands, because the air had that flat, flour-sack quality it always had, because Dominic was sulking like a man whose favorite instrument had been repossessed. The next moment we rounded a shoulder of granite on the mountain pass, and the world *changed*. Not dramatically. That's what unsettled me. There was no line painted on the ground, no sign reading YOU ARE NOW LEAVING THE LAST SANE PLACE IN EXISTENCE. Just a shift in the light, as if someone had opened a curtain one room over, and a faint hum at the base of my skull like a struck tuning fork that wouldn't finish dying.

Dominic felt it too. He stopped so abruptly I walked into his back, which was the first time all day he'd been useful for anything besides complaining.

"There it is," he said quietly.

"There what is?"

"Everything." He spread his hands, and for a heartbeat something moved in his palms — a shimmer, a suggestion of static, like heat off summer stone. Then it guttered out, and his face did something complicated. "Or almost everything. It's thin here. Thin enough to taste."

"You can taste magic?"

"I could." He said it the way you'd say *I used to have a wife*, and I decided not to poke that bruise. We'd been fleeing together for two days; we were not yet at the stage of our relationship where we traded wounds. We were barely at the stage where we shared water.

We kept climbing. The pass narrowed, the wind picked up, and by late afternoon the sky had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to commit. Thunder rolled somewhere behind the peaks — real thunder, or at least thunder that wasn't Dominic's fault, which he confirmed unprompted twice, which made me suspect it might have been slightly his fault.

By dusk we were lost, wet, and out of bread. The path had forked an hour back and we'd chosen wrong, and now the mountainside dropped away into fog on one side and rose into fog on the other, and the fog was closing in like it had opinions about trespassers.

My feet hurt. My wrists — both of them now, since the second sigil had arrived — ached with a deep, bone-level throb, like bruises from the inside. And underneath the ache there was that hum again, louder out here than it had ever been in the valley, rising through me like water filling a vessel.

I want to be clear about what happened next, because for years afterward I tried to dress it up as strategy, or instinct, or some grand awakening. It wasn't. It was this: I was cold, and tired, and scared, and I did the thing children do when they're cold and tired and scared and no one is coming.

I prayed.

Not properly. I didn't know how. Nobody in the valley prays properly — you whisper at shrines the way you apologize to furniture. What I did was stand in the fog with my eyes shut and think, with the full desperate sincerity of a soaked and starving woman:

*Please. Somewhere dry. Please.*

*Please.*

The hum in my bones stopped.

That should have been my first warning. A hum doesn't stop unless something is listening.

Then the fog *moved*.

I don't mean it drifted. Fog drifts. This folded — great grey sheets of it peeling back like bedsheets pulled off a sleeper, curling away from a point in the darkness ahead of us as though the night itself had been asked, politely, to make room. And in the space the fog left behind stood a farmhouse.

It hadn't been there. I will swear that on anything you like, before any god you name — and believe me, by the end of that night I knew exactly how binding such oaths could be. One moment: empty mountainside, fog, misery. Next moment: a farmhouse, solid and warm-windowed, with smoke rising from a chimney that had no business existing, sitting in a shelf of valley that I had looked at ten minutes earlier and seen nothing but rock.

It wasn't built so much as *remembered into being*. Stone walls, slate roof, a door with iron hinges gone orange with rust, a garden plot with bean rows and one stubborn apple tree. Everything about it said *old*. Everything except the fact that it had not existed ninety seconds ago.

Beside me, Dominic made a sound I'd never heard from him — small, involuntary, the sound of a man watching his religion get up and walk around.

"That's not possible," he whispered.

"It just happened."

"No." He grabbed my arm. His fingers were shaking. "Tamsin. That is not *possible*. Folded ground-workings take a circle of nine trained callers three days of preparation and a ley-anchor. You did it in a breath, standing up, in the rain, with—" He stopped. He was looking at my wrists. At the sigils, which had begun, very softly, to glow.

They glowed like banked coals. Like letters written in ember-ink just under the skin. And I realized, with a lurch, that they weren't aching anymore. They were *warm*. They were warm the way a hand is warm when someone has finally taken hold of it.

"Get inside," Dominic said. "Get inside and don't touch anything and don't say thank you to anyone."

"Who would I say thank you to?"

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

Inside, the farmhouse was worse. Better. Worse-better. It was perfect, is what it was — a fire already lit in the hearth, a kettle already singing, bread already risen under a cloth on the table, two chairs drawn up as if expecting us. Dried herbs hung from beams. A cat — a *cat*, grey and enormous and self-satisfied — lay curled on the windowsill and regarded us with the boredom of a creature that has been part of the scenery forever, rather than for four minutes.

"This is a trap," Dominic said, circling the room with his hackles up. "This is the oldest trap there is. Comfort first, hook later."

"You're welcome to wait outside in the rain."

"I'm saying we should be aware it's a trap."

"We're aware. Sit down. Eat the bread. If it's poisoned, at least we'll die warm." I was already pulling my boots off, because my feet had staged a mutiny and I am only human. Or I had been, until recently. The jury on that was increasingly out.

The bread was still warm. Of course it was. I ate half a loaf before I let myself think about it, and then I sat by the fire and held my hands out to the flames, and that's when I saw her reflection in the window glass.

She was standing behind me. In the room. Where there had been no one.

I turned around so fast I nearly went into the fire, and she was still there — a tall woman in robes the color of storm-light on water, silver-grey shot through with moving brightness, as if she wore weather instead of cloth. Her hair was dark going to white at the temples, arranged with the kind of care that takes either great vanity or great patience. Her face was beautiful the way a cliff face is beautiful: impressive, composed, and entirely uninterested in your opinion of it.

But her eyes — her eyes were the problem. They were kind. Warm, attentive, patient kindness, focused on me with the undivided attention of someone who has waited a long time for this exact moment.

"Hello, Tamsin," she said. Her voice was low and rich, the voice of a woman who reads bedtime stories to cities. "You must be exhausted. Please don't get up."

"What," said Dominic from the corner, in a voice like gravel being crushed, "are you."

The woman glanced at him with mild, distant courtesy, the way you'd glance at a dog barking at the postman. "Dominic Eriksen. Yes. I know what you are, too. We'll come to that." She turned back to me and inclined her head — actually inclined it, a small formal bow, which somehow frightened me more than anything else she'd done. "Forgive the intrusion. I am Veyra. You prayed, and I answered. It seemed rude not to introduce myself."

"The farmhouse," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which surprised me. "You did that."

"You did that." She smiled, and the smile was gentle and terrible. "I merely... opened the door you knocked on. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Nine callers and three days, as your friend there could attest. You did it in a breath. Because you are not a caller, Tamsin Vale. You are the thing callers knock against." She stepped closer, and the firelight bent toward her, subtly, like plants toward sun. "You are the Seal."

"I'm a candlemaker."

"For now." She said it kindly. That was the worst part. Every monstrous thing she said that night, she said kindly, in the tone of a doctor delivering a diagnosis she genuinely regretted. "You were a candlemaker. Before that you were other things, and before *that*—"

"Don't," Dominic said.

"—before that," Veyra continued, as if he hadn't spoken, "you were stolen. Do you want to know how? Do you want to know from whom, and why, and what was done to you while you slept?" She crouched down to my level by the fire, robes pooling like poured mercury, and took my hands in hers. Her skin was cool and smooth and thrummed faintly, like a wire under tension. "Because I can give them back. All of them. Every year, every hour, every name. Your mother's face. Your own name, the first one, the true one. The tower. The reason. Everything they buried in you to make you safe, and small, and *theirs*."

The fire popped. The cat purred. Somewhere outside, the rain had stopped without my noticing, and the silence was enormous.

And I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one discipline I've never been able to escape: for one long, shameful moment, I wanted it. Not the power. Not the service, whatever that meant. I wanted the *memories*. I wanted my mother's face the way a drowning woman wants air — instantly, totally, without negotiation. Twenty-two years I had lived with a hole in myself shaped like a childhood, and I had told myself it was fine, I had built a whole life around the hole like a tree growing around a fence post, and here was a stranger offering to pull the post out and hand me the missing rings of my own trunk.

She watched me want it. That was the horror of her — she didn't push. She just watched, with those kind eyes, letting the wanting do its work, the way you let dough rise.

"What's the price?" I asked.

"Tamsin—" Dominic started.

"She asked." Veyra didn't look away from me. "Good. Asking is correct. Service, freely offered. Nothing more sinister than that — no soul-clauses, no blood-ink, whatever he's told you about gods." A flicker of genuine, almost human distaste crossed her face. "I want you to *choose* to stand with me. That's all. Walk with me when I ask. Lend your voice when the conclave convenes. Be the Seal *awake*, instead of asleep and hunted. In exchange, you get yourself back. Whole."

"And if I say no?"

Her thumb traced, very lightly, across the sigil on my wrist. It flared — not painfully, but brightly, a coal breathed upon — and for half a second I saw something in the glow. A tower. Fire. A woman's mouth shaping a word that might have been my name, or might have been another one entirely.

"Then you keep the hole," Veyra said softly, "and the hunters keep coming, and every prayer you stumble into answering will cost you a little more of what remains. I am offering you the gentler road, child. I am the only one offering you any road at all." She rose, smooth as water finding its level, and the warmth in her eyes banked itself down to embers. "You needn't answer tonight. Sleep. The house will hold until morning — it holds as long as you do, incidentally, which you may find instructive."

She walked to the wall and simply wasn't there anymore, the way a word isn't there after you've said it. Just robes and storm-light and then firelight on plain stone, and the cat yawning, and the kettle still singing its idiot song.

For a long time neither of us spoke. Dominic sank into the second chair — the one that had been waiting for him, which he pointedly pretended not to notice — and put his head in his hands.

"'As long as you do,'" he repeated bitterly. "She means the house is anchored to you. You're the ley-line now. Congratulations."

"Dominic." My voice sounded far away to me. I was looking at my wrists, at the two sigils glowing their quiet ember-glow, and thinking about a tower, and a woman's mouth, and the shape of a word. "The name she mentioned. The first one. The true one."

"Yes?"

"How do you know she'd even give it back? How do I know any of it is real? How do I know she isn't just—" I stopped, because the thought finished itself, cold and complete: *how do I know she isn't just selling me my own life back to me, at interest.*

"That," said Dominic, staring into the fire, "is the entire business model of gods."

Outside, the fog had returned to the valley below — but differently now. Shaped now. And far off, down the mountain, along the road we'd fled, I saw lights. Many lights. Moving in formation.

Wardens, I thought. Except Wardens carried lanterns, and these lights burned silver, and they were coming uphill much too fast for anything carrying lanterns.

Dominic saw them a heartbeat later and was on his feet with a curse. "Veyra's hunters. Or Ossuar's. Doesn't matter — both of them want you, and neither takes no gracefully." He grabbed the bread, the blanket, my boots. "Back door. Now."

"There's no back door. I made this house. I'd remember a back door."

"Then make one," he said, shoving the boots into my arms, "and next time, Tamsin — *next time* — pray faster."
