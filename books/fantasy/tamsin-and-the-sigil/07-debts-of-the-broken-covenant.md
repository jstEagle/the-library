## 7. Debts of the Broken Covenant

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a god's departure, and I have heard it exactly twice in my life. The first time was the night I broke the covenant. The second was last night, when Veyra folded her farmhouse back into mist and left us standing in a wheat field at dawn like two actors who'd missed their cue.

Tamsin hadn't spoken since.

She'd eaten the bread I found in the farmhouse pantry — gods leave well-stocked pantries, apparently; hospitality is the oldest trick in their book — and drunk the water I boiled over a fire I built without magic, because my magic was still sulking somewhere beyond the valley's edge and showed no sign of coming home. She'd walked when I walked, stopped when I stopped, and said nothing except "east" when I asked which way she wanted to go.

East. Toward Corvane, the free city, where mortals haggled with minor gods over altar-space and everything had a price including, presumably, the air. It was the only direction that made sense. It was also the direction every hunter would expect us to take, which meant it was either the smartest choice or the stupidest one, and I genuinely could not tell which.

By midday we'd reached a stretch of old road cut through low hills, the kind of road the empire had built back when empires still bothered with roads, and there was a ruined waystation with half a roof and a chimney that still drew. I got a fire going. Tamsin sat down across from it, hugged her knees, and stared into the flames with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

I knew that expression. It was the expression of a person doing arithmetic with their own life and not liking any of the totals.

"You're going to ask me," she said finally, "or you're going to keep not-asking me, and I can hear you not-asking from here. It's very loud."

"I'm a patient man."

"No, you're not. You've been not-asking so hard you nearly walked into a ditch twice."

That was fair. I fed a stick to the fire and considered my options, which were roughly: lie to her, tell her a partial truth, or tell her all of it. The first two were easier. The third was the one that had been sitting in my chest for eleven years like a swallowed stone, and I was tired of carrying it alone, and — this was the part I kept circling and avoiding — she deserved it. Whatever else she was now, whatever the sigils on her skin were turning her into, she deserved to know why.

"When I was nineteen," I said, "I had a sister."

The fire popped. Tamsin didn't move, but something in the quality of her stillness changed — the stillness of listening instead of waiting.

"Maren. She was sixteen. Cleverer than me, funnier than me, better with people, which given how charming I am tells you something." I turned the stick over. "She was born storm-touched, same as me. We grew up in Skaldmark, up north, where the storm-calling families have held the coast for nine generations. You call the winds, you bind the gales before they eat the fishing fleet, and in exchange the land lets you live on it. Old arrangement. Older than the covenant, even. Or so we thought."

"The covenant," Tamsin said. "You keep using that word like I should know it."

"You will. That's rather the problem." I took a breath. It tasted like smoke and cowardice, so I took another. "The covenant is the treaty between the realms. Ours and theirs. The divine and the mortal. Before it, the gods walked here freely. Answered prayers, collected worship, took lovers, took children, took *territory*. Whole kingdoms knelt. And the ones who wouldn't kneel—" I shrugged. "History doesn't record them, because history was written by survivors, and the survivors were mostly kneeling.

"So the covenant was forged to close the border. To make the realms separate. Gods answer prayers only through sanctioned channels, mortals live and die by mortal rules, and nobody crosses. It held for three hundred years. It was holding fine when I was nineteen. It was holding fine right up until the night Maren drowned."

Tamsin's head came up slightly. "Drowned?"

"Fell through lake ice. Storm-touched children run hot, did I mention? She'd been out skating in weather no sane person skates in, because she was sixteen and invincible, the way they all are." My voice came out flat, which was a mercy. Flat was the only way through this part. "I got to her. I got my hands on her. And I called the storm to pull her out — a working no nineteen-year-old should have attempted, a working that needed more power than one caller has, and I reached for it anyway, because the alternative was watching her go blue under the ice.

"And the power answered. Not my power. *Their* power. A god's. One of the old ones who'd never liked the covenant, who'd been waiting three centuries for someone desperate enough to be a door." I watched the fire instead of her face. "He lent me the strength to tear her out of the water. And the price of the lending was simple: he wanted the covenant broken, and I was holding the other end of it."

"You mean—"

"Storm-callers aren't just weather-workers, Tamsin. We're anchors. Every calling family holds a thread of the binding — it's woven into our bloodlines, our oaths, the rites we do at solstice that everyone thinks are just tradition. Nine families, nine threads. Tear enough threads and the weave comes apart." I finally looked at her. "I tore mine. Willingly. Knowingly. He told me what it would do, and I did it, because Maren was dying and the god was offering and I was nineteen and in love with my sister the way only brothers are, which is to say completely and without a single rational faculty remaining.

"The covenant didn't shatter all at once. It *tore*. And a torn binding doesn't just vanish — it has to go somewhere. Energy seeks ground. The whole weight of three hundred years of separation, the entire architecture of the wall between realms, ripped loose from its anchor and went looking for a new one."

The fire crackled. Somewhere up the hill a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"And it found me?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"It found your father." I said it as gently as I could, which wasn't very. "Aldous Vale. Covenant-priest. Third rank, minor orders, keeper of a shrine so unimportant the gods themselves had forgotten it existed. When the binding tore loose, it should have scattered — dissipated into a thousand thousand fragments, weakened past recovery. But your father was standing in the one place in the world where a fragment could be *caught*. Held. Bound into something stable." I spread my hands. "Into a bloodline. Into a child not yet born, or newly born — the accounts differ, and I've had eleven years to stop wanting to know which."

Tamsin was very still. "My grandmother."

"Was paid well, yes. Or coerced. Or deceived. Possibly all three; the Wardens are thorough. The point is, the Hollow Valley exists for one reason. It isn't a refuge from magic. It's a *container*. Magic-dead ground, warded, sealed, maintained by an order whose entire purpose is making sure the seal inside it stays asleep. You weren't hidden there, Tamsin. You were *stored* there."

* * *

I want to record, for honesty's sake, that I expected her to cry. I want to record that I braced for tears, because tears are manageable, tears are a thing you can sit through with a hand on someone's shoulder while feeling appropriately terrible about yourself.

She didn't cry.

She stood up. Slowly, the way water stands up when it decides to become a wave.

"My whole life," she said. Her voice was level. That was worse than crying. "Every winter my hands cracked from candle-wax because we couldn't afford gloves. Every time I asked about my parents, and Gran changed the subject. Every birthday I spent wondering why I felt like a guest in my own head." She turned to face me, and the sigils on her wrist — the new one, the key-shaped one — caught the firelight and seemed almost to pulse. "You're telling me there's a reason for all of it. There's a *reason*, and the reason is that I'm furniture. I'm a strongbox. Some god tore a hole in the sky and my family got handed the bill, and I've spent twenty years being the bill."

"Yes."

"Don't *yes* me like that's an answer. Whose idea? Your storm-god, he just — he pointed you at my father? At a priest of a dead shrine?"

"He didn't need to point. The binding sought ground; your father was the ground. Whether anyone chose it or whether it was pure chance—" I shook my head. "I've wondered. Gods don't do chance. But I've never been able to prove design, and I'll tell you why that distinction matters to me: because if it was chance, then I broke the world for nothing. If it was designed, then I was a weapon, and weapons get some small forgiveness. I've spent eleven years needing it to be one or the other, and I still don't know."

"And Maren?" Tamsin said. "Your sister. Did saving her work?"

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. Because it was the question I'd have asked, and because the answer was the worst part.

"She lived," I said. "She married a shipwright in Skaldmark two years before my exile was formalized. She has two daughters. She thinks I died in a rockslide." I smiled, and it felt like wearing someone else's teeth. "So yes. It worked. I traded the integrity of creation for one girl from a fishing town, and the girl lived, and creation sent me the invoice, and the invoice was you."

For a moment I thought she'd hit me. I want to be clear that I would have deserved it, and that some treacherous part of me was braced for it the way you brace for medicine.

Instead she laughed. One short, awful syllable, nothing in it but anger.

"*You* feel guilty. Of course you do. You've had eleven years to curate it, haven't you? Eleven years of polishing this confession, deciding which parts to lead with. 'I broke reality, but for love.' Do you know what I had?" She stepped closer to the fire, and I swear the flames leaned away from her. "I had *nothing*. No reason, no story, no villain to hate. Just gaps. Twenty years of gaps, and I built a person out of candle inventory and my grandmother's recipes and the assumption that the gaps were ordinary grief. And you're telling me the grief wasn't even mine. It was *installed*. I'm a jar someone put a lid on, and you're the man who knocked me off the shelf."

"Tamsin—"

"No. You don't get to soften it. You don't get the absolution of having told me gently." Her hand came up, and the sigils along her forearm were glowing now, faint gold lines rising under the skin like veins of ore, and I felt something I hadn't felt in eleven years: pressure. The pressure of a storm front. Except it wasn't the sky. It was her. "Veyra called me the Seal. She offered me my memories back like they were hers to give. And I sat there thinking, *why me, why does a god want a candle-maker from nowhere.* And the answer is that I'm not from nowhere. I'm from *your* mistake. I'm the collateral on a loan you took out when you were nineteen and heartbroken, and every god in the sky has been circling me ever since because you couldn't watch one girl drown."

The words were cruel and precise and every single one of them true, and I sat there and took them, because the alternative was defending myself and I had nothing to defend with.

"Yes," I said again. "All of it. I won't argue with a word."

"You should argue! Argue something! Tell me you'd do it differently, tell me you regret it, tell me—"

"I don't." The admission came out of me before I could weigh it, and once it was out there was no pulling it back. "I've tried to regret it, Tamsin. Eleven years, in exile, in the wet cold, eating food that fights back. And every time I get to the edge of it — *I wish I'd let her drown* — I can't step off. I look over that edge and I see her going blue under the ice and I cannot wish it. That's the truth. I broke the covenant, I made you into a vault, I put every horror that's coming for us into motion, and if you put me back on that lake tomorrow, I'd do it again, and I think that makes me worse than a monster, because at least monsters don't know better."

The silence afterward was long. The fire burned down a notch. The sigils on her arm dimmed by degrees, gold fading to faint ink, and the pressure eased off my chest like a hand withdrawing.

"That," she said at last, "is the most honest thing anyone's ever said to me. And I hate it."

"I'd expect nothing less."

She sat back down. Not beside me — across the fire still, a careful distance, and I noted the distance and resolved to respect it. But she sat, and she pulled her knees up, and she looked at me with red-rimmed eyes and a jaw set like masonry.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You don't get forgiven tonight. Maybe not ever — that's not a threat, it's just arithmetic; I don't know yet if it balances. But you're the only person alive who knows what I am, and I'm the only person alive who knows what you did, which means we're stuck with each other until one of us dies, and I'd prefer it not be soon. So you're going to tell me everything else. All of it. What the Seal does, who wants it, what happens if it breaks, what happens if it *doesn't*. Starting now, starting with the part you've been avoiding, which is the part where you explain why your magic flickers around me like a bad lamp."

I blinked. "You noticed that."

"Dominic. I've spent twenty years noticing things in a valley designed to make sure I noticed nothing. Flickering lamps are my *specialty*."

And despite everything — the guilt sitting in my gut like ballast, the fury still banked in her voice — I laughed. It surprised us both.

"All right," I said. "Everything. In order. Though I warn you, the order goes: what you are, what you're becoming, and why the city we're walking toward is full of gods who buy and sell prayers like fishmongers — and every one of them, the moment you walk through those gates, is going to smell you on the wind."

Tamsin pulled the journal from her coat — she'd started it two nights ago, writing herself down in a cheap hand against the erosion, though she hadn't yet told me why she thought she was eroding, and I hadn't asked, and the not-asking between us was getting to be a whole language — and uncapped her pencil.

"Good," she said. "Because I'm writing all of it down. Everything you say. Word for word." She glanced up at me, and there was something new in her expression, something that hadn't been there in the valley: not trust, not yet, but its rough draft. "If the world's going to use me as a document, I'm going to be the one holding the pen."

Outside, far off to the west, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

Neither of us mentioned it. Neither of us had to. We both knew what answered storms meant now, and we both knew it wasn't weather.

I started talking. She started writing. And eastward, down the long road to Corvane, the god-market opened its stalls for the evening trade, and somewhere among the altars, something with too many names turned its attention toward two travelers on a hill road, and began, patiently, to wait.
