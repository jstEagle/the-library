## 8. The God-Market

Corvane smelled like a temple that had learned commerce.

That was my first thought as we came through the Salt Gate at dawn — incense and roasting chestnuts and something underneath both, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going over. The city rose out of the flats in tiers of pale stone, and every tier was hung with banners, and every banner bore a sigil, and every sigil belonged to somebody who wasn't listening anymore.

"They call it the God-Market," Dominic said, pulling his hood lower. "Officially it's the Concordance of Corvane. Unofficially—" he gestured at a woman ahead of us who was haggling with a shrine the way you'd haggle with a fishmonger, "—it's exactly what it looks like."

The woman had her palm flat on an altar of green marble. "Three years I've been coming here," she was saying. "Three years, and you healed my husband's *cough* when I asked for his *leg*. That's not mercy, that's false advertising."

The altar said nothing, because altars generally don't. But the air above it shimmered faintly, the way water does when something is just beneath the surface deciding whether to bite, and the woman's expression shifted from fury to the particular desperate hope of someone who has been insulted by a god and would still very much like one.

"Come on," Dominic murmured, and steered me past.

I want to be honest about what I felt in those first hours, because later I'd feel other things, worse things, and I don't want the wonder to get rewritten the way everything else in my life gets rewritten. What I felt was joy. There. I said it. For twenty-some years I'd lived in a valley where magic went to die, where prayers were a habit like knitting, performed for the shape of them and never once answered. And now I stood in a city where the sky itself seemed attentive. Where a child could lose a copper coin, cry about it, and have the coin turn up warm in her pocket ten minutes later because some minor godling of small mercies had decided to make a name for itself.

It was terrible and it was beautiful and I wanted all of it.

"Stop staring," Dominic said.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring like you did at the farmhouse. Last time you stared like that, a building folded itself out of fog."

"That was Veyra."

"Veyra started as a stare," he said grimly. "Everything starts as a stare."

* * *

We found lodging above a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Tier, which is the kind of neighborhood where nobody asks questions because everybody has answers they're hiding. The dyer was a broad, cheerful woman named Sefa with forearms stained blue to the elbow, and she took our coin without looking up from her ledger until Dominic mentioned we'd come from the Hollow Valley. Then she looked up.

"Hollow folk," she said. "Huh. You lot must be lost."

"Why's that?" I asked.

"Because nothing follows you home." She said it lightly, but there was something under it, the same sweetish-wrong smell the city had. "Prayers don't stick to you. Gods can't find the door." She snapped her ledger shut. "Down here, prayers stick to everything. You'll see."

We saw.

The market proper opened at midday along the Canal of Small Debts — yes, that was its actual name; no, nobody in Corvane thought this was strange — and it made the ordinary bazaars I'd known look like children playing shop. Altars lined the canal on both sides, hundreds of them, great ones of veined marble and little ones of river stone, gilded ones and chipped ones and one that appeared to be a repurposed washbasin. Each had a keeper: a priest or priestess or, more often, a self-appointed broker who sat behind their god's altar like a merchant behind a stall, weighing petitions against payment.

And the gods came. That was the part that undid me. They didn't descend in glory; they *seeped*. A warmth in the crowd. A face in a fountain that held a moment too long. A pigeon that watched you with entirely too much comprehension. Minor gods, mostly — Dominic called them the small clergy, godlings of headaches and harvests and safe childbirth and finding lost things — drawn up from wherever minor gods live by the scent of negotiation.

"Watch," Dominic said quietly. He nodded toward an altar where a farmer was speaking with a broker, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the patient smile of a moneylender.

The farmer wanted rain. Not a storm — he was specific about this, glancing nervously at Dominic, which everyone pretended not to notice — just a soft rain over three days for his barley. The broker consulted a slate covered in columns of figures, then named a price: the farmer's eldest daughter's firstborn name. Naming rights. The godling of that particular canal district would choose what the child was called, and the name would carry a small binding, a lifelong thread of obligation.

"That's obscene," I whispered.

"That's cheap," Dominic said. "Rain is expensive. Look further down."

Further down, a young man was selling two years of his own good luck for his mother's fever to break. Further down than that, a widow was offering her grief — literally her grief, boxed and labeled and carried under her arm like a cake — in exchange for a sign that her drowned son had gone somewhere gentle. The broker at that altar looked genuinely sorry as he explained that grief was a common currency and hers, being five years old and much handled, would fetch less than she hoped.

I stood in the middle of it with my hands clenched and my heart doing something complicated, because here is the thing I couldn't say out loud: part of me understood. In the valley, prayer was free and therefore weightless. Here, prayer had *weight*, and weight meant it mattered, meant the gods actually listened, meant a coughing husband got his leg mended if you paid enough. It was horrifying and it was the most honest theology I'd ever seen. Every answered prayer is a contract. Nobody in Corvane pretended otherwise. They'd built a whole city on the refusal to pretend.

"Your sigils," Dominic said, low, watching me watch them. "Tamsin. Your sigils are clauses."

"I know." I didn't know, not yet, not really. But I'd felt it since the farmhouse — the way Veyra's gift had settled into my skin like a signature into wet ink. "I can feel it. Every time one lights up, it's like... turning a page I didn't know I'd agreed to write on."

"Then we keep you away from altars."

"We're in a city made of altars."

"Then we keep you away from *praying*."

I laughed, and it came out bitter. "Dominic. I prayed for shelter and got a farmhouse. Do you think intention matters? I sneezed near a shrine this morning and apologized to it out of habit. What if apology counts?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer.

* * *

Sefa, it turned out, knew a broker who owed her a favor — a debt-broker named Ilsette who worked the middle tiers and dealt in contracts rather than miracles. "If anyone can read your skin," Sefa said, scrubbing dye from her hands, "it's her. But mind — Ilsette doesn't do charity, and she doesn't do curiosity for free. Whatever she tells you, you'll owe her the telling."

"Owe her how?" I asked.

"That," Sefa said cheerfully, "is between you and her."

Ilsette kept an office above a candle-maker's — I noted the irony, being a candle-shop girl myself, though these candles were stamped with tiny sigils and sold as prayer-fuses, each one timed to burn through the length of a petition. She was small and gray-eyed and perhaps sixty, with the unlined face of someone who had long ago stopped being surprised by anything, including herself.

She looked at my wrist for a long time. The new sigil there — the one I'd woken with after the dream of the burning tower — had faded to a dull bronze shimmer, visible only when the light caught it wrong.

"Take off your sleeve," she said. "All of it."

I did. She counted. Her lips moved.

"How many?" Dominic asked.

"Eleven active clauses." She said it the way a physician says a number of tumors. "Plus a master clause I can't parse, wrapped around the others like a spine. Girl, do you know what you are?"

"A seal," I said. "So I'm told."

"A seal is a stamp." Ilsette's eyes came up. "You're a *contract*. A whole contract, walking around in boots. Every sigil on your body is a term, and every term is already executed — signed, witnessed, filed. You haven't been making bargains since you left your valley. You've been *performing* them. The bargain was struck before you could walk."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below, the candle-maker was singing to himself, something about wax and wicks.

"By whom?" My voice sounded far away. "Signed by whom?"

Ilsette turned my wrist gently, tracing the bronze lines. "Standard covenant architecture. Principal parties unnamed — sealed, appropriately enough. Consideration rendered: memory, childhood, identity, the usual currencies when gods deal with mortals." She tapped the master clause at the center of my forearm. "This one here. This is the interesting bit. This isn't a term. This is a *signature block*. There's a space here where a name should go, and instead—" she squinted, "—there's a hold. A reservation. As if the contract is waiting for someone to sign it."

"Waiting for whom?"

"If I knew that, girl, I'd be charging you considerably more." She released my wrist and leaned back. "Here's what I'll give you for free, because Sefa will sulk otherwise. In this city, every prayer is a negotiation, and every negotiation leaves paper. Mortals keep copies. Gods keep copies. But there's a third archive — the Registry of Small Debts, down in the Canal district — where brokers file duplicate contracts, the ones they don't want their gods reading. Corrupt brokers, greedy brokers, brokers covering mistakes. If any piece of your contract exists outside divine hands, it'll be there."

"And the price for telling us where it is?"

"Oh, I told you where it is for free." Ilsette smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "The price is for what you'll find when you get there. Payment due on delivery. We'll settle up after."

"I hate this city," Dominic said.

"Everyone does," Ilsette agreed pleasantly. "That's why it works."

* * *

We went at dusk, when the market was closing and the brokers were tallying their day's takings in the amber light. The Registry was a long stone hall beneath the canal embankment, shelves rising into shadow, millions of contracts filed in the dark like the sediment of every desperate bargain ever struck. A clerk with a lamp admitted us for a fee, asked no questions — questions cost extra, apparently, and we hadn't paid for any — and pointed us toward the covenant archives.

I found it by touch before I found it by sight. My arm grew warm. Warm, then hot, then burning, and I shoved back my sleeve and watched the sigils light one after another down my forearm like windows in a house at evening, and at the end of the aisle, on a shelf at eye height, a single scroll was glowing in answer.

My handwriting was on it.

Not quite my handwriting. My handwriting grown up, aged, formalized — the same slant, the same crossed t's, but written by a hand that had practiced for decades. The header read: *Covenant of Separation — Executory Instruments — Living Seal.* And beneath it, clauses. Eleven of them. I didn't need Ilsette to translate. I could feel each one as I read it, a small cold click behind my ribs, like a lock accepting a key I'd been carrying without knowing.

*Clause the First: In exchange for continuance of the Separation, the Bearer surrenders all memory prior to consecration.*

*Clause the Fourth: The Bearer shall be sheltered, moved, and preserved by such means as the contracting powers determine, without further consent required or sought.*

Without further consent required or sought. I read that line four times. The farmhouse. The dreams. The healing at the shrine that had started all of this — old Marta praying at a dead shrine and standing up cured, and me waking marked. None of it had been grace. None of it had even been Veyra's initiative. It had been *execution of terms*. I was not a person to whom things were happening. I was a document executing itself, and everyone around me — the dying neighbor, the storm-caller, the goddess with her soft voice and her offer of memories — was a party to paperwork I'd never seen.

"Tamsin." Dominic's hand on my shoulder. "Breathe."

"There's a signature block," I said. My voice was steady, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "Ilsette said there was a hold on it. Look."

He looked. The blank space where a name should be. And beneath it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, a line neither of us had noticed:

*Signature reserved pending final ratification at the Conclave of Ash.*

The Conclave of Ash. Veyra had spoken of a conclave. Of delivering me to it. Of my memories, whole and perfect, waiting on the far side of my cooperation.

"She wasn't courting me," I said. "She was *notarizing*."

The lamp-light guttered. Down the long dark aisles of the Registry, I swear the contracts rustled — thousands upon thousands of them, every desperate bargain this city had ever struck, stirring like leaves — and my sigils burned brighter, answering them, clause by clause by clause.

And then, from the shadows at the end of the aisle, a voice I didn't know said, conversationally:

"You really shouldn't have read that."

Dominic's hand closed around my arm, storm-grey light flickering uselessly at his fingertips. The voice stepped forward into the glow of my own skin, smiling.

"Now," it said, "we'll need to discuss terms."
