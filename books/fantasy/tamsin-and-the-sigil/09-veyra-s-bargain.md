## 9. Veyra's Bargain

I have been cornered by gods before. It is a specific kind of geometry — the way the room shrinks, the way every exit becomes a suggestion rather than an option. The first time it happened I was twenty-three and stupid and full of lightning, and I mistook being noticed for being valued.

The second time was in a roadside chapel outside Corvane, at an hour when honest people were asleep, and I did not make that mistake again.

* * *

We had taken rooms above a drover's inn three miles from the city walls, because Tamsin had argued — reasonably, damn her — that sleeping inside Corvane while wearing a skin covered in divine contract clauses was like sleeping in a bank vault with the ledger strapped to your chest. The inn was called the Drowned Ox, which should have been warning enough. Its beds were lumps, its ale was a rumor of ale, and its chapel — every inn had one now, since the prayers started getting answered — was a converted smokehouse that still smelled faintly of ham.

Tamsin slept. I didn't, much. Old habit. Exiles sleep like cats: one ear always up, one eye on the door, waiting for the knock that means *your past has caught up and it brought friends.*

The knock came at the hour before dawn, when the candle in my window had burned down to a thumb's width of blue flame.

Except it wasn't a knock. That was the thing I understood too late. A knock travels through wood; you feel it in the frame, in the floorboards, in your teeth. This sound arrived already inside the room, as if the door had knocked itself from the far side of thinking. And the blue candleflame stood perfectly straight, unmoving, like a soldier presenting arms to someone it recognized.

I knew before I turned around. You always know. The air gets heavy the way it does before lightning, but without the promise of rain — just pressure, and the taste of copper, and a silence so complete you can hear your own blood filing complaints.

"Storm-caller." Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which sounds impressive until you realize it mostly means she hadn't bothered choosing a direction. "You've gotten thin."

Veyra stood by the window where there had been no one a heartbeat before. She does this. Gods do this. They arrive the way weather arrives, and they expect you to be grateful they didn't arrive worse.

She wore the shape she'd shown Tamsin in the farmhouse folded out of mist: tall, gray-eyed, dressed in colors that kept almost resolving into something you remembered wanting. Her hair moved like it was underwater. There was a kindness in her face the way there's kindness in a snare — carefully arranged, functional, designed to hold.

"Lady Veyra," I said, and gave her the bow I'd once given a magistrate who was about to fine me: shallow, insolent, technically correct. "To what do I owe the honor? If it's about the noise complaint, the storm wasn't mine."

"It was entirely yours." She smiled. "That's rather the point. You broke an oath written into the bones of the sky, Dominic Eriksen, and last week on the salt flats' edge you broke it again. Called a storm with no right, no rite, and no sanction. Do you know what happens to oath-breakers in the old law?"

"They get exiled," I said. "I've read the brochure."

"The old law," she continued, as if I hadn't spoken, because gods never let anyone finish a sentence they've decided isn't worth their time, "says they get unmade. Slowly. From the edges inward. Your exile was mercy, storm-caller. My mercy. Remember that."

I remembered it differently. I remembered the tribunal, nine faces behind veils of cloud, and the sentence pronounced in a voice like distant thunder deciding whether to come closer. But arguing theology with a god at dawn in a ham-scented chapel seemed like a poor use of what might be my final minutes, so I said nothing and reached instead — casually, I hoped — for the storm-line.

There wasn't one.

Of course there wasn't. We were still close enough to the valley that the Hollow's dead air clung to the land like fog off a river, and even where it thinned, Veyra had chosen this room, this hour, this candle. My magic sat in me like a bird with a broken wing, aware of the sky and unable to reach it.

"You're wondering why I'm here," she said, "instead of simply taking the girl."

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"Because I don't want to take her." Something moved across her face — not quite pain, not quite patience, something older than both. "Taking is what Ossuar would do. Taking is why mortals stopped building us temples and started building us locks. I am offering, Dominic. That is the difference between me and everything else hunting her. I offer."

"And what," I said, "are you offering me?"

Her smile widened by exactly one tooth. Gods count teeth. I've never learned why.

"I'm glad you asked. Because here is what I know about you, exile. I know you shattered the covenant eight years ago — yes, I know why, we'll come to that. I know you have spent every day since watching the horizon for the hunters you knew would come. I know you sleep sitting up and eat standing and haven't said your sister's name aloud in six years because saying it feels like opening a wound to check whether it's still bleeding." She tilted her head. "Her name is Sera."

The room went very quiet. Quieter than it had been, which I hadn't thought possible. Even my blood stopped complaining.

"Don't," I said.

"She lives," Veyra said. "Did you think we wouldn't check? Did you think we'd exile you and simply lose interest in the family of the man who broke the world? She lives in the fen-country south of Vhal, in a town called Mireholt, under the name her mother gave her at birth because she was clever enough to change it after you fell. She keeps bees. She has a scar on her left hand from a kettle, from when she was seven, from the day you taught her to make tea and then abandoned her to go be important." Veyra's voice softened into something that was either compassion or its flawless counterfeit. "She prays sometimes, Dominic. Small prayers. That you're alive. That you're warm. And lately — oh, lately her prayers have started getting answered, the way everyone's do, and do you know what she asks for now?"

"Stop."

"That her brother comes home."

I put my hand against the wall because the wall was there and I needed something that was. Six years. Six years of telling myself she was better off with no name attached to mine, that every year of silence was a year the hunters looked elsewhere, that love could be expressed in the grammar of absence. And here was a god holding all of it — the kettle scar, the changed name, the bees — like a hand of cards she'd been waiting to play.

"What do you want," I said. My voice came out flat. I was proud of that, later. Not much later. Later that morning.

"Deliver her," Veyra said simply. "The Seal. Bring Tamsin Vale to the conclave at the temple of ash, willingly, trusting you, and when the covenant is reforged under divine stewardship, your exile ends. Pardon, restoration, your name struck from the ledgers of the fallen. And more." She stepped closer, and the candleflame leaned toward her like a plant toward sun. "Your sister. Under the old covenant, the families of oath-breakers carry a taint — a small one, a shadow on the soul, enough that luck runs thin around them. Bees die. Kettles boil over. Husbands sulk. When the new covenant binds, that taint lifts. She'll live the life she should have lived. The one your crime cost her."

"There it is," I said.

"There it is."

"You're asking me to sell her. Again. Last time I sold her future to save the world, and look how that went. Now you want me to sell a stranger's whole self — her memories, her name, her choice — to buy back the piece of my sister I already spent."

"I'm asking you to stop paying for one sin by committing another," Veyra said, and the terrible thing, the genuinely terrible thing, was that she said it gently. "You've carried this alone for six years, Dominic. I watched you carry it. I am the only power in any realm offering to set it down. Everyone else wants you to carry it further. Ossuar wants you dead. The Wardens want you contained. The girl herself—" she paused, delicately, "—the girl herself will hate you when she learns what you did. She will learn. Secrets are mortal things; they rot. Wouldn't you rather she hear it from you *after* you've bought her safety, than before?"

And that was the hook, I'll give her credit. Not the pardon. Not the taint-lifting. The *after*. The fantasy every guilty man marries: that there exists a version of confession that costs nothing, that you can purchase forgiveness in advance, wholesale, and skip the part where you stand in front of someone you've wronged with empty hands.

I thought about Sera's kettle scar. I thought about Tamsin asleep one wall away, dreaming whatever a girl dreams whose childhood was stolen to pay a debt she never incurred. I thought about the journal she'd started keeping, writing herself down entry by entry like a woman bailing out a boat, and how the first page said, in her careful hand: *If I forget this, it was still true.*

"No," I said.

Veyra waited. Gods are good at waiting; it's most of what they do.

"No," I said again, and found my feet under me. "Here's what you got wrong, lady. Everything. But especially this: you think my loyalty is a commodity with a price you haven't found yet. It's not loyalty. Loyalty implies I weighed options. What I feel toward that girl in the next room is closer to what a man owes a fire he started. And you don't pay a man to aim his own guilt at someone else. You just don't."

"You'd choose her over your sister's whole life?"

"I'd choose her *for* my sister," I said. "Because if your covenant reforges, it reforges on the back of another child given up by other parents, and there will always be another Sera — some other little girl with a kettle scar whose brother sells her future to save the world. I broke the covenant because it was built on that trade. I was wrong about the method and right about the math. No."

For a moment — one long, silver moment — Veyra simply looked at me. And I saw, beneath the kindness, beneath the arrangement, something vast and tired and cold turn over, the way a leviathan turns under a calm sea, showing you only the idea of its size.

"Pity," she said.

Then the room snapped back. The candle guttered, caught, burned ordinary yellow. The pressure lifted. The ham smell returned, offensive and mundane and welcome. And I was alone in a cheap room with a broken-winged storm in my chest and the certain knowledge that I had just refused a god to her face, which historically had gone well for approximately no one.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the lump that passed for a bed and did arithmetic.

Refusing Veyra meant the hunters came sooner. Refusing meant Ossuar's people — the ones who wanted the Seal destroyed and everyone near it buried — had one less faction to compete with for our corpses. Refusing meant Tamsin walked into the conclave at the temple of ash with no leverage, no patron, and a body covered in clauses she couldn't read.

Unless.

I got out the map Maerin had scratched for us, the defrocked priest's crooked ink showing the pilgrim roads into the ruined temple, and I started doing the thing I was actually good at, the thing that had made a farm boy from the fens into a storm-caller in the first place: finding the seam in the system. Every covenant had one. Every contract did. You just had to read it like an enemy.

Which is when Tamsin knocked — a real knock, through real wood, felt in the teeth — and came in rubbing her eyes with her sleeves pulled down over her hands, the way she did when the sigils were bothering her.

"You're up early," she said. Then she looked at my face, and at the map, and at the candle burned to a stub though dawn was an hour off. "Someone was here."

"A god."

"Which one?"

"The one with the payment plan."

Tamsin sat down on the windowsill across from me, and I watched her decide — visibly, deliberately — not to ask what I'd been offered. That was the thing about her that kept undoing me. Everyone else I'd ever known would have demanded the terms, would have wanted to run the numbers on their own soul. Tamsin just looked at me with those level valley eyes and said:

"You said no, though."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're still here," she said, "and you look awful, and you're drawing on a map instead of running. People who take gods' deals look relieved. You look like a man who chose the hard thing and is already regretting the paperwork." She paused. "Also your candle burned blue earlier. Woke me up. Blue candles mean a god's been talking. Gran used to say that."

"Your gran," I said carefully, "sounds like she knew things."

"Everyone in the valley knows things," Tamsin said. "That's the problem. We just don't know that we know them." She nodded at the map. "So. What's the plan, storm-caller? And don't lie to me. I've got a journal now. I write down when people lie to me."

I believed her. That was the strange part — six years of trusting no one, and I believed a girl I'd known a fortnight, because she wrote herself down like scripture and treated truth like a candle she was determined to keep lit.

"We go to the conclave," I said. "On our terms. As pilgrims, through the under-roads, with Maerin's text. And when the gods start bidding for you—" I tapped the map, on the ruin marked *temple of ash*, "—we show them a third option. One nobody's offered yet."

"Which is?"

"Honestly? I'm still drafting it. But it starts with the words *no*, and apparently I'm very good at those now."

Tamsin laughed — short, surprised, real — and outside the window the first light came up over the road to Corvane, and somewhere south in the fen-country a woman with a kettle scar checked her bees and prayed a small prayer for a brother who had just, for the second time in his life, chosen the world over her.

I intended to tell her myself. Someday. When there was a someday worth confessing in.

First, we had a conclave to rob.
