## 10. Sigils Rising

There is a particular cruelty in losing your grandmother twice.

The first time, I lost her the way everyone loses people: slowly, then all at once, in a bed that smelled of beeswax and camphor while I held her hand and told her the shop would be fine. The second time happened on a Tuesday, somewhere on the road between nowhere and Corvane, when Dominic asked me what her voice sounded like and I opened my mouth to answer and found nothing there.

Not silence. Silence would have been honest. It was worse than silence — it was the shape of an answer with the middle scooped out. I knew I had known. I could feel the hole where knowing used to be, the way you can feel a tooth that isn't there anymore. Your tongue keeps going back to it.

"Brown," I said finally. "Her voice was brown."

Dominic didn't laugh, which somehow made it worse. A crueler man would have laughed, and I could have hated him for it, and hating someone is very nearly a comfort when everything else is being taken.

"That's not a voice," he said gently.

"It was hers."

* * *

Here is how the arithmetic works, for anyone keeping score, which I am, obsessively, in a cheap ledger book I bought from a peddler who charged me double because my hands were shaking:

Every sigil I use costs a memory.

Not a random memory. Not a memory of, say, the capital of some province I never cared about, or the name of the boy who pulled my hair when I was nine. No. The sigils are selective in the way of all predators. They take what is most mine. The oldest things. The warmest things. The things I would least choose to give.

I have used my power four times since we fled the valley. Four times, four payments, and the ledger reads like this:

*Payment one:* the smell of the candle shop on a winter morning. I know it smelled like something. I know I loved it. I cannot retrieve it. It's like a room in my own house that has been bricked over while I slept.

*Payment two:* my grandmother's laugh. Gone. I remember that she laughed — I remember *that* — but the sound itself has been repossessed. Somewhere in me there is a debt collector walking off with the only recording of a woman's joy.

*Payment three:* the taste of honey bread. This one I barely noticed until I ate some at a roadside stall and it tasted like nothing, like sweetened paper, like a description of bread rather than bread. I cried into it, which did not improve the flavor.

*Payment four:* her face.

I'm saving the worst for last in this accounting because I am a coward even on paper. Her face. I sat down two nights ago to draw her from memory — a practical measure, I told myself, preservation through reproduction — and my pencil made a woman-shaped nothing. I drew eyes. Whose? I drew a mouth. Was hers wide or thin? Did she have a mole? Everyone's grandmother has a mole, surely, statistically, but whether *mine* did is information that has been burned as fuel for a shield I threw up against a hunting wind on the salt road.

The shield worked beautifully. That's the obscene part. It worked *beautifully*, silver light pouring out of my wrist like water out of a broken jug, and the hunters turned away blinded, and Dominic looked at me with something like awe, and I stood there glowing like a small holy disaster while the face of the woman who raised me finished burning off my soul.

Awe. He gave me awe. I wanted someone to give me a funeral.

* * *

The journal was Dominic's idea, originally, though I've made it mine the way I make everything mine — stubbornly and with too much ink.

"Write yourself down," he said, the morning after the honey bread incident. He said it carefully, the way you'd approach a wounded animal, or a stove. "Everything. Everything you don't want to lose. Every day. Write it like—"

"Like scripture," I said, because I'd already been thinking it, and saying it first stole some of his gentleness away, which I needed at the time. Gentleness was unbearable. Gentleness assumed I was breakable, and I couldn't afford to be breakable, so I took the idea and armored it in sarcasm where he'd meant it as a kindness.

But I do it. Every night, by whatever light we can afford, I write myself down like scripture. Not the grand parts — those survive fine, the sigils don't eat drama — but the small parts, the load-bearing parts. Here are actual entries, transcribed faithfully, because if this account survives me I want whoever finds it to know exactly what kind of madwoman wrote it:

*Day 12. Grandmother's hands: flour in the knuckle creases always, no matter the season. She kneaded bread the way other women prayed. Her left thumbnail had a white mark on it shaped like a crescent moon and she claimed she'd been touched by a fairy, which in the Hollow Valley was either a joke or blasphemy depending on the week.*

*Day 13. The shop bell. Brass, cracked, with a sound like a spoon dropped in an empty pot. Customers complained. She refused to replace it. "A bell that rings true tells you nothing about the person coming in." I don't know what that means. I need to keep it anyway.*

*Day 14. I am frightened. Correction: I am terrified, and I am writing it down because terror is also a thing I am apparently allowed to lose, and I refuse. If the sigils want my fear they can come and take it themselves, and then at least I'll see their faces.*

*Day 15. Woke up missing the name of my childhood cat. I had a childhood cat. I am certain of this the way you're certain of a scar. Dominic says naming it helps. His name was — his name was —*

I never finished Day 15. There's a blank line where the cat's name should be, and above it, in the margin, in handwriting that is mine but hurried, mine but wrong, a single word: *don't*.

I have decided not to think about that word. I have decided this firmly and repeatedly, which is another way of saying I think about it constantly.

* * *

Dominic pretends not to watch me write, which is its own kind of tenderness. He sits across the fire sharpening a knife that hasn't needed sharpening in weeks, or mending a cloak that doesn't tear, performing usefulness so that I can grieve without an audience. Once, thinking I was asleep, he picked up the ledger. I let him get three pages in before I spoke.

"Any good?"

He flinched like I'd drawn a blade. "It's—" He closed it carefully, aligning the edges, buying time with precision. "You write her alive," he said. "That's all. You write her so alive it hurts to read."

"She's dead either way," I said. "In here." I tapped my temple. "The journal's just the grave marker. At least a grave marker is something strangers can visit."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." I held up a hand, and the hand glowed faintly at the wrist, the newest sigil stirring like a dog hearing its name, and we both looked at it, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

Because here is the other thing, the thing underneath the grief, the thing that makes the grief worse: the sigils aren't done arriving. They come on their own now. I'll wake with fresh lines inked across my skin — cool, precise, patient — clauses in a contract I never signed, written in a language I read fluently and hate completely. And each new sigil arrives with a faint pressure behind it, a suggestion, almost polite: *you could use me. You could be safe. All it costs is—*

"All it costs is me," I told it aloud once, sitting alone on watch while Dominic slept. "Do you understand that? There won't be anyone left to spend."

The sigil, being ink, said nothing. But it pulsed once, softly, the way a merchant smiles when you've misunderstood the terms in his favor.

* * *

We made Corvane's outskirts by the end of the second week, and I want to record, for posterity, my honest first impression of the free city: it smells like every prayer ever prayed, and prayers smell like panic.

Altars everywhere. Little ones, big ones, ones crammed between a fishmonger and a cobbler like shrines were a trade goods category. People pressing coins and blood and hair and promises onto them, murmuring, bargaining, and — this was the part that stopped me in the street — *getting answers*. Small answers. A boil healed. A debt forgiven. A lover's eye turned. The whole city humming with transactions, and every transaction leaving a faint shimmer in the air that only I seemed to see, threads of light running from mortal mouths up into a sky that was drinking them.

"You feel it," Dominic said quietly. Not a question.

"I feel like a coin purse walking through a market full of cutpurses."

"That's accurate."

But that's the next part of the story, and I'm getting ahead of myself, because tonight — tonight is the bad night, and this account is supposed to be honest, and honesty requires me to set down what happened by the fire outside the walls, before the city, before everything after.

* * *

I was doing the evening's entry. Grandmother's recipe for tallow-and-lavender candles, which I'd recovered partially two days prior and rushed to preserve. Halfway through the sentence, my hand stopped.

Not cramped. Stopped. Like a hand obeying an order from somewhere else.

And then it kept writing.

I watched it happen. I want that on the record: I was *present* for it, fully conscious, fully Tamsin, while my hand moved across the page in my own handwriting and wrote words I had not chosen. The candlelight guttered. The sigils along my forearm warmed like stones left in sun. And the hand wrote:

*She will ask you for everything and you will want to say yes. Remember: a whole self bought from a god is still owned by the god. Count what remains yours. Count it twice.*

Then the hand lifted off the page, and the warmth drained out of my arm, and I sat there in the cold with my heart going like a war drum, staring at words in my own script that I had no memory of composing.

"Dominic," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Apparently some part of me had decided that if I was going to be haunted, I'd be haunted with dignity.

He was awake instantly — the man sleeps like a cat in a house full of dogs — and he read the entry, and I watched his face do several things quickly and settle on grim.

"When did you write this?" he asked.

"I didn't. That's rather the point."

"Tamsin." He set the book down between us like it might bite. "This is your handwriting from years ago. The slant's different. The pressure. This is old."

"Old how? I've owned this book eleven days."

He didn't answer, and his not-answering was louder than most men's shouting, and I understood, in a slow cold slide, that he knew something about what I was that he hadn't told me. That maybe the sigils weren't arriving at all. Maybe they were *returning*. Maybe the ledger I was keeping wasn't a record of what I was losing but a transcript of something I'd already lived through once and paid for once and — 

"Tomorrow," I said, before he could speak, because if he spoke first I'd hear pity in it. "Tomorrow we go into Corvane, and we find out who's been signing contracts with my skin. And Dominic?" I closed the journal on my own ghost's handwriting. "If you're keeping secrets from me to protect me, understand that protection is starting to look a lot like what the gods did. They took pieces of my life 'for my own good' too."

The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the walls, ten thousand prayers rose into a hungry sky.

"I know," Dominic said. "I know. Tomorrow."

I believed him the way you believe weather. And I wrote one final line by the last of the light, plain and deliberate, in the present tense, where my past selves can't reach it:

*Whatever I agreed to before I remember agreeing — I reserve the right to refuse it now.*
