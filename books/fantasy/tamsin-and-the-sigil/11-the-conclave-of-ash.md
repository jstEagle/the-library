## 11. The Conclave of Ash

*Tamsin*

The temple had been a temple once. Now it was mostly suggestion — a ribcage of black stone on the hill above Corvane, its roof long since surrendered to the sky, its columns leaning like old men who'd agreed to hold up nothing in particular. Ash drifted down from somewhere above us, fine and grey, though there was no fire. It simply fell, the way it had fallen for centuries, the way it would fall long after everyone currently breathing had stopped.

"They call it the Conclave of Ash," Dominic said, "because of the ash."

"I'd assumed it was named after someone."

"No. The gods are not subtle. It's one of their few virtues."

We stood at the base of the hill with perhaps two hundred pilgrims, all of us dressed in the grey-and-white of the Penitent Order, which Maerin had assured us was the one order no god bothered to vet because penitents were, by definition, beneath notice. I had to admit the disguise worked on me. I looked at the crowd of bowed heads and threadbare hems and felt an immediate, reflexive urge to confess something. Anything. I'd stolen a ribbon when I was nine. I resented my grandmother's candle shop for most of my adolescence. I was carrying a god's contract inked into my skin like a debt collector's ledger.

That last one wasn't technically a confession. It was more of a summons.

"Rules," Dominic murmured, adjusting the hood over his face. He looked wrong in penitent's grey, like a wolf in a choir robe, but he'd shaved the storm-grey stubble from his jaw and stooped his shoulders, and apparently that was enough. "We don't speak unless spoken to. We don't look up during the Rite of Attendance — looking up is a request, and requests get answered. And if Ossuar's people come near you, you let me handle it."

"And if *Veyra's* people come near me?"

His jaw tightened under the hood. "Then we have a different problem, because Veyra doesn't send people. She sends invitations."

He was right about that. She'd sent one to me three days ago, folded out of candle-smoke while I was trying to sleep in a rented room that smelled of tallow and other people's prayers. *Come witness,* she'd written, in handwriting like frost forming. *See what your gods intend for you. Then choose.* No threat attached. That was the worst part. Veyra never threatened; she just arranged things so that refusing her felt like your own idea going badly.

I hadn't told Dominic about the invitation until this morning, on the road, when lying seemed suddenly expensive. He'd gone very quiet in the way he did instead of shouting, which I was learning was worse than shouting.

"You could have said no," he said now.

"To a goddess? You've met her. Saying no to Veyra is like saying no to weather. You can do it, but you'll still be wet."

A bell tolled somewhere inside the ruin — a sound with weight to it, the kind of tolling you feel in your back teeth. The pilgrims began to move up the hill, and we moved with them, two more anonymous penitents climbing toward a conclave where the subject under discussion was whether I got to exist.

I want to say I was afraid. I was, but fear is a blunt word for what I felt. What I felt was closer to the vertigo of standing at the edge of a well you've just realized is bottomless — except the well was my own life, and everyone at the rim was arguing about who owned the rope.

* * *

*Dominic*

Here is what nobody tells you about gods: they are boring.

Mortals imagine divine politics as thunder and revelation, thrones of bone, voices that crack mountains. In truth, having now attended a conclave, I can report that the gods conduct their business the way merchants conduct theirs — with seating charts, precedence disputes, and long stretches of rhetorical throat-clearing. The difference is that when a merchant loses an argument, he loses coin. When a god loses one, somebody's country changes hands, or a river learns to run uphill, or a girl from a valley that forgets stops being a person and becomes a clause.

Tamsin and I knelt among the penitents in the western gallery, close enough to see the floor of the ruined nave, far enough to be furniture. Below us, the factions had divided themselves along a seam of ash that nobody had drawn but everybody respected. On the southern side: Veyra's coalition, a dozen gods of thresholds and tides and small mercies, arrayed in forms they'd chosen for the occasion — beautiful, restrained, deliberately human-adjacent. Veyra herself wore the shape of a woman in travelling clothes, unremarkable except that your eyes kept sliding back to her the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

On the northern side: Ossuar.

I use the word "side" loosely, because Ossuar brought no coalition. Ossuar brought absence. Where the hollow god stood, the ash didn't fall; it hung, suspended, as if even gravity had decided not to be noticed. His form was a tall vacancy shaped vaguely like a robed figure, and looking directly at him produced a sensation I can only describe as reading a page and finding the words gone. Mortals who worshipped him didn't build altars. They built empty rooms.

Between them, presiding over nothing, sat the Remnant — whatever was left of the original covenant's arbiter, a presence like a hearth that had gone cold but not yet been swept. It did not speak. It had not spoken in a thousand years. Everyone pretended this was ceremonial. Everyone knew it was a corpse at the head of the table.

"The Seal has been located," Veyra said, and her voice reached every ear in the ruin without seeming to travel. "Corvane. As foretold. The covenant can be reforged, and the realms bound whole again — under stewardship."

"Under ownership," said the vacancy that was Ossuar. His voice was worse than silence. It was the sound a room makes when everyone leaves it. "You mistake the instrument for the music, sister-of-thresholds. The seal was made to *end* something. Let it end."

I felt Tamsin go rigid beside me. I put two fingers lightly on her wrist — the wrist, the first sigil, the key-shaped mark that had started all of this — a touch that meant *still, steady, later.* She breathed. We'd been practicing that.

"You would leave mortals naked," Veyra said.

"I would leave mortals *honest.*" The ash shivered around Ossuar's absence. "Your covenant caged us behind a wall of one child's stolen years, and you call that mercy. I call it theft wearing mercy's coat. Tear the seal apart. Let the wall fall. Let them pray to what actually hears them, and learn what prayer costs when it is answered in full."

Somewhere in the gallery, a penitent began quietly weeping. Nobody looked at him. Looking up, remember, is a request.

And here is the thing I need to set down honestly, because Tamsin will read this journal someday — she reads everything I write now, since the night she caught me drafting contingency plans and made me explain each one — here is the thing: for one full breath, some cold pragmatic cellar in my mind agreed with Ossuar. Not with the cruelty of it. With the clarity. The covenant had been built on Tamsin's erased childhood. Every year of peace between the realms had been purchased with a life she hadn't consented to live. There was an argument — a real one — that the cleanest justice was to let the whole rotten structure fall.

Then I looked at her kneeling beside me, jaw set, watching the creatures debating her existence with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she already knows ends badly, and the cellar door shut. Justice that requires her destruction isn't justice. It's just arithmetic with better manners.

Veyra was still speaking. "The Seal consents," she said, and the lie landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone. "She has prayed. She has accepted guidance. She will stand at the Worldspine and be reforged willingly, and the age of closed doors will end in light rather than ruin."

"She has done no such thing," Tamsin whispered beside me, so quietly only I could hear, and I heard the fear under the anger, because Veyra wasn't lying exactly. Tamsin *had* prayed. Tamsin had prayed for shelter, and Veyra had answered, and every answer was a signature, and every signature was being read aloud right now as consent.

That was the political horror of the conclave, if you want it in one sentence: the gods were negotiating over a document written in a language only they could parse, and the document was a person, and the person was kneeling twenty feet above them pretending to be furniture.

Ossuar turned. I felt it before I saw it — a coldness moving through the gallery like a draft under a door. The vacancy that served him for a face inclined toward our section of penitents, unhurried, and stopped.

It stopped on us.

No. Not on us. On *her.*

"You brought her here yourself," Ossuar said, and his voice was soft the way a grave is soft. "Steward of storms. Exile. You walked the Seal into my reach and called it witnessing." A pause, in which the ash hung utterly still. "How generous of you. I accept the gift."

Two hundred penitents knelt in perfect ignorance around us. Veyra's eyes found ours across the nave, and for the first time since I'd known her, the goddess of thresholds looked genuinely surprised — which meant, I realized with a lurch, that she hadn't planned this either, that Tamsin's presence was a card even she hadn't known she held, and that both factions now believed the other had engineered our arrival.

Which meant neither of them controlled the board anymore.

Which meant, for the moment, we were the only free agents in the building.

"Tamsin," I breathed. "When I move — don't run. Walk. Pilgrims walk."

"Where are we walking *to?*"

"Away from the nice empty gentleman." Beneath my sleeve, my hand had begun to ache — the old exile-scar, the oath-brand that had strangled my storm-calling for seven years. Except it wasn't strangling anything tonight. Tonight it burned like a struck flint, and outside the ruined walls, past the hills, past the coast, I could feel weather gathering that had no business existing this far inland, answering a man who had no right to call it.

The Remnant stirred. Cold hearth, silent corpse — and yet the whole ruin tilted toward it, gods and penitents alike, as the dead arbiter's attention settled on the nave like snowfall.

And then it spoke. First time in a thousand years, and what it said was:

"*The Seal will approach the altar.*"

Not a request. Not Veyra's velvet or Ossuar's void. Something older than both, speaking with the flat authority of a law nobody remembered passing.

Tamsin's hand found mine in the dark of our sleeves.

"Well," she whispered. "So much for walking away."
