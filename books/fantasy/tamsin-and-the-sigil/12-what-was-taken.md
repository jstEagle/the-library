## 12. What Was Taken

I have been grabbed by a lot of things in my life. Grandmothers, mostly, and once a very determined goat named Buttercup who had opinions about my hair. But I had never been grabbed by a god before, and I want it on the record that the experience is deeply overrated.

Ossuar's hand closed around my wrist — the one with the oldest sigil, the key-shaped birthmark that had started this whole catastrophe — and the world went white and then very, very quiet.

Not silent. Quiet is different. Silence is an absence. This was a presence, like being held underwater by something that loved me.

*Little seal,* said a voice that was not a voice so much as a pressure behind my eyes. *Let us look at what you are.*

"I'd rather not," I said, because apparently my survival instinct had been replaced entirely by sarcasm somewhere around chapter nine.

The hollow god did not answer. Hollow gods, Maerin had warned us on the road to the ruined temple, don't argue. They *empty*. And Ossuar began, with the patience of something that had been waiting since before there were calendars, to pour me out.

* * *

Here is what I remembered of my childhood before that moment: a candle shop that smelled of beeswax and tallow. Grandmother Vale's hands, cracked and strong, folding wicks. The particular gray light of the Hollow Valley, which never quite warmed and never quite darkened. A dog named Sorrel who died when I was nine and whom I still missed with a specific, embarrassing ache. My first kiss behind the tannery at fourteen, which was wetter and less romantic than anyone admits. Learning to trim a wick so it wouldn't smoke. Learning to count change without looking. Learning, slowly, to be a person made entirely of small things.

It was a good life. It was a small life. I had never thought to question its size, because who questions the walls of the house they were born in?

Ossuar took those walls down like a child dismantling a fence.

* * *

The first thing that came back was not a memory. It was a smell: incense, but not the cheap kind Grandmother sold to tourists. Something resinous and old, burned in a stone room where the ceiling was painted with constellations that didn't match our sky. I was small. I was sitting cross-legged on cold flagstones. A man's voice was reciting words in a language I shouldn't have known and understood perfectly.

My father's voice.

I had no father. That was one of the facts of my life, filed neatly next to *the valley has no magic* and *you were born here, Tamsin*. Grandmother had told me my parents died when I was an infant, in a fire, far away, and grief for them had been like grief for a photograph — real, but secondhand.

Except now I could hear him. Covenant-priest Aldous Vale, third of his line to keep the terms, and he was saying: *"Say it back, little star."*

And I did. I said it back. I was four years old and I recited the opening clause of the covenant between the realms, all of it, flawlessly, while he wept quietly in a way he thought I couldn't see.

The memory hit me like cold water and I made a sound I'm not proud of. Somewhere above me, distantly, Dominic was shouting my name. Veyra's voice cut through the temple like a blade through silk, furious, demanding. None of it mattered. Ossuar held my wrist and poured, and I drank.

* * *

More came. It came the way floodwater comes — first a trickle under the door, then the door is gone.

I remembered the tower study where my father kept the covenant texts, and how the candles in it never guttered because the room itself was under contract. I remembered my mother, Sera, who had laughed like a hinge that needed oiling and who braided my hair each morning with a charm worked into every plait — not magic, exactly, but *adjacent*, the way a lock is adjacent to a key. I remembered that I was happy. That's the part that gutted me. I wasn't a sad child stolen from a sad life. I was a *loved* child, doted on, taught, sung to, and every single adult around me knew what was coming, and they smiled at me anyway, every day, knowing.

Because I was five when they explained it to me. Not the whole of it — no one explains the whole of anything to a five-year-old — but enough. There was a war coming, or rather a resumption of an old war, because the covenant that kept the divine realm folded away from ours was fraying, and fraying covenants need mending, and mending requires payment, and the payment had been agreed upon generations ago and written in a hand I would later see half-eaten by rot in Maerin's satchel:

*A life freely given, sealed in blood, its memory the binding.*

They sat me down in the tower study. My father knelt so we were eye to eye. My mother held my hands. And they asked me. They actually asked me, which I think was meant to be a mercy and might have been the cruelest thing anyone ever did, because they made a five-year-old complicit in her own erasure.

*"If you say yes,"* my father said, *"you will forget us. You will forget this tower, and your name-day feasts, and Sorrel's puppies, and everything. But the realms will stay closed, and no god will ever again burn a city to prove a point. Do you understand?"*

And I — five years old, solemn as a magistrate, already trained in the gravity of old words — said: *"Will everyone be safe?"*

*"Yes."*

*"Then yes."*

I said yes. I *chose* it. And then they performed the unsealing-that-was-a-sealing, and my mother's hands shook so badly she couldn't finish the last braid, and my father carried me sleeping to the edge of the Hollow Valley and gave me to a woman named Vale who owed the priesthood a debt older than either of them, and he stood at the border of the silence for three days, they told me later — told the me that no longer existed — until the Warden made him leave.

His last words to my sleeping self were: *"Grow up ordinary, little star. It's the only gift we can give you that they can't take back."*

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor of the ruined temple with my cheek against cold stone and someone's fingers digging into my shoulder, and I was screaming. I don't know for how long. My throat felt flayed.

"Tamsin." Dominic's face swam into focus above mine, gray-skinned, storm-light flickering weakly in his eyes — his dead magic doing something strange and stuttering near my sigils, as it always did. "Tamsin, look at me. What did it show you?"

"Everything," I rasped. Then, because the wry narrator in my head had apparently survived the drowning: "Well. Everything up to age five. Turns out I peaked early."

He didn't laugh. Neither did I, really. It came out as another sound halfway between a sob and a hiccup, and I let him haul me upright because my legs had unionized and decided against work.

Around us, the Conclave of Ash continued its terrible business. Veyra stood on the broken altar-dais in her mantle of woven dawnlight, and even from across the ruin I could feel her attention on me like sun through a magnifying glass. She had wanted this — I understood that now with sickening clarity. Every kindness, every offer, every *I can give you back what was taken*: she had known precisely what was taken, because her faction had helped take it. She hadn't been offering me a gift. She'd been offering me a receipt.

And Ossuar — Ossuar was a shape of absence at the temple's far end, a hole in the world wearing the suggestion of a gaunt figure, and where it stood the torches burned without illuminating anything. It had unsealed me not out of mercy. Of course not. It had done it because a Seal who knows what she is can be *negotiated with*, and a Seal who knows her own consent was given freely —

Oh.

Oh, that was the trap. I saw it unfold in my mind with horrible elegance. The covenant required a life *freely given*. My five-year-old consent was the load-bearing wall of the entire pact. Ossuar hadn't shown me my past to wound me. It had shown me my past to *prove* to me that I had chosen this — that the seal was legitimate, that my erasure was contractual, that if I dissolved it now I would be breaking my own word, spoken in a child's voice twenty years ago.

"You're wondering if they're right," Maerin murmured. She had appeared at my elbow, defrocked and defiant, her grip white-knuckled on her walking staff. "That you agreed. That it's binding."

"Wasn't it?"

"It was." Her face was old and tired and honest. "That's what makes it monstrous, child. The best cages are built from willing sacrifices. But a bargain made by a five-year-old on behalf of every mortal soul alive isn't consent. It's *theft wearing consent's clothes.*"

Across the ruin, Ossuar's hollow voice finally spoke aloud, and every candle in the temple bowed toward it like grass in wind.

"*She remembers. The terms stand. The Seal is proven willing.*"

"The terms," Veyra countered, silk over steel, "*stand* — which is why the conclave must reforge the pact under proper stewardship, rather than let it rot in a—"

"They're arguing about ownership," I said numbly. "They're standing in front of me arguing about who owns me, and neither of them has said my name once."

Dominic's jaw tightened. "Veyra will. When it profits her."

"No." I found my feet properly. My sigils were burning — all of them, the whole constellation of inked clauses crawling my arms and collarbone, lit from beneath like coals — and I realized with distant surprise that I wasn't afraid anymore. Something else had moved in where the fear had been. It felt like the moment in the dream when you realize you're dreaming and the rules loosen. "You know what I keep coming back to? Not the tower. Not the erasure. My father stood at the valley border for three days. He *wept* reciting clauses to a four-year-old. They weren't monsters, any of them. They were people doing arithmetic with a child's life because the alternative was cities burning, and they hated every digit." I swallowed. "And it was still wrong. It was wrong the way a well-built gallows is well-built."

"Tamsin—" Dominic started.

"I'm not breaking my word," I said. "I'm *renegotiating* it. There's a difference, and I think it's the whole difference, and I think it's why Maerin kept that rotten text in her bag for thirty years."

Maerin looked at me for a long moment. Something shifted in her face — recognition, maybe, or dread, or both. "You've read ahead," she said quietly. "In the fragments."

"A covenant-priest's daughter doesn't forget clause structure," I said, and heard my father's cadence come out of my mouth, and had to stop and breathe through the fresh grief of it. "The original text has a dissolution clause. Article Nine. It needs a living signatory of the founding line, freely consenting, at a site where the realms touch." I turned to Dominic. "The Worldspine altar."

"That's suicide," he said flatly. "Both factions will—"

"Yes," I agreed. "Probably. But here's the thing nobody at this lovely conclave has considered." I raised my wrist, and the key-shaped sigil flared, and for one heartbeat every torch in the ruined temple bent toward *me* instead of the gods. "I'm not the vault anymore. I'm the clerk. And clerks can amend the filing system."

The silence that followed lasted exactly long enough for me to feel triumphant.

Then Ossuar moved, and the triumph curdled, because a hollow god does not debate. It simply reaches. The darkness at the temple's heart unfolded like a lung, and Veyra screamed something in a language that predated language, and Dominic grabbed my arm and shouted the one word I'd been dreading and hoping for since the salt flats:

"*Run!*"

We ran. Behind us, the Conclave of Ash stopped being a meeting and became a war, and Maerin's staff struck sparks off the stones as she led us toward the stairwell that went, impossibly, *down* — toward the Under Roads, toward whatever came next.

I carried my father's voice with me as I fled. *Grow up ordinary, little star.*

Too late, Papa, I thought, sprinting into the dark with two gods at my heels. But I'm going to grow up *honest*.
