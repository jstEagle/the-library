## 13. The Long Way Down

The priest found us before the gods did, which I have since decided was either providence or very good bookkeeping.

We'd been three days in the foothills below Corvane, moving at night, sleeping in drainage culverts and the occasional generous barn, and I had reached that particular stage of exhaustion where your own thoughts start charging you rent. Tamsin slept against my pack with her journal open on her chest, one hand curled around it like a child holding a blanket. Every few hours she'd wake, read a page, frown at herself, and write something new. I pretended not to watch. I was bad at pretending.

"You're the storm-caller," said a voice from the dark, "the one who broke the world's oldest contract over a girl who didn't survive to thank you."

I had a knife out before my eyes finished opening. Old habits. The figure standing at the mouth of the culvert was small, grey-robed, and unimpressed by my blade in the way only certain people can be — the way of someone who has been threatened by professionals.

"And you're dead," I said. "Maerin Solenne. Defrocked eleven years ago. The conclave burned your name off the rolls."

"They missed a copy." She stepped into the moonlight. She was older than her reputation — sixty, maybe, with a face like a folded map and hands that looked like they'd done honest work after the dishonest kind. "And you're being followed by two divine factions, one of whom wants to wear your friend as a crown and the other of whom wants to crack her open like an egg. You need a guide into the Under Roads. I'm the only guide left who knows them. Shall we skip the part where you threaten me and I let you?"

Tamsin stirred. "Dom," she mumbled, "who's the grandmother?"

"Guide," I said.

"Assassin," Maerin corrected pleasantly.

"Grandmother," Tamsin decided, and went back to sleep. That, more than anything, is what made me trust her. Nobody sane trusts a stranger that fast. It takes a specific kind of tired.

* * *

The entrance to the Under Roads was behind a waterfall two valleys east, which sounds romantic until you're walking through it. The water hit like a fist and then, abruptly, stopped hitting at all, because the passage behind it was cut so precisely that the fall sheeted past the doorway without touching the threshold. Ancient work. Pre-covenant work, if Maerin was to be believed, and she believed it the way other people believe in breathing.

"Mind the third step," she said, lighting a lamp that burned without oil. A small magic. Harmless, she assured us. I kept my hand near my knife anyway. Exile teaches you that harmless magic is just violence running late.

The Under Roads were nothing like I'd imagined, which was tunnels. They were roads — proper ones, wide enough for four abreast, with gutters and milestones and the ghost-architecture of rest stops: alcoves where benches had once stood, niches for lamps, carved reliefs worn smooth by centuries of travelers' hands. And everywhere, everywhere, writing.

"What is all this?" Tamsin asked. Her voice did the thing it did when she touched a sigil — went quiet and reverent and slightly afraid, as if the words might answer.

"Covenant text." Maerin ran her fingers along a wall inscription without reading it, the way you'd touch a banister in your own house. "The original. Not the version the conclave recites — theirs is a summary. This is the full document, or pieces of it. The Under Roads were built to carry it. Every mile of stone holds a clause."

Tamsin stopped walking. "You're saying there's a law written under half the world."

"I'm saying there's a *contract* written under half the world, and laws are what contracts become when everyone forgets who signed them." Maerin glanced back at us, lamp-light pooling in the creases of her face. "That's the first thing they take from you in seminary. Not knowledge — context. They teach you the covenant as scripture. Divine ordinance, handed down whole. It wasn't. It was negotiated."

"Negotiated with who?" I asked, though I already dreaded the answer, because I've broken this covenant once and I knew exactly how lopsided my understanding of it had been.

"With mortals." She said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "Co-authored. Clause by clause, altar by altar, over generations. The gods brought power; we brought terms. Prayer freely given, answered in measure. Boundaries between realms. Rights of refusal — do you know there used to be a right of refusal? A mortal could look a god in the eye and say no, and the god had to hear it. There's a wall forty miles from here that says so in letters taller than you."

Tamsin's hand drifted to her wrist, to the faded scar-lines beneath her sleeve. "Then what happened?"

"The same thing that happens to every contract." Maerin's voice didn't change, but something behind it did — a door closing somewhere far away. "One side got stronger. The clauses about mortal authorship were quietly reclassified as commentary. Then as tradition. Then as heresy. By the time I was ordained, we sang hymns about the covenant's divine origin, and anyone who asked questions got a career like mine." She smiled, thin as paper. "Defrocked. Exiled. Alive, which is more than most questioners managed."

We walked on. The lamp threw our shadows long down the road, and the old writing kept pace beside us, patient as sediment.

* * *

We made camp that night in one of the rest alcoves, and I learned two things about Maerin Solenne: she carried dried plums and would share them grudgingly, and she could cook a passable stew over a lamp-flame through means she declined to explain.

Tamsin sat cross-legged with her journal on her knees, and I watched her do the thing that had started to break my heart in installments: she read herself. Page by page, checking. *My name is Tamsin Vale. My grandmother's name was Edith. Edith made candles and hummed when she worked.* Testing each fact against whatever remained inside her, like a woman counting coins after a pickpocket passed through.

"How much is gone now?" Maerin asked. No preamble. Assassins and priests, I suppose, share a distaste for small talk.

Tamsin's pen paused. "Faces mostly. I have the names but the faces go soft when I reach for them. And last week I lost—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "There was a dog. I know there was a dog because I wrote about it. Brown, I think. I don't remember it."

"That's the seal spending you," Maerin said. "Every miracle draws on the account."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a purse." Tamsin closed the journal. Her knuckles were white around it. "Everyone keeps saying it like that. Veyra. The Warden. Now you. Like I'm a resource that happens to talk."

For a moment Maerin said nothing. Then she set down her spoon and looked at Tamsin with an expression I couldn't parse until later, when I understood it had been guilt wearing patience as a mask.

"You're not a purse," she said. "You're a signature. There's a difference, and it matters, and I'll tell you why when you're ready to hate me for it."

"Cheerful," I muttered.

"You wanted hope, storm-caller? Hope is cheerful. Truth is load-bearing." She pointed her spoon at the wall behind us, at the worn-smooth relief of two figures — one crowned, one not — both holding a document between them, neither holding it alone. "But here's your hope anyway, since you're sulking. The covenant was co-authored. Which means it can be *re*-authored. The gods didn't build that lock. They inherited it, and they've been squatting ever since. Squatters can be evicted. It's in the text. I helped draft some of the relevant clauses myself, back when I still thought the conclave could be argued with."

Tamsin stared at the carving. Two figures. Equal hands. Something moved across her face — not recognition exactly, but its shadow, the shape recognition leaves behind.

"I dreamed about this," she said quietly. "Before. In the valley. A room with a table, and voices arguing, and someone laughing — laughing while they wrote. Gods don't laugh like that, do they?"

"No," Maerin said softly. "They don't. Mortals do. Mortals wrote the best parts of it, child. Remember that, whatever else happens."

* * *

On the fourth day underground, the hunters found us anyway.

Not Veyra's — hers announce themselves, all gold light and trumpeting pressure, a god's idea of a knock. These were quieter. Ossuar's people, if you could call them people: hollow-eyed pilgrims who walked without lamps and whose shadows fell the wrong direction. We heard them before we saw them, which was the sound of a road going silent — the drip of water stopping, the faint eternal hum of the covenant-stone cutting out, as if the dark itself had held its breath.

"Off the road," Maerin breathed. "Now. Side gallery, fifty paces. Move."

We moved. I got Tamsin into the gallery and turned to cover Maerin, and that was when I felt it — the wrongness rolling up the main road like cold off a lake. Ossuar's faithful didn't hunt. They *unmade*, quietly, the way rot works. One of them paused at the mouth of our gallery, head tilting, listening for heartbeats.

I did the stupid thing. Of course I did. I stepped out onto the road and put myself between them and the dark of the gallery, and I reached for my storm.

Nothing. Dead, as always, as it had been since exile — except. Except for a flicker. A thread of it, thin as wire, humming somewhere deep in my chest, and I knew — I *knew* — it came from behind me, from Tamsin, from whatever the sigils had decided I was to her now. Conduit, anchor, accomplice. Take your pick.

"Hear me," I said to the hollow-eyed thing, buying seconds, my voice steadier than my knees. "Your master wants the Seal destroyed. Fine argument. But she's standing behind me, and I've broken one covenant already, and I am *very* good at breaking things."

It tilted its head further. Further. Past comfortable. And then, from the gallery behind me, Tamsin's voice — clear, furious, ringing off the old stone:

"*No.*"

One word. The right of refusal, spoken by a girl who'd never read the clause, and the thing on the road *flinched*. Flinched like a struck animal, shadows snapping straight, and fled — not retreated, fled, the silence peeling off the road behind it until the water-drip resumed and the covenant-stone hummed back to life like a struck bell settling.

I stood there shaking. Not from fear. From the feel of that word passing through me on its way, using my chest for a doorway.

Maerin emerged slowly. She looked at Tamsin. Looked at the wall. Looked at Tamsin again, and her face did something complicated.

"You've never been taught the rites," she said. "No one taught you the old forms. And you just invoked the First Clause by instinct."

"What clause?" Tamsin asked.

"The one that starts everything." Maerin laughed — short, disbelieving, almost young. "The one that says a mortal may refuse a god, and the god must hear it. The founders put it first on purpose. Everything else in the covenant is built on top of that single word." She shook her head. "The conclave will tell you the Seal is their property. Their lock, their key, their instrument. But you just proved the original authors disagree. Whatever you are, Tamsin Vale, you are not *only* theirs. You never were."

Tamsin looked down at her wrist, at the scars, and I watched her decide something. I watched it happen the way you watch weather change — not the outcome, just the turning.

"Then teach me," she said. "All of it. The clauses they buried. If I'm going to be spent, I want to know what I'm *for*."

Maerin bowed her head. When she raised it, her eyes were wet, and she pretended they weren't, and we all pretended along with her, because that's what family does.

* * *

That night, deeper in, we made camp in a chamber where the ceiling opened into a natural skylight — a crack in the world showing stars. Tamsin slept between us, journal under her hand, and for the first time since the valley she slept without checking herself against the pages first.

Maerin sat sharpening a knife that I suspected was ceremonial and possibly illegal.

"You knew her mother," I said quietly. Not a question. I'd watched her watch Tamsin all day, with that terrible tenderness people aim at ghosts.

"I knew her father," Maerin said. "Covenant-priest. Good man. Better than me — he asked the questions I was too careful to ask." She tested the blade's edge with her thumb. "He'd want me to tell you something, storm-caller, so I'll tell you and you can carry it however you like. What's coming — Veyra, Ossuar, the conclave, all of it — they think this ends with the Seal. Reforged or shattered, their choice, their property." She nodded toward the sleeping girl. "But the covenant was written by people who believed one voice saying *no* should stop a god. And tonight I heard that voice come out of her like it had been waiting in her bones since before she could walk. So no. I don't think this ends the way they've planned it. I think it ends the way it was *written* — and they've forgotten how that was."

I lay back and looked up through the crack at the stars, and for the first time since Corvane, since the salt flats, since the beginning of this whole wretched pilgrimage, I let myself imagine an ending that wasn't a grave.

Which was, naturally, when the lamp guttered blue and every piece of covenant-writing on the walls began, very softly, to glow.

Maerin was on her feet instantly. "That's not us," she whispered. "Something's reading the road."

Far below us, down miles of ancient stone, something answered.
