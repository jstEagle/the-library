## 14. Maerin's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a young woman deciding whether to forgive you, and I have heard it exactly once in three hundred years. It sounds like a candle guttering. It sounds like Dominic Eriksen pretending not to listen while listening with every bone in his body.

We were two days into the Under Roads when I finally told them.

I had been putting it off with the usual tools — practicality, route-finding, a lecture on which fungi glow and which merely want you to think they do. The Under Roads reward deflection. They are long enough to lose anything in: armies, gods, the thread of a conversation you dread finishing. But Tamsin Vale had been carrying the rotted scroll-case I'd given her for two days without opening it, because she was polite, and politeness in the young is a form of suspicion, and she had begun to suspect me.

"You keep looking at my wrist," she said that evening, in the way people say things when they've decided not to say them gently anymore. We'd made camp in a waystation carved by hands that predated the covenant — smooth basalt, benches worn to satin, a ceiling of pale stone that drank our firelight and gave back nothing. "When you think I'm asleep. You look at it like it's a debt."

"It is," I said.

Dominic's head came up from where he'd been fussing with his boots. He was very good at fussing with boots at moments that required him not to speak.

"I helped write it," I said. "The clause. The one that made you the seal."

* * *

Let me tell you how it happened, because the version the gods tell has music in it, and the version Tamsin deserved had none.

Three hundred years ago the realms were open, and everything came through. Not just gods — weather, plagues, hungers, things that wore the shapes of beloved dead. The war before the covenant lasted a generation, and a generation then meant most of everyone. I was a priest of the middle temple, which is to say I was twenty-six and certain, which are the same thing.

The conclave met at the Worldspine for eleven days. Eleven days of gods posturing in their borrowed grandeur and mortals — actual mortals, the ones who'd bled for every league of ground — drafting the actual text in a side chamber that smelled of tallow and wet wool. There were nine of us. I remember all their names, which is its own punishment. Halvard, who kept falling asleep. The twins from the river country who finished each other's clauses. Old Sef, who wept through the third day and wrote better than any of us for it.

And me. Maerin, youngest, quickest, the one they gave the hard clauses to because I could make an atrocity sound like bookkeeping.

Here is the problem we were solving, and I want you to actually hold it, because everyone since has preferred not to: the covenant needed a lock that no god could pick. Anything divine could be unmade by something divine. That was the whole disease. So the lock had to be mortal. But a mortal lock can die, can be killed, can be bribed — unless it wasn't one mortal. Unless it was distributed, scattered, held everywhere and nowhere.

That was the elegant answer. That was the answer we should have chosen. Thousands of keepers, each holding a fragment, none knowing they held it, the covenant woven into ordinary human memory like a thread through cloth. I proposed it. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger keeps records of this sort. I proposed the good version first.

It was voted down. Not by malice. By arithmetic. Distributing the seal across ten thousand minds meant ten thousand points of failure, ten thousand chances for a god to find one keeper and squeeze. A single seal, hidden in a single bloodline, warded inside a valley where magic itself went to die — one door, guarded absolutely, versus ten thousand doors guarded barely. The math was cruel and the math was right, and I have spent three centuries learning that being right is the most seductive lie there is.

So we needed a child. The seal had to be laid down early, before memory set like concrete, so the binding would take as instinct rather than knowledge. And it had to be willing — the covenant's own terms demanded consent, because a coerced seal was just another god's property waiting to change hands.

You see the trap we built for ourselves. A child too young to understand what she was consenting to, whose consent therefore had to be given by someone else on her behalf, which is not consent at all but a costume we put on it.

Her father was a covenant-priest. He understood better than any of us what failure meant — he'd buried a parish, after the third year of the war, all of them, and he carried the list of names folded against his heart until the day he handed me his daughter instead. He wept. I want to say that too. He wept while he did it, and he did it anyway, and I took the child from his arms and told him she would be safe and happy and never know, and I believed that mercy was the same thing as kindness.

Tamsin hadn't moved. She sat with her hands around a cup of tea she'd stopped drinking somewhere around *middle temple*, and her face was doing something I recognized from funerals: the careful stillness of someone holding themselves together by main strength.

"Say her name," she said quietly. "My father. Say his name."

"Corin Vale," I said. "He played the fiddle badly and sang anyway. He used to say the covenant was the only prayer he'd ever written that worked."

She closed her eyes. Dominic made a small sound, half a word, and swallowed it.

"And my mother?"

"Gave her blessing. Whether she ever forgave herself, I don't know. We left the valley before—" I stopped. Some truths arrive better in installments. "I don't know."

* * *

Here is what I have learned about good intentions, in the long classroom of my guilt: they are not a defense. They are a weapon that fires backward, slowly, over years, and the wound always lands on someone who wasn't in the room when the aim was taken.

I believed — truly, sincerely, with my whole certain twenty-six-year-old heart — that one life was a fair price for peace. Say it fast and it sounds reasonable. Say it slow and you hear what's hiding in it: *fair to whom?* Fairness priced by the people who don't pay. I did the accounting the way priests and generals and every well-meaning monster in history does the accounting: I put myself in the column of payers because I suffered the decision, and I put her in the column of goods purchased, and I called the ledger balanced.

A girl grew up smelling of candle wax, loving a grandmother who couldn't tell her the truth because the truth would crack the seal, and I called that peace. She dreamed of a burning tower and woke with her skin writing secrets she couldn't read, and I called that stability. Every answered prayer since the old gods stirred — every miracle, every healing, every wonder the world got back — was drawn against an account she never opened.

And the worst part, the part I've only admitted to this cave and these two witnesses: if the conclave met again tomorrow, with everything I now know, with her sitting across the table looking at me like that — I don't know that I'd vote differently. Because the war was real. Because the dead were real. Because someone always puts a burning world on one side of the scale and a child on the other, and someone always, always finds the child wanting.

That is what complicity is. Not villainy. Arithmetic performed by tired, decent people who mistake the weight of numbers for the weight of right.

* * *

"Why give me the text?" Tamsin asked, eventually. Her voice had gone flat and clean, the anger pressed down under it like coals under ash. "If you're confessing, why hand me the evidence?"

"Because it's yours." I drew the scroll-case from my pack and set it between us on the basalt bench. "Not metaphorically. The original covenant named its own keepers, and when the seal was laid, the master text passed to the seal's line. Corin's line. Yours. I've been carrying your inheritance for three hundred years like a thief who's forgotten how to spend what he stole."

She didn't touch it.

"There's more," I said, "and you'll hate it, so I'll say it plainly. The text is damaged. Rot — actual rot; paper remembers being a tree, and trees want to go back to dirt. Whole clauses gone. But what remains includes the dissolution rite. The way to unmake the seal without killing you. And it includes—" I hesitated, and hated myself for hesitating, because hesitation was how all of this started, nice people softening ugly sentences. "It includes the distribution clause. My clause. The one they rejected. Scattering the covenant across many keepers. It's in there, Tamsin. Written in my hand. The alternative nobody wanted."

"The one that wouldn't have cost anyone their childhood," Dominic said. Not a question. His voice had a storm-flat quality I'd learned to read as danger, though for once, I thought, the danger wasn't aimed at me. Or rather: it was aimed at me, and he was lending it to her.

"The one that wouldn't have cost anyone anything," I said. "Except the effort of guarding ten thousand doors instead of one. The gods called it impractical. What they meant was inconvenient. A single seal can be found. Can be bargained with. Can be *owned*. Ten thousand keepers scattered through mortal memory — that covenant belongs to everyone, which means it belongs to no one with the power to abuse it." I looked at the case, at her hands still not reaching for it. "They didn't reject it because it was weak. They rejected it because it was free."

The firelight did its best and failed. In the Under Roads even flames seem to whisper.

Tamsin reached out at last. She lifted the case the way you lift something that might be sleeping — carefully, with respect for what waking it might cost. Inside, the scroll lay in coils, browned, furred at the edges with grey rot, whole passages eaten to lace. But the header was intact, and the signatures, and the great wax seal of the conclave, cracked down the middle like a heart that had agreed to keep beating anyway.

"This is my father's name," she said. Her finger hovered over the ink without touching it. "He signed it. As her guardian. As—" Her voice caught and steadied. "As me."

"He signed believing he was saving the world," I said. "People generally are, when they do their worst work. That's the trick of it. Nobody signs their name to cruelty. They sign it to necessity."

"And you?" she asked. "What did you sign it to?"

I had three hundred years to prepare an answer and the honest one arrived in under a breath. "Ambition," I said. "I wanted to be the one who solved it. I wanted the others to see I was young and know I wasn't. A child paid for my vanity, Tamsin, along with everyone else's fear. I won't insult you by ranking the sins. Mine fits comfortably among them."

She was quiet a long moment. Then, in a voice that had nothing of forgiveness in it and something better — something like accuracy — she said:

"You kept it safe. Three hundred years, and you kept my inheritance safe. Why? If you believed the seal was necessary. If you believed the price was fair. Why carry the one document that could undo your life's work?"

Because I am a coward, I almost said. Because guilt makes excellent pack-mules. Because some nights the rot on that scroll smelled like the side chamber at the Worldspine and I couldn't breathe.

"Because I was wrong," I said. "And wrongness, unconfessed, becomes policy. I kept the proof that there had been another way, so that someday — if the seal ever broke, if the girl ever came asking — there would exist in the world one scrap of evidence that the price was chosen, not fated. That we chose it. That it could be unchosen." I spread my hands. "Confession is just evidence with a face on it."

* * *

Dominic spoke then, for the first time in an hour, and what he said surprised me, though little about him should have by then.

"The distribution clause. Could it work? Actually work — not as theory, not as a young priest's elegant answer. Now. With the seal already laid and fraying."

I considered lying, or at least hedging. Hedging had gotten me here. "It would need rewriting," I said. "The original draft assumed a fresh covenant, not a transplant. And it would need a mind that understands both divine law and mortal stubbornness — the text alone isn't enough; the rot ate the anchoring formulae. Someone would have to reconstruct them." I glanced at him. "Someone who once shattered a covenant, perhaps. Someone with a professional understanding of how such things break."

"That's not funny," Dominic said.

"No," I agreed. "It's a job description."

Tamsin had the scroll unrolled across her knees, tracing the eaten places with one fingertip, reading the gaps as carefully as the words. I watched her and felt the strange vertigo of the very old watching the very young: she was doing in an evening what the conclave had taken eleven days to do, and she was doing it with more honesty, because she was the only person at this fire who hadn't yet decided what the answer should be before asking the question.

"There's a clause here," she said suddenly. Her finger had stopped at a passage near the end, half-legible, the margins furred grey. "About amendment. About the covenant being amendable by—" she squinted— "*joint assent of signatories and seal.*" She looked up. "Maerin. Who counts as a signatory now? Everyone who signed is dust."

"Not quite," I said, and the words came out slower than I intended, because I was hearing them properly for the first time myself. "Signatories pass their standing to successors. That was standard covenant law — otherwise every pact dies with its drafters. The temples kept succession rolls. Most burned in the wars. But the middle temple's roll survived. I checked, about eighty years ago, when I still thought I was only curious." I paused. "There are living successors, Tamsin. Priests, defrocked priests, laykeepers — maybe forty souls who inherit signatory standing whether they know it or not. One of them is sitting at this fire."

Dominic went very still. "Me."

"You broke the covenant," I said. "Which the law treats, oddly enough, as an act of signature. Destruction is a kind of authorship. The gods never prosecuted you properly, did you notice that? Exile, yes — exile is what you do to a signatory you can't afford to kill."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the earlier one. Not the silence of judgment. The silence of a lock, deep underground, testing whether the key fits.

Tamsin rolled the scroll closed with hands that trembled only slightly, and I chose to believe the trembling was purpose — the shaking of something being carried, not something breaking.

"Then we need the rest of the roll," she said. "Names. Places. Before Veyra finds us, before Ossuar does, before whatever's left of the seal decides for us." She stood, tucking the case inside her coat, against her heart, which I suspected was exactly where her father had carried his list of names. "How far to the nearest successor?"

I had been dreading this question for two days, and now that it arrived, it arrived wearing worse clothes than I'd imagined.

"One day's walk," I said. "Through the salt flats. Open ground. No cover, no shelter, and—" I met Dominic's eyes, because he deserved to hear it from me and not from the sky. "Veyra's hunters crossed the ridge behind us this morning. I felt the pressure change. She knows where we're going. She may know why."

Tamsin looked at the two of us, at the dying fire, at the dark mouth of the road ahead, and she smiled — a thin, fierce, entirely unconsoling smile that I suspect she inherited from a fiddle-playing priest who sang anyway.

"Then we'd better pray," she said, "to whatever's still listening. Carefully. In writing. With terms."

And God help me — God help all of us, whichever of them is still taking calls — I laughed.
