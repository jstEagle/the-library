## 15. Storm Over the Salt Flats

*Dominic*

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a god's hunters find you, and I have heard it exactly twice in my life. The first time was the night I broke the covenant. The second was now, on a salt flat so white and so flat it looked like the world had been scoured down to its ledger page, waiting for someone to write on it.

Tamsin felt it too. She stopped mid-stride, one boot sinking a half-inch into crusted brine, and said, "Dominic."

"I know."

"The birds stopped."

Birds had been following us since dawn — gulls, absurdly, three hundred miles from any sea, riding thermals off the flats like they knew something we didn't. Now there were none. Just the sky, enormous and pale, and our two shadows stretched out long and thin as accusations.

"They've been herding us," I said. "Since the Under Roads. Maerin's route wasn't a route. It was a funnel."

"Then why haven't they—"

The horizon answered for me. It moved.

Not the way weather moves — not a wall of cloud rolling in, gradual, honest about its intentions. This was a line of darkness that simply *arrived*, the way a verdict arrives, all at once and from every direction at once. I counted shapes within it. Seven. Maybe nine. They flew wrong, wings beating out of rhythm with their bodies, and where they passed over the salt, the crust went grey behind them, dead as ash.

Veyra's hounds. I'd seen them once before, from a very great distance, the night my exile was pronounced. I had hoped never to see them again. Hope, I have found, is mostly a way of scheduling disappointment.

"Tamsin." I caught her arm. "How many sigils are you carrying right now? Live ones."

Her hand went to her wrist, where the newest mark still faintly glowed through her sleeve — the one she'd paid for in the Under Roads, though she hadn't told me what memory it cost. She'd been quiet for two days after that. Quiet in the specific way of someone checking an empty room inside themselves and finding the furniture rearranged.

"Three that answer when I ask," she said. "One that answers whether I ask or not." Her jaw tightened. "Why?"

"Because we're going to need all of them, and I'm going to need to do something stupid."

"You're always doing something stupid."

"Yes, but this time I'm going to do it *professionally*."

The hounds came on. Behind them, riding the dark line like a figurehead, came something worse: a woman-shaped absence, tall and silver-crowned, whose face I couldn't hold in my mind no matter how directly I looked at it. Veyra didn't come herself. She sent her *attention*, which is worse. A goddess's body can be dodged. Her attention is a hand closing around the whole horizon.

*Tamsin Vale,* the attention said, and the salt sang under our feet with the vibration of it. *You were offered everything. You chose fragments. Come home to what you are, and no one needs to be hurt.*

"She's lying," I said.

"I know she's lying." Tamsin's voice was steady, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "That's the awful part. She means it. She genuinely doesn't think of this as hurting anyone."

"That's what makes gods gods," I said. "They've never once had to live inside the consequences."

I knelt and pressed my palm flat against the salt.

Here is what you should understand about what I did next. When I broke the covenant, I did it with the full storm — the deep one, the one that lives under the skin of the world, the one storm-callers spend thirty years learning to *ask* rather than command. My oath of exile wasn't just banishment. It was a severing. I swore myself out of the storm's confidence, and the storm, being older than oaths and considerably better at holding grudges, agreed. Since that night, lightning has passed me by the way water passes oil. In the Hollow Valley it was dead entirely. Outside it, it flickered near Tamsin's sigils like a dog remembering a whistle, but it would not come to my hand.

So I stopped asking it to come to my hand.

I opened my hand instead. All of it. Every channel, every gate, every scar-tissue seal I'd built over ten years of exile to keep the wanting quiet. I gave the storm my name, my history, my standing invitation, and I said the words I'd sworn never to say again: *Come through me.*

The storm considered this.

Storms are not kind. People imagine them as rage, but rage implies a self, and the storm has no self — only appetite and geometry. What came into me came in the way a river comes into a cracked dam: without malice, without mercy, and without any intention whatsoever of stopping.

I remember thinking, quite calmly, *ah. So this is how much it costs.* And then thought became difficult.

Lightning took my spine for a road. I heard myself make a sound that wasn't a word. Somewhere very far away, Tamsin was shouting my name, and somewhere even farther away than that, seven or nine things made of hunger were falling out of the sky toward us, and I put my hands up — or I think I put my hands up; by then my hands were mostly weather — and I *pulled*.

The sky over the salt flats tore open.

I want to be clear that I did not aim it well. There was no artistry left in me. It was a drowning man's swing, wide and wild and far too much, and the bolt that came down split into nine and then into nineteen and hit the salt in a forest of white fire, and the thunderclap arrived not as sound but as a physical fact, a fist of pressure that flattened the brine-crust outward in a ring a mile wide. Two of the hounds simply ceased. Three more fell burning. The rest wheeled back into the dark line, shrieking in frequencies that made my teeth ache — except by then my teeth were also mostly weather.

And Veyra's attention — the great silver hand of it — paused. Considered. And withdrew, unhurried, the way a chess player withdraws a finger from a piece she has decided isn't worth the exchange. Not defeated. Recalculating.

I got all of that in fragments, because by then I was falling, and the last thing I remember clearly is the ground coming up at me with terrible patience, and Tamsin's voice cracking across the flats like a second thunderclap—

* * *

*Tamsin*

He fell like a felled tree, which is to say: all at once, and with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life.

I don't remember deciding to use the sigil. That's the thing nobody tells you about power that lives in your skin — by the time your mind catches up, your body has already signed. I was running, I was screaming his name, and between one stride and the next my hand was pressed flat over his heart and the sigil on my palm — the oldest one, the key-shaped birthmark that started all of this — was burning like a coal.

*I'll pay whatever it costs,* I thought. Which was, I realized much later, exactly the kind of prayer the old gods love best. A blank contract. A signature with the amount left empty.

Something answered.

It wasn't Veyra. I want to believe it wasn't Veyra. It was smaller and stranger than that — a warmth moving up my arm like sunrise climbing a wall, and the warmth said, in no language at all, *yes, and here is the price,* and showed me.

A year.

Not a dramatic year. That's what I hadn't understood until then, what I'd been refusing to understand since the Under Roads. The sigils don't take the big memories, the ones you'd guard. They take whatever's nearest the door. Whatever's ordinary enough to slip out quietly.

I saw it go. I actually saw it — a year of my life laid out like laundry on a line, and I had one instant to read it before it was gone. I was seventeen in it. I could tell because of the coat, the green wool coat I remembered loving, and I watched a whole year happen in a single heartbeat: rain on the shop windows, Mrs. Aldery's cat having kittens in the stockroom, a boy whose face I couldn't fix on even while I watched him, some small sweet recurring thing involving apples, the smell of candle wax in summer, my grandmother laughing at something I'd said —

— and then it folded up like paper and was smoke, and the smoke was gone, and I was kneeling on salt with my hand on Dominic's chest and a new sigil cooling on the back of my neck like a brand, and I could not remember what I had lost. Only that I had lost it. Only the shape of the hole.

That's the cruelty of it. Grief needs a face. Mine had been confiscated.

"Dominic." I shook him. His eyes were open but the storm was still in them, little white threads crawling across the blue, and his lips were burnt grey, and when he breathed it whistled like wind under a door. "Dominic, stay with me. Stay *here*. The storm's done, you gave it everything, you can stop now —"

His mouth moved. No sound. Then, faintly, cracked down the middle: "Did it work?"

I laughed, which surprised both of us, I think. A wet, awful laugh, the kind that's mostly relief wearing a disguise. "You dropped the entire sky on them. Yes. It worked. You absolute idiot, it worked."

"Mm." His eyes drifted shut. "Good. Tell my sister I was — "

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. I knew about his sister by then; I knew what Veyra held over him, and I knew what Veyra had told him in Corvane, and I knew that the sentence he couldn't finish ended with a woman who had been dead for six years. Some debts you keep paying even after the creditor admits there's nothing owed. That's not loyalty anymore. That's just love with nowhere to go.

I got his arm over my shoulders and stood up under his weight, which was considerable, because apparently storm-calling burns through body mass the way a furnace burns through coal. He was lighter than he should have been. He'd been lighter for weeks, and I'd told myself it was the road rations.

Behind us, the salt flats steamed. Glassy craters everywhere, the brine fused into strange white mirrors, and in each mirror I could see the sky — and in the sky, the dark line retreating, and at its center the silver crown of Veyra's attention, watching us go.

She wasn't finished. I understood that with total clarity, the way you understand a door is locked. But she'd seen what Dominic had spent. She'd seen what *I'd* spent. And something in the arithmetic of it had given her pause — not mercy, gods don't have mercy, but caution. We were expensive to collect.

"Walk," I told him, or told myself, or told the flats. "Just walk."

We walked. Or I walked, and he was dragged along in a state halfway between unconsciousness and prayer. The sun crawled westward. My neck throbbed where the new sigil sat, and every few minutes I caught myself reaching for the missing year the way your tongue goes to a broken tooth — testing the gap, hoping the ache will prove it's still there.

Seventeen. I'd been seventeen once. I'd worn a green coat. Someone had laughed with me about apples.

That was all I had. A coat, a number, a fruit.

I started talking, out loud, to fill the hole. Told Dominic — unconscious, unhearing — about the shop, about the candles, about anything I still owned. And somewhere past the third hour, hoarse and half-delirious, I made myself a promise:

I would start writing it down. All of it. Every day, every face, every name. If the sigils were going to take pieces of me, then I would leave copies. I would become a book too large to burn.

Ahead of us, the salt gave way at last to scrubland, and beyond the scrubland, dark against the sunset, rose the jagged silhouette of the Worldspine — and on its shoulder, faint as breath on glass, a light that was not a star and not a fire and not natural at all.

Maerin had described it to us in the Under Roads, in her dry, guilty, careful voice: *the Worldspine altar. Where the covenant was first written. Where it can be unwritten.*

Dominic stirred against my shoulder. His eyes opened a crack, found the light on the mountain, and something that might have been a smile moved across his ruined mouth.

"There it is," he whispered. "End of the road, Seal."

"No," I said, watching the impossible light, feeling the year-shaped hole in my chest ache like weather coming. "End of the road is wherever we decide to stop. That's just the next chapter."

I adjusted his weight, turned toward the mountain, and walked us both into the dark.
