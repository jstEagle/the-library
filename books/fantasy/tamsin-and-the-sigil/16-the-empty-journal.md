## 16. The Empty Journal

The rain had followed us into the ruins of a shepherd's bothy two miles past the salt flats, and it was still there in the morning, drumming on the slate roof like an impatient creditor. Dominic slept sitting up against the doorframe, one hand curled around a knife he hadn't let go of since the flats. Maerin's covenant text lay between us, half-rotted, smelling of cellar and old incense.

And my journal sat in my lap, open to a page I had no memory of writing.

I should explain about the journal. Three months ago — or what I *believed* was three months ago; time had gone soft as overcooked parsnip since I started spending years of memory like a drunk spends wages — I had started writing myself down. That was Maerin's advice, actually, back when she was still alive and still mostly honest with us. *You're burning yourself as fuel, girl,* she'd said. *So keep a ledger. Write down everything you can't afford to lose.*

So I did. Every night, or every morning, depending on which version of me was holding the pen: what happened that day, who we'd met, what I'd paid. What I remembered. What I'd caught myself forgetting. The name of my grandmother's shop. The smell of candle wax and tallow. The way the Hollow Valley went blue at dusk, like the whole world holding its breath.

It was, I realize now, exactly like scripture. Which should have worried me sooner.

* * *

Here is how you find out you've been lying to yourself: you read your own handwriting and it tells you something your heart doesn't recognize.

I'd been flipping backward through the journal, doing inventory. It's a grim little ritual. You start at the latest entry and work back until the entries stop sounding like you, and then you know where the erosion line sits this week. Most weeks the line moved forward by inches. A face here. A birthday there. My mother's voice — I'd written *my mother's voice sounded like* and then nothing, just a blank space and a small furious asterisk, because even the me of six weeks ago couldn't remember.

But this morning I found entries that didn't sound like anyone.

They were in my handwriting. That was the horrible part. Not the shaky scrawl of someone writing under duress — no tremor, no smudged ink from a shaking hand. These were calm. Measured. Even neat, neater than I usually managed. And they were dated on days I could account for, days I remembered, days when I had apparently been doing one thing while some other Tamsin was writing these.

The first one said:

*Day 41. Paid a year for the shield on the flats. Worth it. He would have died otherwise, and I find I am not willing to trade him for anything, including myself. Note this down, future self: there are things worth more than memory.*

That one I almost liked. That one felt like me, if me had gotten organized.

The second one said:

*Day 44. Tested something today. If I write an intention clearly enough, the sigils treat it as a standing order. They don't care whether I remember making it. The seal executes its clauses. Remember that word. Clauses.*

I read that three times. Then I looked at my wrist, where the oldest sigil sat under its fading scar, and felt the particular chill of a lock turning somewhere inside you that you didn't know you had.

Because she was right. Of course she was right. Veyra had told us as much in Corvane, all silk and patience: *your sigils are clauses, little Seal.* Every answered prayer I'd ever made was a contract with terms I'd never read. And now it seemed I'd been drafting my own contracts in my sleep — or in whatever state I dropped into when the power took too much and left the pen behind.

The third entry was dated Day 47. Yesterday.

*Plan,* it began, without preamble, the way I begin things when I've stopped being polite to myself.

*Veyra will make a final offer. She always does; it's how she hunts. She'll offer everything back — every year, every face, every name. Whole. Perfect. And she'll want the old seal in exchange, divine stewardship, forever.*

*I have decided to accept.*

*Reasoning: I am losing anyway. Every fight costs me another piece. By the time we reach the Worldspine I will be a stranger wearing my own boots, and strangers make bad decisions. Better to be whole and owned than hollow and free. At least a whole Tamsin will remember why any of this mattered.*

*Instructions to future self (that is, to me, tomorrow): do not argue. You will want to argue. You will say identity requires the right to change, and other pretty things. But you have not lived with the forgetting. I have. I am the one standing in the wreckage every morning, reading this journal like a letter from a dead woman.*

*Accept the offer. This is not a request. It is a clause.*

I sat very still for a long time. The rain kept up its creditor's knocking. Somewhere outside, a curlew cried, thin and rising, like the valley birds I'd grown up with, and for a second I nearly cried too, because I couldn't remember if I'd ever actually heard one in the Hollow Valley or only read about hearing one.

Then I got angry, which is my usual cure for terror, and marginally more useful.

* * *

"You knew," I said, when Dominic woke. I held the journal open across my knees like evidence at a trial. "You knew she writes to me."

Dominic blinked the sleep off his face and focused on the page. He read fast — exiled storm-callers apparently get drilled in contract law along with lightning etiquette — and I watched his expression go through recognition, then alarm, then that careful blankness he deploys when he's deciding how much truth a situation can hold without cracking.

"I suspected," he said.

"Suspected."

"The nights you don't remember. After big expenditures." He rubbed his jaw. "You talk, sometimes. Stand up and walk around the fire. Once you recited the terms of a bargain in Old Covenant verse and I didn't understand a word of it, but it *rhymed*, Tamsin. Power doesn't usually rhyme."

"Wonderful. I'm haunted and I sing."

"Tamsin." He came and crouched in front of me, and his voice did the thing it does when he stops being wry, which is rare enough that it lands like weather. "What does it say?"

I handed it over rather than say it aloud. There's a superstition in that, I know — the words being safer on paper than in air — but I've learned to trust my superstitions lately. They're the only inheritance I have left.

He read it twice. His jaw tightened on the second pass, and when he looked up, his eyes had that storm-light in them, the gray-green flicker that used to mean thunderheads and now mostly meant he was frightened and covering it with fury.

"She's right about one thing," he said quietly. "Veyra will make the offer. She's been circling it since Corvane. Restore everything, whole and perfect, in exchange for the seal and her stewardship of it. Forever." He said *forever* the way you'd spit out a bone.

"And my other self says take it."

"Your other self," Dominic said, "is exhausted, terrified, and has been paying for our survival in installments of her own soul. I wouldn't trust my judgment either, in her position." He paused. "I *have* been in her position. That's rather the story of my exile."

I looked at him. He almost never talked about the breaking of the covenant — the sister, the shattering, the scattering of the binding into mortal blood, all of it spilled out once on a bad night on the road and then locked away again like something shameful in a cellar. But I saw it move behind his face now.

"You shattered a covenant to save someone you loved," I said slowly, working it out as I said it. "And now I'm being asked to hand myself over to save someone I—" I stopped. He waited, patient as stone, letting me arrive there myself. "Someone I love," I finished, because it was true and because refusing to say it felt like one more small surrender to the forgetting.

"So you understand the temptation better than most gods alive," he said. "Which is exactly why you can't decide this tonight. Or alone. Or—" he tapped the journal, gently, "—on the authority of a woman who signed her own name to a bargain while she wasn't home."

"It's my handwriting, Dominic. It's my reasoning. It's *me*. Where exactly do I draw the line between the parts of me I obey and the parts I overrule?" I heard my own voice climb and made myself stop, breathe, start again lower. "If I throw out Day-Forty-Seven-Tamsin's plan, why keep Day-Forty-One-Tamsin's promise? She wrote that Dominic was worth more than memory. Should I honor that? It's the same pen. The same person. Just a different amount of her left."

Dominic was quiet for a moment. Outside, the rain eased, and the light through the broken shutters went from gray to a pale, washed silver.

"When I broke the covenant," he said, "I was certain. Completely certain. My sister was dying, the gods refused to help because her prayer wasn't properly contracted, and I was certain that any law that let a child die for paperwork deserved to be broken." His mouth twisted. "I was right about the law and wrong about the consequences, and both of those things were true at once, and neither one un-did the other. Certainty isn't the problem, Tamsin. Certainty without witnesses is."

"Witnesses?"

"That's what a covenant *is*. Not a rule. A witness. Something that holds you to your word after the feeling that made you give it has passed." He nodded at the rotted text in its oilcloth. "Maerin understood that. The original covenant wasn't the gods binding mortals. It was everyone binding each other. Mortals included. Especially mortals." He looked at me. "You've been trying to carry the whole thing alone. One girl, one seal, one signature. No wonder your past selves keep trying to seize the pen. Nobody should hold that much of themselves unwatched."

I wanted to argue. She was right, the journal-me; I did want to argue; I had a whole speech loaded about identity requiring the right to change, about how a self you can't revise is just a prison with good handwriting. But the speech suddenly sounded thin, because I could hear whose voice it would be delivered in. Mine. All mine. Unwitnessed.

"What do I do with her, then?" I asked. "Day Forty-Seven. She gave me instructions."

Dominic almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked thing, but real. "You answer her. In writing. Same book, same pen. You tell her what you've decided and why, so that whichever of you reads it next knows there was an argument — that there were *two* of you, and you disagreed, and neither one got to simply win." He shrugged. "That's more covenant than most gods manage."

* * *

So that's what I did. I sat by the cold hearth while Dominic went out to check the pass, and I turned to a fresh page, and I wrote to myself.

*Dear Day Forty-Seven,* I began, and immediately hated it, and crossed it out, and started again.

*To whoever's holding the pen next:*

*You wrote that you'd rather be whole and owned than hollow and free. I've thought about that all morning, and here is what I think you got wrong: those aren't the choices. They never were. Veyra's offer isn't wholeness — it's a portrait of wholeness, painted once and varnished forever. The woman she'd hand back to me wouldn't be able to change. Wouldn't need to. Wouldn't get to. She'd be complete the way a tomb is complete.*

*And you're right that I don't live with the forgetting the way you do. But you don't live with the future the way I do. That's the whole trouble with us. We keep trading places and calling it one life.*

*So here are my terms — and yes, I'm using your word, clauses, because you taught me the power takes them seriously:*

*I will not accept Veyra's offer. Not because I'm braver than you. Because you were alone when you decided, and I'm not anymore, and that turns out to matter more than courage.*

*I will, however, keep your promise about Dominic. That one stands. Witnessed.*

*And I make you this counter-offer: when this is done — if any of us survives to be done — we go back to the valley, and we open the shop, and we write down everything that's left, honestly, including the holes. Some of you will come home. Some won't. I think that has to be all right. I think a self that admits its gaps is worth more than a perfect one that can't say sorry.*

*Signed,*

*Tamsin Vale. All of her. Arguing.*

I read it over. It wasn't elegant. It wasn't scripture. It was better than scripture, maybe, because scripture doesn't expect to be answered.

Then I did the last thing, the thing that scared me most, which was to turn back to Day Forty-Seven's entry and write beneath it, in the margin, small: *Overruled — see reply. With respect. With love. With witnesses.*

The sigil on my wrist warmed faintly as I wrote it. I chose to believe that was acknowledgment and not objection. With the seal, you learn to take what interpretation you can afford.

* * *

Dominic came back wet to the knees and moving fast, which was answer enough to the question of how long we had.

"Riders on the far ridge," he said, wringing out his sleeve. "Not Veyra's colors. Worse — pilgrim grays. Ossuar's people travel quiet." He glanced at the journal in my hands, and something in my face must have told him how the argument had gone, because he nodded once, sharply, like a man confirming a heading. "Good. Whatever you decided — good. Now decide faster, because we need to move, and there's something Maerin's text says about the Worldspine altar I finally understand, and Tamsin—" he paused, and the storm-light came back into his eyes, but steady this time, aimed, "—it says the seal was never supposed to belong to one person. It says there's another way to write it. We're going to need every scrap of you that's still home to help me draft it."

I closed the journal. Held it against my chest, this ragged, contradictory, indispensable book full of women arguing across the years.

"Well," I said, standing, "they'd better all buckle in."
