## 17. Terms of Reforging

The Under Roads had a way of making you honest. There was nothing down there to perform for — no gods, no wardens, no audience but the drip of water counting out the hours like a miser with a ledger. So when Maerin spread the rotted covenant text across a slab of stone and said, "Well, Eriksen? You broke it. Think you can write?" I didn't bother with false modesty.

"No," I said. "But I can copy."

"That's how most law gets made," she said, and lit her third candle of the hour.

We'd been in the Roads for six days by then. Six days of walking through tunnels that predated the gods' current arrangements — older than Veyra's temples, older than Ossuar's hunger, carved by hands that had believed, apparently, that mortals and the divine ought to share a table rather than a food chain. The walls were covered in script. Not decoration. *Terms.* Clause after clause after clause, some of it so worn you could only read the shapes of arguments, like hearing two people shouting through a wall long after they'd stopped shouting.

Maerin could read it. That was the thing about defrocked priests — the church took your vestments, your title, your right to enter a temple, but nobody had figured out how to take what was in your head. She walked those tunnels trailing her fingers along the walls like a woman greeting old friends, and every few hundred paces she'd stop and say something like "Ah, here's where they argued about thresholds," or "This is the clause about grief. They fought over it for a generation."

"Fought over grief?"

"Whether prayers made in grief counted as freely given." She'd said it lightly, the way people say things that aren't light at all. "The gods wanted them. Grief makes the best prayer — desperate, unreserved, no bargaining left in it. The mortal authors said a prayer squeezed out of suffering isn't consent, it's extraction. They compromised." Her finger traced a line of script half-eaten by mineral rot. "Here. Somewhere in this line is the compromise. I used to know it by heart."

"And now?"

"And now I'm an old woman whose memory has been eating itself for forty years, same as everyone's does, just slower than yours has been doing it." She glanced at me. "No offense meant toward Tamsin."

"None taken on her behalf," I said, which was a lie, because everything anyone said about Tamsin's erosion landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

* * *

Here is what I knew, laid out on the stone slab beside the rotting original: the covenant between realms had been broken once — by me — and its binding power had scattered into a bloodline, into Tamsin, who was now the living seal holding the realms apart at the cost of her own memory, sigil by sigil, year by year. Two factions of gods wanted her. Veyra wanted the seal reforged under divine stewardship, which was a polite way of saying owned. Ossuar wanted it destroyed entirely, which was a polite way of saying he wanted mortals back on the menu.

And everyone — everyone — had spent centuries treating the covenant as a *thing*. An object. A chain with a lock, and the question was only ever who held the key.

I sat there in the dark with a stub of candle and Maerin's fragmentary text, and I finally asked the question I should have asked nine years ago, before I shattered anything.

"What if it isn't a thing?"

Maerin looked up from her copying. "Say more."

"The covenant. Everyone talks about it like a wall or a treaty document or a — a vessel. Something with edges. But look at what it actually *does*." I turned the fragment toward me. The old script was dense, legalistic, but underneath the formal language I could see the architecture now, the way you see the bones of a building once you've helped knock one down. "It governs answered prayer. That's all it is. It's the rules of exchange between one side that asks and one side that gives. And the reason it needed a seal at all — the reason it needed *Tamsin* — is because both sides kept trying to cheat."

"Naturally," Maerin said. "Cheating is the oldest sacrament."

"So the seal exists to enforce terms nobody agreed to anymore. The gods inherited their side. Mortals forgot theirs entirely. And the enforcement mechanism got concentrated into one person because concentrated power is easier to steal." I tapped the page. "That's the flaw. Not the terms. The *concentration*."

"You're proposing diffusion," Maerin said slowly.

"I'm proposing that we stop building locks." I stood up, because my legs wanted to pace and the tunnel was barely wide enough, which suited me. Pacing in a confined space keeps a man humble. "One seal means one theft takes everything. One priest means one corruption ruins the whole rite. One god holding the covenant means one bad century for everyone beneath him. What if the binding wasn't *held* anywhere? What if it lived in — " I gestured vaguely, uselessly, at the air. "In everyone. In the act itself."

"In the act of what?"

"Prayer. Freely given prayer." I heard myself say it and stopped pacing, because the sentence had arrived whole, the way storms sometimes arrive all at once off the sea, no warning, just weather. "Maerin. The compromise clause about grief. Read me whatever's left of it."

She read. Most of it was gone — a word here, a phrase there, the connective tissue dissolved. But the skeleton was there: *...that no petition be compelled... that the answering be proportioned to the asking... that each soul keep...*

"*Each soul keep,*" I repeated. "Keep what? Keep what, Maerin?"

"I don't know." For the first time since I'd met her, the old priest sounded frightened. Not of me. Of the gap in her own mind, the place where the answer used to live. "I drafted around that clause. I argued about it. I remember arguing about it and I cannot remember the argument. It's like reaching for a stair that isn't there."

I crouched back down by the candle. And I'll be honest, because the Under Roads make you honest: for a moment I wanted to quit. I wanted to sit in the dark and grieve for the version of events where I'd never broken the covenant, never exiled myself, never dragged a candle-maker's granddaughter into a war between things that wore eternity like clothing. Nine years of exile teaches you a lot of postures and none of them are comfortable, but despair is at least familiar. Familiar is seductive.

Then I thought about Tamsin upstairs — well, not upstairs, there was no up, but *away*, in the chamber Maerin had warded, sleeping with her journal open on her chest the way she'd started doing, as if proximity to her own handwriting could hold the borders of herself. I thought about the entry she'd shown me, the one in her own hand that terrified her. *A plan to give herself wholly to Veyra.* Written by a Tamsin who no longer existed, addressed to a Tamsin who hadn't decided yet whether past selves could be trusted.

Her whole life was someone else's transaction. I'd learned that in the worst possible way, standing in the wreckage of my own confession while she looked at me with fury so clean it deserved better than the man it was aimed at. She was collateral. She was a clause. She was the fine print of a deal struck before she could walk.

And the fix, if there was a fix, couldn't be another person standing in the same spot. It had to be no person at all.

"Each soul keeps," I said again, working it aloud, "a piece. That's what the clause must have been. Each soul keeps a piece of the covenant — a memory of it, a witness of it. Not one keeper. Thousands. Millions. Every person who ever prayed sincerely holds a fragment of the terms, without knowing it, without being able to surrender it, because you can't steal what's distributed across an entire species' remembering."

Maerin set down her stylus very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands have started shaking. "You're describing a covenant written in mortal memory."

"I'm describing a covenant that can't be owned. The gods can't seize it because there's nothing central to seize. Ossuar can't destroy it because you can't burn a memory out of every praying mind at once. Veyra can't steward it because there's nothing to steward — no seal, no vessel, no Tamsin." My voice did something strange on her name. I let it. "The enforcement isn't a person. It's *consent*. Prayer works only when it's freely given, and the covenant holds only as long as enough people remember that giving freely is the whole point."

"That's beautiful," Maerin said. "And completely unworkable. How do you bind millions of minds who don't know they're bound? How do you even draft that? Law requires parties. Parties require knowledge of terms. Your keepers haven't consented to anything — they don't know the covenant exists."

"They've prayed, though." I picked up the fragment again. "Look at the structure of the old text. Every clause answers a failure mode. This one against compelled prayer, this one against disproportionate answers, this one — whatever this eaten line was — against concentration. The original authors weren't writing a contract between two governments. They were writing rules for a relationship. And relationships don't need informed signatories on every term. They need good faith and memory." I looked at her. "You told me the covenant was co-authored. Gods and mortals, together, in these tunnels. Where did the mortal half go?"

"Erased. When the divine factions consolidated, the mortal authorship was scrubbed. It was easier to rule a contract than a partnership." Her mouth twisted. "I helped with the scrubbing. Some of it. I told you — good intentions are their own kind of violence."

"Then we un-scrub it. We don't restore the old covenant. We write the new one *as if* the mortal half had never been removed. Terms that assume the keepers are everyone." I pulled a fresh sheet toward me — actual paper, hoarded by Maerin like a dragon, worth more than the gold we'd left behind in Corvane. "Draft with me. Right now. Ugly first. We'll make it pretty later if the world survives."

* * *

We worked until the candles died and relit and died again. I won't reproduce the whole text here; half of it was argument, and the other half was crossed out. But the spine of it came clear by the second night, and I want to set it down while I still trust my memory of it, because I am not exempt from erosion. Nobody is. That turned out to be the point.

*First:* No prayer shall be answered unless freely given. Not coerced by desperation engineered, not extracted by manufactured suffering, not bargained from the ignorant. The gods may hear everything. They may answer only what is offered.

*Second:* Answers shall be proportioned to the asking. No god shall give so much that the giver becomes indebted beyond bearing. We had seen what Veyra's generosity cost — every gift a hook, every miracle a lien. Generosity as predation was still predation.

*Third:* The covenant shall have no single keeper. Its terms shall live distributed in the sincere memory of all who pray, such that no seizure, destruction, or stewardship can ever concentrate it again. Any being — divine or mortal — who attempts to gather the covenant into one vessel, one seal, one person, voids their standing under these terms entire.

That third clause was mine, and I wrote it looking at the space where Tamsin would have been sitting if I'd let her sit in on the drafting, which I hadn't, because some part of me still thought protecting her meant deciding without her, and I want it recorded that I recognized the hypocrisy even as I committed it. Old habits. Exile is mostly old habits with the scenery changed.

*Fourth:* Any soul who learns the terms becomes a keeper by the knowing, and may teach them onward, and no teaching shall be forbidden. Consent requires education. You can't agree to what you've never been allowed to understand.

"There's a problem," Maerin said, near dawn of the second day, her voice sanded down to a whisper. "The transfer. The old covenant bound through the seal — through Tamsin — because the binding power had nowhere else to live. If you dissolve the seal, the power doesn't automatically redistribute. It *dissipates*. Or worse, it looks for the nearest vessel, and the nearest vessels are—" she didn't finish, didn't need to. The gods. The factions. Whoever caught the falling rope.

"So the redistribution has to happen during the unbinding. Deliberately. Actively." I stared at the fourth clause, the fifth, the ones we'd sketched about witnesses and rites. "Someone has to hold the covenant while it scatters. Someone mortal, someone willing, someone who knows exactly what it will cost—"

"No," Maerin said.

"—and I'm not saying Tamsin—"

"You are absolutely saying Tamsin. You're saying it with your whole face." She pushed back from the slab. "Dominic Eriksen. You have spent this entire drafting session building a machine whose only moving part is the girl you claim you're trying to free."

"That's not—" I stopped. Started again, because the Roads make you honest. "Yes. It is. Someone has to be the hand that lets go. And she's the only one the power will listen to, because it already lives in her. I can't scatter it. Maerin, you can't. It has to pass through her, and passing through costs—"

"It costs what it has always cost. Memory. Self. Years." Maerin's eyes were wet and furious. "So your grand design, your beautiful consent-shaped covenant, begins with the largest involuntary sacrifice in its history. Have you told her?"

"She knows the unbinding has a price."

"Does she know the price is *everything she has left?*" The old woman's voice cracked against the tunnel walls. "Because I have watched her count what remains. I have watched her read that journal like scripture, like a woman rationing bread in a siege. Whatever she consents to, she consents to from inside a shrinking room. Is that free? By your own first clause — is a prayer wrung from a narrowing life *freely given?*"

I didn't have an answer. I sat with the silence where the answer should have been, and it was the loudest silence of my life, and I have stood inside thunder.

* * *

Tamsin found us there when the second day's candles burned out — found us surrounded by drafts and wax and the wreckage of an argument, Maerin asleep sitting up with the original text still open on her knees like a hymnal. Tamsin picked up the cleanest copy of the new terms and read it in full, lips moving, and I watched her face do several things in sequence and settle on something I couldn't name.

"This is good," she said finally.

"It's unfinished. And it has a problem I haven't solved."

"The scattering." She set the pages down. "I heard the last part. Sound carries, down here. Everything carries." She looked at me, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse. "You were going to figure out how to tell me."

"I was going to fail to tell you, probably. Then feel terrible about it."

"Honest, at least." She sat down across from me, cross-legged, journal in her lap, and I realized she'd brought it deliberately — brought her record of herself to the table where her future was being drafted. "Read me the first clause again. Out loud."

"*No prayer shall be answered unless freely given.*"

"And you think what I'd be doing — carrying the covenant while it scatters, paying for it in memory — you think that could qualify? As freely given?"

"I think," I said carefully, "that it's the only part of this whole rotten architecture that might. Because you'd know the price going in. Because you'd choose it with your eyes open, which is more than anyone ever gave you." I hesitated. "But Maerin's right that choosing from inside a siege isn't simple. So I'm not asking you to decide tonight. I'm asking you to help me solve the last problem instead. How to scatter the power so it costs less than everything."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. Then she opened her journal to a marked page and turned it toward me, and my stomach dropped, because it was a list — her handwriting, dated three days ago, before we'd reached the Roads. A plan. Not the terrifying one, not the Veyra entry. A different plan, one I'd never seen.

*If they build a new covenant,* it read, *it will need a first keeper to start the distribution. Ask them to let it be me — but ask on MY terms. I decide what I pay. I decide what I keep. Write it into the text. Make my consent a clause, not an assumption.*

"You were listening," I said stupidly. "Down here. All along."

"I'm always listening. It's the only power I have left that doesn't cost anything." She took the pen from my hand, dipped it, and began writing directly into the margin of the draft — amendments, in her own script, in her own words. "Clause six. The first keeper's sacrifice shall be bounded by her own declaration. She names her price. She names what is withheld. And the covenant itself shall protect the withholding, because a covenant built on consent that overrides consent is just Veyra with better handwriting."

I watched her write and felt something I hadn't felt in nine years of exile, which was hope with weight to it — not the feathered kind, the kind with mass, the kind that could bruise you.

Then the candle guttered, and in the dark beyond our little circle of light, deep in the tunnels where sound carried everything, something answered.

Not a voice. A pressure. The particular atmospheric hush I knew better than my own heartbeat — the silence before a storm that is not a storm, the feeling of something vast turning its attention toward a very small flame. Maerin woke with a gasp. Tamsin's wrist, where the oldest scar lay, glowed faintly through her sleeve.

"She's found the Roads," Tamsin said quietly. "Veyra. She's been listening too."

And far above us, through miles of stone, the air began to taste like lightning.
