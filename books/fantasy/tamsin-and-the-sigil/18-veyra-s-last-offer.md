## 18. Veyra's Last Offer

She came at dusk, the way gods do — when the light is too tired to argue and you're too hungry to be wise.

I was sitting on a ledge above the Under Roads' last mouth, eating hardtack that tasted like punishment, watching the sun go down over the salt flats we'd crossed two days ago with most of my blood still inside me. Dominic was below, arguing with Maerin's fragmentary covenant text as if volume could restore the missing pages. The campfire smoke smelled like pine and desperation.

And then the air went soft, the way it does before rain, except there was no rain coming. There was only her.

"Tamsin Vale." Veyra stood at the edge of the ledge where no one had climbed up, wearing a body made of evening light and good intentions. Her hair moved in a wind that touched nothing else. "You look terrible."

"You look like a sunset someone left out overnight," I said. "We've all been through a lot."

She laughed. That was the worst part of Veyra — she genuinely enjoyed me. Most of the gods who'd wanted things from me had looked at me the way a locksmith looks at a door. Veyra looked at me like a reader looks at a book she intends to finish tonight.

"May I sit?"

"It's a public cliff," I said. "As far as I know."

She sat, folding herself down with the grace of something that had never once had to worry about its knees. For a while she said nothing, which I understood was a technique. I ate my hardtack and let her have her silence. It cost her nothing. It cost me a little dignity, but I'd been spending that freely all season.

"I'm not here to threaten you," she said finally.

"No. You're here to offer me things. Threats are for people who can't afford the price of what they want."

Her smile flickered — genuine surprise, quickly banked. "Maerin's been teaching you theology."

"Maerin's been teaching me contract law. Theology is just contract law with worse lighting."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Veyra by then, and I want to be fair about it, because fairness is the trap she sets:

She had answered my first prayer honestly. A farmhouse out of mist, warm bread on the table, a hearth lit without flint. She hadn't poisoned it, hadn't hidden a hook in the walls. She'd told me what I was — *the Seal* — when everyone else in my life had spent nineteen years telling me I was nothing special, a candle-maker's granddaughter with ink-stained fingers and an unremarkable face.

That's not nothing. When you find out your whole childhood was amputated, that your memories were harvested like a crop to pay a debt you never signed for, the person who tells you the truth feels like a friend even when the truth is a knife. Especially then.

But I'd also seen her at the Conclave of Ash. I'd watched her negotiate with Ossuar, the hollow god, the one who wanted mortals to stay prey forever, and I'd noticed that Veyra didn't disagree with him about whether mortals should serve. Only about how gently.

Veyra wanted stewardship. She wanted the seal reforged under divine rule, with me as the living clause at the center of it — kept, protected, cherished, and never, ever free. She'd said as much in front of forty gods and two disguised pilgrims, one of whom had stepped on my foot so hard during the tense parts that I still had the bruise.

So when she appeared on my cliff at dusk, I knew the shape of what was coming. What I didn't know — what nobody could have prepared me for — was how much it would hurt.

"I've been reviewing the terms of the old covenant," she said. "The original text. Not Maerin's rot-eaten copy — mine. I helped write it, you know. Before your father's father was born."

"I did suspect you weren't young."

"I was younger than I am now. Everyone is." She turned her hand over, and in her palm lay a small light, gold-white, pulsing like a heartbeat. "This is yours. Or rather — this is what was taken from you. Every memory the old seal consumed. Your mother's voice. Your father's hands. The name you were called before Tamsin. The tower burning. The reason you were given up, and the words your parents said when they did it. Nineteen years, whole and perfect, exactly as you lived them."

The hardtack turned to chalk in my mouth.

I want to tell you I felt suspicion first. I want to tell you the wry narrator in my head immediately flagged this as a con, because that's the version of me I prefer — the girl who sees the trap because she's read enough contracts to spot one.

What I actually felt was hunger. Pure, animal, shameless hunger. Somewhere below us, Dominic was arguing with dead paper about clauses and consent, and every word he wrote was for a future I might never have, because I couldn't remember my past well enough to know what kind of person I'd been. I'd lost my grandmother's face to a sigil burned in battle. I'd lost whole years to Ossuar's forced unsealing. My journal was full of entries in my own handwriting that read like letters from a stranger — some brave, some frightened, one of them planning to give everything away to the woman currently sitting next to me holding my stolen life in her palm like a lamp.

"Say what you want for it," I managed.

"Ah." She closed her hand, and the light went out, and something in my chest went out with it. "There it is. You see, this is why I like you, Tamsin. You don't waste time pretending you're not tempted."

"I'm tempted by pie, too. Doesn't mean I'd sign a covenant for it."

"This isn't pie." She turned to face me fully, and I made the mistake of meeting her eyes. They were the color of the moment just after sunset — the blue that isn't quite dark yet, the hour when anything still seems possible. "Here are my terms. No tricks, no hidden clauses. I'll speak plainly, because you'll check anyway, and I'd rather you find me honest than clever."

"Flattery noted."

"The old seal, restored. You become what you were always meant to be: the living bond between the realms. Divine stewardship — mine, specifically, though the conclave will have ceremonial oversight. In exchange: every memory returned. All of them. Whole. And more than that—" she leaned closer, and her voice dropped into something almost tender, "—you will never lose another one. Not to a sigil, not to a bargain, not to time itself. The seal will hold you the way you hold it. Perfect continuity of self, from your first memory to your last breath. Do you understand what I'm offering? No one mortal has ever been offered this. You would *know yourself*, Tamsin. Completely. Forever."

Forever. She said it like a gift. She said it like a gift because to her, it was one.

* * *

I made her wait while I thought, which I suspect amused her enormously, since she could presumably wait out geological eras without getting bored.

Below me, Dominic's voice drifted up, low and frustrated, reading Maerin's fragment aloud: *"...the covenant shall be held not in any one hand, nor any one blood, but distributed among all who witness..."* He'd been working on that line for three days. Distributed among all who witness. Thousands of keepers instead of one seal. A pact written in mortal memory — ordinary, forgetful, dying, human memory, renewed generation after generation by people who would never meet each other.

It was a beautiful idea and a terrifying amount of work and it might not function at all. Against that, Veyra was offering me certainty. My whole self, guaranteed, insured against loss, administered by an immortal who had personally watched civilizations rise and fall and had learned, apparently, to feel tenderness about it.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing I didn't understand until that moment on the cliff:

I believed her.

That's the horror of it. She wasn't lying. Every word of her offer was true. I would get my memories back — all of them, perfect, permanent. I would know my mother's voice. I would know why the tower burned. I would never again open my journal and find my own handwriting feeling like a forgery.

The trap wasn't in the terms. The trap was the terms themselves.

Because a self that cannot change isn't a self. It's a portrait. And portraits belong to whoever hangs them.

"Do you know what I remember about my grandmother?" I said at last. "Almost nothing. Her face is gone — I spent it shielding Dominic on the salt flats, and I'd spend it again. But here's what I do have. I have the fact that I miss her. I have the shape of missing her. And that shape changes. Some days it's sharp and some days it's soft, and last week I realized I'd started missing things about her I couldn't name, which means I was inventing her a little, which means—" I stopped, because I was about to say something true and I wanted to make sure I meant it. "Which means my grief is *mine*. It moves. It grows. Nobody gave it to me and nobody can take it back."

Veyra's expression didn't change, but the light around her did — dimmed by a degree, like a cloud crossing her.

"You're offering me a finished book," I said. "Every page printed, bound, sealed. 'Tamsin Vale, complete works, final edition.' And I'd get to read it — oh, I'd get to read it, every recovered year, every stolen day. But I'd never get to write another word that mattered, because the seal doesn't hold the covenant, Veyra. The seal holds *me*. Fixed. Preserved. A bookmark the size of a person."

"That is a deeply ungrateful metaphor."

"I've been saving it."

"Tamsin." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile. "You think choice is freedom. I have watched ten thousand years of mortals choose. Do you know what they choose? Badly. Fearfully. They choose small and then they die, and their choosing amounts to nothing but weather. I am offering you a self that *endures*. Isn't endurance worth more than—"

"Than becoming?" I finished. "No. And that's the whole answer, so let me give you the long version anyway, because you came all this way."

I stood up. My legs were shaking, which I decided she didn't need to know.

"You want to know the real trap in your offer? It's not the stewardship. It's not even the seal. It's that you'd return my memories *whole and perfect* — meaning fixed, meaning final. I wouldn't get to remember my childhood. I'd get to *be had by it*. Every recovered year locked in place, every grief preserved at its original intensity, forever, under warranty. And I'd never be able to change my mind about any of it, because changing your mind requires forgetting a little. Requires letting yesterday loosen its grip. You're offering me a self with no give in it. And a self with no give—" I spread my scarred hands, the sigil-scars pale in the dusk, "—breaks. I've seen it break. I'm what's left of it breaking."

For a long moment, neither of us spoke. Below, the fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, a nightbird tried out three notes and gave up.

Then Veyra smiled, and it was the saddest expression I have ever seen on something immortal.

"He rewrote it, didn't he. Your storm-caller. *Distributed among all who witness.*" She shook her head slowly. "We wrote the original covenant in one child because one child can be found, and held, and — if necessary — replaced. His version can't be enforced. It can barely be read. It will leak. Gods will cheat. Mortals will forget their obligations, because mortals forget *everything*, that's the entire condition."

"Yes," I said. "That's the feature."

"Forgetting is not a feature."

"It's the only reason anyone ever gets to start over." I crouched down to her level, close enough to see that her eyes weren't really eyes, just places where the evening concentrated. "You're asking me to trade the right to change for the guarantee of being known. And I understand why. It's the same trade you offered every mortal who ever prayed to you. Certainty for consent. But here's what Maerin's rot-eaten book taught me, Veyra, the line that survived the mold better than anything else: *a covenant held by memory must be renewed by the remembering.* Not preserved. Renewed. Which means it has to be allowed to change, which means it has to be allowed to be wrong sometimes, which means—"

"—which means it can't be owned," she said quietly.

"By anyone. Including me." I took a breath. "Especially me. That's my counter-offer, since you came all this way. Give me back what I can carry. Let the rest go the way everything goes. And take your place in the new covenant like everyone else — answering prayers that are freely given, bound by thousands of keepers who'll forget you regularly and have to be reminded. You'll be remembered badly and briefly and by mortals. It's a terrible deal. It's the best one on the table."

She looked at me for a long time. And I swear on my grandmother whose face I cannot recall — she looked *tempted*. Just for a heartbeat. Just enough to break my heart a little, because even gods get lonely, apparently, and eternity is a long time to be remembered perfectly by no one.

Then the light in her palm flared back to life, gold-white, beating.

"The offer stands," she said. "Until the Worldspine altar. Until the rite begins. After that, Tamsin, there is no restoring anything — not for you, not for anyone. If your storm-caller's covenant fails, the realms stay shut and every prayer in the world goes unanswered, and you will have traded your mother's voice for a theory."

"I know."

"And if Ossuar reaches the altar first—"

"I know that too."

"Do you?" She rose, unfolding upward, the dusk gathering itself around her like a cloak. "You know what you're refusing. I'm not sure you know what you're risking. There's a difference, and it's about to matter." She began to fade, edges dissolving into the darkening sky, and her last words came from everywhere and nowhere, gentle as ash falling: "He's already moving, little seal. Ossuar. He's not waiting for your rite. Ask your storm-caller what happens to the Under Roads when a hollow god walks them."

And she was gone, and the cliff was just a cliff, and the stars came out like they always do, indifferent and punctual.

I sat there until my heart remembered how to beat normally. Then I shouted down the slope, my voice cracking on the second word:

"Dominic. Get up here. Now."

He came up fast, covenant pages still in his fist, firelight catching the old scar on his jaw. Behind him, far below in the black throat of the mountain, the Under Roads had gone very, very quiet — and I could have sworn the ground beneath us shivered, once, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"What happened?" he asked. "What did she offer?"

"Everything," I said. "And something's coming up the roads. We need to move for the Worldspine *tonight*."
