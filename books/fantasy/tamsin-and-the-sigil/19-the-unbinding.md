## 19. The Unbinding

*Tamsin*

The Worldspine altar was not built. That was the first thing I understood about it, standing in the wind at the top of the world with my journal clutched to my chest like a shield that had never once worked.

It was *grown* — a shelf of black stone thrust out of the mountain's shoulder like a bone through skin, ringed with pillars that leaned at angles no mason would forgive. The sky above it was the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Below us, past the cloud line, the world went on being ordinary: valleys, rivers, the small lights of towns where people were saying their prayers to gods who had stopped listening politely.

"Charming spot," Maerin said, hauling herself up the last stretch of path. She was seventy if she was a day and she'd climbed faster than both of us, which she would never let us forget. "You can see why they picked it. Nowhere to run, nowhere to hide, and the acoustics are perfect for screaming."

"You're a comfort," Dominic said.

"I'm a defrocked priest with a rot-eaten book and a walking stick. Comfort is above my pay grade."

I knelt at the center of the altar and set down the journal. The original covenant text lay beside it — Maerin's fragment, half its lines eaten away, the rest written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. My sigils were already awake. I could feel them the way you feel a fever coming: heat under the skin, crawling up from my wrists, my collarbone, the key-shaped mark behind my ear that had started all of this.

Twenty-two years of stolen childhood, inked across my body in a language I'd learned to read by losing things.

"Tamsin." Dominic crouched next to me. Up close his eyes were the gray of weather about to happen. "Once you start, there's no version of this where you get to stop halfway."

"I know."

"And if Ossuar's right — if unsealing the realms lets everything old and hungry come back through —"

"He's not right." I said it firmly because I needed one of us to believe it out loud. "He's just wrong in a way that sounds like safety. There's a difference."

Dominic almost smiled. "That's the most god-botherer thing you've ever said."

"Maerin's been teaching me."

"Traitor," Maerin said fondly, from her post at the altar's edge, where she'd laid out salt in a pattern she refused to explain. "Begin when you're ready, girl. The moment you cut the first line, every power in the sky will feel it. We'll have minutes. Maybe fewer."

I looked at the journal one more time. *If you're reading this and you don't remember writing it — good,* said the entry from three nights ago, in my own handwriting. *That means you're still changing. Keep going.*

I pressed my palm flat against the black stone and spoke the first line of the covenant backward.

Unmaking, it turns out, is just making read aloud in reverse. The words came up out of me like swallowed stones rising, and the first sigil — the oldest one, the key behind my ear — flared white and began, slowly, to lift off my skin like steam off water.

The mountain screamed.

Not the wind. The mountain. Every pillar on the altar rang at once, a sound I felt in my teeth and my sternum and the backs of my eyes, and far below us the cloud deck split open along a seam miles long, as if something enormous had drawn a fingernail across the sky's throat.

"They felt it," Maerin said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very steady, the way voices do right before people do brave stupid things. "Minutes, I said. I was an optimist."

*Tamsin.* The voice arrived without traveling. It didn't echo; it *replaced*, filling the space where sound should be, cold and vast and patient as a grave. *Little seal. You are doing a foolish thing.*

Ossuar. The hollow god. I'd heard him once before, through a dead man's mouth in Corvane, and I had hoped never to hear him again, which is the kind of hope the universe treats as a personal invitation.

"Go away," I said, through gritted teeth, still holding the second line of the unbinding in my mouth like a coal. "I'm busy."

*You were made to keep the door shut. Do you imagine your feelings matter more than your function? A lock does not weep. A lock does not choose.*

"A lock," I said, "doesn't write a journal either." And I spoke the second line.

* * *

*Dominic*

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a storm-line alone: it is not like holding anything. It is like being held — by something that wants to pass through you and does not care what it breaks on the way.

I stood at the western edge of the altar with my arms raised and my oath broken for the second time in my life, and the storm came down the Worldspine toward me like a debt collector.

The first time I broke my oath, I was nineteen, and I did it to tear a hole between realms big enough to pull my sister through. This time I was doing it to hold one shut long enough for a candlemaker's daughter to unmake herself. I want it noted, by whatever ledger keeps such things, that my motives had improved even if my odds had not.

The storm hit the line and I *caught* it.

Gods, the weight of it. Lightning doesn't strike; it *asks*, over and over, a thousand times a second, and each asking is a fist against every wall you have. I'd been a storm-caller since I was nine years old. I had never once been a storm-*holder*. Calling is a conversation. Holding is drowning while standing up.

"Hold, Eriksen," I told myself, through teeth that had gone numb. "Just hold."

Below the cloud seam, shapes were climbing. Not flying — climbing, up the sheer face of the Worldspine, things made of the space between stars, Ossuar's hollow ones, their bodies like holes wearing the idea of bodies. Behind them, higher up, gold light moved in formation. Veyra's host, arriving late and luminous, the way she did everything.

And between the two hosts, descending on a column of pale fire, came Veyra herself.

She landed on the altar thirty feet from Tamsin without disturbing a single pebble, which is the most frightening thing a powerful being can do. She looked exactly as she had in the farmhouse folded out of mist: beautiful the way a contract is beautiful, every feature precise, nothing wasted, nothing true.

"Step away from her, storm-caller," Veyra said. Her voice carried perfectly under my thunder, which felt rude. "What she attempts will kill her. What Ossuar intends will kill everyone else. I am offering the only outcome in which anyone wakes tomorrow."

"She's mid-rite," I said. My voice came out shredded. "You'll have to take it up with her."

"Tamsin." Veyra turned, and her voice gentled into the register she used for offers, the one that had nearly worked on me twice. "Child. Every memory, whole. Your mother's voice. Your father's hands. The name you had before Vale. All of it returned, and the seal reforged gently, with stewardship instead of sacrifice. Or you can finish this and die knowing only the fragments you wrote down in a little book."

I saw Tamsin waver. I'll be honest about that. I saw her whole body lean toward the offer like a plant toward light, and I understood it, because I knew what it was to want a version of yourself back that the world had taken.

Then she laughed. It was a terrible, wonderful laugh, wet at the edges.

"You're offering to *complete* me," she said. "Like I'm a set of dishes missing a plate. Veyra — I've read my own handwriting. I know what I look like when someone else holds the pen." She lifted the third line of the unbinding off the stone. "No."

Veyra's beauty did something I had never seen it do.

It *left*.

What remained was older and larger and much less interested in being liked, and she turned — not to Tamsin, but to me, and then past me, to the hollow shapes swarming up the cliff face, and said, in a voice like a verdict:

"Then none of you leave this mountain."

* * *

*Tamsin*

The fourth line burned going out of me, and it took something with it. I felt it go — a specific something, a warm particular something — and I knew before I checked the journal that fell open at my feet that I'd lost the smell of my grandmother's shop. Beeswax and cedar and the match-strike of mornings. Gone. Paid out, installment by installment, to buy the world another hour.

Keep going, said my own handwriting, somewhere under the wind.

The battle, when it arrived, arrived everywhere at once.

Ossuar's hollow ones crested the altar's rim and Maerin met them — met *them*, seventy years old and armed with salt and a walking stick and forty years of liturgy she'd memorized back when the covenant still had authors. She walked the altar's edge laying down words the way other people laid down barricades, and where her words touched the hollow ones, they *stopped*, stuttering like insects against glass.

"THE COVENANT IS MINE TO UNMAKE!" Ossuar's voice came out of the ground itself now, out of the stone under our knees. "IT WAS WRITTEN IN BLOOD AND IT WILL BE CLOSED IN BLOOD —"

"It was written in *ink*," Maerin snapped, "by committee, you melodramatic fossil, and half the signatories were mortals, which you have always found embarrassing—" A hollow one got past her words. Her walking stick took it across what might generously be called a face. "—and *that's* for the clause about children, you absolute horror—"

Gold fire fell from the sky. Veyra's host hit Ossuar's swarm above the altar and the collision made a sound I have no word for, a sound like two arguments about the nature of love happening inside a thunderclap. Shards of divinity rained down around us, burning holes in the stone, and through it all Veyra herself advanced on the altar's center, on me, her face stripped of every gentle thing.

"The rite ends now," she said, "or I end it."

"TAMSIN!" Dominic's voice, from the western edge, ragged as torn cloth. He was on one knee, lightning running down both arms into the stone, holding the storm-line against a pressure I could feel from here — the whole weight of the broken sky trying to pour through the gap my unbinding was opening. He was bleeding from the ears. He was smiling anyway, the madman. "DON'T STOP FOR ANY OF US! THAT'S THE WHOLE POINT OF US!"

I spoke the fifth line.

My left hand went numb. Then my left arm. Something in me was being spent faster than I could count it, and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that I was not going to survive this rite. The seal wasn't a knot you untied. It was a life, woven through mine, and unweaving meant pulling every thread.

Fine. I had a journal. Whatever was left after, someone could read it aloud.

Veyra reached the circle of salt. Maerin stepped into her path.

"Daughter of the first conclave," Veyra said, almost kindly. "Move."

"I helped write the clause that made her," Maerin said. "Every sin of this covenant runs through my hands. So no, I don't think I'll move. I think I'll stand here, in the last office I'm fit for, and be a *door*."

"Maerin, don't—" I started.

She glanced back at me, and her eyes were bright and absolutely calm, and she winked.

"Witnessed and sealed," she said to the sky, to the stone, to whatever kept the oldest records. "Let it be entered that the mortal party objects."

Veyra struck her with a hand made of pale fire, and Maerin caught it — *caught it*, both palms, the way you catch a falling child — and held, and the light poured through her fingers and around her shoulders and she did not fall, she *held*, buying seconds the way she'd promised, spending seventy years of faith in fistfuls, and she was laughing, actually laughing, saying something I couldn't hear over the storm, and I spoke the sixth line with tears cutting tracks through the ash on my face.

The seventh line was the last one. I could see it waiting in the covenant text, half-eaten, the final clause, the one that ended *and the seal shall be no more*.

I reached for it.

And the world went white, and then it went *open*, and I was falling — or rising — or standing in a place that was neither, where every prayer ever prayed sincerely hung around me like lanterns in fog, thousands upon thousands of them, mortal voices, mortal hopes, and I understood at last what the covenant had always been reaching for and what my whole ruined, borrowed, rewritten life had been the wrong answer to.

They didn't need a seal.

They'd never needed a seal. They needed *keepers*. And here they all were, every praying soul, holding their small lights, waiting for someone to hand them the flame.

"Dominic," I whispered, into the storm, into the white. "Hold on. I've got an idea, and it's a terrible one, and it's going to cost me everything."

His voice came back through the thunder, cracked and laughing and utterly unafraid:

"Wouldn't trust you with a cheap one."
