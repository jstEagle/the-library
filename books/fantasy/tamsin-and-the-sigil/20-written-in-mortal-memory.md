## 20. Written in Mortal Memory

Here is the thing nobody tells you about dying: it's mostly bookkeeping.

I had imagined — in the rare moments I let myself imagine it at all — that the end would be loud. Storm and fire, Ossuar's hollow voice unmaking the mountain, Dominic screaming my name across a battlefield of ash. And all of that had happened, more or less, in the last hour. But now the noise had drained away like water through cracked stone, and what was left was me, flat on my back on the Worldspine altar, doing arithmetic.

The sigils covered both arms now, shoulder to fingertip, and most of my chest, and I could feel them burning out one by one like candles in a draft. Each one that guttered took a piece of me with it. That had always been the price. Use the power, lose the self. The gods had built the seal that way on purpose, Maerin told me once — a lock designed to punish anyone who picked at it.

So: arithmetic. How many sigils left? Maybe forty. Maybe thirty. How many did I need?

All of them. Every last clause of a covenant older than any kingdom, written in my skin because once upon a time a priest and a god and a defrocked woman with good intentions decided one child's memory was a fair price for peace.

And I was about to spend every clause at once.

"Tamsin." Dominic's voice came from somewhere above me, wrecked and raw. He'd held the storm-line alone for what the priests would later swear was an impossible length of time, and it had cost him — his hands were burned to the wrist, his hair gone white at the temples where the lightning had run through him looking for an exit. He knelt, and even kneeling looked like it hurt. "Stay with me. You stay with me, you hear? You're terrible at following instructions but this once—"

"Dominic." My voice came out as a whisper. I tried again. "I need you to do something for me, and you're going to hate it."

"That's been the whole arrangement from the start."

"Get everyone back. Away from the altar." I could see them at the edge of my vision — the pilgrims we'd come in with, the handful of mortal keepers who'd survived Ossuar's attack, huddled behind a fallen pillar. One of them was a girl from Corvane who'd traded three years of good luck for a loaf of bread during a famine winter. Another was an old man who prayed every night to no one in particular, just to keep the habit. "Everyone. Now."

"Why?"

"Because what I'm about to do is going to be very bright, and I refuse to have my last act be blinding my friends."

He didn't move. Of course he didn't. "There has to be another way. Maerin's text, the distributed clauses — we could find other seals, we could—"

"We *are* the other seals, Dom. All of us. Everyone who ever meant a prayer." I laughed, and it hurt, which felt appropriate. "That was always the answer. Not one keeper. Thousands. Millions. A covenant so spread out that no god can own it, because you can't steal what lives in everybody."

"You'll die."

"I'm already dying. The question is whether I die as a vault or as a door." I reached up — my arm shook, and the sigils along it flared weakly, gold going grey — and touched his burned hand. "You broke the old covenant to save your sister. I'm breaking this one to save everyone else. It runs in the company we keep."

Something moved through his face — grief and fury and that stubborn, storm-caller obstinacy that had gotten us out of a dozen disasters and into two dozen more. Then he leaned down and pressed his forehead to mine, careful as a man handling glass.

"When this is over," he said, "you'd better be there to be smug about it."

"No promises," I said. "Get them back."

* * *

They went. Slowly, unwillingly, the girl from Corvane crying, the old man muttering a prayer that — for the first time in his tidy, habitual life — he actually meant. I felt it land. That was the strange part, the part I hadn't expected: now that I was paying attention, now that the seal was coming loose, I could *feel* prayers. They arrived like weather. Small ones, mostly. *Let her fever break. Let the road be dry. Let me be brave tomorrow.* The whole world quietly talking to the dark, hoping the dark was listening.

It hadn't been, not really. Not since the covenant broke. Except when it answered me, because I was the dark's filing clerk, apparently, and everything routed through my skin.

Ossuar lay broken at the far end of the temple terrace, unmade down to a suggestion of a shape, its hunger still radiating off it like cold off ice. Veyra was gone — fled, or withdrawn, or simply waiting, because gods are patient in ways that are indistinguishable from cowardice. The Worldspine hummed under me, the old altar-stone warm as a sleeping animal.

I sat up. It cost me something enormous to sit up, and I paid it gladly, because if I was going to do this I was going to do it upright, like a person, not sprawled like a dropped marionette.

"All right," I said to the sigils. To myself. To whatever was listening. "Let's read the terms aloud."

I started with the first one I'd ever gotten — the key-shaped mark on my collarbone, the one that started all of this, the one a stranger once crossed a valley to ask about. I remembered getting it. I remembered waking with ink-bright lines on my skin and no idea what they meant, and being afraid, and then deciding — because that's what I do, it's apparently the only thing I reliably do — deciding to write it down anyway. *Day one. Something is wrong with me. Keeping notes.*

The sigil flared. And instead of taking a memory, it gave one back.

Not mine. Someone else's. A shepherd on a hillside four hundred years ago, praying over a sick ewe, meaning it so completely that the prayer rang like a bell — and the covenant, young then, whole then, catching that meaning and answering. That was the original design. Not worship. Not sacrifice. Just sincerity, gathered up by the millions, each small true prayer a thread, and the threads woven into law. The gods hadn't authored the covenant. They'd *signed* it, along with every mortal who ever meant what they asked for. Co-authors. Maerin had been right. The contract had always belonged to everyone who'd ever signed it in the only ink that mattered.

Which meant every one of those signatories still held a copy.

"Oh," I said aloud, to nobody, feeling the shape of it settle into place. "Oh, you absolute idiots. All of you. Me included. You can't own a covenant that's written in everybody. You can only ever *borrow* it."

The gods had known this, once. And then they'd forgotten, or chosen to forget, or — worse — arranged to forget, condensing the whole sprawling shared document into a single child, a single seal, a single point of failure they could guard, or trade, or shatter. Convenience. Ownership. The oldest sin.

Well. Time to put it back the way it was written.

I pressed both palms flat against the altar and opened every sigil at once.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. After months of paying in installments — a grandmother's face here, a birthday there, whole years shaved off and spent like coin — I braced for agony, and instead got something like the moment when you've carried a heavy pack up a long climb and finally shrug it off your shoulders. Relief so total it was almost vertigo.

The covenant poured out of me.

I could feel it going — every clause, every term, every binding promise — streaming off my skin in threads of light and flying outward, down the Worldspine, across the valleys, into the minds of everyone who had ever prayed sincerely. The shepherd's descendant on his hillside. The feverish mother in some river town. The girl from Corvane, fifty yards away behind a pillar, gasping as something settled into her like a second heartbeat. The old man with his habit-prayers. Strangers whose names I'd never know, in countries I couldn't find on a map, waking at that moment with the faint sense that they'd just been made party to something enormous and were now, technically, legally, responsible for it.

Including the valley. Including Hollow Valley, where magic wasn't supposed to follow, where the silence had been failing for weeks — I understood that now too, the valley's deadness had never been protection, it had been *storage*, a vault's thick walls, and vaults don't get to opt out of what happens to their contents. Somewhere below, in a candle shop that smelled of beeswax and lavender, the terms of a new-old pact were settling into the minds of neighbors who'd never in their lives wanted anything more dramatic than a good harvest. Mrs. Aldery with her rheumatism. The cooper's boy who prayed for his dog. All of them keepers now. All of them co-signers.

Unownable. That was the word, and it sang. No conclave could claim it. No god could hold it. No future priest could stuff it into one child and call the theft a mercy. From this moment, the covenant lived wherever people meant their prayers, which is to say everywhere, which is to say it could never again be taken, only *kept*, by everyone, forever, badly and imperfectly and humanly.

Power given away becomes law. I thought that, distinctly, in the last clear moment, and hoped someone would remember to write it down. Knowing this crowd, someone would.

The sigils faded as the covenant left them. I watched it happen — gold dimming to silver, silver to pale rose, rose to nothing but thin white scars, fine as spider silk, tracing the paths where divinity had lived in me. My arms looked like something a bored apprentice had practiced on. They looked like mine.

And the memories — the ones the sigils had eaten, the ones I'd journaled against losing, my grandmother's face, my tenth summer, the smell of the shop in the morning — they didn't come back. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of what I did. The power went out; the stolen years stayed gone. Whatever Veyra had promised, whatever perfect restoration she'd dangled, that door closed with the rest of it. Some of my past was simply over, the way a burned page is over even if the book survives.

But the journal survived. And the fragments people had helped me recover. And — this is the part that made me laugh, alone on an altar, bleeding light — the new memories. Because the covenant, scattering, had passed *through* me, and a few things stuck. Faces. Prayers. Ten thousand strangers' small hopes, filed briefly in a dying girl's head and left there like sand in the seams of a coat. I would carry other people's wishes for the rest of my life. It seemed fair. I'd been carrying everyone's consequences long enough; a few wishes were practically a promotion.

The light went out of the Worldspine. The humming stopped. The mountain was just a mountain.

I was still lying on it, though. Still bleeding, still broken, still — by any reasonable accounting — dying. The relief hadn't fixed anything. Relinquishing power doesn't heal the body; it just stops the body from being a bank.

Footsteps. Running. Dominic's face above mine, white-lit and furious with fear, and behind him the girl from Corvane and the old man and a dozen others, all of them wearing the same stunned expression of people who have just discovered they are shareholders in creation.

"It worked," I told him. My voice was barely there. "You're welcome."

"Don't." His burned hands hovered over me, useless, afraid to touch. "Don't do the joke thing. Tamsin. *Tamsin.*"

"The covenant's in everyone now. Every sincere prayer, everywhere. Nobody owns it." I got a breath in. It rattled. "Nobody ever will again."

"You're not allowed to—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter, the way you talk to something fragile. "The sigils are gone. The scars — they're just scars. So the seal isn't killing you anymore. Do you understand? The thing that was eating you, it's *out*. You can stop spending. You can just — heal. If you heal."

I considered this. Dying, it turns out, is largely a matter of bookkeeping, and I had just balanced the largest ledger in history, and somewhere in the columns there was — maybe — a small remaining credit. Not magic. Nothing divine. Just blood and bone and the ordinary stubborn machinery of a body that had, against all odds, finished its work.

"I'm keeping notes," I whispered. "Day one. Something is finally right with me."

His laugh came out half sob. Above us the sky was clearing, the storm he'd called breaking apart into harmless clouds, and far below — impossibly far, but I saw it, or dreamed it, and I've decided not to distinguish — lights were kindling in the valley town. Candlelight. Shop windows. People waking, rubbing their arms, murmuring to each other in the dark about the strange warm feeling they couldn't name, the sense of having just been trusted with something vast.

Somewhere among those lights was a candle shop that smelled of beeswax and lavender, waiting for a keeper who might, if the arithmetic was kind, come home to it.

"Hold on," Dominic said, gathering me up with ruined, gentle hands. "Just hold on. I've got you."

And for once — for the first time in this entire wretched, miraculous story — somebody's prayer got answered without costing anybody anything at all.

Mine. I meant it.
