## 21. Aftermath of Quiet Gods

The gods left the way a fever breaks — not all at once, but in stages, each one leaving you wondering whether you'd imagined how bad it had been.

I stood on the terraces above Corvane three days after the Unbinding and watched it happen. The city's thousand altars, which had blazed like festival fires for weeks, dimmed one by one. Not extinguished. Dimmed. As if someone had walked through the god-market turning down lamps, muttering *that's enough of that* under her breath.

Veyra's shrine went last. I know because I waited for it, arms crossed against the wind, feeling like a man watching a creditor's house burn and trying to decide whether to feel justice or grief. When the light finally guttered down to something a candle could outshine, I laughed. It came out wrong — too loud, too jagged — and an old woman selling roasted chestnuts gave me the look you give a drunk at midmorning.

"Sorry," I said. "Long war."

"Aren't they all," she said, and gave me a chestnut for free, which is more charity than any god ever showed me.

Here is what the new covenant actually did, stripped of Maerin's careful legal language and Tamsin's dying radiance: it took the machinery of miracle out of divine hands and distributed it into ours. Every sincere prayer now carried a fragment of the pact inside it — not power, exactly. Obligation. A god who heard a prayer honestly prayed was bound to weigh it, gently, the way you'd weigh a child's request rather than a summoner's demand. And they could no longer take payment in anything but what was freely given. No memories. No years. No children delivered to valleys that forget.

The gods hated it, of course. Or most of them did. But hatred, it turns out, binds just as well as love when you write the contract properly, and Maerin's rot-eaten text had been proper indeed. Co-authored with mortals, she'd said. She died before she could see it finished, which spared her the sight of me — a storm-caller who'd once shattered the old covenant out of love — helping draft its replacement out of guilt. There's a symmetry there that would have made her smile. It makes me want to put my fist through a wall.

But walls are expensive in Corvane, and I've developed a frugal streak.

* * *

They pardoned me eleven days after the Unbinding. A formal thing, conducted by a conclave of gods who no longer had the authority to exile anyone but hadn't yet accepted this, so the ceremony had the strained politeness of a divorce finalized by lawyers who used to be married.

Veyra presided. Of course she did. Even diminished — even bound — she filled the ruined temple the way weather fills a valley. She had lost the war for the Seal and won something subtler instead: relevance. Under the new covenant, prayers still needed hearing, and Veyra had always been the best listener among them. She had simply traded dominion for employment, and seemed, infuriatingly, at peace with the arrangement.

"Dominic Eriksen." She said my name like she was tasting an old wine and finding it improved by age. "Your oath-break stands forgiven. Your exile is dissolved. The skies are open to you."

"The skies were always open," I said. "That was sort of my whole problem."

A ripple went through the assembled divinities — amusement from some, disgust from others, and from Ossuar's remnant faction, that hollow hunger that never quite learned to be anything else. Ossuar itself had shrunk to almost nothing after the Unbinding. A god whose entire theology was *mortals as prey* does not thrive in an age where mortals hold the deed. Last I heard, it had retreated to some lightless corner of the Under Roads, growing smaller and quieter and, if Maerin's notes were right, possibly planning to grow again someday. Some problems don't die. They just go dormant and wait for people to get comfortable.

"You could have everything back," Veyra said, quieter now, and here was the thing about Veyra: even bound, even beaten, she never stopped making offers. It wasn't malice. It was architecture. She was built of doorways. "Your storms. Your name. The towers that used to light when you passed over them. All of it restored, and my gratitude besides."

"And the price?"

"No price." She smiled, and it was almost honest. Almost is doing heavy lifting in that sentence, but credit where due. "That is rather the point of the new world, isn't it? Freely given. Consider it an apology."

An apology. From the god who'd told me my sister was alive when she'd been dead for six years, because a grieving man is a leverable man. I looked at her for a long moment, and she let me look, and I will say this for her: she didn't flinch, and she didn't pretend she hadn't done it.

"I'll think about it," I said, which was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and she accepted it anyway, because the new covenant apparently required her to accept things gracefully. Small mercies. Enforceable ones.

I walked out of the temple into rain — real rain, ordinary rain, the kind that falls because of pressure gradients and cold fronts and has no opinion about anyone's soul. I stood in it until I was soaked through, and then I laughed again, and this time it came out right.

* * *

What do you do after the end of the world?

Nobody warns you about this part. The songs all stop at the climax — the altar, the dying light, the covenant scattered into ten thousand mortal minds like seed thrown to the wind. The songs don't tell you that afterward there is laundry. That there are debts to settle and rooms to rent and meals to eat alone at tables built for two, because the person you crossed the world with is lying in a healer's house in Corvane, breathing shallowly, having spent nearly everything she was to buy a law that would outlive her.

Tamsin didn't die. I need to say that plainly, because for three nights I believed she had, and I have carried dead people before and did not think I could carry her. She didn't die. But the sigils faded to scars — thin white lines across her skin like writing erased by a careful hand — and with them went whatever she might have become. More than human. Less than mortal. Whatever the seal had been building her into, that architecture collapsed the night she gave it away, and what woke up on the fourth day was a woman of perhaps thirty years with no magic, partial memories, and a journal full of handwriting that was hers and wasn't.

She woke up hungry. That was the first sign she'd be all right. She ate an entire loaf of bread, half a wheel of cheese, and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *deeply unimpressed*, and said:

"You look terrible."

"You died," I said.

"I didn't, though." She tore more bread. "You're going to have to get better at telling the difference."

And that was it. That was the reunion. No tears, no declarations. Two people who had run out of words somewhere around the salt flats and never quite found their way back to speech. We sat in the healer's house while rain ticked against the shutters, and she read her journal, and I pretended to read a shipping manifest, and neither of us said anything true for six hours.

It was Maerin's funeral that finally broke us open — or mine, anyway. They buried her ashes at the foot of the Worldspine, where the old altar had cracked down the middle and gone silent. Tamsin spoke. I couldn't. I stood at the back with my hands in my pockets like a man attending the funeral of someone he'd only met once, which was almost true; I'd known her nine days, and she'd known herself for sixty years, and somehow both of those felt insufficient.

Afterward, Tamsin found me sitting on a fallen pillar, watching the mountain.

"She gave you the text," Tamsin said. "The original."

"She did."

"Do you regret it? Any of it?" She sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. "Breaking the first covenant. If you'd known what it would cost — her, me, your sister —"

"Yes," I said. Then, because she deserved better than easy answers: "No. Both. I'd do it again and I'd burn the world down first to stop myself. I don't know how to hold both of those, Tamsin. I've been trying since the salt flats and I can't make them fit in one pair of hands."

She was quiet for a while. Below us, the valley where the conclave had gathered was already returning to its ordinary business — carts, arguments, a dog stealing a sausage with practiced shamelessness.

"My grandmother used to say grief isn't a room you leave," she said finally. "It's a room you learn to furnish."

"That's very wise."

"She stole it from a candle-maker in Corvane. Everything wise in my family is secondhand." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "You should come see the shop. When I go back."

"When?"

"Soon." She looked at her wrists, at the faint white lines that had been sigils, that had been clauses, that had been a childhood. "There's nothing keeping me here. That's rather the point of everything we did, isn't it? Nothing keeping anyone anywhere anymore. Just what we choose."

I didn't answer. Because there was a doorway standing open behind my ribs, the one Veyra had offered — storms, glory, the old name spoken with respect in a hundred cities — and I hadn't chosen yet, and choosing felt like closing every other door forever, and I had spent one lifetime learning what closed doors cost.

* * *

I stayed in Corvane another month. Long enough to see the god-market settle into its strange new shape — fewer altars, humbler ones, gods working quiet trades instead of grand bargains. A hearth-goddess who blessed kitchens for the price of a shared meal. A river-spirit who took payment in songs. Prayers answered rarely, and gently, and never more than asked. The world got smaller and kinder at roughly the same rate, and I kept waiting for it to feel like loss, and it mostly didn't, and that unsettled me more than any battle had.

I also stayed long enough to try calling a storm. Just once, on the cliffs north of the city, with the pardon warm in my pocket and the sky wide open and legally mine.

Nothing happened.

Well — not nothing. Something flickered, deep in my chest, like a lamp turned low. Enough to raise a breeze, to taste lightning on the back of my teeth. But the great rolling thunderheads I used to build like architecture, the storms that had made kings send envoys — those were gone. Whether the Unbinding had burned them out of me, or breaking my oath on the salt flats had, or whether I had simply spent everything I had in one final act of defiance and there was nothing left to spend — I couldn't tell. The healers couldn't either. Magic, they said, is like memory. Sometimes it comes back. Sometimes what you lost stays lost, and the shape of the absence becomes part of you.

I stood on those cliffs for a long time, tasting lightning I couldn't reach, and I realized I wasn't angry. That frightened me more than the loss itself. Dominic Eriksen, who had once held a grudge against heaven itself, standing in the wind feeling — what? Relieved?

Maybe that's what survival costs. Not blood or years or memory, though it takes all three. It costs you the version of yourself that wanted the big things. You come through the fire and you find the fire took your ambitions along with your friends, and what's left is a person who looks at a throne and sees a chair, who looks at a legend and sees work, who looks at the open sky and thinks: *it would be nice to sleep indoors tonight.*

* * *

Tamsin left for the Hollow Valley on a Tuesday. I know because she told me, twice, in the tone of someone issuing an invitation disguised as information.

"The pass is clear this time of year," she said. "If a person were traveling that direction."

"If a person were."

"They'd find the roads dull. Good inns, though. Terrible wine." She folded a shirt with tremendous concentration. "The shop needs reopening. Grandmother's candles don't sell themselves, and I'm told I have a talent for wax."

"Told by whom?"

"The innkeeper. This morning. While you were brooding on the cliffs." She glanced up. "You brood very loudly, did you know? The whole street can hear it."

"I'm considering my options."

"You're considering whether you're allowed to be happy." She said it without cruelty, which is the only way that sentence can be said to a man like me. "You're not, by the way. Nobody is. It's not a permission thing. You just do it badly for a while and then less badly, and eventually you look up and realize you've been doing it for years."

I opened my mouth to argue and found nothing there. She kissed my cheek — briskly, the way you'd stamp a document — picked up her pack, and walked out of the healer's house into the morning.

I stood in the empty room for a long time. Then I packed.

* * *

The road north took nineteen days. I could have flown it, once, in a fraction of the time. Now I walked it like everyone else, and here is the confession at the heart of this chapter, the thing I'll deny saying if anyone asks: I loved it. Every dull mile. The good inns. The terrible wine. The carters' gossip and the children who ran alongside my horse — I bought a horse, a fat placid mare named Biscuit, because a man walking north with no destination needs at least one indignity — shouting questions about whether I'd seen the Seal, whether the stories were true, whether gods really answered prayers now.

"Sometimes," I told them. "Rarely. Gently."

"Is that good?" one asked, a girl of maybe eight with a gap in her teeth.

"It's the best thing I know," I said, and meant it, and watched her run off satisfied, and thought: *there it is.* The purpose I'd been pretending not to look for. Not glory. Not restoration. Just this — being a man who tells children the truth about the sky.

The valley, when I reached it, was exactly as I'd left it: green, sheltered, stubbornly ordinary. The Warden met me at the boundary stone. He'd aged a decade in a season — the ledger-keeper whose records had nearly gotten Tamsin re-contained, whose bureaucratic diligence had been the instrument of a crime he hadn't fully understood until it unraveled around him. He looked at me the way men look at executioners they've decided to forgive.

"Eriksen." He consulted a ledger out of pure habit, then seemed to remember that ledgers didn't rule anything anymore, and put it away. "The valley council voted. You're welcome here. Formally." A pause. "Informally, the baker wants to meet you, because his daughter swears you killed a god, and his wife says you're too thin."

"Both accurate."

"Then you'll eat at their table tonight." He hesitated, and something human moved behind the officialdom. "The Vale girl reopened the candle shop. It smells like honey and juniper all the way to the square. People complain. Nobody means it."

I found the shop at dusk. Lights in the window, warm gold, and through the glass: shelves of candles in ranks like a small congregation, and Tamsin behind the counter, trimming a wick, her hair tied back, the scar on her wrist catching the lamplight — faint, white, ordinary-looking, the signature of a woman who had signed away a kingdom and kept herself.

She saw me through the window. She didn't wave. She just pointed at the door with the scissors, in the universal gesture of *well, are you coming in or are you going to stand out there composing poetry about it.*

I went in. The bell over the door rang, small and bright, and the smell of honey and juniper wrapped around me like something I'd been walking toward for nineteen days without admitting it.

"You're late," she said.

"The roads were dull. Good inns. Terrible wine."

"Mm." She set down the scissors. Behind her calm, I saw it — the same thing I was carrying, the furnished room, the grief and the gratitude sharing a lease. "There's soup. It's terrible. Grandmother's recipe, and I only recovered half of it."

"Half a recipe," I said, taking off my coat, "is more than I had this morning."

We ate soup at the counter because the table was buried under candle molds, and outside the window the valley settled into evening, and somewhere far off — beyond the mountains, beyond the bounded heavens — a god heard a child's prayer and answered it gently, or didn't, and either way the world held.

I slept that night in the room above the shop, in a bed that smelled of beeswax, and dreamed of nothing at all.

For the first time in twenty years, that felt like peace.

* * *

It lasted four days.

On the fifth morning, a letter arrived. No courier would own to carrying it; it was simply on the doorstep when Tamsin opened the shutters, sealed with grey wax pressed in the shape of a keyhole. Inside, a single line in handwriting I recognized from ledgers and from dreams:

*The vault was never emptied. One item remains unaccounted for.*

Tamsin read it twice. Then she looked at me, and her face did something complicated — fear and recognition braided together, the expression of a woman who had believed her account closed and found a column still open.

"I know that seal," she said quietly. "From the journal. From a page I don't remember writing."

Outside, the valley went on being beautiful. Somewhere beneath it, I suspected, something old had just remembered it was owed.
