## 22. Tamsin and the Sigil

The bell over the door of Vale & Grandmother's Fine Candles still rings in the key of B-flat, which I know because I checked it against a tuning fork three days after we came home, because apparently that is the kind of thing I do now. Verify small things. Count my spoons. Write down what the weather did.

Some people come back from a war with scars. I came back with a shop, a journal, and an inventory of myself that reads like a ledger with half the pages torn out — and I have decided, after considerable argument with the mirror, that this is not a tragedy. It's just bookkeeping.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The candle wax doesn't pour itself. Well. It didn't used to.

* * *

We came back to the Hollow Valley in the last week of autumn, Dominic and I, walking down the pass like two travelers who had very sensibly decided the world outside was overrated. Which, in fairness, it mostly is.

The valley's silence closed around us at the old boundary stone like water closing over a dropped coin. No gods answering prayers here. No sigils waking on my skin. Just the smell of pine and cold stone and, faintly, someone's bread baking, because the Hollow Valley has never once cared about my dramatic entrances or exits.

"You're smiling," Dominic said, suspicious, the way he always is when I display an emotion he can't fight with weather.

"I'm home," I said. "It's allowed."

He looked at me for a long moment — that grey-eyed look of his, the one that used to make Warden clerks flinch and now mostly makes me want to throw something soft at him — and then he said, "Your grandmother's shop will need a new roof beam. The left one. I noticed on the way out."

"You noticed the roof beam. While we were fleeing for our lives past your own house."

"I notice things. It's a curse." He shifted the pack on his shoulder. Inside it were exactly three possessions: a spare shirt, a whetstone, and Maerin's half-rotted covenant text, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, which I suppose it was. He hadn't decided where to put it yet. Neither had I. Some things you don't shelve; you just carry them until carrying becomes keeping.

The valley remembered us the way valleys do — not warmly, but completely. Mrs. Aldous from the mill house nodded at me as if I'd been gone a fortnight instead of a season. The Warden's office had been quietly emptied of everything pertaining to a girl delivered here under terms no one would name; the ledger page was simply gone, cut out with a razor, and the new Warden — a tired woman named Corvane-born Hesk who had inherited the job like a bad knee — looked at me across her desk and said only, "You'll be wanting your grandmother's shop reopened, then. There's forms."

There are always forms. It was almost comforting.

"Any conditions?" I asked, because I'd learned to ask.

Hesk considered. "Don't answer prayers," she said. "Not even little ones. Not here."

"Ma'am," I said, "I can't answer prayers anywhere anymore. That was rather the point."

She almost smiled. Almost. In the Hollow Valley, that's a parade.

* * *

The shop smelled of dust and tallow and my grandmother, or the idea of her, which is not the same thing but is the only version I have left.

Here is what I recovered, in the end, when the covenant scattered into ten thousand mortal minds and the seal dissolved off my skin like sugar in warm water: fragments. Shards. My grandmother's hands folding dough, vivid as noon. A dog named Button who died when I was six — I wept for him properly, finally, having grieved a hole for twenty years without knowing its shape. The sound of my mother's voice saying my *other* name, the priest-daughter name, Tamsin-of-the-Tower, and the way she said it like an apology.

And here is what stayed gone: her face. Both my parents' faces. The tower's name. Whatever my grandmother whispered over my cradle the night before they took me up the mountain. I have the *shape* of those memories the way you have the shape of a missing tooth — constantly, uselessly, with your tongue.

Dominic found me crying about it once, early on, standing in the shop holding a candle mold I'd apparently loved as a child and could not remember loving.

"The ritual could have—" he started, and stopped, because we'd had this argument, and because Veyra's last offer still sat between us sometimes at dinner like an uninvited guest. Every memory, whole and perfect, forever — purchased with my future choices, my right to become anyone other than their Seal. I'd watched the trap close in slow motion and walked out of it sideways, bleeding power the whole way down, and some nights the bleeding felt less like sacrifice and more like amputation.

"Don't," I said.

"I wasn't going to say you chose wrong."

"You were going to say it kindly, which is worse."

He leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, storm-caller turned candle-shop handyman, and said, "My sister's face went years before Veyra ever offered me anything. I built a whole rebellion out of one memory of her laughing. You know what I've learned since? The laugh isn't the person. I kept the laugh and lost her anyway." He shrugged. "You kept yourself. That's rarer."

"That's a terrible comfort."

"I'm exiled and unemployable. My comforts are all terrible."

I laughed, which I think was the point, and put the candle mold on the shelf where my hands said it belonged, even if my head couldn't confirm.

* * *

The journal sits behind the counter now, next to the account book, and customers occasionally mistake it for inventory. It is not inventory. It is scripture, if scripture were written by a woman with ink-stained fingers and a grudge against forgetting.

That was the plan, near the end — write myself down like a holy text so the burning away of each sigil couldn't take everything. And then the plan inverted, beautifully, terribly: when I scattered the covenant into every sincere prayer ever prayed, the writing became the law itself. Mortal memory, distributed. Ten thousand keepers instead of one seal. Maerin died for the seconds we needed to finish it, and I try to honor that by being meticulous.

So the journal holds both kinds of entries now. The ones I wrote to survive:

*Day 41. Used the binding-sigil at the ford. Lost the smell of rain on the valley road. Note to self: ask Dominic what rain smells like here, write it down in his words.*

And the ones that came back to me afterward, drifting home on the new covenant's currents like leaves returning upstream:

*I remember the tower now. Its name was Vesperhold. I remember why my parents gave me up — not because they didn't love me. Because love was the only currency the gods accepted, and they paid it gladly, and I hate that I understand them.*

Some pages are mine. Some pages are recovered. Some pages are both, my handwriting arguing with itself across the years. I used to find that frightening. Now I find it honest. A self you can recover whole is a self that never got to change, and I have changed, thank you, extensively, at great cost, and I would not give the changing back to buy the missing pieces.

Grandmother's face is gone. But I have her recipe for beeswax polish, in her handwriting, and I have the way she said "mind the wick, girl" in a voice I can still hear, and I have decided — deliberately, on purpose, as an act of authorship — that this counts. That I count. Incomplete and counting.

* * *

The shop reopened on a grey Tuesday with no ceremony, because ceremonies attract gods and we were done with those. I made forty candles the first week. Sold thirty-one. The seven unsold were the ones I'd poured while thinking too hard about the Under Roads, and they burned crooked, and I gave them to the shrine-less corner by the mill where people used to whisper prayers back when whispers did nothing. Let the crooked candles burn for nobody. It felt right.

Prayers still get answered out there in the wide world — rarely, gently, per the new terms. A farmer in Corvane swears his well filled the morning after he spoke to no one in particular about his luck. A sailor says a storm parted around his ship like water around a stone, and no god claimed it, and no god billed him. That's how we wrote it: freely given, freely answered, owned by everyone and therefore no one. Ossuar called it chaos from whatever hollow he retreats into. Veyra called it theft, at length, in front of witnesses, and I have the testimony transcribed in the journal under the heading *Sore Loser, Divine.*

In the valley, nothing answers at all. The silence here was always the point. I used to think it was a prison wall. Now I think it was a vault door, and I was the contents, and the difference between those two things is the whole story of my life — except it isn't anymore, because I'm not contents. I'm the shopkeeper. Contents and shopkeeper are very different positions, pay-wise and otherwise.

Dominic took the room above the shop. Officially he's "helping with repairs." Unofficially he stands on the roof during thunderstorms — we get maybe two a year, polite ones — and doesn't call anything, just listens, the way a musician listens to someone else play. His oath is broken and remade and broken again; the pardon from the storm-caller conclave arrived in autumn, sealed in lightning-struck vellum, offering him his old title back, his old glory, storms at his command.

He uses the letter as kindling for the shop stove. I've stopped asking whether that's peace or grief. Probably it's both, and probably both are allowed.

* * *

The child comes in on a Thursday in the first snow, trailing her mother and a smell of wet wool, and makes directly for the counter the way children make directly for whatever they've been told not to touch.

She stares at my forearm — bare, because I roll my sleeves for work now without thinking, without flinching, most days — at the faded mark there where the first sigil woke. It's a scar now, pale as old candle wax, a curling line like a key turned halfway.

"What's that?" she asks.

Her mother hushes her. I wave the hush off.

"It's a signature," I say.

"A signature of what?"

Good question. Excellent question. From the mouth of a babe, as the priests used to say, back when priests brokered with gods and called it mercy.

I consider the true answers. *A seal that held two realms apart. A debt I didn't owe, paid in full. The place where a god touched me and I declined to be touched.* All true. None of them the whole truth, and the whole truth is that it's still being written — that the scar is just where the pen rests between sentences.

"It's a signature of me," I say. "Everything important gets signed eventually. This just happened early."

She considers this with the gravity of a magistrate, then points at the shelf. "Can I have the blue one?"

"You may. Blue's for clear thoughts. That's not magic, that's just good wax."

Her mother pays, apologizes twice, and they go out into the snow, the bell ringing its reliable B-flat behind them. I watch through the fogged glass until they turn the corner, and then I stand a moment in the quiet — the good quiet, the valley quiet, the kind that keeps nothing and hides nothing and asks nothing of me.

Behind the counter, the journal waits with today's entry half-written. I pick up the pen.

*Tamsin Vale. Candlemaker. Keeper of nothing but herself, which is plenty. Weather: snowing, gently, as if someone up there remembers how.*

I pause, then add, because honesty is the whole discipline:

*Still missing: Mother's face. Father's voice. The tower's bells. Found again: the reason none of it was ever mine to carry alone.*

Then I sign it — my name, plain, no sigil, no seal, just ink — and go to pour the evening's candles, blue ones first, because tomorrow deserves clear thoughts, and the wicks won't mind themselves.
