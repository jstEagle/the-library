## 1. The Last Lit Road

The pass through Thornfell had a hundred names, depending on who was asking and what they were smuggling. The king's surveyors called it the Cold Spine. The shepherds called it the Widow's Teeth. The smugglers — Edmund's people, or the closest thing he had to people these days — called it the Quiet Road, because everything that traveled it did so without song, without fire, and without names.

Edmund called it work.

He paused where the trail narrowed between two slabs of granite furred with frost-lichen, and let his client catch up. She was doing better than he'd expected. Most soft-handed folk from the valley towns gave out by the second hour of climbing, all apologies and wheezing. This one — Tamsin Vane, though he doubted that was her whole name; nobody paid in honest coin under an honest name — kept her breath measured and her eyes moving. Watching the ridgelines. Counting the shadows.

That was either very good or very bad, and Edmund hadn't yet decided which.

"Rest," he said, and sat down on a stone as if he owned it. Twenty years of walking these mountains had taught him how to sit like the rocks did: patient, permanent, indifferent to weather. "Water. Small sips."

"I'm not tired." She said it the way people said things they wanted to be true.

"Nobody's tired on the Quiet Road until they fall off it. Drink anyway."

She drank. He watched her do it — small sips, no gulping, capping the flask between mouthfuls against the cold. Someone had trained her, or hunger had. There was a difference, and he was fairly sure he'd know which by the time they crossed the high shoulder of the fell.

The morning was the color of pewter, low cloud snagged on the crags above them like wool caught on brambles. Below, the valley they'd left at dawn was already a rumor — smoke from hearths, a thread of river, nothing else. Above, the mountain went up and up into grey, and somewhere past all that grey lay the border.

The border wards. Which was, of course, the first thing she asked about. Again.

"The wards," Tamsin said, screwing the cap back onto her flask. "You said last night they're old. How old?"

"Older than the road."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I sell cheap." He stood, brushed frost from his coat, and started walking. Behind him, after exactly three heartbeats, her boots crunched into step. Good. A client who argued but followed was manageable. A client who argued and lingered got eaten, or worse, got *noticed*.

"They say," she went on, undeterred, voice pitched to carry over the wind without carrying past it — another point in her favor, another thing to be suspicious of — "that the border wards are anchored to starlight. That the old kings bound them to the sky itself, so no army could cross while the stars were watching."

"People say a lot of things about borders. Mostly they say them from the safe side."

"Is it true?"

Edmund considered the question the way he considered most questions: whether answering it would cost him anything. The truth was that yes, it was true, or true enough. The ward-stones along the Thornfell line were cut with star-channels, grooves that filled with light on clear nights, and whatever fed them came from up there, from the slow wheeling of heaven. He knew this partly from smuggler lore and partly from a life he didn't talk about, back when he'd known a great deal more than smuggler lore about how power moved through the world.

"It's true," he said, because refusing to answer would only make her ask louder. "The stones drink starlight. On a clear night you can see them glowing along the ridge like a string of pale beads. Cross where they're lit and the mountain remembers your name. Cross where they're dark — a cave, a deep defile, a night of heavy cloud — and the mountain forgets you were ever born."

"And tonight? Will they be lit?"

He glanced back at her. She wasn't looking at the ridge. She was looking at *him*, with an intensity that had nothing to do with curiosity and everything to do with planning. Clients who planned were clients who got themselves killed, usually taking their guide's fee and reputation partway down with them.

"Tonight there'll be cloud," he said. "There's always cloud before snowfall. We cross in the dark and the dark is on our side. That's the whole trick of my trade, if you're wondering. I don't fight the wards. I just know when they're blinking."

"You make it sound simple."

"It is simple. Simple isn't the same as easy." He nodded ahead, where the trail bent around a shoulder of black stone. "From here we climb four hours to the high camp. We sleep cold, we eat cold, we speak softly, and tomorrow we cross the line while the whole sky is blindfolded. After that—" he shrugged, "—after that you're someone else's problem, in a country I don't guide in."

"You don't go past the border?"

"I deliver people *to* borders. It's in my rules."

"You have rules?"

"Everyone has rules. Mine are just written down." Three of them, actually, carved into memory rather than slate, each one purchased at full price. *Never carry what can't be dropped. Never sleep twice in the same bed. Never take a side.* Twenty years since he'd broken the third one, and the breaking had cost him his name, his craft, and every road south of the mountains. He'd rebuilt himself out of the wreckage into something simpler: a man who walked, who guided, who asked no questions and answered few.

It was a thin sort of life. But thin things bend. Thick things shatter, and Edmund Coldharbour had done enough shattering for one lifetime.

* * *

They climbed. The mountain did what mountains do, which is make you earn every yard twice.

By midday the cloud had thickened from wool to wet felt, and the first flakes came down — fat, unhurried, the kind of snow that meant business. Edmund read the sky the way other men read letters, and what it said was: *two days of cover, then a hard freeze.* Good crossing weather, if you were quick. Bad, if you weren't.

Tamsin Vane climbed like a woman with a schedule.

He noticed it the way he noticed everything, sideways, without seeming to. She didn't complain. She didn't chatter. When they stopped, she stopped breathing hard and resumed within moments. And all day long, in the gaps between her questions about wards and borders and the customs of the high passes, she kept glancing north. Not at the view. At something beyond the view.

People fled south across Thornfell all the time — debtors, deserters, widows of lost wars, folk whose faces didn't fit anymore. They looked over their shoulders. Tamsin Vane never once looked back. She looked *forward*, hungry, like a woman approaching a door she'd been locked out of for years.

Which raised the obvious question: what kind of person flees *into* the dark country?

Not his business. He told himself that with the ease of long practice. His business was getting her over the line alive and collecting his fee, which was generous enough to keep him in bread and lamp-oil through the winter. Whatever waited for her on the far side of the wards — a lover, a fortune, a grave — belonged to her, the way everyone's reasons belonged to them.

Still. When they halted at noon behind a windbreak of stacked stone, some ancient shepherd's cairn taller than a man, he heard himself say: "You ask a lot of questions about the wards for someone who just wants to get past them."

"Do I?" She was unwrapping bread, hard as roof-slate, and didn't look up.

"Most clients ask about the price. Some ask about the danger. You ask about the wards like you're studying for an examination."

"Maybe I like to know how things work."

"Maybe." He chewed. The bread tasted of rye and nothing else, which was what bread was supposed to taste of. "Or maybe you've got business on the other side that needs the wards to behave a certain way, and you want to know if they will."

For a moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — she went very still. It was the stillness of a hare that has heard the hawk, and Edmund had spent twenty years learning to see it, because that stillness always meant the same thing: *she knows I'm right.*

Then it passed, and she smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and crooked and entirely false. "I'm a wool merchant's factor," she said. "I have contracts in the lowland towns past the border. Cloth, mostly. Dye stuffs."

"Wool merchants don't pay in mint coin."

"This one does."

"Wool merchants don't hire guides through the Quiet Road. They take the king's road through the pass at Wethergate, tolls and all, because cloth doesn't care if the mountain remembers its name."

The smile held. Her eyes didn't. "Maybe I'm not a wool merchant's factor."

"There it is," Edmund said, almost gently. "Honesty. First one I've had from a client in years. I'd celebrate, but honesty on the Quiet Road is worth about as much as the snow." He rose, packing the bread away. "Here's what I know, and then we stop discussing it. You paid half up front, in good coin, and the other half waits at the crossing. My rules say I don't ask what my clients carry, and I don't ask why. So I won't ask. But I'll tell you this for free, since you seem fond of free things."

She waited.

"Whatever you're going to do over that border," he said, "the wards don't care. They're not moral. They're not the king's justice or anybody's justice. They're just an old, old hunger in the stone that drinks light and remembers names. You can't argue with them, bribe them, or outrun them. All you can do is be quiet and be dark and be quick." He shouldered his pack. "So save your questions. Save your plans. And when I tell you to move, move."

For a long moment she looked at him, and he had the uncomfortable sense of being weighed — measured against something in her mind, found heavier or lighter, he couldn't tell. Then she nodded, once, and picked up her own pack, and said nothing at all.

They walked.

* * *

The high camp was a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, floored with pine boughs gone brown and brittle, sheltered enough to hold a small fire if the fire was small and the wood was dry and the wind agreed — which, tonight, it did. Edmund built it the smuggler's way: a keyhole of stones, flame no higher than a hand, heat reflected instead of radiated, invisible from anywhere but inside the hollow itself.

They ate in near-darkness. Snow ticked against the overhang. Somewhere below, faint as memory, a wolf spoke and was answered.

Tamsin sat with her knees drawn up, hands around a tin cup of melted snow gone lukewarm, staring into the fire's small heart. The light moved on her face and made her look younger than she probably was and older than she wanted to be. There was a scar on her knuckles, old and white. There were lines around her eyes that hadn't come from laughing.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "that isn't about the wards?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends."

"What did you do? Before this." She gestured at the hollow, the fire, the whole gray economy of his life. "Before you were a guide."

The wind moved through the pines below with a sound like the sea, if the sea were frozen mid-sentence. Edmund fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch.

"I walked," he said.

"Everyone walks. You walk better than most. That kind of walking is learned."

"Is it."

"I grew up in hill country too," she said. "I know what it looks like when someone's at home on a mountain. You're not at home. You're *familiar*. Like an old married couple who don't speak anymore." She took a sip of her water. "Somebody taught you. Somebody with money, or a college, or both. Your boots are army pattern, resoled six times. You read the sky like scripture. And when I said the word 'wards' yesterday, you flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"You flinched. Right here." She touched the corner of her own eye. "Like a man hearing an old dog's name."

Edmund looked at her across the fire, and for a moment — a dangerous moment — he was twenty years younger, standing in a hall full of candle-smoke while an old man with ink-stained fingers drew circles in the air and said, *you feel it, boy, don't you? The pressure before the storm? That's not weather. That's a door.* He could smell the candle-wax. He could hear the rain starting against the windows of a palace that no longer had a king.

He put the memory down the way he put down everything heavy: carefully, and without looking at it.

"My teacher is dead," he said, which was true, or ought to be, or deserved to be. "My boots are comfortable. And the sky says snow by morning, so sleep while you can."

Tamsin held his gaze a moment longer. Then, to his surprise, she let it go — lay back against her pack, pulled her cloak to her chin, and closed her eyes. "Goodnight, Master Coldharbour."

"Edmund. Titles cost extra."

A ghost of a smile. "Goodnight, Edmund."

He kept the first watch, as he always did, sitting with his back to the stone and his face to the dark. The snow thickened. The fire shrank to a red eye and winked out. And overhead, where the cloud tore open for a handful of minutes — the way mountain clouds sometimes do, grudgingly, like a miser counting coins — the stars came out.

They were wrong.

Edmund had spent twenty years reading this sky. He knew its constellations the way he knew the lines of his own palm: the Ploughman, the White Hart, the Long Ladder of souls climbing toward the winter crown. He knew which stars the ward-stones drank from, knew their colors, their habits, their slow procession through the seasons.

Tonight, looking up through that ragged tear in the cloud, he counted them and came up short.

Not by one. Not by a few. Whole quarters of the sky were simply *empty* — black patches where the Hart should have stood, where the Ladder's rungs should have climbed. As if something enormous had passed between the world and heaven, drinking as it went. As if the oldest lights in creation were going out, one constellation at a time, and the dark left behind was not empty but *attentive*.

The hair rose along his arms. The storm-craft he no longer used stirred in his chest like an old wound remembering rain — a pressure, a wrongness, the taste of copper at the back of his tongue. Beneath the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep. Or stopped sleeping.

The cloud sealed shut. The stars were gone. The night was ordinary again, black and snowy and silent, and Edmund sat very still with his heart hammering like a fist on a door, telling himself: *clouds. Weather. High altitude and a tired mind.*

But he had been a storm-caller once. He knew the difference between weather and omen the way a widow knows the difference between sleep and death.

Behind him, in the dark of the hollow, Tamsin Vane whispered — so softly he might have imagined it, except that he had never once imagined anything on this mountain:

"They're going out. It's starting early."

And Edmund Coldharbour, keeper of rules, walker of neutral roads, understood that the fee he'd quoted was about to become the cheapest thing in either of their lives.
