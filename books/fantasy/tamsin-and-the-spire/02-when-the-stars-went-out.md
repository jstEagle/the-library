## 2. When the Stars Went Out

Tamsin Vane had counted the coins of her life so many times she could do it blind: eleven silver, a copper bent double from her mother's hand, and one soul, sold at seven years' interest. She had not expected to be counting stars.

There were too many to count. That was the trouble with the high camps in Thornfell — the mountains stripped away everything soft and low, all the haze and hearth-smoke of the valleys, and left the sky naked. From the shelf of stone where Edmund Coldharbour had made camp, the stars came down close enough to bite. They hung over the black teeth of the peaks like frost on a window, dense and cold and utterly indifferent, and Tamsin sat wrapped in two blankets with her back against a boulder that still held the day's warmth, and she hated them a little for being so beautiful.

Because she knew what was under them.

Not precisely — not the shape of it, not the name of it — but she knew something lived beneath Thornfell that bought what could not legally be sold, and she knew it kept its accounts by starlight. The broker's mark on the contract she'd signed seven years ago, pressed into the wax by a ring like a splinter of night, had been a small tower drawn upside down, its point driven downward into darkness instead of up toward heaven. Beneath it, in letters fine as spider silk, the terms: *Payable at the turning of the year. Interest accrues nightly, as the stars do.*

She had been nineteen and starving. She had read the words and thought them poetry. Everyone thought the brokers were poets; that was how they got you.

"Your fire's dying," Edmund said from across the pit.

He crouched at the edge of the light with his hood down for once, and without it he looked older than his voice suggested — fifty, maybe more, with grey coming into his beard like frost claiming a field one row at a time. His eyes stayed on the flames, not her. He had a way of looking at everything sideways, as if direct attention might cost him something.

"I know," Tamsin said. "I'm saving the wood."

"For what? It's not going to get warmer."

"It might get darker."

That earned her a glance. Brief, assessing, gone again. He fed two sticks to the coals anyway, which she chose to count as a kindness, though with Coldharbour it was impossible to say. Everything he did was folded up small, tucked into some inner pocket where a man keeps the things he doesn't want weighed.

They had been three days in the pass. He talked the way water dripped — steadily, usefully, never enough to fill anything. *Mind the scree there. Eat before the climb, not after. Don't drink the meltwater on the north faces; the old mines poison it.* Never: *Why are you crossing Thornfell in the dead season, alone, with no trade goods and no story worth believing?* He had asked once, on the first morning, and she had lied, and he had accepted the lie with a nod so flat it had frightened her more than any interrogation would have. A man who didn't care what you carried was a man who had stopped expecting anything from people. Or a man who already knew.

The wind came off the glacier above them with a sound like slow breathing. Below, the valley they'd climbed out of was a trough of blue-black shadow. Above, the pass wound up toward the border stones — and beyond those, the high fells where the wards began.

Tamsin had been asking about the wards since the first day. She couldn't help herself. Every question about them was a coin turned over, an edge examined, and if Edmund noticed her hunger — and he did, she could feel him noticing, the way you felt weather change behind a shut door — he gave nothing back except facts, dry as gravel.

*The wards are old. Older than the kingdom. Nobody living set them.* *They fail sometimes, in deep winter. The wardens re-hang them.* *No, I don't know who pays the wardens. Nobody knows. That's rather the point of wardens.*

Tonight she tried again, because tomorrow they would cross beneath them and her nerve would need every scrap of knowledge she could carry.

"The night they failed before," she said. "When you saw it. What did it look like?"

Edmund didn't answer at once. He turned a stick in the coals, end over end, watching the ember crawl along it.

"Like a curtain falling," he said finally. "You don't see the curtain until it moves. Then you realize the whole sky has been cloth all along." He dropped the stick. "It comes back, though. Always has. Whatever eats the light up here spits most of it out again."

*Most of it.* Tamsin turned that phrase over and found the sharp edge on it. "And the rest?"

"The rest goes down." He nodded at the mountain beneath them, at all the mountains, the whole rooted dark massif of Thornfell with its smuggler tunnels and its drowned mines and its buried things. "Everything in these mountains goes down eventually. Water. Rockfall. People."

"People," she said, "or souls?"

The wind filled the silence she'd made. Edmund looked at her then, properly, for the first time since noon, and his face in the firelight was unreadable as a closed ledger.

"You've been chewing on that question for three days," he said. "Like a dog on a bone. I'd ask what's buried under your floorboards, Miss Vane, but I made a rule about clients' floorboards twenty years ago and it's the only reason I'm still alive." He rose, brushing ash from his knees. "Sleep. We cross the border stones at first light, and after that we walk the warded miles in the dark, because I'll not have a fire up there drawing attention. So sleep while you can burn wood honestly."

He rolled into his bedroll facing away from her, and the conversation was over, sealed, done — the way he ended everything, with a door closing so quietly you almost missed that it had been open at all.

Tamsin did not sleep.

She lay under her blankets and watched the stars through the gap between her hood and the boulder, and she did arithmetic she had forbidden herself for seven years. Wren would be fifteen now. Fifteen, if the Spire let its purchases age. She had been told — by a priest who drank, by a hedge-witch who took her last copper, by everyone who had ever taken her money to tell her comforting things — that the souls the brokers held were kept safe, kept *whole*, that the Spire was a bank and not a larder. But she had seen the broker's face when she signed. She had seen how he looked at Wren, asleep in the cart with famine hollowing his cheeks, and it had not been the look of a banker appraising collateral.

It had been the look of a man deciding which apple to eat first.

Three weeks until midwinter. Three weeks until the turning of the year, when the contract came due and whatever the Spire did with what it was owed, it would do to her brother, and she would be a woman who had sold him twice — once to save him, and once, by her cowardice, to feed the thing below.

Above her, the stars burned on, vast and patient and numbered, and Tamsin Vane lay awake among them and rehearsed, for the ten-thousandth time, the words she would say to him. *I'm sorry. I came back. It took me seven years but I came back.*

She was still rehearsing when the sky broke.

*

It began as a sound.

Not loud — that was the horror of it. Not thunder, not rockfall, none of the great mountain noises she had learned these three days to sort in her sleep. It was small and crystalline and intimate, a sound like a wineglass tapped with a fingernail, and then another, and then dozens, hundreds, a rain of tiny fractures somewhere very far above, as if the night itself were a pane of ice and someone had struck it with a needle.

Tamsin sat up so fast the blankets fell away.

Edmund was already standing. She hadn't heard him move; he was simply upright at the edge of the dying fire, head tipped back, and the look on his face was one she would carry the rest of her life — not fear, not yet, but the terrible attention of a craftsman hearing his own work fail in someone else's hands.

"No," he said. Quietly. To himself. "No, that's wrong, that's—"

The sound swelled. The little fractures joined into long running cracks, a lattice of breaking spreading across the heavens, and Tamsin realized she could *see* it — faint lines of pale light stitching themselves between the stars, a web, a net, a pattern of wards hung across the whole bowl of the sky like lace across a window. She had walked beneath the border wards for three days and never once seen them. Now, dying, they showed themselves: a geometry of silver seams spanning peak to peak, anchored — she understood it all at once, the way you understand a fall only once you're falling — anchored to the stars themselves. Each thread of the web ran from one bright point to another. The wards had been hung on the sky. The sky had been their frame.

And the sky was being pulled apart.

A seam snapped directly overhead with a crack like the world's largest hearthstone splitting, and the stars along it went out — not dimmed, not veiled, *out*, snuffed clean, leaving holes in the night. True holes. Absences. Tamsin's mind refused it and presented it again: stars do not go out like lamps, stars die over ages, but these died like candles pinched between wet fingers, one, then five, then a constellation's worth, the Swan unraveling feather by feather above the eastern ridge.

"What is it?" Her voice came out thin. "Coldharbour. What's happening?"

He wasn't listening. He had both hands raised now, palms out, fingers spread, the stance of a man catching something or holding something back, and she saw — she was almost sure she saw, in the last of the light — a shimmer around his hands, a taste of ozone on the air, the ghost of a storm gathering in a man who had sworn off storms.

"They're anchored to starlight," he said, and his voice had changed, gone rough, gone young. "Twenty years I crossed under these wards and never — the old stories said the first wardens climbed to the roof of the night and sewed the borders shut with stars, and everyone laughs at that, everyone *laughs*—" A seam tore from ridge to ridge with a shriek of glass. "Something's pulling them out. Something's eating the anchors."

The web collapsed all at once.

There was no other word for it. The whole luminous lattice let go, and the sound was the sound Tamsin would hear in her dreams ever after: not one glass breaking but every glass in the world, a chord of shattering that rolled down the mountainsides and came up through the stone under her knees and through the bones of her hands where she braced against the ground. And the stars went with it. All of them. In a single breath, from horizon to horizon, the sky emptied — the Swan, the Hunter, the North Nail that sailors steered by, the thousand cold fires she had counted an hour ago as if numbering her enemies — gone, all gone, swept away like sparks up a chimney, and the darkness that rushed in behind them was not the ordinary dark of cloudy nights.

It was a dark with weight. It leaned on the world.

Tamsin heard herself making a sound, a small animal noise she was ashamed of, and pressed her fist against her mouth. The fire had shrunk to a red eye no bigger than a coin. Beyond its reach the mountains had ceased to exist; she knew intellectually that the ridges and the glacier and the border stones were still there, but her eyes reported nothing, a smooth and absolute blackness, as if the world ended at the firelight's edge and she stood on the last lit island of creation.

Beside her, Edmund lowered his hands slowly, the way a man lowers a shield after a blow that never landed.

"It swallowed them," he said. His voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "The wards and the stars together. Whatever's down there —" he turned his head, and even in the near-dark she felt the direction of his gaze, down, always down, into the roots of Thornfell — "it just drank the sky."

"That's not possible."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. And yet."

The cold came next. Not gradually, the way mountain nights cooled, but all at once, a tide of it rolling up from below, from the deep places, and Tamsin's breath plumed white and her fingers went wooden inside her gloves. The fire fought it, guttering, flaring, guttering, a candle arguing with the sea. She scrambled for the woodpile and fed it blindly, stick after stick, and Edmund knelt opposite her and did the same, and for a while neither of them spoke and the only work in the universe was keeping one small flame alive against a dark that wanted it.

It held. Barely. A stubborn orange circle four feet across, and the two of them huddled inside it like the last survivors of something.

"The wardens," Tamsin managed. Her teeth chattered around the words. "You said the wardens re-hang them. When they fail."

"They will." But he said it strangely, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ice. "In spring. They come in spring."

"It's three weeks to midwinter."

"I'm aware of the calendar, Miss Vane."

"Then who re-hangs them now? Who walks up there tonight and sews the sky back on?" She heard her own voice climbing and could not stop it. "Because my brother's debt comes due at the turning of the year, and if the turning happens under a sky with no stars in it, if the Spire does what it does with the whole night swallowed and nothing watching—"

She stopped. Too late. The words were out, hanging in the firelight between them, and Edmund Coldharbour had gone very still.

The wind moved. Somewhere in the blackness below, loose stone clattered down a slope, a small indifferent music. Tamsin sat with her secret spilled at her feet like dropped coins and waited for the guide to decide what she was worth.

Edmund stared into the fire for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet and level and terribly tired.

"Say that again," he said. "Slowly. And this time, don't stop at the part where you think I'll walk away."

Above them, where the stars had been, the darkness leaned closer, and the fire burned low, and Tamsin Vane opened her mouth to tell the truth for the first time in seven years.
