## 3. A Debt in Winter

The dark did not lift with the morning. That was the first wrong thing.

Edmund had spent twenty years learning the habits of night — its patience, its small mercies, the way it always thinned at the edges before dawn like a held breath released. But this darkness had no edges. It sat on them like wet wool, total and indifferent, and when he struck a match against his thumbnail the flame burned small and frightened, as if it too understood that something larger than fire had gone out of the world.

They were still at the high camp, in the lee of a shelf of granite that smugglers called the Widow's Shoulder because it shrugged off avalanches the way a widow shrugs off grief — eventually, and without warmth. Edmund had gotten the tent up by touch alone, which was a skill he'd hoped never to need again after the war years. Tamsin Vane sat inside it with her knees drawn to her chest, and she had not spoken in four hours.

He counted that as data. In his experience, clients who talked too much were nervous; clients who went silent were terrified; and clients who had been asking pointed questions about border wards for three days straight and then stopped entirely were usually guilty of something.

"Water," he said, setting the skin down within her reach. He heard her fumble for it, drink, set it back. Small sounds carried strangely now, flattened, as though the dark were eating the echoes too.

"You should eat," he said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You ate half my salt pork on the second day and pretended you hadn't." It was meant to be dry, almost kind. She didn't laugh. He let the silence stand and turned to the mouth of the tent, where he could watch nothing at all with great attention.

His rules were simple, and he recited them to himself the way other men said prayers. Get the client where they paid you to go. Ask no questions you don't want answered. Never carry anything across the Thornfell line that has a heartbeat or a name. And above all: when the world breaks, walk away from the breaking. There was no shame in it. The mountains didn't reward loyalty. They rewarded the ones who left early.

The problem was that the breaking was everywhere. There was nowhere to walk *to*. The pass behind them was warded — had been warded, he corrected himself, past tense now, since whatever had swallowed the stars had evidently swallowed the wards with them. Ahead lay the high col and beyond it the deep country under Thornfell's eastern eaves, the territory every map in Hollowmere marked with a black inverted tower and the single word of warning Old Sable charged extra to repeat: *Don't.*

And here he was, camped on the doorstep of it, sharing salt pork with a woman who had lied to him about why she was crossing.

He wasn't angry about the lie. Lies were currency; he dealt in them daily. What troubled him was the shape of it. She hadn't lied like a criminal or a coward. She'd lied like someone carrying something so heavy she couldn't set it down even to sleep.

"Tamsin." He kept his voice level. "I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to understand that I don't care about the answer. I care that you stop deciding what I can handle."

A long pause. Outside, wind moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing.

"I sold my brother," she said.

* * *

She told it badly at first, the way people tell things they've only ever told themselves — out of order, in fragments, with the important parts buried. Edmund let her. He banked the little flame lower and lower until it was a bead of light no bigger than a lentil, because some confessions need shadow to grow in, and he knew that too well to pretend otherwise.

"It was the famine winter. Seven years ago. You'd have been — I don't know where you were. Somewhere warm, probably." Her voice had a flatness to it, scraped clean. "The valley farms failed two years running. Blight in the barley, then rot in what was left, then the granaries — the lord's men took the granaries. For taxes, they said. For the war." A breath. "My mother died in the first month. Not of hunger. Of hunger's arithmetic. She gave us her portion and called it a stomach ailment, and we were children, and we believed her, which was the cruelest part."

Edmund said nothing. Outside the tent, the dark pressed close enough to feel against his skin, like standing near a cold window.

"There were brokers in the valley that winter. I thought they were merchants at first. Fine coats, soft voices, scales of black glass they'd set up in the market square where the church used to be. They weren't buying grain. They were buying—" Her hands moved in the dimness, shaping something invisible. "Souls. They called it *consignment*. They had such gentle words for everything. They said it wasn't selling, it was *storing*. They said the soul would be kept safe beneath the mountain, safe from famine, safe from death even, and the family would receive food enough to live. Three years' bread for one soul. More if the soul was young."

The bead of flame trembled. Edmund told himself it was the draft.

"Wren was six," Tamsin said. "Six years old, and light as a bird, and he used to follow me everywhere and ask questions the way you breathe — constantly, without thinking. And there was this moment. I need you to understand there was a moment. The broker looked at me and he said, *your brother will never be hungry again*, and I was so hungry myself that those words sounded like music. Like salvation. I signed the ledger with a pen that wrote in silver, and the ink sank into my skin and vanished, and I walked home carrying a sack of flour heavier than Wren was, and I told him we were rich."

She stopped. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"He asked me where we'd sleep that night, because the broker's men had taken our house as part of the price, and I said somewhere better. And he believed me. He *always* believed me. That's the thing about a six-year-old — they hand you their whole trust like it's nothing, like it grows back."

Edmund found his voice somewhere low in his chest. "What exactly did you sell? Say it plainly. I deal better in plain terms."

"Their claim on his soul. His— " she made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could bleed, "—his *debt*, they called it. The Spire holds him. Not his body — he stayed with me, he grew up, he's fourteen now and taller than me and he snores like a dray horse. But the part of him that isn't flesh. They hold it. And the consignment comes due at midwinter. Three weeks from tonight. When it comes due, they collect. He goes beneath the mountain, body and all, and he doesn't come back, and I—" 

Her composure, which had held through the telling like ice over deep water, finally cracked. Not into sobs. Into something quieter and worse: a stillness, a folding-in, the way a person becomes very small when they've said the unforgivable thing aloud and are waiting to find out if the world will agree that they're unforgivable.

"So that's why you wanted to cross the wards," Edmund said. "That's why you asked about them every hour of every day. You weren't curious. You were watching for signs of the Spire."

"They say the way in is unguarded from the north. Through the old passes. That ordinary people have walked in and bargained and walked out."

"People also say the moon is cheese and the king is just. People say a great many things with their eyes shut."

"Then tell me I'm a fool," she said, and now there was an edge under the ruin of her voice, and he respected it, because anger was load-bearing. "Tell me to turn around. Tell me there's no bargain to be made with a thing that eats souls, that the debt is written and the ink is silver and silver doesn't wash out. Tell me, and then watch whether I listen."

* * *

Edmund stepped out of the tent.

It was a foolish thing to do — the cold came through his coat like a blade finding the gap between ribs — but he needed the dark around him instead of in front of him, needed to look at it rather than sit inside it listening to a woman dismantle herself. Above the Widow's Shoulder, where the sky should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover nothing, which has texture and movement and the promise of weather. This was absence with weight. He raised his hand and his fingers vanished at the wrist.

Twenty years ago, he could have lit this whole mountainside. Twenty years ago, storm-light would have poured off him in blue-white sheets, and the thunderheads would have come when he whistled, and the dark would never have dared sit on him like this. He had given that up — or it had been taken, depending on who told the story, and both tellings were true — and in twenty years he had learned to live small. Small fires. Small roads. Small promises, kept precisely.

*I don't take sides,* he told clients. *I take coin.* It was a good line. It had kept him alive through the purges and the border wars and the years when having once been the Storm-Caller of Coldharbour was worth more dead than alive. And it was a lie, of course, the way all good lines are lies: neutrality isn't the absence of sides, it's the side you chose without admitting it, the side of whoever pays, the side of going home.

Wren. Six years old. *Your brother will never be hungry again.*

He thought about the village of Marrow's Bend, though he tried not to, though he had spent two decades building walls around that particular door. He thought about a king's messenger with soft gloves saying *the village harbors rebels, call the storm, it's a soldier's mercy, quick and clean*, and he thought about the starving faces he'd seen from the ridge on his way down afterward, after refusing, after the stripping of his name, and how mercy had looked on those faces like something no army had ever delivered.

The famine had been engineered. He'd suspected it even then — granaries full while valleys starved, lords profiting on both ends. And now here was a woman who had lived inside that machine, who had fed her brother into it one silver signature at a time, and the machine was coming due, and every road out of these mountains ran through the machine's teeth.

He could walk away. The thought arrived with perfect clarity, wearing its best clothes. Go south, circle wide, take the loss of her fee, be in Hollowmere by week's end drinking Sable's terrible plum brandy and telling no one anything. The Spire was older than kingdoms. Men had challenged it before — wardens, priests, one entire order of star-mages whose names survived only as curses — and the Spire had digested them all. One exiled storm-caller and one guilty sister were not a rescue. They were an appetizer.

But.

But the child-soul fragment he'd once seen in a warden's cage, years ago, on a run he'd never spoken of — the small grey flicker that had pressed its face to the bars and watched him pass with eyes like the last coals of a fire. He had walked past it. He had told himself *rules* and *coin* and *not my cargo*, and he had walked past it, and he had never once in twenty years managed to stop hearing it.

*Tamsin.* He went back inside. The bead of flame had nearly died; her face floated above it, pale and waiting, already braced for his refusal.

"Here's what I know," he said. "The Spire doesn't bargain. It buys, and it collects, and the only people who walk out are the ones it hasn't finished weighing. Anyone who told you otherwise was selling you the road, and I'd like to know who, because I'll want words with them later." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Here's what I know besides. The wards failed last night. All of them, at once, anchored to starlight — which means the Spire did something unprecedented, something desperate or triumphant, and either way it means the mountain is *busy*. Busy things make mistakes. Busy things leave doors ajar."

Her breath caught. He hated himself a little for the hope he was feeding her, and hated the world more for making hope a cruelty.

"And here's the last thing I know," Edmund said, and heard, to his own surprise, the sound of a wall cracking somewhere inside him, quietly, like lake ice in spring. "Three weeks is not enough time to talk you out of this. I've watched you for three days. You'd spend them walking anyway, alone, into country that kills trained guides for practice. So the choice isn't whether you go. The choice is whether you go stupid or go with someone who knows the drowned stair and the warden-tolls and which of the Spire's servants can be bribed with silence."

Tamsin stared at him. In the dying light her eyes were wet and fierce. "You'll take me in?"

"I'll take you *as far as makes sense*," he said, harsher than he intended, because the crack in the wall was widening and he could hear his own twenty years of careful neutrality groaning under the strain. "There's a haven under the eastern eaves. Hollowmere. If anyone alive knows a way into the Spire's country and back, it's Old Sable, and if Sable says impossible, then impossible it is, and you'll grieve and live, and that will be the end of my part in it. Do you accept those terms?"

"Yes," she said instantly. Then, softer, with the first human warmth he'd heard from her since the stars went out: "Thank you. Edmund. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm still deciding whether I'm helping you or just slowing down your suicide." He pinched out the flame. The dark rushed in, but it felt different now — shared, at least. "Sleep. We move at what should be dawn, if dawn still remembers how to happen. And Tamsin — the brother's name. Wren. Say it once more."

"Why?"

"Because names are cargo," Edmund said, settling against the cold stone, "and I've decided to carry this one."

Above the Widow's Shoulder, far off in the direction of the deep country, something beneath the mountain sighed — a vast, subterranean sound, like a ledger being opened, like pages turning in the dark. And in the total blackness, faint as memory, Edmund thought he saw, for half a heartbeat, a single point of light where no star had any right to be.

It was moving. It was descending. And it was coming toward them.
