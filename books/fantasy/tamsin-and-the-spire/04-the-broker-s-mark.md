## 4. The Broker's Mark

The waystation appeared out of the dark like a promise neither of them had asked for.

Tamsin saw it first — or thought she did. A shape in the blackness where no shape should be, square-shouldered and stubborn, crouched against the mountainside the way a tired man crouches against wind. Then Edmund raised his storm-lantern higher, and its pale blue light caught the edges of it: a low stone building with a slate roof furred in frost, a door of iron-banded oak, and above the lintel a carved sign whose letters had been worn to suggestions.

*The Kettle & Crow*, she read, tracing the ghost of the letters with her eyes. *Waystation. Travelers Welcome.*

"Smuggler rest," Edmund said behind her. His voice had gone flat in that particular way she was learning meant he didn't like something. "There are eleven between here and the pass. This is the fourth."

"You've counted."

"I count everything." He moved past her, boots crunching on the frozen scree, and Tamsin noticed again how quietly he walked for a big man — as though the mountain had agreed, long ago, not to announce him. "It'll have a hearth, a well, maybe stores if we're lucky. We can burn wood without the smoke showing. Sleep in shifts."

"And you don't like it because—"

"Because I said travelers welcome." He stopped at the door and set his palm flat against the oak, the way a physician touches a fevered brow. "And nobody's welcomed anyone anywhere on this stretch since the stars went out. If this place is standing open, either someone's inside who shouldn't be, or someone left in a hurry and didn't bar the door behind them." He glanced back at her, and the lantern-light turned his face into planes and shadow. "Both of those are reasons to be careful. Neither is a reason to freeze on an open slope. Stay behind me. Touch nothing until I say."

The door wasn't locked. It swung inward at his push with a groan of cold-swollen hinges, and the smell that came out made Tamsin's stomach clench — not rot, exactly, though there was a sweetness under it that her hindbrain insisted was rot wearing a disguise. It was the smell of a room where a fire had died a long time ago. Cold ash and old tallow and stone breathing out its stored chill.

Edmund went in low, lantern held wide, and she followed close enough that his cloak brushed her knuckles. The main room opened around them: a long table scarred by knives and tankard-rings, benches pushed haphazard from it, a bar along one wall with bottles still ranked behind it like soldiers abandoned mid-parade. Dust lay thick over everything — but not evenly. There were tracks in it. Boot prints, many sets, crossing and recrossing toward the back of the room, and every single one of them pointed *away* from the hearth.

Nobody had come in. Everybody had gone out.

"The well's through there," Edmund murmured, nodding at a side door. "Stores beyond. But look at the hearth first."

She looked. The great fireplace dominated the far wall, big enough to roast an ox, and it was full of ash — but the ash was wrong. It hadn't burned down naturally. It had been *snuffed*, packed hard and dead, and pressed into its surface, deep enough to survive, was a mark.

Tamsin leaned closer despite every instinct screaming at her not to. The sigil was perhaps the length of her forearm: a tower, drawn in a single unbroken line, but inverted — its point driven downward into the ash instead of rising toward heaven, its base a crown of radiating strokes like the rays of a black sun. Around the tower's waist coiled a serpent eating its own tail, and where the serpent's eye should have been, the artist had pressed a single fingerprint into the ash, whorled and precise, like a signature.

"It's beautiful," she heard herself say, and hated the word even as it left her mouth.

"That's the trouble with it." Edmund hadn't come near the hearth. He stood by the bar, and Tamsin realized he'd poured himself nothing — just stood with his hand wrapped around an empty glass, the way men do when they need their hands to hold something that isn't shaking. "I've seen that mark twice before in my life. Once chalked on a prison wall in Greyharbour, the night before the whole cell block went quiet. Once branded into the shoulder of a man who sold me passage and then tried to sell *me*. Both times, everyone nearby ended up dead or worse." He set the glass down with exaggerated care. "The Spire doesn't leave graffiti, Tamsin. It leaves receipts."

*The Spire.* She'd said the name aloud only once, three nights ago, in the black camp under the murdered stars, when the truth had torn out of her sideways — *my brother, I sold my brother* — and Edmund had listened without a flicker of judgment and then, worse, without a flicker of surprise. Since then they had spoken of it only in the oblique grammar of people carrying something breakable. *The debt. The mountain. What's below.* Never the name itself, as if naming it might make it turn its head.

But it was written here now, pressed into a dead fire in an empty inn, and there was no more pretending the road and the thing beneath the mountain were separate countries.

"There were wardens here," she said. "Weren't there? That's what these boot prints—"

"Wardens travel in threes." Edmund was moving along the bar now, reading the dust the way other men read books. "Faceless ones, the stories say. They trade in silence; they take warmth. And they don't stop for drink." He lifted one of the bottles, squinted at the seal, put it back. "These prints aren't theirs. Too shallow. Too many. These are *traders'* prints — smugglers, couriers, the usual traffic. They came through after the wards fell, found this place already marked, and ran." His jaw tightened. "Every one of them running the same direction. Downhill. Toward the lowlands. Nobody runs downhill in Thornfell unless the alternative is worse."

Tamsin hugged her arms against her chest. The cold in the room had teeth. It wasn't the honest cold of altitude — that she knew, had grown up with, had hated and survived. This was a cold with *intent* in it, a cold that felt less like weather than like attention. The same cold she'd felt at the high camp the night the sky went blind, pressing against the tent canvas like something listening.

"Then where are the wardens?" she asked. "If the traders ran from them—"

Edmund didn't answer. He had gone very still by the end of the bar, and he was looking at the doorway beside the hearth, the one that must have led to the keeper's quarters. Tamsin followed his gaze and saw what he saw: a thin line of frost on the floorboards, leading from that doorway out into the room, precise as a drawn border. Frost in a perfect rectangle, the size and shape of a bed.

No. Not a bed.

A *body-shaped absence* of warmth, outlined in rime, as if the floor remembered something lying on it that had been colder than the stone itself.

"He's in there," Edmund said quietly. "Or what's left."

* * *

The keeper's room was small: a cot, a chest, a shelf of chipped crockery, a window shuttered against the night. The warden lay on the floor beside the cot, and Tamsin's first thought — absurd, shameful — was that it was a statue someone had abandoned mid-delivery.

It had been tall once. The robes were still intact, grey wool gone stiff as parchment, and the hood was up, and where a face should have been beneath the hood there was only smoothness — not skin, not bone, just a featureless plane like the inside of an eggshell, faintly luminous, faintly *frosted*. One arm was flung out across the floorboards, fingers splayed, and from each fingertip a thread of ice had grown outward, branching and rebranching, until the whole corner of the room wore a lacework of frozen filigree, delicate as ferns, terrible as a spiderweb.

But it was the *thinness* that undid her. The warden hadn't bled. Nothing had cut it. It had simply been — emptied. Drained the way a wineskin is drained, the way a lamp is drained, left collapsed inside its own robes with all the heat and all the whatever-else-it-had been taken out of it, and the frost around its fingers wasn't violence. She understood that suddenly, looking at the branching ice. It was *reaching*. It had been reaching for something when it was taken, grasping at the last warmth in the room, and the warmth had walked away untouched.

"Don't touch it," Edmund said. He had come in behind her, and his voice had changed — dropped its dryness entirely, gone rough and low, the voice of a man speaking from somewhere further away than the small room they stood in. "Don't go near the hands."

"What could take a warden?" Her own whisper sounded loud. "You said they trade in silence. You said they *take* warmth. What takes *them*?"

He didn't answer at once. He crouched — carefully, outside the reach of the frozen fingers — and studied the warden's hooded blankness, and Tamsin watched his face while he did it, watched something move through it that she couldn't name. Recognition, yes. But underneath the recognition, something older and more complicated. Grief's awkward cousin. The feeling of seeing handwriting you know on a letter bearing bad news.

"Tamsin," he said. "When I told you I was exiled. When I told you I refused the king's wars and lost my name for it. Did I tell you who taught me?"

She shook her head. In seven days on the road together he had given her the shape of his history but never its furniture — the exile, the refusal, the twenty years of neutral roads, all of it sketched in a few dry lines he clearly resented having to draw at all.

"A broker," Edmund said. "That's what he called himself. Master Corvane. He came to the capital when I was nineteen, a storm-caller of the second rank with more power than sense, and he took me as an apprentice because he said I had, and I quote him because I've never forgotten it, 'a talent for the arithmetic of weather.'" Edmund rose slowly, and the lantern in his fist threw his shadow huge against the shuttered window. "He taught me to read pressure like ledger-lines. To call lightning down a wire instead of loose. To hold a storm in a bottle-glass and bank it like coin. Everything I know about discipline — everything that kept my gift from killing me before I was twenty-five — came from him. Three years. He was the closest thing to a father the crown ever gave me."

The frost-lace on the floor glittered. Somewhere in the walls of the waystation, the mountain ticked and settled, patient as a clock.

"And then?" Tamsin asked, though she already knew the shape of the answer. Everyone's story broke somewhere. Hers had broken in a famine winter with a ledger and a broker's pen.

"And then the war got hungry." Edmund's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The king wanted storms called on villages that fed his enemies' armies. Starve them out, freeze them out — cheaper than sieges. Corvane drew up the orders himself. Elegant work. He always said destruction was just accounting done at scale." He turned away from the warden, and his jaw worked once, twice. "I refused. You know the rest. My name struck from the rolls, my gift bound under sanction, twenty years of smuggler roads because every honest road belonged to a king I wouldn't serve." A pause. "Corvane signed the order of my exile personally. I saw the signature. Beautiful penmanship."

Tamsin stood in the cold keeper's room with a drained warden at her feet and a dead fire in the next room bearing an inverted tower, and she felt the two halves of the night click together with a sound only she could hear.

"Edmund." Her mouth was dry. "The sigil. The fingerprint in the ash. The way this warden was *emptied* — you recognize the handiwork. Say it."

He was silent long enough that the silence itself became an answer.

"Corvane didn't stay the king's broker," Edmund said finally. "Exile taught me that much by rumor, if nothing else. Somewhere in the years after, he stopped serving crowns and started serving something older. Something that buys." He looked down at the reaching, frozen fingers, and when he spoke again his voice had gone very quiet indeed. "This is his work. The economy of it. The *neatness*. He never wasted anything — not heat, not light, not souls. Whatever passed through here, he took all of it and left the container standing, because containers make better warnings than corpses." He straightened. "And he signed it. He always signs. Vanity dressed up as professionalism. The fingerprint in the ash — that's not a Spire sigil, that's *his* addition. His little flourish. Twenty years, and the man still wants credit."

The horror arrived in Tamsin slowly, the way floodwater arrives in a valley — filling the low places first, the practical places, before it rises to drown anything higher. *The broker who trained him. The broker who exiled him. The broker whose ledgers hold a page with Wren's name on it, bought for nine sacks of barley and a bolt of cloth in the worst winter of the century.* The road she had hired Edmund to walk had just narrowed to a corridor, and at the end of the corridor stood a man who had already touched both their lives, who had *made* Edmund in one room and *unmade* her family in another, and who was, by every sign in this frozen inn, somewhere ahead of them on the same dark mountain.

"He knows the roads," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was its own kind of frightening. "Your roads. Your ways. The eleven waystations, the passes, the smuggler law. If he trained you—"

"He knows everything I know." Edmund picked his way back through the doorway, past the frost-lace, careful as a man crossing a shrine. "Which means every route I might choose, he's already priced. Every shelter I might trust, he may already own." He stopped in the main room, beside the dead hearth with its inverted tower, and stared into the packed ash as though he could read the future in it. "Twenty years I kept neutral roads. No banners, no debts, no masters. And now the one master I ever refused is standing between me and the thing you paid me to reach."

Outside, the wind changed. Tamsin felt it before she heard it — a shift in the pressure against the shutters, a new note in the mountain's voice, and beneath the ordinary sound of weather there was something else, faint and rhythmic, rising from the direction of the high pass. Not wind. Not water.

A bell. Deep, bronze, tolling slow, from somewhere beneath the stone.

Edmund's head came up sharply. "That's not possible," he said. "There's nothing up there but scree and the old watch-fort ruins. There's no bell on this mountain."

The bell tolled again. And again. Counting something. Or calling something home.

Tamsin looked at the inverted tower pressed into the dead fire, at the fingerprint that signed it, and understood with cold certainty that whatever was counting, it had already reached them.

"We move tonight," Edmund said, snuffing the lantern. "Now. While it's still counting."
