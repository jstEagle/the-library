## 5. Smugglers' Law

The storm came down off the Thornfell shoulders two hours before dawn, and Edmund walked into it the way other men walked into church.

He felt it first as a pressure behind his breastbone, a gathering, a low note struck on an instrument only he could hear. Twenty years of exile had not dulled that sense; if anything, the hunger for it had sharpened until every cloud was a temptation and every temptation an accusation. He kept his hands in his pockets and his gift leashed behind his teeth, and he let the rain fall where it pleased.

Beside him, Tamsin Vane pulled her hood lower and said nothing, which he had come to appreciate about her. Three days since the stars had gone out. Three days since she had told him what she'd done to her brother, laid it out flat and unflinching the way a woman lays a knife on a table — here is the blade, here is my hand, judge me if you can. He hadn't judged her. He hadn't forgiven her either. Both of those were luxuries, and luxuries were the first thing a man gave up when he took to the smuggler's roads.

"Rain's wrong," she said at last, when the light had gone grey and thin through the pines.

"It's rain."

"It's falling *up* a little. At the edges."

Edmund stopped. He looked. She was right — fine threads of spray lifting off the stones, drifting sideways against the wind, as though the water itself had grown uncertain which master to serve. His jaw tightened. Since the wards had failed, the whole mountain had been behaving like a body with something wrong beneath its skin. Rivers running sluggish. Birds gone quiet in a way that wasn't winter-quiet but *listening*-quiet. And always, overhead, that blank black ceiling where the stars should have been, pressing down like a lid.

"We're close," he said. "Hollowmere."

"You said no one would look for us there."

"I said no one would *find* us there. Different thing." He started walking again, boots finding the smuggler's path by feel — a trail that existed only for those who already knew it, marked by blazes cut into bark at knee height and turned outward so they read as nothing to any lawful eye. "Hollowmere isn't a place so much as an agreement. A hundred years of agreements. The king's men don't raid it because the king's men buy from it. The wardens of the Spire leave it alone because—" he hesitated, then finished honestly, "—because nobody's ever been fool enough to test whether they'd leave it alone."

"And you're about to be that fool."

"I'm about to *ask*. There's a difference." Though even as he said it, he heard how thin the difference sounded. Asking Old Sable for passage into Spire territory was like asking the sea for a loan. She would hear him out, because she owed him — or because he owed her, which amounted to the same arithmetic in Hollowmere — and then she would say no, and she would be right to say it, and he would hate her a little for being right.

But there was nowhere else. That was the truth he carried up the mountain with him: three days of hard travel had bought them distance from the dead warden and the defaced waystation and the sigil burned into the doorpost like a brand, and had bought them nothing else. The drowned stair, the broker's mark, Wren Vane's soul sitting in some ledger beneath the stone like coin in a miser's purse — none of it could be reached from anywhere but inside. And inside was death, unless someone who knew the dark agreed to sell them a lamp.

Tamsin stumbled on scree. He caught her elbow without thinking, steadied her, let go. Her arm was thinner than it had been a week ago. Fear did that; he'd seen it eat people before, quietly, from the inside out.

"When we get there," she said, "what do I call you?"

"What?"

"To these people. You'll have a name among them. Everyone does." She wiped rain from her face. "You told me your rules. No real names on the road. So what do I call you?"

Edmund considered lying, found he didn't have the energy for it. "Coldharbour," he said. "Just Coldharbour. It's not my name — my name went into the king's fire twenty years ago along with everything else — but it's the one this mountain knows. It'll open doors. Whether it opens *this* door..." He shrugged. "We'll see."

They walked on. Behind them, far below, the valley lights burned small and warm and unreachable, and above them the starless dark pressed closer with every mile, patient as debt.

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and tallow and wet stone, and under it all the green rot-sweetness of the mere itself, the black pool that gave the haven its name and swallowed whatever was thrown into it and never gave anything back. The entrance was a cleft in the mountainside disguised by a curtain of frozen waterfall, and Edmund knew the knock — three, pause, two, pause, one — rapped on the ice-slick rock with the heel of his fist.

The curtain parted. Lamplight spilled out, gold and greasy. A boy of perhaps twelve peered up at him, crossbow held with the practiced boredom of someone raised to it.

"Coldharbour," the boy said. Not a question. Word traveled fast in places where words were currency.

"Merrin. Still ugly, I see."

"Still breathing, though." The boy stepped aside. "She's expecting you. Has been since the stars went out. Says trouble walks downhill same as snow."

The haven opened around them like the inside of a throat: caverns hollowed over generations, galleries cut into living rock, lamplit walkways strung between stalls and sleeping lofts and cookfires whose smoke rose to vanish through fissures no one had ever mapped. Perhaps two hundred souls lived here — smugglers, fence-menders, coin-clippers, widows of hanged men, children learning lockwork with wooden picks. Every one of them looked up when Edmund passed, and every one of them looked away just as quickly, because looking too long at trouble was bad luck, and everyone in Hollowmere paid their superstitions promptly.

Tamsin pressed close at his shoulder. "It's a town," she breathed. "A whole town inside the mountain."

"It's a *market* inside the mountain. The town part is incidental." He steered her past a stall where a woman sold bottled breath — air from high places, sealed with wax and prayer, worth more than wine to miners and worse fools. "Keep your hood up. Keep your hands visible. If anyone offers you a drink, take it, smell it, set it down half-finished. Refusing outright is an insult; finishing it is a mistake."

"That's a rule?"

"Everything here is a rule. They're just never written down, because writing things down is how kings find them."

At the back of the great cavern, where the lamplight thinned and the mere began its black gleam, stood the house of Old Sable. It wasn't a house, really — it was a ship's stern built into the rock, salvaged timber and tarred rope and a figurehead worn faceless by decades of smoke, and it sat at the water's edge like a vessel forever beached, forever waiting on a tide that would never come. Lanterns hung from its rigging. Beneath the largest one, in a chair made from a captain's seat, sat the oldest woman Edmund had ever known.

Old Sable had been old when he was young. No one knew her age; no one asked; the one man who'd guessed aloud had spent a season hauling bilgewater for the impertinence. She was small and folded and brown as old rope, with eyes the pale grey of weathered lead, and she was shelling peas into a bowl as though the end of the world were a rumor she'd declined to confirm.

"Coldharbour." Her voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Three days late. I had money on two."

"The roads are dark, Sable."

"The roads are *always* dark. You're just noticing now because the pretty lights quit." She flicked a pea at the bowl. "Sit. Both of you. Merrin — tea, and none of the stuff we sell."

Merrin vanished. Edmund took the stool across from her, and Tamsin perched beside him, and for a long moment the only sounds were peas clicking against clay and the mere lapping at its stony shore with a sound like slow swallowing.

"You've brought me a client," Sable said at last, without looking up. "In midwinter. With the sky broken. And you've brought her to *me*, which means you want something I sell, and there's precious little left on my shelves you'd want." Now she looked up, and her lead-grey eyes went through Tamsin like a fish-spear through shallow water. "Girl. What's your cargo?"

"My brother," Tamsin said. "His soul. The Spire holds it. I mean to take it back."

The peas stopped clicking.

Around them, Edmund felt the haven listening. Not obviously — Hollowmere never did anything obviously — but conversations dimmed along the walkways, and lanterns seemed to lean in, and somewhere a child was firmly hushed. Sable set down her bowl with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a thing that might go off.

"Say that again," she said, "slower, so I can enjoy deciding exactly how much I don't want to hear it."

"Wren Vane. Sold seven years ago, famine winter, to a broker wearing a crow mask. Debt comes due at the turning of the year. Three weeks." Tamsin's voice didn't shake. Edmund gave her credit for that. "I'm going down after him."

"No," said Sable.

"Sable—" Edmund began.

"*No.*" She said it the way she said everything — without volume, without heat, with the absolute finality of a headstone. "Coldharbour, you've run my routes for twenty years. You've carried my goods through passes the king's own scouts can't read. When the Harrow boys tried to cheat me, you brought me their ledgers and their fingers, in that order, and asked nothing for it. I owe you. I know I owe you. I keep accounts better than any bank on either side of this mountain." She leaned forward, and the lantern-light caught the deep seams of her face. "And I am telling you, with all that debt between us, that there is no price, no favor, no blood I will spend to send anyone under the eaves into Spire ground. Not you. Not her. Not my worst enemy, and I have a list."

"People have crossed before."

"People have *entered* before. Crossing implies coming out the other side." She sat back. "Forty years I've run this haven. In forty years, eleven fools have gone down toward the Spire owing debts or chasing them. Eleven. I can name them: Aldous Crane, the Fen sisters, Tam Roke, the priest whose name nobody caught, four miners from Deephollow, and a tax collector who thought he was cleverer than a god. You know how many came back?" She held up one hand, fingers curled, and showed him a fist. Empty. "None. Not bones. Not boots. Not a whisper on the wind. The mountain eats them and the mountain keeps them, and the Spire pays me — pays *me*, Coldharbour, in silver I don't ask questions about — to make sure the supply of fools stays thin."

Edmund's stomach dropped. "They pay you."

"They pay everyone. That's rather the point of them." Sable's voice stayed level, but something moved behind her eyes, something old and tired and ashamed, quickly buried. "You think a haven survives forty years on charm? The Spire wants the roads quiet, the fools few, the talk buried. I keep the roads quiet. In exchange, my people eat through winters that would otherwise eat us. You want to stand in my house and call that sin, go ahead. But you'll do it hungry, outside, in the snow."

"I'm not calling anything sin." Edmund kept his voice low and even, the way he kept storms. "I'm asking for information. One route. One name. The drowned stair — you've heard of it, everyone's heard of it, and half of what everyone's heard is yours."

"The drowned stair is a story we tell to keep smarter fools from finding the *real* ways down, which are worse." She picked up her bowl again, deliberately, and shelled another pea with vicious calm. "And even if it weren't — even if I drew you the map myself in gold ink — you'd be walking into a court that has spent centuries learning exactly one lesson: how to collect. You don't steal from collectors, girl. You don't bargain with them. You stay out of reach, and you count yourself rich every morning you wake up still owning your own name."

Tamsin leaned forward. "Then I'm asking you to help me stop counting myself rich."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Something passed over the old woman's face — recognition, maybe, or memory, the ghost of a famine winter of her own, decades deep. Then it was gone, shuttered away behind the lead-grey eyes, and she returned to her peas.

"Merrin!" she called. "Show our guests to the blue loft. Feed them. Let them sleep on it, since sleep is the only thing under this roof I'm selling tonight."

* * *

The blue loft was a shelf of rock hung with dyed sailcloth, warmed by a brazier and smelling of cedar and old rope. Tamsin ate what Merrin brought and said little, and Edmund sat by the brazier turning his own thoughts over like stones, looking for the cool side of each one.

He owed Sable. That was the stone that wouldn't cool. Not the other way round, whatever she claimed — the ledgers-and-fingers business had been payment, not gift, and both of them knew it. No: the true debt ran older and stranger. Nineteen years ago, a nameless exile had staggered into Hollowmere half-dead with lightning still singing in his scars, and Old Sable had taken him in, fed him, taught him the roads, and never once asked what he'd refused to do for the king. Never asked. In a world where everyone wanted to know what a storm-caller wouldn't sell, she had simply handed him work and silence, and silence, Edmund had learned, was the most expensive commodity on the mountain.

Which meant that when she said *no*, he had no honest grounds to push. Debts didn't entitle you. Debts *obliged* you. And the obligation here was to accept her refusal with grace and walk Tamsin Vane back down to the valley and let her brother's soul sit in the dark where seven years of everyone's sensible choices had put it.

He looked across the loft at her. She was awake, staring at the sailcloth ceiling, and she caught him looking.

"She's right," Tamsin said quietly. "Isn't she. Statistically."

"Statistics are what cowards use to do their grieving in advance."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give for free." He fed a twist of pine to the brazier. Sparks climbed. Somewhere beyond the sailcloth, the mere swallowed and swallowed. "Ask me tomorrow."

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because tomorrow I'm going to try her again, and I'd rather argue with her rested." He lay back. "Sleep, Vane. Whatever else is true, you're safer tonight than you've been in seven years. Nobody collects in Hollowmere. Even the Spire honors that much."

Even as he said it, he wondered if it was still true — if the wardens' silence outside the falls was respect, or patience, or a hand cupped around a candle flame, waiting for the wind to die before it closed.

* * *

He woke to voices, low and urgent, and to the particular quality of silence that meant the haven was holding its breath.

Blue pre-dawn light seeped through the fissures. Below, at the water's edge, Sable stood on her salvaged deck, and facing her — standing quite calmly on the black glass of the mere itself, as though the water were flagstone — was a figure in a grey robe with no face at all.

A warden. Inside the falls. Inside the lawless heart of Hollowmere, where no collector had ever dared set foot.

Edmund was moving before he was fully awake, dropping from the loft, putting himself between the deck and Tamsin's doorway. Around him the haven's people lined the walkways, silent, weapons held but lowered, watching their matriarch negotiate with the impossible.

The warden spoke without sound, and yet the words arrived anyway, cold and clear as frost forming: *THE DEBT OF THE GIRL VANE IS KNOWN. THE SPIRE THANKS HOLLOWMERE FOR ITS YEARS OF QUIET. THE SPIRE WILL CONTINUE TO PAY.*

Sable's chin came up. For a moment — just a moment — Edmund saw her weigh it, saw the arithmetic move behind her eyes: silver against souls, winters fed against winters stolen, the survival of two hundred people balanced on a scale with a boy named Wren on the other pan.

Then Old Sable spat, deliberately, into the black water at the warden's feet.

"Tell your masters," she said, gravel and honey and iron, "that Hollowmere's prices have changed."

The faceless figure regarded her for a long, soundless moment. Then it turned — and its regard swept the walkways, the lofts, the shadows, and stopped, briefly and precisely, on Edmund Coldharbour.

*THE STORM-CALLER,* it said, in the voice of frost on a grave. *THE MASTER WILL BE PLEASED YOU ARE COMING HOME.*

And it sank into the mere without a ripple, leaving the water smooth as a closed eye, and every soul in Hollowmere looking at Edmund as though they had just learned something about him that he himself had not yet confessed.

Old Sable turned slowly on her ruined deck. She looked at him across the black water, and her face was unreadable as the mere itself.

"Well," she said. "*Now* we talk about the drowned stair."
