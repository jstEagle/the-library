## 6. What Sable Knows

The haven of Hollowmere smelled of tallow, wet stone, and old smoke that had never quite left the walls. Tamsin felt it settle on her skin like a second coat as they descended the last rope-ladder into the eaves-hollow beneath the mountain's shoulder — a cavern the size of a chapel, ribbed with smuggled timber, hung with lanterns that burned a greenish oil she didn't want to ask about. Crates were stacked into walls, walls into rooms, rooms into a whole crooked village of contraband and quiet people.

She had imagined a den of cutthroats. What she found was closer to a market at dusk: women mending nets that had never seen water, a boy grinding something bitter in a mortar, an old man playing a three-stringed fiddle with two fingers and infinite patience. Everyone glanced up when Edmund entered. Everyone looked away just as quickly, as if his name were a lamp too bright to stare at.

"Stay close," he murmured. "And let me do the talking."

"You've said that twice."

"And you've ignored me once already. I'm hoping for improvement."

* * *

Old Sable received them in a room built entirely of ledgers — not paper, but objects. A wall of keys, each tagged with string and a scrap of writing. A shelf of boots, worn to the shape of their dead owners' feet. A jar of teeth. Tamsin tried not to look at the jar.

Sable herself was small and dense as a knot in oak, wrapped in a shawl that might have been black or might simply have absorbed every color offered to it over eighty years. Her eyes were pale as skimmed milk and missed nothing, which Tamsin learned immediately, because they went to her first and stayed there while Edmund made his case.

"—passage east, under the wards, no questions asked. I'll carry it myself. You know my word is—"

"Your word." Sable's voice was dry as the rind of old cheese. "Your word bought you credit here for twenty years, Coldharbour, and I've never once had to call it in. That's why this hurts."

"It doesn't have to hurt. It's one crossing."

"One crossing *into* Spire territory." Sable rose, unhurried, and began walking the wall of keys, trailing one finger along them like a blind woman reading braille. "You know what happens to folk who go under Thornfell's root. The wardens don't kill. Killing leaves a body, and bodies make questions. They *keep* you. There are men down there who walked in forty years ago, still walking, still smiling, hollow as gourds. My mother sold thread to a woman whose husband went after their daughter. She came back. Her shadow didn't. It walks behind her to this day, half a step late, and everyone pretends not to see."

Tamsin's stomach turned over. She thought of Wren — wherever Wren was now, if he was anywhere at all — and made her hands stay still in her lap.

"I'm sorry, Edmund," Sable said. "Truly. You're the closest thing to a son the roads ever gave me. But no one crosses. Not for gold, not for blood, not for love. Especially not for love. Love makes folk stupid, and stupid folk get my people kept."

* * *

They were given a corner of the fiddle-room and a pot of barley broth, which was hospitality, and a blanket each, which was charity, and nothing else, which was the answer. Tamsin sat with the bowl cooling in her hands and watched Edmund across the fire. He hadn't touched his.

"You knew she'd say no," she said.

"I hoped she'd say yes. Knew she'd say no. Hoping's cheaper."

"Why come at all, then?"

He looked into the flames. "Because if anyone knows another way in, it's Sable. And because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because twenty years ago I stood where you're standing, asking her for something impossible, and she said no then too. And I walked away, and I told myself it was wisdom." His jaw tightened. "I've been telling myself that for twenty years. It gets easier. It never gets true."

Tamsin set down her bowl. Something in her chest had gone very quiet and very clear, the way it had gone seven years ago in the famine winter, kneeling in the snow outside the broker's tent with Wren's small hand in hers — the terrible clarity that comes when all other doors have closed and only one remains, and it is a door you swore you'd never open.

"What does she value?" Tamsin asked. "Above gold."

Edmund frowned. "Sable? Nothing. That's rather the point of Sable."

"Not her. The Spire. You said the wardens trade in silence, that the gate reads souls like ledgers. Souls are coin down there. So what's the rarest coin?"

He stared at her. Understanding moved through his face slowly, like ice shifting on a lake, and what came after the understanding was fear — real fear, for her, the first she'd seen him show.

"No," he said.

"She gave me a mark. When I sold Wren. A sigil burned into my wrist that lets me pass their gates. But that's just the receipt. The debt itself—" She pulled back her sleeve. In the firelight the mark looked like a scar shaped like a keyhole. "It's written against my name. My whole name. The one my mother whispered to me at night, the one that's mine all the way down. That's what holds Wren's page in their book. And a name freely given—" 

"—is worth more than a soul," Edmund finished hoarsely. "A soul they can take. A name has to be *surrendered*. The Spire can't buy what isn't offered. That's the one law even they keep." He was on his feet now. "You give Sable your name, and Sable gives it to whom she pleases, or keeps it, or sells it back to you someday at a price you can't imagine. You'd be a woman without a name walking into the dark that eats names."

"I've been a woman without a brother for seven years," Tamsin said. "I know the price of that one. I don't know the price of the other. But I know which debt is older."

* * *

Sable listened without moving. That was the frightening thing about her — she could go so still that the fire seemed to lean toward her for warmth.

When Tamsin finished speaking, the silence lasted long enough that the fiddle-player in the next room faltered and stopped.

"A name freely given," Sable said at last. She said it the way a priest says scripture, or a merchant says a sum. "Do you understand what you're offering, girl? Not the word. The *thing*. Names aren't sounds. They're the thread your mother tied to your wrist before you could walk, the thing that answers when the dark calls roll. Give it away and you'll still answer to Tamsin, the way a dog answers to any whistle. But the deep part — the part the Spire reads, the part God wrote in the ledger before either of us was born — that part would belong to whoever holds it."

"I understand."

"Do you?" Sable leaned forward, and her milk-pale eyes were suddenly young and ancient at once. "I held a name once. A man's. He gave it to me to buy passage for his family out of the famine country, same winter you're always hearing about, same hunger. I kept it safe thirty years. Never spent it, never spoke it, never even thought it whole. And when he died — good death, warm bed, grandchildren — I carried his name to his grave and spoke it over the ground so it wouldn't be orphaned." Her hand closed around something at her throat; Tamsin realized she wore a locket, and had assumed it held a portrait. "That's what a name costs the one who *keeps* it, child. Are you prepared to put that weight on me? Because if you hand it over, I will keep it. That's my law. I don't spend what's trusted to me."

For a moment Tamsin wavered — not from fear, but from the strange tenderness of it. Someone offering to carry her name the way you'd carry a wounded bird.

Then she thought of Wren at six years old, trading his coat for her thinner one because she coughed. Wren at eight, naming the crows. Wren's soul, filed somewhere beneath the mountain, coming due at midwinter like a bill for a meal he'd never eaten.

"Yes," she said. "I'm prepared."

* * *

Sable worked by candlelight at a table of black wood, and what she did was not writing. She took Tamsin's hands, turned them palm-up, and asked her to say her name — the whole of it, the secret heart of it, the syllables her mother had used only twice: once at her birth and once, weeping, the night she sold her brother.

Tamsin said it aloud.

The candles guttered. Somewhere in the haven a dog began to howl and then stopped, confused, as if it had forgotten what it was howling at. Tamsin felt the name leave her the way blood leaves you when you stand up too fast — a lightness that was also a wound. The world did not change. That was the horror of it. The fire still burned, the fiddle still scraped, Edmund still watched her with an expression she couldn't bear to look at directly. Everything was exactly the same, except that some thread in her had been untied at one end, and she could feel it trailing loose inside her, going nowhere.

Sable caught the name — Tamsin saw it happen, though she couldn't say what she saw: a shimmer, a weight passing between cupped hands — and sealed it into the locket at her throat. Then the old woman exhaled, long and shaking, like someone setting down a load heavier than she'd expected.

"Well," she said. "Now I'm poorer and richer both. Sit down, girl, before you fall down. And you—" she jabbed a finger at Edmund, "—stop looking at her like that. She did arithmetic. You should try it sometime. You've been avoiding sums for twenty years."

* * *

She unrolled the map herself: a sheet of cured hide, soft as cloth, inked with routes in three colors of ink and one color that wasn't ink at all but something that faintly glowed.

"The drowned stair," Sable said, tapping the glowing line. "Older than the Spire, older than the wards. The first miners cut it looking for silver and found the dark instead. It floods in spring, freezes in deep winter — right now, with midwinter three weeks off, it's low. Ice-slick, black water to the knee in places, and cold enough to stop a heart if you sit down. But it's unguarded, because the Spire doesn't believe anyone's fool enough to use it. Their one blind spot is their contempt."

"How far down?" Edmund asked.

"Three days' descent. There's a waystation at the second landing — dry, if the seals hold. After that you're below the waterline and past caring." She rolled the map and pushed it into Edmund's chest hard enough to rock him. "You take her down, you bring her back up, and if you can't bring her back, you bring back word of how she died, because I'll have her name in my keeping and I'll not have it wondering."

"Sable—"

"Don't thank me." She turned away, and her voice, when it came again, was smaller. "I said no to you once, Edmund Coldharbour, and you called it wisdom, and we both let that lie stand because it was comfortable. I'm too old for comfortable lies. Go. Before I remember I'm a sensible woman and lock the door."

* * *

They climbed out of Hollowmere before dawn, into air so cold it tasted of iron. Tamsin walked a step behind Edmund, and the loose thread inside her swung with every stride, and she found she was counting — not steps, but costs. Seven years ago: a brother's soul for a barrel of flour and a sack of meal. Tonight: a name for a map and a road. Always the same arithmetic, the same terrible ledger, survival priced in pieces of yourself until nothing remained but the debt.

Except—

Except that this time, at the end of the road, there was something to buy back.

"Edmund," she said, as the first grey light found the snow. "If we get Wren out. If we actually do it. What then? The Spire won't just let a debt walk away."

He didn't break stride. "No," he said. "It won't."

"So we're not really buying passage, are we. We're picking a fight with the dark itself."

He was quiet for three steps. Four. Five.

"That," said Edmund Coldharbour, "is roughly the size of it."

Ahead of them the drowned stair waited beneath the ice, and somewhere below, in the court beneath the mountain, a ledger turned its pages in the dark — and on one of them, a name that no longer belonged to Tamsin Vane was about to become very, very interesting.
