## 7. The Drowned Stair

The drowned stair began where Hollowmere's lanterns ended, a black mouth in the rock that breathed cold air against Edmund's face like something sleeping.

"Charming," Tamsin said behind him. Her voice was steady, but he had learned to hear the seams in her steadiness — the way it was stitched over fear rather than woven through it. "You said tunnels. You didn't say *underwater* tunnels."

"The water's ankle-deep at worst," Edmund said, and did not add: *at first.* He struck his palm flat against the stone beside the entrance and let the old thing wake in him, slow as a banked fire remembering itself. Storm-light came up through his skin, pale blue-white, guttering like a candle in wind even though there was no wind down here. It had been guttering for three days now. He told himself it was the damp. He told himself a great many things about the damp.

The light spilled into the passage and showed them what waited: steps cut into living rock, descending into water so still it looked poured rather than gathered, a black mirror running away below them into the dark. The stone around the doorway was carved — old work, older than the smugglers who had repurposed it — spirals worn nearly smooth, and here and there the unmistakable sigil of the Spire, its inverted tower, chiseled deep and then defaced by later hands, scratched at, spat upon by history.

"Your people used this once," Tamsin said.

"My people used everything once." Edmund stepped down onto the first stair. The water took his boot to the ankle and it was colder than any water had a right to be, cold that climbed. "Smuggler law says every road is somebody's road before it's yours. You just have to be willing to pay the toll."

"What's the toll here?"

He didn't answer. The toll, if Sable's map could be believed, was paid further down, and it wasn't coin.

* * *

They walked. There was little else to do but walk, and listen, and count.

Edmund counted the things he knew how to count: the stairs (Sable's map promised four hundred and eleven before the landing), the times Tamsin stumbled (seven in the first hour; she stopped stumbling after that, learning the rhythm of the dark the way one learns a dance), the flickers in his own light. That last count he kept private. Eleven flickers in the first hour. Nineteen in the second. Each time the light died and surged back, something in his chest felt scraped, like a hearth raked too clean.

Twenty years since he'd called a storm. Twenty years of small magics only — light, warmth, a whisper of wind to fill a sail or turn a blade aside. He'd rationed himself like a miser because some part of him had always known the account was finite, that the gift was not a river but a cistern, and cisterns run dry. Now, with the stars gone out above Thornfell and the Spire drinking the sky, he was spending his cistern drop by drop to light a drowned stair, and he could not decide whether that was waste or the first honest use he'd ever made of it.

"You're dimming," Tamsin said. Not accusing. Observing, the way she observed everything — wards, ledgers, the exact weight of a debt.

"I'm conserving."

"You're dimming," she repeated, gentler. "I can see it."

Ahead of them the tunnel bent, and where it bent the water deepened — knee-high now, then thigh-high on Tamsin, who was shorter, and Edmund heard her breath go tight and controlled as the cold closed around her. He lifted the light higher. It cost him. Everything cost him now.

"Hold onto my pack strap," he said. "If the current pulls, I'm heavier than you. I'll hold."

"There's no current."

"There will be. Water down here isn't water. It's been sitting under the Spire's shadow for centuries. It forgets how to behave."

She took the strap anyway. Her fingers were freezing through the canvas, and he found himself walking slightly angled, putting his body between her and the deeper channel without deciding to. Old habit. He'd guided enough frightened clients through enough bad places to know that half the work was simply being the larger animal between them and the dark.

But she wasn't only frightened. That was the trouble with Tamsin Vane. Underneath the fear ran something harder, a wire of purpose pulled taut, and Edmund had spent twenty years avoiding people with wires like that because they always, always led somewhere he'd sworn not to follow.

"Wren," he said, because the silence had gone on too long and silence down here bred things. "How old was he? When you sold him."

The water lapped. Somewhere far off, a drip echoed with the patience of geology.

"Four," she said. "Almost five. He remembers nothing. That's what they told me — the broker, the one in the market tent with the silver scales. He said the soul goes to sleep, and the child doesn't dream of it, and everyone is fed through winter, and no one suffers except on paper." Her laugh was short and terrible. "On paper. As if Wren was ever paper."

"And does he remember?"

"He remembers being cold all the time. He remembers that I stopped singing." She was quiet a moment. "That's all. That's what a stolen soul looks like from outside. A child who is cold, and a sister who stops singing, and nobody can say why."

Edmund kept walking. The stair turned again, descended again, and the light guttered — twelve, thirteen — and steadied.

* * *

At stair two hundred and six, the water went still in a way that water should not.

Edmund felt it before he saw it: a hush spreading through the flood like oil, the surface flattening until it reflected his own failing light back up at him with perfect fidelity, a second Edmund walking upside-down beneath the world, dimmer than the first. He raised a fist. Tamsin stopped. They stood hip-deep in the flooded gallery, breathing, listening.

Then the reflection moved when he didn't.

It was slight — a tilt of the head, a hand rising toward the surface from below, small fingers breaking the mirror-still water without making a single ripple. And then the rest of it came up, or seemed to come up, though the water never broke, never splashed: a child, standing on the flooded stair as if the water were a floor, translucent as breath on glass. A girl, perhaps. It was hard to tell. Hard to look at directly, the way a star is hard to look at — except there were no stars anymore, and this was the next best thing, or the next worst.

She was made of the dark the way other children are made of skin. Where his storm-light touched her it didn't cast shadow; it passed through, and she glowed faintly at the edges, silver-grey, like moonlight remembered rather than seen. Her feet ended before the stone did. Her eyes were enormous and ancient and utterly, heartbreakingly young.

Tamsin made a sound low in her throat. Edmund put out an arm — not to shield her, he realized, but because his body wanted to step between this creature and anything that might harm it, including himself.

"Don't run," he said quietly. "Don't shout. She's not a ghost."

"What is she?"

"A fragment." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "When the Spire buys a soul whole, it... uses most of it. Burns it for light, for warmth, for whatever endless night needs fuel. But souls don't divide evenly. There's always a remainder. A scrap." He looked at the child standing on the water, and twenty years of neutrality sat in his chest like swallowed stone. "They throw the scraps away. Down here. Like gristle."

The child tilted her head, mirroring — or originating — the gesture he'd seen in the reflection. She drifted closer across the flood, and where she passed the water did not ripple, and the cold did not touch her, and Edmund understood with a lurch that the cold was afraid of *her*, or she of it, or both were afraid of the same thing further down.

"Hello," Tamsin said softly. Kneeling would have meant going under; instead she bowed her head, the way you greet something holy or wounded. "Hello. We're not going to hurt you."

The child looked at Tamsin for a long moment. Then at Edmund. Then at his hands, which still held their fading light, and something moved across her face — recognition, or hunger, or grief. She spoke, and her voice was the sound the space between raindrops makes, if that space could want anything.

"*Warm,*" she said. "*You carry warm. Give me warm and I will show you the way the wardens do not watch.*"

Edmund's stomach turned over. Bargaining. A discarded scrap of a soul, thrown away like gristle, and the first thing it had learned in its long drowning was how to bargain — because that was the Spire's whole grammar, wasn't it? Everything traded. Everything priced. Even the leavings.

"What happened to you?" Tamsin asked, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and Edmund thought: *she's thinking of Wren, she's seeing Wren.*

The child considered. "There was a man," she said. "Whole. He owed a debt. They took him apart for light. Most of him burns in the high halls now. This part was too small to burn and too much to keep." She smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was a child's smile, sweet and gapless and wrong. "So they dropped me. But dropping doesn't unmake. Nothing down here unmakes. That's the secret. That's why they're hungry forever."

Edmund's light flickered — fourteen, fifteen — and in the stutter of darkness the child did not vanish, which somehow made it worse. She remained, patient as stone, waiting on the water.

"The way past the wardens," he said. "Show us, and you'll have your warm. I'll sit with you. As long as you need. My kind of warm doesn't burn."

"It burns eventually," the child said, with terrible mildness. "Everything burns eventually. That's why they buy." She extended a small translucent hand toward him. "But yours burns slowest. I can tell. You've been saving it for something you're afraid of."

Edmund did not answer that. Some things were too true to dignify.

* * *

She led them off the stair at two hundred and forty, through a crack in the wall no wider than a coffin, into passages the smugglers' maps had never marked — or had marked and then crossed out, Edmund suspected, by men who valued their lives more than their curiosity. The water shallowed, then gave way to wet stone, then to stone merely damp, and always the child drifted ahead, always the same distance, close enough to follow and too far to catch.

Her name, she said, when Tamsin asked, was nothing. "They took the name with the rest. Names are the handle. You can't burn a soul properly if it still has its handle." She shrugged, a small sad motion. "You may call me what you like. Everyone down here calls everyone what they like. It's one of the few freedoms left."

"Moth," Tamsin said immediately, and then flushed. "Because — the light. You come toward it. I'm sorry, that's—"

"Moth," the child repeated, tasting it. She smiled again, and this time the smile was only a smile. "Yes. Moth. I was a moth before, probably. Burning in the high halls. Now I am a moth down here instead. It's better. Down here the flames are farther away."

Edmund walked behind them both and said nothing, and felt something in his chest that he had boarded over twenty years ago creak against its nails.

* * *

They rested in a dry chamber where the ceiling hung with stone teeth, and Edmund paid his debt.

He sat against the wall and opened his hands, palms up, and let the storm-gift rise through him — not as light this time but as heat, the deep remembered warmth of summer thunderheads, of air charged before rain, of the whole hot breathing weight of a storm. It pooled in his palms and spilled gently outward, and Moth curled into it like a cat into a sunbeam, and made a sound that was half sigh and half sob.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's what it was. That's what we were *for*, before they—" She stopped herself. Pressed her face against the warmth. "More. Please. Just a little more."

And Edmund, who had rationed his cistern drop by drop for twenty years, who had counted every flicker, who had sworn on his stripped and nameless self that he would never again spend his gift for anyone's sake — Edmund gave more. He watched the light in his own hands dim by a degree he would not get back, and he found he did not care, and that frightened him more than the Spire did.

Beside him, Tamsin watched with her arms around her knees, and when Moth finally slept — souls sleep, apparently, even fragments, especially fragments — Tamsin spoke without looking at him.

"You're spending yourself."

"I'm paying a toll."

"That's not what this is." She turned to look at him then, and her eyes in the dying light were fierce and wet. "You could have walked away at Hollowmere. You almost did. Why are you here, Edmund? Really. Under the professional courtesy. Under the guide's fee. Why?"

He looked at Moth, small and silver and discarded, sleeping in the shape of his warmth.

"Because they threw her away," he said at last. "She was bought fair. Paid for. Part of a whole man who owed a debt, and they burned him and dropped the scraps, and no ledger records her, no court counts her, no law anywhere in Thornfell or beneath it says she matters." His jaw tightened. "I've spent twenty years telling myself the roads are neutral. That what happens at the end of them isn't my affair. And then the stars went out, and I walked down here, and I met the arithmetic the neutral roads lead to."

Tamsin was very still.

"They waste what they buy," Edmund said. "That's the proof. That's all the proof I'll ever need. A thing that wastes souls doesn't deserve to keep them."

Above them — miles above, beyond stone and water and the drowned stair's four hundred and eleven steps — the place where the stars had been stayed black. But far below them, deeper than the stair went, deeper than the maps dared, something vast shifted in its hunger, and the water in the flooded galleries behind them trembled once, all together, like a held breath.

Moth woke with a start, silver eyes wide.

"They know you're here," she whispered. "The wardens are coming up the stair. We have to move *now* — or hide — or—" She grabbed Edmund's warm hand with her cold one, urgent, terrified. "Or the gate will read you before you ever reach it."
