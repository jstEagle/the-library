## 8. Wardens of the Dark

The dark had learned to breathe.

That was Tamsin's first thought when the water ahead of them went still — not the stillness of a pool left alone, but the stillness of something listening. The drowned stair had swallowed them hours ago, or what felt like hours; time moved strangely down here, stretched thin as tallow, and the only light was the pale grey shimmer that clung to Edmund's hands like the memory of lightning. He called it storm-light, though he'd told her it was failing, guttering like a lamp with no oil, and she could see it in the way his fingers trembled when he thought she wasn't looking.

She was always looking. She had nothing else to do in the dark but look at him and count her fears.

"Stop," Edmund said.

He didn't raise his voice. He never raised his voice — twenty years of smuggler's roads had worn all the volume out of him, leaving only edges. But Tamsin stopped as if he'd shouted, her boots finding purchase on the slick stone step beneath the black water. The stair descended before them into a flooded gallery where the ceiling vanished overhead and the walls were carved — she could feel this more than see it — with rows upon rows of niches, like the columbarium in the churchyard at Harrow's Bend where they'd buried her mother.

Niches for souls, she thought, and hated herself for thinking it.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Edmund didn't answer. His head was tilted, that particular angle he got when he was listening to something below hearing — weather, he'd once said, or the absence of it. Down here there was no weather. There was no anything. Just the drip of water counting seconds like a miser counting coin, and the faint warmth of the child-thing that walked beside them.

The lost one. The discarded soul. It had no name it could remember, so Tamsin had taken to calling it Ember, because when it pressed close to her side it gave off heat like a banked fire, like a hearth in a famine winter, like everything she had sold her brother to buy. It was small. It might have been six years old once, or sixty; the Spire's discards came out wrong-shaped, worn smooth as river stones, their edges eaten away by whatever process had decided they weren't worth keeping. Its eyes were two points of dim amber in its featureless face.

Ember had gone rigid against her hip.

Tamsin felt it then too — a pressure, a change in the water's weight, as if the whole flooded stair had drawn a breath and was holding it. The drip of water stopped. Not slowed. *Stopped*, mid-fall, a bead of moisture hanging from the ceiling above them like a jewel on a string, refusing gravity.

"Edmund," she said.

"I know."

The storm-light around his hands flickered. In its stuttering glow she saw the water ahead of them — and the shapes standing in it.

They had not been there a moment before. She was certain of that, certain down to her bones, and yet there they stood: four figures, waist-deep in the black flood, arranged across the width of the stair with the patient geometry of things that had been waiting far longer than they had been walking. They were tall — taller than Edmund, taller than any man she'd known — and they wore robes of a grey so deep it seemed to drink the light, robes that did not ripple even though the water should have dragged at them.

And they had no faces.

Not masked. Not shadowed. Simply *absent* — smooth expanses of skin where features should have been, like eggs, like stones worn blind, like the surface of Ember's face but vast and terrible and utterly still. Where eyes should have been there was nothing, and yet Tamsin felt herself *seen*, felt attention settle over her like a wet wool blanket, heavy and inescapable.

"Wardens," Edmund breathed. He said the word the way other men said *plague*. "Don't run. Whatever you do, don't run. They trade in silence — movement is speech to them, and running is a confession."

"Confession of what?"

"Everything." His voice was flat, controlled, but she'd spent three weeks learning to read him, and underneath the flatness she heard something she had never heard from Edmund Coldharbour before.

Fear.

The wardens began to move. They didn't walk so much as *arrive*, each step placing them closer without seeming to cross the distance between, and the water made no sound around them, no splash, no ripple, as if it too were afraid. The hanging drop of water finally fell. Tamsin heard it strike the surface somewhere behind her, a single small note, and then silence closed over the gallery like a lid.

Ember pressed harder against her side, burning hot now, and a sound came out of the child-thing — not a whimper, nothing so articulate, just a thin vibration of distress that Tamsin felt in her ribs rather than heard.

"It's all right," she whispered, though it wasn't, though she had no right to promise anything, and she put her hand on Ember's head the way she used to rest her hand on Wren's hair when the famine winter got into his dreams and made him cry without waking. "It's all right. I have you."

The nearest warden stopped an arm's length away. This close, she could see that its robe was stitched — or grown — with lines of tiny characters, thousands of them, dense as ledger entries, flowing down the fabric in columns. She recognized none of them, and yet some deep animal part of her understood them anyway, the way a debtor understands the shape of a sum without being able to read the figures.

The warden extended one hand. Long fingers, grey skin, no nails, the palm open and upward. An offering. Or a demand.

On the palm lay a small disc of black metal, and on the disc, a mark: a spiral descending inward, tighter and tighter, toward a point that was somehow also a mouth.

Tamsin's own hand rose before she decided to move it.

"No," Edmund said sharply. "Whatever it shows you, don't—"

But her fingers were already at her collar, drawing out the cord she'd worn for seven years, the cord with the broker's token knotted at its end — a sliver of bone-white material she'd been told never to remove, never to lose, never to question. The Spire's receipt. Proof of purchase. Proof of sale.

She hadn't shown it to Edmund. She hadn't shown it to anyone. It was the one honest thing about her, and honesty was the last currency she had wanted to spend.

The warden took the token from her palm — she never saw it move, it simply *was* gone from her hand and present in its own — and held it up beside the black disc. For a long moment nothing happened. The gallery held its breath. Even Edmund had gone still, and when she glanced at him his face was unreadable in the way of locked doors.

Then the warden turned the disc toward her, and the spiral on its surface began to turn.

It turned slowly at first, and then faster, and as it turned Tamsin felt something reach into her — not physically, nothing touched her, but she felt it the way you feel a hand in your pocket, the way you feel a page turning somewhere inside you. It read her. That was the only word for it. It read her the way a clerk reads a ledger, line by line, entry by entry, and every line was true.

*Famine winter.* The taste of bark bread. *Wren crying in the night, three years old, hollow-eyed.* The broker's booth behind the tithe-house, warm as a wound, smelling of ink and cloves. *Her own voice saying yes. Her own hand signing. The weight of two silver crowns and a sack of flour, and how light they seemed, and how heavy.* Seven years of looking over her shoulder. Seven years of counting days. Three weeks left.

Every shame she owned, opened and itemized.

Tamsin made a sound she couldn't stop, small and broken, and clapped her hand over her mouth, and the warden regarded her with its beautiful blank face, and she understood with perfect clarity that it was not cruel. That was the worst of it. Cruelty would have been comprehensible. This was worse than cruelty. This was *accounting*.

The reading passed over her like cold water receding. She sagged, and Ember caught her — the little thing stronger than it looked, holding her up with arms that burned like coals through her sleeve.

Then the wardens turned to Edmund.

* * *

She watched him stand very straight, the way men stand when they've decided dignity is the last thing they'll be allowed to keep. The storm-light had shrunk to a faint halo around his knuckles, barely enough to outline him against the dark, and she realized he was deliberately dimming it — making himself smaller, plainer, less remarkable. A smuggler's instinct. Be nothing. Be overlooked.

The wardens were not going to overlook him.

The first warden drifted toward him — arrived near him — and held out the black disc. Edmund looked at it. Looked at the faceless thing offering it. And Tamsin saw him weigh his choices in the space of a heartbeat, the way he weighed everything, tolls and risks and debts, and then he set his jaw and let the disc be pressed to his chest.

The spiral turned.

What came out of him was different. She felt the difference in the water, in the air, in the pressure against her eardrums — a low rolling undertone, like thunder heard through a mountain's bones. The warden's blank face tilted. The reading went deeper. And Edmund Coldharbour, who had kept himself sealed for twenty years the way a man keeps a wound bandaged, stood in the flooded dark while strangers read the scar tissue under the bandage.

She couldn't hear what they found. But she saw it land.

The lead warden recoiled — actually recoiled, the first ungraceful motion any of them had made — and the others closed in, and the character-stitched robes began to *move*, the thousands of tiny sigils rearranging themselves, flowing like ants, converging on the front of the lead warden's robe until they formed a single enormous glyph that blazed with a light that had no source.

Edmund staggered. The storm-light around his hands flared wild, blue-white, throwing their shadows huge against the niche-carved walls, and in that light she saw his face and it was terrible — not afraid, exactly. *Known.* Known all the way down, by a thing that had no business knowing him, in a place where knowing was the same as owning.

"They're reading my exile," he said through his teeth. "The king's writ. My name struck from the rolls. Corvane's seal on the order." He laughed, one short bitter note with no humor in it anywhere. "I'm branded twice, Tamsin. Once for what I refused to do. Once for who refused me. To these things, I'm not a man. I'm a discrepancy."

"A discrepancy?"

"An entry that doesn't balance." His eyes found hers in the flare of light, and for one naked moment she saw everything he kept behind the flatness — the grief, the rage, the long loneliness of a man who had chosen exile over obedience and paid for it every day since. "The Spire hates discrepancies. Everything must balance. Every debt collected, every ledger closed." His voice dropped. "Which means we're either going to be erased, or we're going to be *filed*."

The lead warden raised both hands, and the water around them rose with it — rose smoothly, silently, climbing their bodies like glass, and Tamsin had a heartbeat of pure animal panic, drowning, drowning in the dark, before she understood that the water was not covering them. It was carrying them. Lifting them. The whole gallery tilted in her perception, or perhaps they tilted within it, and the stair, the niches, the dripping dark fell away beneath her feet as the wardens bore them upward — or downward, she could no longer tell, direction had come unstuck — through passages that had not existed a moment before.

Ember clung to her, burning. Edmund hung in the grip of the water beside her, bound at wrist and ankle by bands of it, his storm-light crushed down to embers. His eyes met hers.

"Don't fight," he mouthed. "Save it. Save everything. We'll need it below."

Below. The word dropped through her like a stone through well-water. Below was the Spire. Below was the court beneath the mountain, the hunger that had swallowed the stars, the brokers who bought souls and the vault where her brother's was written in a book she meant to burn.

Below was where Wren had been waiting for seven years.

The water carried them through a throat of stone, and the last thing Tamsin saw of the drowned stair was the niches in the wall sliding past — hundreds of them, thousands, and in many of them, faint as breath on glass, small lights still glimmered. Souls in storage. Souls on shelves. Waiting for the turning of the year, when the hunger below would open its mouth.

One of the lights flickered as she passed, almost like a wave.

Almost like a small hand, raised in greeting, or in hope.

* * *

They came at last to the gate.

There was no other word for it, though gate was too small a word — it was a wall, a cliff-face of black stone standing in a cavern so vast that the storm-light died before it found the ceiling or the floor. The wall was carved with the descending spiral, repeated at every scale, spirals within spirals within spirals, and at its center, tall as a cathedral door, was an opening that was not an opening: a darkness so complete it seemed less like an absence of light than like a presence of *hunger*, a doorway that was also a mouth.

The wardens set them down before it. The water released them gently, almost courteously, and drained away into channels cut in the floor, and Tamsin stood on solid stone for the first time in hours and discovered her legs barely remembered how.

The lead warden stepped forward and pressed its palm to the gate.

The spiral carvings lit, one ring after another, rippling outward from the touch like rings in water, and a voice came from everywhere and nowhere — not spoken, not heard, but *understood*, arriving fully formed inside her skull, dry as old paper:

**ENTRY.**

The word was not addressed to the warden. It was addressed to them. To her.

The gate was reading them. Reading them again, deeper than the wardens had, deeper than she had ever been read in her life, and Tamsin stood in the wash of that attention and felt her seven years peeled back layer by layer — the famine, the sale, the flour, the crowns, the nights she'd lain awake doing arithmetic with her brother's soul on one side of the scale and his body, living, breathing, fed, on the other — and she wanted to scream *stop*, wanted to say *you don't understand why I did it, you weren't there, the winter was so long* —

But the gate already understood. That was the horror of it. It understood perfectly, and it did not care, because understanding and mercy are different countries, and the Spire had never issued passports between them.

**DEBTOR,** the gate said, and the word settled over her like a yoke. **MARKED. BOUND. ADMITTED.**

Warmth bloomed briefly at her throat, where the token had hung, and she knew without looking that the cord was gone, absorbed, filed. She was inside the ledger now. Formally. Irrevocably. A line in a book.

Then the attention swung to Edmund, and the gate went quiet in a way that was worse than speaking. The silence stretched. The spiral rings pulsed slow and deep, like the heartbeat of something enormous deciding.

**ANOMALY,** the gate said at last, and the word came out with something she had not heard in that dry paper voice before.

Something like distaste.

**NAME STRICKEN. STORM-GIFT REVOKED BY ROYAL WRIT. WRIT COUNTERSIGNED BY THE BROKER OF THE SPIRE.** A pause, long enough for Tamsin's heart to beat three times. **THE SPIRE HOLDS YOUR OATH-BREAKING IN ITS BOOKS, EDMUND COLDHARBOUR. YOU ARE AN UNBALANCED ENTRY. YOU DO NOT ADMIT.**

Beside her, Edmund exhaled slowly. She couldn't tell if it was relief or despair.

"You're mistaken," he said aloud, to a wall, in a dead cavern, and his voice was steady as bedrock. "I broke no oath. I refused to make one. There's a difference, and your master knows it better than anyone alive."

The gate's silence curdled.

**THE BROKER,** it said, **IS NOT ALIVE.**

Before Tamsin could decide what that meant — before she could do anything but feel the words slide cold into her stomach — the great dark mouth in the wall shuddered, and widened, and the lead warden gestured with one grey hand: *forward, debtor; forward, anomaly.*

They walked into the Spire together, the marked woman and the unbalanced man, and behind them the gate began, softly, patiently, to close — and ahead of them, far down a hall lit by caged and drifting lights, something that had once taught Edmund Coldharbour to call storms was waiting to see what his student had learned.
