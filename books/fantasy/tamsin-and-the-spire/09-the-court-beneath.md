## 9. The Court Beneath

The gate did not open so much as *admit* them, the way a throat admits a swallowed thing.

Edmund felt it in his teeth first — that slow, muscular pressure of stone parting around them and closing again at their backs, sealing the stair behind with a finality that made the drowned tunnels seem, in retrospect, almost hospitable. At least down there the dark had been honest. Here, beyond the gate, the dark had been *arranged*.

He had imagined many things in twenty years of imagining what lay beneath Thornfell. He had imagined fire, because men always imagine fire when they imagine punishment. He had imagined ice, because the mountain's breath was cold and cold was easy to extrapolate into cruelty. He had even imagined nothing — a vast and lightless hollow where the sold souls drifted like sediment.

He had not imagined a court.

But that is what spread before him now, and there was no other word for it. The passage opened onto a gallery of black stone so smoothly wrought it seemed poured rather than carved, and from the gallery the eye was drawn — compelled, really; Edmund resented the compulsion even as he obeyed it — outward and *down*, along tiers of balconies that spiraled inward like the rings of a felled tree, each ring lower than the last, each ring darker, until all of them converged on a single point far below: a dais of pale stone, and upon it a throne.

And above the throne, descending.

That was the thing that took his breath and held it hostage. The Spire was not a tower rising. It was a tower *inverted* — a spire driven downward into the heart of the mountain, point-first, its broad base lost somewhere in the stone above them and its needle-tip plunging toward depths no lantern would ever plumb. It hung over the court the way a blade hangs over an anvil. Its surface was not smooth. As Edmund's eyes adjusted he saw that the whole length of it was faceted, cut into ten thousand panes, and behind every pane something burned.

Not flame. He knew flame. Flame flickered, guttered, danced to the whims of air. What burned behind those panes was steadier than any fire has a right to be. It pulsed — slowly, patiently, in a rhythm he recognized without wanting to. It was the rhythm of a heartbeat slowed to the pace of geology. A rhythm that said: *I have time. I have all the time there is.*

"Those are—" Tamsin began beside him, and could not finish.

"Caged souls," Edmund said. His voice came out flat, which was a mercy, because anything else would have betrayed him. "Every pane a soul. Every soul a lamp." He had heard smugglers' talk of the Spire's lights, the way sailors talk of sea-serpents — half boast, half prayer that they'd never see one. He had assumed exaggeration. The Spire did not need to exaggerate. It simply hung there, lit by everything it had ever taken, and let the light do its own confessing.

There were thousands of panes. There were, he understood with a lurching certainty he could not have defended, more than thousands. The tower's upper reaches vanished into gloom, and every visible facet glowed, and the glow was not uniform — some souls burned white and steady, some flickered like candles in draft, some had dimmed to embers barely distinguishable from the stone. Those dim ones, he thought, were old debts. Souls spent down over years of consumption, drawn thin to keep the courts lit and the hunger fed. A soul was not stolen here. It was *spent*. That was somehow worse.

The gallery itself was busy. That was the next horror, arriving quietly after the first: the place was *populated*. Figures moved along the tiered walks below — wardens, faceless and silent, their steps making no sound on stone that should have rung — and among them others, shapes that might once have been people, moving with the unhurried compliance of sleepwalkers. Servants, perhaps. Or debtors working off interest. Their faces, when the light caught them, had the same quality as the dimmest panes: present, but drawn thin.

None of them looked up. That frightened Edmund more than pursuit would have. In the world above, everything watched everything; a smuggler lived by reading eyes. Here, nothing needed to watch. Everything already belonged.

*Keep walking,* he told himself, and walked, and Tamsin walked beside him, her hand finding his sleeve and gripping it with a desperation she'd have denied under torture. He did not shake her off. Some rules bend in the dark.

They were not walking freely. He understood that too. The wardens who had taken them at the gate flanked them now — two ahead, two behind, their blank faces turned forward, their silence total. They did not speak. They did not need to. The corridor itself seemed to know where they were meant to go, and the tiers of the court arranged themselves around their descent like water arranging itself around a sinking stone.

Down. Always down. Past balconies where hooded figures sat at desks of black glass, writing with pens that left no ink he could see — only small bright marks that sank into the page and stayed there, glowing faintly, like sparks buried in ash. Ledgers. The word arrived in his mind uninvited and settled there with terrible comfort. *Ledgers.* The Spire kept accounts. The Spire was, beneath all its architecture of awe, nothing more than a counting-house — except that what it counted was the only currency that mattered, and it never, ever forgave a debt.

"You're staring," Tamsin whispered.

"I'm memorizing," Edmund said. "Exits. Sight lines. Which balconies are empty."

"And?"

"There are no exits." He said it without drama, the way he'd report a washed-out bridge or a closed pass. "There are sight lines. And the third balcony on the western arc is empty, which means either it's reserved for someone important or it's a trap, and in my experience those are usually the same thing."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled. Good. He'd take a strangled laugh over despair. Despair was heavy cargo and they were already carrying enough.

The tiers ended. The last stair delivered them onto the floor of the court itself, a plain of polished stone so vast that footsteps — theirs, the wardens', everyone's — fell into it and were gone, absorbed without echo. The inverted tower hung directly overhead now, close enough that Edmund could feel its warmth. That was the detail that would stay with him, he suspected, longer than any other: the warmth. The whole monstrous engine of the place ran on stolen life, and the life gave off heat like any living thing, and so the court beneath the mountain was *warm* — warm as a hearth-room, warm as a sickbed, warm as the palm of a friend pressed to your forehead. Warm as Wren's hand must have been, seven years ago, before Tamsin carried him to a broker's door.

He glanced at her. Her face was bloodless, her jaw set in the particular way of someone chewing on their own courage to keep it down. She was looking at the tower, at the panes, at the thousands of small burning lives, and Edmund knew exactly which thought was behind her eyes, because it was the same thought behind his own: *somewhere in all that light, one flame is Wren.*

Then the herald spoke — if it could be called speaking. One of the wardens stepped forward, faced them, and opened its mouth, and what came out was not sound but *absence*: a pressure against the ears, a shape of silence that nonetheless carried meaning the way a mold carries the shape of the thing cast in it. Edmund's bones translated it for him:

*The court receives the marked and the exiled.*

"The marked," Tamsin breathed. "My mark. It knows I'm—"

"It knows everything you've paid for," Edmund said quietly. "That's what a ledger does."

*Approach,* the silence said. *The Broker will hear petition.*

And the throne room — for it was a throne room, whatever else it was — unfolded its final arrangement. The tiers above went quiet. Even the sleepwalking servants paused in their rounds. The great faceted tower seemed to lean, though towers do not lean, and the light of ten thousand caged souls angled inward, converging on the dais, illuminating at last the figure seated upon the throne of pale stone.

Edmund had known, since the defaced waystation, since the warden's corpse drained of warmth with that particular economical cruelty. He had known in his head.

Knowing in his head, it turned out, was nothing like seeing.

Corvane had not aged. That was the first obscenity. Twenty-two years — twenty-two years since the night Edmund had stood in a rain-lashed courtyard and refused, and been stripped of name and standing while Corvane signed the order with the same pen he now used, presumably, for souls — and the man on the throne looked precisely as he had on the day he taught a hungry orphan boy how to taste a storm coming three days out. Tall, spare, silver at the temples in a way that suggested distinction rather than age. Hands folded with a scholar's patience. Eyes gray as the underside of a thunderhead, and just as full of waiting weather.

He wore black, of course. But it was not the black of mourning or menace. It was the black of a master craftsman's apron — practical, unostentatious, the garment of a man who worked with his hands and saw no reason to apologize for what his hands produced.

"Edmund Coldharbour." Corvane's voice filled the court without effort, the way a storm fills a valley. Not loud. Simply *everywhere*, the way it had always been. "Twenty-two years, and you walk into my court wearing exile like a coat you've grown fond of."

Every instinct Edmund owned screamed at him to say nothing. Rules of the road: never trade words with a man who owns the ground you stand on. But his mouth had never fully learned obedience, and twenty years of scar tissue split along an old seam.

"Master Corvane." He made the title an insult purely through placement, the way you'd set a knife on a table pointing the wrong way. "They told me the Spire's broker bought souls. Nobody mentioned he'd become a king."

"A king." Corvane smiled, and it was the same smile he'd used over a student's first successful calling — warm, approving, and utterly without warmth anywhere behind it. "Kings sit atop. I merely keep accounts. The difference matters, Edmund. Kings want worship. I want *balance*. Every entry honored. Every debt collected. You of all people should appreciate order."

"I appreciated it fine," Edmund said, "until your order required storms on starving villages."

Something moved behind the gray eyes — not guilt; Edmund would have bet his remaining years there was no guilt anywhere in that skull — but recognition. The flicker of a man consulting an old ledger.

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "There it is. Twenty-two years and still the village. Still the rain." He rose from the throne, unhurried, and descended the three steps of the dais, and the court watched, and the tower's thousand lights pulsed their slow geological heartbeat. "Do you know what I saw, that day in the courtyard? I saw a boy I had taught better than that. I taught you to read weather, Edmund. I should have taught you to read *necessity*. The king wanted his war. The war wanted its storm. The storm did not care whose fields it drowned. Weather never does. I thought I'd taught you that too."

"You taught me plenty," Edmund said. His hands had curled into fists without his permission, and he made himself loosen them, finger by finger, because Corvane was watching the hands the way he'd once watched them in lessons — reading tension, reading readiness, reading the man. "You taught me that craft without conscience is just a weapon that talks. Best lesson I ever got. Cost me everything, and it was still worth the price."

"Was it?" Corvane stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Edmund caught his scent — cold stone and ozone, the smell of the air before lightning, unchanged across two decades. That was the second obscenity: that the man smelled like home. Like the best years of Edmund's life, the years before the courtyard, when magic had been a language and Corvane its patient, brilliant translator. "Look around you, Edmund. Truly look. Ten thousand lamps, and not one of them wasted. Every soul in this tower feeds ten thousand others through the winter of the world. Above, your famine villages froze in the dark because the stars failed and the wards died and no storm-caller would serve. Below, we built a light that cannot fail. Tell me which of us served the suffering."

It was beautifully done. That was the revulsion's sharpest edge — not that Corvane had become a monster, but that he had become a monster with *arguments*, each one polished smooth as the court floor, each one built on a truth bent just far enough to bear false weight. Edmund had spent twenty years constructing his neutrality out of similar timber. He knew the joinery when he saw it.

Beside him, Tamsin had gone very still. He could feel her gathering herself — he'd guided her long enough now to read her weather too — and he shifted, subtly, putting half a step between her and the throne, the way you'd shelter a candle with your body.

"You've mistaken me for a petitioner," Edmund said. "We're not here to bargain."

"Everyone who enters my court is here to bargain," Corvane said. "Some simply don't yet know the price of what they want." His gaze slid past Edmund, deliberate as a hand, and settled on Tamsin, and softened into something almost kind, which was the cruelest thing Edmund had seen in the entire court beneath the mountain. "And you, Tamsin Vane. Seven years of interest on a winter's bread. You've come so far to settle your account. How brave. How *filial*." He smiled again, and behind him the tower's lights pulsed, and somewhere in all that burning, Edmund thought, a boy named Wren waited without knowing why.

"But first," Corvane said, turning back, and his voice dropped into the register he had once used for private lessons, intimate and grave, the register that had made a lonely orphan boy feel *chosen* — "first, an old student and I have matters to discuss. Matters of craft. Of legacy." He extended one hand, palm up, an invitation and a summons in a single gesture. "Walk with me, Edmund. There is a bargain I have kept warm for twenty-two years. It is time you heard its terms."

The court waited. The tower pulsed. And Edmund Coldharbour, who had crossed borders and broken embargoes and out-waited blizzards, found that the hardest thing he had ever done was to keep his feet planted on that warm stone and not — as the boy he had been would have done, hungrily, gratefully — take his teacher's hand.

* * *

They walked the western arc, alone. The wardens did not follow; Corvane had dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, and the blank-faced things had simply ceased, stepping backward into alcoves and going still as furniture. That frightened Edmund afresh. Power that could spend servants so casually was power that had never once worried about running short.

Corvane strolled as he had always strolled, hands clasped behind his back, pausing at the balustrade to regard the tiers below with a proprietor's mild satisfaction. Below them, the desk-bound figures wrote their invisible entries. Above, the tower burned.

"Do you remember the first storm I showed you?" Corvane asked. "Not called. Shown. You were eleven. You wept."

"I remember."

"You wept because you finally understood that the thing you'd felt your whole life — that pressure, that gathering — had a grammar. That you weren't mad. You were *literate* in a language with maybe a dozen speakers alive." Corvane's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was the most dangerous weapon in the court. "I gave you yourself, Edmund. Whatever you think of me, that gift stands. It will always stand."

"That's the trouble with gifts from you," Edmund said. "They come with ledgers attached."

Corvane laughed — genuinely, warmly, and Edmund hated how much of him responded to that laugh, some starved cellar-dwelling piece of the boy he'd been turning toward the sound like a plant toward a window. "Still sharp. Good. I'll need you sharp for what comes." He turned from the balustrade, and the playfulness drained out of his face, leaving the scholar, leaving the accountant, leaving the thing that had signed orders and harvested winters. "The girl. Tamsin Vane. You've guided her this far — impressive work, by the way; the drowned stair has eaten better smugglers than you. Now let me tell you what happens next, so that you can choose your part in it knowingly. That's the courtesy I extend to students. Choice, clearly framed. The consequences itemized."

"I don't make bargains with the Spire."

"You made one the moment you passed the gate," Corvane said mildly. "Everyone does. The only question is the terms." He gestured, and the light of the nearest panes seemed to gather toward him, attentive, obedient. "She wants a soul back. Souls are not returned, Edmund — they are *transferred*, or they are *forgiven*, and forgiveness requires an authority higher than mine, and there is no authority higher than mine. So. Either her brother burns another seven years, or another soul of equal weight takes his page. Or—" and here the gray eyes fixed on Edmund with an intensity that had nothing of kindness left in it, only appetite wearing scholarship's robes— "or someone changes the terms entirely. Someone with the weight to do it. Someone whose name still carries storm."

The court was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone, Edmund thought he heard — or imagined he heard — the first far-off mutter of weather that had no business existing underground.

"What are you offering?" he asked, and despised himself for asking, and asked anyway, because a guide reads the pass before he refuses it.

Corvane smiled, and behind him the tower's ten thousand lights pulsed together, one vast slow heartbeat, and for a moment Edmund could have sworn the whole inverted spire leaned closer to listen.

"Everything you lost," his teacher said, "and everything you refused. Walk with me further, Edmund. The terms require context — and the context begins with a famine, a king, and a lie you've believed for twenty-two years."
