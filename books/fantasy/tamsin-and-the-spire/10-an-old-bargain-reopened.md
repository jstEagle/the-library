## 10. An Old Bargain Reopened

They gave us rooms, and that was the first wrong thing.

I had expected cells. I had expected the dark, the cold, the weight of stone pressing in like a hand closing. What we got instead was a chamber carved high on one of the Spire's descending galleries, with a bed for each of us, a basin of water so still it looked like a held breath, and light that came from nowhere — a soft amber glow that seeped out of the walls themselves, warm as lamplight and utterly without source. It took me an hour to understand what fed that glow, and when I understood it I could not stop looking at the walls, because the light had a texture to it if you stared long enough, a faint pulse, a rhythm like sleeping breath, and I knew — the way you know a face across a crowded market — that I was looking at souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Poured into the stone like honey into comb, burning slowly, lighting the halls of the court beneath the mountain.

Wren might be in these walls. Wren might be *in the walls*, and I was lying on a soft bed above him, being kept comfortable until midwinter.

I did not sleep. I sat by the basin and watched the water and listened to Edmund breathing in the next alcove — he slept, or pretended to, his back to the room, his coat still on. Even here, even now, he wore the coat like armor. Like a man who expected at any moment to be thrown out of wherever he stood.

The knock, when it came, was gentle. That frightened me more than shouting would have.

* * *

A servant of the Spire — I could not tell if it had ever been human; its face was smooth as an egg and its hands were too many-jointed — led us down a gallery that spiraled inward like the inside of a shell. The amber light thickened as we went, and the pulse in the walls quickened, and I realized we were walking toward the heart of the thing, toward whatever beat at the center of this inverted tower. Edmund walked beside me with his hands loose at his sides, and I had learned enough of him in three weeks of mountain roads to read the tension underneath: the set of his shoulders, the way his eyes moved constantly, cataloguing exits the way other men counted coins.

"They're not going to kill us," I said quietly. "Not yet."

"No," he said. "Killing's cheap. They want something."

"And if they don't get it?"

"Then they'll settle for cheap."

The gallery opened into a hall that stole the air from my lungs.

It was round, and vast, and it descended — that was the only word for it. Where a king's hall would rise toward a vaulted ceiling, this sank, tier upon tier of black stone steps spiraling down into a depth my eyes refused to measure, and at the bottom, far below us, something glowed. Not amber. Not the caged-soul light of the walls. This was older, hungrier, a red like the last coals of a fire that has eaten everything there is to eat. The heat of it rose up the shaft and touched my face, and it did not feel like warmth. It felt like attention.

And between us and that depth, on a dais of glassy obsidian, sat a man.

He was old, but old the way a blade is old — worn to a finer edge. Silver hair swept back from a face all planes and patience, robes of deep grey that drank the light around them. He did not rise when we entered. He simply watched us descend the last steps, and his eyes — pale, almost colorless, like winter sky through ice — went first to Edmund, and stayed there, and something happened in that face. Something almost soft. Almost glad.

"Coldharbour," he said, and his voice carried up the shaft without effort, without echo, as though the Spire itself were passing it along. "Twenty years, and you walk into my hall wearing exile like a badge. Come down. Both of you. I have waited a long time to finish a conversation."

Edmund did not move. "Corvane," he said, and the name came out of him flat and hard, a stone dropped on a table.

"You remember." The broker smiled, and it transformed him — for one moment he looked like any grandfather at any hearth, warm and lined and kind, and I understood with a lurch how many thousands must have signed their names across that smile. "Good. Memory is the first thing they take from you, in service. I'm pleased yours held." His gaze slid to me then, and it was like being weighed on a merchant's scale; I felt him find my mark, the cold sigil burned into my wrist seven years ago, and read it the way Sable read a ledger. "And Tamsin Vane. The sister. You've come a very long way to make a withdrawal."

"I've come to take my brother home," I said.

"Yes," Corvane said pleasantly. "You have. Sit."

We did not sit. We stood at the top of the dais steps, and Corvane regarded us with the unhurried amusement of a man who owned the ground, the air, the light, and the dark beneath it, and after a moment he sighed like a teacher whose pupils refuse the obvious lesson.

"Then let me speak plainly, since neither of you seems built for courtesy." He folded his hands. "In nineteen days, the year turns. At the turning, every debt in my ledger comes due at once — that is the law beneath laws, older than kings, older than the mountain. Your brother's page comes due, Mistress Vane. His soul passes below, into the hunger, and becomes what you see burning in these walls. There is no appeal. There is no court above mine. But—" and here he raised one finger, gently, "—there are amendments. Debts can be restructured. Terms can be renegotiated. Contracts, properly drawn, contain clauses. And your contract, Coldharbour—" the pale eyes turned, and the warmth in them was the worst thing yet, because it was genuine, "—your contract contains a clause I wrote myself, twenty years ago, hoping someday you'd be standing where you stand."

Edmund's voice was very quiet. "My exile wasn't a contract. It was a sentence."

"Everything is a contract," Corvane said. "The king stripped your name and cast you out, yes — but the paperwork of your binding, the storm-craft written into your bones, that was never his to revoke. I drew those papers. I hold them still. Every lesson I taught you, every storm you carry in your blood — it is all still there, Coldharbour, coiled and waiting, twenty years of silence in a man who was born to thunder." He leaned forward, and the firelight from the depths caught the edge of his smile. "Walk away from her. Let the boy's debt stand. And I will tear the sentence apart. Your name restored. Your storms restored. The roads open to you again — not smuggler roads, but the high roads, the honest ones, a place in the world you were cheated of. All of it, returned. For the price of one soul already sold, one debt already owed, one boy who has been property for seven years whether you intervene or not."

The silence that followed was enormous. I could hear the walls breathing.

* * *

I should say now what I felt, because I have tried since to dress it in better clothes and it will not be dressed. What I felt was fury. Not the hot flash of anger that comes and goes like weather — this was the other kind, the kind that starts in the belly and spreads outward slow and total, the kind that makes the world go very clear and very quiet. Because I knew — I knew with the certainty of someone who has done arithmetic in the dark — that Edmund was tempted. Not by cruelty. Never by that. He was tempted because Corvane had found the exact shape of the wound and pressed his thumb into it: twenty years of exile, of neutral roads and rented silences, of a gift rotting unused in a body built for it. Any man would want it back. Any man would feel the offer like a hand extended over a chasm.

And I hated him for it, and understood him for it, and both at once, which is the cruelest arithmetic of all.

But it was what Corvane said next that turned the fury into something with edges.

"You doubt me," the broker said, watching Edmund's face the way a card-player watches a tell. "You think this is flattery, leverage, a broker's patter. So let me give you something free, Coldharbour, as a token of old affection. Ask yourself — ask *her* — why a famine strikes exactly when it strikes. Seven years ago. The worst winter in living memory. Crops black in the fields before harvest, granaries empty, wells fouled. A whole valley brought to its knees in a single season." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "Weather does not do that. Weather is stupid. Weather is a drunkard stumbling through the sky. What happened to that valley was *craft*. Sown scarcity. A harvest of desperation — because desperate people sell. They sell anything. They sell their labor, their land, their children's names. In one winter, my ledger grew fatter than in the century before it. The famine was not a misfortune, Mistress Vane. It was a *crop*. And you were the yield."

The floor tilted. Or I did. I put my hand against the obsidian and it was cold, cold as the night the stars went out, and somewhere very far away Edmund was saying my name.

"And here is the second gift," Corvane went on, softer now, savoring it, "free of charge, because I want you to understand the shape of the world you live in. Your king knew."

"That's a lie," Edmund said. His voice had changed — gone low, gone rough, and beneath it something was moving, something I had heard only once, on the night the wards broke, when he swore at the dark in a language that made the air taste of iron.

"Is it?" Corvane's smile did not waver. "Who signed the grain requisitions that never came? Who ordered the valley roads closed 'for security' the same week the crops failed? Who accepted my brokerage licenses, year after year, in exchange for quiet borders and full coffers? Aldric was not a fool, Coldharbour. He was a partner. He knew what the winter was for. He let it happen, because the Spire paid better than wheat, and because dead peasants don't rebel." He settled back on his throne, and the red glow from the depths painted half his face, and he looked, in that moment, like every portrait of every powerful man I had ever been taught to bow to. "Your precious neutrality, Coldharbour. Your careful roads, your rules, your refusal to choose. You built all of that on the belief that the world was merely broken. It isn't broken. It is *fed*. And it has been fed, deliberately, by men with your king's seal and my signature, for longer than you have been alive. Now." He gestured, gracious as a host pouring wine. "The offer stands. Name restored, storms restored, exile ended tonight. All you must do is nothing. Walk away. Let the ledger close. It is the easiest thing I will ever ask of you — and the last thing anyone will ever blame you for, because no one, Coldharbour, no one in the world above, will ever know."

* * *

Edmund was silent for a long time.

I watched him from the corner of my eye because I could not bear to watch him straight on. I watched his hands, which had gone still in a way that frightened me — Edmund's hands were never still; they were always adjusting a strap, tracing a map, doing the small busy work of a man keeping himself tethered to the world. Now they hung at his sides like things belonging to someone else. And I thought: he's counting. He's doing what he always does, weighing the road, the risk, the cost. And the scales in front of him hold everything he's lost on one side and a boy he's never met on the other, and I am not fool enough to think love tips scales between strangers.

"Twenty years," Edmund said finally, and his voice was strange — flat, distant, a man reading terms aloud. "You could have lifted it any time. Why now?"

"Because now you're here," Corvane said simply. "And because the turning approaches, and I am sentimental about completions. I made you, Coldharbour. I would like, before the end, to unmake what was done to you. Consider it an old man's vanity."

"You didn't make me." Something shifted in Edmund's stance — barely, a fraction, weight moving from heel to ball of foot, and I remembered Sable's people talking in Hollowmere about how you can always see a fighter decide. "You taught me craft. The rest you sold off piece by piece, same as everything else in this hole." He turned his head, then, and looked at me — really looked, the way he had looked at me on the pass when I asked too many questions about the wards, weary and appraising and, underneath, awake. "What do you say, Vane? It's your brother's debt. Your call carries weight here. Should I take the deal?"

And that — that was the moment I understood him, fully, finally, after three weeks of roads and one night of stars going out. Because he wasn't asking my permission. He was showing me the choice, laying it open on the table between us like a smuggler spreading contraband, so that whatever he decided, I would see it made in daylight. He was refusing to be the man who decided alone behind a closed door. After twenty years of neutrality, it was the most political act I had ever witnessed.

"He's nine years old," I said. My voice cracked and I let it. "He was nine years old when I signed him away, and he's been down here — or something like down here — ever since, and you sit there in your grey robes and call him *property*. You sowed the famine. You starved my village to fill your ledger, and my king — if my king knew, then my king can burn in whatever dark is darker than yours, because I will drag his name into the light with the rest of it." I stepped forward, past Edmund, up onto the first step of the dais, and Corvane's pale eyes followed me with something that might have been interest. "You want to know what I say? I say you're afraid."

The hall went very still. Even the walls seemed to hold their breath.

"Afraid," I said again, and the fury in me had gone calm now, calm as deep water, and I spoke the way Edmund spoke on the mountain roads, low and certain. "Nineteen days before the turning, and the great broker beneath the mountain offers a smuggler half the world to walk away from one small soul. If Wren's debt were safe, you'd have let us rot in a cell. If the ledger were beyond reach, you wouldn't need him to *do nothing* — you'd simply take. You're offering because you're not sure. Something about us frightens you, Master Corvane, and you're spending your oldest lever to snuff it out before it catches." I smiled at him, and it was not a kind smile, and I had never in my life been less sorry. "So no. He doesn't take the deal. And neither do I. And when your ledger burns — and it will burn — I hope someone reads your name off the last page."

For a heartbeat — one long heartbeat, in which the red glow from the depths seemed to lean closer, listening — Corvane's face did something I will remember until I die. The warmth drained out of it entirely, and what remained was not anger. It was appraisal. Cold, patient, and newly hungry, the look of a man who has just discovered the goods are rarer than advertised.

"Oh," he said softly, "you're worth more than I priced you."

He flicked two fingers, dismissal or benediction, and the egg-faced servant was at our elbows, guiding us back up the spiral, and behind us Corvane's voice followed, pleasant as ever, echoing down the shaft:

"Nineteen days, Mistress Vane. Do spend them wisely. The house always collects — the only question worth asking is *what*."

* * *

They locked us in — softly, courteously, the door sealing itself with a sound like a held note — and for a long while neither of us spoke. The amber light pulsed in the walls. Somewhere below, the hunger breathed.

Then Edmund crossed to the basin, wetted a cloth, and handed it to me without a word, because my hands were shaking and I hadn't noticed. I pressed it to my face and it smelled of cold stone and, faintly, impossibly, of rain.

"The famine," I said through the cloth. "Was he telling the truth?"

Edmund was quiet long enough that I lowered the cloth. He stood with his back half-turned, looking at the pulsing wall, and when he answered his voice was rough as gravel underfoot.

"The requisitions never came. I told myself it was incompetence. Kings are incompetent; it's nearly a qualification." A muscle worked in his jaw. "But the roads. I rode those roads that winter, Vane. Closed gates, turned carts, soldiers with fresh pay in a starving country. I told myself that was cruelty, ordinary cruelty, the kind the world grows like weeds." He turned to look at me at last, and his eyes in the soul-light were grey as the sky before a storm breaks. "It wasn't weeds. It was planted. I think I've known it for twenty years and called it neutrality so I wouldn't have to say the word."

"Which word?"

"*Complicit.*" He said it like spitting out a stone. Then, quieter: "Get some sleep if you can. Tomorrow we find the vault."

"Tomorrow? How—"

But he was already turning away, pulling his coat tighter, and the look on his face stopped my question in my throat. It was not the face of a man planning an escape.

It was the face of a man who had just decided, after twenty years, which side of the road he stood on.

And far below us, faint through miles of breathing stone, something in the dark began — very softly, very patiently — to hum.
