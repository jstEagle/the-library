## 12. Tamsin's Choice

The quarters the Spire gave them were meant to be a kindness, which was how Tamsin knew they were a trap.

Everything down here was. The room had walls of polished black stone that drank the light and gave nothing back, and yet she could see — dimly, sourcelessly, as if her eyes had learned to lie for her. A bed with sheets soft as moth-wing. A basin of water so still it looked like a sheet of glass laid over a well. A table bearing bread and fruit that smelled almost right, almost like the orchards above Thornfell in late summer, but with something underneath the sweetness, something like the smell of a candle just after you blow it out.

She didn't touch any of it. She sat on the floor instead, back against the wall, knees drawn up, and turned Wren's page over in her mind the way she used to turn a coin when there wasn't enough food and not enough winter left to earn more.

*Debt transferable by substitution. Willing soul of equal weight.*

Equal weight.

She had been doing arithmetic since she was nine years old. How many turnips could be stretched across how many days. How much a pair of boots cost against how much a fever cost. What a brother's soul fetched at market when the snow came early and the grain came never. She had done that last sum herself, in the broker's warm little office above the frozen mill, while Wren slept against her shoulder with his cheek sticky from the honeyed water she'd bought him with the first coins. The broker had been kind. That was the worst part, the part she'd never told anyone. He'd poured tea. He'd said *you're saving his life, you understand* — and he'd been right, and she'd signed, and Wren had woken the next morning already fading, already lighter, though it took her two more years to understand what she was watching leave him.

So she did the sum now, sitting on the cold floor of the Spire's hospitality, and it came out simple, the way the true ones always did.

Wren's page weighed what it weighed because of what she'd written on it. Her name. Her signature. Her choice. Seven years of interest compounded in the dark below the mountain, seven years of a boy growing up hollowed, waiting without knowing what he waited for. The debt was hers. It had always been hers. The ledger simply hadn't bothered to say so out loud.

And the ledger accepted substitutions.

She heard Edmund before she saw him — his boots had a particular sound on stone, deliberate, unhurried, the walk of a man who had spent twenty years making sure no road ever surprised him. The door opened without a visible hinge and he came in carrying storm-light cupped in one hand like a bird he was afraid of startling. Even guttering, even this far under the mountain where his gift thinned to almost nothing, it made her chest ache a little to look at: blue-white, restless, alive. Everything down here was dead or caged, and then there was this small wild weather in a smuggler's palm.

"You're not sleeping," he said.

"Neither are you."

"I don't sleep in places that watch me." He set the light on the table, where it hovered rather than rested, and lowered himself onto the bench opposite her with the care of an old man, though he couldn't have been more than five-and-forty. Twenty years of exile had put the age in him early. "The bread's drugged. Or worse than drugged. Food down here is grown on what the Spire takes. I wouldn't."

"I know." She'd known without knowing how she knew. Some lessons the famine taught went deeper than sense. "I wasn't going to eat it. I was going to ask you to read it again."

"The page?"

"What it says. All of it. Every clause." She made herself look at him straight. "I want to be sure I understood."

Edmund Coldharbour had a face built for giving nothing away — weathered, closed, the kind of face that had learned its blankness the hard way and kept it like a weapon. But she had walked three weeks beside him through the dark, and she had learned to read the small tellings: the way his jaw set a half-second before he decided to refuse something, the way his eyes went to the middle distance when he was weighing a thing he didn't want to weigh.

He was weighing it now.

"You understood," he said.

"A willing soul of equal weight." Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. "Mine is heavier. You know it is. I'm the one who sold him."

"That's not how weight works down here—"

"Isn't it?" She unfolded from the wall and stood, because sitting felt wrong for this, felt like begging. "You saw the vault. You read the pages. Guilt has weight, Edmund. They *measure* it. Why do you think my mark opened their gate? Not the mark itself. What's underneath it. What I've been carrying since the mill." She held out her hands, palms up, as if she could show him. As if the weight were visible. Maybe down here it was. "A soul that sold a child weighs more than a child's soul. Any honest scale would say so. Theirs especially."

The storm-light flickered between them, throwing shadows that moved wrong, that moved a half-beat behind the hands that cast them.

"No," Edmund said.

It was very quiet, and very final, and it landed on her like a door closing.

"You don't get to say no."

"It's the only word I've got that's mine." He rose too, slowly, and for the first time since she'd met him on the lit road above Thornfell, he looked tired all the way through — not the tiredness of the road but of something older, some exhaustion he'd been carrying longer than she'd been alive. "Tamsin. Listen to me. I've spent twenty years keeping one rule. One. Don't spend other people. Not their coin, not their blood, not their names. I've watched whole kingdoms run on spending other people, and I watched what it made, and I chose the empty roads instead. So no. I don't get to say no. I *have* to."

"And I get to say yes." She stepped closer. The light between them shivered. "That's what willing means. Nobody can give it for me and nobody can take it from me. That's the whole point of the clause — I read it four times. *By substitution of a willing soul.* It has to be free. Mine would be the freest soul in this entire mountain, because every other one down here was taken, and mine—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the crack in it, pushed through anyway— "mine would be *given*. Do you understand what that's worth to them? To the hunger? I'm not just equal weight. I'm better coin."

"That's exactly why I'm saying no." His voice roughened. "You're not offering yourself. You're offering them the thing they want most and calling it justice. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"It's my brother."

"I know whose brother it is."

"Do you?" The words came out of her hot and fast now, seven years of banked fire finding a draft. "Do you actually? Because you keep your rules the way other people keep relics — dusted and honored and never once *used*. You wouldn't call storms for the king, fine, noble, I've heard the shape of that story even if you haven't told me the truth of it. But your rule didn't save anyone, Edmund. It just kept your hands clean while the world went on selling children. My village starved while men like you kept neutral roads. And now you want to stand in front of me and lecture me about spending people, when the only person I am spending is the one who deserves to be spent?"

The moment the words were out she wished them back. Not because they weren't true — some of them were, maybe most of them — but because of what crossed his face when they landed. Not anger. Something worse. Recognition. Like she'd named a wound he'd stopped letting himself look at, and the naming hurt more than the wound.

The storm-light guttered low, nearly out, and in the near-darkness his voice came quiet.

"You think I don't know what neutral roads are worth?" he said. "I've had twenty years to do that arithmetic, Tamsin, and I do it every night. Every single night. You're not telling me anything I haven't already charged myself." He looked at his own hands, the hands that had once held storms. "But there's a difference between a debt you owe and a debt you invent. You owe Wren. Gods, yes — you owe him more than any soul can pay, and that's the trap, isn't it? Because the debt can't be paid, you want to pay it with the biggest coin there is. Yourself. And the Spire will take it, gladly, and Wren will walk out of here free and hollow, and he'll spend the rest of his life learning that his sister bought his freedom with her own damnation. You think that's a gift? You think that boy — the one who's been waiting seven years without knowing what for — you think he waits all that time to find out what it cost?"

"He doesn't have to know."

"He'll know." Edmund's voice didn't rise. It dropped, which was worse. "Souls know. That's the one thing I learned from Corvane that was true. Nothing down here stays hidden from what it touches. You'd carry it into the dark with you, and he'd carry it out, and neither of you would ever be free of it. You'd be trading one cage for two."

Tamsin turned away from him. The black wall gave her back nothing, no reflection, no comfort, just smooth cold stone, and she pressed her forehead against it because the cold helped, the way snow on a burn helps.

"I sold him," she said, and her voice broke properly this time, and she let it, because there was no one left to perform strength for. "I signed my name. I took the coins. I ate the bread I bought with him. Every day of my life since, I've eaten something, and every bite has been him. Do you know what that is? Do you know what it is to be fed by your own brother's soul for seven years?" Her breath fogged the stone. "You talk about clean hands. I don't have clean hands. I have hands that fed me. And if the only way to make it right is to go into that ledger myself, then that's not a trap, Edmund. That's just the bill coming due. Bills come due. That's all this is."

For a long time he said nothing. The storm-light breathed between them, dimming, brightening, like something sleeping.

Then she heard him move, and when she turned he had come around the table to stand close, closer than he ever stood to anyone, and his face in the failing light was stripped of everything — the smuggler's calm, the exile's armor, all of it gone, leaving just a man looking at a woman holding a knife to her own soul.

"When I refused the king," he said quietly, "it wasn't because of the village. Not really. The village was the reason I told myself." He paused, and she realized she was about to hear the truth he'd been carrying since the lit road, maybe longer. "Corvane taught me storm-craft. Did you guess? The broker down here, the one enthroned in his court of caged lights — he was the finest teacher I ever had, and I loved him like a father, and when the king ordered the storm, Corvane was the one who brought the order. He stood next to me while I said no. He didn't argue. He just watched. And afterward, when they stripped my name, his signature was on the writ." Edmund's hands curled slowly shut. "I told myself for twenty years that he'd changed. That power did it. That the man who taught me weather and mercy had been worn down into something else. But he hasn't changed, Tamsin. He was always this. And the order I refused — the storm on the starving village — that was the same arithmetic you're doing right now. *Some must be spent so others live.* I've heard that sum before. I've heard it in his voice."

"So you'd let Wren stay caged rather than break the pattern."

"I'd let *you* stay free," Edmund said, "and we'd find another way, because there is always another way, and the moment you believe there isn't is the moment Corvane wins — not the battle, the *argument*. That's what he sells. Not souls. Certainty. The lovely, terrible certainty that someone has to be spent." He reached out — hesitated — and then, gently, took her hands. His were scarred and cold and steady. "You sold Wren once because you believed there was no other way to survive the winter. I will not stand here and watch you sell yourself for the same reason. That's not love paying a debt. That's the famine winning twice."

Tamsin wanted to pull her hands away. She didn't. She stood in his grip and shook, silently, the way she'd shaken at nine years old in the broker's warm office, and hated that too, and let it happen.

"If not me," she whispered, "then what? The turning is close. Days. The ledger opens, and it collects, and Wren goes into whatever furnace feeds this place, and I'll have climbed all the way down into hell and carried him out nothing but a story I tell myself."

"Then we find the flaw." Edmund's eyes had gone distant, the way they did when he was reading weather that hadn't arrived yet. "Every contract has one. Every ledger. Corvane taught me that too — the one lesson he never meant to teach. A bargain is a structure, and structures bear weight, and anything that bears weight can be broken if you know where the load sits." His grip tightened, just slightly. "The turning is when all debts come due at once. You said it yourself — the whole mountain's books open at midnight. For one breath, every soul in this place is *between* — paid and unpaid, claimed and unclaimed. Nothing owns them in that breath. Not even the Spire."

"That's a smuggler's tale."

"That's a smuggler's *specialty*." And there — briefly, crookedly, gone again — something that was almost a smile. "I've moved contraband through wards older than kingdoms. I've slipped past gates that read souls. If there's a gap in the world's accounting, I can find it. But I need you whole, Tamsin. Free, and furious, and *not signed away*. Because the moment your name goes into that book, you stop being the thief and start being the cargo, and I have never once in twenty years managed to steal anything from myself."

She laughed. It came out wet and cracked and surprised her, but it was a laugh.

"You're breaking your rule," she said. "Getting involved. Caring what happens to a client."

"Don't tell anyone." He released her hands and stepped back, and the armor came up again, mostly, the dry flat tone sliding back into place. "I have a reputation."

The storm-light chose that moment to die altogether, sinking into the table like water into sand, and the darkness that rushed in was total and soft and full of listening. Somewhere far below them — floors down, depths down, in the vault where pages waited like moths in a sealed box — Tamsin felt, rather than heard, a stirring. A rustle. Ten thousand written names shifting in their sleep, the way a forest shifts when wind finds it.

And beneath the rustle, fainter, threaded through the stone under her feet, a hum. Low and rising. The caged souls, singing to each other in the dark. Singing, she thought with a chill that was not entirely fear, to the storm-heart walking among them.

Edmund had gone very still.

"Tamsin," he said quietly. "They can hear me."

* * *

They did not sleep. Sleep was a currency down here and they had none to spare.

Instead they sat on the floor of the watching room with their backs against the black stone, and Edmund talked — low, fast, the way he must have planned runs in the old days, drawing routes in spilled salt on the floor with one scarred finger. The drowned stair behind them. The gate that read souls ahead. The vault, and the turning, and the breath between debts. Tamsin listened and corrected him where the mountain disagreed with his memory, because she had spent three weeks learning to hear the Spire the way he heard weather, and she knew things now that he didn't: which corridors breathed, which walls remembered, which silences were wardens standing still.

Once, in the middle of it, she said, "If it comes to it — if there's no other way — I need you to promise you won't decide for me. Whatever happens at the turning. Promise you'll let me choose."

Edmund's finger stopped in the salt.

"No," he said.

"You just said—"

"I said I wouldn't sign you away and I wouldn't let you sign yourself. Both of those are still true. I'm allowed to be inconsistent; I'm a criminal." He resumed drawing, but slower. "What I promise is this: I will fight for every other way until there are no ways left. And if we reach the last one, we'll look at it together, awake, with both our names still ours. That's the only promise I have that's worth anything."

It wasn't the promise she wanted. She suspected it was the only one he had.

She lay back against the cold stone and closed her eyes and did not sleep, and in the humming dark below, ten thousand souls sang their thin song, and somewhere among them, older than he should be and hollowed and waiting, a boy she had sold was waiting still — and did not know, could not know, that his sister had come down into the hungering dark to unwrite her name from his life, and that the hardest part would not be getting him out.

The hardest part, she was beginning to understand, would be what Edmund had just taught her without meaning to:

Refusing to pay a debt is not the same as being free of it. Someone, somewhere, always holds the paper.

And far below, in a court of caged light, the man who held theirs had begun, very softly, to laugh.
