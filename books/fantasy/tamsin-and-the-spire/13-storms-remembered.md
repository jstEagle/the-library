## 13. Storms Remembered

The quarters the Spire gave them were a mockery of hospitality: a chamber of black stone hung with tapestries that showed no scenes, only patterns, spirals within spirals, like water going down a drain. There was a brazier that burned without heat. There was a window that looked out onto nothing — not night, not stone, but an absence so complete it made Edmund's eyes ache to dwell on it.

They had been given these rooms as a courtesy, and the courtesy was the trap. Corvane's voice still rang in Edmund's skull from the audience hall: *You will be comfortable until the turning. Comfort is a kind of patience.* As if comfort were something the Spire understood. As if anything down here understood anything but hunger and arithmetic.

Tamsin sat on the edge of the bed with her boots off — a small surrender she'd fought for three days, insisting they might need to run — and turned her brother's page over in her hands for perhaps the hundredth time. She'd copied it in the vault, quick and furtive, her handwriting cramped with fear. *Wren Vane. Seven years. One soul, whole, unspoiled. Debt due at the turning of the year.*

"He was six," she said, to the page or to Edmund or to no one. "When I sold him. He was six, and he thought we were going to see a man about a cow."

Edmund stood at the false window with his arms crossed, watching the nothing watch him back. He had been counting the hours since the ledger room. Counting, too, the thing Tamsin had said there, in the dark among all those written souls: *I sold him once already. I owe him this.*

He had refused her then, flatly, with all the warmth of a locked door. She had looked at him like he'd struck her. And neither of them had spoken of it since, because the Spire's halls had ears — he could feel them, a pressure against the skin of silence, listening for the shape of plans.

But now the pressure eased. The listening presence withdrew, or pretended to, the way a spider pretends to sleep at the center of its web. Edmund felt it go the way a sailor feels wind shift — in his teeth, in the old scar along his forearm where lightning had kissed him at fourteen and left a white seam like a river on a map.

"They're not listening," he said. "For now."

Tamsin looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady, which was, he had learned, her natural condition: grief worn like armor polished by use. "Then say what you've been chewing on since the vault. You've been chewing it so long your jaw must ache."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You want to know why I won't let you take Wren's debt."

"I know why. Because you're a coward with rules instead of a spine—"

"Because I watched a man do exactly what you're proposing," Edmund said, "and I have spent twenty years trying to forget his face."

The words came out lower than he intended, rougher, scraped raw on the way up his throat. Tamsin went very still. Even the brazier's cold flame seemed to lean toward him.

So this was how it would come out. Not in some tidy fireside confession, but here, in a cell disguised as a guest chamber, in the belly of the thing that had eaten his life whole twenty years ago and was now digesting Tamsin's brother. The Spire had a talent for symmetry. He supposed he should be grateful for the efficiency.

* * *

"You told me once," Tamsin said quietly, "on the pass, the first night, when I asked why you left the king's service. You said you'd been paid to leave."

"I was paid to leave." Edmund turned from the window. "That part was true. What I didn't tell you is who did the paying, or why, or what I was before I was a smuggler." He crossed to the brazier and held his hands over it anyway, out of habit, though it gave no warmth. Old gestures die hard. "You asked me once if I'd ever called a storm for anyone. Do you remember?"

"You said storms don't take requests."

"They don't. But storm-callers do. We choose. That's the whole of the gift, Tamsin — not power, choice. Any hedge-witch can whistle up rain when the clouds are already heavy. A caller stands under a clear sky and decides whether it will fall." His hands curled slowly into fists above the flameless fire. "I was good at it. Better than good. Corvane trained me himself — you saw how he moved in that hall, how the wardens bend around him. Everything he knows about command, he taught me first. How to read a sky. How to feel the weight of water in the air a hundred miles off. How to stand in the middle of a storm and be the calmest thing in it."

"And then?"

"And then there was a village called Harrowmere." The name tasted like ash and copper. It always had. "Autumn of my nineteenth year. The war in the east had gone badly — you'll be too young to remember it, but the king needed grain, and the grain roads ran through the valley country, and the valley folk had stopped sending their tithe because there was nothing to send. Drought. Three years of it. Their wells were mud and their children were eating boiled bark."

Tamsin's breath caught. He heard her recognize it — the shape of another famine, older than hers, wearing different clothes.

"The king's marshal rode to Harrowmere with two hundred soldiers and a writ," Edmund said. "The writ said the village was in arrears and would be made an example of. And the marshal had a storm-caller with him. Me." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked sound with nothing in it. "They didn't need me to burn anything. Fire leaves evidence. But a storm — a storm is an act of sky. No one can prove a sky was ordered. The hail would break their roofs. The flood would drown their fields. And next spring, every valley village would pay its tithe on time, because they'd have seen what the sky does to those who don't."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far below them, deeper than any cellar, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the black stone floor hummed faintly underfoot.

"There were forty-one people in Harrowmere," Edmund said. "I counted them. From the ridge, through the marshal's spyglass, while he explained my orders. Forty-one. Eleven of them children. I stood on that ridge with the whole weight of the sky leaning on my shoulder like a friend asking a favor, and I could feel every drop of it, every ounce of rain and hail and wind, waiting to know what I wanted. Twenty years I've told myself I didn't know what I would have done. That's a lie. I knew in that moment exactly what I would do. I said no."

Tamsin had risen from the bed without seeming to move. She stood three feet away, and her face had gone through fury and past it into something else, something harder and more careful.

"What happened to the village?"

"Nothing." The word broke in half coming out. "That's the poison of it. Nothing happened. The marshal cursed me, wrote to the capital, and we marched home, and Harrowmere kept its roofs and its starving children, and I was stripped of my name in the spring. Not imprisoned — exiled. My name struck from the rolls, my calling forbidden on pain of death, my family's lands given to a cousin who'd had the sense to keep his mouth shut. Corvane signed the order himself. I watched him do it. He dipped the pen and he looked at me across the table and he said—" Edmund stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "He said, *You mistake the nature of the gift, boy. It was never yours to refuse.*"

* * *

Tamsin sat back down slowly, as if her legs had decided the matter without consulting her. For a long moment she said nothing, and Edmund was grateful, because his chest felt like the inside of the brazier — full of fire that gave no light.

"Twenty years," she said finally. "Twenty years of neutral roads and paid passages and never asking questions. I thought you were careful. I thought you were greedy, maybe, or just tired." She shook her head. "It wasn't peace at all, was it. It was a scar."

"Scar tissue doesn't feel," Edmund agreed. "That's the point of it. You build it thick enough and nothing gets through — not guilt, not hope, not the memory of a girl selling her brother to a broker while he thinks he's off to see a cow." He met her eyes. "Until you walked into Hollowmere with your questions and your dead man's coin purse, and started cutting through mine like it was wet paper."

"Don't." Her voice sharpened. "Don't put that on me. I'm not your redemption, Edmund Coldharbour. I'm a woman who sold a child to save herself, walking through hell to un-sell him, and I will trade my own soul to do it if the arithmetic comes to that."

"And that's the arithmetic Corvane wants you doing." He crouched in front of her, close enough that she had to look at him or turn away, and she chose to look, because she was Tamsin, and looking away was not in her nature. "Listen to me. In the vault, when you said you owed Wren — you were right. You owe him. Debts of love are real; I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But a debt paid with your soul isn't payment. It's just a second sale. The Spire doesn't care whose name is on the page. It cares that a page exists. You'd be feeding it, not beating it. You'd be teaching it that love can always be converted to currency — which is the one lesson it must never learn, because it's the lesson that keeps it fed."

"Then what do I teach it?" she whispered.

"That some things aren't for sale." He reached out — slowly, giving her time to pull back — and took her hand. Her fingers were cold as the tunnels, cold as the drowned stair, and he wrapped both his hands around them as if he could warm them by stubbornness alone. "I said no once, Tamsin. On a ridge, with the sky begging me. One word, and it cost me everything I had, and I have regretted the cost every single day since." His grip tightened. "But I have never once regretted the word. Never. Not in the dark watches, not in the worst winters, not when I was hungry and nameless and sleeping in ditches. The cost was terrible. The refusal was free."

Her eyes filled, and she blinked it back furiously, the way she did everything — fighting even her own tears for territory. "Wren waited seven years down here," she said. "Seven years. And I keep thinking — what if I can't get him out? What if the only door is the one I walk through?"

"Then we find a third door. Or a fourth. Or we tear a hole in the wall." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked and true. "I've spent twenty years learning every road in Thornfell, and I'll tell you a secret about roads: there is always another one. The trick is being willing to look stupid looking for it."

A sound escaped her — half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled. But her hand turned in his and gripped back, and something passed between them that had no name he knew: not trust exactly, not yet, but the ground trust grows in. Thawed earth. Softened stone.

"The child," she said suddenly. "The little one on the stair. The fragment they threw away." Her jaw set. "If the Spire discards what it buys — wastes souls it already owns — then it's not a bank, Edmund. It's a stomach. And stomachs can be starved."

He stared at her. Then he laughed — really laughed, for the first time in longer than he could reckon, a short startled bark of sound that echoed strangely off the dead stone. "Gods help me, you sound like Sable."

"I'll take that as the compliment it is."

"It was meant as one." He rose, pulling her up with him, and turned toward the door — toward the corridors beyond, where the caged souls burned in their lattice-lights and the wardens drifted like smoke given purpose. "The turning is nine days off. Corvane thinks he's fattening us for it. Let him think so."

"And meanwhile?"

"Meanwhile," Edmund said, and felt the old scar on his arm prickle, faint as a ghost's touch, like weather gathering somewhere far beyond the stone, "we find that child again. Because if anyone knows the way through this mountain's belly — the ways even the wardens don't watch — it's the piece they threw away."

Beyond the doorless doorway, the corridor lights flickered — all of them at once, a ripple running down the hall like a shiver, like something vast below turning its attention toward them.

And in the silence where his storms used to live, Edmund felt — unmistakably, impossibly — the first distant pressure of gathering weather.
