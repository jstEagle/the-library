## 14. The Turning Approaches

The Spire was counting down, and it counted in light.

Tamsin noticed it first in the corridors outside the quarters Corvane had given them — a cell, really, though a gilded one, with a bed of grey velvet and walls of stone so polished they held their reflections like water. On their first night below, the lamps along the hall had burned a low, sullen amber, the color of tallow gone old. By the third day they had brightened to gold. By the fifth they were white as frost, and when Tamsin passed beneath them she could feel them on her skin like sun through winter glass — warmth without kindness, brightness without any source she could name.

"They're feeding the halls," Edmund said, when she asked. He stood at the narrow window slit that looked out onto nothing — not rock, not air, but a darkness so complete it seemed less an absence than a substance, pressing against the glass like a sea against a porthole. "Every soul they've bought and caged gets drawn a little thinner each day. What leaks out becomes this." He nodded at the lamp. "Light for the court. Warmth for the wardens. The Spire dresses its hunger in comfort."

"And at midwinter?"

"At midwinter they stop leaking." He turned from the window, and in the strange white lamplight his face looked older than it had on the mountain, carved deeper. "At midwinter they drink."

She had known, of course. She had known since the night on the high pass when the stars went out and she finally said Wren's name aloud to a stranger. Knowing and feeling it were different countries, and every day the lamps climbed another shade brighter, she crossed a little further into the second one.

*Three weeks,* she had thought at first. Then two. Now, when she counted on her fingers the way she'd counted days between meals during the famine winter — a habit she could not break, counting always, measuring always, the arithmetic of scarcity worn into her like a scar — now there were nine days left. Nine days until the turning of the year, until the debt she had sold came due, until her brother's soul was poured out into the dark beneath the mountain to keep its endless night burning one more season.

Nine days, and she still did not know how to unmake a ledger.

* * *

They were not prisoners. That was the worst of it, some days — worse than chains would have been. Corvane had given them quarters, given them leave to walk certain corridors, given them meals that arrived on silver trays tasting of everything and nothing, rich food with no memory in it, as if it had been grown in the dark and knew no other way to be. *Guests of the court,* the faceless wardens called them, in the silence-language they spoke with gestures and pressure against the skin. *Guests until the turning.*

Tamsin walked those permitted corridors the way she had once walked the famine roads: looking for anything edible, anything usable, anything that could be stolen or bartered or broken. She catalogued. It was all she knew how to do. Here a gate of black iron where the wardens passed through and the walls themselves seemed to lean away from it. Here a stair going down further than the eye could follow, breathing cold. Here — and this she memorized most carefully of all — the mouth of a corridor lined with niches, and in each niche a cage.

Not iron cages. She had expected iron. These were cages of glass, or something like glass, clear as ice and just as cold to look at, and inside each one hung a light.

She had seen souls before, or what passed for them in the Spire's economy — the warden's ledger-gate had read hers like a merchant reading coin, weighing her, finding her marked and wanting. But seeing them caged was different. Each light was small, no bigger than a candleflame, and each one moved. Not flickering — *moving*, the way a moth moves, the way a trapped bird throws itself again and again at the same impossible wall. Some of the lights pressed against the glass toward her as she passed. She felt them the way you feel eyes on your back in an empty street.

One of them hummed.

It was a low sound, barely a sound at all, more in her teeth than her ears, and it stopped her mid-stride. The light in that cage — seventh from the end, she noted, because noting was survival — pulsed as she stood there, slow and steady, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. And faintly, underneath the hum, she thought she caught something almost like a voice. Not words. A shape of words. A reaching.

"Tamsin." Edmund's hand closed on her shoulder, firm, drawing her back. His voice stayed low, pitched under the hearing of the wardens that drifted at the corridor's far end like smoke given errands. "Don't linger."

"It knows me," she whispered. "Edmund, it's humming at me."

"They all will, soon enough." But his hand on her shoulder had tightened, and when she glanced up at him she saw him looking down the row of cages with an expression she had never seen him wear on the road — not the dry mask, not the wary professionalism, but something rawer. Recognition, maybe. Or grief. "Your storm-heart," he said quietly, half to himself. "Corvane said I carried it like a brand. Maybe it carries further than I thought."

"What does that mean?"

"It means we should be in our quarters before the watch turns." He steered her gently back the way they'd come. "And it means we're running out of time to be clever."

* * *

The child found them that evening.

Tamsin had named her Ember, back in the drowned stair, because that was what she looked like — a scrap of guttering light, a coal someone had dropped and not bothered to stamp out. The Spire had made her once, or bought her once, and then discarded her, cracked and incomplete, a soul-fragment too small to burn usefully and too stubborn to simply go out. She had led them through the flooded tunnels in exchange for warmth, curling against Edmund's storm-light like a cat against a hearthstone, and Tamsin had not been able to look at her without thinking of Wren, seven years old and hollow-eyed in the famine winter, watching her count coins that weren't there.

Ember came through the wall.

There was no door in the wall, but there didn't need to be; the discarded things of the Spire moved through its cracks the way water moves through stone, unseen and uncounted. One moment the room was empty except for the two of them and the tray of tasteless food, and the next there was a small dim glow hovering by the window slit, gathering itself into the shape it liked best — a girl-shape, thin as paper, with two points of pale light where eyes would be.

"You're shining more," Ember said. Her voice was like wind through a keyhole. "Both of you. The whole Spire is shining more. It's nearly time."

"Time for what?" Edmund asked, though Tamsin saw by his face he already knew.

"The turning." Ember drifted closer, and the temperature of the room dropped around her, pleasantly cool at first and then less pleasant, breath-fog blooming from Tamsin's lips. "Midwinter midnight. When the year ends and begins in the same breath. All the debts come due at once — every contract, every bargain, every page in the ledger turns itself over. For one moment. Only one."

"For one moment what?" Tamsin leaned forward. Her heart was doing the famine-counting thing again, quick and hard, three beats and a pause, three beats and a pause.

"For one moment, the ledger is open." Ember's light-pulse quickened. "Not unlocked — open. The Broker reads it aloud. Every debt spoken, every soul summoned to account. It has to be spoken, you understand. A debt that isn't named can't be collected. And a ledger that is being read—" the little light trembled, and Tamsin realized with a jolt that the trembling was fear, ancient and worn smooth by repetition, "—can be interrupted."

"Interrupted how?" Edmund was on his feet now, moving to the window slit as if the answer might be written in the darkness outside. "Speak plainly, little one. We haven't time for riddles."

"No riddles." Ember sank slowly toward the floor, folding herself down until she sat cross-legged on the stone, a habit she must have copied from some living child long ago, some child who had been real. "A ledger is only ink and binding and the hunger that keeps it. Break the binding while it stands open, and every page is loose. Loose pages aren't debts anymore. They're just... names. Names go free."

"And can it be broken?" Tamsin asked. "The binding?"

Ember was quiet for a long moment. The lamps in the corridor beyond their door pulsed, very slightly brighter, and Tamsin thought of the caged lights throwing themselves against their glass, over and over, patient as grief.

"I was part of a binding once," Ember said at last. "Before I cracked. Bindings are made of held things. Held tight enough, long enough, they become the holding itself." She looked up, and her two points of light fixed on Edmund with an intensity that made the air feel thin. "You are a held thing, storm-caller. You've been holding your storm for twenty years. I've felt it since you came down the stair. It hums the way the caged ones hum. It wants out the way they want out."

The silence that followed had weight. Tamsin watched Edmund stand very still by the window, his profile cut sharp against the absolute black, and she saw something move behind his face that she recognized from her own mirror in the worst years — the look of a person being shown the price of a thing they want, and finding it exactly as terrible as they feared.

"That's not an answer," he said finally.

"It's the only kind I have." Ember rose, drifting, already thinning at the edges where the wall waited to take her back. "Nine nights. At the turning, the Broker opens the book and speaks the debts. If something breaks the binding in that moment — something loud enough, something wild enough, something the Spire has never held — the pages tear free. All of them. Even mine, maybe. Even mine." Her voice caught on that, a small crack in the wind-sound, and Tamsin's throat tightened.

"Ember," she said. "Wait. How do we get to the ledger vault? It's guarded. The wardens—"

"The wardens serve the ledger," Ember said. "When the ledger opens, they kneel. Everything in the Spire kneels at the turning. Everyone looks at the book." The girl-shape was nearly gone now, dissolving into the stone like sugar into tea, and her last words came faint and fast, urgent as a pushed bird from the hand. "Everyone looks at the book. No one looks at the storm. Nine nights. Don't shine too bright before then. Brightness gets harvested early."

And she was gone, and the room was only a room again, gilded and grey and silent, lit by lamps that burned a fraction whiter than they had that morning.

* * *

Tamsin did not sleep. She sat on the edge of the grey velvet bed and counted, because counting was prayer and arithmetic and armor all at once, and after a while she realized Edmund was awake too, sitting across the room with his back against the wall, turning something over in his hands. In the white lamplight seeping under the door she could see it was nothing — just a pebble, a plain grey pebble he must have picked up on the mountain and carried down here like a talisman, worrying it between thumb and forefinger the way other men worried coins or rings.

"Nine nights," she said.

"Eight, if you count tonight, which I do." He flipped the pebble, caught it. "Ember's right about one thing. The turning is our only door. There isn't another. I've spent twenty years learning every back way through every dark place in these mountains, and there is no back way into a moment. You can't smuggle yourself past time."

"So we do what she said. We wait for the book to open, and we break it."

"We." He said the word carefully, testing it, the way you test ice before putting weight on it. "Tamsin. Listen to me. Whatever happens at that turning — whatever Ember thinks I am, whatever's left in me that hums — you stay near the door of it. Your brother's page tears free, you take it, and you go. Up the stair, out, done. You don't stay to see how it ends."

"And what will you be doing, while I'm fleeing with my page?"

He was quiet long enough that the lamps pulsed twice. When he answered, his voice had the flat calm she had learned to distrust on the road — the calm he wore when the pass was narrowing or the weather was turning, the calm of a man describing a cliff he intended to walk off.

"Something loud," he said.

Tamsin hugged her knees to her chest and looked at him across the darkening room — the exile, the storm-caller, the man who kept rules instead of friends — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he had already decided. Had maybe decided in the drowned stair, or in the corridor today when the caged souls hummed at him, or perhaps twenty years ago in a king's court, when he refused one storm and lost everything and had been carrying the refusal ever since like a stone in his chest, waiting for somewhere to set it down.

"Edmund," she said softly. "What does it cost? To let it out. The storm."

The pebble stopped turning in his fingers.

"Everything I have left of myself," he said. "But that's tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight we have eight days, a cracked ghost-child, a vault full of names, and a broker who thinks we're flattered to be guests." He set the pebble on the floor between them, deliberate as a man laying a wager. "Get some sleep, Tamsin Vane. Tomorrow we find the ledger room, and we learn every corridor to it by heart. Because when the turning comes, we'll have exactly one moment — and I intend to spend it well."

Beyond the door, the lamps of the Spire burned on, a shade brighter than the hour before, drinking their slow light from ten thousand caged and waiting souls. Eight nights, Tamsin thought. Eight nights until the book opened, and everyone knelt, and everyone looked at the ledger —

— and no one, no one at all, would be looking at the storm.
