## 15. Corvane's Lesson

The summons came as a whisper of candlelight.

Edmund had been awake in the quarters they'd been given — quarters, he noted bitterly, with no door that locked from the inside — when the flame in the wall sconce guttered sideways, bending against the draftless air like a reed in wind. Then it righted itself and burned a shade too blue. He had spent twenty years reading the language of small wrongnesses, and this one said: *come alone, or don't come at all.*

He went. Not because he was brave. Because there was a question twenty years old lodged under his ribs like a fishhook, and only one man alive who could answer it.

The corridors of the Spire did not behave like corridors. They descended without descending, turned without turning, and the walls were veined with a pale luminescence that Edmund recognized, with a lurch, as the same light he'd once coaxed out of stormclouds — bottled, domesticated, made to serve. Caged souls hung in niches along the way like lanterns in a shrine, each one a soft gold ember behind glass, and each one dimmed slightly as he passed, the way candles lean toward an open window. Toward weather. Toward him.

He kept his hands in his pockets and his face empty. Twenty years of practice.

* * *

Corvane's study was not what Edmund expected, which was itself a lesson. He had imagined a throne room scaled down, all obsidian and ostentation. Instead he found something almost humble: a round chamber lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, their spines cracked and bloated with use, thousands upon thousands of them, arranged with the loving disorder of a man who knows exactly where everything is and wants you to know he doesn't need to look.

A fire burned in a low hearth — a real fire, wood-smoke and all, though where wood came from this far beneath the mountain Edmund couldn't guess. Two chairs faced it. Corvane sat in one, and for a moment, seeing him thus, Edmund's heart performed a treachery of its own: it leapt.

Because Master Corvane looked exactly as he had on the last day Edmund saw him, twenty-two years ago, on the battlements of Coldharbour Keep in the rain. Tall and spare, silver at the temples even then, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the grey of a sky deciding. He wore plain wool now instead of court finery. He could have been any scholar in any tower, waiting up past midnight for a student to return from errands.

"Edmund." The old man didn't rise. He simply said the name the way he used to say it across a training yard — not warmly, but with ownership. "You've let your stance go. You stand like a smuggler now."

"I am a smuggler."

"You're a storm-caller standing like a smuggler. There's a difference, and the difference is why you're here." Corvane gestured to the second chair. "Sit. The fire's real. I have it brought down at some expense. A man should be able to smell smoke somewhere in the world."

Edmund did not sit. "You sent wardens to kill us at the waystation."

"I sent no one. The wardens act on the ledger's hunger, not my instruction. If I'd wanted you dead, boy, you'd never have seen the gate." He turned his hands toward the fire, palms open, an old teaching gesture. Edmund hated how well he remembered it. "You've come all this way down my mountain. The least we can do is be honest with one another before the end. Sit."

Edmund sat. It was, he would think later, the first tactical error he'd made in years, and he made it gladly, because some hungers are older than tactics.

"You knew," he said. "At the gate. You knew my name before Tamsin spoke hers."

"I wrote your name into a book when you were nine years old. First entry in your own hand. *Edmund Coldharbour, called the Weathercock, apprentice.* You pressed so hard the nib tore through the page." Corvane smiled, and it was the smile from the training yard, dry and fond and utterly without warmth. "I remember everything I write. That is the burden of my office."

"Your office." Edmund leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough to feel the fire's honest heat on his face. "Then let me ask you plainly, Master, since honesty is on offer. The famine. The winter the crops blackened from Thornfell to the coast. The winter children were sold by the sackful because there was nothing else to trade. Was that the old king's work? His wars bleeding the granaries? His taxes?"

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not guilt. Edmund searched for guilt and found instead the particular stillness of a man about to enjoy himself.

"No," Corvane said.

The word fell into the room like a stone into a well, and Edmund listened for the bottom and heard nothing.

"The king believed the famine was his misfortune. He prayed about it. He taxed for it. He started two wars over the grain routes it closed." Corvane rose, unhurried, and walked the length of the shelving, trailing one ink-stained finger along the spines. "But a famine is not weather, Edmund. Black rot does not spread across four hundred miles in a single season by chance. Blight has a shape. Hunger has a direction. And I—" he stopped, and turned, and the firelight made his shadow climb the ledgers behind him until it seemed to hold up the whole wall of them, "—I gave it both."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far above, or perhaps far below — distance meant nothing here — a soul-lantern flickered.

"You," Edmund said. His voice came out flat, which surprised him; he'd expected it to shake. "You starved them. Ten thousand people. Children. You starved a country so that mothers would sell—"

"So that mothers would *choose*," Corvane corrected gently, and that gentleness was the worst thing yet. "Do you imagine the Spire breaks down doors? Do you imagine we drag souls howling from their beds? No. Every soul in this mountain was carried here in love. A sister's love. A father's desperation. A lover's bargain. Love is the finest courier in the world — it passes every border, it asks no questions, it delivers." He tapped one of the ledgers with a fingernail, a small dry click. "Hunger is merely how we write the invitation. The choosing — ah. The choosing must always be theirs. That is the law beneath the law, and even I keep it."

"And the king? You served the king. You trained callers for his armies. You signed my exile in his name."

"I signed your exile," Corvane said, "in the name of a man who was already mine. Do you truly believe a mediocre king holds a kingdom through twenty lean years and three wars on his own wits?" He laughed softly, and the sound was terrible because it was familiar — Edmund had heard that laugh a hundred times over some botched lesson, some fumbled sigil, and had loved it. "I fed him just enough competence to keep him upright. The way I feed the lamps downstairs. Everything runs on something, Edmund. Kingdoms. Storms. Men. I learned that lesson young, and I learned it *here*."

"Here." Edmund stood. The chair scraped back and the sound was swallowed instantly, eaten by walls built to eat sound. "You speak of the Spire like a master you serve. But you were human once. You had a name before Corvane. You taught me that names have weight — yours first lesson, do you remember? *A name is the handle by which a soul is lifted.* Whose name did you set down to sit enthroned in the dark?"

For the first time, the broker's composure flickered. It lasted half a breath — a hairline crack across old glaze — and then it was gone, smoothed over, and Edmund understood that he had just seen the only wound the man still carried, and that it had been cauterized long ago.

"That question," Corvane said quietly, "is why you were exiled rather than killed. Did you ever wonder? A caller who refuses the crown's order, in wartime, should hang. Instead you got a road and a reputation. I argued for leniency. My colleagues thought me sentimental." He smiled again. "I was not sentimental. I was patient. Exile was simply a longer lesson plan."

"Lesson plan." Edmund's hands had begun, without his permission, to ache. Deep in the bones, in the place where the storms lived. "Everything is a lesson to you. The famine was a lesson. My exile was a lesson. What is Tamsin? What is the girl who sold her brother to buy bread you engineered the price of?"

"A test," Corvane said simply. "Yours."

He crossed to the hearth and crouched beside it, and for a moment, silhouetted against the flames, he looked again like the teacher on the battlements, showing a shivering boy how to cup lightning between careful hands. *Feel how it wants to serve you,* he'd said. *That's the whole secret. Everything wants to serve what feeds it.*

"Do you know what happens at midwinter, Edmund? At the turning?" Corvane didn't wait for an answer. "All debts come due at once. Every contract in every ledger, read aloud in a single night. The Spire consumes a year's harvest of souls in one sitting, and its hunger — its true hunger, the thing at the root of the root of this mountain — grows. It has grown for longer than your kingdom has had a name. Longer than mine. The old courts, the old kings, the little wars and little famines — they were all husbandry. Keeping the herd healthy. Keeping the deliveries coming." He looked up at Edmund across the fire, and his eyes were the grey of a sky that had decided, long ago, never to break. "And now the wards above have fallen, the stars are in our belly, and the world outside is going dark acre by acre. The age of borrowed light is ending. What comes next needs stewards. Not brokers. Stewards."

"Slaves," Edmund said.

"Gardeners," Corvane replied, unbothered. "There is a difference, and the difference is consent — which, as I told you, is the law beneath the law. So." He rose, brushed ash from his knee, and folded his ink-stained hands. "Here is my offer, student. Your exile is a page in a book I can amend. Your gift, which you have been strangling for twenty years, I can restore to full throat — more than full. Down here, near the root, a storm-caller is not a weapon. He is a *priest*. The souls in the cages already lean toward you; you've felt it walking the halls. They want to serve what feeds them. Imagine what you could be, to ten thousand souls in the dark. Their shepherd. Their weather. Their god, if you have the stomach for the word."

"And the price."

"Tamsin Vane walks to the turning unresisted. Wren's debt stands. One soul, freely owed, freely collected — the law beneath the law, kept. In exchange: your name restored, your exile ended, your storms returned, and a seat at the root of the world when the last light upstairs goes out." Corvane tilted his head, and his voice dropped into the register he'd used for the hardest lessons, the ones delivered after failure, in private, without cruelty. "You refused to call a storm on a starving village, and they stripped you for it. Fine. Noble. But look around you, Edmund. The starving village is the whole world now, and the storm is coming regardless. The only question left is whether you'll stand in it as a victim or a steward. I am offering you the chance to matter. To *govern* the dark instead of fleeing it. I am offering you, at last, the full curriculum."

Edmund looked at his teacher for a long time.

He looked, and he grieved. Not for the man — the man had died sometime in the deep centuries and left this efficient custodian wearing his face — but for the boy in the training yard who had pressed so hard on the nib that it tore the page. For the boy who had believed that power was a thing you could learn cleanly, from a good man, for good ends. Twenty years of neutrality, twenty years of scar tissue, and underneath it all, he realized, he had kept a small warm coal of hope that Corvane had been coerced, corrupted, compromised — anything but willing. That his teacher's betrayal had a floor.

It had no floor. It had never had a floor. The man had dug, and dug, and called the digging a curriculum.

"You asked once," Edmund said slowly, "why I never took another apprentice."

"I confess the question has interested me."

"Because the first thing I'd have to teach them is what you taught me. That everything wants to serve what feeds it. And I could never say it without hearing your voice." He stepped back from the fire, away from its honest warmth, into the cold blue light of the soul-lanterns. "So here is my answer, Master, and mark it well, because it's the last lesson I'll ever give you. You're right that everything wants to serve what feeds it. But you taught me the other half and then forgot it yourself: *everything fed eventually turns.* The ox turns. The dog turns. The storm turns. And the hunger at the root of this mountain has been fed for longer than either of us has been alive."

Corvane's expression did not change. But the fire did. It leaned hard sideways, all at once, as if the whole room had inhaled.

"You're refusing," the broker said.

"I'm declining the curriculum."

"Then you understand what happens. The girl substitutes herself or she doesn't; the brother burns either way; and you will watch it from whatever cell we keep for guests who mistake sentiment for principle." Corvane sighed, and it was almost genuinely sorrowful, and that was the final obscenity of it. "I had such hopes for you. I named you myself, you know. *Coldharbour.* A harbor against cold. I thought you'd grow into the name."

"You named me for the job you wanted done."

"Yes," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "And I named the famine for the harvest I wanted brought in. Naming is the whole of my craft, Edmund. It's why I'm still here, and why you were always going to end up on the other side of my desk." He returned to his chair and sat, and picked up a pen, and the interview was visibly over. "Go back to your quarters. Kiss the girl's hope goodbye, if it helps either of you. The turning comes in six days, and the ledger keeps excellent time."

Edmund walked to the door. At the threshold he stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been burning in him since the waystation, since the drained corpse and the defaced sigil.

"The warden at Hollowmere waystation," he said, without turning. "Drained of warmth. Left as a message. That was you."

Behind him, the scratch of the pen paused.

"It was a warning to stay out of Thornfell," Edmund continued. "To keep the roads clear for the harvest. You killed your own keeper and wrote it in his cold flesh, because that's what you do. You write in whatever's cheapest." Now he turned. "One day soon, Master, someone is going to read you the same way. And I intend to be holding the book."

The pen resumed. Corvane did not look up.

"Six days," he said mildly, to the ledger. "Mind the halls on your way back. The lanterns find you interesting, and interest, in this house, is rarely safe."

* * *

The corridors returned him by a route he did not choose and could not have retraced. The soul-lanterns leaned after him as he passed, gold embers straining behind their glass, and for the first time Edmund did not flinch from the attention. He studied it instead. The way they bent. The rhythm of their flickering — not random, he realized. Not random at all. Like grass before a squall. Like the held breath of a crowd.

Like a thousand small voices, practicing a single note.

In the doorway of their quarters, Tamsin sat awake, wrapped in her own arms, and the look on her face told him she'd been counting his heartbeats by the hour-glass of her fear.

"Well?" she said.

Edmund closed the door — the one that didn't lock — and leaned against it, and told her the truth entire: the famine, the king, the root-hunger, the offer, the refusal. She took it the way she took everything, silently and completely, the way ground takes rain.

When he finished, she was quiet a moment. Then: "Six days."

"Six days."

"And the lanterns lean toward you."

"They lean toward anyone who carries weather," Edmund said. "It means nothing."

But even as he said it, he could feel them through the walls — the whole mountain's worth of them, ten thousand small flames, all bending together in the dark like wheat before a storm that hadn't broken yet. Waiting. Practicing.

And far below, deeper than the ledgers, deeper than the court, something vast turned over in its sleep — and for the first time in centuries, it dreamed of thunder.
