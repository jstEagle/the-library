## 16. Allies in the Cages

The cells of the Spire did not have doors. They had mouths.

Tamsin understood this on her third day in the quarters Corvane had granted them — a stone room with no window, no seam, only a single archway that opened and closed at hours no clock would recognize. The whole tower was like that. It descended into the mountain in slow spirals, and every hall breathed. Walls swelled and shrank by fractions. The air came warm from below, carrying a smell like hot iron and old honey, and Tamsin had learned not to think about what fed that warmth, because she knew. She had signed the paper herself, seven winters ago, with a shaking hand and Wren's name written small at the bottom like an apology.

Now they walked the halls again — Edmund's idea, though he framed it as reconnaissance, as smuggler's habit, as anything but what it was: two people who could not sleep pacing the throat of the thing that meant to eat them.

"Count the turns," Edmund murmured. He kept his voice low out of caution, but there was something else under it tonight, something taut as wire before it snaps. "Left spiral, nine landings, then the gallery of cages. If we're going to move at the turning, we need to know the ground better than our own hands."

"And if the turning is when all debts come due," Tamsin said, "it's also when the Spire will be most awake."

"Then we'd best be quick."

The gallery opened around them without warning, the way everything in the Spire did — one moment corridor, the next vastness. Tamsin's breath caught, as it always did. The cages hung in their hundreds from a ceiling lost to darkness above, iron lanterns of a kind, each one holding a soft light that moved. Not flame-light. *Person*-light. Some of the lights drifted like moths against glass. Some pressed toward the bars as the visitors passed, and some had stopped pressing long ago and simply hung, dim and patient, the way a lamp burns down when no one comes to trim the wick.

She had made herself look at them on the first night, because looking away felt like the famine winter all over again — the shutters closed, the neighbors' doors shut, everyone pretending not to see who was starving. So she looked now, and she named them silently as she passed, the way you name the dead so they aren't only numbers. A light the color of late butter. A light like green riverglass. One that flickered in rhythms too regular to be random, and Tamsin thought, absurdly, *that one was a drummer once*, and her chest hurt.

Edmund had stopped walking.

He stood in the center of the gallery with his head tilted, the way he listened to weather — and Tamsin had seen him do it a hundred times on the road, reading storms three valleys off, standing very still while the world spoke to him. But there were no storms here. There was nothing here but stone and dark and the caged lights, and yet he listened as if—

"Edmund?"

"Hush." His voice had gone strange. Reverent and afraid at once, a man hearing his own name called from a house that burned down years ago. "Do you hear it?"

She strained. At first: nothing. The Spire's silence, which was not silence at all but a held breath, the hush of a court waiting for payment. Then, beneath it — faint as a pulse through a wall —

A hum.

It came from the cages. From the nearest of them, the butter-colored light, which had drifted close to its bars and was trembling — not with fear, Tamsin realized, but with *recognition*. The hum was thin and wavering, barely more than a thread, and it rose and fell in a pattern she almost knew, the way you almost know a face across a crowded market.

And then a second cage caught it. And a third.

"Oh," Tamsin said softly. "Oh, they hear *you*."

Edmund's hand had gone to his chest, flat over his sternum, where — she knew now, since the drowned stair, since his confession in the dark — the storm lived. Had always lived. The gift the old king had tried to buy and then to break, the thing he'd refused to spend on a starving village and been stripped of his name for keeping. He stood among the cages like a tuning fork struck amid bells, and the souls were answering him.

"It's the resonance," he said, half to himself. His eyes were bright and wet in the soul-light. "Storms are just... pressure finding its voice. Everything that's ever been squeezed past bearing sings the same note. I've heard it in thunderheads. In avalanche country." He turned to her, and his voice cracked on the next words. "In famine winters, Tamsin. I just never — I didn't know it could hear me back."

More lights were waking. The hum spread through the gallery like wind through wheat, cage to cage, a wave of sound with no source and no end, rising and falling, and Tamsin pressed her hands over her mouth because it was beautiful and terrible and it sounded like *grief*, like ten thousand griefs finding one voice, and underneath the grief — unmistakably, impossibly — there was hope.

The green riverglass light pressed hard against its bars. The drummer-flicker light beat faster. Somewhere far above or far below — distance meant nothing here — a deeper hum joined, and another, until the whole inverted tower seemed to thrum like a plucked string that would not stop.

"They know," Tamsin whispered. "They know what you're going to do."

"I don't even know what I'm going to do."

"Yes, you do." She took his arm. Through his sleeve she could feel it — a tremor, a gathering, like the stillness before lightning. "You've known since the stars went out. That's why you can't sleep either."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. Around them the humming swelled, and dust sifted from the unseen ceiling, and Tamsin thought: *the mountain itself is listening.* Then, distantly, from the direction of the drowned stair — from *outside*, from the world of roads and rain and honest dark —

A whistle.

Three notes, low-high-low. Old Sable's signal, the one every smuggler in Thornfell knew, the one that meant *friend coming in, hold your fire, count your debts later.*

Tamsin's heart slammed against her ribs. "That's not possible. That's—"

"That's Sable," Edmund said, and for the first time since Tamsin had known him, he laughed — short, disbelieving, alive. "The stubborn old horror. She said no one crosses into Spire territory and returns."

"She said *no one crosses*—"

"She lied." He was already moving, pulling Tamsin toward the stair, and the humming followed them through the galleries like a tide following the moon. "Or rather — she told us the truth the way smugglers tell truth. *No one returns alone.*"

* * *

They reached the mouth of the drowned stair as the first of them came up out of the water.

Tamsin would remember it always as a picture: the black flooded tunnel, the failing grey shimmer of Edmund's storm-light cast along the walls, and then shapes rising from the water one after another — lean figures in oilskin and wool, wringing out sleeves, passing children-sized bundles hand over hand up the steps with the practiced tenderness of people moving contraband, except the contraband was *souls*, freed ones, dim little lights cupped in lanterns of woven reed that Sable's people must have learned to make somewhere, somehow, in the time since Tamsin had last stood in Hollowmere's eaves.

Old Sable came up last, and she came up the way she did everything: slowly, deliberately, as if the world were a ledger she was choosing to balance on her own terms. Water streamed from her grey braids. Her eyes found Edmund first, then Tamsin, and her expression did not change, because it never changed, but her voice, when she spoke, was rougher than usual.

"You're both thinner. Good. Means you've been working."

"You said no," Edmund said.

"I said the haven wouldn't carry you." Sable wrung out one braid with terrible patience. "I never said *I* wouldn't. There's a difference, boy, and it's the difference between law and loyalty, and you of all people ought to know it." She looked past him, up the stair, toward the humming galleries. Her jaw tightened. "We heard it from outside. Three days ago, the whole mountain started to sing, and every smuggler in my hall got quiet at once, and I knew — I *knew* — that someone down here had finally given these poor souls a reason to make noise." Her gaze came back to Edmund, sharp as a gutting knife. "That was you."

"The storm-heart," Tamsin said. "The cages resonate off it. They've been humming ever since — growing louder. Sable, the whole Spire can hear it. The wardens—"

"Are busy." Sable smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a woman who had spent forty years profiting from other people's emergencies and had decided, at last, to spend her hoard differently. "Midwinter's four nights off. The halls brighten. The wardens march the harvested souls to the deep courts for the reading, and they leave the outer galleries thin. We counted. We've been counting since we came through the stair." She jerked her chin at her people, who were fanning out along the landing with the silent economy of those who had robbed caravans together for decades. "Bramm has the eastern passages. The Fen twins are marking the warden rounds. Merrin—" a wiry woman with a crossbow slung across her back raised two fingers in salute "—Merrin shot a warden yesterday, and we all agreed to never speak of how difficult that was."

"It was very difficult," Merrin said solemnly. "Faceless things don't bleed right."

"Why?" Tamsin asked. It came out harder than she intended, raw-edged. She stepped toward Sable, and the question tore loose from wherever she'd been keeping it. "Why come? You told us the crossing was death. You told us no one goes into the dark and comes back. You let us walk into it. Why follow?"

Sable regarded her for a long moment. Around them the freed souls in their reed lanterns glimmered softly, and from the galleries above the great hum continued, steady now, like breathing.

"Because you paid me your name," Sable said at last. "Freely given. Do you know what I did with it?"

Tamsin shook her head.

"Nothing. I couldn't. A name freely given isn't coin, girl — it's a *trust*. The Spire buys names to own people. But a name *given*..." The old woman's voice caught, just slightly, on the word. "Given means the giver chose. And I have spent thirty years watching people sell what they should have given and give what they should have sold, and I'll tell you what I've learned: the lawless are the only ones left who still understand the difference. Kings take. Brokers buy. But us — the smugglers, the exiles, the ones with no flag and no court — all we've ever had is what we choose to owe each other." She turned, gesturing at her crew, at the lantern-souls, at the dark stair behind them. "So. You chose to walk down here for a brother. I choose to follow. That's the whole arithmetic. Don't make it grander than it is."

But it was grander, and Tamsin saw that it was, because Bramm was kneeling at the waterline letting a frightened soul-light drift close to his cupped palms, warming it, and the Fen twins were teaching one of the freed souls a smuggler's counting rhyme in low voices, and Merrin was restringing her crossbow by soul-light without being asked, and none of them had been promised anything, because there was nothing left to promise.

Edmund stood beside her, and she felt him exhale — a long, shaking breath, twenty years of held solitude leaving his body all at once.

"Sable," he said quietly. "The ledger vault. At the turning, when the Spire opens its books to collect — that's when it can be unmade. That's what the child-soul told us. But the vault sits at the heart of the deep court, behind the broker's own seal, and I don't have my storms anymore, not really — not enough. What I have is resonance. What I have is *them*." He nodded upward, toward the humming. "If the caged souls can reach the vault — if we can bring the song down to the heart of it—"

"Then the whole tower becomes the weapon," Sable finished. Her eyes gleamed. "Every soul it ever bought, singing one note at the one moment all its debts come due. Oh, that's *smugglers' law*, that is. Turn the cargo against the ship." She spat into the dark water, an old blessing or an old curse. "Four nights. We hold the stair, we hold the galleries, we move the weak ones out as fast as the tunnels allow. And when midnight comes—"

The mountain interrupted her.

The hum changed.

Tamsin felt it before she understood it — a wrongness in the note, a discord threading through the harmony like ice through water. Above them, the gallery-lights guttered, dozens at once, and the warmth that breathed up from the Spire's depths went suddenly cold, cold as the famine winter, cold as the night the stars went out. The freed souls in their reed lanterns whimpered — actually whimpered, small sounds like children in sleep — and pressed inward, away from the stair, away from the deep places.

From far below, rising through miles of stone, came a sound Tamsin had heard once before and prayed never to hear again: the Spire, opening its mouth.

Not for midwinter. Not in four nights.

*Early.*

"They know," Edmund breathed. His hand flew to his chest again, and Tamsin saw the storm-light flicker there beneath his skin, wild and insufficient. "Corvane knows what we are. He's pulling the collection forward — he's going to read the ledger *now*, before the turning, before we—"

"No," Sable said, and her voice cut through the panic like a keel through chop. She was already moving, already shouting orders in the clipped cant of the roads, and her smugglers were already obeying, souls bundled, weapons drawn, the whole ragged company becoming a single purpose. "No, boy — early collections mean early *weakness*. A court that rushes its reading leaves its vault half-guarded and its seals half-spoken." She looked back at them, at Tamsin, and her old eyes held no comfort and all the courage in the world. "We wanted four nights. Fate's given us one. Smugglers take what's offered and haggle later."

Above them, the humming of the caged souls rose to a pitch that hurt the teeth — terror and defiance in the same long note — and the mouths of the cells began, all together, to open.
