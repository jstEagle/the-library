## 17. The Turning of the Year

The Spire kept no clocks, but every soul in it knew the hour.

Edmund felt it first as a change in the pressure of the air — a slow tightening, like the sky before hail, though they were a hundred fathoms under stone and there had been no sky here for an age. The caged souls in their lantern-globes along the corridor walls brightened by degrees, each one swelling like a held breath. Their light was not warm. It had never been warm. But tonight it trembled, and the trembling had a rhythm to it, and the rhythm was counting down.

*Midnight,* said the child-soul at his hip, in its voice like ash settling. *The turning. He is opening the book.*

Edmund pressed his back to the cold wall of the gallery above the ledger vault and looked down into the chamber where Corvane stood.

His old master had dressed for the occasion. That was the thing about Corvane that twenty years had not dulled: he understood ceremony the way other men understood breathing. His grey robes were shot through with threads that caught the soul-light and gave it back silvered, so that he seemed lit from within, a man become his own lantern. Before him, on a plinth of black glass, lay the Ledger — a book the size of a tomb door, its cover stitched from something that might once have been leather and might once have been skin, its pages stirring without wind.

Around the chamber's rim, set into niches like saints in a cathedral, burned the harvest. Hundreds of globes. Thousands. Each one a name, a winter, a hunger, a bargain made in desperation and sealed with a thumbprint of blood or tears or both. They had been brought up from the deep cages over the past three days, arranged, curated. Corvane had always believed presentation mattered. *A contract signed in squalor feels like theft,* he used to say, when Edmund was young enough to sit at his feet and call him teacher. *The same contract signed in candlelight feels like fate.*

Below, Tamsin waited among the souls in her cage-niche, small and still, her breath fogging the glass. She had insisted on being taken with the others rather than hidden — *they'll be watching for a hidden thing* — and Edmund had hated agreeing with her. Now he counted her breathing the way he'd once counted the seconds between lightning and thunder, and told himself the same thing he'd told himself then: *soon, soon, soon.*

Above him, in the dark of the gallery, Old Sable's smugglers crouched among the buttresses like rooks on a roofline. Forty of them, maybe fifty, come down the drowned stair with knives and crowbars and the particular silence of people who had spent their whole lives being invisible to law. Sable herself knelt at Edmund's shoulder, her ancient face half-lit by the rising glow, and she said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that hadn't been said in the tunnels. The plan was simple. Simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with a place like this.

The Ledger opened.

It did not open the way books open. The cover lifted like a jaw, and the sound it made was the sound Edmund had heard on the mountain the night the stars went out — glass breaking, but slowed down, drawn out across whole seconds, so that the breaking became almost musical. Pages turned themselves. And from the book rose a voice that was not Corvane's, that was not any voice, that was the Spire itself reading its own accounts aloud.

Names. It spoke names.

Each one it spoke, a globe flared white, and Edmund watched the light get pulled out of it in a long thread, drawn down into the open pages like thread off a spindle. He had seen souls harvested before, in theory, in rumor, in the stories smugglers told to frighten each other over cheap wine. He had never seen it done *industrially*. This was not cruelty. Cruelty required attention. This was accounting.

"Wren Vane," said the Spire.

Down in the niche-row, one globe flared brighter than the rest — a small one, a stubborn one, its light flickering like a candle arguing with wind. Edmund's chest went tight around something that had no name either. Seven years old when he was sold. Seven years in a jar. And still flickering. Still arguing.

*Tamsin,* he thought, *hold. Hold, girl.*

The thread of Wren's light began to draw out.

And Edmund Coldharbour, who had kept twenty years of neutral roads, who had sworn off storms on a mountainside with a dead village smoking below him and his king's seal burning his name off the rolls — Edmund stepped off the gallery edge and let himself fall forty feet into the vault.

He landed badly, because he was fifty-three years old and had never once claimed otherwise, and he came up with his shoulder screaming and Corvane already turning, already smiling, because of course the old man had known. Had probably counted on it. There was a version of this night where Corvane wanted exactly this, wanted Edmund standing in front of the Ledger at the moment of the turning, because a storm-caller's gift spent against the Spire would only feed it —

That was the trap. Edmund could see its shape now, the way you see the shape of a snare only when your foot is already in it. Every lesson Corvane had ever taught him circled back to one doctrine: *power given to anger belongs to whoever aimed the anger.* If Edmund raged, the rage would pour down into the book like rain into a well, and the Spire would drink him too, and the ledger would grow fat on the last free storm in Thornfell.

So he did not rage.

He remembered.

"Master Corvane," he said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. "You taught me that a storm isn't weather. You said it's memory. Every storm remembers every storm before it — the sea remembers, the stone remembers, the air remembers what was done to it." He straightened, slowly, his bad shoulder cradled. "I swore I'd never call one again. I've kept that oath for twenty years. So understand me when I say I'm not calling a storm."

Corvane's smile thinned by a degree. "Then what, Edmund? What do you imagine you're doing?"

"I'm giving one back."

He reached inward — past the scar tissue, past the sealed door he had built behind his sternum the day the king's men took his name, past all the careful nothing he had made of himself — and opened it.

Not rain. Not wind. Not the clean violence of weather, which was only ever the sky's smallest vocabulary. What came out of Edmund Coldharbour was everything a storm had ever been in the history of the world, the way a single bell holds every bell that ever rang it. The monsoon that had drowned his grandfather's coast. The dry lightning over the Thornfells that started fires the old songs still sang about. The soft gray drizzle on the morning he was born, which his mother had sworn was a blessing and which he now knew was simply the sky, keeping its oldest appointment. The storm he had refused to call on Harrow Vale — that one most of all, that one he had carried longest, and it came out of him not as destruction but as *witness*, as if the unspent thunder itself had been waiting twenty years to testify.

The vault filled with light that was not the Spire's light.

Every caged soul felt it. Edmund felt them feel it — a shiver running down the niche-rows like wind through wheat, and then the humming began, the resonance Sable's people had spoken of in whispers, the thing the discarded child had promised. The souls had been bought and bottled and starved, but they had not been silenced, and now, hearing the memory of open sky in a place that had never known one, they began to sing. Not words. Pressure. The hum of a world remembering itself. Globes vibrated in their settings. Cracks spidered across the nearest ones, fine as frost on a window.

"No," said Corvane, and for the first time in Edmund's living memory, Master Corvane sounded his age. "No — the turning takes *all debts*, you fool, you cannot —"

"You taught me contracts, too," Edmund said. The storm was pouring out of him now visibly, silver and blue-white, streaming from his hands and eyes and mouth, and he could feel it going the way he had always known it would go: like blood. Like the last warm thing leaving a body. He had perhaps minutes. Perhaps less. He found he didn't mind. He found, in fact, that some clenched fist inside him that had been clenched since Harrow Vale was finally, gratefully, opening. "And you taught me the oldest clause of all. A debt collected by fraud is void. You engineered the famine. You wrote the hunger into the world and then sold these people the cure. Every page in that book is a lie wearing a signature."

"The signatures are *binding* —"

"They're binding on the signers," said Edmund. "Not on the storm."

He raised both hands, and the resonating souls rose with them — not physically, not yet, but in pitch, in fury, the hum climbing toward a note that stone could not bear. Down in her niche, Tamsin had her palm flat against the glass of Wren's globe, and even from here Edmund could see her lips moving. Not praying. Counting. Counting her brother's flicker the way he'd taught her to count lightning, three weeks ago, on the last lit road either of them would ever walk again.

"Now," Edmund said — to Sable, to the dark, to everyone who had ever chosen the margins over the law. "Now, now, *now*."

The smugglers came off the galleries like water finding every crack in a dam. Crowbars into niche-seals. Knives under globe-brackets. Sable herself went down the vault wall on a rope, sixty-eight years old and faster than anyone had a right to expect, and put her blade through the lock of Tamsin's cage while the Spire's voice rose from the Ledger in something that was, unmistakably, for the first time, fear.

The first globe shattered.

The soul inside tore free — a farmer, Edmund somehow knew, a beet-farmer from the lowlands, sold in the famine year like ten thousand others — and instead of winking out, instead of being drawn down into the book, it *caught* the storm-light. Held it. Burned brighter. And the globe beside it cracked, and the one beside that, and then the whole niche-row went at once, a hundred souls bursting free into the silver air, and the sound they made together was not a scream and not a hymn but something between — the sound of a held breath released after seven years, after seventy, after an age of the world.

The Ledger screamed.

Pages began to turn backward, frantically, the Spire trying to re-read its accounts, to reassert its arithmetic over a room that had stopped believing in numbers. Threads of harvested light snapped and recoiled. Somewhere deep beneath the floor, the hunger that Corvane served howled up through the stone, and the whole inverted tower shuddered on its root.

Corvane lunged for the book.

Edmund saw it clearly — his teacher's hands closing on the black cover, the old instinct of the broker, *save the accounts, save yourself* — and he understood, with the strange clarity of a man spending his final coin, that this was the last thing he would ever choose. He put what remained of the storm into one word.

"*Void.*"

The word hit the Ledger like the dawn hits a lamp.

The cover tore from Corvane's grip. Pages ripped loose by the hundred, by the thousand, and every page burned as it flew — not consumed, *freed*, each line of ink unraveling into a thread of light that shot upward toward the distant, buried memory of sky. Names unwriting themselves into names again. Debts dissolving into the people they had been taken from. The vault filled with a blizzard of burning paper and blazing souls, and in the middle of it Edmund Coldharbour sank to his knees and felt his gift go out of him the way a tide goes out — completely, irrevocably, down to the sand.

It did not hurt. That was the miracle of it. It hurt the way a fever breaks hurts: the agony was in the having-been, not the ending. He knelt in the ruin of the greatest work he would ever do, empty as a struck bell, and he was *glad*.

Through the smoke and the silver, he saw Corvane.

The old man stood ankle-deep in burning pages, clutching a single sheet to his chest — his own contract, Edmund realized, the original, the one that bound him to the hunger below, the deed to his own damnation. Around his feet the torn fabric of the vault floor had split, and something was looking up through the gap. Something patient. Something that had waited a very long time for its broker's accounts to come due.

"Edmund." Corvane's voice was very small. His silver robes were dimming. "Edmund, we can renegotiate —"

"You taught me better than that," Edmund said. "Read the clause."

The floor took him gently, the way the Spire took everything — without hatred, without mercy, as a matter of bookkeeping. The last Edmund saw of his teacher was the man's face turned up through the closing stone, and it was not a monster's face. It was worse. It was the face of a man who had run the sums his whole life and only now discovered he had miscounted.

Then the tower shook to its roots, the freed souls wheeled overhead like a migration of stars, and somewhere in the blaze and the falling paper, a small stubborn globe cracked open at last — and a voice that had been waiting seven years to argue said, clearly, in the hearing of everyone:

"Tamsin? *Tamsin, where are you?*"
