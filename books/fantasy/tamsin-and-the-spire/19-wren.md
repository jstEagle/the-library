## 19. Wren

The pages were still falling.

They came down through the dark of the ruined vault like the first snow of a famine winter — Tamsin knew that snow, had learned its weight on her shoulders at nine years old, had learned to count flakes the way other children counted coins. But these did not melt and did not chill. They burned as they fell, each one a small gold flame unraveling into nothing, and where they touched the freed souls they left no ash at all.

Tamsin stood in the middle of it with her arms out and her hands empty, and she could not move.

The ledger room was a shambles of light. Edmund's storm had torn the vault open from crown to floor, and now the Spire's great inverted tower groaned around them like a ship in heavy weather, its caged lamps swinging on their chains, throwing long shadows that spun and broke and spun again. Somewhere above, stone was coming loose. Somewhere below, the hunger that had lived beneath the mountain for longer than any kingdom was waking to find its table empty. Sable was shouting names — smugglers' names, soul-names, the true names of the freed ones she'd come down the drowned stair to carry home — and her voice cracked across the chaos like a whip keeping time.

Tamsin heard none of it clearly. She was looking at the souls.

They hung in the air where their pages had held them, hundreds of them, thousands, pale shapes without faces or with too many, some no larger than moths and some vast as weather. They drifted up out of the broken vault in slow spirals, rising toward whatever waited beyond the mountain's roots, and most of them went without hesitation, drawn upward like smoke finding a chimney. They passed through the falling burning pages and brightened. They passed through Tamsin herself, once — a small cold thing, a child's soul by the feel of it — and she felt it pause inside her chest for half a heartbeat, curious, before it went on.

*Go on,* she told it silently. *Don't wait. Waiting is the worst part.*

She would know. She had done seven years of it.

"Wren." She said his name aloud because she could not find him and because saying it was the only prayer she had ever trusted. "Wren. Wren."

The vault floor was littered with the dead contracts, curling as they burned, and among them she saw fragments of handwriting she recognized — not Wren's, he'd been six and could barely shape his letters, but the broker's hand, Corvane's hand, the same elegant slant she remembered from the paper she had signed in a famine winter with fingers too cold to hold the pen properly. *One soul, minor, unclaimed. Term: seven years, due at the turning.* She stepped on it as she walked. She hoped it hurt the paper.

"Tamsin!" Edmund's voice, roughened by the storm he'd spent, hoarse as an old rope. He was somewhere behind her, near the shattered doorframe, one hand pressed to the wall to keep his feet. She could hear the wrongness in his breathing even from here — the silence where his storms had lived, the emptiness he'd bought for all of them with the last coin of himself. Later. She would have time to grieve for him later, and he would wave it off and make some dry remark about the weather, and she would let him, because that was how Edmund Coldharbour survived things. But not yet.

"Where are you going?" he called after her. "The stair's this way — Tamsin—"

"I'm not leaving without him!"

She didn't know if he heard. The tower screamed, a sound of settling stone and starving dark, and then Sable's voice cut through it, closer now: "Girl! The vault's coming down around your ears!"

"He's here!" Tamsin shouted back, and her voice broke on the word *here*, because she didn't know it was true, couldn't know it, and the not-knowing was a knife she'd been carrying since the drowned stair. "He has to be here. His page burned. I *saw* his page burn."

Pages burned free and every written soul tears loose — that was what Edmund had promised, what he had spent everything to make true. But promises were paper too. She had signed enough of them to know.

She waded deeper into the ruin.

* * *

The far end of the vault had been the oldest part. She understood that without being told; the stone there was different, darker, worn smooth in ways that had nothing to do with masons. The oldest debts were kept there, the first bargains struck when the Spire was young and hungry and the mountain still had a name anyone remembered. And it was there, in that oldest dark, that the souls moved slowest.

They clustered near the walls like moths that had forgotten the moon. Some of them had been sold so long ago that the shape of them had worn away entirely — just warmth without outline, just a hum. Others kept their forms stubbornly, the way old people keep their accents: a woman with her hair in a braid, a man with a smith's broad shoulders, a child holding something that might once have been a wooden horse.

Tamsin walked between them and they turned toward her as she passed, all of them, slowly, like flowers finding a sun that wasn't there. They could smell the living on her, she thought. Warmth. Breath. The debt of blood that says *someone came back.*

"I'm sorry," she whispered to them, because she couldn't carry them all and because sorry was the only currency she had left. "I'm sorry. Go up. Go home. Don't wait for anyone — go."

And then, against the far wall, beneath a shelf of empty ledgers whose pages had already burned:

A boy sat with his knees drawn up.

He was older than six. That was the first thing, the thing that hit her like a fall onto ice. Seven years had passed outside and seven years had passed inside, and the boy against the wall was thirteen or nearly — tall in the way of boys who grow fast and haven't learned where their own elbows end, all wrists and collarbones and hollow cheeks. He had her mother's jaw. He had their father's crooked left ear, the one the midwife swore she'd never seen the like of. He had a smudge of soot on his chin that she wanted, absurdly, to lick her thumb and wipe away.

His eyes were open. They were the color of the sky over Thornfell in the hour before dawn, gray going to silver, and they were fixed on nothing, and they were *his*, and Tamsin made a sound she had never made before, a sound with no word in it at all.

"Wren."

He looked up.

For a moment — a moment she would keep for the rest of her life, folded away like the last dry cloth in a wet year — nothing happened. He simply looked at her the way the other souls had looked, with that slow turning attention, that hunger for the living. And terror opened under her like a crevasse: *they've hollowed him, they've kept him so long there's nothing left, I'm too late, I sold him and I've lost him twice—*

Then his brow furrowed. It was such a small thing. Such an ordinary, human, irritated little frown, the frown of a boy interrupted at something important, and it was so completely, so unbearably *Wren* that Tamsin's knees gave out and she went down on the stone in front of him.

"You're crying," he said. His voice was rusty, a hinge unused. "Why are you crying? Did the dark lady say you could take me?"

"The—" Tamsin wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Who? What dark lady?"

"The one who counts." He said it matter-of-factly, the way he'd once reported that the neighbor's dog had gotten into the turnips. "She comes round sometimes. She writes in her book and looks at me and asks if I remember my name. I always tell her yes. She doesn't like that." A pause. His eyes narrowed, and there it was again, that flicker of the boy who had once hidden frogs in her boots and blamed the cat. "You know my name."

"Wren," she said. "Wren Vane. You're my brother. I'm Tamsin. I'm your sister, Wren, I'm—"

"Tamsin." He tried it out. His head tilted. "You used to sing the mill song wrong on purpose so I'd correct you."

Her heart stopped and started again, bruised but beating. "I did."

"And you said if I ate the frost-flowers off the window I'd grow up backwards."

"You *did* eat them, though."

"They tasted like nothing," Wren said, with the grave disappointment of a connoisseur. "Everything tastes like nothing here."

And that — that flat, childish complaint, that small and terrible truth — undid her more than anything the Spire had shown her, more than the caged lamps and the wardens' faceless patience and the broker's silken voice. She reached for him and he flinched, actually flinched, drew back against the wall, and she froze with her hands hovering in the air between them like a woman caught mid-bargain.

"I won't hurt you," she said. "Wren. Little brother. I would never—"

"You're warm," he said. Not accusing. Wondering. "Souls aren't supposed to be warm. She said I'd get used to it. I never got used to it." He looked at his own hands, translucent at the edges, faintly luminous, the hands of a boy rendered in candlelight and memory. "Am I dead?"

"No." The word came out of her like a hammer blow, hard and certain, and she believed it because she had to. "No. You're not dead. You're — borrowed. That's all. You were only ever borrowed, and I've come to bring you home."

"Borrowed," Wren repeated. He chewed on the word. Above them the tower groaned again, louder, and a crack ran up the wall beside the empty shelves, shedding dust that fell straight through his shoulder without touching him. He didn't seem to notice. "Borrowed from whom?"

And there it was. The question she had carried down the drowned stair and through the wardens' gate and into the court beneath the mountain, the question she had rehearsed answers to in the dark while Edmund slept and the child-soul watched her with its patient discarded eyes. *From me,* she could say. *I sold you. I signed the paper. I took the grain and the oil and the salt pork and I fed us both through the winter and I have hated myself every day since, and I am sorry, I am sorry, I am sorry.*

She opened her mouth to say it.

What came out instead was: "Doesn't matter. From someone who doesn't get a vote anymore. Come on."

* * *

Getting him up was harder than she expected. Not because he resisted — he didn't, exactly — but because he moved like a boy walking in his sleep, and every few steps he stopped, arrested by something only he could see.

"That was my bed," he said once, pointing at a bare stretch of floor. "Wasn't it? Before. It had a blanket with a fox on it. Or I dreamed the fox."

"It had a fox," Tamsin said steadily. Her voice only shook a little. "It had three foxes. You counted them every night. You said the third one was the fastest."

"The third one was the fastest," Wren agreed, satisfied, and walked on.

That was the shape of it, she learned. He did not remember the sale. He did not remember the famine, the brokers' parlor with its smell of ink and tallow, the paper, the pen, her hand shaking. He did not remember her face from before. What he remembered was waiting — waiting as a texture, waiting as a country he'd been living in, seven years of it compressed into impressions like river stones worn smooth. The counting lady. The tastelessness. The foxes. Once, quietly, staring at nothing: "There was a lot of dark. But it wasn't scary dark. It was just — empty. Like a room after everyone's gone home." And then, after a pause that Tamsin did not dare fill: "Were you scared? Out there?"

"Yes," she said.

"Me neither," he said, and she understood that this was a kindness, a lie offered the way children offer lies, generously and badly, and she loved him so much her ribs ached.

The tower was failing around them. They climbed toward the vault's broken mouth through air full of drifting embers and liberated light, and the freed souls rose past them in slow rivers, and Wren watched them go with his head tipped back. "Where do they think they're going?" he asked.

"Up," Tamsin said. "Out. Home."

"Is our home still there?"

The question landed in her chest and stayed there, sharp. The farm had been sold for seed-grain two winters after the famine, she and her mother scattered to work-rooms in Millbrook, the valley itself a day's walk and a lifetime away. "It's different," she said finally. "But it's ours. We'll make it ours again."

He considered this with the seriousness he'd once given the fox-blanket. "Okay," he said. "But I want the window with the frost-flowers."

"We'll negotiate."

Sable found them at the mouth of the vault. The old woman was gray with dust and streaked with soot, her coat scorched along one sleeve, and she had a freed soul riding her shoulder like a tame starling, and when she saw Wren she stopped dead. For a moment her craggy face did something complicated — grief and relief and an old, old anger at the world — and then it closed back into its usual granite.

"Well," she said. "Seven years of coin and worry, and he comes out taller than you. That'll teach you to bargain with brokers." She jerked her chin toward the corridor. "Stair's flooding. Your storm-man's holding the landing with nothing but his voice and sheer bloody stubbornness, and I don't know how long either will hold. Move, girl."

Tamsin moved. But at the threshold she stopped and looked back, one last time, at the vault where she had spent her childhood in ink.

The pages were almost all burned now. The last few drifted down like fireflies, and among the empty shelves and the smooth dark stone there was nothing left of the ledger at all — no record, no debt, no proof that a nine-year-old girl had once traded her brother's soul for a barrel of salt pork and a sack of barley. It had never happened, the room said. Except for the boy standing beside her, translucent and real and complaining that everything tasted of nothing, it had never happened at all.

She reached down and took his hand.

She couldn't feel it — his fingers were cool and slight as breath against her palm — but his grip tightened anyway, deliberately, the way you hold on when you mean it, and together they turned toward the water and the dark and the long climb into morning.

Behind them, deep in the mountain's roots, something vast and starving began to howl.

Ahead, faint through the flooding dark, she could hear Edmund shouting — not spells, not storms, just words, plain human words, telling everyone where to put their feet.
