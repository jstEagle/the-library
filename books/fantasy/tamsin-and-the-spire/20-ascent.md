## 20. Ascent

The Spire did not fall the way buildings fall.

Tamsin had imagined collapse as thunder — stone cracking, towers toppling, dust and ruin. What came instead was a long, slow *inhale*, as if the whole inverted tower were drawing breath into lungs that no longer worked. The walls of the great court shivered. The caged souls along the galleries flared once, all together, like a field of lamps catching wind, and then their light began to gutter — not dying, she realized, but *leaving*, drawn upward through the broken vault, streaming toward the surface in threads too thin to see.

The hunger below was starving. And a starving thing does not rage. It shrinks.

"Move," Sable said, and her voice carried the flat authority of a woman who had outlived three kings and intended to outlive this too. "It's coming down from the top. Everything it held up is letting go."

Tamsin looked up — up, into the dark throat of the Spire, where an inverted tower descended for what might have been a mile into living stone. She could hear it now: a groaning deep beneath her feet, the sound of foundations forgetting their purpose. Somewhere far above, the mountain itself was settling into the empty space where the Spire's will had been.

Edmund caught her arm. His face was grey with exhaustion, streaked with soot and something that might have been tears he hadn't noticed shedding. Where his storm-gift had lived behind his eyes there was now only a strange, naked quiet — a man looking at the world without armor for the first time in thirty years.

"The stair," he said. "We go now or we don't go at all."

* * *

The drowned stair had been flooded when they came down; now the water was rising faster, chasing them upward step by step, black and cold and smelling of old iron. Tamsin climbed with Wren's hand locked in hers and did not dare look back.

He was lighter than she remembered. That was the cruelty of it — seven years in the Spire's keeping, fed on nothing but waiting, and her brother had come out of it thin as winter kindling, hollow-cheeked, his eyes too large for his face. But his hand gripped hers with real strength, and every few steps he would squeeze it, twice, the way he had when they were children crossing the frozen ford at Vane's Hollow, and each time Tamsin's chest seized so hard she nearly stumbled.

*He doesn't remember being sold.* He remembered waiting. Only waiting. Seven years of standing at a window that didn't exist, watching for a sister who never came, and he bore her no anger for it because he didn't know there was anything to forgive.

She would tell him someday. On a warm evening, maybe, with bread in the oven and the valley green outside. Or maybe she never would, and carry it alone, which might be its own kind of payment. Either way — not here. Not on these stairs, with the water climbing at their heels.

Behind them, Sable's smugglers moved like a chain of lanterns. There were eleven of them, weathered men and women who had spent their lives hauling contraband through the mountain's veins and who now hauled something else entirely: the freed souls too weak to climb. They carried them wrapped in oilcloth and blankets, cradled against their chests like infants, murmuring to them in the low crooning tones people use for the dying and the newborn alike. One soul — a girl, or the memory of one, faint as breath on glass — had wound herself around Old Sable's neck like a scarf, and Sable, who had not permitted another living creature to touch her in forty years, walked with her head tilted slightly, as if listening.

"They're lighter going up," one smuggler said to another. "Souls. Lighter than any cargo I ever ran."

"Aye," said the other. "And worth more."

Tamsin's legs burned. The stair was cut into the rock in a tight spiral, slick with spray, and the darkness pressed close except where Edmund's failing storm-light sputtered ahead of them — a pale blue glow around his hands that grew dimmer with every hour, a candle burning down to its last inch of wick. He had spent everything in the ledger room. Every storm he'd ever swallowed, every thunderhead he'd hoarded through twenty years of exile, poured out in one act of ruinous generosity. What remained was dregs. He rationed it anyway, holding the light high for the weakest climbers, never for himself.

"You should save it," Tamsin told him once, gasping between steps.

"For what?" He almost smiled. "A rainy day?"

* * *

They rested at the third landing, a shelf of rock barely wide enough for them all, while the water rose past the landing below and kept rising. Tamsin counted heads. Eleven smugglers. Fourteen souls strong enough to walk. Nine carried. Edmund. Herself. Wren.

Wren sat with his back against the wall, watching the water with an expression she couldn't read.

"It's going down," he said suddenly.

"What?"

"The dark." He pointed at the flood churning below them. "Look."

She looked. At first she saw only black water. Then she saw what he meant: the darkness in the water was *thinning*. It had been absolute when they descended — a blackness you could taste, thick as tar, the Spire's endless night made liquid. Now it was merely dark. Ordinary dark. Water-dark. Somewhere in the last hours, without anyone marking the moment, the night had stopped being infinite and started being simply the absence of a lamp.

"The Spire's letting go," Edmund said quietly. He crouched at the edge of the landing, trailing his fingers above the flood without touching it. "All those years it held the dark like a held breath. Now—" He flexed his hand, and Tamsin saw him reach, instinctively, for the storm that wasn't there anymore. His fingers closed on nothing. He stared at his own palm for a long moment, and then folded it closed, gently, like a man closing a door on an empty room.

"Does it hurt?" she asked.

He considered the question honestly, which was more than most men would have done. "It's quiet," he said at last. "I haven't heard quiet since I was six years old. I keep thinking someone's died." A pause. "Someone has, I suppose. Someone I was."

Above them, deep in the rock, something vast shifted its weight.

"Up," said Sable. "Now."

* * *

The second half of the climb blurred into a single continuous effort of will. Tamsin's world narrowed to the next step, and the next, and Wren's hand in hers, and the sound of water rising behind them — no longer a chase now but a tide, patient and total, filling the stairwell from below as the Spire's hollow bones drank the mountain's groundwater and gave up its own.

Twice the stair was blocked.

The first time, a section of wall had buckled inward, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a child. The smugglers passed the soul-bundles through one by one, arms straining, swearing softly, and then squeezed themselves after. Tamsin went last with Wren, pushing him through ahead of her, and for one horrible moment she was stuck — hips wedged, ribs screaming, the water lapping at her boots — before Edmund's hands found her shoulders and shoved, and she popped free and lay gasping on wet stone while the flood licked the threshold behind her.

The second time, there was no gap at all. A fall of rubble sealed the stair completely, slabs of stone the size of cottages tumbled together like dropped toys.

For a moment nobody spoke. The water rose. And then the little lost soul — the child-fragment who had led them down through the flooded tunnels days ago, who had traded guidance for warmth, whom Tamsin had half believed lost in the chaos of the turning — drifted up through the rubble as if it were mist.

It turned and looked at them. It had no face, exactly, but it managed somehow to convey an expression of mild reproach, the way a cat looks at you when you've forgotten how doors work.

Then it sank into the stone, and the stone *remembered* it was water, and the rubble softened and flowed apart, and there was a passage where there had been none.

"It remembers," Wren whispered. "The discarded ones. The Spire threw them away, but the mountain took them in. They live in the walls now." He touched the wet rock almost shyly. "They want to see the sky."

So they climbed through a passage made by ghosts, and Tamsin wept without stopping, silently, because there was no time to do it properly.

* * *

Near the end, the air changed.

It came gradually — a loosening, a freshness, the mineral stink of deep stone giving way to something cleaner. Then cold. Real cold, mountain cold, the cold of open night rather than buried dark. The smugglers smelled it and began, despite themselves, to move faster, their exhaustion burned away by hope the way fog burns off a valley at sunrise.

And then the last turn of the stair, and the last landing, and a ceiling of rock overhead that was not rock at all but the underside of ice — a frozen waterfall, centuries old, through which starlight was beginning, very faintly, to seep.

"There," Sable said. "The eaves. We come out under the glacier lip, half a mile below the pass. Everyone up. Souls first."

They broke through the ice into a night so wide it made Tamsin dizzy.

She crawled out onto a slope of blue snow with Wren beside her and lay on her back, chest heaving, and looked up. The stars were returning. Not all at once — they came back the way feeling comes back to a frozen limb, painfully, in patches. First a handful near the horizon, trembling as if unsure of their welcome. Then dozens. Then hundreds, spilling across the sky in rivers, constellations reassembling themselves like scattered beads finding their string again. The Thornfell peaks stood black and sharp against them, wearing snow like crowns.

Below, far below, the mountain rumbled once — a long, settling sigh — and went still. Somewhere inside it, the Spire finished collapsing into the hunger that had birthed it, and the hunger, unfed, unlit, unwitnessed, starved into silence. Tamsin felt it go the way you feel a fever break: not a sound, just an absence where an ache had been.

The dark let go. All of it. Every debt, every cage, every stolen year.

Wren stood beside her, snow to his knees, staring up with his mouth open. Starlight moved on his hollow face and filled it, and for the first time since she'd found him among the freed souls he looked less like a ghost and more like a boy.

"I used to watch for them," he said. "In there. I didn't know what I was watching for. Just — lights. I thought if I waited long enough, the lights would come back." He reached up, as if he could cup a handful of stars. "You came instead."

Tamsin pulled him against her and held on, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing left that needed saying, and the cold was bitter, and her body was ruined, and she had never in her life been so full.

* * *

They made camp at dawn in the lee of the glacier, and Sable's smugglers built fires with the practiced economy of people who have slept rough in worse places than the roof of the world. The souls too weak to travel were laid near the flames, wrapped in every blanket the company owned, and slowly, slowly, they brightened — fed now on woodsmoke and starlight and the simple animal warmth of living bodies gathered close.

Sable came and sat beside Tamsin without asking, which was, from Old Sable, a knighthood.

"You'll want to take him down to the valley," she said. It wasn't a question.

"In spring. When the passes clear."

"Mm." Sable produced a flask from somewhere in her coats and pressed it into Tamsin's hands. "Vane's Hollow way. The south road's washed out, but my people know the goat tracks. I'll send Rill with you." She watched the fire for a moment. "Routes are changing. Did you know that? Twenty years I've run contraband through Thornfell, and last night my best runners stood in a collapsing hellhole carrying souls like sacks of flour, and not one of them complained." Her voice was dry as always, but something underneath it had shifted, tectonic. "Turns out we were never smugglers. We were ferrymen who forgot the river."

Across the fire, Edmund sat apart, as he always did. He was looking at his hands again — turning them over, studying them, the way a man studies a house after a fire. Tamsin caught his eye. He didn't smile, exactly. But he nodded to her, once, slow and deliberate, the nod of one survivor acknowledging another across a battlefield, and she understood that whatever he had lost in the dark below, he did not regret the price.

Wren fell asleep against her shoulder before the sun cleared the eastern peaks. Around them the company settled into exhausted silence, and the stars faded one by one into the blue morning, going home.

Tamsin stayed awake a little longer, watching the light come into the world.

Then, far off down the southern slopes — where the valley waited, where the farm she'd fled seven winters ago stood empty under new snow — she saw movement. Small figures on the goat track, climbing toward them. Too many to be wolves. Too steady to be lost travelers.

Rill was already on his feet, shading his eyes. "That's odd," he said. "Nobody uses that road in winter."

The figures raised something as they came — cloth, on poles. Pale banners, catching the dawn.

Tamsin's hand found Wren's sleeping shoulder, and held on.
