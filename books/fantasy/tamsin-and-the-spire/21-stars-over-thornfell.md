## 21. Stars Over Thornfell

The first star came back while they were still climbing.

Edmund saw it through a gap in the drowned stair's mouth — a single point of white above the black teeth of the crags, so small and so ordinary that for a moment he thought his eyes had made it out of longing. He stopped on the wet stone with the water rising around his boots and stared at it the way a man stares at a face in a crowd that he has spent twenty years pretending not to look for.

"There," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant it to.

Tamsin looked up. Wren was asleep against her shoulder — truly asleep, not the hollow drifting of the freed souls below, but the heavy boneless sleep of a boy who had waited too long in the dark and finally been told it was over. She shifted him carefully, one arm under his knees, and followed Edmund's gaze upward.

"It's small," she said.

"They all are." Edmund found he was smiling, and did not know what to do about it. "That's how you know they're real."

The second star came an hour later, when they reached the shelf of rock where the stair gave up its last claim on them and opened onto the high pass. The third came while Sable's smugglers were hauling the weakest of the freed souls up the final pitch, slinging them across shoulders like sacks of grain, except that these sacks weighed nothing at all and wept quietly into their carriers' collars. By the time the company stood on open stone beneath the open sky, there were eleven stars, then nineteen, then more than Edmund could count without stopping to try.

They came back the way a fever breaks. Not all at once — the sky seemed to remember them reluctantly, one bright stitch at a time, as though something vast were being unpicked and resewn by a careful hand. Between each new light there was a pause, a held breath in the dark, and then the small cold flare of another point of certainty.

Edmund sat down on a boulder at the edge of the camp Sable's people were making and watched until his neck ached.

He had not realized, until this moment, how much of his life had been spent under a sky he trusted. Twenty years of guiding clients through Thornfell's passes, and every night of those years the stars had been there — indifferent, yes, distant, certainly, but *there*, the fixed furniture of a world that could be relied upon to keep its promises. When they had gone out three weeks ago, swallowed whole along with the border wards, Edmund had felt something shift in the deep architecture of himself, some load-bearing wall cracking. He had told Tamsin it was nothing. He had told himself it was nothing.

It had not been nothing. A sky without stars was a room with no doors. He had walked through those three weeks of darkness feeling the walls of the world pressing closer every day, and only now, watching the lights return one by one, did he understand how tightly he had been holding himself.

"You're doing it again," Tamsin said.

She had settled Wren near the fire Sable's people had coaxed from damp wood — a real fire, fed with real resinous pine, crackling and spitting and throwing honest heat. The boy slept on. Tamsin lowered herself onto the boulder beside Edmund, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and followed his eyes upward.

"Doing what?"

"Counting them like you're afraid they'll stop."

"I am counting them like I'm afraid they'll stop."

"Mm." She pulled her cloak tighter. Below them, the mountain was quiet in a way it had not been quiet in seven years — or perhaps in a way it had never been quiet, since the Spire's hunger had been eating sound as well as light, drawing everything down into itself like a drain. Now the wind moved through the passes with its old voice. Somewhere below, meltwater ran in a channel that had been dry since autumn. The world was filling back in.

"Does it hurt?" Tamsin asked. "Your hands?"

Edmund looked at them. They lay in his lap, scarred and weather-browned, ordinary as any guide's hands on any road. For twenty years they had carried the storm — a low hum in the bones, a pressure behind the knuckles, the constant awareness of the weight of water hanging in the air and the charge sleeping in the clouds. Even in stillness, even in exile, the gift had been there, the way a man who has once broken a bone always knows the weather in the mending.

Now there was nothing. He flexed his fingers and felt — skin, tendon, the faint ache of cold. Nothing else. The silence where the storms had lived was not empty exactly; it was *quiet*, the way a house is quiet after the last guest has gone, full of the shapes of what had been there.

"No," he said. "It doesn't hurt. That's the strange part."

* * *

Sable found him an hour before dawn, when most of the company had finally surrendered to sleep and the stars overhead had grown from a scattering into a river — the whole spilled breadth of them, the River of the Hunter and the Weeping Sisters and the pale smear of the Gate, all of it returned, all of it burning.

She lowered herself onto the rock beside him with the groan of a woman whose joints had opinions, and handed him a tin cup of something hot and bitter. Smuggler's tea. It tasted like bark and forgiveness.

"You knew," Edmund said. "In Hollowmere. When you refused us. You'd already decided you were coming."

Old Sable blew on her tea. In the firelight her face was a map of every hard winter the valley had ever produced. "I knew no such thing. I knew you'd get yourself killed, and I knew I'd have to decide whether to mourn you or follow you, and I didn't care for either option." She sipped. "So I chose the third one. There's always a third one, Coldharbour. That's the first law of the roads, and you never learned it, because you're a man who thinks rules are a substitute for choices."

"That's unfair."

"It's entirely fair, which is why it stings." She nodded toward Wren, curled against Tamsin's side by the fire. "The boy will live?"

"He'll live. Whether he'll be—" Edmund turned the cup in his hands. "He doesn't remember being sold. He remembers waiting. Seven years of waiting, and he can't say for what. That's what the Spire leaves you, when it lets anything go. The shape of the wound without the story of it."

"And the girl?"

"She'll carry both halves. Her own guilt and his absence. She'll spend years learning which parts of him are real and which ones she invented to survive the selling." Edmund drank. "I know something about that arithmetic."

Sable said nothing for a while. The fire popped. Above them the sky went on quietly proving itself.

"The routes," she said at last. "I've been thinking. Forty years I've run contraband through these mountains — salt, steel, secrets, whatever paid. And in forty years I have watched this valley bleed people into the dark and called it weather. Called it the cost of business." Her jaw worked. "There are other souls down there besides the ones we brought up. The Spire kept ledgers, and ledgers have copies, and copies have locations. Some of my people are already talking about going back down for them."

"Through the drowned stair? It'll be flooded for months."

"We're smugglers, boy. Water is a toll, not a wall." She finished her tea and stood, joints cracking like green wood. "I'm telling you because you know the passes better than anyone breathing, and because you owe me, and because—" she paused, and for a moment the iron of her bent, just slightly, "—because I'm old, and I'd rather do this last thing right than die rich. Think on it."

She shuffled off toward the fire, leaving Edmund alone with the stars and the strange new silence in his hands.

* * *

Dawn came up gold behind the eastern crags, and Edmund watched it the way he had once watched storms build — with his whole attention, reading the light the way he used to read pressure and cloud. But there was nothing to read now. The dawn was simply a dawn, arriving on schedule, asking nothing of him, owing him nothing. The sun cleared the ridge and laid long shadows across the scree, and the frost on the stones caught fire all at once, ten thousand small diamonds, and Edmund Coldharbour sat in the middle of it with empty hands and felt —

He searched for the word for a long time.

Grief was part of it. He would not pretend otherwise. The gift had been his longest companion — longer than Sable, longer than Corvane, longer than any friend or enemy. It had been with him before he had words for it: the four-year-old in his mother's kitchen making the rain tap the window in patterns, delighted, unafraid. It had been with him through the training, through the wars he would not fight, through the stripping of his name and the long gray years of neutrality. Losing it was losing the person he had been longest. There was a boy who could call lightning standing somewhere inside him forever out of reach now, and Edmund intended to mourn that boy properly, in his own time, the way you mourn anyone you loved.

But grief was not all of it. That was the discovery.

Because the gift had also been a debt. Every storm he had ever called had come with a question attached — *for whom, for what, at whose cost* — and for twenty years he had answered that question by refusing to answer it, keeping the power holstered, calling it peace. It had never been peace. It had been a held breath. And now the breath had gone out of him, and what remained was not emptiness.

What remained was choice. Finished, irreversible, his.

He had spent the storm the way you spend blood — all of it, gladly, into the ledger vault, into the resonant chorus of caged souls, into the shattering of every written name. And the storm was gone, and the magic was gone, and the silence where they had lived was the silence of a man who has finally put down a weight he forgot he was carrying. His arms ached with the ghost of it. His shoulders sat differently. He kept reaching for the hum and finding morning instead — birdsong, meltwater, the murmur of freed souls waking by the fire, Tamsin's low voice as she talked softly to her brother.

Bearable. More than bearable. He turned the word over and found it true.

"You're awake early."

Wren stood at the edge of the firelight, wrapped in a blanket one of Sable's people had given him, blinking at the sunrise like a creature seeing it for the first time — which, Edmund supposed, in every way that mattered, he was. Seven years underground. Seven years of caged light and ledger-dark. The boy's face was thin and older than his years and younger than his body, all at once, and his eyes had the careful watchfulness of someone relearning that things might stay where you left them.

"I'm always awake early," Edmund said. "Habit. Roads don't wait for late risers."

"Tamsin says you're a guide."

"I was."

"What are you now?"

Edmund considered the question seriously, because the boy had asked it seriously. He looked at his hands. He looked at the mountains, washed clean and sharp in the new light, every pass and shelf and hidden stair laid bare as if the winter itself had been scrubbed away. He looked at the smoke rising from the smugglers' fire, and at Old Sable arguing cheerfully with two of her people over a map spread on a flat stone, and at Tamsin Vane sitting up beside her brother with her hair full of straw and her eyes red-rimmed and her spine straight as a blade.

"I don't know yet," he said. "Ask me in spring."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and went to sit by his sister. She put her arm around him without waking fully, the way you hold something you have checked on a thousand times in the night and will check on a thousand times more, and Edmund looked away to give them the privacy of it.

The sun climbed. The stars faded one by one, giving way — not dying, he reminded himself, just yielding the stage, the way they always had and always would. The last one to go was the first one that had returned, that small stubborn point above the eastern crags, and Edmund watched it go with something that was not quite sadness and not quite gratitude and was, he decided, simply the feeling of a man watching the world keep its promises again.

He got up. He brushed the frost from his coat. And he walked down toward the fire to see what the roads would ask of him next — empty-handed, unhurried, and for the first time in twenty years, entirely free to answer.
