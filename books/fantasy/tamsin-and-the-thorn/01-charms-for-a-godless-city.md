## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The fog came off the harbor at dawn the way it always did in Corvane — slow, gray, smelling of brine and tar and the particular sourness of a city that had learned to stop hoping. Bram Vasquez stood at her stall in the Saltmarket with her hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea gone cold, and watched the smugglers' quarter wake up around her like an old dog stiff from sleep.

She had been there eleven years. Long enough that the fishwives called her by name without warmth or malice — *Vasquez, have you the salve?* — long enough that the boy who swept the gutters knew to leave her step clear of muck, long enough that no one asked where she'd come from or why she never spoke of before. In Corvane, in the quarter where cargo moved without ledgers and names changed like shirts, nobody's history was anyone's business. That was the whole religion of the place: mind your own, keep your head down, pay the watch when you must and bribe them when you can.

Bram had made her peace with it. She told herself that every morning, tying on her apron, arranging her wares in their careful rows. She had made her peace.

Her stall was modest even by Saltmarket standards: a plank table on trestles under an awning of patched sailcloth, a hand-painted sign reading *VASQUEZ — SALVES, KNOTS, REMEDIES* in letters she'd done herself, deliberately clumsy. No charms. No wards. No *magic*, gods forbid, because Corvane had hanged its last hedge-witch nine years ago from the customs-house beam, and the Lord-Regent's edicts were read aloud at every market day: *divine manifestation is treason; sorcery is sedition; prayer is permitted only in silence and only to nothing.*

So she sold fever-salves made of goosefat and meadowsweet, which worked because meadowsweet worked, and only a little because she hummed over the kettle while it rendered, a tuneless lullaby her hands remembered though her mouth had forgotten the words. She sold luck-knots braided from three colors of cord — red for blood, blue for water, gold for whatever you were short of — knotted in patterns that meant nothing to anyone who didn't know, and she charged two coppers apiece and swore up and down they were only cord.

They were not only cord. But they were small. That was the bargain she had struck with herself across twenty years of running: magic in thimbles, never in buckets. Enough to ease a birth, enough to turn a fever, enough to make a fisherman's nets hang heavy one more season. Never enough to be seen. Never enough to matter.

Smallness was safe. Smallness was almost happiness.

"Vasquez." Old Maren the netmender loomed out of the fog with her basket of mending and her face like a folded sail. "You've the salve? The little one's coughing again."

"For the little one I'll give you the good jar." Bram reached beneath the table for the crock she kept back from the display — the one with a whisper more of the sweet herb, a whisper less of the cheap filler. "Tell her mother to rub it on the chest and throat, warm, not hot, and to keep the window shut against the river damp."

"You're too generous," Maren said, in the tone of a woman who intended to say so and pay anyway, and slid three coppers across the plank instead of two.

"The window," Bram said, taking two and pushing one back. "Damp gets into small lungs and makes itself at home."

Maren grunted and went, and Bram watched her go with something that wasn't quite fondness but lived next door to it. This was the shape of her life now. Jars and knots and coppers, the fog burning off toward midmorning, the gulls screaming their idiot arguments overhead. A woman of forty-one with gray coming in early at her temples and calluses on her palms from work that was honest, or honest enough. Tamsin would have laughed at this stall. Tamsin would have said —

She did not finish the thought. Twenty years of practice had taught her how to set those thoughts down gently, the way you'd set down a hot iron, and walk away before they burned.

* * *

Midmorning brought the crowd, such as it was. The Saltmarket never emptied entirely — smugglers' quarters ran on odd hours, ships coming in on tides that ignored the law as cheerfully as their captains did — but between the dawn fish sales and the afternoon tavern trade there was a stretch of quiet when the light came slantwise down Rope Walk and turned the puddles to hammered silver.

It was during this quiet that the girl came.

Bram noticed her first as a hesitation at the edge of the awning's shadow — a thin figure, maybe sixteen, wearing the uniform gray of a laundry-crew drudge, hands red from lye water, standing as if she'd forgotten what feet were for. Bram had seen that look before. It was the look of someone carrying news too large for their body.

"You're the salve woman," the girl said. Not quite a question.

"I'm the salve woman."

"They say—" The girl stopped. Started again, lower, glancing at the passersby. "They say you're lucky. Your knots. My da bought one last spring, before the *Kestrel* sailed, and he came home, and the *Kestrel* didn't."

Ah, thought Bram. Here it is. The thing she both dreaded and depended on: grief, looking for somewhere to put itself.

"Cord," she said gently. "It's cord, love. Your da came home because he's a good sailor, or a lucky man, or because the sea hadn't finished with him. The knot gave him something to hold while he waited. That's worth two coppers, but it isn't more than cord."

"My mam's sick." The words came out flat and fast, the way stones fall. "Fever. Three days. The physicker wants six marks to come and he wants it *before* he comes, and we haven't got six marks, we haven't got six *coppers* after the rent, and Nan said — Nan said come see the knot woman, because when Auntie Dellie had the birthing fever and everyone said she'd die, she bought a jar off you and she didn't die—"

"That was the physicker's tincture," Bram said, "and your auntie's own stubbornness."

But even as she said it, her hands were already moving, lifting the lid of the remedy box, and she hated herself a little for how easy the motion was, how practiced — the whole choreography of denial and charity she'd built over eleven years, the dance of giving without seeming to give. Because the truth, the truth she kept in the same locked cellar as everything else, was that her salves worked better than meadowsweet alone could account for, and the knots held a thread of something real, and every soul in the Saltmarket half-knew it and agreed not to know it, because in the smugglers' quarter a useful lie about cord was worth more than a dangerous truth about witchcraft.

"Feverfew and willow-bark steeped in the salve base," she said aloud, all business. "Rub it into the soles of the feet and along the spine every four hours. Cool cloths on the wrists. If she'll drink it, willow tea, bitter as sin but it brings the heat down." She pressed the jar into the girl's chapped hands and closed the girl's fingers over it. "Two coppers."

"We haven't—"

"Then you owe me two coppers, and I'll collect when your mam's up and complaining about the price of bread. Go on."

The girl looked at her with an expression Bram recognized from mirrors she'd stopped owning decades ago — that raw, disbelieving gratitude of someone who has just been handed mercy in a city that priced everything — and then she was gone, running, jar clutched to her chest like an infant.

Bram sat back down on her stool and drank her cold tea and told herself the familiar lie: *it was the herbs. It was only ever the herbs.*

Under the table, hidden in the lining of her satchel where no one had ever looked, something old and round and heavy lay silent as it had lain silent for twenty years.

Mostly silent.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble in Corvane usually did, with a ship.

The *Gilded Mercy* came in on the evening tide flying no colors at all, which in the Saltmarket was its own kind of flag, and by full dark the taverns along Rope Walk were doing the roaring business that meant cargo was moving and coin was loose. Bram had packed up her stall at dusk as always — she did not work the night markets, had never worked them, another small discipline, another wall between herself and attention — and was climbing the outside stair to her rooms above the chandler's shop when she heard the screaming start.

Not the ordinary screaming. Corvane had a whole vocabulary of screams — drunk, amorous, robbed, arrested — and after eleven years she could sort them the way a sailor sorts winds. This was none of those. This was the scream of people seeing something they had no shelf in their minds to store.

She froze on the stair, one hand on the rail, and looked north toward the harbor square.

Light was coming out of the *Mercy*'s hold. Not lantern-light. Not fire. It rose through the deck boards and the open hatch like water finding its level, gold-white and utterly still, and it made no shadows, and every gull in the harbor went up at once, a white ragged sheet of them against the dark.

And then — Bram's breath left her body — then came the voice.

It did not come from any direction. It came from underneath things, from the ground and the water and the bones of the city, vast and slow and patient as a tide, and it was not words, exactly, but it carried meaning the way deep water carries cold:

***Answered.***

One word. One word that fell over the Saltmarket like a dropped bell, and Bram felt it go through her teeth, her breastbone, the old scar-tissue of her life, and somewhere below her feet, below the floorboards of her rented room, in the cellar she was forbidden by her lease even to enter—

Something woke up.

She felt it the way you feel a struck bell through a wall: a single deep pulse of heat and recognition, ancient and enormous and *hers*, calling a name she had buried in a hollow in the eastern hills two decades ago. *Bram.* Not Vasquez. The other name. The true one. The one stitched into that stone by hands long dead, the covenant-name, the heir-mark—

"No," she said aloud, to the night, to the stone, to whatever god had just spoken through a smuggler's hull. "No, no, no."

Below her, in the cellar, the pulse came again, stronger, and this time it was not a feeling but a light — she could see it leaking up through the seams of the cellar doors across the yard, a thin gold line glowing in the chandler's dark, brightening, brightening—

And all around the harbor square, the crowd that had gathered to gawk at the burning ship fell suddenly, terribly silent, and then began, all together, to kneel.

Bram did not stay to see more. She was down the stairs and across the yard before her mind caught up with her body, wrenching open the cellar doors, and the light that poured out was the light of twenty years ago, of a cottage hearth and a woman's stern kind face, of everything she had run from and paid eleven years of smallness to escape — and there on the dirt floor, half-unearthed where the stone had shoved its own grave aside, lay the covenant-stone, blazing like a captive star, and across her right palm, searing itself letter by letter in fire that did not burn flesh but burned everything else—

Her name.

The true one.

She heard the boots then — many boots, and the flat official voices of the watch, and beneath them a colder voice giving orders in the accent of the capital, the accent of the Lord-Regent's inquisitors — and she understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only at the exact moment it is useless, that the light from her cellar was visible from the street, and that the past she had buried had not waited politely to be dug up.

It had found her first.

She snatched the stone from the dirt — it should have weighed as much as an anvil, it weighed nothing, it warmed her hands like a living thing — and ran.
