## 2. The Stone That Wakes

The stone had been quiet for twenty years, and Bram had made a religion of not thinking about it.

Not a true religion — she'd seen what happened to those lately — but the small domestic kind: the ritual of descending the cellar stairs each morning with her lamp held high, of glancing at the corner beneath the root shelf where the floorboards were newer than the rest, of telling herself *still there, still buried, still mine to carry* — and then climbing back into the grey light of Corvane and selling fever-salves to people who would burn her if they knew what slept under their feet.

She told herself it was duty. She told herself it was penance. Mostly it was habit, and habit was the only prayer the city allowed anymore.

That morning in late autumn, when she lifted the trapdoor and the smell of cold earth rose to meet her — loam and old turnips and the mineral ghost of stone — something was different. She couldn't have said what. The lamp flame leaned toward the corner as if drawn by breath. The dark down there felt *attentive*, the way a room feels when someone has been standing in it a long time.

Bram stood on the top stair for the length of ten heartbeats, telling herself it was rats.

Then she went down anyway, because she had spent twenty years learning that fear ignored grows teeth, and because some stubborn, buried part of her — the part that had once been Tamsin's apprentice, that had once known every herb by its night-name and every star by its hunger — wanted to look.

The floorboards over the stone were warm.

She knelt. Pressed her palm flat against them. Not warm the way a hearth is warm, or a body. Warm the way a hand is warm — as though something beneath the boards had a pulse and was pressing back.

"No," she said aloud, to the cellar, to the stone, to whatever god or memory was listening. "No, you're dead. I buried you. I buried all of it."

The boards did not answer. But the warmth spread, slow as spilled honey, from beneath her palm up through her wrist, her arm, her shoulder, until it sat behind her breastbone like a second heart learning its rhythm. And in that moment Bram understood with terrible clarity that the stone had never been asleep at all. It had been waiting. There is a difference between a thing that sleeps and a thing that waits, and the difference is patience, and patience is the oldest magic there is.

She should have run then. She knew the streets of Corvane like the lines of her own palm; she could have been through the smugglers' gate before the bells rang terce, out onto the mudflats, gone. Twenty years ago she had run from a cottage in a thorn-choked hollow with a stolen stone against her ribs and fire behind her, and running was the one skill she had never let rust.

Instead she did the worst possible thing. She pried up the board.

* * *

The covenant-stone lay where she had left it, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with three iron nails driven through the knots — Tamsin's binding, copied from memory, imperfect, desperate. It was smaller than she remembered, no bigger than a loaf of bread, an oval of grey river-stone shot through with veins of white quartz. Unremarkable. A farmer's field-stone. Except that the quartz veins were moving.

Not flowing. *Brightening.* Light traveled along them the way dawn travels along a mountain ridge — slowly, inevitably, from one end to the other, and where it passed the grey of the stone warmed to gold, and the gold was not reflected lamplight but light of its own, light with weight, light that made the shadows in the cellar lean away from it like startled animals.

Bram's mouth had gone dry. Her hands, hovering above it, trembled so badly she clasped them together to still them.

"I stole you," she whispered. "I know what I did. Whatever you want, it isn't me. Find someone else. Find anyone else."

The stone brightened.

And her right palm began to burn.

It started as heat, deep in the meat below her fingers, and then became lines — she watched them draw themselves across her skin in fine black threads, precise as a scribe's work, painless and yet somehow worse than pain, as if her own body were being written in without her consent. She turned her hand toward the lamp and saw the sigil take shape: a circle broken at the north point, crossed by three descending strokes, with a small thorn-hook at the center.

Her mark. The mark she had burned into luck-knots and salve-jars for twenty years, disguised as a maker's stamp, hidden in plain sight on a hundred thousand small charms sold to a hundred thousand godless customers.

Except this was not her mark. This was the mark's *original*, the seal of the stewardship, the thing she had copied all those years ago without understanding she was copying her own inheritance. And beneath it, threading through the black lines like roots through soil, new characters were surfacing — old script, pre-Imperial, the letters Tamsin had taught her to read by candlelight when she was nine years old and still believed love was a simple country.

They spelled her name. Not *Bram*. The other name. The name Tamsin had given her in the old tongue on the night of her binding, the name meaning *she who stands between*, the name she had not spoken aloud in two decades because speaking it felt like opening a door she had nailed shut.

The stone went dark. The cellar went ordinary. The boards overhead creaked with the footsteps of Marda the fishwife, who rented the rooms above and complained about the damp and knew nothing of what lived beneath her kitchen.

Bram sat back on her heels in the cold dirt and looked at her burning palm and understood that everything she had built — the stall, the salves, the careful smallness, the peace she had told herself she'd made — was already ash. It just hadn't finished falling yet.

Because the stone waking meant one thing, only one thing, and she had known it since she was a child memorizing covenant-law by firelight:

*The stone wakes when the pact stirs. The pact stirs when the gods return. And when the gods return, they come looking for their steward.*

The gods were answering prayers again. She had heard it herself, two days ago, in the market — old Mother Cass praying over her grandson's fever, and the fever breaking between one word and the next, and the vast attentive silence afterward, the whole street holding its breath around a miracle. Bram had told herself it was coincidence. Coincidence, and the way the air had seemed to bow, and the way every candle within thirty feet had flared at once.

She had lied to herself the way she always lied to herself: thoroughly, and with craft.

Now her own hand called her a liar in letters of old script, and somewhere beyond the city walls something enormous had opened its eyes and begun to remember her.

* * *

She worked the afternoon stall with shaking hands.

Luck-knots in red and grey wool. Fever-salves in clay pots stamped with the sigil — her sigil, which now lived on her skin, which now could never be disguised again. She smiled at customers. She made change. She kept her right hand curled closed like a fist around its secret, and every time she flexed the fingers the black lines pulled at the skin, tender as a fresh scald.

"You're pale today, mistress," said the cooper's wife, counting out coppers for a knot against colic. "Coming down with something?"

"Just the season turning," Bram said. Her voice came out level. Twenty years of practice will do that. "Wear it against your husband's chest. Change the wool at the new moon."

The woman bustled off, blessed her without thinking about it — a habit of speech, empty as a dropped glove — and Bram watched her go and thought: *she prays without knowing it. They all do. And something out there has started listening again.*

By dusk the light had gone the color of tarnished brass, and the smugglers' quarter lit itself in the usual way: tallow dips in windows, cook-smoke standing straight in the windless cold, the taverns coughing out fiddle-music and argument. Bram packed her unsold wares, folded her awning, and was reaching for the latch of her shutters when she heard the horses.

Not cart-horses. Warhorses — shod heavy, moving slow, in formation. In the smugglers' quarter, where the streets were too narrow for carts and the cobbles were more gap than stone, that sound was wrong the way a knife in a bread-basket is wrong.

Then the bells. Not the hour-bells. The alarm bell at the customs tower, ringing fast and ragged, and beneath it a voice amplified by some charm or horn, flat and official and terrible:

*"By edict of the Lord-Regent: this quarter is sealed for inquiry. Remain in your dwellings. Open to the King's inquisitors upon demand. Concealment of witchcraft is death."*

Bram stood in her doorway with her hand on the latch and felt the past arrive like weather.

*Inquisitors.* The word tasted of smoke. She had been eleven years old the first time Tamsin spoke it, had learned to douse a fire in three seconds and bury ash under moss and mask a working's scent with juniper smoke, all the small survival-craft of a hedge-witch in a kingdom turning hostile. Then the purges had faded, the edicts had gathered dust, the usurper Casimir had settled onto his stolen throne and found other things to fear, and the hedge-witches had crept back toward their candles. Bram had crept furthest of all — all the way to nothing, to salt-salves and wool knots, to a magic so diluted it was barely a lie.

But the stone had woken. And if the stone's waking rang any bell in the world — and it would, it must, such things rang through wards and dreams and the bones of sensitive men — then the inquisitors would come looking, and they would come here first, because the stone had woken *here*.

She could run. The thought arrived calm and reasonable, dressed in twenty years of practice. Out the back, over the wall, along the drainage channel to the mudflats—

Down the street, torchlight bloomed.

There were perhaps forty households in the close, huddled between the tannery and the customs wall, and the inquisitors moved through them with the unhurried efficiency of men doing familiar work. Six riders first, sealing the lanes. Then the footmen in grey-and-black, torches high, and among them — Bram's stomach clenched — the *readers*: thin men in cowled robes carrying instruments she remembered from her childhood nightmares, brass rings and bone needles and censers burning a resin that smelled of myrrh cut with something sharper, something that made the air itself flinch.

Magic left residue. Everyone knew that now; it was the first lesson of the new catechism. Residue could be read. And Bram's cellar, tonight of all nights, was singing with it.

She blew out her lamp. She pressed herself into the shadow beside her door, and she watched, because watching was the price of knowing, and knowing was the price of surviving.

They took Old Ferren first, the eel-catcher, whose only crime was a shrine to the river-god he'd kept secretly in his boathouse for forty years — a bowl of river-water and a carved fish older than the usurper's grandfather. They carried the bowl out into the street and smashed it on the cobbles, and the water, when it spilled, did not splash. It *rose*, briefly, a silver ribbon hanging in the torchlight, shaped for one impossible instant like a reaching hand — and then fell, and soaked into the stones, and was gone.

The readers went very still. One of them began writing rapidly.

"Manifestation," the reader said, and his voice shook, and Bram could not tell whether it shook with fear or with something hungrier. "Confirmed manifestation. Here. In the quarter."

The officer in charge — hard-faced, young, wearing the black sash of the Inquisitory over his mail — looked at the wet cobbles for a long moment.

"Sweep the houses," he said. "All of them."

* * *

They reached Bram's door near midnight.

She had prepared as much as she could in the hours given her: the stone reburied, the boards replaced, a layer of dried turnips scattered over the fresh-cut wood as camouflage. The sigil on her palm she had covered with a salve-stained rag, bound like a burn — plausible; she was a salve-maker; burns were her trade. She had scrubbed the cellar stairs, swept away the scuff of her kneeling, burned juniper in the hearth until the room wept sharp clean smoke.

When the knock came — three blows, measured, patient — she counted to five and opened the door with a sleep-drunk face she had practiced in a polished bronze mirror for twenty years.

Two soldiers. Behind them, a reader.

He was younger than she expected, soft-handed, with ink stains on his fingers and the myrrh-censer swinging from his belt. He looked past her into the single room — the bed, the hearth, the shelves of clay pots, the bundles of wool — and his nostrils flared.

"Salve-maker," he read from a slate. "Vasquez. You are registered?"

"Twenty-two years," Bram said. "Corvane guild of apothecaries. I can show the mark."

"Show your hands."

The world narrowed to a point. Somewhere outside, another door splintered; a woman began to scream, then stopped abruptly, which was worse than screaming. Bram held out her left hand, open. Then, slowly, letting the tremble show — let it show, let him see fear, fear was innocent — she unwound the rag from her right.

The sigil stared up from her palm, black and fine and unmistakable.

The reader bent close. His censer swung. He did not touch her; readers never touched, that was doctrine, contamination ran both ways. His eyes tracked the broken circle, the three strokes, the thorn-hook.

"A maker's stamp," Bram said, before he could speak. "Guild-standard. Luck-charms, fevers, birthing knots." She recited it the way she had recited it a thousand times to a thousand customers, bored and honest and small. "You'll find it on every pot I've sold since the guild licensed me. Ask the guild-master. Ask anyone in the quarter."

The reader's lips moved slightly, counting strokes, comparing some internal template. The silence stretched. In it, Bram heard her own heartbeat, and beneath it — faint, rising from the floorboards beneath her feet, from the cellar, from the buried stone — a pulse like a second heartbeat answering hers, and she prayed with genuine desperation to whatever vast thing had recently resumed listening: *not now, be quiet, be dead, be anything but awake.*

The reader straightened. Something flickered across his face — doubt, or disappointment, or the particular frustration of a man whose instruments tell him *yes* while his eyes tell him *nothing provable*.

"The residue in this house," he said carefully, "is consistent with licensed charm-work. Nothing more." He marked his slate. "You will remain indoors until the sweep concludes. If you recall anything unusual — lights, voices, answered prayers — report to the customs tower."

"Of course," Bram said.

They moved on. The door closed. Bram stood with her back against it and slid down until she was sitting on the cold flags, the rag crushed in her fist, breathing the juniper-sharp air in ragged pulls.

Through the shutter-slats she watched the rest of it. They dragged three neighbors into the street — Ferren among them, bleeding from the mouth, and the cooper's son, and a widow named Anthe who had once bought a luck-knot from Bram every season for her grandchildren. The readers questioned them by torchlight. The answers satisfied no one. Near the end, the young officer stood over Old Ferren, who knelt in the filth of his own blood, and asked him a question the wind tore away — and Ferren, the old man, the eel-catcher, the keeper of a bowl of river-water, lifted his ruined face and said something back, loud enough to carry:

"She's real. The Lady of the River. She *answered* me. Forty years I prayed and last week she answered, and you can break every bowl in Corvane and she'll still—"

The butt of a spear ended it. They loaded the prisoners into a cart — not to the city gaol, Bram noted, but eastward, toward the Inquisitory's tower beyond the walls, from which fewer people returned than entered — and the torches withdrew, and the bells fell silent, and the quarter exhaled into the particular silence that follows violence: not peace, just the absence of noise, like the pause after a blow.

Bram stayed on the floor a long time.

Her palm throbbed. Beneath the floorboards, patient as ever, the stone pulsed its slow gold attention, and she understood at last that hiding was over — not because the inquisitors had found her, they hadn't, not quite, but because the thing she hid had decided to stop being hidden. The stone had waited twenty years for the pact to stir, and now the pact stirred, and the stone would keep waking, brighter each night, louder each day, until every reader in Corvane came running with his censer, until the whole machinery of the usurper's fear ground its way down her street and through her door.

*Run,* said the part of her that had survived this long.

*Return,* said the part of her that was written on her hand.

Outside, far off toward the river, a beggar's voice rose in the dark — thin, cracked, singing something old, something from before the edicts, a hymn to Vesh-of-the-Crossroads that Bram hadn't heard since she was a child at Tamsin's knee. The candles in her hearth, banked to embers, flared all at once, every one, though there was no draft.

Bram looked at her marked palm in their sudden light, and the old script had not faded, and the name it spelled was hers.

She began, quietly, to pack.
