## 3. Inquisitor Hale

The rain had not stopped since the raid began, and by the time the last of the smugglers' quarter was quiet, it had washed the soot from the burned house into long grey runnels that ran down the cobbles like veins. Corvane's gutters had always run strange colors — tallow, dye, worse — but this was different. This looked like the street itself was bleeding out something old.

Edmund Hale stood in the doorway of what had been a luck-seller's shop and let the rain fall on his bare head. He did not wear his hat indoors of ruins. It was a small piety, unremarked, but he kept it. The dead deserved to see your face.

The house had been a witch's den; there was no doubt of that now. His men had pulled forty luck-knots from beneath the floorboards, each one braided with a precision no mortal fingers could sustain through a full night's work, each one humming faintly against the palm like a trapped moth. They had found fever-salves that healed in hours what should have taken weeks. They had found a cellar, sealed and swept, empty except for a circle of clean stone in the middle of the floor where the dust refused to settle — as though something had lain there for years, pressing the air flat around itself, and had recently been taken up.

Or had woken and left.

Hale crouched at the edge of that circle and did not touch it. Twenty-two years in the Office of Inquiry had taught him the grammar of such places: the way magic left a room was never silent. It left a shape, the way a body leaves an impression in a bed.

This shape was wrong. This shape was *fresh*.

"Ser." One of his men, Voss, young still, with the soft chin of a boy who'd joined the Office for the coat rather than the work. "We've got them gathered. The survivors."

"How many?"

"Eleven living out of the row. Three will need a physiker. The rest—" Voss hesitated. "They're praying, ser. Down on their knees in the muck, all of them, even the ones with broken arms. Praying to the old names. We can't make them stop."

Hale rose. Rain dripped from his jaw. "Don't make them stop," he said. "Listen to them. Write down every name they use."

Voss blinked. "Ser?"

"A god answered a prayer on this street three nights ago, Voss. Something vast, something loud enough that half the quarter heard it and the other half dreamed it. I want to know which name they called. Names are handles. You cannot catch a thing without its handle."

He said it evenly, because he always said things evenly; his voice had been sanded smooth by two decades of interrogations until nothing rough remained. But underneath the evenness, in the place where he kept the other thing — the older wound, the one that had made him — something leaned forward, hungry, and he hated it and obeyed it both.

*Three nights ago.* A fishwife named Odell had stood in her doorway and prayed aloud for her son, lost off the Greywater shoals, and the sky over Corvane had opened like a book, and something had answered her in a voice like a tide going out. Hale had been four streets away, and he had felt it pass through him the way cold passes through a wet coat. Every ward-charm on his person had flared and died at once, like candles snuffed by a draft from a door opening somewhere very far away.

The gods were answering again. After sixty years of silence. After the regency had built an empire on the certainty that heaven had abandoned mankind and only men need be governed.

And magic was waking with it. Starting here, in a smuggler's quarter, in a luck-seller's cellar.

*Starting*, he thought, looking again at the clean circle in the dust, *or returning.*

* * *

They held the survivors in the tithe-barn at the edge of the quarter, because the gaols were full and because Hale preferred barns: high rafters, good light, straw underfoot that recorded footsteps honestly. Eleven people sat in a row on benches, dripping, wrapped in the grey blankets his men had brought. None of them would look at him. All of them kept glancing toward the door, toward the rain, as if expecting the sky to come in after them.

Hale took his time. He removed his wet coat. He poured water from the jug into a basin and washed his hands slowly, thoroughly, letting them watch. Ritual mattered. Fear was a fire that needed feeding at intervals, and he was careful with his fires; careless fear made liars of everyone, and he could not afford liars tonight.

Then he sat on the stool across from the first of them and said, gently, "Tell me about the woman who sold you charms."

Her name was Maret Duncalfe, a net-mender, sixty-odd, hands like knotted rope. She wept almost immediately, which was common, and lied almost immediately, which was also common, and Hale listened to the lies with the patience of a man reading a familiar psalm. She had bought thread. She had bought buttons. The luck-knots? She had won them at cards. From whom? A stranger. A tall stranger. A short one. She couldn't recall.

"Mistress Duncalfe." He waited until her eyes came up. "I am not going to burn you."

The barn went very quiet. Even his own men stilled.

"I am going to write your name in my ledger as a witness, not a practitioner, provided you tell me the truth. I have no interest in the small magics. Knots and salves, charms against rheumatism — these are the folk-craft of every grandmother in Corvane, and the Lord-Regent has better uses for his fires than grandmothers." He let that sit. It was true, mostly, in the way that mattered. "What I want is the big thing. The thing that answered the fishwife's prayer. The thing that lived under the luck-seller's floor. Tell me about her."

"The luck-seller?" Maret's chapped hands twisted in the blanket. "Bram? Bram Vasquez?"

The name went through Hale like a hook through cloth.

"Tell me about Bram Vasquez."

"She's — she's nobody, ser, that's just it, she's nobody at all. Been on the lane six years, maybe seven. Sells her knots and her salves, keeps herself to herself. Never haggles. Never drinks. Never—" Maret faltered, searching for the crime, and finding none, grew frightened of the absence. "She mended my grandson's fever when the physikers gave him up. Didn't charge me. Said it was just a good poultice. But I held the boy's hand all that night, ser, and I'll swear on anything you like — the fever didn't break. It *left*. Like something came and carried it off."

"And did you pray?" Hale asked softly. "When the boy was sick. Did you pray to the old names, even though it's forbidden?"

Maret's face crumpled. "Everyone prays, ser. That's the dirty secret of the whole city, isn't it? Sixty years of edicts and every soul in Corvane still whispers to somebody when the night gets long enough." She wiped her nose. "But nothing ever answered. Not once, not in my whole life. Until three nights past. And now everything's waking up and I don't know whether to be glad or terrified, and you come asking about Bram, who never hurt a flea—"

"Where is she now?"

"Gone." The word came from the far end of the bench, from a dockhand with a bandaged arm, bitter and certain. "Gone before your soldiers even finished kicking doors. Nobody saw her leave. Nobody ever sees her leave. She's like smoke, that one. Kind smoke," he added, defiantly, "but smoke."

Hale wrote the name in his ledger. *Bram Vasquez.* Beneath it, after a moment, he wrote: *gone before the raid. Knew.*

A practitioner who could feel an inquiry coming. Who kept a cellar scrubbed of everything except the ghost-shape of something powerful enough to flatten dust. Whose charms hummed like live moths. Who had vanished into a city locked down by his own men within the hour.

Either she was the source of the awakening, Hale thought, or she was running from it. In his experience the two categories overlapped more often than the Office liked to admit.

He moved down the bench, and the stories accreted, as they always did, into a portrait drawn by twenty pairs of hands: the woman who never bargained, who paid her tithes early, who sat up with the dying for free, who once — this from a child of perhaps nine, brought in by mistake and released weeping — had stood in a thunderstorm with her arms out and *not gotten wet*. Everyone loved her. No one knew her. In eleven accounts, the same woman appeared and disappeared behind the same wall of small kindnesses, and not one witness could say where she slept, or what she wanted, or why a person with that much power had spent six years selling penny charms in a smugglers' quarter.

Power hiding as poverty. That was discipline. That was someone who had made a study of being overlooked.

Hale knew that discipline intimately. He had spent his life studying it, because he had met it once before, at fourteen years old, in a farm kitchen that smelled of blood and burnt rosemary, and he had never stopped meeting it in every dark room since.

* * *

Near midnight, when the survivors had been released with bread and warnings, Hale went back alone to the burned house. His men thought it compulsion. It was, but not the kind they imagined. He simply did not trust a scene read by lamplight. Scenes changed their testimony between dusk and dawn, the way witnesses did.

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. Inside the ruined shop, the embers under the collapsed stair still breathed a dull orange, and by that light he walked the rooms again, slowly, hands clasped behind his back, reading.

The scorch-mark was on the underside of the stair, low, near the string-board, exactly the height of a hand pressed flat. He had missed it earlier — or rather, he had seen it and filed it among the hundred ordinary burns a fire leaves. Now, in the ember-light, with the day's name written in his ledger, he knelt and looked at it properly.

It was not a random burn. Fire does not draw. Fire smears, blots, feathers outward in chance and draught. This mark had edges. Clean ones. A design the size of a spread hand, seared into the wood so deeply it had gone black through to char: a circle, and within the circle a thorned stem rising, and upon the stem a single closed bud — no, not a bud. He tilted his head. A knot. A stem tied into a knot that flowered. A luck-knot grown into a sigil, or a sigil that had always been a knot.

Around the outer circle ran a ring of fine marks, letters in some alphabet older than any script the Office taught, worn nearly smooth — not by age, Hale realized, but by *touch*. By years of thumbs tracing it. Someone had handled this sign the way the devout handle a saint's medal, thousands of times, until the grooves shone.

His own hand rose without his permission and hovered above it. The mark was cold. Colder than the room. When his fingertips came within a finger's width of the scorched circle, the hair on his forearm lifted, and somewhere behind his sternum the old wound turned over in its sleep.

He withdrew his hand. He took out his ledger and, with a stick of charcoal, made a careful rubbing of the sigil, pressing the page flat against the stair and working the charcoal in slow circles until the design bloomed onto the paper: circle, thorned stem, knotted flower, ring of worn letters.

He knew it. That was the thing that made his breath go shallow in the ember-dark. He had never seen this exact mark before in twenty-two years of inquiry — and yet he knew it, the way you know a face seen once in a crowd decades ago, the way you know a song heard through a wall in childhood. It scratched at a door in him that he had nailed shut at fourteen and re-nailed every year since.

*Willa,* said the thing behind the door. *It smells like Willa's room.*

His sister had been eleven. She had filled the corner of the loft they shared with dried flowers and knotted threads and little clay figures, and she had whispered to them at night, and he, eight years old and loyal, had kept her secret the way children keep secrets: proudly, carelessly, telling only the wrong person at the wrong time. The village priest. The year was the last of the Silence, when the gods had gone quiet and the Church of the Regency was teaching that all whispering was treason against a heaven that had already deserted them.

They had come for her at harvest. He remembered the rosemary smell most — his mother had been boiling rosemary for the cold that was going through the house — and then the other smell under it, and the men in grey coats, and Willa's hand small in his, gripping, believing her brother would fix it because brothers fixed things.

The official record said the girl's own workings had turned on her. Hale had believed that record for exactly as long as it took him to grow old enough to reread it. There had been no workings. There had been a child with knots and flowers and a brother's careless tongue, and there had been a fire, and the fire had been set by men carrying writs signed by the same Office he now served, and Edmund Hale had joined that Office at nineteen for one reason only: to find the thing that had actually killed his sister, the real magic, the true practitioner, the great hidden engine of it all — because if magic existed, and it did, he had *felt* it exist in that loft, then it had chosen to hide behind an eleven-year-old girl while men like him burned the girl instead.

He hunted it with grief. He had never pretended otherwise, not to himself. Grief was the only honest fuel he had, and he fed it carefully, nightly, the way a lamp-man trims his wicks.

And now a sigil of knotted thread and old letters was cooling on a stair in Corvane, and a woman made of small kindnesses had slipped a city-wide net as if she'd known its shape before it was cast, and the gods — silent through his sister's whole life, silent through her death, silent through sixty years of ash — had chosen *three days ago* to start answering prayers again.

The timing was not lost on him. It would never be lost on him. Everything he had suffered had happened inside the Silence, and everything waking now was waking too late, and he intended to know why, and he intended to know *who*, and the ledger under his arm held a name.

"Bram Vasquez," he said aloud, to the empty burned room, and the ember-light guttered as if the house had flinched.

He stood, pocketed the rubbing, and paused at the threshold to look back once — the habit of a man who had learned that scenes confess more to those who linger. The scorch-mark on the stair caught the last of the ember-glow, and for a moment, in the shifting light, the knotted flower at its center seemed less like a seal and more like a door seen from the wrong side.

Outside, the mist had thickened along the lane, and the rain had begun again, soft and steady, erasing footprints. Somewhere east of the city, beyond walls and checkpoints and the wide wet dark, a woman who mended fevers for free was moving through the night with a name freshly burned into her skin — though Hale did not know that yet, and would not for another week, until a shrine-keeper in Dunmarrow told him things that would crack his certainty down the middle like river ice in spring.

For now there was only the ledger, and the rubbing, and the hunt.

He put on his hat, tipped its brim against the rain, and walked out of the smugglers' quarter at a pace that never varied, toward the records office, toward sixty years of files, toward every archived case marked with knots and thorns and worn old letters.

Somewhere in the paper dark of the archives, he was certain, her name was waiting for him. It had been waiting a long time.

He meant to find out how long.
