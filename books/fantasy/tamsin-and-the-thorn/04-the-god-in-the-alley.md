## 4. The God in the Alley

Bram did not sleep that night.

She sat in the dark of her cellar with her back against the cold stone wall and the lamp unlit, because light felt like an invitation. Above her, through the floorboards, she could hear the smugglers' quarter settling into its uneasy rest: a cart rattling home late, a woman laughing too loudly at nothing, a dog barking itself hoarse at shadows on Salt Lane. Ordinary sounds. She counted them like beads on a string, and every so often her thumb found the burn on her palm and pressed it, as if pain could be persuaded to explain itself.

It would not. It only pulsed, slow and patient, in time with something far beneath the city — beneath the world, maybe — like a second heart buried under hers.

*Vasquez*, the stone had said. Or she had dreamed it said. *Vasquez, Vasquez.* Her mother's name before marriage; her grandmother's name; a name from a country she had never seen, carried west in the mouths of refugees until it wore smooth as river stone. A name Tamsin had spoken exactly once, on the day she'd drawn blood from Bram's finger and mixed it into mortar, and had never explained.

She should have thrown the thing in the harbor. She thought about it for a long time, sitting there, imagining the splash, the dark water closing over it, the way she had once imagined burying everything else. But her hands stayed folded in her lap, and the night wore on, and the stone kept its patient pulse, and somewhere above her the city went on pretending — as she had pretended for twenty years — that the gods were gone and would not be coming back.

Near dawn she heard the boots.

Not many. Four men, maybe five, moving with the particular care of people who have been told where to go and what to find. They came down Salt Lane and stopped, and there was the flat knock of a staff against a door, and then shouting, and then a sound Bram knew from her childhood and had hoped never to hear again: the crackle of white fire eating wood.

She climbed the ladder quietly and looked out through the gap in her shutters. Across the lane, old Marta's luck-charm shop was burning. Not burning properly — burning *wrong*, the flames white and smokeless, licking up the walls without heat, while Marta herself knelt in the street in her nightclothes with two men in grey holding her arms. The third man walked slowly along the front of the shops, holding something up. A lantern? No. A shard of glass, or crystal, angled toward each door in turn, catching the pre-dawn grey.

Looking. Reading.

When it passed over Bram's shuttered window, the man paused.

Bram let the shutter fall and stood very still in the dark of her kitchen, listening to her own blood move. The burn on her palm had gone hot enough to feel through the whole hand now, and she understood, with the calm clarity that comes at the very bottom of fear, that whatever the crystal was reading, it was reading her. That it had perhaps been reading her since last night. That the flare in her cellar had been not a candle but a beacon, and that she had spent twenty years building a life of smallness and mundane craft and careful silence, and had undone all of it in one sleepless hour by keeping a stone she should have drowned.

The boots moved on. The white fire died as suddenly as it had begun, leaving Marta's shop blackened but standing, which was somehow worse — a warning, not an execution. Voices murmured. Then the quarter exhaled, and the boots receded eastward toward the gate, and Bram stood in her kitchen until the sky went from grey to the color of dirty wool, and made the only decision left to make.

She was leaving Corvane. Today. Before the sun cleared the rooftops, before the quarter woke and someone remembered the woman who sold fever-salves and asked her questions she couldn't answer.

She packed the way she had learned to pack long ago, in another life: quickly, lightly, nothing that couldn't be abandoned. Salves wrapped in oilcloth. A knife. Bread, cheese, the good needle. Coin sewn into her belt where no honest searcher would think to look. At the last moment, kneeling by the loose flagstone, she stared down at the covenant-stone where it lay in its bed of earth, still faintly warm, its carved lines glowing like embers under ash.

"Stay," she told it, and heard how foolish she sounded, and took it anyway.

* * *

The smugglers' quarter wakes early and lies about it. By the time Bram reached the tanner's walk, the alleys were already full of the morning's first commerce — fishwives arguing over eel-buckets, a boy sweeping steps he'd swept an hour ago for a different coin, two dockhands sleeping off their wages in a doorway with the practiced comfort of men who had nowhere better. Smoke from a hundred cook-fires hung in the windless air, and under it the older smells of the quarter: brine, tar, tannin, the sweet rot of the tidal flats beyond the wall.

She had a route planned. South through the fish market, then the pilgrim stairs down to the mud-gate, where the tide would be low enough to wade the culvert and come out beyond the customs houses. From there, east. Away from the capital, away from Hale and his crystal and his white fire. East, though every instinct screamed at her not to go east, because east was where the thorn hollow waited, and the hollow was the one direction her feet had refused for twenty years.

But the stone pulled east. She could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel a current under a boat — not dragging, just *suggesting*. And besides, the roads north and south ran through garrison towns, and the roads west ran to the capital, and a woman with a god-stone in her pack had fewer choices than most.

She was three streets from the fish market when the beggar spoke her name.

"You're going the wrong way, hedge-witch."

He sat in the mouth of an alley between a chandler's and a derelict granary, in the shadow where the morning sun hadn't reached yet, and Bram's first thought — her ordinary, sensible, twenty-years-practiced thought — was that he was one of the quarter's madmen, of whom there were plenty, and that the best response was none at all. Keep walking. Don't look. Madmen fed on attention the way gulls fed on offal.

But her feet had already slowed, because his voice was wrong. Not cracked the way beggars' voices crack. *Deep*. It came out of him the way water comes out of a well — from further down than seemed possible — and it had a texture to it, a grain, like a voice heard once in childhood and never forgotten.

"I'm not a witch," she said, which was the stupidest possible reply, and she knew it even as she said it, and hated herself for it.

The beggar smiled. He was small and bent, wrapped in a coat that might once have been a soldier's, grey with age and street-dirt, his hands — she noticed this with a strange, detached precision — remarkably clean. His eyes were the problem. Beggars' eyes are dull or sharp or desperate, but always *present*, always fixed in the here and now of hunger and cold. His eyes were old. Not aged — old the way a river is old, the way a standing stone is old. Looking into them was like looking down a well and seeing, instead of your own reflection, the faint suggestion of stars.

"You were," he said. "You are. You'll be more, before this is done." He patted the cobbles beside him, an oddly courtly gesture from a man in rags. "Sit. Your legs have been running since midnight and they don't know it yet."

"I don't know you."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But I knew you. You used to leave honey-cakes on my boundary stones. Terrible baker, even at nine. You put salt in them half the time." He tilted his head, and the starlight in his eyes shifted. "I ate them anyway. All of them. Every year."

Bram's mouth had gone dry. She stood in the alley-mouth with the fish-market noise washing around her like surf, and felt twenty years peel away like bark off a dead tree, and heard — impossibly, absurdly — a voice she had last heard at eleven years old, crooning a charm over a sick ewe in a lamplit byre while a girl watched from the doorway.

"Vesh," she said. Just the name. It tasted like woodsmoke and honey and the inside of Tamsin's cottage, and saying it hurt somewhere under her ribs, in the place where the guilt lived.

"Now she remembers," the beggar said, and his smile gentled into something almost sad. "Though I'll grant you, I'm dressed for it poorly. The vessel situation in Corvane is grim. This poor fellow has been dead three days; I hope you won't hold the smell against me."

"You're wearing a corpse."

"I'm *borrowing* one. There's a difference, and it matters enormously to the fellow, who is currently having a very pleasant dream about his mother's kitchen and will wake tomorrow with no memory and a mysterious full belly." The god shrugged within the dead man's shoulders, a gesture that should have been horrifying and was instead, somehow, weary. "The old doors are opening again, Bram. Slowly, badly, like hinges rusted shut. We take what bodies we can find. Most of my kin got altars. I got a beggar and a boundary stone, which tells you what the ancients thought of me, and they weren't wrong."

"What do you want?"

"Straight to business. Tamsin taught you that, too. She never could abide a god who lingered over pleasantries." He leaned forward, and the smell of him — cold earth, rain on stone, and yes, faintly, three days of death — washed over her. "I want to tell you that the men with the crystal will be at the mud-gate within the hour. That they've already taken the fish market. That the road east is watched from the tollbridge to Ashfield, and the road south is watched worse, and that if you go to ground in the quarter again they will find you by tonight, because that stone in your bag is singing loud enough for the dead to dance to."

Her hand moved to the satchel strap before she could stop it. Vesh's ruined smile widened.

"There. You see. You didn't even try to deny it. Good. Lying to gods is exhausting for everyone involved."

"Why help me?" The question came out harder than she intended, edged with all the anger she hadn't spent yet — on the stone, on the fire, on the past, on the god himself. "Twenty years, Vesh. Twenty years of silence. Where were you when I was burying what I was? Where were any of you? The prayers went up like smoke and nothing answered, nothing ever—"

"We couldn't." And the weariness in his voice deepened into something older and heavier, grief worn smooth by centuries. "The covenant broke, child. Not frayed — *broke*, snapped clean through, the night a king's blood spilled on marble in the capital. Everything after that — the empty shrines, the unanswered prayers, your mistress wasting alone in her hollow — that wasn't abandonment. It was amputation. We have been reaching for you across a severed wrist for twenty years, feeling for a pulse that wasn't there." He raised one clean, dead hand, and touched her wrist, feather-light. "And then last night, there it was again."

Bram pulled her arm back. Her palm burned.

"The stone," she said. "What is it? What did Tamsin—"

"That is a conversation for the road, and we are losing the road." Vesh unfolded himself from the alley-wall with a series of small cracks, joints protesting, the borrowed body failing at its seams. "There is a carter named Odell who owes a debt to a woman he thinks is called Mother Grey, who does not exist, but whose reputation I have been carefully cultivating among the city's rats and beggars for longer than you've been alive. He leaves from the chandler's yard within the hour, bound for Dunmarrow with a load of tallow, and he has room behind the barrels for one passenger if the price is right. The price is right. I have seen to it."

"Dunmarrow." East. Of course. The stone's pull, given a destination. "That's — Vesh, that's days on the open road with Casimir's edicts hanging over every shrine in the free cities. If they're watching for—"

"They are watching for a witch traveling alone. So you will not travel alone. You will travel as Odell's cousin's widow, come from the coast to settle affairs, quiet and grieving and thoroughly dull. I've arranged the details. Grief is wonderful cover; no one interrogates the grieving, they only feed them." He began to shuffle deeper into the alley, gesturing for her to follow, and Bram stood her ground.

"And what does it cost?" she asked. "Gods don't give passage out of kindness. Tamsin taught me that too. Every gift has a hook in it."

For a moment the beggar's face went very still, and the starlight in his eyes banked down to coals, and Bram felt the alley grow colder around her, felt the weight of something vast consider her from behind those ruined eyes — felt, briefly, the true size of the thing wearing a dead man's coat, the way you feel the depth of the sea when a shadow passes under your boat.

"The cost," Vesh said quietly, "is that you stop running from the hollow. Eventually. Not today. Today you run east, and I will walk beside you as far as my strength allows, and I will teach you the old words on the road, because you will need them and there is no one else left to teach you." He held out the dead man's hand. "The cost is trust, Bram Vasquez. Which I know is the one coin you swore never to spend again. I am asking anyway. I knew you at nine years old, covered in flour and salt, feeding a boundary god terrible cakes because Tamsin told you the old bonds must be honored even when no one watches. I have watched you for twenty years. I know what you buried. I know why. And I am telling you that the burying is over, whether you consent to it or not — so you may as well consent, and have company in the digging."

The alley was silent except for the distant fish-market cries and the drip of condensation off the chandler's eaves. Bram looked at the offered hand. Dead flesh, god-light, patience older than kingdoms.

Everything in her that had survived twenty years of self-erasure said *no*. Say no, keep walking, get to the mud-gate alone, stay small, stay hidden, stay nothing. Nothing had kept her alive this long. Nothing was safe.

But nothing had also left Marta kneeling in the street in her nightclothes. Nothing had left a scorch-mark on her own skin and a name burning in her palm. Nothing, she was beginning to understand, had simply been a slower way of being found.

"If you're lying," she said, "about the carter, about any of it—"

"I have lied exactly once in four thousand years," Vesh said, "and I regret it constantly, and it was about a wager involving a river, and it is a long story, and we do not have time for it." His fingers flexed. "Take the hand, child. The gates are closing."

Bram took it.

The dead man's grip was cool and firm, and the moment her skin met his, the burn on her palm flared — not painfully this time, but bright, a warmth spreading up her arm like sunlight through water, and for one disorienting heartbeat she smelled woodsmoke and wet thorn and honey-cakes, and heard, very far away, a woman's voice singing a charm over a sick ewe in a lamplit byre.

Then it passed. The alley was just an alley. The beggar was just a beggar, shuffling toward the chandler's yard, and Bram followed him with her satchel heavy against her hip and the covenant-stone pulsing against her spine like a second heart, eastward, toward everything she had sworn never to face.

Behind them, at the mouth of the alley, sunlight finally reached the cobbles — and in the distance, from the direction of the mud-gate, came the thin, clear note of a watch-horn, and the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving fast.

Odell's cart, Vesh had said. Within the hour.

They had less than that.
