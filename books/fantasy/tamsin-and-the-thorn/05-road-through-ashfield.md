## 5. Road Through Ashfield

The road east out of Corvane was called the Pilgrim's Way once, before the gods went quiet and the name curdled into irony. Now it was just a scar of packed clay running between hedgerows gone feral, and Bram walked it with a stolen loaf in her pack, a false name on her tongue, and a burn shaped like her own signature throbbing beneath the wrapping on her left palm.

Three days out of the city, she passed the first shrine.

She almost didn't recognize it as one. The roadside godstones had been toppled so long ago that most had become furniture — gateposts, stepping-stones, a convenient place to set down a heavy load. This one still lay flat in the grass where it had been thrown, face-down, its carved features pressed into the dirt like a man refusing to look at what the world had become.

Except that the grass around it had grown in a perfect ring, waist-high and impossibly green, though the rest of the field stood brown with early frost. And when Bram stopped — because her feet stopped whether she willed them to or not — she saw that the stone had turned over. Not been turned. There were no drag marks, no lever-scars in the earth. It had simply *turned*, the way a sleeper turns toward a window at dawn, and its weathered face now looked up at a sky the color of unpolished pewter.

Small offerings sat in the crook of its stone arm. A twist of bread. A copper coin gone green. A child's shoe, worn through at the heel.

Bram stood there longer than she could afford, her breath fogging, her bandaged hand aching in time with her heartbeat. Then she walked on, and did not look back, and told herself the ache in her palm was only the healing wound.

* * *

Ashfield earned its name honestly. A generation ago the king's tax-farmers had burned three villages along this stretch of road for harboring shrine-keepers, and the ash had worked itself so deep into the soil that the wheat came up gray-stalked and the wells drew water that tasted faintly of smoke. The people who remained had learned the lesson written in their fields: keep your head down, want nothing loudly, and never — *never* — let the wrong person see you hope.

Which was why the woman at the well in Lower Ashfield took one look at Bram's face and dropped her bucket.

Bram had not yet spoken. She had not done anything but walk up the lane, dusty and footsore, wanting nothing more than water and a barn loft to sleep in. But the woman stared at her as if she'd seen a ghost wearing a familiar coat, and then she was running, calling names over her shoulder, and by the time Bram reached the well there were a dozen villagers gathered in the grey light, and an old man was kneeling in the mud with his hat clutched to his chest.

"Lady," he said. "Lady, we saw the light."

"I think you've mistaken me for someone else." Her voice came out flat and careful, the voice she used for haggling. "I'm a peddler. Salves, mostly. Luck-knots."

"Three nights past," the old man said, undeterred, "the shrine up on Tor Hill lit like a bonfire. Blue fire, cold fire, no smoke. My granddaughter saw it. Half the village saw it. And the next morning the spring ran sweet again — sweet, after forty years of sulfur — and my wife's knees—" His voice cracked. He held up the hat higher, as if offering it. "And then you come walking down the road with your hand bound like a priest's, and the dogs won't bark at you, lady. Did you notice? Not one dog in Ashfield will bark at you."

Bram noticed. She had noticed since Corvane, since the alley behind the tannery where the mongrels that terrorized every stranger had flattened themselves against the walls and watched her pass with their ears back and their tails low, not in fear — in something older than fear. Reverence, maybe. Or recognition.

"I'm a peddler," she said again, and heard how thin it sounded even to herself.

The woman who had dropped the bucket stepped forward. She had flour to her elbows and eyes red-rimmed from something other than the woodsmoke. "My son," she said simply. "He's got the wasting fever. The physician wants eight silver just to look at him, and we both know what he'll say when he does. Please. I don't care if you're a lady or a god or a peddler. You're the first thing that's come down that road in twenty years that felt like an answer."

*No*, Bram wanted to say. *I'm not an answer. I'm the question everyone forgot to ask, and I have been trying very hard for twenty years to stay unasked.*

What she said was: "Show me."

She hated herself a little for it. She hated herself more when they led her to a low room that smelled of tallow and sickness, and she saw the boy — fourteen, maybe fifteen, skin stretched over a fever architecture of bone, lips cracked, eyes bright with the particular patience of the dying young — and felt something in her chest unlock that she had welded shut two decades ago.

Tamsin would have known what to do. Tamsin had always known. That had been the whole unbearable shape of the woman: competence as a form of love, love as a form of demand. *You have the gift, girl. Gifts are debts.*

Bram unwrapped her hand.

The sigil burned there in fine black lines, precise as engraving: the interlocked circle-and-thorn that had been Tamsin's mark, that had been the mark of the covenant's stewards going back further than any living memory, that had appeared on her palm the night the stone woke screaming under her cellar floor and had not faded since, no matter how she scrubbed, no matter how she prayed — and she did not pray, she *did not* — to anything at all.

She didn't know what she was doing. That was the truth of it, the humiliating truth she carried east like a second pack. She had buried her magic the way she'd buried the stone, in a hole behind her ribs, and she had assumed both were dead. But the stone had proven otherwise, and now her hand proved otherwise too, because when she laid her palm flat on the boy's burning forehead and thought — not words, not craft, just a raw wordless *please* aimed at whatever was listening — the sigil warmed, and warmth poured through it like water through a channel cut long ago for exactly this purpose.

The fever broke between one breath and the next. Not gradually. All at once, like a knot slipping.

The boy opened his eyes and looked at her, and Bram saw in his face the same thing she'd seen in the dogs' faces, and she got out of that room as fast as dignity allowed.

They wouldn't take payment. Of course they wouldn't. They gave her bread and cheese and a bed by the hearth, and in the morning there were people waiting outside the door — not just from Lower Ashfield but from Upper Ashfield, from the hamlet at the crossroads, from places she hadn't walked through yet. A farmer whose cow had stopped giving milk. A mother with a child born with a clouded eye. An old woman who only wanted to touch Bram's sleeve and wept when she did, saying *thank you, thank you* over and over as if Bram had already granted something.

Word traveled faster than feet. Word had always traveled faster than feet; that was the first thing Tamsin had taught her about hedge-craft, before any herb-lore or knot-work. *Magic is mostly rumor, girl. The working is the smallest part. What people believe is coming is what arrives.*

Bram had believed she was nobody. The road was busy correcting her.

* * *

By the fifth day she had learned to read the signs and detour around them, and it changed nothing, because the signs were everywhere now, and some of them were looking for her specifically.

She passed a chapel — a true chapel, roofed, though the roof sagged — where someone had swept away twenty years of bird droppings and dust and relit the votive rack. The flames burned without flickering, which was wrong, which was *deeply* wrong, and she made herself look anyway because pretending not to see things had never once saved her. Behind the altar, the painted saint's eyes had been repainted. Fresh pigment, crude brushwork, and the face wasn't a saint's anymore. It was hers. Badly rendered, thirty years too young, but unmistakably hers, down to the stubborn set of the jaw she saw every morning in whatever polished metal passed for a mirror on the road.

Someone had dreamed her face. Someone had dreamed her face and painted it and knelt in front of it, and the candles in front of it burned straight as nails.

She walked faster after that.

At the crossroads hamlet, a shrine-keeper — a real one, one of the old order, a stooped man in a patched cassock with a keeper's iron still hanging at his throat despite the law that said wearing it was death — caught her arm at the well and spoke low and fast, his breath sour with fear.

"They're saying a steward walks the Pilgrim's Way. They're saying the blue fires follow her like gulls follow a boat. Child, listen to me." His grip was surprisingly strong. "If the villages are lighting shrines, so are the beacon-hills, and the beacons are seen from the capital road. Casimir's riders are not slow. Whatever you are, whoever you were — do not sleep twice in the same place, and do not let them take you standing among people who love you. They make examples."

"I don't know what you think I am," Bram said.

"No," he agreed, and released her arm, and smiled with terrible gentleness. "That's the tragedy of stewards. None of you ever do, until it's far too late to be anything else."

She walked out of the hamlet with her spine cold and did not stop until dark, and when she finally made camp in a hollow under a dead oak, she took out the thing she had not let herself examine since Corvane: the shard of covenant-stone she'd pried loose from the cellar floor when the rest of it sank back into the earth like a whale sounding. Fistsized, heavier than it should be, veined with the same black lines as her palm. In the dark, those lines pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. Like a heart that had waited twenty years and could wait twenty more.

"You could have picked anyone," she whispered to it.

The stone said nothing, but the pulse quickened briefly, and somewhere off in the dark — a mile, maybe two — a shrine flared blue against the night, and Bram pulled her cloak over her head and pretended, with the practiced excellence of two decades' work, to be asleep.

* * *

It rained on the seventh day, a thin cold rain that turned the road to grease, and it was raining when she came over the rise above the town of Ashfield Proper and saw the crowd.

Her first thought was *inquisitors*. Her hand went to the knife at her belt before her mind caught up: no. No livery, no white-and-grey, no wagons with their cages. Just people. Hundreds of them, more than the town could hold, spilled out along the road and the common and the steps of the great ruined temple that crowned the town's hill — a temple that had been a hay-barn for as long as Bram had been alive, and that now stood with its doors open and light pouring out of it. Blue light. Cold, smokeless, steady as starlight.

They had cleaned it. In seven days, they had cleaned a ruin that would take a season to clean, because the light had started in the crypt and climbed, and no one had been willing to shut a door on it.

And they had all turned to look at her, because of course they had. Because the rain stopped falling within ten feet of her — she felt it happen, felt the wet air part around her like a curtain drawn by a courteous hand — and there was no hiding a thing like that from four hundred pairs of eyes.

"Oh, no," Bram said quietly, to the sky, to the stone in her pack, to whatever was listening. "No. Absolutely not."

But her feet kept walking, and the crowd parted, and an ancient woman in the temple doorway — the oldest person Bram had ever seen, folded up like a paper lantern, eyes milk-pale and somehow seeing everything — beckoned with one curled finger.

"You're angry," the old woman said, when Bram reached her. Her voice was dry leaves. "Good. Anger's honest. The ones who come here grateful frighten me."

"I don't want this." Bram's voice shook, and she let it, because there was no one left to perform steadiness for. "Whatever this is, whatever's waking up out there — I didn't ask for it. I spent twenty years making sure I'd never be asked. I sold luck-knots. Luck-*knots*. Do you understand? I made myself small on purpose. Small enough to live in."

"Yes," the old woman said. "And the world has finished letting you."

Inside the temple, the blue light rose from the crypt stairs in a column that did not illuminate so much as *reveal* — everything it touched looked more itself, more painfully exact, every crack and cobweb and candle-scar rendered with merciless clarity. Villagers knelt in the nave, not praying aloud. Praying with their hands, with their postures, with the desperate set of their shoulders. Bram could feel it now, feel all of it, the way she'd been able to feel it since the stone woke: every prayer in the building pressing against the inside of her skin like moths against glass. *Heal my father. Find my daughter. Let the harvest hold. Let the riders pass us by. Let it be true, oh, let it be true, let something finally be true—*

"There are too many," she whispered. "There have always been too many. That's why the covenant needed—" She stopped. The word *steward* sat in her mouth like a stone of its own.

"The covenant needed a door with a will," the old woman agreed. "Not a wall. Walls let nothing through, and floods break them. Doors can choose." She tilted her ancient head. "You've been choosing all week, peddler. The boy with the fever. The woman's clouded-eyed babe at the crossroads — yes, we heard, the eye cleared, they're calling it your first miracle, though I suspect it was your third and you weren't counting. You keep saying you don't want it. And you keep answering."

"Because they *ask*," Bram said, and heard the truth of it land in her own chest like a bell struck. "Because I remember what it was like when nobody answered. I was there. I lived in the silence for twenty years and I know exactly what it weighs." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods. Tamsin would be insufferable about this."

"Then perhaps," said the old woman, "you should go and let her be."

Bram went still. "What did you say?"

The old woman's milky eyes fixed on her, and for a moment something moved behind them that was not entirely the old woman — something vast and amused and achingly familiar, something that had once, in another life, in a kitchen that smelled of cedar and burnt honey, had once lifted a six-year-old girl onto its shoulders and called her *little door*.

"The hollow," the presence said, through the papery mouth, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "East, past the fen causeway, where the thorns grow taller every year and no bird nests in them. You know the way, Bram Vasquez. You have always known the way. You have simply spent twenty years walking everywhere else." The vastness withdrew, leaving the old woman blinking and frail and confused about why several hundred people were staring at her, and the blue light in the crypt pulsed once, twice, like a patient heart.

Outside, far to the west, back along the road she had come by, a smudge of dust rose above the tree line. Too regular for weather. Too fast for merchants.

Riders. Three of them, maybe four, moving at the pace of men paid to catch someone.

Bram tightened the straps on her pack, touched the shard through the canvas — warm, insistent, pointing east like a compass needle that had finally found its nerve — and started walking.

Behind her, the crowd began, softly at first and then in hundreds of voices, to sing. It was an old song. A road-song. A blessing for travelers that hadn't been sung openly in twenty years, and every note of it followed her down the hill like a hand between her shoulder blades, pushing her gently, relentlessly, toward the thorn-choked hollow where everything she had buried was waiting to be dug up.
