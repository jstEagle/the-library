## 6. The Usurper's Edict

The candles in the Hall of Petitioners had been lit since noon, and still the light would not hold.

Casimir Vael noticed such things now. He had not always been a man who counted flames and shadows, but eleven years on a stolen throne taught a man to read a room the way sailors read weather — by small wrongnesses, by the way smoke leaned when there was no wind, by how many candles guttered at once as though something vast had drawn breath through the hall.

He stood at the high window of his private gallery above the throne room, watching the petitioners gather below like sediment settling in dark water. Farmers from the river provinces. Guild factors with ink-stained cuffs. A delegation of shrine-keepers under guard, their hands bound with cord because rope was cheaper than iron and iron was needed for the war. All of them come to the capital to ask questions no loyal subject should ask.

*Why do the shrines burn again? Why do the wells taste of old prayers? Why did my dead mother's voice come out of the storm last month and call my name?*

Casimir pressed two fingers against the cold glass. Below, the winter light fell through the clerestory windows in long amber blades, and the crowd shifted and murmured, and somewhere among them — he could feel it, the way one feels a tooth before it aches — someone had lit a votive candle that refused to go out.

They had tried three times already this morning. He'd had reports.

"Your Grace." Ser Aldric Marrow stood at the gallery door, helm under his arm, frost still melting in the seams of his cloak. The captain of the Regent's Guard had a face built for obedience: flat, gray, untroubled. "The hall is full. The heralds await your word."

"And the candle?"

Aldric's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Extinguished, Your Grace. The keeper of the flame was removed."

"Removed," Casimir repeated softly. "That is what we are calling it now."

"She resisted."

"They always resist." He turned from the window. In the polished silver of the mirror mounted beside the door, he caught his own reflection — a tall man of fifty-one years, silver going to white at the temples, dressed in mourning-black and regent's gold though there had been no funeral worth the name in eleven years. His face was still handsome in the way old knives are handsome. His eyes were the color of a frozen lake, and beneath them, faint as watermarks, the sleeplessness of a decade had begun to stain.

He thought of his brother often these days. That was new. For years he had managed not to think of Aurelian at all — not the man, not the fever, not the cup of mulled wine steaming gently on the bedside table while the king slept, not the way the royal physicians had shaken their heads and spoken of a weakness of the heart. Eleven years of disciplined silence inside his own skull. And now, in a single autumn, the gods had resumed answering prayers, and every answered prayer was a door opening in a house Casimir had bricked shut, and behind each door, something remembered.

Not accused him. Nothing so crude. The gods did not accuse. They simply *remembered*, and their remembering leaked into the world like dye into water, and everywhere the water reached, people began to dream of things they had never witnessed. A chamberlain's daughter woke screaming about wine and a sleeping king. A stableboy in the outer ward refused to enter the royal stables, weeping, saying the horses spoke of poison in the night. Nonsense. Superstition. Contagious nonsense.

And yet.

"Your Grace?" Aldric prompted.

"I'm coming." Casimir drew on his gloves — kidskin, dyed black, fitted so precisely they seemed poured onto his hands. Control began with the hands. Control began with the small things: gloves, ledgers, candles, names. A kingdom was only an empire of small things arranged so they could not move.

He descended the private stair with Aldric a pace behind, and the sound of the crowd rose to meet him — hundreds of voices braided into a single anxious hum, the sound a hive makes when the queen has died and the workers know it and do not yet know what to do about it.

The herald's staff struck marble three times. "His Grace, Casimir Vael, Lord-Regent of the Realm, Protector of the Crown, Voice of the King!"

*Voice of the King.* The title still fit him like a glove worn too long — it had taken the shape of his hand, or perhaps his hand had taken its shape; he no longer cared to examine which. There was no king. There had been no king for eleven years, unless one counted the boy in the tower, and Casimir did count him, daily, the way a man counts the bars of a cell he has built for someone else. Prince Edrian was fourteen now. Sharp-eyed. Quiet. Growing up in rooms Casimir chose for him, reading books Casimir approved, asking questions that grew more dangerous each year.

*One problem at a time.*

He crossed the dais and took the throne — not sat, *took*, the way one takes a hill in battle — and looked out over the Hall of Petitioners.

It was a beautiful room. His grandfather had built it to make petitioners feel small: pillars like petrified trees, a ceiling painted with the firmament, the whole architecture of the place designed to remind any man who entered that he was an insect appealing to heaven. Casimir had always appreciated the honesty of it. Most thrones pretended humility. This one admitted what power was.

"Bring forward the first petition," he said, and his voice carried without effort, trained over decades into an instrument of perfect temperature — warm enough to seem just, cold enough to end arguments.

They brought forward a woman. Mid-forties, miller's widow by her clothes, escorted by two guardsmen who gripped her elbows as though she might fly apart. She was made to kneel on the third step of the dais, close enough that Casimir could see the chilblains on her knuckles and the stubborn set of her mouth.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Marta Ostrewe, of Fenlow Mill, Your Grace." Her voice wavered and steadied. She had rehearsed this. "I petition regarding my son."

"Speak."

"He drowned in the spring flood. Two years past." Her hands twisted in her lap. "We buried him. I sat the death-watch myself, seven nights, as custom requires. And last month — Your Grace, last month he came home."

A ripple went through the hall. Casimir let it die before he answered. "Came home."

"Took supper with us. Sat in his own chair. Wore the shirt we buried him in, and it fit, Your Grace, it fit as if it had never been in the ground." The widow's eyes were wet and blazing at once. "He stayed till dawn and told me not to grieve, and said the river was cold but the other side was warm, and then he was gone with the light. And I want to know—" her voice cracked, gathered itself, "—I want to know if that is a sin. Because your criers say the shrines are treason now, and my priest says I must report the visitation, and I will not. I will NOT. It was my son. Whatever law says a mother must give up her dead twice, that law was not written by God or man I recognize."

Silence. The particular silence of five hundred people waiting to see which way the axe falls.

Casimir regarded her for a long moment. He felt nothing for her — that was the gift his brother's murder had given him, the amputation of certain useless feelings — but he understood her completely, and understanding was more dangerous than feeling. A woman like this could not be threatened back into silence. She had already lost everything the threats could touch.

"The crown does not concern itself with grief-hallucinations," he said at last, mildly. "Nor with the tricks of hedge-witches, who grow fat on exactly such stories. You will return to Fenlow Mill. You will say nothing further of this. And you will be grateful the crown concerns itself with your welfare at all, in a season when foreign agitators fill honest heads with ghost-stories to soften the realm for invasion."

"Invasion," she whispered. "My son came home and you speak of invasion—"

"Remove her."

The guards took her arms. She did not struggle; she only looked back at him from the third step, and her look said, with terrible clarity: *You know. You know it was real, and you are afraid, and I see it.*

Casimir held her gaze until the guards turned her away, and his face did not move at all, because he had spent eleven years teaching his face to be a wall, and walls do not flinch even when something knocks.

But that night — later, much later, alone in the map room with the lamps trimmed low — the knocking continued.

* * *

The map room occupied the top of the White Spire, and Casimir had chosen it deliberately: the highest room in the palace, above the chapel (disused), above the old god-shrine in the undercroft (sealed), far from the earth and everything that grew in it. From here the capital spread below him like a circuit diagram — walls, wards, gates, garrisons — every element placed by his own hand over eleven careful years.

Ser Aldric waited with the marshals. Lord Chancellor Deverell hovered at the map's western edge, a soft gray man with soft gray eyes who believed whatever Casimir had most recently told him. Commander Ysolt Rane of the Third Legion stood with her gauntlets clasped behind her back, watching the map the way a hawk watches a hedgerow.

On the map, the free cities were marked in blue: Corvane on the coast, Dunmarrow in the river country, and the smaller leagues strung between like beads on a wire. Around them, in red wax, Casimir's markers stood in their legions — forty thousand men mobilized, more promised by spring.

"Gentlemen." He moved to the head of the table. "Report."

Ysolt spoke first, crisp as a snapped twig. "Third and Fifth Legions are provisioned for a ninety-day campaign. The vanguard can reach Dunmarrow's outskirts in three weeks if the roads hold. The city fathers have sent no answer to our ultimatum."

"They will not answer," Deverell murmured. "Dunmarrow's council believes the gods have returned to protect them. Fanatics make poor negotiators."

"Dunmarrow's council," Casimir said, "believes nothing of the kind. Merchants believe in whatever keeps the caravans moving. But they have seen the shrines relight, same as everyone else, and they are doing arithmetic." He set one finger on Dunmarrow. "Their arithmetic says: if the gods are back, the Regent's writ means less than it did. If the gods are back, a city that shelters the divine may prosper under divine protection. Their arithmetic is wrong, but it is *recently* wrong, and recent errors are the hardest to correct."

"Then we correct it with the Third Legion," Ysolt said.

"With all of them, if necessary." He straightened. "Which brings us to the edict."

Deverell passed him the scroll, drafted in the chancellor's neat hand, awaiting only seal and voice. Casimir read it though he knew every word — he had dictated every word — because reading aloud gave the moment weight, and weight was what made law stick in the minds of men.

"'Let it be known throughout the realm,'" he read, "'that all manifestations of divine agency — visions, visitations, healings, prophecies, relics that move or burn or speak, shrines that kindle without mortal hand — are hereby declared treason against the crown. Let it be known that the covenant between gods and mortals is dissolved, broken, and void, and that any who pray to the old powers, tend the old altars, or shelter those who do, commit treason, and shall be judged as traitors. Let it be known that the crown alone protects the realm, by steel and law, and requires no jealous heaven's permission to do so.'"

He set the scroll down. "Seal it. Criers in every market square by week's end. And the second matter—"

He touched the map again, westward, where a small black pin marked a smugglers' quarter in Corvane. "Inquisitor Hale's report. Three nights ago, in Corvane, a raid on a suspected witch-quarter produced casualties and one anomaly: a scorch-mark on a doorpost matching no sigil in the registries, radiating heat inconsistent with any mundane fire. Hale believes it marks the awakening of a covenant-stone."

"A stone," Ysolt said flatly.

"A relic of the old pact. If Hale is right — and Hale is usually right, which is why I tolerate his methods — then somewhere in the free cities walks a person the stones remember. An heir of the old stewardship." Casimir's finger rested on the pin a moment longer than necessary. "Find that person before the cities understand what they have. Hale has authority to cross borders. He has authority to take whoever he finds alive."

"Alive," Aldric repeated. There was a question in it.

"Dead witches make martyrs. Living ones make confessions." Casimir withdrew his hand. "And a living heir might tell us what the covenant actually requires — knowledge, gentlemen, is the one weapon the gods cannot confiscate."

The marshals bent to the map, moving markers, arguing supply lines. Casimir watched them with half his attention. With the other half he listened to the room itself — the tick of the great clock, the wind worrying the spire's high windows, the small sounds a building makes when it settles.

And beneath all of it, very faint, the sound he had been hearing for six weeks now and had told no one about.

A dripping. Somewhere below. As though the sealed shrine in the undercroft had sprung a leak, though the masons swore the seals were intact, though he had gone down himself with a lamp and found the old altar bone-dry, the godless basin clean, the air stale and safe.

*Drip.*

He dismissed the marshals at dusk and stood alone at the window while the capital kindled below him — ten thousand hearthfires, streetlamps, watch-braziers, a constellation of obedient human light. Somewhere down there, in defiance of his criers and his laws, someone was lighting a candle for a dead son, a dead husband, a dead god. He could not stop it. He could send soldiers into every home in the realm and he could not stop a woman whispering thanks into her folded hands.

That was the thing about the gods returning. It was not armies. Armies he could fight. It was that divinity, once awake, lived in the smallest human gestures, and there were not enough soldiers in the world to search every heart.

*They remember,* whispered something that was not quite a voice, in the register of the dripping water, in the register of the wind. *The wine. The sleeping king. The cup.*

"Casimir Vael," he said aloud to the empty room, using his own name like a blade laid flat on a table, "is Lord-Regent of the Realm, and answers to no memory but his own."

The words sounded strong. They always did. They were also, he knew with the clarity that came only at night, the words of a man standing in a burning house insisting the warmth is pleasant.

Below, in the undercroft, the dripping stopped.

In its place, for three heartbeats, unmistakable, came the sound of a cup being set down on a wooden bedside table.

Casimir did not sleep that night. At dawn he summoned his scribes and dictated orders doubling the pace of the march on Dunmarrow, and added a line in his own hand to Inquisitor Hale's dispatch, four words that cost him more than the whole edict:

*Hasten. Before they remember fully.*
