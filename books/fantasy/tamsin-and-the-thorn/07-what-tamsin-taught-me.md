## 7. What Tamsin Taught Me

The road east of Corvane was a scar of packed clay and old cart-ruts, and Bram walked it with the stone wrapped in three layers of linen against her ribs, as if bandaging a wound that had learned to breathe.

She had not slept. The name on her palm had faded to a pink ghost by morning, but she could still feel it, the way you feel a word someone shouted at you long after the shouting stops. *Bramhilde.* No one had called her that in twenty years. No one alive had ever known it.

Vesh — or the beggar who carried Vesh the way a cracked bell carries its note — had pointed her east and then simply stopped being there, somewhere past the ash-gate, leaving her alone with a road, a god's warmth still lingering in her palm like a handprint on cold glass, and nothing but her own thoughts for company.

Her thoughts were dangerous country. They always had been.

* * *

She was nine years old the first time Tamsin made her hold a dying thing.

It was a wren, fallen from the eaves of the cottage with one wing bent wrong, its heart going so fast under Bram's fingers that it felt like holding a handful of rain. She had wanted to cry. She had wanted to run inside and hide under her cot until the small horror of it stopped being true.

Tamsin had crouched beside her in the dooryard, gray-streaked hair escaping its braid, hands stained green to the wrist from the morning's cutting, and said, "Feel it. All of it. If you look away from a hurt thing, girl, you'll never learn to mend anything."

"It's scared," Bram had whispered.

"Aye." Tamsin's voice had gone soft in a way it almost never did. "So are you. So am I. Being scared and staying anyway — that's the whole of the craft, Bram. Everything else is herbs and cleverness."

And Tamsin had folded Bram's small hands more surely around the bird, and hummed — a low, wordless tune that Bram would later learn was half prayer, half spell — and the wren's wing had knit with a sound like a green twig bending, and it had flown up into the thorn hedge and sat there scolding them both.

Bram had laughed until she cried. Tamsin had watched her with an expression Bram hadn't understood then, an expression like hunger and dread sharing one face.

Now, walking the empty road with the covenant-stone warm against her breastbone, she understood it. Tamsin had been looking at the thing she loved most and counting the cost of it.

* * *

By midday the land began to change. Ashfield earned its name honestly: fields gone gray-white with wood-ash drifted off some burned region to the north, clinging to the fence posts, dusting the ditches, turning every crow that landed a dirty silver. The villages here were poor and watchful. Shutters closed as she passed. A dog followed her for half a mile, decided against it, and turned back.

At a shrine — a waist-high cairn of river stones at a crossroads, crowned with a rusted iron sun — someone had left fresh bread. Someone else had left a ribbon. And someone else again had left a smear of blood on the topmost stone, thumb-printed, deliberate, the way you'd mark a door against plague.

Prayer was getting people killed now. Bram looked at the bread a long time. Her stomach argued. Her caution won, mostly; she took only what a traveler might have left herself, and even that felt like theft.

But standing there, she remembered the first time Tamsin had told her what the stone was.

* * *

She was twelve, and it was midwinter, and Tamsin had taken her down into the cellar where the jars of preserves glowed amber in lamplight and the cold came up through the floor stones like something patient.

In the deepest corner, beneath a flagstone Tamsin could shift with one hand though it took Bram both, there was a hollow lined with wool. And in the wool lay a stone no bigger than a fist, dark as a plum, veined with gold that seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at it.

"What is it?" Bram had breathed.

"The covenant," Tamsin said. "Or the heart of it. The rest is words and blood and old agreements, but this—" she touched it without lifting it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal "—this is the promise itself. Before your grandmother's grandmother, the gods and mortals struck their bargain. The gods would hear every honest prayer and answer what they could. In return, a mortal steward keeps the stone, tends the pact, carries the weight of it. Royal blood, once. The old kings' line."

"Are we royal?" Bram had asked, delighted, and Tamsin had barked a laugh that steamed in the cellar cold.

"We're *witches*, girl. Which is better. Kings are born to it; we're chosen, or cursed, depending on the day." Then her face had settled into seriousness, and she'd gripped Bram's shoulders, and the lamplight had made her eyes look like wet slate. "Listen to me. One day this will be yours, if the world doesn't break first. And if the world does break — if the throne falls to a man who fears what the gods remember — then this stone becomes the most hunted thing in creation. Do you understand? There will be men who would burn a city to get their hands on it. There will be *worse* than men."

"Why keep it at all, then?"

Tamsin had been quiet a long moment. Down in the dark, among the jars, with winter pressing at the trapdoor overhead, she'd said: "Because when the stone sleeps, prayers go unanswered. I've heard unanswered prayer, Bram. It sounds like a mother begging in the snow while her child cools in her arms. I'll carry the weight. You'll carry it after me. That's the bargain under the bargain."

Bram had been twelve. She'd nodded, and thought about supper, and gone up the stairs two at a time.

* * *

Toward evening she reached a hamlet called Marle's Cross, six houses and a well and a tithe-barn, and she meant to walk straight through. She did not mean to stop.

But a woman was kneeling in the road outside the last house, and a man stood over her with his hat wrung in his fists, and between them, on a door laid across two trestles, lay a boy of perhaps seven, gray-lipped, breath whistling through a throat swollen with croup.

They saw her walking and something in the woman's face broke open with hope before she could stop it — because hope, Bram knew, was a reflex in the desperate, not a decision. "Wife," the man said quickly, warningly, "she's just a peddler—"

"I'm not a witch," Bram said. The words came out automatic, worn smooth from use, and they tasted fouler every year.

The woman didn't even look at her. She was looking past her, up at the graying sky, and her lips were moving. Not words, quite. Just *please*, over and over, shaped silently, the way people pray when they've stopped believing anyone hears.

And the stone against Bram's ribs went warm.

Not burning. Warm, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire's out, and through the linen layers Bram felt it pulse once, twice, like a second heart waking up and asking, with something horribly like eagerness: *her? This one? Shall I?*

"No," Bram said aloud, and the family stared at her, and she realized too late how it sounded. She fumbled in her pack. "I — I have a salve. For the chest. Rendered goose-fat and elecampane and — other things." Other things. Twenty years of hiding magic under mundane craft, and here she stood at a crossroads with a god's heartbeat in her bag, offering goose-fat.

She knelt by the boy. His breath was a thin saw-blade sound. She worked the salve into his throat and chest with hands that shook, and murmured the fever-charm under her breath stripped of everything that made it work — just rhythm, just comfort, a lullaby wearing a spell's clothing.

The stone pulsed again. And the boy's breathing eased.

Not cured. Eased — the swelling settling, the whistle softening, his color coming back the way color comes back to a landscape when cloud lifts. Enough. More than enough. The woman made a sound like a hinge and gathered the boy up, and the man pressed a copper into Bram's hand and then, seeing her face, pressed two more.

"Were you praying?" the woman asked her, wild-eyed, cradling her son. "Just now? Did you pray for him?"

"No," Bram said, and walked out of Marle's Cross very fast, and did not stop shaking until full dark.

Because she knew what had answered. It hadn't been her. It had never been her. The stone had heard the woman's silent *please* and reached through the nearest vessel it could find — through *her* — and done a small mercy, casually, the way Vesh had answered a stranger's prayer in a smugglers' alley. The covenant stirring in its sleep. Reaching for its steward.

Reaching for *her*.

* * *

That night she camped in a hollow under a leaning beech, and let herself remember the night she ran, because she was tired enough that the memory came whether she invited it or not.

* * *

She was seventeen, and she had been chafing for a year.

It wasn't that Tamsin was cruel. Tamsin was fierce the way a hearth-fire is fierce — you warmed your hands at her and you kept your distance and you never forgot she could burn. But the lessons had grown heavier. The covenant's demands had grown clearer. *Someday,* Tamsin kept saying, *someday this will be yours,* and someday had begun to feel less like a gift and more like a sentence being read out slowly.

"You don't understand what you're refusing," Tamsin had said that final autumn, when Bram had raged at her — about the isolation, the endless vigilance, the way every kindness in the village came shadowed with fear of what lived past the thorn hedge.

"I understand fine! You want me to spend my life buried in a hole guarding a rock for gods who don't even answer anymore!"

"They answer," Tamsin had said quietly. "You've seen them answer."

"I've seen *you* answer. With herbs and cleverness." She'd thrown Tamsin's own words back at her and watched them land like knives. "Where were the gods when the fever took the Aldery children? Where were they when your knees went bad? You talk about the pact like it's holy, but it's just work. Endless, thankless work, for beings that take and take and—"

"Enough." Tamsin's voice had cracked across the kitchen like a whip, and then, terribly, it had gentled. "Bram. Child. Listen to me once. The stone chose our line because we can bear it. Not because we're strong. Because we can bear it. There's a difference, and you'll learn it, and learning it will cost you exactly what it cost me—"

"And what did it cost you?" Bram had flung at her. "What did you ever lose?"

She had never forgotten Tamsin's face in that moment. Not anger. Something older and sadder, a grief with no story attached, and Tamsin had only said, "Go to bed, girl," and turned back to her chopping, and Bram had gone to bed hating her, and hating the cottage, and hating the thorn hedge and the gray hills and the whole small circumscribed shape of the life laid out for her.

Three weeks later, Casimir's regicide lit fires in the capital, and the news came crawling up the valley with the refugees: the old king dead, the regent enthroned, edicts already circulating — *divine manifestation is treason; unlicensed healing is treason; the shrines are to come down.*

And Bram, seventeen and stupid and suffocating, had thought: *they're coming for the stone. They're coming here. And I will not die in a hole for a rock and a bitter old woman and gods who don't listen.*

She had told herself that was why. She had told herself that for twenty years.

The truth was simpler and worse. The truth was that Tamsin had said *someday this will be yours* and Bram had looked at the whole of that someday — the cellar, the vigil, the weight without end — and she had run. Not from the inquisitors. From the inheritance. She had used the coming soldiers as her excuse the way a coward uses any door that happens to be open.

She had taken the stone from under the flagstone in the deep of night, wrapped it in her spare shift, and slipped out through the gap in the thorn hedge that Tamsin had shown her for emergencies, the one passage cut for flight.

Behind her, as she crossed the dark field, she had heard Tamsin cry out — one raw, wordless sound from the cottage doorway, a sound Bram had spent two decades pretending she hadn't heard.

And behind that, stranger and worse, she had heard the hedge itself begin to grow. A dry, vast, creaking rush, like a forest deciding in a single night. She had not looked back. Looking back was the one thing she had never allowed herself.

Until now. Until the road east, which led, God help her, back along the exact thread she'd fled down, toward the hollow, toward the thorns, toward whatever Tamsin's grief had built in the dark the night her apprentice stole her heart's work and vanished.

* * *

Rain found her in the early hours, a fine cold sift that turned the ash-dust to gray paste. Bram pulled her hood down and kept walking, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the memory of that cry, and she had a destination now whether she wanted one or not.

Dunmarrow lay four days east, the nearest free city, the place Vesh had named. Beyond it, if the old maps in her head still served, the Thornwood, and the hollow, and the wall of thorns that had grown in a single night twenty years ago.

A rider caught her at dawn on the long causeway across the flooded meadows — a drover's horse, a drover's oilskin, but the man's eyes were wrong for a drover, too clean, too assessing. He reined in beside her and touched his hat brim.

"Morning, mother. Road's quiet. Seen anyone else walking east? Tall fellow, gray cloak, foreign accent?"

"No one," Bram said, in the flat Corvane market-tone she'd perfected, and the man studied her a moment longer — her pack, her boots, her hands — and then shrugged and trotted on. But fifty yards down the causeway he paused, and turned in the saddle, and looked back at her once, deliberately, before riding out of sight in the rain.

Hale's men. It had to be. The raid on Rope-walk, the scorch-mark, the sigil burned into the world in her shape — the hunt had her scent now, and riders on the roads meant the net was drawing, meant Dunmarrow's gates might be watched, meant every hour she walked she was leading the usurper's hounds straight along her own trail.

Unless that trail ended somewhere the hounds feared to go.

Bram stood on the causeway in the rain, water running off her hood, and felt the stone pulse against her ribs — slow, patient, insistent, like a second heart that had waited twenty years and intended to wait no longer. East, it seemed to say. Onward. Home, if there was any such word left for the place she'd abandoned.

She thought of Tamsin's face the night she left. She thought of the wren with the broken wing, and a fierce gray-haired woman saying *feel all of it, if you look away you'll never mend anything.*

"All right," Bram said aloud, to the rain, to the stone, to the ghost of a cry she'd spent half her life not hearing. "I'm done looking away."

She tightened the pack straps and walked east, faster now, toward Dunmarrow, toward the thorn hollow, toward whatever reckoning waited inside the hedge — unaware that behind her, on the same causeway, a tall figure in a gray cloak had stepped out of nowhere at all, and was following at a respectful distance, humming a low, wordless tune she almost recognized.
