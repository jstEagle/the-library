## 8. The Free City of Dunmarrow

Ivet Sarn had counted the bells of Dunmarrow for thirty years, and she knew them the way other people knew their own heartbeat. The dawn bell from the Salt Tower, cracked and flat since the year of the great fire. The midmorning bell of the Weavers' Guild, bright as struck silver. And now, this morning, no bell at all — because the bell-ringer of the Harbor Shrine had rung it three times in the night without being asked, and the Council had ordered all shrine bells silenced until further notice.

Silence, Ivet thought, descending the wet stairs of her townhouse into the fog-thick street, is also an answer. It tells everyone exactly what you are afraid of.

The fog off the Graywater had swallowed the harbor whole. She could hear the masts knocking against each other somewhere out in that white blindness, could smell tar and brine and the char-sweetness of incense — someone was burning offerings again, despite the edict, despite everything. The smoke curled out of a doorway she passed, and through the window she glimpsed an old man on his knees before a chipped statue of Vesh-of-the-Tides, his lips moving, his hands open the way beggars hold them out.

Ivet did not stop. Stopping would mean seeing him, and seeing him would mean remembering that she had knelt that way once herself, as a girl, before her mother died, asking a god who still answered in those days for one more season of her mother's laughter.

The gods had gone quiet long before Casimir took the throne. Everyone said so. Everyone agreed the old prayers were like letters sent to a house where no one lived. It was easier to agree. Easier than admitting that maybe the gods had stopped answering because mortals had stopped deserving answers — or worse, that they had never answered at all, and every miracle of her childhood was a story grown generous in the telling.

But then the shrines had begun to rekindle.

Three weeks ago, in the village of Ashfield, a cold hearth-shrine had flamed alight with no hand near it. Ten days ago, a fisherman drowned off the Dunmarrow shallows and walked out of the water alive, weeping, saying something with too-old eyes had held him up. Five days ago, the first pilgrim had arrived at the gates with a scorch-mark shaped like nothing any of the city's scholars could name, and a tale of a woman walking east who left answered prayers behind her like footprints.

And two nights ago, the Harbor Shrine's dead bell had rung itself.

* * *

The Council chamber smelled of beeswax and fear.

Twelve seats ringed the round table of black harbor-oak; eleven were filled, and the twelfth — the seat of the shrine-keepers, unoccupied for a generation — sat empty like a missing tooth. Ivet took her place beneath the window, where the fog pressed gray against the glass, and unfolded her notes though she knew them by heart.

"Let us begin," said Magister Corvel, who chaired the session by right of seniority and clung to it the way drowning men cling to spars. He was a narrow man with a narrow voice, and he gavelled the air once with a knuckle. "First matter. The incident at the Harbor Shrine."

"The incident," said Osla Venn, merchant-principal of the salt trade, "in which a bell rang."

"A bell rang itself."

"Bells fall. Bells crack. Birds land on bells." Osla was a broad woman with forearms like mooring posts and a voice built for shouting across cargo decks; she used it now at half-volume, which was somehow worse. "You want to convene an emergency session over a bird."

"The ringer swears he did not touch it. The watchmen swear the tower was empty. And the bell rang thrice — the old summons, Osla. Three strokes. *Come and witness.* That is not a bird. That is a liturgy."

Murmurs moved around the table like wind through wheat. Ivet let them move. She had learned, in thirty years of councils, that the first quarter hour belonged to the frightened, and that wisdom consisted mostly of waiting for the fear to spend itself.

It did not spend itself today.

"Then let us speak plainly," said Corvel, when the murmuring crested. "Lord-Regent Casimir has declared all divine manifestation treason against the mortal realm. His armies march east. His inquisitors have burned four villages in Ashfield province that we know of, and the number grows weekly. If the gods are truly returning — if these manifestations are real — then harboring them, acknowledging them, so much as *discussing* them in this chamber puts every soul in this city under the usurper's sentence." He folded his hands. "I propose we send envoys to the Regent at once. Assurances of loyalty. Denials. Perhaps a gift."

"A gift," repeated Osla.

"Gold placates."

"Gold placates men. What do you propose to give a god, Corvel? Or an inquisitor, come to that? They don't want our gold. They want our compliance, and after that they want our shrines pulled down stone by stone, and after *that*—" She stopped herself. Ivet watched her jaw work. "My grandmother kept a tide-charm," Osla said, quieter. "Silly thing. Knotted cord. She swore by it, and I swore she was a fool, and last month my youngest daughter's fever broke on the third night, and I found myself sitting up with a knotted cord in my hands like a woman praying." She laid the cord on the table. It was frayed, sea-colored, ordinary. "I don't know what's happening. But I know I'm tired of pretending I know what's happening."

"That," said Corvel, "is precisely why decisions must be made by cooler heads—"

"Cooler heads." Osla laughed, short and hard. "Whose head is cool, Magister? Yours? You've chewed your thumbnail to the quick. Look at your own hand."

He looked, despite himself, and snatched it from the table, and the chamber almost laughed — almost, but not quite, because the fear underneath was too real for laughter to find footing.

Ivet chose her moment the way she chose tides: carefully, and only once.

"There is a third option," she said.

Eleven faces turned to her. Fog breathed against the window at her back like something listening.

"We speak of this as a choice between Casimir and the gods," Ivet continued. "Between bending the knee to the Regent or opening our gates to miracles. But there is a third party at this table, and it is the free cities themselves. Dunmarrow. Vellichor. Saltmere. Four hundred years we have survived every king, every war, every plague, precisely because we belong to no one — not to crowns, and not to heavens either. Our charter says nothing about gods. Our walls were built by merchants' gold and sailors' backs, and they will stand or fall by merchants' gold and sailors' backs." She spread her hands. "So I say: neither bargain. Not with Casimir, whose price is our souls. Not with the returning powers, whose price we do not yet know. We keep our own counsel. We shelter whoever comes to our gates needing shelter — pilgrim, witch, or fisherman — because that is what free cities *do*. And we prepare our walls, quietly, for whatever is coming down the eastern road."

"And if the Regent demands we surrender the pilgrims?" asked young Aldous Ren, who traded in dye and had a dye-merchant's instinct for how much color a thing could bear before it ran. "If his inquisitors arrive with lists of names?"

"Then we lie," said Osla.

"Then we negotiate," said Corvel.

"Then we decide," said Ivet, "when the moment comes, as free cities have always decided — together, and late, and badly, but ourselves." A few mouths twitched toward smiles. "I am not proposing courage, colleagues. Courage is expensive and I have priced it before. I am proposing *patience*. The storm hasn't reached us yet. Let us not sign treaties with the lightning."

For a moment — one breath, two — the chamber steadied. Pragmatism was Dunmarrow's mother tongue; they all spoke it fluently. Ivet felt the old familiar comfort of it settle over the table like a tarpaulin over cargo, and told herself the fraying at its edges was imagined.

Then the doors opened, and the fraying became a tear.

* * *

The messenger was a harbor-watch boy, soaked to the thighs, breathing like a landed fish. He had no right to be in the Council chamber and everyone present understood instantly that he must be.

"Beg pardon — begging pardon, Councilors — there's a — at the Gate of Chains, there's—"

"Out with it, lad," said Osla.

"A woman, Councilor. Come off the east road. Walking. And the shrine at the gatehouse—" The boy swallowed. "The shrine's been dead forty years, Councilor. Cold as the harbor floor. My da used to spit past it when I was small, said it was bad luck even to look at it. And when she came through the gate this morning, it lit. All by itself. Candle and lamp and the little oil-flame in the niche, all lit, and nobody lit them, and the woman just — kept walking. Like she hadn't done anything. Like she didn't know."

The silence afterward had texture. Ivet felt it press against her skin like the fog outside.

"What woman?" Corvel demanded. "Name? Description?"

"Don't know her name, Councilor. Dark-haired. Travel-worn. Keeps her left hand wrapped — bandaged, like she's hurt it. Bought bread at Maren's stall and paid honest and didn't say a word about — about any of it. She's taken a room at the Drowned Lantern. Under the name of—" the boy screwed up his face, "—something plain. Marsh, or Moss, or some such."

"Moss," Ivet heard herself say, very softly, and did not know why the word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Corvel was already talking — *seize her, question her, turn her out before the Regent hears* — and Osla was already answering him, and the chamber broke apart into a dozen overlapping arguments, and through all of it Ivet Sarn sat still beneath the fog-gray window and looked at her own hands.

They were shaking.

Not with fear, she realized. With something older and more terrible than fear. With hope. Thirty years she had buried it, and here it was clawing up through the grave-dirt of her pragmatism, ugly and blinking, because a stranger with a bandaged hand had walked past a dead shrine and the dead shrine had remembered how to burn.

*Hope*, she thought, *is the most expensive commodity on any market. And I have just bought a full cargo of it without haggling.*

* * *

She went to see the woman herself that evening, alone, which was foolish, and which she did anyway.

The Drowned Lantern stood at the crook of Rope Walk where the street gave up on cobblestones and surrendered to mud. Its sign depicted a lantern going under stylized waves, and its taproom was warm, low-beamed, and half-empty in the way of places that served honest food at honest prices. Ivet wore no chain of office, no council colors — just a wool cloak and her thirty years — and she took a table near the hearth and waited, because patience was the one virtue her profession had actually taught her.

The woman came down the stairs at dusk.

Ivet knew her at once, though they had never met. Not from the description — dark-haired, travel-worn, bandaged hand — but from something else, something in the way she carried herself through the room: a carefulness, a contained quality, like a banked fire seen through iron shutters. She moved like a person who had spent twenty years practicing how to take up as little space as possible, and had gotten frighteningly good at it.

She ordered stew. She ate it slowly, watching the door between spoonfuls. She did not light the candle on her table even as the room darkened, and Ivet understood that too — understood it the way one understands a scar's history without having been there for the wound.

When the woman rose to go back upstairs, Ivet stood.

"Forgive me," she said. "Councilor Ivet Sarn. Of the Dunmarrow Council of Merchants."

Something flickered across the stranger's face — weariness, chiefly, and beneath it a wary kind of humor, dry as August grass. "Is that a title or a warning?"

"Both, depending on the day." Ivet kept her voice low. "May I buy you a cup of whatever you're drinking?"

"I'm drinking water."

"Then may I buy you a better cup of water."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. The woman sat. Up close, Ivet saw the fine burn-scarring at the edge of the bandaged palm, saw the shadows under eyes that had not slept properly in weeks, saw the way the woman's gaze kept snagging on the hearth-fire as if it were speaking a language she'd sworn off.

"You're the one from the gate," Ivet said.

"I'm the one who bought bread."

"The shrine lit when you passed."

"Fires do that sometimes. Drafts. Old oil." The words were smooth, worn smooth, like stones carried a long way in a pocket. "You'll want to believe I caused it. Everyone does. It makes a tidier story than drafts."

"And did you?"

The woman looked at her then — really looked, and Ivet felt examined the way cargo is examined, thoroughly and without sentiment, weighed and assessed. Whatever the stranger found seemed to decide something.

"I don't know what I cause anymore," she said quietly. "That's the truth, Councilor. I came to Dunmarrow because it was far and it had walls and I wanted to stop. Just stop. Sleep in a bed. Eat bread that isn't hardtack. Be no one for a week." Her bandaged hand closed, opened. "But things keep happening around me, and I can't make them stop, and I don't know anymore whether I'm carrying something or *being carried*." She laughed, short and bitter. "You wanted honesty. There it is. Your free city has a problem walking around in boots, and I'm sorry for it, and I leave tomorrow."

"Where?"

"East."

Toward the thorn country. Toward the hollow places on the old maps where cartographers wrote *here be witches* in faded ink. Ivet felt the well-stone drop again, deeper this time.

"The Regent's army is east," she said. "His vanguard will reach the Ashfield crossings within the fortnight. His inquisitors are already closer than that — there are reports of a witch-finder, a chief among them, working the river roads. Hale, they call him. He burned the shrine-villages. He keeps lists." She leaned forward. "Woman-who-bought-bread. If you walk east alone, you will meet him. And whatever you are carrying, he will take it, or he will take you, and either way he will learn what happened at that gate, and then Dunmarrow's walls will not matter, because the Regent does not besiege cities he can simply *accuse*."

The stranger was silent a long time. The hearth popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, and both of them glanced at the stairs, and that shared glance — that small, involuntary alliance of the hunted — told Ivet more than an hour of questions would have.

"My mistress used to say," the woman said at last, and her voice changed, went somewhere younger and more wounded, "that the covenant doesn't ask if you're ready. It asks if you're *willing*. And that readiness is just willingness that's had time to regret it." She turned her bandaged hand over, stared at it. "I ran from her twenty years ago. I think — I think everything that's happening is because I ran. And now running east feels exactly like running did then, and I can't tell anymore whether I'm fleeing the Regent or fleeing the same thing twice."

"Then don't decide tonight," Ivet said. "Decide like a merchant. What do you need, what can you get, and who else wants it? You need passage, information, and time. I can offer the first two. As for the third—" she exhaled, and heard in her own voice the sound of pragmatic hope beginning, at last, to fracture along its grain, "—the third depends on a vote. Tomorrow the Council sits again, and I will stand before eleven frightened merchants and ask them to shelter a walking miracle they cannot control, while the most powerful tyrant in living memory marches toward our walls. And I do not know, Mistress-Moss-or-whatever-your-name-is, whether they will say yes. I don't know if *I* would say yes. Hope is expensive, and I have watched this city refuse to pay for cheaper things."

The stranger studied her. Then, unexpectedly, she reached across the table and touched Ivet's wrist — lightly, with the bandaged hand, and warmth bloomed there like sunlight through fog, brief and unmistakable, sinking into old aches Ivet had stopped noticing years ago.

"For the road," the woman said, standing. "Whatever you decide tomorrow. Thank you for not sending soldiers."

At the stairs she paused. "Bram," she said. "My name is Bram."

Then she was gone up into the lamplight, and Ivet Sarn sat alone at her table with warmth fading from her wrist and her ledger-heart open wide, and understood that the vote tomorrow no longer mattered — that it had never mattered — because the choice had already walked through the Gate of Chains on its own two feet, and all the Council could decide was whether to admit what had arrived.

Outside, far off across the fog-bound harbor, a bell began to ring.

One stroke. Two. Three.

The Harbor Shrine again — silenced by order, guarded by watchmen, dead for forty years — ringing the old summons into the dark:

*Come and witness.*

Ivet closed her eyes. Tomorrow she would argue, and maneuver, and count votes like coins. Tonight she simply sat and listened to the impossible bell, and prayed — for the first time in thirty years, helplessly, gratefully — to a god who might finally be listening back.
