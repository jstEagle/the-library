## 9. Hale Closes In

The rain in Dunmarrow smelled of fish and old money, and Inquisitor Hale hated it.

He stood at the harbor gate with his hood drawn up and his warrant-case dry beneath his oilcloth cloak, watching the free city breathe. Corvane had been a city of shutters and whispers; Dunmarrow was a city of noise — gulls screaming over the fish-cries, carters swearing in three languages, a fiddler playing on the customs-house steps as though the world had not begun to tilt. Somewhere beyond the eastern hills, the Lord-Regent's army was gathering like a storm that had not yet decided where to break. Here, the merchants counted their coin and pretended the weather would hold.

Hale did not pretend. He had spent eleven years learning that the weather always broke, and that it broke first over people like him — the ones who stood out in it so others could stay under roofs.

"Third shrine this month," said Sergeant Ovist beside him, huffing under the weight of the evidence satchel. "Rekindled overnight. No lamp-oil, no flint, no cause. The keeper's an old woman named Maren Doss. Lives above her candle-shop on Tallow Row."

"The same keeper who was seen speaking with a woman in grey three days past?"

"So the harbormaster's clerk says. Paid for passage east, then changed her mind and went inland instead. Toward the Ashfield road."

Toward the hollow. Hale kept the thought behind his teeth. He had learned long ago not to say certain things aloud; words had weight, and weight could be felt through walls by the kind of people he hunted.

They walked. Tallow Row was narrow and crooked, its upper stories leaning together like conspirators, and the candle-shop sat at the crook of it with a painted sign of a bee above the door. The smell of beeswax reached him before they knocked — warm, honeyed, innocent. It reminded him, absurdly, of his sister's room. Lissa had burned candles the way other girls burned letters: constantly, extravagantly, until their mother forbade it. *You'll set us all alight, little moth,* he used to tell her.

He put the memory away the way he put away all such things — carefully, in the dark, where it could not interfere.

Maren Doss opened the door herself. She was small and round and silver-haired, with flour or wax dusting her knuckles, and she looked up at the two men in rain-dark cloaks with the frank, unafraid eyes of someone who had buried two husbands and outlived every authority Dunmarrow had ever produced.

"Inquisitor," she said, reading his badge without being shown it. "I wondered when you'd come. The whole street wondered. You might have saved yourselves the walk; I'd have left the kettle on."

"You know why I'm here."

"I imagine you think I do." She stepped back from the doorway — an invitation, not a surrender. "Come in out of the wet, then. I don't confess to men dripping on my floor."

* * *

The shop's interior was warm gold. Candles in every stage of life lined the shelves — dipped tapers, molded saints, great altar-columns for the temples that had gone dark twenty years ago and were, lately and troublingly, going bright again. Hale noted the altar-candles especially. Half of them were sold out.

Ovist took the door. Hale sat in the offered chair and laid his warrant-case on his knees, open, the way he always did: not a threat displayed but a fact acknowledged. Maren Doss poured tea into mismatched cups and sat across from him with her hands folded, waiting, as though *he* were the one under examination.

"Three nights ago," Hale said, "a woman stayed with you."

"A woman has stayed with many people over seventy years. You'll want to be more particular."

"Grey traveling cloak. Dark hair going early to iron at the temples. A burn scar—" he turned his left hand palm-up, showing the ridge of old scar tissue across his own fingers, a habit of gesture rather than display, "—or perhaps no scar at all. She'd have paid in mixed coin, some of it foreign. She asked after the road east."

The old woman's eyes flickered — not toward the door, but toward the small shrine in the corner of the room. Hale followed the glance. It was a household thing, humble: a shelf, a bowl of water, a stub of candle burned down to a coin-sized pool. But the wax had run strangely, he saw. It had flowed *upward*, defying its own nature, and hardened there in a thin spire like a frozen flame.

And on the wall behind the shelf, faint as breath on glass, was a mark.

He rose without hurrying. The mark was scorched into the plaster — not painted, not chalked, but *burned*, though nothing near it showed heat damage. A circle broken at the top, crossed by a single descending line, with three small strokes radiating from the point of the crossing like the beginning of a thorn. He knew it the way he knew his own signature. He had drawn it four times now, in charcoal, in his case-book, each rendering more careful than the last.

Corvane. The smugglers' quarter, the night the raid went wrong and half a street woke to find their prayers answered and their doors scorched. A luck-seller's stall, abandoned, its wares still hanging — knots of colored thread that hummed, very faintly, when he touched them. And on the stall's lintel, this sigil, seared deep enough to survive the fire brigade's ladders and hoses.

The woman who had worked that stall was gone. But the sigil had traveled. It appeared in Ashfield, on the doorpost of a shrine that rekindled itself the night she passed through. It appeared again at a well outside Fenlow, where a dying child had risen from fever the same hour a woman in grey had stopped to drink. Each time, the mark was fresher. Each time, it was less like damage and more like — declaration.

"She left this," Hale said quietly. Not a question.

Maren Doss drank her tea. "She mended my roof-beam," she said. "And she lit that candle." A nod toward the impossible spire of upward-flowing wax. "I've kept a shrine to the Quiet Lady forty years, inquisitor. Forty years of lighting candles into a hole in the world and hearing nothing back. Then this girl comes in off the road, soaked through, looking like guilt itself had learned to walk, and she wouldn't come past my threshold until I told her she was welcome. Wouldn't eat my bread until I said it twice." The old woman's voice roughened. "And when she finally slept, that candle lit itself. And something answered me. For the first time in twenty years, *something answered me.*"

"What did it say?"

"That I was heard." Her chin came up. "That I had always been heard, and the silence was never silence, only distance. Do you know what that's worth to an old woman? Do you have any idea?"

Hale knew what silence was worth. He had lived inside one for eleven years, since the night his sister died.

* * *

Lissa had been a hedge-touched too — though no one used that word in their house, because their father was a magistrate and words like that got families visited. She'd had the knack for finding lost things. Keys, rings, sheep, once a drowning child pulled from the mill-race because Lissa woke at midnight saying *he's under the ice, he's under the ice* and would not be reasoned with. Their mother called it intuition. Their father called it nothing at all, loudly, whenever anyone suggested otherwise.

Then came the plague year, and the gods had already begun to go quiet, and Lissa prayed anyway, the way she did everything — constantly, extravagantly. She prayed to the Quiet Lady for the children in the fever-tents. And one night she went into the tent district against orders, to lay hands on the sick the way she laid hands on lost things, and the sickness took her in nine hours, and the god she prayed to said nothing. Nothing at all. The silence where an answer should have been was the loudest sound Hale had ever heard.

He had been twenty-two. He had stood at her bedside holding her hand while she asked him — begged him — *why won't she answer me, Wren, she always answered before, why is she leaving us?*

And he had not known. And no one had known. And the not-knowing had calcified, over eleven years, into the only shape that could hold it: someone had done this. Someone had broken whatever thread ran between the heavens and the earth, and that someone deserved finding, and he would be the man to find them.

That was the creed he had built his life inside. It had held his weight for a decade.

It was, he noticed with distant unease, beginning to creak.

* * *

"You're not listening," Maren Doss said.

Hale blinked. The tea had gone cold in his cup. He had been staring at the scorched sigil for — how long? Ovist shifted by the door.

"I'm listening."

"No. You're *collecting*. There's a difference. My second husband was a magistrate's clerk; I know the breed." She set down her cup. "You've already decided what she is. You came in here with your decision made and you're just gathering the pieces that fit it. So let me save you the trouble of asking the next question, because I can see it sitting behind your face like a stone behind a window."

"Madam—"

"She didn't want any of it." The old woman leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something fierce and low. "Whatever she carries, she carries it like a wound, not a weapon. She sat where you're sitting and shook. She said — I'll give you her words exact, because I've had three days to learn them by heart — she said, *'Every prayer that finds me takes something I don't have to give. Tell them to pray to someone else.'*" Maren's eyes were wet and furious. "Does that sound like the witch of your warrants, inquisitor? Does that sound like a woman who murdered your plague-dead?"

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted itself.

He had never spoken to her of Lissa. Not to Ovist, not to the Lord-Regent's clerks, not to anyone living. The dead sister was a locked room he carried with him, and no one had ever —

But he hadn't said *plague-dead*. Had he? He searched backward through the conversation and found the gap where the word should have been, and found nothing there, and felt the floor of his certainty shift like river gravel.

"Who told you about plague-dead?" His voice came out flat and strange.

Maren Doss frowned. "No one told me anything. Why would anyone—" She stopped. Her gaze went, slowly, to the corner shrine. To the candle that had burned itself into a spire against the laws of wax and fire. To the water in the offering-bowl, which had, Hale now saw, been still a moment ago and was now moving — circling, gently, counterclockwise, though no draft reached that corner.

"Oh," the old woman said softly. "Oh, you poor man."

"Answer the question."

"Nothing told me. Nothing *had* to." She was looking at him with an expression he had not seen directed at himself in eleven years — pity, unguarded and unashamed. "Inquisitor. What did you lose?"

The water circled. The candle-spire gleamed. And somewhere in the walls of the world, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Hale heard — or did not hear, or remembered hearing, which amounted to the same thing — a voice that was almost his sister's, young and impatient and extravagant, saying the thing she had said a hundred times across a hundred childhood rooms:

*You'll set us all alight.*

He stood up so fast the chair went over behind him.

* * *

Outside, the rain had thickened. Ovist caught his arm at the door. "Sir? Sir, your face — what happened in there?"

"Arrest her," Hale said. His voice sounded like someone else's, worn smooth and hollowed out, a channel where water had run too long. "Obstruction. Sheltering a proscribed practitioner. Take her to the customs cell and keep her apart from the other prisoners. No visitors."

"And the shop?"

"Seal it. Nothing leaves. Especially not—" he stopped himself before *the candle*, and rerouted. "Especially not the shrine."

Ovist went, boots ringing on the wet cobbles, and Hale remained alone in Tallow Row with the rain running down his collar and his hand pressed flat against the doorframe of the candle-shop, where the wood was old and soft and held, if a man pressed hard enough and believed hard enough, the warmth of forty years of small devotions.

*She didn't want any of it.* He made himself consider it, the way he considered all evidence, even evidence against himself. A fugitive who mended roofs. Who refused bread until welcomed. Who begged strangers to pray elsewhere. Who left behind her, everywhere she walked, not corpses but *answered prayers* — shrines relit, fevers cooled, wells sweetened, old women told they had been heard all along.

And what had the raids brought? Corvane: six wounded, one dead — a smuggler who had run a blade at Ovist, not magic's work. Ashfield: a frightened village, a fined shrine-keeper. Fenlow: nothing but a healed child and a frightened constable. Eleven years of hunting the murderers of the gods, and the body count ran entirely one direction, and the direction was *his*.

The sigil was not a threat, he thought. It had never been a threat. It was a trail — the way a bleeding man leaves a trail. Something marked, and hunted, and running.

Or something leading him precisely where it wanted him. The other thought arrived on schedule, cold and professional, and he clung to it briefly, gratefully, the way a drowning man clings to the log that is also pulling him under.

His hand ached. He looked down and found his fingernails had cut crescents into his own palm, and between them, faint as breath on glass, the skin was flushed pink in a pattern — a circle, a line, three small strokes.

No. He closed his fist. Rain, he told himself. Pressure. Nothing.

From inside the shop, muffled by walls and wax and the closing door of the world, he heard Maren Doss begin to sing — an old hymn to the Quiet Lady, one his mother had sung, one Lissa had sung off-key in the fever-tents to children who were going to die anyway, because Lissa believed the singing mattered even when the answer didn't come.

Hale stood in the rain until the song ended.

Then he went to find a map of the eastern roads, and the hollow at the end of them, and the woman whose name he had finally pried that morning from the harbormaster's clerk — Vasquez, Bram Vasquez — written in fresh ink in his case-book beneath a sigil that was no longer only evidence.

It was, he understood now, an invitation. And he was afraid — with a fear that felt disgracefully like hope — of what would be waiting when he accepted it.
