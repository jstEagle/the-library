## 10. The Thorn Hollow

Three days out of Ashfield, the road gave up on them.

It did not end so much as forget itself — first a cart-track, then a footpath, then only a suggestion of passage through bracken and gorse, as though the land had decided long ago that no one worth guiding would come this way. Bram followed it anyway, because Vesh walked ahead of her in the body of a beggar named Odd Wren, and because there was nowhere else left to go.

The god moved strangely for a man. He favored his left leg on the uphill stretches and not on the downhills, which no honest limp would do. He ate when she ate, slept when she slept — or appeared to — but twice she had woken before dawn to find him standing motionless at the edge of their little fire, watching the dark with an expression no beggar's face should have been able to hold: patient, vast, faintly sorrowful, like weather deciding where to fall.

"Are we close?" she asked on the third morning. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She had been asking less and less, dreading the answer more and more.

Vesh turned. In the grey light his eyes were the same too-old eyes from the alley in Corvane, the ones that had seen her name written somewhere before she'd ever spoken it aloud.

"You know we are," he said. "You've known since the river."

She had. The river had told her — that was the only way to put it. Crossing the shallows below the ruined mill two days past, she had felt the water notice her, the way a dog notices its master's hand on a stranger's shoulder. And beyond the water, the land had begun to feel familiar in a way that had nothing to do with memory and everything to do with the stone.

Because the covenant-stone rode against her breastbone now, wrapped in oilcloth and bound tight beneath her coat, and it had not been quiet since Ashfield. It warmed when she prayed without meaning to. It pulsed when she passed the rekindled shrines, those little wayside niches where frightened folk had lit candles to gods they'd half forgotten, and it pulled — always, gently, ceaselessly — eastward. Toward the hills. Toward the hollow.

Toward Tamsin.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, up out of the farmland into country where the trees grew old and crooked and the air smelled of wet leaf-rot and something sweeter underneath, something flowering. Bram knew that sweetness. She had spent twenty years refusing to know it.

Hawthorn. May-blossom out of season, in the dying weeks of autumn.

"Stop," she said.

Vesh stopped. Ahead of them the path bent around a shoulder of granite furred with moss, and beyond it she could see a fold in the hills, a green declivity where mist pooled even now, even in daylight, like breath held too long.

"I know this place." Her mouth had gone dry. "I ran down that path. There was frost on the grass and I remember thinking — I remember thinking the frost looked like salt, like the whole world had been seasoned for grief." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I was seventeen. I thought everything was poetry back then. Tamsin used to say I'd grow out of it."

"And did you?"

"No." She hugged her arms around herself. "I just got better at hiding it. Same as the magic."

Vesh said nothing. That was one of the things she had learned about him — about *it* — in three days of hard road: the god did not fill silences. He let them stand open like doorways and waited to see what you would carry through.

Bram stood in the doorway of this one a long moment. Then she walked past him, around the granite shoulder, and looked down into the Thorn Hollow for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

Her mind had kept a map of the place, and the map was wrong in every particular except one.

In her memory the hollow had been small and domestic: a cup of hillside holding a cottage with a turf roof, a kitchen garden gone riotous with herbs, a beehive under the elder tree, a washing line, a woodpile, a well with a bucket that groaned on its rope. A place that smelled of bread and beeswax and the sharp green bite of tansy. A place where a woman with iron-grey braids and ink-stained fingers had taught her the names of things — the true names, the old names — and had loved her with a ferocity neither of them had ever once spoken aloud.

The cottage was still there. She could see the turf roof, sagging but whole, through a gap high in the wall of thorn.

But everything else —

The thorn had swallowed the hollow entire.

It rose from the rim of the depression on every side, a rampart of blackthorn and hawthorn grown together into something no longer quite plant, woven dense as basketry and tall as a church tower, arched over at the top so that the hollow within lay in permanent twilight. Bram could see no gate, no gap, no seam. The wall simply went on and on, following the curve of the land, and where it met the granite shoulder it climbed the rock itself and rooted into the cracks, gripping the hill like a fist.

And it was blooming. Out of season, out of reason, the thorn was covered in white blossom, thousands upon thousands of five-petaled flowers, and beneath the sweetness of them lay another smell — older, greener, faintly metallic. Bram had stood in enough sickrooms to know blood when she smelled it, even old blood, even blood soaked into wood.

"Twenty years," she whispered. "It's been growing twenty years."

"It began growing the night you left," Vesh said quietly. He had come to stand beside her, and up close his beggar's stoop fell away; he seemed suddenly taller than any man had a right to be, though his shape hadn't changed. "Grief is a seed, Bram Amberdale. Most griefs die in poor soil. Hers did not."

"She did this?" Bram's voice cracked on it. "With her magic? One night of — of being angry, and she built *this*?"

"Not anger." Vesh's too-old eyes stayed fixed on the blossoms. "Anger builds fires. This is what abandonment grows, when the abandoned one has power and nowhere to spend it. Every night she mourned, the wall took root deeper. Every year you did not come back, it flowered again. You are looking at twenty years of a woman calling your name into a hedge that will not answer."

Bram's legs nearly went out from under her. She sat down hard on the cold grass, right there at the rim of the hollow, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and for a while she could not have said whether she was breathing or merely shaking in a rhythm that resembled it.

She had told herself the story for twenty years. She knew every word of it by heart, had polished it smooth as a river stone with handling: *I stole the stone because she was going to bind me to a dying pact. I ran because she wanted my whole life and I was young and I wanted a life. She was cruel. She was wrong. She never came after me, and that proves she didn't love me the way I needed to be loved.*

And here was the wall. Here was the proof, grown in living wood, that Tamsin had loved her exactly that much — and more, and worse, and in the only language a hedge-witch of the old covenant had ever been taught to speak: the language of working. Of making. Of taking unbearable feeling and giving it roots and thorns and a shape the world had to reckon with.

*I never came looking,* Bram thought. *She must have known I wouldn't. She built the wall anyway. Or maybe she built it hoping I would come, and have to fight my way in, and prove—*

Prove what?

That she was sorry. That she was worthy. That she had finally become someone who deserved to walk back through a door she'd slammed.

"Oh, mistress," she said aloud, to nobody, to the blossom, to the woman behind the wall. "What did I do to you?"

The wind moved through the hawthorn, and ten thousand white petals shivered, and none of them fell.

* * *

She made camp at the rim that night, though Vesh told her the hollow's heart was less than a hundred paces from where she sat. She couldn't. Not yet. Her body understood something her mind was still arguing with: that crossing that last hundred paces would end one life and begin another, and she had ended a life once already, at seventeen, in frost that looked like salt, and she remembered what it cost.

She built a small fire and cooked nothing and stared into it.

The stone woke against her sternum as full dark came down. Not painfully — not as it had in the cellar in Corvane, when it had burned her name into her palm in letters she still carried, fine silver scarring she hid beneath a work-glove. This time it woke like a sleeper turning toward a familiar sound. Warmth spread through her ribs, and with the warmth came images that were not hers:

A kitchen. Bread rising under a cloth. A girl of twelve grinding dried yarrow, tongue between her teeth, while a woman with iron-grey braids corrected her grip on the pestle — *gently, child, you're bruising it, everything that gives us medicine deserves gentleness* —

Bram's breath caught. The vision faded, leaving the taste of woodsmoke and a sting behind her eyes.

"It's showing me her," she said. "Memories. Are these mine, or—"

"Hers, given to the stone. Yours, returned by it. By the end there will be no difference, and that is the point." Vesh sat across the fire, and the flames did strange things in his eyes, burning in them without reflecting. "The covenant was never a contract between strangers, Bram. It was a marriage of realms. Mortal need answered by divine mercy, divine hunger fed by mortal devotion, and a steward standing in the doorway between, belonging wholly to neither and wholly to both. When you were a child, the stone chose you. Do you understand what that means? Not Tamsin choosing you. Not the old king's line claiming you. The *stone*. The pact itself looked at a hedge-witch's foundling apprentice and said: *this one.*"

"I stole it."

"Yes. And it let you. And it waited." He tilted his head, birdlike, ancient. "Do you know why it waited twenty years and woke in a smuggler's cellar the very week the gods resumed answering prayers? Because a covenant-stone sleeps while its pact holds, and wakes when the pact breaks. Casimir broke it. Regicide severs the oldest bonds — the throne was part of the working, though no king has known it for centuries. The night Casimir murdered the old royal house, every covenant in the realm began to die. Tamsin's wall began to die with it. Can you feel it?"

She could, now that he said it. Beneath the overwhelming sweetness of the blossom there was a wrongness, a thinness, like music played on a string about to snap. The wall was vast and alive and *failing*, all at once, the way a great tree fails — slowly, invisibly, and then all at once.

"If the wall dies," Bram said slowly, working it through, "and the stone dies, and the pact stays broken—"

"The gods go silent forever. True silence, this time. No more answered prayers, no more shrines relighting, no more mercy at the boundary. And the usurper wins without striking a blow, because a people who cannot reach the divine will kneel to anyone who feeds them." Vesh smiled, and it was a terrible gentle thing. "Or a steward reforges the pact, and everything changes, and nothing is ever ordinary again. You have read the price by now. The stone has shown you."

Bram touched her breastbone through her coat. *No more running,* the stone had murmured to her, in visions, in dreams, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. *Mid-stride, forever.*

"I've read it," she said.

"And still you came."

"Still I came." She looked up at the wall of thorn, black branches and white blossom against the stars. "Not for the pact. Not at first. I came because—" Her voice failed, steadied. "Because there's a woman in there who taught me the names of things, and I left her alone with her grief for twenty years, and whatever the rite costs, I owe her the walk to her own front door. The rest is duty. This part is mine."

For the first time since the alley in Corvane, Vesh looked at her with something that was not patience, not sorrow, not vastness. It looked, improbably, like pride.

"Then sleep, steward," he said. "Tomorrow the thorn will ask its question."

"What question?"

"The only one it was ever grown to ask."

* * *

Dawn came up grey and slow, mist pooling in the hollow's rim like milk in a bowl's bottom. Bram stood before the wall with her pack on her back and the stone warm against her heart, and Vesh stood a respectful distance behind, and the hawthorn waited.

Up close, she could see the wall was not uniform. Near the base, the branches were oldest — black, gnarled, thick as her waist in places, their bark furrowed like a face that had done decades of weeping. Higher up, the growth was younger, more frantic, whip-thorned and tangled. And everywhere, at every height, the white blossom, open in the dawnless light, drinking a sun that wasn't there.

There was no gate. She circled a quarter of the wall's circumference with her palm trailing the bark, and found no gate, no gap, no loosened place. The thorn had grown whole, seamless, a wound closed over the hollow's heart.

At some point she realized she was crying, and let herself, briefly, without shame. Twenty years of shame was enough. She wiped her face on her sleeve the way Tamsin had taught her — *from the eyes outward, child, always outward, grief moves away from the source or it pools* — and stood back, and drew a long breath that tasted of blossom and old blood.

"How do I get in?" she asked.

"The thorn parts," Vesh said, "for blood freely offered."

Bram went very still. "Whose?"

"That is its question." The god's voice was soft. "It will take blood offered in grief, or in guilt, or in fear — and it will close again behind such an offering, because grief and guilt and fear are walls themselves, and what builds a wall cannot unbuild one. But blood offered freely — in longing, in love, in the plain and terrible wish to come home — that blood the thorn drinks and remembers, and opens, and does not close again. Your mistress built a door into her despair, Bram. Even at her worst, even in the deepest night of it, she left a way in for you. She just could not make it easy. Grief never can."

Bram looked at her right hand — the marked one, the one the stone had branded in a Corvane cellar, silver sigil-scars across the palm in the shape of the old covenant knot. Then she looked at the knife at her belt, the good hedge-witch knife with the horn handle, the one tool she had never sold, never pawned, never explained to any customer in twenty years of selling luck-knots and fever-salves in a city that had forgotten the gods.

*Tamsin gave me this knife. My fourteenth birthday. For the harvest cutting, she said. A witch should always cut with something that loves her.*

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Underneath the dread, underneath the longing that had been climbing her throat for three days like ivy, there was a stillness she recognized from the deep of the rite-chamber, from the one time Tamsin had let her watch a binding worked — the stillness that comes when every part of you finally agrees on the same thing.

She bared her left forearm. She set the knife's edge against the skin, just below the elbow, where the old women of the villages drew blood for the spring shrines, back when there were spring shrines.

"I ran," she said aloud. Her voice carried strangely in the mist, flat and clear, swallowed at once by the blossom-hung silence. "I stole from you and I ran and I let you think you weren't worth coming back for, when it was me who wasn't brave enough to stay. I'm not sorry I left, because sorry is a coin and you're owed more than coin. I'm *here*. That's the whole of my offering. I'm here, and I'm not going anywhere, and if you want my blood to believe it — take it. Freely. Gladly. Take it home to her."

She cut.

The pain was bright and clean and shockingly small, a red thread welling along her forearm, dripping from her fingertips onto the roots of the thorn. And the thorn *drank* — she saw it happen, saw the dark bark darken further, wet, glistening, saw the moisture vanish inward as though the wall had a throat — and the whole rampart shuddered, top to bottom, a tremor passing through twenty years of growth like wind through wheat.

Then, with a sound like a sigh the size of a cathedral, the thorn began to move.

Branches thick as masts groaned and swung aside. Whip-thorn laced with blossom drew back like curtains. A passage opened before her, walled in living blackthorn, arched overhead with white flowers, leading down into the green twilight of the hollow — and at the end of it, perhaps fifty paces distant, she could see it: the cottage, the turf roof, the door.

The door was open.

And standing in it, leaning on a staff, straightening slowly as if she had risen from a chair she'd occupied for a very long time, was a figure Bram knew better than her own heartbeat — smaller than memory, whiter than memory, iron-grey braids gone to snow — but unmistakable, impossible, *alive*.

Tamsin looked at her across fifty paces of parted thorn, and her face did something complicated and breaking, and her voice, when it came, was rust and flint and twenty years of unsaid words:

"Well," said Tamsin. "You took your time."
