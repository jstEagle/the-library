## 11. Twenty Years of Silence

The thorn-wall did not look like a wall. It looked like a decision the land had made and never unmade.

Bram stood at its edge as the last light failed, her cloak heavy with road-dust and Ashfield ash, and stared at what twenty years of grief had grown. The hedge rose higher than the cottage it hid — she could see the roofline's ghost behind it, a dark suggestion of slate and chimney — and it rose wider than any hedge had a right to be, swallowing the garden, the well, the whole of the hollow until there was nothing in the world but bramble. The thorns were black at their bases and blood-dark toward their tips, each one curved like a question mark, like a hook, like a hand reaching out to catch something that never came.

*She grew this,* Bram thought. *The night I left. She grew this.*

Vesh stood beside her in his beggar's shape, hands folded into his sleeves, and said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was the old voice, the deep one, the one that had sung her to sleep in Tamsin's kitchen when she was small enough to be sung to.

"She will not open it for words."

"I know."

"She will not open it for tears."

"I know that too." Bram touched the nearest thorn. It was warm. That was the worst part — not dead wood, not winter-killed brush, but something alive and waiting, holding its breath the way a person holds a breath when they hear footsteps on a road they've stopped expecting anyone to walk.

"Blood," Vesh said. "Freely offered. Not taken, not spilled in anger. Given." His too-old eyes turned to her, and in them she saw the reflection of the wall, and something else besides — something that might have been sorrow, if gods could feel it honestly. "You understand what I am asking you to give."

Bram looked at her palm. The name the stone had burned into it had faded to a pale silver weal, but it still ached at dusk, every dusk, like a clock striking an hour only she could hear. She thought of all the small cuts she'd hidden over twenty years — kitchen nicks, splinter wounds, the shallow slice from a smuggler's knife in Corvane — and how she'd pressed them closed and told herself pain was just pain, ordinary and meaningless, the body's dumb arithmetic.

Nothing about her body had been ordinary since the day Tamsin first pricked her finger over a charm-bowl and said, *There. Now the magic knows your taste.*

"I've been giving it pieces of myself my whole life," Bram said. "Might as well give it where it'll do some good."

She drew her belt-knife.

* * *

Tamsin felt the wall shudder before she heard anything, before the kettle even finished singing on the hob.

Twenty years she had lived inside the bramble, and she knew its moods the way she'd once known Bram's — the restless rustle before storms, the sullen stillness of drought weeks, the strange contented creak it made on summer nights when the covenant's pulse ran strong through its roots. But this was none of those. This was the sound of a held door. Of hinges remembering their purpose.

She set down the kettle with a hand that did not shake, because she had practiced not shaking for twenty years, and walked to the window.

Beyond the thorns, a figure stood with a knife against her own arm. Small. Road-worn. That stubborn set to the shoulders that Tamsin would have known in a crowd of thousands, in the dark, in death.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, to no one, to the girl, to the gods who had abandoned her and were now apparently sending her ghosts. "No, you don't. You don't get to come back."

The figure drew the blade along her forearm, quick and clean, the way Tamsin herself had taught her — *don't saw at it, child, respect the wound or it won't respect you* — and pressed her bleeding arm against the wall.

The thorns parted like a crowd making way for a funeral.

Tamsin watched the gap open, watched firelight from her own window spill through it onto the face of the girl she had raised, and felt twenty years of carefully banked fury come off its coals all at once, roaring up her throat like a chimney fire.

She took the iron poker from beside the hearth and went out to meet her.

* * *

The passage through the wall was long — longer than the hollow was wide, which should have been impossible, which meant the wall had grown inward as well as outward, a labyrinth of grief with a house at its center. Bram walked it with her bleeding arm cradled against her chest, and the thorns leaned away from her blood like flowers turning from shade toward sun. Where her drops fell, small white flowers opened on the black canes above, blooming and browning and falling in the space of a breath. The air smelled of green things and old smoke and, beneath it, faintly, of bread. Her stomach clenched. Twenty years, and the smell of Tamsin's bread could still undo her faster than any inquisitor.

Then the labyrinth ended, and there was the cottage.

It was smaller than she remembered. Everything was smaller than she remembered — the crooked chimney, the herb beds gone wild and leggy, the rain barrel furred with moss. But the door was the same door, blue paint gone gray, the horseshoe above it still nailed points-down so the luck poured out onto whoever crossed. And standing in that doorway, backlit by her own hearth, was a woman made almost entirely of angles now, sharp-shouldered and thin, with hair gone from crow-black to a fierce iron white and eyes that Bram knew the way she knew her own heartbeat.

Tamsin held an iron poker the way a soldier holds a spear.

"You," Tamsin said.

"Hello, Tam—"

"Don't." The word cracked across the garden like a whip. "Don't you *hello* me. Don't you stand there bleeding on my ground with that face." The poker trembled; Tamsin stilled it with visible effort, the way she'd once stilled her hands over a failing patient. "Twenty years. Twenty years, Bram Vasquez. I dug your grave. Did you know that? First frost of the first winter, I dug a grave behind the east bed because I couldn't bear the empty one, and I buried your spare cloak in it, and I *grieved* you, and then the wall grew and I understood I hadn't buried you at all. I'd buried the version of you that loved me. The other one got away clean."

"I didn't get away clean," Bram said quietly. "There's no such place."

"Don't philosophize at me. You're not old enough to philosophize at me." But the poker lowered, an inch, two. Tamsin's gaze dropped to Bram's arm, to the welling cut, and something moved through her face like weather — the healer's reflex, ancient and involuntary. "That's too deep. Come inside before you—"

"No." Bram heard her own voice and barely recognized it. Steady. The steadiness cost her everything she had. "No. We do this out here, or I turn around and walk back through, and you can keep your silence another twenty years. Why didn't you tell me? About the bloodline. About what I was. What the covenant needed me to be."

Tamsin went very still. In the doorway light, Bram could see the years carved into her — the deep lines around her mouth, the cords of her neck, the way she held her left hand slightly curled, as if it ached. Sustained by the covenant's dying pulse, Vesh had said. Kept alive by a pact that was starving.

"You were a child," Tamsin said at last.

"I was sixteen."

"You were a *child*, and the covenant would have eaten you alive, and I—" Tamsin's voice broke, and she hated it, Bram could see her hating it, hating the crack the way she'd hate a flaw in a charm-stone. "I swore an oath to the old gods, girl. I also swore, the night they put you in my arms, that no god would ever spend you the way they spent your mother. So yes. I lied to you. I let you believe you were nobody, a foundling, a hedge-witch's charity case, because a nobody gets to choose her life. A steward doesn't. A steward is chosen, and chosen means *taken*."

"And instead I chose nothing," Bram said. "I chose Corvane. I chose luck-knots and fever-salves and lying awake listening to whether my neighbors' prayers got answered. I chose twenty years of being half a person, Tamsin, because you taught me that the truest thing about me was a secret worth running from."

"I taught you how to survive!"

"You taught me how to *hide*. There's a difference, and I've had twenty years on the road to learn it." Bram's arm was throbbing now, blood tracking down her wrist and dripping from her fingertips, and where each drop struck the earth the white flowers bloomed and died, bloomed and died, a little graveyard of blossoms between them. "Casimir killed the king. Casimir severed the pact. None of that was mine. But the stone woke in *my* cellar, and the inquisitors burned *my* street, and every mile from here to Dunmarrow somebody knelt in the road and begged *me* for a miracle I didn't know how to refuse. Your lie didn't protect me. It just delayed the bill."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere beyond the wall, an owl called, and the thorns rustled with something that was almost a sigh.

"You have her hands," Tamsin said suddenly, and her voice came out ragged. "Your mother's hands. She used to gesture like that when she was angry — all fingers, like she was throwing the words at people." She set the poker down against the doorframe, deliberately, the way one disarms oneself in front of someone one has decided not to fight. "Come inside. Your arm needs binding, and the kettle's boiled twice, and I am not discussing regicide and divine pacts in a garden like a pair of fishwives."

"Tamsin."

"*What.*"

"I'm not staying." Bram watched the words land, watched them hit like the poker would have. "I came back because the covenant has to be reforged and I'm apparently the only thread left to forge it with. After that—" She faltered. All that steadiness, spent. "After that, I don't know what I am. But I'm not the girl who ran, either. I need you to know that. Whoever walks out of here when this is done, it isn't her."

Tamsin looked at her for a long, long moment. The hearthlight caught the silver in her brows, the wet sheen she was refusing to let become tears. When she spoke, her voice was low and rough as bark.

"The girl who ran is the only daughter I ever had," she said. "So you'll forgive me if I hold out hope for whoever comes next." She stepped back from the doorway, into the warmth, into the smell of bread and herbs and twenty years of one woman living alone with a wall around her heart. "Well? Are you going to bleed out in my garden out of pride, or are you coming in? Because I raised you better than that, whatever else I failed at."

And Bram — weary past exhaustion, aching in the palm and the arm and the twenty-year-old bruise beneath both — crossed the threshold.

* * *

Inside, the cottage was a museum of her absence.

Bram saw it as Tamsin moved through the room fetching linen and the brown glass bottle of comfrey tincture: the second chair still at the table, kept oiled and usable, its cushion plumped. The apprentice cot in the corner, made up with fresh blankets — *fresh*, recently washed, not twenty years of dust. The shelf where Bram's early charms had sat, the crooked luck-knots and lopsided salve jars of a clumsy beginner, all of them still there, dusted, arranged, preserved like relics.

"You kept them," Bram said, while Tamsin wrapped her forearm with hands that were gentler than her tongue.

"They were well-made." Tamsin tied off the bandage with a sharp tug. "Eventually. After the fourth attempt at everything." She wouldn't look at the shelf. "Sit. Eat. There's stew, and it's been simmering since noon because I—" She stopped. Started again, flinty. "Because I cook for two. Habit. Don't read anything into it."

"I wouldn't dare."

"See that you don't." Tamsin ladled stew into two bowls and set them down hard enough to rattle the table, and then, instead of sitting, she stood at the window with her back to Bram, arms folded, watching the thorn-wall drink the last of the moonrise. "The stone woke, you said. In Corvane."

"In my cellar. It burned my name into my hand." Bram flexed the marked palm. "An inquisitor named Hale has my sigil off a scorch-mark. He's good, Tamsin. He's grieving and he's good, and that's the most dangerous kind. And Casimir knows the covenant can be reforged — Vesh thinks he'll want me taken alive now, not dead. They're coming. Maybe days behind me. Maybe less."

"Then you'd best eat quickly," Tamsin said, still facing the window, "because reforging the covenant is not a thing done in an afternoon, and I will not have you botch it the way you botched the weather-charm of your fourteenth year." She turned then, and her eyes were terrible and bright and full of a love so fierce it had curdled into commandment. "You stole my stone and broke my heart and left me to grow a wall around the ruin, and now you've bled your way back in, and you think you get to do this in *days*?"

"I think I don't have a choice."

"You never had a choice," Tamsin said. "That's the whole cursed point of you. Sit up straight. We start tonight."

Outside, far to the west, past the wall and the hollow and the sleeping hills, Bram felt — rather than heard — something vast begin to move. Armies have a sound below sound, a pressure like a storm deciding. And in her burned palm, the silver scar woke and pulsed, once, twice, like a second heart answering a first.

Tamsin saw her flinch, and her jaw tightened.

"How long," she said quietly, "since you slept a whole night through?"

"I don't remember."

"Then eat," said Tamsin, "and then sleep, and then we work. Whatever's coming—" she glanced at the window, at the wall grown from her own grief, at the girl she'd mourned and raged at and set a place for every single night for twenty years "—it will find us ready. Both of us. Together. That's the only prayer I've got left in me, Bram Vasquez, and I'm telling you now: the gods had better answer it."

In Bram's palm, the scar pulsed again — and this time, unmistakably, it answered.
