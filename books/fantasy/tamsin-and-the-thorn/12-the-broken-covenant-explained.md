## 12. The Broken Covenant Explained

Tamsin had not slept. She doubted she would sleep again, now, with Bram's blood drying on the thorn-cut floorboards and the covenant-stone sitting between them on the table like a guest neither of them had invited.

Twenty years of rehearsing this conversation, and every rehearsal had been a lie. In the good versions, Tamsin was stern and Bram was contrite, and the words came out clean as well-water. In the bitter versions — the ones that had kept her company through the long winters, when the thorn-wall groaned against the wind and the cottage felt less like a home than a wound that refused to close — Tamsin said *I told you so* and *you were a child and you broke us both*, and Bram wept, and some small cruel thing in Tamsin's chest was fed at last.

None of those versions had prepared her for this: Bram sitting across from her with her hands wrapped around a cup of nettle tea she wasn't drinking, looking older than twenty-eight ought to allow and younger than Tamsin remembered all at once, waiting to be told why.

*Why did you never come after me,* her face said. *Why did you let me believe I was nothing.*

Tamsin set down her own cup. Her hands shook. She let them.

"You asked me," she began, and her voice came out like something dragged over gravel, "on the night you left. You asked me what the stone was for. Do you remember what I answered?"

Bram's jaw tightened. "You said it wasn't my concern."

"I lied." The word cost more than Tamsin expected. Twenty years of lying, and still each one paid its toll. "I lied because telling you the truth would have made you a target before you were ready, and because—" She stopped. Breathed. The fire popped in the hearth, throwing shadows up the walls where bundles of dried yarrow hung like hanged men. "Because I was a coward, girl. There. That part at least is true."

"Then tell me now."

So Tamsin told her.

* * *

"Before there were kings," she said, "there were stewards. That's the part they've burned out of every book in Corvane, every chronicle, every song. The usurper's grandfather started the burning; Casimir finished it. But once — and I mean once, in the way that mountains are once, in the way that rivers are once — the gods and mortals made a bargain, and the bargain needed a hinge. A door that stood in both worlds at the same time."

"The stone," Bram said quietly.

"The stone is the seal. The steward is the door." Tamsin rose, joints protesting, and went to the shelf above the hearth where she kept the things she could not bear to look at and could not bear to throw away. A child's carved wooden bird. A cracked mortar. And a book wrapped in oilcloth, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin. She set it on the table but did not open it. Not yet. "The gods are vast, Bram. Vaster than you can imagine, vaster than I can, and I've spoken with them since before your mother drew breath. Vastness cannot hear small things. A prayer is a very small thing — a sparrow's voice in a storm. So the covenant gave the gods an ear inside the world. A mortal heart, beating in both realms at once, that could gather every whispered need and carry it across. In exchange, the gods answered. Rain for the rain-starved. Healing for the fevered. Justice for the murdered — oh, especially justice for the murdered. The old gods never let a death go unwitnessed."

"And the steward was—"

"Of royal descent. Always." Tamsin sat back down heavily. "That was the price the gods set, and no one remembers why anymore, though I have my suspicions. Kings were meant to be servants, do you see? The covenant reached into the palace itself and made the crown kneel. Every royal house carried the steward's line hidden in it, generation upon generation, and most of them never knew. They lived ordinary lives — married, quarreled, died of bad teeth — while somewhere else, a hedge-witch or a shrine-keeper trained the one who'd been called. The stone chose. It always chose. It slept until the old steward died, and then it woke, and it burned the new one's name into their palm, and the training began."

The firelight moved on Bram's face. On her right hand, the mark had faded to a pale silver scar, but Tamsin could see it still catching the light, the sigil of the old royal house of Vael — the stag crowned in thorns — rendered in lines fine as spider silk.

"You knew," Bram said. "Before the stone woke. You knew what I was."

"I suspected from the day you arrived on my doorstep." Tamsin's throat closed around the memory: a girl of eight, mud to the knees, eyes too old for her face, clutching a bundle that held everything she owned. "You had the look. Stewards are born marked — a certain stillness in them, a way of listening that isn't quite natural. Your grandmother was a daughter of the Vael cadet line, scattered when the purges came. I'd been searching for thirty years by then. Searching, and finding nothing but dead ends and graves." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "And then the stone woke in my keeping — early, years early, the old steward barely cold — and it led me east like a compass needle, and there you were. Eight years old. Half-starved. Already running from something."

"From my uncle," Bram said. "He sold me to the tannery."

"I know. I bought you out of it the same week." Tamsin spread her hands flat on the table, steadying herself. "I took you in as an apprentice first and a steward second, and I swore to myself the order would never matter. That you'd be Tamsin's girl before you were the realm's door. And for ten years, it didn't matter. For ten years you were just — mine. Learning knots and salves and which mushrooms lie. Arguing about everything. Burning the bread." Her voice caught. She pushed past it. "The stone stayed quiet. The covenant held. The gods answered prayers the way they always had, quietly, through people like me, and no one thought much about any of it, because that's how a healthy covenant works, Bram. Like a heartbeat. You don't notice it until it stops."

* * *

"It stopped," Bram said, "the night Casimir killed the king."

"The night Casimir killed the whole royal house." Tamsin finally opened the book. The pages were foxed and soft, written in three hands across two centuries — her own hand youngest, cramped and hurried. Diagrams crawled across the paper: the stone, the sigil, the genealogies of a family tree pruned by fire. "Every drop of Vael blood he could find, drowned or burned or vanished, in a single season. He thought he was securing his throne. What he actually did was cut the realm's throat. The steward's line ended in blood, and the covenant — which was never a law, only a promise, and promises need living mouths — the covenant began to die."

"And the gods stopped answering."

"Not all at once. At first it was like a man going deaf — the prayers still came, but fainter, muffled, arriving late. Then the great ones stopped entirely. Rain-prayers over Ashfield went unanswered for three seasons running. Dying women screamed for their goddess and heard nothing but their own hearts. And the gods—" Tamsin's hand trembled on the page. "The gods began to forget. That's the horror of it, girl. A god who cannot hear mortal need begins to forget why it ever mattered. They grow distant. Cold. Some of them sleep. Some of them—" she thought of Vesh, of the beggar's too-old eyes, of the god who had walked beside Bram out of Corvane wearing borrowed flesh, "—some of them hold on. Barely. Remembering one face, one name, one debt. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For a door." Tamsin looked up. "For you."

The silence stretched between them, thin as ice over deep water.

"I found out what Casimir had done six months after it happened," Tamsin went on, quieter now. "Word travels slow to the hollow, but it travels. And I understood immediately — not the full shape of it, not yet, but enough. Enough to know that if the wrong people learned a scrap of Vael blood had survived, they'd come for you. Casimir had men whose whole trade was hunting the last embers of the old house. So I did what I'd sworn I'd never do. I put the secret above the girl. I sealed the stone deeper. I stopped teaching you anything that mattered. I let you think your lessons were ending because you'd failed them." She met Bram's eyes. "You hadn't failed anything. I was burying you alive, and calling it protection."

Bram's breath left her slowly. "The night I left. You told me I had no talent. You said—"

"I said you'd never be more than a hedge-witch, and that you should take your pride to the city and make a living selling luck-knots." Each word came out like a splinter. "Yes. I said that. I engineered your leaving, Bram. I couldn't tell you the truth, and I couldn't keep you and watch Casimir's hunters find you in my arms, so I made you hate me instead. I made leaving your idea. I made it your rebellion, your escape, your triumph — so you'd run far, and fast, and never look back, and never wonder why the road behind you was safe."

"You buried the stone the same night."

"After you'd gone. I walked it down into the cellar dark and I laid wards over it that cost me a year of my own strength, and I told the hollow — I told the thorns—" Her voice broke clean through. She let it. There was no dignity left to protect; there never had been. "The thorn-wall grew that night. From my grief, the old books call it, though grief is too gentle a word. The land itself took my despair and grew it into a wall, and I let it, because a wall was the only honest thing left in me. Everything else was lies. Twenty years of lies, girl, stacked like cordwood, and every single one of them was built to keep you breathing."

Bram was crying. Tamsin watched her cry and did not reach across the table, because her hands were shaking too badly and because some wounds must be allowed to bleed before they can be cleaned.

"You let me believe I was worthless," Bram whispered. "For twenty years."

"Yes."

"You let me hate you."

"Yes."

"Why?" And it was not accusation in Bram's voice now, or not only. It was the raw question beneath every other question, the one she had carried out of the hollow at sixteen and across half the realm and through two decades of small careful magicless days. "*Why?*"

"Because love that costs nothing protects nothing." Tamsin leaned forward. "Because if you had known — if even one whisper of it had reached you — you would have been proud of it. You were a proud girl, Bram. You'd have worn that blood like a banner, and banners get seen, and seen gets killed. The only shelter strong enough was your own belief that you were nobody. So I made you nobody. I made you so thoroughly that you buried your own gift in a cellar and spent twenty years pretending the hole in you was peace." She sat back. "It worked. Gods forgive me, it worked perfectly. You ran. You lived. You're sitting here."

* * *

Outside, the wind moved through the thorn-wall, and the wall creaked like a ship, and somewhere in its depths a blackbird sang as though the world were not ending.

"But it's broken now," Bram said at last. Her tears had stopped; something harder had taken their place. "The hiding. The stone woke anyway. Hale found my sigil scorched into a doorway in Corvane. Casimir knows a steward lives. All your twenty years of lies, and they bought you—" she gestured at the cottage, the hollow, the dying pulse Tamsin had confessed to feeding on, "—this. Two more decades and a wall of thorns."

"They bought you twenty years of life," Tamsin said. "I'd pay it again. I'll pay worse before the end. Don't mistake me, girl — I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you the shape of the cage so you can decide whether to break it."

"Then tell me the rest." Bram turned her marked palm upward on the table, the silver stag gleaming. "Tell me what I am now. Tell me what the reforging costs. Because I've seen what happens when prayers go unanswered — I walked through Ashfield, Tamsin. I watched a village burn its own shrine rather than pray to a god who'd stopped listening. I've felt Vesh fading beside me like a fire going out. Whatever the truth is, I'm done being protected from it."

Tamsin looked at her apprentice — her girl, her door, her sin and her salvation — and felt the last of the old cowardice rise in her throat and be swallowed.

What remained was the truth, and the truth had teeth.

"Then listen," she said, and pulled the lamp closer, and opened the book to the page she had hoped never to show anyone. "There is a way to reforge the covenant. One way. It has been done twice in all the long history of the realms, and both times it required—"

She stopped. Beyond the thorn-wall, far off across the dark hills, a sound rolled through the night that did not belong to wind or bird or beast: the low brazen voice of war-horns, many of them, carried on the wind from the west.

Tamsin's eyes met Bram's across the table.

"They've found us," she said. "And we are out of time."
