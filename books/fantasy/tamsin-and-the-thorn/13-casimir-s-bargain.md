## 13. Casimir's Bargain

The map room of the Winter Palace had not been warm in eleven years, and Lord-Regent Casimir Vael preferred it that way. Cold kept a man honest. Cold kept the hands steady and the mind pared down to its essential edges, like a blade whetted against stone. He had learned the trick of it in the years before the throne — in the cellar beneath his father's house, in the long nights when he had rehearsed what must be done — and he had never unlearned it, though he now owned a palace with forty hearths and servants whose sole duty was to keep them burning.

He stood at the great table where the realm lay rendered in painted wood and inked vellum, and he looked at Dunmarrow.

The free city sat on its peninsula like a coin someone had dropped and could not be bothered to retrieve. Its walls were old, older than the Regency, older than the kingdom it refused to call itself part of. Its merchants were richer than his treasury and more frightened than his priests, which made them, in Casimir's arithmetic, the most predictable men alive. Fear could be bought. Rich men simply paid a higher price for the same commodity.

"Say it again," he said.

The man across the table was called Ferren Ostrewe, and he had been a priest of the small gods before the Edict, and afterward he had become something more useful: a scholar of what the Edict had forbidden. He wore plain wool now instead of vestments, and his hands trembled in a way that Casimir had decided was fear of him rather than of the subject matter, and so he permitted it.

"The covenant," Ostrewe said, "was not a prayer. That is the error everyone makes, my lord. They think the old arrangement was the gods hearing us out of — out of generosity." His mouth twisted at the word. "It was a contract. Bound in blood and stone. The divine answered mortal need because a mortal steward held the other end of the line. A person, my lord. Flesh and descent. The royal line itself, in its oldest branch."

"And when I—" Casimir paused, chose the verb with care, "—when the previous arrangement ended, the contract lapsed."

"When you ended it, yes." Ostrewe's eyes flicked up and away again, quick as a bird's. "The stewardship died with the old royal house. The stone went dark. The gods went silent. For twenty years, my lord, your silence has been proof of your success."

Casimir let the sentence sit between them like a knife laid flat on the table. Ostrewe had said *your* twice now, and both times had pretended he hadn't. There were perhaps four men in the capital who dared speak to the Lord-Regent in the conditional grammar of regicide, and Casimir kept all four alive precisely because they dared it. A court that flinched told him nothing. A court that spoke sideways told him everything.

"And now the silence has broken."

"Now the silence has broken." Ostrewe drew a breath. "Shrines rekindling in Ashfield. Wells sweetening. Fevered children waking whole in villages that haven't seen a true healing in a generation. And in Corvane, a scorch-mark on a smuggler's wall in the shape of a sigil that appears in exactly one place in the surviving records." He produced a folded paper, laid it down without touching the map, as if the map might bite. "The covenant-stone has woken, my lord. Which means a steward lives. Which means the contract can be reforged."

"By whoever holds the stone."

"By whoever *is* the stone's heir." Ostrewe's trembling had stilled; this, at least, he understood completely, and understanding steadied him the way wine steadied lesser men. "That is the terrible elegance of it. It cannot be bought. It cannot be seized by force of arms alone. The rite requires the heir's own blood, freely given, spoken over the hollow where the original pact was cut. But—" and here he raised one finger, and Casimir watched it the way a snake watches a rope, "—freely given does not mean wisely given. A steward who believes she reforges the covenant for the old gods might be persuaded to reforge it for a new arrangement. With a new guarantor. The words are old, but contracts are read aloud, my lord, and read aloud means interpreted, and interpretation belongs to whoever stands closest to the reader."

Casimir looked at the map for a long time.

He thought about the night eleven years past — not with guilt, which he had excised from himself the way a surgeon takes a limb, cleanly and early, but with the cold inventory of a man checking his stores. He had done what was necessary. The old king had been weak, and worse than weak, he had been *listening* — listening to priests, to prophets, to the murmuring of a court that still half-believed the gods would wake and set the world to rights. A realm cannot have two sovereigns. Casimir had understood that before he was twenty. He had understood it better than anyone, and so the crown had come to him, as things always came to him, because he alone had been willing to reach.

But there had been a moment. He did not speak of it to anyone. He barely permitted himself to speak of it to himself, in the hour before dawn when the palace was quiet enough that a man's thoughts grew loud.

The old king had died looking past him. Not at him — *past* him, over his shoulder, at something in the corner of the bedchamber that Casimir had not been able to see and had spent eleven years refusing to imagine. The king's last expression had not been fear of the knife. It had been recognition. As if something old had leaned close and said, *we remember*, and the king had gone content, because being remembered by a god is a kind of salvation even at the point of a blade.

Casimir had ordered the bedchamber burned the next day. The ashes had been scattered in the harbor. It had not helped. Some nights he woke with the certainty that the thing in the corner had turned its attention from the dying king to the living one, and had begun, patiently, without hurry — gods had nothing but patience — to remember *him*.

And now the gods were answering prayers again. Which meant they were listening again. Which meant somewhere in the dark of the world, something vast had opened an eye, and the first thing it would look for was the man who had severed the covenant.

"My lord?" Ostrewe said.

"If the covenant is reforged without me," Casimir said slowly, "the gods return. Fully. Openly. Answering prayers in the streets." He touched the painted coast of Dunmarrow. "And the first prayer every widow and orphaned soldier in this realm will whisper is the same one."

Ostrewe said nothing. He was wise enough not to say *justice* or *vengeance* or *remember*. He let the silence do it, and the silence did it very well.

"And if it is reforged *with* me," Casimir continued, "with me as guarantor, with the rite worded to bind the steward's obedience to the crown — then the gods answer to me. Every miracle becomes my miracle. Every answered prayer becomes proof of my legitimacy. The free cities kneel not because I besiege them but because their own shrines tell them to." He straightened. "Where is the heir?"

"We believe she fled Corvane eastward, through Ashfield. Inquisitor Hale has her trail. She passed through Millbrook three days ago — a fevered girl healed at a wayside shrine within an hour of her arrival. Before that, a well at Crossford ran clean. She leaves a wake of small miracles, my lord. She is either careless or kind, and both are traceable."

"Hale believes she is a witch to be burned."

"Hale believes many correct things that are no longer convenient." Ostrewe folded his hands. "You will need to change his orders. Carefully. Hale is not a man who can be lied to; he can only be *re-aimed*."

Casimir almost smiled. Almost. "Send for him."

* * *

Inquisitor Hale arrived at the palace smelling of road dust and horse and, faintly, of smoke — though whether the smoke clung to him from some pyre behind him or lived in his clothes permanently now, permanent as the ash-sigil he carried pressed between pages of his warrant-book, Casimir neither knew nor cared. Hale knelt when bidden, rose when bidden, and stood waiting with the particular stillness of a man who has made his body into an instrument and resents every hour it spends idle.

"You lost her at the Ashfield marches," Casimir said.

"I did not lose her, my lord. I know where she is going." Hale's voice was low and even, a voice built for reading last rites and death warrants in the same breath. "Every hedge-witch in the eastern villages tells the same story under questioning: the thorn-hollow. There is a place in the far east where a cottage has been swallowed by brambles grown overnight, twenty years ago, around a witch who would not leave. The locals won't name it. They cross themselves when they pass the road that leads there. If the girl has any destination, it is that."

"You've questioned the villagers."

"I've questioned everyone, my lord." Something moved behind Hale's eyes — not doubt, not yet, but the sediment that doubt is made from. "A shrine-keeper in Millbrook sheltered her for a night. The old woman fed her and asked nothing. When I asked her why, she said—" and here Hale stopped, and Casimir watched him stop, watched the instrument hesitate, "—she said the girl had sad hands. Sad hands, my lord. As if that were a defense."

"What happened to the shrine-keeper?"

"She is in custody. She will be questioned further." Hale's jaw tightened fractionally. "She is seventy years old and half-blind, my lord. Further questioning will yield further nothing. But procedure is procedure."

*Procedure is procedure.* Casimir had heard Hale say it before, in reports, in briefings, always in the same flat cadence, and he had always taken it for devotion. Now, with Ostrewe's paper lying on the table between them like a dealt card, he heard it differently. Procedure was what a man hid inside when the thing he actually believed had become unspeakable.

"Your sister," Casimir said.

Hale went very still. It was the stillness of a drawn bow.

"The fever took her when she was nine," Casimir continued, watching him. "In a year when the gods were silent and the healers were quacks and the prayers went up like sparks off wet wood. You have built twenty years of work on that silence, Inquisitor. I am not unmoved by it. Your grief made you the finest witch-finder this realm has produced. But the world has changed, and instruments that do not change with it become scrap."

"My lord?"

Casimir slid Ostrewe's paper across the table. "Read."

Hale read. Casimir watched him read — watched the comprehension arrive in stages, like weather coming over hills. First the scholarship, absorbed easily; Hale was no fool. Then the implication, arriving slower, resisted longer. Casimir could see the exact moment it landed, because Hale's thumb whitened against the edge of the page.

"You want her alive," Hale said.

"I want the covenant reforged under crown guarantee. The girl is the instrument of that reforging. Therefore the girl comes to me breathing, unmarked, and willing — or willing enough. Her blood must be given freely; coercion spoils the rite. Persuasion, Inquisitor. Accommodation. Whatever she needs to hear to walk to that hollow and speak the old words with our scholars standing at her shoulder." Casimir let his voice drop into the register he used for coronation oaths and war declarations, the register that made rooms believe things. "Imagine it. The gods return — and they return *through* the crown. Every healing, every answered prayer, every grandmother's whispered thanks becomes testimony that the Regency is divinely ordained. Your sister's death becomes the founding grief of a new age, honored, avenged, enshrined. No child dies of fever again while the gods are silent. Isn't that worth more than another pyre?"

It was a good speech. Casimir knew it was a good speech because he believed roughly a third of it, and belief, even fractional, carried.

Hale stood holding the paper for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was carefully level, and the levelness itself was a kind of scream.

"You are asking me to protect a witch."

"I am asking you to acquire an asset."

"The woman in Corvane burned three houses with whatever lives inside her. The scorch-mark on that wall is her sigil. I have spent two decades putting people like her into the ground, my lord, and you are asking me to put her in a carriage."

"I am asking you to recognize that the ground has moved." Casimir came around the table, slowly, and stood close enough that Hale could see the total absence of warmth in his eyes. This too was technique. Men obeyed cold faster than they obeyed charm; charm invited negotiation, and negotiation was a door once opened that never fully shut. "There is no order I can give you that will bring back your sister, Inquisitor. There is an order I can give you that will ensure no one else's sister dies unheard. Burn the girl and the silence returns forever — my silence, the silence I purchased, the silence that killed your sister and will kill ten thousand more. Reforge the covenant and the silence ends. Choose."

Hale's face did not move. But Casimir had spent a lifetime reading faces the way other men read ledgers, and he saw the entry being made, the figure tallied: *obedience, debit; belief, debit; the sister, entered under losses.* He saw Hale fold the paper along its existing crease, precisely, and tuck it inside his coat, next to the heart or next to the warrant-book — with Hale it had always been difficult to say which organ governed.

"It will be done, my lord," Hale said.

"See that it is. Alive, unharmed, and delivered to the eastern road within the fortnight. Take what men you need. Take the prisoner if she's useful — the old woman may know the girl's habits." Casimir turned back to the map, dismissal embodied. "And Inquisitor. If she dies — if she dies of accident, of resistance, of a fall from a horse — the covenant dies with her, and I will consider what that failure says about the reliability of my chief witch-finder. Do you understand the shape of your task?"

"Perfectly, my lord."

Hale bowed — correctly, deeply, a bow with no flaw in it anywhere — and walked from the room, and his boots on the marble sounded like a man counting.

* * *

Hale did not go to his quarters. He went to the stable yard, because horses were honest and palaces were not, and he stood in the cold among the saddles and the straw-smell and the steam of the animals' breath, and he let himself think the thoughts he had not permitted in the map room.

*Alive.* Twenty years, and the order had finally come down like a stone through a roof: *alive.* Not because the girl deserved mercy — Hale had read the Corvane reports, had stood in the gutted street himself and smelled the char, had interviewed the smuggler whose hands had blistered from a luck-knot that turned in the night. Because she was *useful.* Because the Lord-Regent wanted a weapon and she happened to be shaped like one.

Hale had served the Regency for eleven years and had never once doubted the shape of his work. Witches were real; he had seen their works since childhood, since the fever-year, since he had knelt by his sister's bed with his mother's hand pressing his head down into prayer while the prayers went nowhere, into the silence, into the terrible administrative void where the gods' office stood empty. Magic was the reason the prayers failed — that was the doctrine he had built, plank by plank, pyre by pyre: the witches drank the power that should have flowed to the faithful, dammed the river for their own gardens, left the fields of ordinary grief to die of thirst. Every heretic he burned was a stone pulled from the dam. Every confession was water beginning to move.

And now the Lord-Regent handed him a paper saying the river had been dammed by the crown itself. That the silence was policy. That the empty office of the gods had an occupant after all, and the occupant was Casimir Vael, sitting in the dark with his feet on the desk, letting the petitions pile up unread.

*Your sister's death becomes the founding grief of a new age.*

Hale put his hand flat against the stable wall and felt the cold of it climb his arm. The old woman in Millbrook was in a cell beneath the Inquisition's chapter-house, and she had said *sad hands,* and he had written it down in his report as evidence of delusion, and tonight, standing in straw-dust and horse-breath, he heard her voice again with terrible clarity — not deluded at all, just old, just tired, just certain of something Hale had not yet permitted himself to see.

The girl had sad hands. The girl healed a fevered child at a roadside shrine and asked nothing. The girl left wells running clean behind her like footprints.

Witches did not leave clean wells behind them. Hale knew witches. Witches left scorched walls and blistered palms and mothers weeping over luck-knots that curdled. What left clean wells behind it was something else, something the doctrine had no drawer for, and the doctrine had no drawer for it because Hale had built the doctrine himself, board by board, over his sister's grave, and a man does not easily open the chest he has nailed shut over the thing he loves.

But the Lord-Regent had asked him to nail it shut a little tighter. *Alive, unharmed, willing.* Bring me the witch in a carriage. Bring me the miracle in chains soft enough that she doesn't notice them. And the words of the rite, Ostrewe's paper said, would be *interpreted* — by whoever stood closest to the reader. By scholars of the crown. By men like Ostrewe, who had sold his vestments and kept his gods' grammar, ready to conjugate obedience in the old holy tongue.

Hale understood, then, standing in the stable yard with the frost forming on the rails, exactly what he was. Not a witch-finder. Not an instrument of justice. A tool — a good one, a sharp one, the finest of its kind — and tools were not consulted about the work. Tools were picked up when needed and put down when the shape of the work changed, and the hand that held them did not explain itself, because hands never explained themselves to hammers.

His sister had died at nine years old with her hands curled around a wooden saint whose paint had worn away at the folded palms. Their mother had said, *the gods are testing us.* Their father had said, *the gods are silent because the wicked prosper.* And Hale, aged twelve, kneeling on cold flagstones, had sworn the only vow available to a boy with no power: *I will find the wicked. I will make the silence make sense.*

Twenty-three years later, the silence had made sense at last, and the sense of it was a regicide's arithmetic, and the wicked — the paper in his coat said so, in Ostrewe's careful scholarly hand — the wicked prospered in the Winter Palace, keeping the maps cold and the hearths unlit and the gods' office empty on purpose.

"Fortnight," Hale said aloud to the horses, and his breath smoked. "Alive and willing."

One of the geldings turned its heavy head and regarded him with a dark, patient eye, the way animals regard men who have begun to lie to themselves.

Hale swung into the saddle an hour later with thirty riders and a prisoner's cart, and pointed them east, toward Ashfield, toward the thorn-hollow, toward a girl with sad hands who was walking toward the one place in the world where the truth about the covenant waited under twenty years of brambles.

He rode at the head of his men with the warrant-book against his ribs and the folded paper beside it, two documents pressing against each other through the leather like opposing blades, and he did not yet know which one he would draw when the moment came. He only knew that the moment was coming, that it waited for him out east among the rekindled shrines, and that for the first time in twenty-three years, Inquisitor Hale was afraid — not of the witch ahead of him, but of what he would find when he caught her.

Behind him, the Winter Palace receded into the grey morning, every window dark, every hearth cold, the Lord-Regent's silence spreading out from it like frost across a field — and somewhere far to the east, faint as a pulse under snow, the old gods were waking, and remembering, and beginning, patiently, to count.
