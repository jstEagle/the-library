## 14. Apprentice Again

The storm came down off the western hills like something with intent.

Bram felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of iron on the wind, the covenant-stone in her satchel humming a low note that she had learned over the past weeks to read the way sailors read cloud. She was splitting firewood behind Tamsin's cottage when the first thunder rolled, and she paused with the axe half-raised and said, without turning around:

"That's not weather."

"It's weather enough." Tamsin stood in the doorway with her shawl pulled tight, her white hair whipping loose from its braid. "It's the boundary thinning. The old roads remember what they were, and the sky remembers with them. Get the wood under cover, girl, unless you'd rather spend the night chopping kindling in the dark."

*Girl.* Twenty years of silence between them, three weeks of wary cohabitation inside the thorn-wall, and still the word came out of her mouth as easily as breath. Bram found, to her private irritation, that she no longer minded it.

She stacked the wood beneath the lean-to as the light went strange and green, and by the time the first fat drops struck the thatch, the two of them were shut inside the cottage with the shutters barred and the fire built high. It should have been suffocating — the same low room where Bram had slept as an apprentice, the same bundles of dried herbs hanging from the rafters, the same crooked shelf of jars labeled in Tamsin's spiky hand. Instead it felt, treacherously, like shelter.

The storm arrived properly at dusk.

It did not rain so much as fall upon them, a whole river's worth of water let go at once, hammering the roof until speech required shouting. Lightning came so close that the smell of ozone threaded through the woodsmoke, and each flash lit the window in white stutters, showing Bram glimpses of the thorn-wall beyond — black branches writhing in the wind like a thing alive, which, in its way, it was.

"The hollow's protected," Tamsin said, settling into her chair with the groan of old joints. "Nothing rides through that wall in this. Not soldiers, not spirits, not Hale and his pious butchers." She caught Bram's look. "Yes, I know about the witch-finder. Vesh told me. That god gossips worse than a washerwoman."

"He's worried," Bram said. "He says Casimir knows what I am now. Knows what the reforging requires."

"Then Casimir is cleverer than I credited him." Tamsin's mouth went thin. "Or someone whispered in his ear who shouldn't have." She waved the thought away like smoke. "It changes nothing tonight. Tonight we work."

"Work?" Bram looked out at the drowning dark. "In this?"

"You think storms are for hiding from? Girl, a storm is the finest teacher you'll ever have." Tamsin's eyes caught the firelight, and for a moment Bram saw her as she must have been forty years ago — fierce, quick, dangerous as a drawn blade. "When the veil is thin and the world is loud, magic comes easy. Anyone can work power in a storm. The lesson is learning to hold it *quiet*. To be the still point while everything howls around you." She nodded toward the hearth. "Fetch the kettle. We begin with tea."

Bram laughed before she could stop herself — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse.

Tamsin's eyebrows rose. "Something amusing?"

"No. Yes. I just—" Bram shook her head, fighting the smile. "Twenty years ago you taught me my first lesson the same way. I'd run away from my father's house, walked three days through the rain to find you, and you opened the door, looked me up and down, and said, 'Well, you're dripping on my floor. Fetch the kettle.'"

"I remember." Something moved behind Tamsin's expression, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "You were a scrawny thing. All elbows and stubbornness."

"And you took me in anyway."

"I took you in because your father paid me." But her voice wasn't cruel when she said it, and they both knew it wasn't true, and neither of them said anything else about it. Bram fetched the kettle.

* * *

The lesson, once it began, was harder than tea.

Tamsin set a single candle on the table between them. "Keep it lit," she said. "All night if you can. Through the thunder, through the drafts, through whatever the storm shakes loose. And do it without touching the flame, without cupping it, without coddling it. You hold a shield of will around it and nothing else. If you smother it with too much care, it drowns. If you forget it, it dies. Find the middle."

"That's it? A candle?"

"That's everything." Tamsin leaned back. "A candle is a small life. You keep it burning through the storm, you've learned to keep anything burning. Now sit. And don't fidget. You always fidgeted."

"I did not."

"You fidgeted like a cat in a sack. Sit."

Bram sat. She fixed her attention on the flame and began, slowly, to build the working — not with words, though words helped at first, but with the older language underneath: intention shaped into a bowl, a sphere, a shell of quiet attention wrapped around that small gold trembling. The storm hammered. The draft from the shutters breathed cold across the table. Each time thunder cracked, the flame flinched, and Bram's shield flinched with it, and twice the candle guttered down to a blue bead before she caught it.

"You're gripping it," Tamsin said from her chair, where she appeared to be dozing. "You're holding it the way you held that stone when you ran. Like it might be taken from you."

Bram's jaw tightened. "Maybe because things I hold *do* get taken from me."

The words came out sharper than she intended, and the candle snuffed itself in a puff of smoke — her attention fracturing, her shield collapsing inward instead of outward. In the sudden dark, lit only by firelight, she heard Tamsin sigh.

"Relight it."

"Tamsin—"

"*Relight it.*"

Bram struck a taper from the coals and touched it to the wick, hands steadier than she felt. The flame stood up again, small and defiant.

"There," Tamsin said quietly. "That's the second lesson, and you didn't even notice me teaching it. The flame goes out. You relight it. You don't stand there blaming the wind, or the storm, or yourself, or the woman who taught you badly. You relight it and you go on." She rose, joints cracking, and shuffled to the cupboard. "Again. And this time, when it gutters, breathe. Fear is just breath held wrong."

They worked through the long hours of the storm. Bram lost count of how many times the candle died and lived again. Somewhere past midnight her arms ached with a fatigue that had nothing to do with muscle, and her vision swam, and still Tamsin would not let her stop — though Bram noticed, without letting herself notice, that the old woman had draped a blanket over her shoulders somewhere in the third hour, and had put bread and honey within reach of her free hand, and had stopped correcting every small error and begun only naming the large ones.

That was Tamsin's love, Bram remembered. It had always been. Not spoken — never spoken, not once in all her childhood years here — but folded into bread and blankets and the relentless demand that Bram become everything she could be. Love as labor. Care as criticism. You knew you were cherished because someone refused to let you be less.

Around the fourth hour, soaked in sweat despite the cold room, Bram got it. One moment the shield was effort, and the next it simply *was* — a thing she wore rather than carried, effortless as skin. The candle burned straight and calm in the heart of the roaring night, and Bram sat inside her own stillness and felt, for the first time in twenty years, entirely at home in her own power.

She looked up. Tamsin was watching her with an expression she couldn't read.

"Well?" Bram asked. Her voice came out hoarse.

"Now you're ready to learn why the candle matters," Tamsin said. "Sleep. Tomorrow we start the rite proper, and you'll wish the candle was the worst of it."

* * *

Morning came washed and silver, the storm spent, the hollow dripping and gleaming. Bram woke on her old pallet — her old pallet, the straw still smelling faintly of the lavender Tamsin had always tucked into it — to the smell of porridge and the sound of her mistress muttering at the fire.

They ate in something like peace. Then Tamsin cleared the bowls away and set three objects on the table: a knife with a blade of grey glass, a bowl of water, and a length of red thread.

"The rite of reforging has three movements," she said. "Speaking, bleeding, and binding. Most stewards spent years preparing for each. You have — what does Vesh say? Weeks, perhaps. So we compress." She tapped the knife. "This cuts more than flesh. It can cut a vow open, cut a lie out of a soul, cut a door between what a person is and what they pretend to be. Before you speak the old words, every lie you carry must come out. The covenant won't bind around falsehood. It would simply refuse you, and the refusal would burn."

"What lies do I carry?" Bram asked, and then heard herself, and winced.

"Mm," said Tamsin. "Exactly so. The water shows us. Hands in the bowl, and speak your name — your true one, the one you were born with, not the one you've been hiding under in Corvane."

Brimham Vasquez. She hadn't said it aloud in twenty years. Her hands trembled as she lowered them into the cold water, and when she spoke her name, the surface shivered and showed her — not a reflection, but a memory. Herself at nineteen, running through the rain with the stone clutched to her chest, Tamsin's voice behind her screaming words she had never let herself fully hear.

"She cursed me," Bram whispered. "That night. I heard her — I thought she cursed me."

"I did no such thing." Tamsin's voice was flat. "Look again."

Bram looked. And listening now with apprentice ears instead of terrified ones, she finally heard it: not a curse at all, but a warning, repeated, desperate — *the road east floods, take the shepherd's path, take the shepherd's path* — a woman trying to save a fleeing girl from a drowned ford, and being answered with a stolen stone and twenty years of silence.

The bowl-water rippled. Bram realized she was crying.

"I thought—" Her voice broke. "All these years I thought you hated me. I built my whole life on it. On being the girl Tamsin cast out."

"And I built mine on being the woman who drove you to it." Tamsin's hand came down over hers in the water, old and rough and warm. "We were both liars, girl. That's why the thread is red. Blood and thread and truth. That's the whole craft, in the end. Everything else is decoration."

They worked until the light failed. The knife taught Bram to cut truth from evasion — small exercises first, speaking simple facts into the bowl and watching the water cloud or clear. The thread taught her knot-work, the luck-knots she'd sold in Corvane revealed now as the skeleton of far older bindings, prayers disguised as commerce, and Bram laughed bitterly to realize she had never actually stopped practicing the covenant's craft, only lied to herself about what she was doing.

By evening they argued.

It started small — Tamsin correcting her knot-tension, Bram snapping back that she'd been selling knots to smugglers for two decades and knew how they held — and escalated along grooves worn smooth by old habit, the way water finds the channels it carved centuries ago. Tamsin called her careless. Bram called her a tyrant. Tamsin said she'd always been too soft to finish anything, and Bram said that was rich coming from a woman who'd spent twenty years growing a wall of thorns instead of walking five miles to Corvane to look for her, and then the silence fell, huge and ringing, both of them breathing hard, the fire popping.

"I did walk," Tamsin said at last, very quietly. "The first year. Three times. The thorns wouldn't let me pass. They grew from my grief faster than I could cut them, and by the fourth year I understood they weren't a wall at all. They were a question. *Will she come back?* And every year I didn't answer it, they grew thicker." She turned her hands over, scarred and knuckled. "You want to know the worst thing about growing old, Bram? You run out of time to be proud."

Bram crossed the room. She didn't embrace her — neither of them was made for that, not yet, maybe not ever — but she sat down on the floor beside Tamsin's chair, shoulder against the old woman's knee, the way she had as a child during winter lessons, and stayed there. After a moment, a gnarled hand came to rest on her hair, clumsy and careful, like something that had forgotten how to be gentle and was relearning.

Neither of them said anything aloud. Neither needed to. The fire spoke for them, and the dripping eaves, and the slow pulse of the stone in its satchel, keeping time like a patient heart.

* * *

The days found their rhythm after that, and Bram hated how much she loved it.

Dawn: lessons in the old tongue, the language of the covenant, which lived in the back of Bram's memory like a song half-remembered and came back line by line under Tamsin's merciless drilling. Midday: work in the garden, gathering what the rite would need — rowan bark, grave-moss, the first snowdrops pushing up through the ash-grey soil, blooming impossibly early because the land itself was waking. Afternoon: practice, practice, practice — the candle, the bowl, the thread, and then larger workings, Tamsin pushing her further each day, until Bram could hold a shield against a summoned gust, could light the hearth with a word, could feel the thorn-wall's living attention and speak to it and be, tentatively, answered.

Evenings were for food and argument. They cooked together, which was its own negotiation — Tamsin salted everything like the sea owed her money, and Bram insisted on vegetables that hadn't been boiled into surrender, and they bickered over the pot like an old married couple, and Bram caught herself smiling into the steam more than once and sternly ordered her face to stop it.

On the fifth evening, braiding rope for the rite-circle, Bram said, without planning to: "I used to dream about this kitchen. In Corvane. Twenty years, and I dreamed about this kitchen."

Tamsin's hands didn't pause on the rope. "Dreams are just guilt wearing comfortable clothes."

"That's the bleakest thing you've ever said, and I once heard you describe love as 'a fever with paperwork.'"

That startled a sound out of the old woman — half cough, half laugh, quickly strangled. But Bram had heard it. Twenty years ago she'd have given anything to make Tamsin laugh, and had managed it perhaps a dozen times, each one a trophy. Some things, apparently, survived everything.

"Go on with your braiding," Tamsin said gruffly. "And mind the third turn. You always slack the third turn."

"I have never once slacked the third turn."

"You're slacking it now."

Bram looked down. She was. She tightened it, and said nothing, and felt Tamsin's approval settle over her like the blanket had on that first storm-night — unasked-for, unacknowledged, warm.

* * *

On the seventh night, the dreams changed.

Until then they had been the usual traffic of her sleeping mind — Corvane streets, her little stall, the faces of customers whose names she'd never learned. But that night she dreamed of the hollow's edge, and the thorn-wall, and beyond it a column of men in grey marching through the hills with fire-pots and chains, and at their head a figure in a witch-finder's coat whose face she couldn't see, though she knew it anyway. Hale. And behind him, riding a pale horse, a man in regent's black whose face was smooth as an egg, featureless, waiting to be written on.

She woke before dawn with the stone burning cold against her sternum and Tamsin already standing at the window, shawl over her shoulders, looking east.

"You felt it too," Bram said.

"The boundary rang like a struck bell an hour ago. Something crossed it, far off, moving this way." Tamsin didn't turn. "Vesh's warnings were right. Casimir isn't sending soldiers to negotiate."

"How long?"

"A week. Less, if they push." Now she turned, and the firelight showed Bram something she had never seen on that face in all her childhood: fear, admitted, worn openly. "So we stop training gently. From today, we do the rite's movements at full strength, and if you tear something learning them, we tape it and continue. Do you understand me?"

Bram understood. She rose, and took the grey-glass knife from the table, and held it out hilt-first between them — an offering, an acceptance, an oath.

"Teach me," she said. "Everything. However it costs."

Tamsin took the knife. For a moment her hand covered Bram's, and neither of them looked away, and the space between twenty years and none of them narrowed to the width of a blade.

"Then we begin with blood," Tamsin said, as the eastern sky went the color of a healing wound. "Yours first. And girl — when it starts, whatever you see in the cutting, whatever the truth of you turns out to be —" her grip tightened, brief and fierce — "come back to me after. Whatever you see. Come *back*."

Far to the east, faint as a heartbeat, something howled.
