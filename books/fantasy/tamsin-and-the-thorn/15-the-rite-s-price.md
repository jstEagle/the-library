## 15. The Rite's Price

The storm had been Tamsin's doing, though she would never say so.

She had worked it up from the western clouds three nights past, when she felt the inquisitor's hounds sniffing along the ley like dogs after a wounded hare — a gray and howling wall of weather to seal the hollow, to buy her time. Time for what? That was the question that had kept her awake since, staring at the rafters of the cottage Bram had once called home, listening to the rain hammer the thorn-wall like a fist on a door, and knowing that the answer was a thing she did not want to speak aloud.

Some truths you can carry your whole life in a closed hand. Sooner or later the fingers must open.

Tamsin Amberdale — no, not Amberdale; that name had died with her mother on the pyre at Corvane — Tamsin of the Hollow sat at her worktable in the grey hour before dawn and opened her fingers over the covenant-stone.

It lay where Bram had set it after the rite's first rehearsal, on a bed of dried yarrow, and even now, even dormant, it breathed. She could see it if she let herself look properly: a slow pulse of light beneath the stone's skin, like a heart under a shroud, keeping time with something far away. Keeping time with the realms themselves. Twenty years she had lived as its keeper, its nursemaid, its widow. Twenty years of feeding it drops of her own strength through the thorn-roots because there was no steward to do it properly, because the girl who should have been steward had run into the dark with it clutched to her chest like a stolen loaf.

And now the girl was back. A woman now. Grey threading the black at her temples, hands scarred from twenty years of salves and luck-knots and small dishonest magics, eyes that flinched from every memory Tamsin could see her walking through. Back. Sleeping in the loft where she'd slept at fourteen, breathing slow, while the storm Tamsin had summoned kept the world out.

Tamsin touched the stone and let herself feel what it wanted.

It wanted to be whole. It wanted the words spoken by royal blood freely given, at the hollow's heart, witnessed by one of the old ones. All of that they had. Bram's blood was royal — had always been royal, though the girl had never known it, though Tamsin herself had only learned it the winter before the theft, when the last of the old king's line came begging shelter and died in the loft bed with a newborn's cry already fading from the air. *Her blood is the pact,* the dying mother had gasped, pressing the bundle into Tamsin's arms. *Keep her from the throne. Keep her from them.* And Tamsin had sworn, and had meant it, and had spent two decades learning exactly how badly a witch can keep a vow.

But wanting to be whole was not the same as being whole. The stone had a price, and Tamsin had known the price since she was young enough to believe she'd never have to pay it, because stewards were born rare as comets and the last one had been her own teacher, old Maren, who had walked into the boundary at seventy years of age and never come out, smiling all the way.

The reforging required a steward bound permanently between the realms. Not an hour's service. Not a season's. Permanently. The mortal half of the steward stayed mortal — flesh, hunger, grief, all of it — but the other half stood forever in the gods' country, answering prayers, holding the door. No more ordinary life. No more running. The moment the words were spoken, Bram Vasquez would become a hinge, and hinges do not walk away.

*There are worse fates,* said the practical voice, the hedge-witch voice, the one that had buried three masters and outlived a kingdom. *There is Casimir's fire. There is the slow death of every prayer unanswered, every shrine cold, every child's fever met with nothing but boiled willow bark and hope. You've seen what a godless realm becomes. You built your whole life in its wreckage.*

Yes. And she had also seen what the boundary did to those who touched it even briefly. Maren had come back once, in the first year, standing at the edge of the thorn-wall with her face lit from within like a lantern, saying *it doesn't hurt, Tam, it doesn't hurt at all, you stop being one thing and start being the space between things* — and her voice had sounded like wind through a keyhole, and Tamsin had wept for a week, because that wasn't Maren anymore. That was what remained of Maren wearing Maren's smile.

And Bram would not merely touch the boundary. Bram would *be* it.

Tamsin took her hands off the stone as if it had bitten her.

*Then don't tell her.*

The thought arrived quietly, the way the worst thoughts do, dressed in reason. Don't tell her until the rite is underway. Let her speak the words believing the binding is symbolic, a renewal, a candle relit — then let the stone take what it takes. She'd forgive you eventually. Or she wouldn't, and she'd be alive, and the world would turn, and you'd have your apprentice back behind a wall of light instead of a wall of thorns.

Tamsin sat with that thought for a long time. Outside, the storm threw hail against the shutters, and somewhere below, in the root cellar where the stone had slept for twenty years, the old wards hummed in their sleep.

It was a lie, of course. The rite could not be done under false pretenses — the words required *freely given*, and the stone knew a coerced heart the way a dog knows fear. But that wasn't why she rejected it. She rejected it because she had watched this girl at fourteen stand in this very kitchen and swear, fists balled, tears cutting tracks through flour dust, *I will never lie to anyone the way you lie to me, Tamsin* — and Tamsin had laughed, cruel and tired, and said *you will, everyone does, it's how people survive each other* — and the girl had run, eventually, exactly as predicted, and Tamsin had spent twenty years understanding that the lie had been hers all along: she had told Bram she was teaching her craft when she was really teaching her exile, had told her the thorn-wall was protection when it was punishment, had told her *go, then, see if I care* when her heart had torn down the middle like wet bread.

No more lies dressed as love. If the price had to be paid, Bram would count it out herself, coin by coin, with her eyes open. Even if counting it cost Tamsin everything twice.

* * *

Bram came down at midmorning, hair damp from washing in the rain-barrel, moving around the kitchen with the unconscious competence of someone returning to a language she'd thought forgotten. She banked the fire without being asked. Found the good kettle — the dented copper one, third hook from the left — without looking. Set oats to toast and reached for the honey jar on the high shelf, and paused, and looked at Tamsin, and something passed across her face too quick to name.

"You still put it on the top shelf," Bram said. "Where I couldn't reach."

"You were a small creature."

"I was fourteen."

"Aye, and grew four inches the year you left, just to spite me." Tamsin heard her own voice come out rougher than intended and covered it with the grinding stone, crushing dried nettle harder than the nettle deserved.

They ate. The storm muttered against the walls. Vesh had gone out at dawn — gods did not need sleep, but they apparently needed to *walk*, which Tamsin found both touching and unnerving — leaving word that the hounds had lost the scent at the river but would not lose it forever. So there was time. There was perhaps a week. Perhaps less.

Tamsin turned her cup three times on the table, the way she did when she needed her hands to agree with her mouth, and made herself begin.

"The rite," she said. "Before we go further with the rehearsals, there's a thing you need to know. A thing I should have told you the night you walked back through my thorns."

Bram set down her spoon. Twenty years ago she would have braced for a scolding. Now she simply waited, and that patience was somehow worse.

"When Maren forged the covenant — the first forging, ninety years ago — she didn't bind the gods to us out of charity, and they didn't bind themselves out of kindness. It was a trade. They answer mortal need — truly answer it, hearth-prayers and harvest-prayers and the desperate three-o'clock-in-the-morning kind — and in exchange, a mortal stands at the boundary and holds the door open from our side. A steward. Royal blood, freely given, because the pact was struck between crowns and gods and the crown's line carries the obligation in its veins." Tamsin looked at her hands. "You know whose veins those are now."

"Mine." Bram said it flatly, still not quite able to make it sound real.

"Yours. And here is the part I have been choking on for six days." Tamsin lifted her eyes and forced herself to meet Bram's, because this was going to hurt either way and it would be cowardice to hurt her from behind a shield. "The binding is permanent. Not a duty you perform. A thing you *become*. Half of you stays flesh — you'll eat, and grieve, and grow old, all the ordinary weight of it. But the other half stands in the gods' country, always. Every prayer in every free city will pass through you like water through a weir. You will feel them, Bram. All of them. The dying child and the lonely widow and the soldier praying not to die before spring. Forever. There is no putting it down. There is no walking away from it, not ever, not for one afternoon, not for one hour. The moment the words are spoken, the ordinary life ends. Whatever life you might have had — the shop in Corvane, the luck-knots, some quiet man or woman to grow old beside — none of it survives the rite. You stop being a person who lives in the world and become part of what holds the world together."

The silence afterward was enormous. Rain filled it. The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls the thorn-vines shifted their grip, groaning like an old ship.

Bram's face did not crumple. That was the terrible thing. Tamsin had braced for tears, for anger, for the girl's old habit of turning pain into fury — but the woman just sat very still, looking at the middle distance, and Tamsin realized she was watching her do arithmetic. Watching her weigh a world against a self, the way Tamsin herself had weighed it a hundred times in the dark hours, and always arrived at the same unbearable sum.

"And if we don't do it," Bram said quietly. "If I refuse. What happens?"

"Casimir burns the free cities one by one, because he cannot abide anything the gods might remember him by. The shrines go cold again, or stay cold — the gods can't hold the door from their side alone, that's the whole design of it, the door needs hands on both sides. Within a generation the old ones fade to whispers, and then to nothing, and the realm goes back to what it was when I was young. Which was not unlivable." Tamsin's voice cracked and she hated it. "People survived. People always survive. It's just — colder. Everything's just colder, and the prayers go nowhere, and I—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment. Started again, lower. "I have watched a godless realm from the inside, Bram. I raised you in one. I know exactly what it costs, because I paid it, and so did you, and so did every soul who ever knocked on my door at midnight with a fevered child and nothing left to bargain with."

"So the choice is the world, or me."

"The choice is the world, or you," Tamsin said, "and I want you to understand something before you answer, and I want you to remember it, because I am about to say the most selfish thing I have said in twenty years." She made herself lean forward. Made herself say it plainly, no hedge-craft, no deflection. "I would let it burn. Do you hear me? If it were mine to choose — if the gods came to *me* and said, Tamsin, give us the girl or give us ash — I would hand them ash. I would watch Casimir's fires eat the coast from the top of this hill and I would not open this door. I lost you once to my own pride and my own truth-telling, and I dug your grave in my heart and lay down in it every night for twenty years, and I am *not strong enough* to lose you twice. Any strength you think you see in me is a story I tell so I can get up in the morning. So whatever you decide — decide it knowing that the one person who loves you most in either realm is praying, every hour, that you'll choose yourself. Choose the ordinary life. Run again. I'll help you pack."

Bram stared at her. And then, horribly, wonderfully, she laughed — a short, wet, disbelieving sound, half sob.

"You're telling me to run," she said, "from inside the house you walled up with thorns to keep me from running."

"Yes."

"The woman who told me *if you walk out that door, don't come back*—"

"—meant every word and has regretted every syllable since the moment your footsteps faded." Tamsin's eyes were burning now and she let them. "I built that wall out of grief, girl, and grief grows fast and thorny and it does not ask permission. You want to know what I learned in twenty years? That love that punishes isn't love, it's just loss wearing love's coat. I'm not doing it again. Not with stakes like these. So I'm giving you the truth with the price tag attached, and then I'm going to sit here and drink this tea, and whatever you choose, I will spend the rest of my life not punishing you for it."

Bram looked down at her palm — at the name the stone had burned there weeks ago, the fine silver scar that had not faded and never would. She turned her hand over slowly, as if reading it.

"You know what the worst part is?" she said at last, and her voice was steady in a way that frightened Tamsin more than weeping would have. "I ran for twenty years trying to find an ordinary life. Luck-knots and fever-salves and a room above a chandler's shop. And every single day, some part of me was listening — at doors, at windows, at the bottom of prayers I pretended not to hear. I was never ordinary, Tamsin. I was just hiding. This rite doesn't take an ordinary life from me." She closed her fist over the scar. "It takes the hiding."

"Don't," Tamsin whispered. "Don't decide like that. Not tonight. Not out of defiance, not out of guilt, not to settle scores with the girl you used to be. Promise me you'll sit with it. A week. Three days. Anything."

"I'll sit with it." Bram rose, gathered the bowls, and paused at the doorway — the same doorway, Tamsin realized, she had stormed through at seventeen with a stolen god-stone in her satchel. She didn't look back. "For what it's worth," she said, "I believe you'd let it burn. That's the first honest thing anyone's offered me since I got here."

She went up to the loft. The ladder creaked under her weight, a sound Tamsin had memorized once and never forgotten.

* * *

That evening Vesh returned, shaking rain from a beggar's coat that had never been woven by human hands, and found Tamsin alone at the table with the covenant-stone glowing faintly between her palms like a coal she was afraid to drop.

"She's decided," the god said. Not a question. Old ones heard decisions the way owls hear mice.

"She hasn't said it."

"She said it on the ladder." Vesh lowered himself onto the bench across from her, and for once did not bother with the disguise of age; his eyes were the color of deep river water and just as old. "You gave her the truth entire. Most mortals would have lied to her and saved themselves the grief. I confess I expected you to."

"I considered it."

"I know. I was watching." He smiled, and it was not entirely kind. "You've changed, hedge-witch."

"I've been hollowed out and refilled with regret. It's a similar process." Tamsin slid the stone across the table toward him. "Tell me honestly, since you're to be witness. When the binding takes — when she stands between — is there anything left of her that's *her*? Or do I get twenty more years of talking to a lantern?"

Vesh was quiet for a long moment. Rain ran down the window glass in bright threads.

"Maren is still in there," he said finally. "When the door opens wide enough, sometimes I feel her on the other side, tending. She hums when she works. She always hummed." He looked up. "Your girl will still be your girl, Tamsin. She will simply be your girl *everywhere*. Is that a comfort?"

"It's a wound," Tamsin said, "dressed as a comfort. Which is the only kind I've ever been able to afford."

Outside, far to the west, beneath the storm's gray skirt, the horizon flickered — and it was not lightning. It was too regular, too orange, too patient. Beacon-fires, lighting one after another along the ridgeline like a rosary being counted. The vanguard of Casimir's army, come down from the capital, burning the shrines as they marched.

Vesh followed her gaze and his ancient face went grim.

"They've found Dunmarrow's road," he said. "We have days, Tamsin. Perhaps one."

Tamsin closed her hand around the warm stone and held it against her heart, where it beat, and beat, and beat.
