## 16. Dunmarrow Besieged

The gulls stopped coming the morning the vanguard arrived.

Ivet Sarn noticed it first from her window above the counting-house, the way she noticed everything that touched Dunmarrow's harbor: the absence of wings, the strange clean silence over the water where a thousand birds should have wheeled and cried. She stood with her hands flat on the sill, still in her nightclothes, and watched the horizon east of the Teeth, where the sea met sky in a line that had always meant safety.

Smoke stood on that line now. Not one column but many, thin and orderly, like the tines of a comb laid against the dawn.

"Gods' teeth," she said softly, to no one, to the room, to the ledger open on her desk with its columns of grain prices and tar contracts — the small arithmetic of a city that had believed itself too useful to burn.

She dressed quickly, in the dark blue wool of her office rather than the merchant's silks she'd worn for twenty years of council meetings. There was a difference between negotiating and defending, and she had known since the autumn, since the first shrines rekindled along the Ashfield road, that this day would come. She had simply hoped it would come to someone else's walls.

* * *

By midmorning the council chamber was full and useless.

The Salt Hall had hosted arguments for three centuries — herring quotas, harbor dues, the eternal war between the fishmongers' guild and the chandlers — but never an argument like this one. The long table was crowded with councilors, guildmasters, the harbormaster, two captains of the watch, and old Maren Ostrey, who kept the shrine of the Drowned Mother down by the fishmarket and had not been invited to anything in forty years until this winter made her suddenly the most consulted woman in the city.

"They're not an army," Captain Brell was saying, jabbing a finger at the map spread across the table, weighted at its corners with candlesticks and a dead crab someone had thought amusing. "Not yet. It's a vanguard — maybe four thousand, light horse and foot, no siege train visible. They'll sit outside our walls and wait for the main host."

"Then we have time." That was Corvin Hale-adjacent thinking, Ivet noted sourly — Councilor Dree, silver-tongued and gold-hearted, already angling toward surrender. "Time to send envoys. Casimir isn't a madman. He'll take terms. We open the gates, we hand over anyone who's been—"

"Anyone who's *prayed*," Maren Ostrey said. Her voice was dry as old rope. "That's the crime now, isn't it? Kneeling."

Dree flushed. "Anyone who's broken the Regent's edict, which is the law of the land—"

"The law of a man who murdered his way onto a throne," the shrine-keeper said, "and knows the gods remember it. That's why he burns shrines, girl. Not because prayer hurts him. Because memory does."

"Enough." Ivet put both palms flat on the table, and the room quieted, because Ivet Sarn's palms on a table had ended a dock strike and a famine-price war and once, memorably, a duel between two shipwrights. "Dree, you'll have your vote before nightfall, and if the council votes to treat with Casimir, I'll sign the letter myself and bear the shame of it. But we don't decide blind. Harbormaster — what did your outriders see?"

"A camp at Gray Ford, twelve miles east." The harbormaster was a broad, gray woman named Skell, and her voice carried the particular flatness of someone reporting what they wished they hadn't seen. "They've got a gallows up already. Two bodies on it when my riders turned back. One of them—" she paused, and Ivet watched her swallow, "—one of them was wearing a shrine-keeper's sash. Blue and white. The Drowned Mother's colors."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water.

Maren Ostrey looked at her own sash, faded blue, frayed white, knotted at her waist for longer than most of the people in this room had been alive. She didn't touch it. She just looked at it, and then at Ivet, and her eyes were steady as ballast stone.

* * *

The plea went out at dusk.

It was Ivet's idea, though she'd needed Maren's words to shape it. Not a letter to Casimir — there would be time enough for surrender or defiance once the council voted. This was something older. Something the free cities had promised each other in their founding charters, back when the covenant still held and the gods still answered: *when need is true and prayers are honest, help will come.*

A rumor of a witch, moving east along the Ashfield road. A scorch-mark sigil on a cellar floor in Corvane. Shrines relighting like lamps behind her as she passed. The story had reached Dunmarrow three weeks ago in the hold of a smuggler's brig, half-garbled, wholly improbable, and Ivet had done nothing with it except keep it, the way she kept every piece of information that might someday be worth money or more than money.

Now she sat at her desk with good paper and the city's seal, and found she did not know how to address a witch.

"To the Steward of the Covenant," Maren suggested, standing at the window while the last light died over the occupied ford. "If she's real, that's what she is. If she's not real, then it doesn't matter what we write, does it?"

"And if she's real but not that?" Ivet asked. Her pen hovered. Ink gathered at the nib, dark as the water under the pier. "What if she's just a woman, Maren? A hedge-witch with a stolen stone and a bad conscience, running from the same army that's sitting at our gate? What are we betting then?"

"Everything," Maren said simply. "Same as everyone else who ever lit a candle in the dark. You don't get certainty, Ivet Sarn. You never did, in trade, in weather, in love. You get courage, and you spend it, and sometimes the tide comes in and sometimes it doesn't." She nodded at the blank page. "Write."

So Ivet wrote.

*To the Steward of the Covenant, wherever the road holds you —*

*The vanguard of Lord-Regent Casimir sits at Gray Ford as of this morning. They have raised a gallows and hanged a keeper of the Drowned Mother in her own colors. The main host follows. Our walls are old and our garrison is four hundred men and our council cannot yet agree on whether to fight, kneel, or flee.*

*But I am writing to you, not them, because you are the only hope left that isn't a lie.*

*The charter of the free cities says the covenant protects those who keep faith. We kept faith even in the godless years — quietly, badly, in cellars and fishmarkets, but we kept it. Maren Ostrey can tell you what the old rites were. I can tell you what we have: grain for eight months, walls that have never fallen to assault, and four hundred people willing to die on them if someone tells us dying means something.*

*I do not know if you are a miracle or a rumor. I suspect you do not either.*

*Come to Dunmarrow. Or send word. Or send a sign, if signs are things you can still do. We have bet everything on you, and we never even met you, and I find I am not sorry.*

*Ivet Sarn, First Councilor of Dunmarrow*

She read it twice, hated the last paragraph, kept it anyway, sealed it with wax and the city's arms — a lighthouse over crossed oars — and gave it to Skell's fastest rider, a boy of seventeen named Pell who volunteered before she finished asking.

"East road's watched," she told him. "Go by the cliff path, cut inland at Saint's Rest, and whatever you meet between here and the hills, don't stop to be brave. Bravery's for people with armies."

"Yes, First Councilor." He took the letter like it might bite, which, Ivet thought, it might.

* * *

Three days later, the main host came into view, and the voting stopped mattering.

Ivet stood on the wall above the Eastgate with Brell and Skell and half the council, watching the plain fill. It filled slowly, the way floodwater fills a field — not dramatic, not fast, just inevitable, banner after banner after banner, the black stag of House Casimir multiplying until the whole eastern horizon seemed to ripple with it. Siege engines on ox-carts. Pontoons in bundles. Forage wagons stripping the countryside ahead of them like locusts.

"Ten thousand," Brell said. "Maybe twelve. And that's not all of it — he'll have reserves at the ford."

"Twelve thousand," Dree repeated faintly. He had stopped arguing for terms around noon, when the first herald rode up under a white banner and read the Regent's proclamation from the saddle: the city would open its gates, deliver all practitioners of forbidden arts and all who sheltered them, and submit to the purging of its shrines, upon pain of fire.

"He wants Maren," Ivet said. "And me. And anyone whose name is on his lists."

"He wants more than that," said a new voice, and they turned to find Pell the messenger boy standing in the gateway below, mud to his thighs, his horse lathered dead-lame behind him. His face was gray with exhaustion and something else. "First Councilor. I found her. Or — I found where she'd been."

Ivet came down the wall stairs faster than dignity allowed.

Pell stood in the guardroom, cradling a cup of wine he wasn't drinking, and held out his hand. In his palm lay a knot — a luck-knot, woven of three cords, blue and white and a deep earthy brown, tied in a pattern Ivet had never seen but Maren Ostrey, arriving breathless at the commotion, clearly had. The old shrine-keeper took it with shaking fingers and pressed it to her lips.

"This is hedge-work," she whispered. "This is the old craft, the craft from before the breaking. Where—"

"She wouldn't meet me herself," Pell said. "I was on the cliff path at Saint's Rest, and there was a woman waiting at the shrine there — the shrine that lit itself, everyone's talking about it. She said she couldn't come. Said she had to go somewhere first, somewhere the road ends, and that if she lived past what she had to do there, she'd come. But she gave me this for the city, and she taught me the words to say with it." He swallowed. "She said: *tie it to the gate, and tell them the thorn remembers its promises.*"

Nobody spoke. Outside, distantly, came the sound of the enemy's camp being pitched — ten thousand small noises of an army settling in for a siege.

Maren closed her fist around the knot. "The thorn," she said. "Tamsin's hollow. Gods above and below, it's true. It's all true. The stone, the steward, the hollow — it's all true, and she's gone to it."

"Then we hold," Ivet said.

Every head turned. She heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone taller and calmer, some ancestor of hers who had faced down raiders in the city's founding wars. She felt nothing like that person. She felt like a woman of fifty-three whose feet hurt and who had just bet a city on a knot of string and a sentence from a tired boy.

But she said it again, louder, so the guardroom heard, so the wall would hear, so the whole frightened city would hear by morning, because courage without certainty was still courage, and it was the only coin she had ever had to spend.

"We hold the walls. We tie the knot to the Eastgate tonight, in sight of their camp, and let Casimir's heralds report *that* to their master. Let him know what we believe is coming." She looked around at them — Brell, Skell, Dree pale and silent, Maren radiant and terrified, Pell swaying on his feet. "The free cities bet everything on a rumor of a witch. So did I. Now we find out what rumors are worth."

* * *

They tied the knot at moonrise.

Maren Ostrey did the tying herself, climbing the Eastgate stair on legs that complained, and Ivet went with her because someone should, and because she wanted to see it done. Below them the enemy camp sprawled under a thousand cookfires, and above them the moon hung thin and cold, and between those two lights Maren knotted the charm to the iron ring beside the gate with hands that shook and did not stop.

"There," she said, when it was done. The little knot stirred in the night wind, blue and white and brown, absurdly small against the stone. "Now we're either the bravest fools on this coast or the beginning of something."

"Both," Ivet said. "In my experience they usually travel together."

She stayed on the wall a long while after the old woman went down. The wind off the sea smelled of salt and tar and, faintly, of smoke from the enemy's fires. Twelve thousand men. Old walls. Four hundred defenders. A knot of string.

Far to the south, past the plain, past the hills, somewhere along a road that ended in thorns, a woman she had never met was walking toward something terrible on all their behalf. Ivet Sarn did not pray — she had spent thirty years in trade and trusted ledgers over heavens — but she stood at the parapet with the wind pulling at her coat, and she spoke aloud to the dark, to the witch, to whatever was left listening:

"Hurry."

And far away, deeper in the hills than any road ran, the wind changed. It came up from the south carrying a scent none of them had smelled in twenty years — green growth, wild rose, the sweet sharp smell of thorn in flower — and in the enemy camp below, sentries shifted and muttered at a breeze that moved against the wind, and on the Eastgate the little knot trembled once, like a pulse, like an answer, like a door beginning to open.
