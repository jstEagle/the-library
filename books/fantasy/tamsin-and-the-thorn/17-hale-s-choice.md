## 17. Hale's Choice

The road to the hollow smelled of rain and old smoke, and Josiah Hale rode through it with a writ in his breast pocket that felt heavier than any warrant he had ever carried.

*Seize the woman alive.* The Lord-Regent's seal pressed against his ribs like a second heart, wax and gold and threat braided together. *Alive, Captain. Unmarked. She is worth more breathing than dead.*

Twenty years of witch-finding, and never once had Casimir's chancery cared whether his quarry lived or burned. Hale knew what that meant. He had known since the courier handed him the order at Ashford Cross, known with the cold certainty of a man who has spent his life reading the grammar of power: the Regent did not want Bram Vasquez for her crimes. He wanted her for her blood.

He wanted to use her the way one uses a key.

Hale rode on anyway.

That was the thing about himself he could no longer look at straight — the thing that had been growing in him like rot in a wall since Dunmarrow, since the shrine-keeper Maren Colm had died on the flagstones of her own sanctuary with his hand around her wrist. *You're grieving someone too,* she had said, blood on her teeth, no fear in her voice at all, only a terrible gentleness. *I can see it. It's why you're so good at this. You think if you burn enough of us, yours will come back.*

He had told himself she was a witch, and witches lied. That was doctrine. That was the whole architecture of his life: witches lied, magic murdered, and grief was just fuel for the fire that kept the realms clean.

But Maren Colm had not lied. And his sister —

His sister had died with thorn-scratches on her palms and fever-bright eyes, whispering prayers into a darkening room while the village priest stood by and the covenant-stone in the shrine stayed cold as riverbed gravel. Lissa had prayed until her voice gave out. Nothing had answered. Nothing had answered because the gods were gone, severed from the world by whatever treachery had happened in the capital a generation before — and Hale had built his whole life on punishing the desperate for praying into that silence.

He had called it justice. He had called it protecting people from the frauds and predators who fed on hope. Some of them *had* been predators; he was honest enough to know that. But underneath the doctrine, underneath the ledgers of the condemned, there had always been a boy kneeling beside a bed, holding a small hot hand, listening to prayers go up like sparks and fall back down as ash.

*If anything is listening,* twelve-year-old Josiah had thought, *please.*

Nothing had been listening. And he had decided, in the way children decide things, absolutely and forever, that this was the fault of everyone who claimed otherwise.

Now the old gods were answering again. Shrines rekindling across three provinces. Soldiers on the eastern road crossing themselves and weeping. And somewhere ahead, past the wall of thorns that every peasant in the district spoke of in whispers, the last heir of the broken pact was preparing to do something that would either save the free cities or unmake the throne his master sat upon.

Hale crested the ridge above the hollow at dusk, and stopped his horse so abruptly that the animal tossed its head in protest.

The thorn-wall rose out of the misted ground like a wave caught mid-break. It was not a hedge. Hedges were planted, tended, obedient. This was a living rampart thirty feet high, black branches woven dense as chainmail, and it moved — slowly, the way a sleeping animal breathes, the whole mass shifting with a rhythm that had nothing to do with wind. White flowers opened along its face as he watched, absurdly delicate against the black, and closed again. The air around it hummed. Not sound, exactly. More like the feeling of standing near a struck bell after the ringing stops.

His men reined in behind him. Six of them, hand-picked, loyal to the crown rather than to him — he had noticed that selection now, understood its meaning. Sergeant Odo Vass cleared his throat.

"Captain? That's the wall?"

"That's the wall."

"It's *moving*, sir."

"Yes." Hale dismounted. His knees cracked; he was fifty-one years old and had spent thirty of those years sleeping badly. "Wait here."

"Sir, the writ says—"

"The writ says I seize her, Sergeant. It doesn't say you do it for me." He took the lantern down from his saddle. "If I'm not back by full dark, ride for the garrison at Corvane and report that the witch-finder Hale went mad and walked into a bramble."

Nobody laughed. They never laughed anymore. He missed that, oddly — the rough jokes of the early years, when they had all still believed they were doing something clean.

He walked down toward the hollow alone.

* * *

The thorns let him approach to within ten paces and then simply refused him. There was no violence in it. He reached out — some part of him needing to test, to touch, to make this real and therefore manageable — and the nearest branch bent away from his fingers like water refusing oil. He tried again. Again the yielding absence, the polite impossibility.

"You have to mean it."

Her voice came from behind him, and he turned with his hand already at his sword — an old habit, useless here, he suspected, but habits were the bones of a man.

Bram Vasquez stood at the edge of the clearing where the thorn-wall began, and she looked nothing like the fugitive described in his files. The files said *hedge-witch, dangerous, desperate*. The woman before him was tired in a way that had gone past exhaustion into something calmer, a woman who had set down a weight she'd carried twenty years and found her arms still shaped to hold it. Her left palm was bandaged. Thorn scratches marked her forearms. Behind her, faint through the black weave of the wall, a light burned warm in a cottage window.

"You're Hale," she said. "The witch-finder."

"I am."

"Maren Colm's blood is on your hands."

It was not an accusation. That was the worst of it. She said it the way one states weather, and it landed in him like a blade finding the gap between plates.

"Yes," he said.

She studied him. Her eyes were brown and level and very old for her face, and he had the disorienting sense of being read — not searched, not interrogated, but *read*, the way one reads a letter left out in the rain, taking in both the words and the damage.

"Why did you come alone?" she asked.

"Because I wanted to see the wall before my men burned it."

"They can't burn it."

"I know. I've read the reports from Corvane. Fire doesn't take. Steel doesn't cut. One of the Regent's engineers suggested siege trebuchets." He heard his own voice go dry. "I told him he might as well bombard the sea."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe — the grim fellowship of two people who have both spent their lives serving institutions that mistake stubbornness for strength.

"You came alone," she said again, "and your men are loyal to Casimir, not to you, and you know it. So either you're the most reckless man in his service, or you already know what's in that writ isn't justice." She lifted her bandaged hand slightly. "Which is it, Captain?"

The rain began then, soft and steady, beading on the thorn-leaves, silvering the dark. Somewhere behind the wall, a kettle whistled — a small, domestic, impossible sound, and Hale felt it strike something in him that thirty years of doctrine had armored over: the memory of Lissa's kitchen, the smell of bread, the ordinary evening before the fever came.

"My sister," he said, and stopped.

The word hung between them in the rain.

"Lissa," he said. "Lissa Hale. Died eleven years ago, in the village of Marrow's End, of a wasting fever. She was nineteen. She prayed for nine days." His voice did not shake. He had trained it not to, and now he hated the training, hated the iron discipline of his own throat. "I held her hand. I was already a witch-finder by then. I told her the gods weren't real, that prayer was a comfort and nothing more, that the charlatans selling charms outside her window were vultures. I told her—" 

He stopped again. The rain filled the silence.

"What did you tell her?" Bram asked quietly.

"That if she stopped believing in them, she'd stop being afraid."

Bram closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she did not wipe them, and somehow that undid him more than weeping would have.

"And did she?" Hale asked. He hadn't meant to ask. The question came out of him like a splinter working loose. "Stop being afraid."

"No," Bram said. "She prayed harder. Because dying people aren't afraid of gods, Captain. They're afraid of the silence where gods should be. And you made her pray into it alone, and then you spent eleven years burning anyone who reminded you of her bedside." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the fine lines at her eyes, the thorn-scratches, the steadiness. "You didn't hunt witches because you believed magic was evil. You hunted them because every hedge-charm and luck-knot was a promise that somebody, somewhere, was listening — and it wasn't fair. It wasn't *fair* that they got answers and Lissa got ash."

"Careful," he said, and his hand was on his sword hilt, and he did not remember putting it there.

"Draw it," she said. "See how far that gets you. See if the wall cares. See if *she* cares." 

And the strangest thing happened, the thing he would carry for the rest of his life however long that proved to be: the thorn-wall shifted. The great black mass leaned, almost imperceptibly, toward him — toward *him*, the witch-finder, the burner of shrines — and along its face the white flowers opened, dozens of them, pale as moths, turning in the rainless air beneath the rain like faces at a window.

"She knows about Lissa," Bram said softly. "Vesh remembers everything. Every unanswered prayer ever prayed. He showed me, the first night. All of them, going back centuries — a sky full of falling sparks." She raised her bandaged hand, palm outward, and Hale saw that the bandage was spotted through with fresh red. "You want to seize me for the Regent. Fine. Here I am. But first — first, Captain — I'm going to show you something, and then you're going to choose. Not me. You."

"I don't want your sorcery."

"I know. Nobody ever does. That's rather the point of it." She held out her hand. "Give me your arm. The one that aches. The one that's ached since the night she died, though you've never told a single physician, because telling them would make it real."

His arm *did* ache. Had ached for eleven years, deep in the meat of it, a dull grief-pain no salve touched. He had told himself it was an old training injury. He had told himself many things.

"This is a trick," he said.

"Everything true is a trick, the first time you see it."

And Josiah Hale — chief witch-finder of the Regency, breaker of the Corvane circle, the man whose name made hedge-witches flee their own weddings — rolled up his sleeve and gave his arm to a witch.

* * *

Her fingers were cool. The bandaged palm settled over the ache like a compress over a fever, and for a moment nothing happened, and he almost laughed with relief—

Then the wall of the world went thin.

He was in the bedroom at Marrow's End. Rain on the shutters — the same rain, he realized, the same grey autumn rain, eleven years folded up inside it. Candlelight. The smell of tallow and sickness and, underneath, bread from the kitchen, because their mother had insisted on baking even now, even at the end, because grief needed somewhere to put its hands.

Lissa lay in the bed, nineteen and translucent, her hair stuck to her forehead, and she was praying. Not aloud anymore — her voice had given out on the sixth day — but her lips moved, and her fingers moved, tracing shapes on the blanket, and Hale recognized the shapes because he had seen them carved on seized charms a hundred times since: the old signs, the covenant-signs, the alphabet of a conversation with heaven.

*Please,* her fingers said. *Please. Please.*

And Hale — the Hale of now, standing in the memory like a man waist-deep in cold water — watched the space above the bed. Watched the candle flame lean. Watched something vast and sorrowful press against the room from the outside, like a face against glass, like a hand against a door, straining to reach a child it loved and could not touch.

*Severed,* the darkness seemed to say, in a voice like wind through a canyon. *Severed, severed, severed. Oh, little one. Little one. If I could—*

The candle guttered. The presence withdrew. Lissa's fingers slowed, and stilled, and her face in the last moment was not peaceful and was not terrified — it was *listening*, listening to a silence, waiting for an answer that had been murdered in the capital before she was born.

The memory released him.

Hale came back to himself on his knees in the wet grass, and he was screaming, or had been screaming, his throat raw, and Bram Vasquez knelt in front of him with her ruined hand hovering an inch from his shoulder, not touching, letting him come back on his own.

"She heard," he rasped. "She — something heard her. Something was *there*—"

"Vesh. Pressing against the severance. He hears everything. He has heard every prayer since the pact broke, and answered none of them, because he couldn't, because answering requires the covenant and the covenant requires a steward and the steward's line was—" She stopped herself. "That's not mine to tell. But she wasn't praying into nothing, Captain. She was praying into a locked door, and something on the other side was breaking its heart."

Hale knelt in the rain and wept. Not gracefully, not quietly — the way men weep when the dam goes all at once, thirty years of it, the boy at the bedside and the young officer signing his first death warrant and the middle-aged man watching Maren Colm die saying *you're grieving someone too* — all of it coming through him in great racking waves while the thorn-wall stood over him with its white flowers open like a congregation.

When it was done — not finished, he understood it would never be finished, but done for now — he knelt there a while longer, breathing. Then he drew the writ from his breast pocket.

The rain had gotten to it. The wax seal had softened; the ink had begun to run, Casimir's orders dissolving into grey ghosts. He looked at it for a long time.

Then he stood, walked back up the slope to where his six men waited under the dripping trees, and held the writ up so they could see the seal.

"Sergeant Vass," he said. "Who gave me this order?"

"The Lord-Regent, sir."

"And what does it say?"

"*Seize the woman alive, unmarked, by any means necessary, sir.*"

"By any means necessary." Hale nodded slowly. "Do you know what that means, Sergeant? It means torture, if she resists. It means chains and a cage and the capital's questioners, because the Regent needs her *breathing* — needs her blood, needs what's in her blood, needs to forge her into a weapon against the free cities and against the gods themselves." He let the paper drop into the mud. "I have spent my life carrying out warrants. I have never once asked what the warrant served. Tonight I asked."

Odo Vass's face worked. He was a hard man, a Corvane man, but he had a daughter in the free cities, and everyone in the company knew it, and everyone knew what Casimir's armies did to cities that resisted.

"Captain," Vass said carefully, "you're asking us to commit treason."

"I'm asking you to remember your dead," Hale said. "Everyone in this company has one. Everyone in this whole godforsaken realm has one — someone who prayed into the silence and got nothing. Casimir is the reason for the silence. He broke the pact himself; I have evidence — letters, ledgers, the testimony of the shrine-keepers his inquisitors silenced. I was going to deliver it to him as a report. Instead—" He turned, and looked down the slope at the thorn-wall, at the witch standing before it with her hand extended, at the warm light in the cottage window where an old woman waited. "Instead I'm delivering it to her. To the covenant. Let the gods judge him. They remember everything. It's the only court he's ever feared."

For a long moment, only the rain.

Then Vass dismounted. Slowly, creakily, like a man setting down a weight carried too far. And knelt — not to Hale, Hale saw, but facing the hollow, facing the wall of thorns and its white flowers, the way a man kneels at a shrine when he has finally admitted there is something to kneel to.

One by one, the others followed. Five of six. The sixth, young Pell, sat on his horse with tears running freely down his face and said, "My mother. She prayed eleven years for my father's soul. Eleven years, Captain. I'll stand wherever you stand."

Hale looked down at them, these hard, tired, complicit men, and felt something in his chest that he had no doctrine for.

Behind him, the thorn-wall sighed — an actual sound now, leaves and branches moving — and began, very slowly, to open. A corridor through the black weave, walled with white blooms, leading inward toward the light.

"She's calling you," Bram said from below. Her voice had changed; there was wonder in it, and warning. "All of you. Tamsin says — Tamsin says the wall knows a penitent when it grows one." Then her head snapped up, eastward, toward the darkening hills, and the wonder drained out of her face. "Captain. How far behind you is the rest?"

"A day," Hale said. "Perhaps less. And Bram Vasquez — it isn't soldiers following me. The Regent sent the Grey Company. Thirty of them. They don't take prisoners, and they don't wait for dawn."

Through the opening in the thorns, from the cottage at the hollow's heart, an old woman's voice rang out — cracked, furious, magnificent, the voice of a woman who had been waiting twenty years for exactly this kind of trouble:

"Well then! Somebody fetch my herbs. We've a wall to feed and a rite to finish, and I'll not have the bastard's dogs interrupting supper twice!"

Hale picked his way down through the blooming gate, a traitor to his king and, for the first time in thirty years, faithful to something else — and behind him, far off in the east, he could already hear, faint under the rain, the belling of the Grey Company's horns.
