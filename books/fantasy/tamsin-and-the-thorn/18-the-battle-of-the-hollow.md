## 18. The Battle of the Hollow

The rite had three days left in it, and the thorns knew it before we did.

I felt them change on the second morning — a shiver running through the whole wall of the hollow, root to crown, like a dog lifting its head from sleep. I was kneeling at the covenant-stone with my palms pressed flat against its warmth, learning the old words syllable by syllable, when every bramble on the eastern slope turned its leaves at once, all facing outward, all facing the road.

"They're coming," Tamsin said.

She stood at the hollow's rim with her hands folded into her sleeves, and she did not sound afraid. She sounded like a woman reading weather. Behind her the morning mist lay over Ashfield in a long grey sheet, and somewhere beneath that sheet were soldiers — Casimir's elite, the ones he called his Quiet, because they killed without trumpets and burned without proclamations. Hale had turned his evidence against him; Hale's letters had bought us these three days. But letters travel slower than men who have nothing to say.

"How many?" I asked.

"Enough." Tamsin's eyes were pale as frost-rimed glass. "The wall will hold."

"The wall held for twenty years against rain and rot and one guilty woman walking up a hill. It has never held against fire and iron."

"It held," she said, "because it grew out of grief. Grief is patient, Bram. It can wait twenty years for an apology. It can wait an afternoon for soldiers." She looked at me then, and something moved behind her face that she did not let reach her mouth. "Finish your lessons. The words won't learn themselves."

* * *

They came at dusk, because Casimir's Quiet understood fear the way farmers understand soil — you plant it where it grows best.

I heard them before I saw them: not the crash of a charge but a silence moving through the trees, a silence with weight to it, the way snow has weight. The birds went first. Then the small rustling lives of the underbrush. Then even the wind seemed to hold itself aside, and the only sound left in the world was the covenant-stone humming under my hands like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

From the hollow's heart I could see the thorn-wall rising against the last light — thirty feet of living blackthorn and briar-rose, grown dense as woven iron, its thorns as long as my forearm and dark as old blood. Tamsin stood before it with her sleeves pushed back and her bare feet planted in the moss, and she looked, for the first time since I'd climbed that hill, like what she truly was: not a bitter old woman in a crumbling cottage, but a hedge-witch of the old pact, the last steward before me, the woman who had kept a god's promise alive with her own two hands while everyone else forgot it existed.

The first torch crested the ridge.

Then ten. Then fifty. They came down through the trees without a word, without a battle-cry, in that terrible trained quiet, and their armor was black-lacquered so they would not flash in the dark, and their faces were painted grey so they would not show in the gloom, and they moved like water finding its level — spreading along the hollow's rim, encircling us, cutting off every path I might have run by.

Not that I would have run. That was the difference between this Bram and the girl who had fled down this same slope twenty years ago with a stolen stone burning in her pack. This Bram had spent two days learning the old words, and the old words do not fit inside a person who is still trying to be small.

"Tamsin," I called. "Come away from the wall."

"The wall is me, girl. Where exactly would I come away to?" But she glanced back, just once, and her voice gentled in a way it never did in daylight. "Stay at the stone. Whatever you hear — whatever you hear me say — stay at the stone and keep speaking. The rite needs your voice more than it needs anything else in this world. More than it needs me. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Liar," she said fondly, and turned back to face the dark.

* * *

They brought fire first, as I'd known they would. It is the oldest answer to thorns.

Flaming arrows arced over the ridge in slow orange curves, beautiful as festival lights, and buried themselves in the briar. And the thorns drank the fire.

I watched it happen and did not believe it, even as it happened: the flames sank into the black wood the way rain sinks into summer ground, and the wall glowed along its whole length like a banked forge, and where the arrows had struck, new growth burst forth — thick, fast, hungry, thorned branches uncoiling toward the ridge like whips. A soldier too close to the wall screamed and was dragged screaming into the dark of it. The Quiet did not break formation. They did not even slow. They simply began to cut.

Axes rang against the living wood. The sound carried into the hollow's heart and into my chest, each blow landing somewhere behind my sternum, because the wall was Tamsin's grief made flesh and every axe-blow was a blow against her. I could feel her holding it — feel her the way you feel a rope gone taut in your own hands, the strain transmitted straight down the line. Her arms were raised. Her lips were moving. Sweat ran silver down her temples despite the cold, and where her feet stood, the moss had browned in a perfect circle, her life pouring out of her into the roots faster than the earth could give it back.

Keep speaking, she had said. So I spoke. The old words poured out of me in a language I hadn't known a week ago, syllables like river-stones turning under my tongue, and the covenant-stone answered each phrase with heat, with light, with a pulse that beat now in time with my own heart. Around me the hollow's air had begun to thin and brighten, as if dawn were being born underground, hours early, from the stone upward.

Vesh was there. I couldn't see him, but I felt him the way you feel a hearth in the next room — a vast warm presence folded down small, waiting, watching, bound by the old law to witness and not intervene. *Hold, little thief,* I thought I heard, or maybe it was only the stone singing. *Hold. We are so close.*

The axes reached the heartwood.

I heard it — a crack like a bone breaking in a giant's hand — and felt Tamsin stagger, forty yards away, her whole body jerking as if struck. Through the gap in the wall I saw torchlight pour in, and shapes in grey paint, and the first soldier to breach the thorns came through the hole with his axe high and his face blank as an egg.

Tamsin did not step back.

She stepped *forward*, into the breach, into the torchlight, a small grey-haired woman in a patched dress standing alone against the elite guard of Lord-Regent Casimir, and she spoke a word I had not heard her speak in any lesson, in any memory, in any of the twenty years of nights I'd spent reconstructing everything she'd ever taught me. The word was old. The word was a name — half of a name, the mortal half, the part of Vesh that had once walked the world wearing skin.

Every soldier within ten feet of her dropped his weapon and knelt.

It didn't last. Of course it didn't last. The Quiet were chosen for what had been cut out of them, and whatever Tamsin's word woke in them, their training drowned it in seconds. But it lasted long enough. Long enough for her to seal the breach with a gesture, thorns knitting across the gap like fingers closing into a fist. Long enough for me to see her face clearly in the torchlight — flushed, blazing, alive in a way I had never seen her, the way a candle is most alive in the moment before the wax gives out.

And long enough for the crossbowman on the ridge to find his line.

I saw it happen the way you see things in nightmares: slowly, completely, without any power to stop it. A flicker of movement above the wall. A black-fletched bolt arcing down through the smoke. And Tamsin — Tamsin, who had taught me to read the weather in a cow's joints and the truth in a stranger's hands, who had loved me fiercely and unforgivably and had waited twenty years at a door she built out of her own broken heart — Tamsin turned, mid-word, mid-working, to check that I was still at the stone.

She was still looking at me when it took her through the chest.

* * *

The sound I made was not a word. Later I would not remember making it. What I remember is the wall.

The wall *screamed*. Every thorn on thirty feet of living blackthorn flexed at once, and the whole hollow filled with a shriek of wood and grief, and the soldiers below stumbled back from it clutching their ears, and I was already running — I had broken every instruction she ever gave me, I was running with the old words still pouring out of my mouth because I could not stop them any more than I could stop my heart — running through the smoke and the trampled ferns to where she lay crumpled at the foot of her own thorns.

The bolt had gone in under her collarbone. Black fletching, black lacquer, a shaft as long as my forearm, and around the wound her skin was already greying, the working she'd been holding draining out of her like water from a cracked bowl. Above us the wall shuddered and sagged, thorn-tips drooping, and beyond it I heard the axes start again, patient as tide.

"Bram." Her hand found my wrist. Her grip was still strong. That was the worst of it — how strong it still was. "Bram. Listen to me. You listen better than you obey, so listen."

"Don't talk. I can— there are salves, I know the fever-salves, I can—"

"You cannot salve this, child." And she almost smiled. Blood at the corner of her mouth. "Twenty years I kept this wall on spite and guilt and the covenant's dying pulse. There's no salve for a life that's been spent. Mine was spent the night you left. Everything since has been—" she coughed, wet and deep— "has been interest."

"Then I'll stop the rite." The words tore out of me before I knew I was carrying them. "I'll stop it, we'll go, I'll take you down the mountain, I'll—"

"No." Her grip crushed my wrist. Her eyes were frost and fire both. "You will *not*. You will climb back to that stone and you will finish the words, and you will bind yourself, and you will save every praying soul in these free cities, and you will do it *now*, while I still have breath enough to hold this wall for you. Do you hear me? This is the last lesson. The last one. The price is always paid by someone, Bram. The covenant was never free. It was only ever *worth* it."

"I can't—"

"You can." Her hand slid from my wrist to my cheek, and her palm was cool, and gentle, and shaking. "You've been able to since you were nine years old and you mended the miller's boy's fever when I told you it was beyond you. I have loved you angry and I have loved you absent and I have loved you across twenty years of silence, and if you let those men through that wall to kill you before the words are done, I will haunt you until the end of both realms. Now *go*."

Above us, the wall cracked again. Torchlight spilled through a second breach, and grey-painted shapes poured toward it, and Tamsin turned from me — turned with a bolt in her chest and her spine straight as a young tree — and raised both arms into the dark and *held*.

I ran.

* * *

The rest happened the way a storm happens: all at once, and forever.

I reached the stone. I put my bleeding palms against its burning face. And I stopped speaking the old words as a student speaks them — carefully, haltingly, a child reciting — and began to speak them as they were meant to be spoken, which is to say the way a heart beats: without decision, without pause, without any possibility of stopping.

Fury and heartbreak fused in me like two rivers meeting, and the current they made was singular, and it had a name, and the name was *purpose*. Every axe-blow against the wall landed in my chest and came out of my mouth as words. Every drop of blood Tamsin spent behind me went into the stone through my hands. I could feel the covenant waking under my skin, layer upon layer, the old pact unrolling through me like a scroll, and I could feel the gods gathering on the far side of it — Vesh nearest, vast and grieving and *ready*, and behind him the others, the forgotten ones, the silenced ones, pressing close against the thinning veil like faces against frosted glass.

The wall fell.

I felt it fall — felt Tamsin's strength end, felt the great thorn-wall sigh down into ordinary brambles, felt the hollow open like a wound. Torches flooded in. Boots on moss. The Quiet came across the clearing in their terrible silence, and the lead soldier raised his black axe over me, and I did not stop speaking, and I was not afraid, because the words had passed the point where fear could reach them.

The axe never fell.

Warmth took the clearing — a warmth like noon, like harvest, like every hearth-fire in every home of my childhood lit at once — and the soldier froze mid-swing, and his grey-painted face turned slowly upward, and mine did too.

The sky above the hollow had opened. Not clouds parting — *opening*, the night peeled back like skin from fruit, and behind it was not stars but *presence*, ranks upon ranks of gathered light, the old gods of the broken pact assembled at the threshold of the world, and at their fore stood a figure I had known since before I could name him: a beggar's stoop unfolding into a giant's height, too-old eyes kindling like the first fires ever struck.

Vesh looked down at the armies of Casimir, and at me, and at the small grey shape lying still at the foot of the ruined wall.

*Witnessed,* he said, and his voice was the voice of the stone, and the voice of the storm, and the voice of every prayer ever whispered into an empty room. *The price is offered. The blood is given. Speak the last words, daughter of the pact. Speak them, and we will answer.*

My hands were on the stone. My blood was in its grain. Tamsin's blood was on my hands, and her last lesson was in my mouth, and outside the hollow the vanguard of an army was already turning in panic, carrying back to the capital the first true news the world had heard in twenty years:

The gods were answering.

I drew the breath that would finish it — and through the smoke, at the tree-line, I saw a single rider coming down the slope at a gallop, black cloak streaming, and riding beside him, impossibly, a second rider whose horse cast no shadow at all.

Hale. And something older, riding with him.

The last words rose in my throat like dawn.
