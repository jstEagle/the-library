## 19. Reforging

The thorn-wall was burning.

Bram felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the air like the moment before lightning, a taste of copper and scorched green. She stood at the hollow's heart with Tamsin's blood still wet on her hands and looked back through the archway of living wood to where the wall of bramble that had guarded this place for twenty years was catching fire under Casimir's siege-oil, torch after torch arcing over the top like falling stars.

*Hold,* Tamsin had said, an hour ago, pressing her back toward the cottage door with hands that shook only a little. *The wall will hold.*

The wall had held for twenty years against rain and rot and the slow gnawing of seasons. It could not hold against men who had come to burn a god out of the world.

"Bram." Vesh's voice came from everywhere at once, from the beggar's mouth standing beside her and from the old stones beneath her feet and from somewhere vast and patient beyond both. "It is time."

She looked down at her palms. The name burned there — her own name, written in the covenant-stone's fire twenty years too late, and beneath it now the darker stain that was not hers. Tamsin lay in the cottage behind her, propped against the hearthstone where Bram had dragged her, her side soaked black, her breath coming in shallow pulls that each one sounded like a door closing.

"Say it again," Tamsin whispered. "The words. Say them so I know you have them."

"I have them." Bram knelt, and her knees found the grooves worn into the floor by two generations of apprentices kneeling exactly there. "I've always had them. You made sure of that. You said I'd curse the day you taught me, and you were right, and I'm grateful, and I hate you, and—"

"Hush." Tamsin's mouth twitched, the ghost of the smile that had once been fierce enough to frighten tax collectors. "Save your breath. You'll need all of it."

Outside, the first section of thorn-wall went down with a sound like a scream. Men shouted. Steel rang on stone. Somewhere in the smoke, Hale's voice rose above the rest — not commanding the assault but arguing against it, and being ignored, and Bram sent a silent, desperate prayer to whatever remained listening that the witch-finder would find his courage faster than the soldiers would find their torches.

*They are coming for you,* Vesh said. Not unkindly. Simply true, the way gods were true.

"Then we'd best be quick."

* * *

The hollow's heart was not the cottage. It never had been. It was the well.

Bram had forgotten — no, she had *buried* it, the way she buried everything — but her feet remembered the path around the eastern gable, past the bee-balm gone feral, to the ring of white stones where the ground fell away into darkness. As a child she had been forbidden to look into it. As a runaway she had stolen its stone. And now she stood at its lip with blood on her hands and the whole world burning at her back, and she looked down, and the dark looked back.

The well was not empty. It had never been empty. Far below, deeper than any water, something turned over in its sleep — or rather, many somethings, the vast slow attention of every god the covenant had ever bound, waking one by one as the stone in her pocket sang to them.

She drew it out. The covenant-stone was warm as a living thing, veined now with light, and when she held it over the well's mouth it pulled toward the dark like a compass needle finding north.

"The words," Vesh said. He had taken his beggar-shape again, rag-wrapped and bent, because — he had told her once — mortals trusted what they pitied, and pity opened doors that majesty sealed shut. But his eyes were the same eyes that had watched her steal plums from Tamsin's drying racks when she was nine years old, and they were full of something that in a mortal face she would have called grief. "Speak them, daughter of the line, and mean them. That is the whole of the rite. It was always the whole of the rite. We only ever asked to be *meant*."

Bram closed her eyes.

And the words were there, the way Tamsin's lessons were always there, carved somewhere below memory in the place where childhood lived. She had recited them as a game. She had recited them as a rebellion, spitting them at the ceiling of her cot the night before she ran. She had never once spoken them *meaning*, and that, she understood now, was why the covenant had broken, why the gods had gone silent, why prayers had fallen like seeds on stone for twenty years. Not because Casimir killed a king. Because the last heir of the pact had stopped meaning anything at all.

She opened her eyes.

"Old ones," she said, and her voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second, "who walked before walls and will walk after them. Who answered bread with rain and grief with harvest. Who kept faith when faith was cheap."

Below her, in the deep of the well, the turning slowed. Listening.

"I am Bram Vasquez," she said. "Hedge-witch. Thief. Runaway. Daughter of no one, which is a lie, and heir of everyone, which is also a lie, and I am done lying." Her hands shook. She let them. "You knew me small. You watched me grow crooked. You saw me take what wasn't mine and bury what was, and you did not strike me down, though by every law you had the right. So I will say the rest, and you will judge whether I mean it."

The firelight from the burning wall painted the clouds red. Smoke rolled across the stars. Somewhere out there, men were dying for a regent's fear, and here in the hollow's heart a woman stood over a hole in the world holding a stone and twenty years of guilt, and there was no one left to run to, and nowhere left to run *to*, and Bram felt — with a clarity that was almost gentle — the exact shape of the trap close around her.

Except it was not a trap. It had never been a trap. It was a door, and she had spent her whole life mistaking doors for walls.

"I offer the covenant," she said. "Not the stone. Not the bloodline. *Me.*"

* * *

The old words came then, the binding-words, and they were not grand. That was the thing no one told you about the sacred: it wore work-clothes. Tamsin had taught her the liturgy of the reforging in fragments across a hundred ordinary evenings — while salting meat, while mending nets, while walking fence-lines — and Bram had thought it strange even then that the holiest speech she knew was built from the vocabulary of chores. *Bind and be bound. Keep and be kept. Answer and be answered.* It was the grammar of a household. Of a promise between people who intended to see each other tomorrow.

She spoke it now, and the well drank the sound.

"Bind and be bound," she said. "I will answer the need that is cried aloud, and the need that is swallowed. I will keep the door open between what is mortal and what is not, and I will stand in it, and I will not step aside."

The stone grew hot in her hand. Light climbed her wrist like ivy.

"Keep and be kept. I ask no throne and no temple. I ask only this: that when I fail — and I will fail — you remember that I stayed."

The ground trembled. In the well, the darkness began to rise — not water, not void, but *attention*, the gathered regard of everything old and hungry and faithful, rising toward her like dawn rising toward a mountaintop.

"Answer and be answered." Her voice broke clean in half, and she spoke the last of it through the break, because Tamsin had taught her that too: *the words don't need your strength, girl, they need your honesty.* "I am afraid. I do not want this. I wanted a small life in a smugglers' quarter with luck-knots and fever-salves and no one knowing my name. And I am giving it up, freely, with my eyes open, because someone has to hold the door, and I am so tired of running that staying feels like mercy."

She held the stone out over the well.

"And I mean it."

* * *

The stone left her hand.

It did not fall. It *descended*, slowly, the way a leaf descends, trailing light, and where it passed the air itself changed texture — thickened, brightened, became the kind of air that exists only in the first hour of morning or the last minute of a life. Bram felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave the jaw: absence first, then a strange ringing wholeness.

Then the well answered.

Light came up out of the dark like a tide, and it did not burn her; it *recognized* her. It poured over her hands — Tamsin's blood and her own burnt name and all of it — and sank in, and Bram felt every year of her body at once, every scar, every cold night sleeping in ditches, every small kindness she had ever done and every small cruelty, weighed and known and *kept*. This was the price. Not death. Death would have been easy. The price was to be *known*, entirely, forever, by things that did not forget.

She heard her mother's voice — a voice she had not heard since she was four years old, singing a lullaby about a boat.

She heard the fevered prayers of strangers whose names she'd never learned, the luck-knot buyers, the fever-salve children, all of them crying out into a silent sky for twenty years, and she felt each prayer land in her now like a stone dropped into her chest, and she understood: they were hers to carry. All of them. Forever.

*I stop running mid-stride,* she thought, and the thought was not despair. It was the opposite of despair. It was the feeling of a long flight ending in solid ground. *Here. Here is where I stop.*

The light reached her heart and stopped.

And the world outside the hollow changed.

* * *

She came back to herself kneeling at the well's rim, and the battle-sounds had gone strange. Not quieter — stranger. Shouting had become confusion. Confusion had become something else, something Bram had no word for until she crawled to the edge of the garden and looked out through the smoke.

The soldiers were stopping.

Not retreating. Stopping — mid-stride, weapons half-raised, faces turned upward as though the sky had called their names. And perhaps it had. Along the burning wall of thorns, Bram saw shapes moving in the fire that were not fire: tall figures wearing the silhouettes of harvest and storm and hearth-smoke, the old gods walking openly through the flames without harm, and where their shadows touched the soldiers, the soldiers *remembered*. She could see it happening. Men dropping swords to clutch at their own heads. A sergeant sinking to his knees with his mouth open around a name — his grandfather's, maybe, or his dead son's — a name the usurper's edicts had forbidden him to speak aloud for years.

Vesh stood beside her, and for the first time since she'd known him, he was not wearing a beggar's shape. He was tall, and grey as morning, and crowned in something that was not a crown but was unmistakably one, and he was looking at her the way Tamsin used to look at bread she'd baked correctly.

"You stayed," he said.

"I stayed."

"Then come away from the wall, steward. The night is not finished with you. Your teacher is calling."

Bram turned. Through the smoke and the falling embers, through the ruin of the wall that had cost Tamsin twenty years of grief to grow and one hour of fury to lose, she could see the cottage door standing open, and the thin figure in it, and the hand raised — not in blessing, not in farewell, but in the oldest gesture there was, the one that meant *come home*.

Bram ran.

For the first time in twenty years, she ran toward something instead of away, and the ground rose to meet her feet like a promise kept, and behind her the gods walked out of the fire and into the world, and ahead of her the door stood open, and she did not look back, because there was nothing behind her anymore that could make her.

There was only what waited — and whether it was Tamsin's last breath or Tamsin's last lesson, she would not know until she crossed the threshold, and the smoke parted, and she saw her mistress's face.
