## 20. The Gods Answer

It began, as all great changes do, with something small.

In the fishing village of Saltmere, a woman named Odile knelt at a shrine she had swept every morning for thirty years without once being heard. Her son's boat had gone out into the storm-dark water and not come back, and she had nothing left to offer the drowned god but her knees on cold stone and the same words she had said ten thousand times: *Bring him home. Bring him home.*

The tide went out.

It went out further than any living fisher had ever seen, the sea drawing back like a held breath, and there in the wet gleam of the exposed sand lay her son's boat, upright, whole, and her son beside it, shivering but alive, clutching a piece of driftwood carved — though he would swear this to his grave — by no human hand into the shape of a hand that had let go of him only when the shore was near enough to touch.

Odile did not weep. She stood very still in the enormous silence of the retreating water, and then she began to laugh, high and cracked and rising, and the sound carried down the coast from village to village like a spark along a fuse.

*They answer,* the laughter said. *They answer again.*

* * *

Two hundred miles east, in the trenches before Dunmarrow, a soldier named Perrin Voss felt the ground shudder and thought it was siege engines. It was not siege engines.

He was twenty-three years old, and he had marched under Casimir's black banner since he was sixteen, because his father had marched under it, and because a man who owns nothing marches for whoever feeds him. He had burned three shrines in his time. He had watched Hale's inquisitors take a shrine-keeper from a temple in Ashfield and had looked at his boots the whole while, because looking up meant thinking, and thinking meant dying, or worse.

Now the sky over Dunmarrow was doing something the sky did not do.

It was *remembering*.

Light moved through the clouds in slow rivers, gold and green and a deep bruised violet that made his teeth ache, and in that light Perrin saw faces. Not visions. Faces. His grandmother, dead eleven years, looking down at him with an expression he had never seen her wear in life — not anger, not love, but *recognition*, as if she were saying: *I know you. I have always known you. What have you done with what I gave you?*

Around him, the trench came apart. Men dropped their pikes. A sergeant began screaming the names of his dead children, over and over, and the light bent toward him each time he spoke, gentle as a mother's hand, until the sergeant fell to his knees in the mud and was quiet.

"Stand fast!" someone shouted — a captain, young, terrified, his sword shaking. "The witch-craft is a trick! Stand fast or hang!"

Perrin looked at the captain. He looked at the sky full of ancestors. And he understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only once or twice in a life, that he had been standing fast his whole existence against something that had been calling his name since before he was born.

He laid his pike down in the mud. Then the man beside him did. Then the next, and the next, a sound like rain beginning — iron on earth, iron on earth — spreading down the trench line faster than any officer could walk it, faster than fear, faster than orders.

By dawn, four thousand men stood unarmed in the fields outside Dunmarrow, facing the walls, waiting. No one had told them what they waited for. They knew anyway. You know, when your dead are watching, that you wait to be seen.

* * *

In the capital, Lord-Regent Casimir stood at the window of the high tower and watched his kingdom forget him.

That is how he experienced it — not as armies deserting, not as shrines rekindling in every market square, but as a slow erasure, a tide going out from around his feet. The servants had stopped coming. The guards on the stair had simply set down their halberds and walked away, unhurried, like men leaving a room where something embarrassing had been said. Below, in the great square, a crowd had gathered at the fountain where the old god-statues had stood headless for twenty years, and the statues were standing again — whole, serene, dripping water that ran upward against gravity into their cupped hands — and the crowd was not cheering. That was the worst of it. They were not cheering. They were *listening*, heads tilted, as if the air itself had begun to speak in voices only the dead could use.

Casimir turned from the window. His hands were steady. He had trained them to be steady for twenty years, and they obeyed even now, even here.

"It's a trick," he said to the empty chamber. "A hedge-witch's trick. Stone and smoke."

But the chamber did not agree with him. The chamber had begun, softly, to whisper.

He heard his brother's voice first. That was fitting; it was always his brother first, in dreams, in wine, in the small hours when the crown pressed its true weight down. *You were supposed to guard me,* the voice said, from somewhere behind the tapestries, from inside the walls, from the well of his own skull. *I named you my shield, Kazi. I put my back to you.*

"You were weak," Casimir said aloud, to the whisper, to the dark, to the brother whose throat he had cut in a garden twenty years ago while the nightingales sang on, oblivious. "You would have given everything back to them. Everything. Someone had to rule."

*Someone,* the whispers agreed, from everywhere now, multiplying like frost on glass. *Yes. But you did not rule. You only silenced. There is a difference, little brother, and the difference has a name, and the name is ours to speak.*

The candles guttered. In their place, light rose from the floor itself — old light, patient light, the color of memory — and in it Casimir saw them assembling. Not ghosts as the peasants imagined them, weeping and rattling chains. Something far more dreadful. They came as *witnesses*. Every soul his edicts had burned. Every shrine-keeper dragged from her temple. Every child who had prayed to a deaf heaven during the plague years and died unanswered because answering had been made treason. They stood in ranks that filled the chamber and passed through the walls and went on filling the tower to the sky, and none of them accused him. None of them needed to. Their presence was the accusation. Their remembering was the sentence.

"I took a broken realm," Casimir said, and his voice cracked on the last word, the steadiness failing at last, twenty years late. "I made it whole."

*You made it quiet,* said his brother, stepping forward out of the light with the garden's blood still dark on his collar, wearing the face Casimir had spent two decades refusing to dream. *And now the quiet is over.*

What happened next, no chronicler saw clearly, and those who claim otherwise are liars. Some say the light took him. Some say the dead closed around him like water. In the morning the throne room stood open, the crown lying on the steps of the dais, tarnished black as if it had been buried a century, and of Casimir there remained only a scorch-mark on the marble floor in the shape of a kneeling man — and even that faded within a year, as if the stone itself preferred to forget him.

The gods did not kill him. Killing was mortal work. They did something older. They *remembered him completely*, every act, every lie, every small cruelty, and held that memory up around him like a mirror with no escape from it, and whatever remains of Casimir exists inside that mirror still, seeing himself, seeing himself, seeing himself, forever.

Tyranny, it turns out, cannot survive being truly witnessed. It dissolves when the past refuses silence.

* * *

At the thorn-hollow, Bram Amberdale stood at the center of the world.

She did not feel like the center of anything. She felt like a wire through which an ocean was passing — every prayer in every tongue pouring through the covenant she had just sealed with Tamsin's blood cooling on her hands and Vesh's presence wrapped around her shoulders like a cloak of warm dark. Outside the thorn-wall, the battle was ending in a way battles do not end: not with rout but with *laying-down*, swords dropping, soldiers falling to their knees mid-charge as their own grandmothers appeared in the smoke above the field and simply looked at them.

"They're stopping," Vesh murmured. The god wore the beggar's shape still, ragged and gray-eyed, but the shape fit poorly now, glory leaking through the seams. "Look at them. Twenty years I forgot what that looks like. Mortals choosing."

Bram could barely speak. The covenant ran through her like a second heartbeat, vast and impersonal and strangely tender, and threaded through it she could feel everything — Odile's laughter on the coast, the pikes falling in the mud, the whispering tower in the capital, a thousand answered prayers blooming across both realms like stars coming out. "Is it — is it always going to feel like this?"

"No." Vesh smiled, and the smile was old and kind and sad. "It will settle. Become ordinary. That is the miracle, little heir — that the divine can bear to become ordinary again. That you will bear it."

Inside the cottage, Tamsin lay dying on her own bed, and Bram had not yet been able to make herself go in. There would be time. There would be so little time. Through the window she could see the thorn-wall blossoming — actually blossoming, white flowers opening along every black branch as the grief that had grown them finally, after twenty years, had something else to do.

"Bram," Vesh said quietly. "The soldier."

She turned. At the edge of the clearing, past the fallen and the kneeling, one man in Casimir's colors was walking toward the thorn-wall — not charging, walking, slowly, his helmet off, his face wet. Behind him came another, and another, dozens of them, drawn across the field like pilgrims, and Bram understood with a lurch that they had come to see the place where it had happened. The hollow where the world had turned. Word was already running down the lines faster than any rider: *the witch, the steward, the girl who opened heaven.*

One of them stopped at the thorn-wall, close enough to touch the flowering branches, and looked through the gap at Bram — at the blood on her hands, the light in her eyes — and dropped to his knees in the trampled grass.

Not to her. She realized that a heartbeat later, and it undid her more than worship would have. He was kneeling to the wall itself, to the flowers, to the old god standing beside her, to the whole restored order of things. To the fact of being answered.

"Get up," Bram said, and her voice broke on it. "Get up, please. I'm just—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, and found the truth waiting where the old denial used to be. "I'm the door. You don't kneel to doors."

Vesh laughed softly beside her. "You will spend the rest of your life saying that," the god said, "and they will spend the rest of theirs not believing you. Come. There is a bed inside, and a woman in it, and a debt of twenty years that the covenant cannot pay for you. That one is yours alone."

Bram looked out once more over the field — the laying-down arms, the rising light, the free city's bells beginning to ring beyond the hills, wild and unsynchronized and joyful — and then she turned and walked into the cottage, into the smell of herbs and woodsmoke that she had run from half a lifetime ago, and knelt beside the bed where Tamsin lay with her eyes already half-open, watching her come.

"Took you long enough," the old witch whispered.

"I know," Bram said. "I'm here now."

Outside, the last of Casimir's army laid down its arms, and the thorns bloomed, and everywhere in both realms the gods — patient, wounded, forgiving past all mortal understanding — got back to work.

* * *

But peace, Bram would learn, is not an ending. It is a practice. And hers was about to begin.
