## 21. Steward of Two Realms

The first frost of autumn came to Dunmarrow on a morning when Bram was elbow-deep in beeswax and feverfew, and she felt it arrive the way she now felt everything — twice. Once as the cold slipping under the shrine-house door and licking at her ankles like any ordinary draft. And once as a slow silver tide moving through the boundary itself, the membrane between realms shivering the way water shivers when a stone is dropped into it far away.

She wiped her hands on her apron and stood in the doorway of the little stone building that had once been a counting-house, back when Dunmarrow's merchants measured their wealth in ledgers instead of answered prayers. Now the counting-house held three altars, a rack of luck-knots she still tied by hand because some things should not be done by miracle, and a hearth where an old woman's kettle sang every morning whether anyone came or not.

Through the open door she could see the city waking. Smoke from a hundred chimneys rose straight in the windless cold. On the corner opposite, a fishwife paused at the wayside shrine — a niche in the wall no bigger than a bread oven, holding nothing but a bowl of river water and a sprig of rowan — and pressed her palm flat against the stone. Bram felt the touch land inside her own chest, gentle as a knock.

*Safe passage on the water,* the fishwife didn't say aloud. She only thought it, the way people thought prayers now, half-embarrassed, half-hopeful, the way you might speak to a parent who had been silent for twenty years and had suddenly begun answering again.

Bram answered. It cost her almost nothing these days — a thread of warmth drawn up through the soles of her feet, through the stone, into the woman's palm. The fishwife smiled at nothing, shouldered her baskets, and went down toward the harbor whistling.

That was the work. That was all of it, most days: ten thousand small mercies, each one costing less than a candle's worth of herself, each one leaving her a little more translucent than the year before. She had learned to stop being afraid of that. Mostly.

She poured the melted wax into molds, set the salves to cool, and let the morning be ordinary for as long as it would allow.

* * *

In the old life — the one before the hollow, before the rite, before Tamsin's blood dried on her hands and Vesh stood witness with his too-old eyes gone bright as coins — ordinary had been a thing she hoarded. Ordinary was selling luck-knots in Corvane's smugglers' quarter and pretending the knots were only knots. Ordinary was a cellar with a buried stone she never looked at, and guilt she never dug up either, and nights when she lay awake listening to the silence where the gods' voices should have been and told herself the silence was freedom.

She had made peace with smallness, she'd thought. She had built a whole life the size of a shoebox and called it contentment.

Now she walked out of Dunmarrow's east gate on a road that hummed underfoot, and the world was the size it had always secretly been, and there was no going back to the shoebox. The road itself was part of the covenant now — every mile of it threaded with shrines, and every shrine a finger of the divine reaching into mortal clay, and every finger connected, somewhere past seeing, to her.

The first village beyond the walls was Ashfield, or what Ashfield had become. When she'd fled through it two years ago, running east with Hale's hounds behind her, the village had been a scar: burned fields, a shattered shrine, folk who watched her pass with hunger and terror tangled together in their faces. Now the fields were gold with late barley. The shrine had been rebuilt twice its old size, roofed in green copper gone soft with verdigris, and its keeper — a round, cheerful man named Odo who had been a swineherd before the gods came back and decided he could hear them — met her at the edge of the village with bread still warm from his wife's oven.

"Steward." He always said it like a title, and she always flinched, and he always pretended not to notice. "You'll take dinner. No arguments. Marta's made the goose you like."

"I like every goose," Bram said. "I'm not fussy. I was a hedge-witch's apprentice; we ate whatever the hedge gave us."

"And look what the hedge made of you." Odo beamed, unbothered, and took her horse's bridle as if the animal were his.

Dinner was long and loud, full of neighbors dropping in with ailments and thanks and, inevitably, requests. A child with a cleft lip the surgeons couldn't mend. An old man who wanted to know if his drowned son had gone where the gods could hold him. A young widow asking, in a whisper, whether her husband's prayer on the night he died had been heard — he'd prayed, she knew he'd prayed, but the gods had been silent then, in the years of the breaking, and she needed to know the prayer hadn't fallen into nothing.

Bram sat among them with goose grease on her fingers and answered what she could. The child she touched, and the bone beneath the skin shifted and settled with a sensation like a knuckle cracking in her own jaw, and the mother wept into her apron. The old man she could only comfort: yes, the dead were held, all of them, even the ones who died in the silence; the gods kept faith with the faithful dead retroactively, which was a sentence she'd learned from Vesh and still didn't entirely understand. The widow she took aside, and held her hands, and reached back along the thin bright thread of the man's last prayer to find it — not unanswered, only unheard, waiting all those years in the dark like a letter in a dead man's pocket — and she carried it the rest of the way herself, and the widow went home lighter by exactly the weight of one grief.

It was good work. She wanted that written plainly somewhere, in case she ever forgot: *it was good work.* But walking out of Ashfield at dusk, past the shrine where Odo's candle burned, she felt the familiar ache open under her ribs — the ache of the door that had closed behind her. Somewhere in another life, a version of her was still selling luck-knots in Corvane, going home to a rented room, growing old in the ordinary way, unremarked and unburdened and free.

Free. She tested the word and found it hollow, the way a gourd is hollow — shaped like something nourishing, containing nothing. She hadn't been free in Corvane. She'd been buried alive and calling the grave a house.

Still. The ache stayed with her as she made camp under a rowan tree, and she let it stay, because Tamsin had taught her that grief refused is grief doubled.

* * *

Tamsin's grave stood on the rise above the thorn-hollow, where the witch had asked for it — asked with her last breath, in fact, which had been typical of her, arranging matters even while dying. Bram came four times a year, at the turnings of the seasons, and this time she came in the first week of winter, when the hollow below wore its thorns like a sleeping beast wears its spines.

The cottage was gone. In the rite's aftermath, with the covenant reforged and the thorn-wall's purpose fulfilled, the great briar had loosened its grip over the course of a single spring, unwinding branch by branch like a fist deciding, after twenty years, to open. What remained was a ring of ancient thorn-trees around a clearing of deep grass, and in the middle of the clearing, the foundation stones of a cottage, and growing up through the foundation stones — because the world has a sense of humor, or the gods do — a single wild rose, blooming out of season since the day it first appeared and showing no intention of stopping.

Bram knelt at the grave. The marker was a flat fieldstone she'd cut herself, and the words on it she'd argued about with Vesh for a full day before settling:

*TAMSIN OF THE HOLLOW*
*SHE KEPT THE DOOR*

Not *witch*, though she had been the finest hedge-witch in living memory. Not *steward*, though she had carried the covenant's dying pulse alone for twenty years, feeding it drops of her own life the way you'd feed a fire one straw at a time. *She kept the door.* Because that was the truth underneath all the other truths: Tamsin had stood in the gap between realms when no one else would, had kept the door from closing forever, and had done it while hating the gods a little, and loving her runaway apprentice completely, and never once saying so in words.

"I brought honey," Bram said aloud. Her breath smoked. "From the Dunmarrow hives. You'd have complained they weren't wild enough. You always said tame bees made lazy honey."

The wind moved through the thorn-trees, and it sounded almost like laughter, and Bram chose to believe it was.

She talked for a while — about the shrines, about Odo's goose, about the trouble in the northern cities where the new temples were arguing over doctrine as if the gods were a text instead of a presence. She talked about the dreams she still had, sometimes, of the night she'd run: the stolen stone heavy in her satchel, Tamsin's voice cracking across the yard behind her, and the terrible liberty of the empty road. In the dreams she always stopped. In the dreams she turned around and went back, and Tamsin opened the door without a word, and neither of them ever mentioned it again, and Bram woke with wet eyes and an ache like a healed bone before a storm.

"You'd tell me to stop apologizing," she said. "You'd say guilt past its usefulness is just vanity wearing sackcloth." A pause. "You'd also say it while making me scrub the cauldron, so don't think I've forgotten you were not an easy woman."

The rose at the grave's foot — it had seeded itself here too, from the clearing below, a second impossible bloom — turned its face toward her in the windless cold.

Bram pressed her palm to the fieldstone, and let a little warmth go down into the earth, not magic exactly, not prayer, just the mortal gesture of warming your hands at a fire someone else built. Then she stood, brushed off her knees, and went down into the hollow to wait for Vesh, because it was nearly solstice, and on the solstices they walked the boundary together.

* * *

He came at moonrise, the way he always did, resolving out of the shadows between the thorn-trees. He wore a beggar's shape still — it was the shape she'd known him in first, and gods are sentimental about their entrances — ragged cloak, bare feet that left no prints in the frost, and eyes that had been old when the mountains were young. But the disguise fit worse every year. Light leaked from him now at the seams. When he smiled, which was often, the frost around his feet melted into a circle of green grass, and he seemed genuinely sorry about it every time, and it happened anyway.

"Steward," he said.

"Beggar," she said.

They fell into step together, out of the hollow and onto the boundary-road — not a road any cart could follow, a path that ran along the seam of things, where the mortal world showed its lining. Walking it was like walking the shallows of a shore: solid ground underfoot, but the whole weight of another ocean pressing close on one side. To her left, the world: frost, hills, the distant lamps of Dunmarrow, smoke, sheep, the smell of woodsmoke and cold earth. To her right, the other: not light exactly, and not dark, but *attention* — the vast, patient, many-eyed attention of the old gods, who had waited twenty years in silence and now leaned close to everything at once.

Vesh walked the line between without effort, being native to neither side and at home in both. Bram walked it because she *was* native to both now, stitched through, and the seam accepted her the way a well-made garment accepts its wearer.

They walked, and they talked, the way they always did.

"The northern temples are fighting again," Bram said. "Three schisms since midsummer. They're writing creeds."

"Creeds." Vesh tasted the word with distaste. "We answer prayers. We do not require essays."

"They say you're inconsistent. That you answer some petitions and refuse others according to no visible rule."

"We answer according to need, not want. Mortals find the distinction insulting." He glanced sideways at her, and the light at his seams flickered with something gentler. "Your Tamsin understood. She raged at us for twenty years, and every rage was a prayer, and we heard every one."

Bram's throat tightened. "Did you answer any?"

"All of them," Vesh said quietly. "Just not in the coin she was asking for. We kept her alive, Steward. Twenty years past her natural span, keeping the door with nothing but stubbornness and spite. You may call that an answer or not. She'd have called it interference."

"She'd have called it a lot worse than that."

"Yes," Vesh said, with real fondness. "She had a gift for worse."

They crossed a stream that ran half in each world — on the mortal side it was frozen; on the divine side it ran silver and warm — and Bram felt the crossing in her teeth, the old double-sensation, and thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that wonder could become routine and still stay wonderful.

"Tell me what you see," Vesh said, as he always did at the stream. It was a lesson disguised as a request; he had been teaching her since the rite, patiently, in the god-fashion, by questions rather than answers.

Bram stopped and looked along the boundary, north and south, as far as her doubled sight could reach. South, the free cities glittered with shrine-lights, thousands of them, each one a point of warmth in the mortal dark, each one fed by her. North, past the mountains, Casimir's old capital sat under its pall — quieter now, since the soldiers deserted and the regent was unmade, but not quiet enough; fear lingers in stone longer than tyrants linger in palaces. Farther north, something else. A smudge on the seam. A place where the boundary was thin in the wrong way, worn like a rope worn by weather, fraying.

She frowned. "There. North-northwest, past the capital. Something's rubbing at the edge. It's not a god. It's not mortal. It's—" she searched for the word— "*hungry.*"

Vesh was silent for a long moment, and the silence had weight, and Bram realized she had never heard him go silent before, not once in two years, and her stomach dropped.

"So it begins already," he said at last, softly. "The old pact did more than bind us to answer. It bound other things *out*. Things that learned, during the long silence, exactly how thin the wall had grown." He looked at her, and his too-old eyes were kind and grim at once. "You are the door now, Steward. And doors, as your Tamsin knew better than anyone, do not only keep things in. Sometimes they must keep things out."

Far off in the north, at the very limit of her sight, the smudge shifted — and for one heartbeat Bram felt it notice her noticing, the way you feel eyes between your shoulder blades on a dark road.

The frost crackled. The rose in the hollow behind them, miles away, bloomed one flower wider.

"When?" Bram asked.

"Soon," said Vesh. "Walk with me. There is much I must teach you, and the winter will be short."

They walked north together along the seam of the worlds, the beggar-god and the steward, and behind them the shrine-lights of the free cities burned steady and small and brave, and ahead of them the dark leaned in to listen.
