## 22. The Thorn Blooms

The road to the hollow had been walked by a girl with blistered feet and a stolen thing in her satchel, and every mile of it she had rehearsed what she would say.

Her name was Wren Halloway, and she was fourteen years old, and she had been running for nine days. The satchel held three apples gone soft at one end, a wool cloak that smelled of someone else's hearth-smoke, and a charm-book bound in grey leather with her mistress's sigil pressed into the cover — a spiral like an unfurling fern, stamped in silver that had begun to flake. She had not stolen it, exactly. That was what she told herself, over and over, in time with her footsteps. *I did not steal it. I took what was already mine.* Mistress Corvane had said a hundred times that an apprentice's first working belonged to the apprentice, that the craft passed hand to hand like water and no one could own it — but Mistress Corvane had also said *you are not ready*, and *put it back*, and *Wren, if you attempt this rite before your training is done you will do damage no one can mend*, and Wren had heard only the part that sounded like a door closing.

So she had opened the door herself, in the dark, and taken the book, and left a note that said *forgive me* in handwriting that shook.

Nine days. Her boots had given up pretending they were boots somewhere outside Ashfield, where the shrines along the roadside stood freshly garlanded and an old woman had pressed bread into her hands without asking anything except *are you going to the hollow, child?* Everyone knew about the hollow now. Everyone knew a witch lived there who had once been a runaway too, though the stories disagreed on everything else. In one version she had died at the Battle of the Hollow and risen again crowned in thorn-blossoms. In another she was seven feet tall and made of smoke. In a third — the one the shrine-keepers told, quietly, when children asked — she was just a woman, middle-aged, with a scar across her palm shaped like a fern uncurling, who came down twice a year to walk among the villages and listen to prayers as if listening were the whole of the miracle.

Wren believed the third version, because it was the only one that frightened her properly. A woman made of smoke might turn her away without cruelty. A woman might look at her — at the book, at the lie she'd have to tell or the truth she couldn't bear to — and see exactly what she was.

The last mile was all hawthorn. It grew wild along the old cart-track, white blossom heaped on every branch like late snow, and the smell of it was so thick and sweet that Wren's eyes stung, or perhaps they were already stinging. Bees worked the flowers in a low continuous hum, a sound like the road itself breathing. And ahead, where the track bent between two hills and the trees closed overhead into a green tunnel, she saw the wall.

She had imagined thorns. Everyone imagined thorns; the stories were full of them, the great bramble rampart that had grown overnight from an old woman's grief, higher than a house, impassable for twenty years until the day it wasn't. But no story had prepared her for what the wall had become. The thorns were still there — she could see them beneath, dark wood and long pale spines as thick as her finger — but they bloomed now, extravagantly, recklessly, hawthorn and wild rose and something white-flowered she had no name for, growing through each other and over each other until the barrier looked less like a fortress than a hedge in some god's garden, soft-edged and humming and utterly alive. Petals came loose in the breeze and drifted across the path, settling on her shoulders, catching in her hair. One landed on the satchel strap, right beside the flaking silver spiral, and lay there like a small deliberate blessing.

The gate was simply there. No door, no arch — just a place where the wall parted, a gap barely wide enough for one person, floored with fallen blossom. Beyond it she could see a garden going softly wild, a cottage with a turf roof and smoke rising from its chimney, beehives, a vegetable bed, laundry on a line. An ordinary life, arranged inside a miracle.

Wren stopped at the threshold. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

She had planned to lie. She had planned it the whole way: *my mistress died, my mistress sent me, I'm seeking sanctuary, I don't know how the book got in my bag.* But standing here, with petals in her hair and the bees humming and the whole flowering wall leaning over her like something that had been waiting, the lies curdled in her mouth. She thought of Mistress Corvane's face when she'd found the empty shelf — not rage, Wren realized now, not even anger. Fear. The same fear that had made her say *you are not ready* a hundred times, the fear of a woman who had seen what unfinished magic did to the person who loosed it.

*I did not steal it,* Wren told herself one more time, and heard, finally, how thin it sounded.

From somewhere beyond the gate came the clink of a bucket, the creak of a well-handle. Footsteps on packed earth. Then a voice — a woman's voice, unhurried, a little rough, the voice of someone who talked to plants and gods and had lost the habit of performing for people:

"You can come in, if you're coming. The bees won't bother you, but standing in the gateway makes them nervous."

Wren's whole body went hot and cold at once. She stepped through.

* * *

Bram Amberdale had felt the girl coming since Ashfield.

It was not a dramatic knowing. After twenty years as steward — after learning to hold the boundary the way other people held a breath — awareness had become simply the texture of her days. She felt prayers the way she felt weather: pressure changes, distant storms, small clearings. Most prayers were small. *Let the fever break. Let him write back. Let the rain hold off until the hay is in.* She answered what she could, which was most things, because answering had turned out to be mostly a matter of attention — of being willing to stand at the boundary and lean the world, gently, in the direction of mercy. Vesh called it *the work*. Tamsin, in the years she'd had left, had called it *what we should have been doing all along*.

But a runaway she felt differently. A runaway rang through the covenant like a cracked bell. She had felt this one nine days ago — a small discordant note out of the west, guilt and terror and a scrap of half-trained power flickering like a badly trimmed lamp — and she had known immediately, the way you know a song from its first bar, what it was. What *she* had been.

She had let the girl walk the whole way. That had cost her something, those nine days. Every instinct screamed to go out and meet her, spare her the blisters and the fear and the nights under hedges. But Bram remembered her own road, and understood at last — understanding that had taken Tamsin's death and a war and the slow grace of years to arrive — that some distances had to be walked. You could not carry someone to a threshold. You could only keep the gate open and trust them to reach it.

So she had drawn water and baked bread and waited, and now here the girl was, standing in her garden with hawthorn petals in her tangled hair and a grey charm-book clutched against her chest like a shield, looking around at the beehives and the bean rows with the expression of someone who has expected a palace and found a kitchen.

"You're her," the girl said. "You're really—" She stopped, swallowed. Up close Bram could see the whole disaster of her: chapped lips, eyes swollen from crying or wind or both, a scrape healing crookedly on one shin, and beneath all of it a raw bright talent that flickered off her like heat-shimmer. Untrained. Unguarded. The old gods' power moved through her the way water moves through a cracked jug, wasting itself everywhere, hurting her a little with every step.

"I'm Bram," she said. "You'll want to put the satchel down. Your shoulders have been up around your ears since the second hill."

The girl flushed and didn't put the satchel down. Of course she didn't. Bram remembered the weight of a stolen stone and how impossible it had seemed to set it down anywhere, ever, for any reason.

"Are you hungry?"

That undid her more than anything else could have. Wren Halloway's face crumpled — she fought it, jaw tight, blinking hard at the bean rows — and lost. "I— yes. I'm sorry. I practiced what I was going to say, the whole way, and now I can't remember any of it."

"Don't say any of it yet." Bram picked up the water bucket and nodded toward the cottage. "Eat first. Confessions keep. Bread doesn't."

Inside, the girl sat at the table like someone sitting on a cliff edge, back straight, hands folded, watching Bram cut bread and slice cheese and set out honey with the wary attention of a creature deciding whether the trap was sprung. Bram let the silence be comfortable. She ladled soup from the pot that never quite emptied — a small covenant courtesy Vesh had insisted on, *if you must be divine, at least be useful at lunch* — and pushed the bowl across, and watched the girl eat like eating was a battle she was winning.

Halfway through the bowl, the girl's hands slowed. She stared into the soup. When she spoke, her voice had gone small.

"I took my mistress's book." The words came out flat, rehearsed after all, worn smooth by nine days of repetition. "Not stolen. Taken. She said the first working belongs to the apprentice, she *said* that, but she wouldn't teach me the rite, she kept saying I wasn't ready, and I found it in her strongbox and I—" Her breath hitched. "There was a village past Ashfield. A little one. There was a child sick with lung-fever and everyone praying at the shrine and nothing happening, and I thought — I thought if I did the rite, the real rite, the one in the book, I could save her. I could *be* —" She gestured helplessly at the cottage, the wall, the whole flowering legend of the hollow. "I could be like you."

Bram's scarred palm tingled. She kept her hands still around her cup.

"What happened to the child?"

"She lived." The girl's voice cracked clean through. "But not because of me. Because I did the rite wrong. I pulled power I didn't know how to shape and it went *everywhere* — it burned the shrine, it scorched the well-house, it knocked the fever-child's grandmother off her feet, and the fever broke anyway because the *shrine-keeper* had sense enough to do the ordinary laying-on-of-hands while I was lying in the churchyard seeing sounds. And my mistress came for me the next morning, and she looked at me and she said—" The girl stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "She said she was glad I was alive. That was worse than if she'd been angry. So I ran. I've been running ever since, and I told myself the whole way here that you'd understand, because you ran too, everyone says you ran, and—"

"Wren." Bram said the name quietly — the girl had offered it at the gate, in a strangled burst of manners — and waited until the hands came down and the red-rimmed eyes found hers. "Look at my hand."

She turned her palm up on the table. The scar sat there in the lamplight, the fern-spiral the stone had burned into her flesh thirty years ago, silver-pale now, old as an answered question.

"A man gave me a stone once," Bram said. "A god wearing a beggar's coat. He told me it was mine by right, and he was telling the truth, and it nearly destroyed me anyway. Right and ready are different words, child. Your mistress wasn't keeping something from you. She was keeping something *for* you." She closed her fist. "I know exactly how much that answer helps right now. It helped me about as much, at your age. But you'll want it later, so I'm saying it."

The girl was crying again, silently, soup and salt. Bram rose, fetched the good towel — the soft one, the one Tamsin had woven, which she still used for exactly these occasions — and put it in the girl's hands, and then, because some things needed doing and not explaining, she held out her other hand, palm up, and waited.

Wren Halloway looked at it for a long moment. Then she took it.

Her grip was small and fierce and shaking. Bram held on. Outside, the bees hummed in the blossoming thorns, and the evening light came gold through the window, and somewhere at the edge of the garden — where the air always shimmered very slightly, where the boundary ran like a river under the grass — something vast and patient and fond leaned close to watch.

"You'll stay tonight," Bram said. It was not quite a question. "Tomorrow we'll talk about whether you write to your mistress, or travel back to her, or send word and wait for her to come to you — and she will come, child, mark me, mistresses always come. And after that, if you still want it, we'll talk about lessons. Real ones. Slow ones." She smiled, and felt the smile reach all the way down to the scar. "No one runs through my gate twice. Not because I lock it. Because I make sure there's nothing left out there worth running *to* instead of *from*."

The girl laughed — wet, broken, astonished, the first true sound she'd made since the gate. "Everyone said you'd be taller," she managed.

"Everyone says that." Bram squeezed her hand once and let go, and went to bank the fire, and began, without hurry, to set a second place at the table for morning.

Beyond the window the thorns stood heavy with bloom, white and pink against the dusk, petals letting go and drifting down like slow snow onto a path that had been walked by a runaway once, long ago, and was walked again now, and would be walked again after — and the gate stood open, and the light in the cottage burned steady, and no one, ever again, would stand at that threshold and find it closed.
