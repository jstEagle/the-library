## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Gilded Scale guild kept its books in a room with no windows, which Edmund had always considered a small mercy. Windows invited looking, and looking invited questions, and questions were the one commodity Edmund Galloway had spent ten years refusing to trade.

So: no windows. Just lamplight, the scratch of a good nib on good paper, and the ledger open before them—*Volume Fourteen, Impossible Acquisitions*, bound in green leather gone soft at the corners from handling. Edmund's handling. Nobody else's. That was rather the point.

*Item 331,* Edmund wrote, in the small, precise hand that had won them this job and kept them safely beneath everyone's notice. *One echo, bottled. Seller: a widow of Tanner's Row who could not bear to keep hearing her late husband's laugh in the stairwell. Buyer: anonymous, paid triple asking, requested delivery by night. Contents verified: approximately eleven seconds of laughter, warm, male, slightly wheezing. Note: bottle hums when held near anyone grieving. Recommend storage away from the mourning section.*

Edmund paused, read it back, and allowed themselves the smallest private smile. *Recommend storage away from the mourning section.* As if the guild had a mourning section. As if they didn't simply shelve grief between the bottled sunsets and the jars of last words.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, through three floors of stone and timber, the beasts began their nightly argument.

Most people couldn't hear it. Most people heard nothing at all from the menagerie level except the ordinary complaints of caged animals—the pacing, the rattling of bars, the occasional shriek that sent apprentices running with buckets of water for reasons nobody could explain. The guild's buyers came up from the trading floor, paid their coin, collected their crates, and went home to Vaelmoor's better districts telling themselves that the creatures were sleeping.

Edmund heard everything.

They heard the marsh-wren in Cage Nine, who had been singing the same four notes since sundown, over and over, not because wrens sing but because she was counting. *Four notes, four bars of dark, four notes again.* She'd been caught in a snare meant for something larger, sold as a curiosity because her song made listeners feel watched. She was right to make them feel watched. She was watching. She was counting the hours until someone remembered she existed.

They heard the old boar in the far pen, whose name—his real name, the one his mother had grunted into his ear when he was pink and blind—was a long rolling sound like stones in a riverbed. He dreamed it aloud every night. *Stones-in-the-riverbed, stones-in-the-riverbed.* And every morning he woke and remembered that no one here called him anything but Lot 44, and the dream-name wore a little thinner, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands.

And they heard the fox.

The fox had arrived in autumn, traded by a fur-trapper who claimed she was worthless because she wouldn't stop staring at him. She stared at Edmund too, through the first time they'd come down with the feed inventory, and she had said—plainly, in the crisp voice foxes use when they think they're being reasonable—*You hear me.*

It hadn't been a question. Foxes don't waste questions.

*I hear you,* Edmund had answered, silently, the way one does, and then had stood very still in the straw-smelling dark and understood that ten years of careful, meticulous invisibility had just developed a crack in it shaped exactly like a red-eyed creature in a cage.

That had been four months ago. Since then, Edmund had found excuses to pass the menagerie level on most evenings. Feed inventories. Cage inspections. A suspiciously thorough audit of straw expenditures. And every evening the fox said some version of the same thing:

*You hear me. When do we leave?*

And every evening Edmund said some version of the same lie:

*Soon.*

The nib scratched on. Edmund made themselves write.

*Item 332. One moth, live, captive-bred. Seller claims it remembers futures. Verification: inconclusive but unsettling—the specimen has twice landed on documents prior to any decision being made regarding those documents, including once upon a blank contract three days before said contract was signed. Buyer: Master Corvane, personal acquisition, funds drawn from the discretionary account. Note: Corvane has asked that this entry be kept "discreet." All entries are discreet. I am discreet. I am so discreet I am practically furniture.*

There it was again, that bitter little taste under the humor. Edmund set down the nib and pressed their fingertips against their eyes until colors bloomed.

Furniture. That was the role. That was the masterpiece they'd been performing since they were nineteen years old and had learned, in the worst classroom imaginable, what happened to people whose gifts became known. They'd been young and stupid and trusting once; they'd told someone—a friend, or what they'd thought was a friend—that they could hear what animals said. The friend had laughed. Then the friend had told others. Then others had looked at Edmund differently, the way people look at a well that might be poisoned, and Edmund had learned the lesson that governed their whole life now: *the world does not punish the gift. It punishes the gift-bearer for being interesting.*

So Edmund had become uninteresting. Spectacularly, devotedly uninteresting. The quiet bookkeeper with ink-stained fingers and a forgettable face and opinions about paper stock. Who noticed things, yes—but only the things a bookkeeper notices. Prices. Dates. Signatures. Never the way Guildmaster Corvane's hooded visitors never seemed to blink. Never the way the ledgers' numbers sometimes didn't add up in ways that felt less like error and more like intention. Never, ever, the impossible thing living behind their own ribs, straining against its cage the same way the fox strained against hers.

Two cages, Edmund thought. One of straw and iron, one of habit and fear. At least hers had bars you could see.

From above, the boar's dream-name rolled down through the floors like distant thunder. *Stones-in-the-riverbed.* And underneath it, thin and persistent, the wren's four notes. And underneath that, fainter than all, the fox, awake as always, murmuring to herself in the dark—not begging tonight, just talking, the way lonely creatures talk to keep the silence from settling in their bones. Reciting, Edmund realized with a jolt, the smells of the forest. Pine. Wet earth. Rabbit. Rain coming from the west. An inventory of home, kept perfectly, recited nightly, in case she should ever need to prove where she belonged.

Edmund's throat went tight.

They knew that trick. They used it themselves, in the privacy of their own skull, more often than they'd admit. *My name is Edmund Galloway. My mother's hands smelled of bread. I was born in a house with a blue door in Lowmarsh before the floods took it. I can hear what beasts say. I can hear what beasts say. I can hear what beasts say.* An inventory of the self, kept against the day the self might be needed.

Because that was the truth under all the careful bookkeeping, wasn't it? Not that Edmund hid the gift out of mere cowardice, though there was plenty of cowardice, honestly accounted for. It was that the gift was *them*. Every secret thing they were lived in that one strange sense—and to expose it would be to hand the world the key to the only room they'd ever furnished. People who heard beasts were useful. Useful people got owned. And Edmund had seen what happened to owned people; they ended up in ledgers too, eventually. Entries. Assets. *Lot* numbers.

Better to be furniture. Better to be invisible. Better to ache quietly, every single night, than to risk—

Above, the wren stopped counting.

Edmund's head came up.

It was such a small thing. A bird stopping its song. But the wren had counted without pause for eleven nights—Edmund knew because Edmund had listened to all eleven, lying awake in their narrow bed above the counting room, cataloging her persistence in the mental ledger where they kept everything that mattered. And now: nothing. Not sleep-silence, either. Sleep had a texture, a soft breathing quality. This was different. This was the silence of an animal listening to something else.

Then the boar stopped dreaming aloud.

Then the fox.

Then—Edmund rose slowly, chair scraping, heart beginning a slow heavy knock against their ribs—then everything. Every cage. Every pen. Every creature in the guild's menagerie and, if Edmund's straining senses were right, beyond it, out past the walls, out across the rooftops and the tanneries and the frozen gardens of Vaelmoor—all of it dropping into silence at once, like a great hand laid flat over the city's mouth.

The cats in the alley. The pigeons on the eaves. Something vast and unseen holding its breath in the border woods ten miles east.

Edmund stood alone in the windowless room with the lamp burning steadily and the impossible ledger open to Item 332, and listened to the first true silence they had ever heard, and understood—with the cold, certain clarity of a bookkeeper whose columns refuse to balance—that something had just entered the accounts that did not belong there.

In the new quiet, very faintly, from the shelf behind them, the bottled echo began to laugh.

* * *

Edmund did not run. Running attracted attention, and attention was death, and ten years of discipline did not dissolve in a single terrible moment even if every hair on their body was standing at attention. They walked. Up the back stairs, lamp held steady, face arranged into the mild blankness of a bookkeeper fetching a second pot of ink, while inside them a voice screamed *something is wrong, something is wrong, something is wrong* in perfect time with their footsteps.

The menagerie level was cold. Colder than it should have been, though the braziers still burned. The animals stood motionless in their cages—not asleep, not calm, but *attentive*, every eye turned toward the eastern wall, toward the direction of the border wards that ringed the city like an old dog circling a flock.

Only the fox moved. She came to the front of her cage and pressed her nose to the bars, and her eyes in the lamplight were bright and urgent and afraid.

*You hear it too,* she said. It wasn't a question. Foxes don't waste questions. *Whatever it is. Out there. You hear it the way I smell it.*

"I don't know what—" Edmund began aloud, then caught themself, glancing at the empty corridor. They lowered their voice to almost nothing. "I don't know what you mean."

*Liar,* said the fox, without malice. *You're the best liar I've met. You lie so well you've forgotten which parts are false. But your hands are shaking, bookkeeper. And the birds stopped singing. And the old stone teeth around this city—the wards, the humans call them—they've gone quiet as dead bones. Ask yourself why the beasts went silent all at once. We went silent because for the first time in our lives, we couldn't hear ourselves think over the sound of something listening back.*

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "Listening."

*Listening,* the fox agreed. *Something is reading the city, little liar. Reading it the way you read your ledgers. Line by line. Name by name.* She tilted her head. *And I would very much like to not be in a cage when it finishes reading yours.*

Somewhere far off—so far it might have been imagination, might have been wind, might have been the first hairline crack spreading through a wall that had held for centuries—Edmund heard the wards hum.

Wrong.

Like old bones creaking under a weight they were never meant to carry.

Edmund stood in the freezing dark among a hundred silent beasts, holding a lamp that suddenly seemed very small, and thought of Item 331, the widow's bottled laughter, humming near the grieving. Thought of how the whole city of Vaelmoor was about to learn what it was grieving for.

"Tomorrow," they whispered to the fox, and hated how much it sounded like *soon*.

The fox stared at them a long moment. Then she settled back on her haunches, wrapped her tail over her feet, and delivered the last thing Edmund would hear any beast say for a very long time:

*Then count fast, bookkeeper. Whatever's coming—it's already balancing the books.*
