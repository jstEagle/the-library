## 2. Wards Like Old Bones

The trouble with a gift you're hiding, Edmund had learned over ten years of careful, deliberate nothing, is that it never lets you sleep in.

Most people's mornings began with bells, or gulls, or the groan of the Gilded Scale's loading cranes. Edmund's began with the marmalade cat who lived behind the tannery and considered herself owed an audience before dawn. She didn't want anything, particularly. She wanted to be heard. *The gray one with the soft hands,* she'd told the alley sparrows, who told the pigeons, who told anyone with ears like Edmund's, *the gray one listens. The gray one knows I was a temple cat once. Before the temple burned. Before everything.*

Edmund had never asked how a temple burned down. Some mornings it was all they could do not to ask.

So when Edmund came down the guildhouse stairs on the third morning of autumn, ledger under one arm, tea going cold in the other hand, and heard *nothing* — truly nothing, no sparrows arguing over the gutters, no rats conducting their usual committee meeting beneath the floorboards, no cat demanding her due — they stopped on the fourth step from the bottom and did not move for a long moment.

Silence, for a beast-speaker, is not empty. It is a room with all the furniture removed. You keep reaching for things that aren't there.

"Hello?" Edmund said softly, to no one, which was ridiculous, because if anything were there to answer, they wouldn't have needed to say hello at all.

Nothing answered. Not even the mice, who would ordinarily have answered *anything*, including questions nobody asked them, including questions asked by people who couldn't hear mice at all — the mice held opinions about everyone in Vaelmoor and shared them freely among themselves, and Edmund had spent years pretending not to know that the baker's second wife was planning to leave him by midwinter.

Edmund set down the tea. That was the first sign of how wrong things were: Edmund Galloway did not set down tea. Tea was armor. Tea was the small warm prop that made a person look busy and unremarkable, and an unremarkable person holding tea could stand in any doorway in Vaelmoor without being remembered by nightfall.

They walked to the guildhouse door and out into the yard, where the morning fog lay over the pens like a sheet pulled up to the chin.

The Gilded Scale kept beasts the way other guilds kept coin: as inventory, as leverage, as collateral. There were the draft oxen for the caravans, the hawks for the message trade, the exotic lots in the long stone barn — a snow lynx from the northern passes, a marsh-wyrm no bigger than a hound whose scales fetched more per ounce than silver, a pair of griffon chicks still downy and furious about it. And there were the horses, forty-odd of them, and this was the thing that stopped Edmund's breath in their chest:

The horses were standing perfectly still.

Not sleeping. Not spooked. Standing in their stalls and in the paddock with their heads up and their ears forward, every one of them facing north, as if listening to something on the far side of the hills. And silent. Horses are never silent, not inside; horses mutter constantly, complaints and observations and the low continuous gossip of herd-life, a sound like a river running under the world. Edmund had grown up on that river. It was the sound of home, though no one but Edmund knew it.

Now the river had stopped.

"Hallo," Edmund said to the nearest mare, a dapple named — well, she called herself *Longgrass* after a meadow she'd been foaled in, and Edmund had privately thought for ten years that it was the loveliest name they'd ever heard. "Longgrass. What is it? What are you looking at?"

Longgrass's ear flicked toward them. Her dark eye rolled, found Edmund, and held — and what Edmund saw in it made the cold of the morning feel like a summer breeze. It was fear. Not the bright fear of a snake in the grass or a whip-hand raised, but the old, deep, patient fear of an animal that has smelled something it cannot fight and cannot run from.

*Quiet,* Longgrass said. Her voice in Edmund's head was thin as thread. *Everyone's quiet. Everyone everywhere. The little ones went first, then the birds, then us. Something told us all to be quiet at once.*

"Told you? Who told you?"

*I don't know.* Her nostrils flared. *It wasn't a voice. It was like — like when the far-off thunder makes your bones hum before your ears hear it. Only it said be quiet. And we are quieting.*

"How far?" Edmund asked, though their hands had already gone cold around the stall rail. "How far does it reach?"

*As far as the ground goes,* said Longgrass simply. *Maybe farther. Maybe all the way to where the stars sit.*

Edmund stood in the fog with forty silent horses staring north, and understood two things with perfect clarity. The first was that whatever this was, it was not natural, and it was not local, and it was not going away. The second was that there was no one in Vaelmoor they could tell — not one living soul — without explaining *how* they knew, and that explanation had cost better people than Edmund everything they had.

A beast-speaker. In a city built on the buying and selling of beasts. It was rather like being a counterfeiter employed at the mint: useful knowledge, catastrophic discovery. The Gilded Scale paid its beast-speakers — the three licensed ones upstairs, with their guild rings and their official seals — very well indeed, and then worked them until their gifts went gray and dull as old pewter, because a gift that's used daily becomes a tool, and tools can be owned. An *unlicensed* speaker was worse than useless to Corvane. Unlicensed speakers were loose coin. Loose coin got melted down.

So Edmund did what Edmund always did. They picked up their tea, straightened their collar, and went to work looking exactly as worried as any bookkeeper would be, and no more.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane received the day's reports in his office above the counting floor, a room designed to make visitors feel like sums that hadn't yet come out right. It was all dark wood and darker ledgers, and Corvane himself sat behind the desk like the ledger everything else was written in: tall, dry, precise, with a face you forgot while you were still looking at it.

"The northern caravan's late," he said, without looking up. "Third time this season."

"There's fog on the pass," Edmund offered. "And—" They hesitated. This was the moment. This was the door, standing open, and Edmund had been rehearsing a version of this conversation since the stairs. "Guildmaster, the stock is unsettled this morning. All of it. The horses won't eat, the hawks won't fly, the lynx hasn't moved since dawn. It might be worth sending someone to check the border wards. If the wards are humming wrong, animals sense it first. Every drover knows that."

Corvane looked up. He had gray eyes, the exact color of ledger paper, and Edmund had spent a decade making sure those eyes slid off them the way water slides off wax.

"Every drover knows that," Corvane repeated mildly. "Do you now spend your evenings with drovers, Galloway?"

"I spend my evenings with invoices. Invoices mention everything."

Something moved behind the gray eyes — not suspicion, quite. Calculation. Edmund watched him decide, in real time, that a bookkeeper noticing restless stock was worth less attention than the cost of noticing it.

"The wards are the Warden-Corps' concern," he said. "And the Corps says the wards are fine. Old bones hum. That's all it is. Old bones hum when the weather turns." He returned to his papers. "Feed the stock double. Frightened animals burn through feed. See to it."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

"And Galloway." Edmund paused at the door. Corvane didn't look up. "You have a good instinct for numbers. Keep it applied to numbers."

It was, Edmund reflected on the way back down, almost impressive — the way the man could turn even a warning into a reprimand. *Old bones hum.* Except Edmund had grown up three miles from the border stones, and their grandmother had been ward-adjacent in the way rural grandmothers are, full of charms and rules, and one rule Edmund had absorbed with their porridge: *when the bones hum loud enough that the beasts go quiet, you don't feed them double. You count your family and get indoors.*

But Corvane was the guildmaster, and Edmund was a ledger-clerk with a secret that would fit in a thimble and burn down a city if spilled. So Edmund fed the stock double, and wrote it all down in neat columns, and hated every figure.

* * *

It was near midnight when Edmund came back down for the forgotten invoice-case — they'd left it in the reading room, and Master Ferren wanted the Marsh Trade tallies at dawn, and dawn waited for no clerk — and that was when Edmund heard the voices.

Two of them, in Corvane's office. One was Corvane's, dry and unhurried, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. The other—

The other was wrong.

Edmund had heard many voices in ten years of listening: beast-voices, bird-voices, the great slow thoughts of oxen, the quick silver chatter of swallows. Every voice was *one* voice, even when many creatures spoke together. But the voice coming through Corvane's door sounded like several voices wearing one mouth — pitched a half-tone apart from itself, agreeing with itself a beat too late, the way a crowd murmurs in a cathedral. It made the fine hairs on Edmund's arms stand up and lie back down again, twice.

"—the shipment is satisfactory," the many-voice was saying. "The quality improves yearly. You have been a faithful supplier."

"I supply what's contracted," Corvane said. "No more. The arrangement holds so long as the arrangement holds."

"Naturally. Though we note the market shifts. The northern demand grows. Our principal grows... hungry." A sound followed that might have been laughter, if laughter were something practiced by something that had read about it. "There will be need of a larger consignment soon. Much larger. Perhaps you have begun preparing inventory?"

"I prepare nothing I haven't agreed to prepare."

"A pity. Preparation is rewarded." Papers rustled. "Your wards hum, Guildmaster. Do your people ask questions?"

"My people ask about wages."

"Mm. Questions are unprofitable. We do hope you continue to find them so." A pause, and then the many-voice said something else, softer, and though Edmund caught only fragments through the heavy door, the fragments were enough to turn their blood to well-water:

"...ten miles... all of them, at once... such obedience..."

Then silence, the ordinary kind, and footsteps — only one set, Corvane's, measured and dry — and Edmund was moving, invoice-case forgotten, heart hammering a rhythm no ledger had a column for.

They made it around the corner of the reading-room stacks before the office door opened. Light spilled across the counting floor. Edmund pressed against the cold stone of the shelving, among the records of a decade of honest trades, and did not breathe.

Footsteps crossed the floor below. Paused.

Somewhere in the dark of the guildhouse, a rat — the first rat to speak all day, its voice shaky and small — whispered to the others in the walls: *it's still here. The quiet-thing. It's standing in the yard. It's looking at our house.*

And beneath the rat's whisper, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the front doors: a sound like several voices breathing, just once, in unison.

Then the doors opened and closed, and the fog outside swallowed whatever walked into it, and the horses in the paddock — Edmund felt it rather than heard it, a shiver through the floorboards — finally turned their heads away from the north.

Edmund stayed among the shelves a long while, in the dark, holding a case of invoices they no longer cared about, listening to the ordinary sounds of the guildhouse resume around them: the settling timbers, the wind in the eaves, the distant, blessed return of sparrows muttering in the gutter about the weather.

Everything sounded normal. Everything sounded exactly as it should.

That was the worst part. Because Edmund had heard the buyer's voice, and the buyer's voice had sounded like a crowd, and crowds — Edmund knew this from the ledgers, from the strange entries they catalogued late at night, the bottled echoes and the moth that remembered futures and all the impossible inventory the Gilded Scale pretended was metaphor — crowds were made of individuals.

And every individual in that crowd had sounded, underneath, a little bit hungry.

Upstairs, dawn was three hours off. Edmund climbed toward it slowly, and on the landing they stopped, and looked north through the window at the hills and the invisible line of the border wards, old bones humming their old song in the dark.

Humming, tonight, like something tuning itself to break.
