# Chapter Three — The Night Without Stars

The silence came first.

Edmund had spent ten years learning the city's noises the way other people learned faces: the Gilded Scale's night-bell, the groan of the ward-stones along Vaelmoor's border like old men settling into chairs, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the murmur of beasts. Dogs dreaming in kennels. Rats arguing in the granaries. The guild's menagerie muttering its endless grievances. Edmund did not listen to it—listening was dangerous—but it was there anyway, a tide against the walls of their skull, and they had long since stopped being able to tell whether it was comfort or captivity.

At half past midnight, the tide went out.

Every beast within the walls stopped speaking at once. Not quieted—*stopped*, the way a heart stops, all at once and everywhere, and the absence rang louder than any sound Edmund had ever heard. They sat up at the ledger desk so fast the ink bottle went over, black spreading across the page of impossible trades like a wound.

"No," Edmund said softly, to no one, to themselves. "No, no—"

Then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*—every one of them, in the same instant, as if a hand had closed over the whole sky and snuffed it like a candle between wet fingers. The darkness that followed was not ordinary dark. It was total, and it was *interested*. Edmund felt it press against the window glass the way a face presses to a window from outside.

They were moving before thought caught up—ten years of habit firing like a trap springing. Lamp shuttered. Ledger locked. Hands steady, face blank, because if the world was ending then it would end with Edmund Galloway looking exactly as unremarkable as always. That was the covenant they'd made with themselves: survive by being no one.

The wards broke before they reached the courtyard.

It sounded like glass, but wrong—a shattering that kept going, that spread outward instead of falling inward, each note of breaking higher and thinner than the last until it wasn't sound anymore but something behind the ears, behind the eyes. Through the window Edmund saw the ward-stones along the guild wall split like rotten teeth, and the light that had lived inside them for three hundred years spilled out onto the cobbles and *ran away*, pooling and sliding off toward the north like water finding a drain.

North. Toward the capital. Toward the king.

People were screaming now, spilling into the streets with lamps they didn't need—the dark ate lamplight too, Edmund noticed distantly, or drank it, or simply made it irrelevant. Guildmaster Corvane's voice cut through the panic from the hall, bellowing orders about inventory and locks, and Edmund understood with a cold, clarifying horror that Corvane was not surprised. Corvane was counting.

But none of that mattered, because the stablehorse spoke.

Edmund was crossing the yard when it happened. Old Bramble, the gray gelding who had carried guild freight for nineteen years and never once said a word to anyone, thrust his great head over the stable door, and his voice—when it came—was not a horse's voice at all. It was layered, resonant, a voice like a chord played on an instrument built for one string.

"*Edmund Ashvale Galloway,*" said the horse. "*Third of that name. Born under a roof that burned. Keeper of the ledger of impossible things. Hearer.*"

The name went through Edmund like a blade finding the gap in armor.

No living soul knew it. Their mother had died with it; their father had never learned it; the guild records held only *E. Galloway, junior clerk*. Edmund had not spoken it aloud since childhood, had half persuaded themselves they'd invented it, a private thing kept in the same locked room as everything else. And now a cart horse stood in the dark saying it back to them in a voice with too many harmonics, a voice that belonged to something wearing the horse the way a hand wears a glove.

"Bramble." Edmund's own voice came out small and cracked. "Bramble, what is—"

"*It reads the city now,*" the horse said, and the harmonics thinned, and underneath them Edmund heard—barely, terribly—the old gray gelding's actual voice, frightened, exhausted, a horse's voice saying a horse's words: "*it asked me your name and I couldn't stop my mouth, I'm sorry, little hearer, I'm sorry, it took it out of me like pulling a splinter—*"

And then Bramble's head drooped, and he was only a horse again, breathing hard in the dark, and Edmund stood alone in the courtyard with their true name loose in the world like blood in water.

Something knew them now. Something that had been reading the city the way Edmund read ledgers—line by line, entry by entry—and had just recorded them in whatever book such things kept.

Around them Vaelmoor screamed and stumbled and lit useless fires. Above them the sky was a black lid. And Edmund Galloway, invisible for ten years, felt the first crack run through their hiding place—not loud, not yet, but deep, structural, the kind of crack that does not close.

They pressed their back to the stable wall, put a shaking hand over their mouth, and did the only thing left that felt like theirs.

They listened.

And far to the north, past the broken wards, past the dead villages whose names would soon be missing, something vast and patient turned a page.
