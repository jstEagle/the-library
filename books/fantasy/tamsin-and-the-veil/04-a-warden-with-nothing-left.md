## 4. A Warden With Nothing Left

The shrine at the bottom of Cooper's Gutter had been dedicated to some minor god of drainage, back when Vaelmoor still bothered to honor its small gods. Now it was a stone alcove with a chipped basin, a dead candle, and room enough for one disgraced warden to sit and drink herself into a philosophical state.

Sera Voss had achieved philosophy around noon. By dusk she was working on transcendence.

"To the wards," she told the basin, raising her flask. "May they rest in pieces."

The flask was nearly empty, which was the only reason she heard the footsteps. Sober Sera would have heard them three streets off; drunk Sera heard them only when they stopped, which meant whoever it was had been very quiet or very lucky, and nobody in Vaelmoor got lucky anymore. Not since the sky had gone black as spilled ink two nights ago. Not since the wards—the great humming bones of the border, the thing she'd spent eleven years tending—had shattered like a dropped plate.

Not since everyone in the city had decided it was her fault.

She didn't turn around. Turning around took effort, and effort was for people who hadn't been stripped of their badge, their post, and their name from the duty rolls all in one afternoon.

"If you're a clerk," she said, "the answer is no comment. If you're a thief, I've already been robbed by the city itself, so you're late. And if you're a ghost—" she squinted up at the dark sky where stars should have been, "—you'll have to take a number. The whole city's haunted lately."

"It's not a ghost," said the stranger.

That voice. Young, careful, pitched low the way people pitch their voices when they've spent years practicing not being noticed. Sera knew that particular sound. She'd worn it herself once, back before she'd learned that standing tall just gave them a bigger target.

She turned anyway.

The stranger stood at the mouth of the alcove, wrapped in a guild clerk's gray coat that fit badly, hood up against a rain that hadn't fallen yet but felt like it was thinking about it. A ledger satchel hung at one hip. Their face stayed in shadow, but their hands were what caught Sera's attention: ink-stained fingers laced together so tightly the knuckles had gone white, like someone holding themselves shut.

"You're the warden," the stranger said. "Voss."

"Was." She toasted them with the last inch of the flask. "Now I'm the woman in the gutter shrine. We're a growing demographic."

"You're drunk."

"I'm *thorough*." She let the flask drop into the basin, where it rang out a small, mournful note. "There's a difference. Drunk is an accident. This took planning. Eleven years of planning, actually, though I didn't know that's what I was planning for." She waved a hand at the starless sky. "So. Clerk of the Gilded Scale. Come to buy something? The god of drainage takes coin, but he's never once delivered."

"I need ward-craft."

The words landed like cold water down the back of her collar. Sera laughed—a short, ugly sound she barely recognized as her own.

"Ward-craft. From me." She pushed herself up the wall, and the world tilted politely and settled. "Let me tell you how my expertise is regarded these days, clerk. There's a warrant with my name on it. Not my name—my *title*, because apparently even my name is too good for them now. 'The warden responsible.' Eleven years I walked those lines. I knew every anchor-stone by its crack, every hum by its mood. When the eastern line went sour in the spring, I flagged it. Do you know what Corvane's man told me? He told me the guild's instruments disagreed with me, and that perhaps I should consider whether eleven years of night work had affected my judgment." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "Then the sky went out, and suddenly my judgment is the only thing anyone wants to talk about. In the past tense."

The stranger hadn't moved. Hadn't flinched, either, which put them ahead of most listeners.

"The instruments were wrong," the stranger said quietly.

"Yes. They were."

"And you weren't."

"No. I wasn't." Sera blinked at them. Most people, offered her tragedy, performed sympathy and then changed the subject. This one just... agreed. Like it was a fact being entered into a ledger. "You're not here to arrest me, are you."

"Do I look like I could?"

She looked. Gray coat, ink stains, shoulders that suggested a great deal of hunching over desks and none whatsoever of swinging swords. "No. You look like a strong wind would file a complaint about you."

Something happened to the stranger's face then—there in the gloom, Sera almost missed it. The corner of a mouth twitching upward, quickly suppressed, like a mouse darting across a floor. It made them look, for half a heartbeat, like an actual person instead of a walking apology.

"My name's Edmund," they said. Then, immediately, as if the name had cost more than they'd expected: "That's—that's what people call me."

"*What people call you.*" Sera snorted. "And here I thought clerks were precise."

"We're precise about what matters."

*Interesting*, thought the sober little accountant in the back of Sera's skull, the one the gin usually couldn't reach. *Interesting indeed.*

* * *

They ended up walking, because sitting still felt too much like waiting to be found, and Edmund—walking with the quick, soft steps of someone used to going unnoticed—seemed to prefer motion. Sera led them up out of the gutter district through alleys she knew from her patrol days, and Edmund followed without complaint, which was another point in their favor. Guilty people argued about routes.

"The silence," Edmund said, somewhere around the tannery lane. "When did you first notice it?"

Sera stopped. "What silence?"

"The beasts." Edmund's hands had gone to their satchel strap again, gripping. "Two nights ago. Before the wards broke. Everything within—within earshot, everything feathered or furred, went quiet. All at once. Didn't they."

It wasn't a question, and that was the strange part. It was spoken like a confirmation request, like Edmund already knew the answer and needed it witnessed.

"How do you know that?" Sera asked slowly. "I filed a report about it. One report. Corvane's office buried it under a stack of tariff disputes. Nobody knows about the silence except me and whatever wrote the report."

Edmund was quiet for four full steps. Five. Six.

"I hear things," they said finally, to the cobblestones rather than to Sera. "Beasts. What they say. It's—it's called beast-speaking, and it's rare, and it's worth money to the wrong people, and so I don't mention it. Ever. To anyone. For ten years I've cataloged the guild's impossible inventory and pretended I can't hear the menagerie begging, and two nights ago every animal in this city went silent at once like something had pressed its hand over the whole border, and I am telling you this because you tracked that same silence with nothing but ward-sense and a grudge, and I think we might be the only two people in Vaelmoor who noticed the same wrong thing."

The alley was very still. Somewhere above, a raven sat on a drainpipe—not cawing, not shifting, just watching them with one flat eye, and Sera thought: *even the birds are listening now.*

"Well," she said at last. "That's the most words anyone's said to me since the sky went out."

"I'm sorry. That was—I shouldn't have—"

"Don't apologize." She started walking again, faster now, the gin burning off into something sharper and more useful. "Do you know what I did for eleven years? I listened to the wards. Everyone thinks ward-craft is stones and sigils and chanting. It isn't. It's listening. The wards hum, and when they're healthy they hum like a well-fed cat, and when they're sick they hum like a cat that's swallowed something it shouldn't have, and if you spend long enough with your ear against them you learn to tell the difference. I told Corvane the eastern line sounded *hungry*. His instruments said ninety-seven percent integrity. Ninety-seven percent!" She kicked a loose cobble and watched it skitter. "You know what I think? I think his instruments measured exactly what he paid them to measure."

"You think the guildmaster sabotaged the wards."

"I think the guildmaster is a man who has never once in his life paid full price for anything, and I think the wards failed at precisely the moment it was most profitable for them to fail." She glanced sideways. "Your turn. Why does a guild clerk who can talk to beasts need a disgraced warden? You've clearly been managing your secret fine for ten years. Whatever's coming—you could keep hiding. People like us are good at hiding. It's practically our trade."

Edmund stopped walking.

For a moment Sera thought they wouldn't answer. The hood tilted down; the ink-stained hands worked at each other. And then, in a voice so quiet she had to lean in:

"Because a horse said my name."

"...I'm sorry?"

"A stablehorse. During the panic, when the wards broke. It looked at me and it said my true name. My *whole* name. The one my mother whispered to me once, the day I was born, the one I've never spoken aloud, never written, never told a living soul." Edmund's breath came fast and shallow. "No one living knows it. No one *can* know it. And something in this city read it right out of the air, and spoke it through a horse's mouth, and Edmund—" they caught themselves, jaw tight, "—and *I* understood that hiding is over. Whatever ate the stars already has my scent. Hiding just means it finds me alone."

The raven on the drainpipe shifted its weight. Sera found she'd gone cold all over, and not from the gin wearing off.

"So," she said, when she trusted her voice. "Two liars walk into a gutter shrine."

"That's not how the joke goes."

"All my jokes go however I need them to. It's a warden privilege. Former privilege." She stuck out her hand, then reconsidered, then stuck it out again, firmly, because if she didn't do it now she'd find a reason not to. "Here's the bargain, Edmund-what-people-call-you. I know ward-craft—the real kind, the listening kind—and I know the old paths along the border, and I know how to move without being seen, which is apparently a skill we share. In exchange, you get me into the Gilded Scale's forbidden inventory. Every sealed crate, every vault shelf, every item Corvane catalogs as 'do not open, do not ask.' Because if there's anything left in this city that can mend a broken ward or fight whatever wore a hole in the sky, it's locked behind *that* door, and you're one of maybe three people with the key."

Edmund looked at her hand. Looked at her face. And Sera watched them make the calculation she recognized from her own mirror: *this person is a risk, this person is damaged goods, this person might be the only person.*

"What happens," Edmund asked, "when this goes wrong? Because things like this go wrong."

"Then we go wrong together, and honestly?" Sera grinned, and it felt rusty and enormous, like a gate opening after years shut. "I've spent two days drinking alone in a shrine to a drainage god. Going wrong in company sounds like a promotion."

Edmund shook her hand. Their grip was ink-stained and surprisingly steady.

"One condition," Edmund said. "You don't tell anyone about the gift. Not until you must. And when you must, you tell me first."

"Same goes for the warrant on my head. You're traveling with a fugitive, clerk. Congratulations."

"I've had worse company." A pause. "Actually, no, I haven't. But the beasts in the guild menagerie speak highly of you, for what it's worth. The magpie says you once fed it cheese during a winter inspection and lied to the inspector about it."

Sera barked a laugh that echoed down the alley. "That magpie remembers *cheese*? That was six years ago!"

"Beasts remember everything," Edmund said softly. "It's people who forget."

Above them, the raven finally spread its wings and lifted into the starless dark, silent as a held breath. And far to the north—so far it might have been imagination, might have been the gin's last argument—Sera felt the broken wards ache in her teeth like an old wound remembering weather.

Something was walking toward the capital. Something hungry.

"Well," she said. "Shall we go be visible, then?"

Edmund pulled the hood tighter and stepped out of the alley's shadow, into the open street, where anyone might see.

"After you," they said.
