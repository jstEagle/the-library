## 5. What the Beasts Know

The silence had lasted four days now, and Edmund had learned to hate it the way one hates a toothache: intimately, constantly, with a growing suspicion that it was only going to get worse.

Beasts were supposed to make noise. That was the whole arrangement of the world. Dogs barked at nothing and everything. Cats complained. The rats in the Gilded Scale's cellar held what Edmund could only describe as committee meetings at two in the morning, squeaking arguments about cheese jurisdiction that went on for hours. For ten years those voices had been the wallpaper of Edmund's life—so constant they'd become invisible, so familiar that their absence should have been a relief.

It was not a relief. It was like going deaf in one ear while the other ear insisted something terrible was being said just out of range.

So on the fourth night, Edmund did the thing they had sworn never to do.

They opened the cellar door, sat down on a flour barrel in the dark, and answered back.

* * *

It is important to understand what Edmund's gift actually was, because Edmund had spent a decade lying about it even to themselves. It wasn't that animals talked—not exactly. It was more that when Edmund looked at a creature and let down the lock behind their ribs, meaning arrived. Not words, usually. Impressions wearing the shape of words, translated by some faculty Edmund had never been able to name without feeling foolish. The guild's caged griffon didn't say *I am sad*; it said a gray weight of sky and clipped wings and the memory of thermals, and Edmund's mind dressed it in language because minds are tidy creatures that hate receiving gifts unwrapped.

For ten years, Edmund had listened and never once spoken back. Listening could be an accident. A flinch, a glance, a stablehand who seemed to know what a horse wanted—who could prove anything? But answering meant opening the door from both sides. Answering meant standing in the doorway where anyone might see.

Edmund sat on the flour barrel in the dark and said, aloud, in the smallest voice they'd used since childhood:

"Hello."

The silence in the cellar changed texture. It had been empty silence, dead silence. Now it became *attentive* silence, the silence of forty small bodies going still all at once, and Edmund's skin prickled with the unmistakable sensation of being perceived.

Then, from the shadowed corner behind the salt stores, a rat emerged.

She was an old one, gray-muzzled, half her left ear gone to some ancient argument, and she moved with the unhurried confidence of a creature who has survived everything worth surviving. She sat up on her haunches perhaps three feet from Edmund's boots and regarded them with black button eyes.

And she said—there was no other word for it—she said:

*"You're the quiet one."*

Edmund's breath caught. "You can—I mean. Yes. I'm the quiet one."

*"Ten years,"* the rat said, and there was no accusation in it, only wonder, the way a person might say *ten years* about a neighbor finally returning a borrowed ladder. *"Ten years you've walked past us hearing every word, and never once said good morning."*

"I'm sorry," Edmund said, and found, to their surprise, that they meant it down to the marrow. "I was afraid."

*"Of us?"*

"Of what being known would cost me."

The rat groomed her whiskers, considering this. Around the cellar, Edmund felt the other rats emerging from their walls, drawn by something—the first conversation their kind had ever had with the quiet one, perhaps, or simple curiosity. In the rafters, the guild's resident pigeons shuffled into a listening row.

*"Names,"* the old rat said at last. *"That's what's gone missing. You've noticed."*

Edmund leaned forward. "You know about that?"

*"We know everything that walks on the ground,"* she said, not boastfully, merely as fact. *"Birds know what flies over. Fish know what sinks. But the ground is ours, groundling, and I will tell you what the ground knows—for a price."*

Edmund's stomach dropped. Ten years of guild bookkeeping had taught them exactly what shape trouble took when it started asking prices. "What price?"

*"Say my name."*

"I don't know your name."

*"No,"* the rat agreed. *"But you can hear it. Listen."*

And Edmund listened—and there it was, folded into the rat's small gray presence like a seed in fruit: *Skitter-under-the-salt, first of her litter, survivor of nine winters.* Not a word. A whole small history. Edmund spoke it back as best they could, haltingly, and the old rat closed her eyes as if drinking something warm.

*"Nine winters,"* she murmured, *"and I thought I'd die unnamed. Well then, hearer-of-the-quiet-one. Here is what the ground knows."*

She told them about the night the stars went out. Rats had been in the walls of half the city; they'd seen the panic from below, felt the wards shatter through the stones like a bone breaking in a leg. But before that—this was the part that made Edmund's hands go cold on the barrel—before that, the rats of the north road had seen something walking.

*"Out of the fens,"* Skitter-under-the-salt said. *"Big as a man but wrong, the way a coat is wrong when there's nobody in it. It came to the village of Harrow's End and stood in the square, and it asked people their names. Politely. Very politely. And the ones who told it—"*

She stopped. Groomed her whiskers again. When she resumed, her voice had gone flat in the way voices do around unbearable things.

*"The ones who told it smiled afterward. They're smiling still. We don't go into Harrow's End anymore. There's nothing in there to talk to. Just meat that remembers how to wave."*

"And it's moving?" Edmund asked, though Sera Voss had already guessed it, though the dying silence had already said it.

*"North,"* the rat said. *"Always north. Toward the capital. And here is the worst of it, hearer—it isn't walking alone. It wears a king's shadow. Casts it too, if you follow me. Wherever it goes, the shadow goes first, and the dogs won't bark at the shadow, and the cats won't look at it, and we—"*

Her whiskers trembled.

*"We run. That's all any of us know to do. We run, and it doesn't matter, because it isn't hunting bodies. It's hunting names. And every living thing has one."*

* * *

Edmund climbed out of the cellar at dawn with their heart hammering and their head full, and made a decision that would have horrified the Edmund of yesterday: they were going to do this again. Properly. Systematically. If the beasts knew what was coming—if they'd known for days while humans stumbled around blind—then the beasts were the only intelligence network in Vaelmoor, and Edmund was its sole translator.

Which meant finding Sera Voss, which meant admitting, out loud, to another human being, what Edmund was.

One catastrophe at a time. First: corvids.

Ravens knew roads. Ravens knew everything that happened under open sky between here and the mountains, because ravens treated the world as one long buffet with occasional interesting murders. The guild kept none caged—ravens couldn't be caged, they simply grew spiteful and died out of principle—but the temple bell-tower three streets over hosted a parliament of them every morning, and Edmund had fed them scraps for years precisely because a bookkeeper never knew when a bird might be useful.

The tower was cold and smelled of pigeon and old stone. Edmund stood in the shadow of the bells with a pouch of bacon ends and whistled the particular three-note whistle they'd used for a decade, and the parliament came down like a ragged black cloak.

There were eleven of them. Edmund counted twice, because something was wrong: they landed too quietly. No banter, no squabbling over the best perch, none of the raucous politics that usually governed raven society. Eleven birds arranged themselves on the tower rail and looked at Edmund with eyes like wet obsidian, and the silence of the last four days pressed in again, close as a hand on the back of the neck.

Edmund opened the lock behind their ribs and let the gift come forward, and asked: *What have you seen?*

The eldest raven—a scarred female missing two tail feathers, whom Edmund privately thought of as the Guildmaster of the Sky—took the bacon pouch with surprising delicacy and answered.

*"We flew north three nights ago,"* she said, *"following the dead star. You saw it fall? No—you heard it. Everyone heard it. It fell past the Graywater hills, and we went to see, because carrion is carrion and a fallen star is the biggest carrion there is."*

"What did you find?"

*"A crater. Cold. Colder than winter has any right to be, in autumn. And in the crater, a girl."* The raven tilted her head. *"Standing. Not moving. We circled her for an hour and she never blinked, and her eyes were the color the sky used to be at night, and we got the strong impression—we always get impressions, you understand, but this one was loud—that looking at her too long would be like looking at the sun. So we left. And on the way home, we saw it."*

"The thing from the fens."

The raven's feathers rose along her neck. *"You've heard."*

"A rat told me."

*"Hm. Ground intelligence."* There was, Edmund was almost certain, a note of professional disdain. *"Yes. It was on the north road, walking at the pace of a funeral. And above it—around it—"* the raven struggled, and Edmund felt the shape of the thing she was trying to describe arrive in his own mind before she finished: *"—it had a shadow that didn't match. The light came from the east and the shadow pointed east. Shadows don't do that. And the shadow wore a crown."*

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "A crown."

*"Or the shape men agree to call a crown. We are birds, hearer. We know crowns the way we know scarecrows: as things that mean danger and stand very still."* She hopped closer, fixing Edmund with one obsidian eye. *"Here is what matters, and why we came down for your bacon instead of ignoring you as usual. The thing on the road stopped at the milestone outside the city and it turned its head—all the way around, like an owl, which nothing that wears a man's shape should be able to do—and it looked at Vaelmoor. At the whole city. And it said, in a voice like many voices, one word."*

The tower seemed to lean in. Even the bells hung silent.

*"It said: 'Soon.'"*

* * *

Sera Voss was exactly where Edmund had left her two days ago—at the gutter shrine behind the tannery, sitting with her back against the little rain-worn stone figure that might once have been a border god, working methodically through a bottle of something that smelled like it could preserve a corpse.

"You look terrible," Sera said by way of greeting. Her eyes were red-rimmed but sharp underneath, the way a blade stays sharp even when the hand holding it shakes.

"You look drunk."

"It's called coping, ledger-keeper. Some of us don't have accounting to hide inside." She squinted up at Edmund. "You've got the look. The 'I've done something stupid and it worked' look. Out with it."

Edmund hesitated. This was the doorway. This was the moment the lock either held or broke forever.

"There's something I need to tell you," Edmund said, "and if you laugh, I'm leaving, and you can drink yourself to death wondering."

Sera raised the bottle in a toast to this. "Promises, promises."

So Edmund told her. All of it—the gift, ten years of hiding, the rat named Skitter-under-the-salt, the ravens, the shadow with the crown, the single word *soon*. They told it badly, rushing, watching her face for the flicker of disbelief or pity or that worst expression, the polite one people wore right before they started keeping distance. Sera's face did none of those things. Sera's face went still and hard, the way it must have gone when she'd first seen the wards fail, and when Edmund finished she was silent for a long moment.

"That's why you noticed the silence first," she said finally. "In the guildyard. Before anyone else. Because you could hear them stop."

"Yes."

"And Corvane doesn't know."

"No one knows. Until five minutes ago, no one knew."

Sera set the bottle down—a significant gesture, Edmund suspected, in the economy of Sera Voss—and rubbed her face with both hands. When she lowered them, something had rearranged itself behind her eyes. Hope, maybe. Or the military cousin of hope, which is a plan.

"A beast-speaker," she said. "An actual, honest-to-the-old-gods beast-speaker, hiding in a counting house for ten years. Do you have any idea what the Wardens' Corps would have given for you? What the *old* corps would have given? There hasn't been one since my grandmother's day. They used to station them on the border specifically because—"

"Because the beasts see everything," Edmund said quietly. "I know. I've read the histories. That's rather the problem, isn't it? Everything I've read says the Veil eats names. And mine has apparently already been spoken aloud by a horse."

Sera winced. "Right. The horse. Still sorry about laughing at that."

"You laughed for a full minute."

"It was a *very* dramatic horse." But her humor had a thin skin over something else, and Edmund recognized it because it matched their own: fear, freshly laundered. She stood, swaying slightly, and steadied herself against the shrine. "All right. Here's what we do. Your rats and ravens are worth more than the entire Warden intelligence service, may it rot. I say we question everything that'll talk to you between here and the capital. Build a map out of mice. Find out where this thing is, how fast it moves, what it does to the places it passes through."

"And then?"

Sera looked north, past the rooftops, toward a horizon where no stars would come tonight or any night until someone did something about it.

"And then we figure out how to kill something that eats names," she said. "Wardens are good at impossible assignments. It's the paperwork afterward that kills you."

* * *

They left the city at dusk, two travelers with a hand-cart of "guild inventory awaiting appraisal"—which was true, in the sense that the inventory included a bottled echo and Edmund's nerve. Sera knew the warden patrol schedules still, despite everything, and steered them through a gap in the north gate checks with the ease of long habit and short morals.

Three miles out, in the last straggle of farmland before the moor began, Edmund heard it.

Not a voice. A smell of blood and pine, a weakness, a fading warmth at the edge of the gift's range—and following it off the road, they found the elk.

He was enormous, a great gray-brown king of the treeline, and he was dying. An arrow stood in his shoulder, snapped off at the shaft, some poacher's work gone wrong. He had dragged himself to the edge of a birch copse and lain down among the white trunks, and his breathing came in long, laboring pulls, and when Edmund knelt beside him and opened the lock, the elk's presence washed over them like a tide going out.

*"Little listener,"* the elk said. His voice was slow, vast, patient the way mountains are patient. *"I hoped one of your kind would come. We are few who remember you."*

"I'm sorry," Edmund whispered, and put a hand on his neck, and meant it more than they had ever meant anything. "I'm so sorry. Is there—can I—"

*"Nothing to be done. Arrows happen. Winters happen. Listen instead. Spend my last minutes well."* The elk's dark eye reflected the first stars—which were, Edmund realized with a lurch, still absent, the sky above the birches bare and black as a burned page. *"I come from the north moor. I have walked past the places where the villages smile. I have smelled the walker on the wind."*

"Tell me."

*"It is three days behind me and moving faster than I did. It does not tire. It does not eat. It walks north with a king's shadow wrapped around it like a cloak, and where it steps, the grass forgets how to grow."* The elk's breathing hitched. *"But little listener—that is not the worst news I carry."*

Edmund waited. Somewhere behind them, Sera stood watch on the road, a silhouette against the last gray light.

*"The walker is not the hunter,"* the elk said. *"It is the herald. The thing it serves is already here. It has been here for years, little listener, wearing the city like a glove. It sits in bright rooms. It signs papers. It buys and sells."* The vast dark eye fixed on Edmund, gentle and terrible. *"And it has bought from a man with silver rings on his fingers, who keeps a tall house full of cages and ledgers. I smelled him on its agents' robes. Guildmaster. Your master."*

The world went very quiet and very small.

Corvane. The hooded buyer whose voice sounded like many voices. The trades in the forbidden inventory Edmund had catalogued and never questioned, because questioning meant visibility, and visibility meant ruin—

The elk sighed, a long sound like wind through a valley, and his warmth began to ebb.

*"Run clever, little listener,"* he said. *"And when you face it—remember what I taught you just now: the loudest trap is the one you've lived inside so long it feels like home."*

By the time Sera came off the road to find him, the elk was gone, and Edmund was kneeling in the birches with tears freezing on their face and one thought repeating like a heartbeat:

*He sold the names. He's been selling them for years. And I work for him.*

"Edmund?" Sera's hand landed on their shoulder. "What did it say? What's wrong?"

Edmund looked up at her, and heard themselves answer in a voice that no longer sounded like the quiet one at all.

"We have to go back to the guild tonight. Before Corvane realizes I know. There's a ledger in his vault—" They swallowed. "—and I think it's full of names."
