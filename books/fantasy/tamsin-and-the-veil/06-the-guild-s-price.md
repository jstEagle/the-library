## 6. The Guild's Price

The morning after the stars went out, the Gilded Scale guild did what it always did: it opened for business.

Edmund stood in the counting room at dawn, watching Guildmaster Corvane direct a dozen clerks with the brisk calm of a man rearranging furniture after a mild earthquake. Outside the tall windows, Vaelmoor was still smoking in places. The great ward-stones along the border had cracked like old teeth, and half the city had spent the night praying in the streets. But here, inside walls of polished oak and older money, someone had already lit the lamps, swept the floors, and posted the day's exchange rates as though the sky were exactly where they'd left it.

"Ledger Nine to me," Corvane said, not looking up from his correspondence. "And someone tell the night clerk that a bottled echo is *not* collateral, whatever he promised the widow Hesse."

Edmund gathered Ledger Nine — the impossible trades, the ones that lived behind three locks and a polite fiction of not existing — and carried it across the room. Their hands were steady. They had practiced steadiness for ten years, and it held.

But their ears were still ringing.

*Edmund.*

The horse had said it last night, in the chaos, in the dark, while the wards shattered and people screamed about the sky. A stablehorse, mud to the fetlocks, had turned its great head and spoken Edmund's true name in a voice like a bell dropped down a well. Not Edmund Galloway. The other one. The one given at birth by a mother who was dead, in a house that no longer existed, known to no living soul.

Which meant something was reading the city. Something that read all the way down.

"You look pale, Galloway." Corvane took the ledger without thanks. He was a long man, dry as winter paper, with silver at his temples arranged so precisely it might have been combed into place by contract. "Bad night?"

"The whole city had a bad night, sir."

"Mm. The whole city will have a bad week, then, and a bad year after that, and the guild will still be standing when it's over. That is what we do, Galloway. We are the fixed point." He opened the ledger, ran one finger down the columns, and paused. "You catalogued the moth correctly. The one that remembers futures."

"I catalogued everything correctly, sir. I always do."

"Yes." Corvane looked up then, and for a moment his eyes were not dry at all. They were wet and dark and very old, like wells you could drop a scream into without hearing it land. "Yes, you do. Come to my office at the ninth bell. Alone."

He went back to his correspondence.

Edmund stood there for three full seconds, which in the counting room of the Gilded Scale was roughly a decade, before returning to their desk and pretending, with tremendous professionalism, that nothing in the world was wrong.

* * *

Ninth bell found Edmund climbing the stairs to Corvane's office, past the portraits of guildmasters dead and prosperous, past the case where the guild's founding charter sat under glass. The office itself was famous for its view — the whole sweep of Vaelmoor's rooftops, the river, the border hills beyond — though Corvane kept the curtains drawn more often than not. Today they were drawn tight, and the lamps burned low, and the air smelled faintly of ink and something else. Something cold. Like the inside of a locked cellar in winter.

Corvane sat behind his desk. Across from him, in the visitor's chair, sat a figure in a grey hood.

Edmund stopped in the doorway.

The hooded buyer. The one from the night the wards hummed wrong, whose voice sounded like many voices speaking just slightly out of step with each other. It was sitting perfectly still, hands folded on its knees, and Edmund realized with a lurch that it had no face beneath the hood — only a smooth pale blankness, like an egg, like a page never written on.

"Galloway," Corvane said. "Shut the door."

Every instinct Edmund owned — ten years of careful, invisible living — screamed at once. They shut the door.

"You know our guest," Corvane said. It wasn't quite a question. "Of course you do. You hear things, don't you? You've always heard things."

The floor tilted gently under Edmund's feet. Ten years. Ten years of ledgers and lowered eyes and answering every question about why they lingered near the menagerie with *I find the numbers calming, sir.* Ten years, and it had taken this man four words to peel it open like fruit.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't insult us both." Corvane leaned back. "The rats in the cellar go quiet when you walk through. The caged songbirds sing *to* you — I've watched them turn their heads. And three nights ago, when I asked you whether anything was wrong with the wards, you said the beasts within ten miles had gone silent at once. Which, Galloway, is not something a bookkeeper knows. Unless the bookkeeper asks them."

The hooded thing made a sound. It might have been laughter. It sounded like a hundred people trying to laugh in unison and each of them starting a half-breath apart.

"It told me you would be useful," Corvane said. "It has excellent instincts. Better than mine, frankly, and mine built this guild."

"Selling names." The words came out of Edmund flat and strange, as if someone else had said them. Because Sera had said them first, two nights ago, drunk in a gutter shrine, laughing bitterly at her own paranoia: *Your guildmaster's too rich, Galloway. Nobody gets that rich honestly, not on a border. Ask yourself who's buying.*

Corvane didn't flinch. That was the worst part. He didn't flinch at all.

"For eleven years," he said, in the tone he used for quarterly reports, "the Veil's agents have come to this office, and I have sold them names. Peasants mostly, at first. Wanderers. People who wouldn't be missed quickly. Then debtors, criminals, the inconvenient. In exchange, the guild prospers. Our caravans pass through territories where every other caravan vanishes. Our rivals suffer catastrophes of paperwork and fortune. Vaelmoor eats because the Gilded Scale feeds it, and the Gilded Scale feeds because I keep the account balanced." He folded his hands. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Galloway? I am the reason this city still has bread."

"The wards fell," Edmund said. "The stars went out. Your *account balance*—"

"Was a cost of doing business." For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's face — not guilt; Edmund searched hard for guilt and found none — but something like weariness. "The binding was going to fail regardless. It fails every few centuries; the archives are clear enough if you know where to look. All I did was make sure that when it failed, the survivors would be people who could afford to survive. Someone must be solvent at the end of the world, Galloway. It may as well be us."

The hooded thing turned its blank face toward Edmund, and Edmund felt it *look* — felt it move through them the way cold water moves through cloth, tasting, weighing.

"It wants your name," Corvane said simply. "Not today. It says you're worth more intact for now. But it wants it eventually, and it has offered a considerable sum."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I sell it anyway. I've sold better names than yours for less." Corvane rose, straightened his coat, and became, horribly, kind. That was the mask Edmund had trusted for a decade — the dry, fair, tired man who paid wages on time and never asked questions. "Here is what I offer instead, because you've been adequate to me for ten years. Forget tonight. Forget the silence, forget the horse, forget whatever you heard in the dark. Go back to your desk. Catalogue your impossible things. Live out your small careful life, and die old, and be buried under your own name. That is the price of staying. Silence, and forgetting." He picked up a pen. "Or refuse, and be entered into the inventory. I'll need your birth-name for the entry, of course. Though I suspect our guest can find it without your help."

Silence stretched across the office like a held breath.

Edmund thought of the stablehorse saying their name into the burning dark. Thought of the elk, dying north of the city, whispering about *the thing that eats names walking in a king's shadow*. Thought of Sera Voss, asleep in the loft above the tannery where Edmund had stashed her, snoring like a woman with nothing left to lose.

They thought about their small careful life. It fit in the palm of their hand, they realized. It always had. They had just never held it up to the light.

"No," Edmund said.

Corvane sighed, the sigh of a man whose clerk has misfiled something minor. "I was afraid you'd say that. Galloway — the door stays shut until our guest is finished. I'd ask you not to struggle. It makes such a mess of the carpets."

The hooded thing rose from its chair.

And Edmund — invisible, careful, quiet Edmund, who had spent ten years perfecting the art of being overlooked — did the one thing nobody in that room expected.

They spoke to the building.

There were rats in the walls of the Gilded Scale. There had always been rats; there are rats in any building old enough to have a founding charter, and Edmund had been leaving them crusts and quiet words since their second week of employment. They had never asked the rats for anything before. It seemed suddenly, enormously, overdue.

*Now,* Edmund said, in the language under language, the one with no sound in it. *Now, please, all of you, NOW—*

The wall of Corvane's office erupted.

Rats poured out of the wainscoting like spilled ink — dozens of them, a hundred, a chittering grey flood that hit the desk, swarmed the lamps, and went for the hooded thing with the fearless fury of small creatures defending the only human who had ever been kind to them. The faceless agent recoiled with a sound like a choir inhaling. Corvane shouted something about the charter case. The lamp went over.

Edmund was already moving. Over the desk, shoulder into the door, out into the corridor — and behind them, impossibly, the screaming continued, because the rats were not stopping, because Edmund had asked *please*, and somewhere in the language under language, please apparently meant something enormous.

They ran.

* * *

The vaults beneath the guild were older than the guild. Everyone knew that and nobody said it aloud, the way everyone knew the third stair creaked and the wine cellar smelled of more than wine. Edmund had catalogued the deep archive twice in ten years — a rare honor, or a test, they'd never decided which — and their feet remembered the way even now, down and down, past the wine racks, past the door with the iron band, into the cold stone gallery where the guild kept the things too dangerous or too shameful to display.

Their heart was slamming. Their breath came in white rags. Somewhere above, muffled by six feet of stone, the office was still in chaos.

*Third alcove,* they thought. *Iron box, green corrosion, seal of the first Corvane.*

Because here was the thing about a man who sold names for eleven years: he kept accounts. Corvane kept accounts of everything — it was practically a religion — and a man like that did not trust the Veil's agents to remember their purchases fairly. Somewhere in the deep archive there was a ledger of every name ever sold, dates and prices and the small cold annotations of a merchant's conscience, and Edmund had seen it once, years ago, and filed the memory away in the same place they filed everything they weren't supposed to know.

The iron box was where they'd left it in memory. The lock was old. Edmund's picks were older, and patient, and it gave with a click that sounded obscenely loud.

Inside: a ledger bound in grey leather, unmarked. Edmund opened it with hands that shook and did not care anymore.

Names. Hundreds of them. Columns of them, in Corvane's neat, upright script. *Marta Ilves, tanner's widow, 4 crowns. The boy Pell, foundling, 2 crowns. Serah Ostwick, warden, third border company —* Edmund's blood went cold — *12 crowns, premium rate, military name.*

Sera. Her name was in the book. Sold years ago — which meant the Veil had known her, marked her, perhaps been wearing away at her long before the wards ever fell. Perhaps that was what disgrace really was. Perhaps she'd been *sold* first and blamed after.

Edmund closed the ledger, held it against their chest, and felt the weight of it settle into their bones. This was the point of no return. They understood that with a strange, floating clarity. Steal this book and there was no desk to go back to, no small careful life, no invisibility. There was only north, and a king's shadow walking, and two liars with nothing left but each other.

Above them, distantly, a bell began to ring. Not the ninth bell. The alarm.

Edmund ran.

* * *

Sera was awake, of course. Sera was always awake; sleeping was apparently another thing she'd lost along with her commission. She took one look at Edmund's face, one look at the grey ledger, and reached for her boots.

"That bad?"

"He offered to let me keep my life if I forgot everything."

"Generous." She laced the boots. "Did you forget?"

"I stole his accounting of every soul he's ever sold instead."

Sera grinned — the first real grin Edmund had seen on her, sharp and crooked and alive. "Oh, I *like* you. Back door?"

"They'll watch the back door."

"Roof, then. Tannery roofs connect to the dye-works, the dye-works to the river stairs. I used to chase smugglers across this exact route before I was professionally ruined." She paused at the loft door, and for a moment the grin faded into something rawer. "Galloway. Once we run, we're running. No guild wages, no warden's pension, no coming back. You understand that?"

Edmund looked down at the ledger in their arms. Marta Ilves. The boy Pell. Three hundred names in a dead woman's handwriting, waiting for someone to carry them somewhere safe.

"I haven't been coming back for years," they said. "I just hadn't told myself yet."

They went up through the roof hatch together, into the smoke-hung dark of a starless Vaelmoor, as the alarm bells rang on behind them — and below, in the walls of the Gilded Scale, a thousand small voices whispered after the only person who had ever listened:

*Run. Run. We will remember the way.*
