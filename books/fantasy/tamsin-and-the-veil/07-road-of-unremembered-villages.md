## 7. Road of Unremembered Villages

The road north out of Vaelmoor was the color of old pewter, and it smelled like rain that hadn't decided to fall yet.

Edmund walked with their hood up and their ledger of stolen names strapped against their ribs like a second heart. Sera Voss walked beside them with a warden's long stride and a hangover she refused to acknowledge, chewing on a sprig of bitterleaf like it had personally wronged her.

"You're limping," she said, without looking at them.

"I'm favoring my left foot, which is a completely different thing."

"It's limping with extra steps."

"That," Edmund said, "is what limping is."

Sera snorted. It was, Edmund was learning, the highest form of praise she offered before noon.

They had been walking for two days since fleeing the guild—two days of hedgerows and waystones and the particular silence of a countryside where every bird had stopped singing at once, three nights ago, and hadn't started again. Edmund kept waiting for the silence to feel normal. It refused. Silence, they were discovering, was not an absence. It was a presence, like a hand pressed flat against the back of your neck.

"Tell me about ward-theory again," Edmund said, mostly to fill it. "The part where you compared the border wards to soup."

"I did *not* compare the wards to soup."

"You said, and I quote, 'a ward is just a soup you've convinced to hold its shape.'"

Sera stopped chewing. "I was drunk."

"You were explaining it beautifully."

"A ward is a pattern," she said, in her lecturing voice, which was flatter and more tired than her arguing voice but somehow warmer underneath. "That's all. Threads of intention laid over a place until the place agrees to be one thing instead of many things. The border wards agreed to be *Vaelmoor is safe* for four hundred years. And then—" she made a small, awful gesture, fingers opening like something letting go, "—someone unpicked the agreement."

Edmund said nothing. They both knew who the someone was now. Corvane's name sat between them on the road like a stone neither wanted to pick up.

"And the Veil?" Edmund asked. "What kind of pattern is *that*?"

"The anti-pattern." Sera kicked a pebble off the road with unnecessary violence. "A soup that eats the bowl. Names are how things agree to be themselves, Edmund. A name is the first ward anything ever gets. Your mother gives it to you, and suddenly you're not just a squalling lump—you're *you*, held in shape by five syllables. Take the name away and..." She trailed off. Ahead of them, the village of Harrowmere came into view, sitting in its little valley with smoke rising from its chimneys, looking from this distance exactly like a village should.

"Take the name away and what?" Edmund asked.

Sera didn't answer. She was squinting down at the village, and her face had gone the particular gray of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"There's no dog," she said.

"What?"

"Every village has dogs. Chickens, geese, a dog asleep in the road that you have to step around. I've walked into two hundred villages in fifteen years of warden work. There is always a dog." She started walking faster. "And look at the fields. It's harvest-adjacent. Where are the people in the fields?"

The village received them the way water receives a stone: without comment, without ripple.

The people of Harrowmere were all outside, which was somehow worse than if they'd been hiding. They stood in doorways and along the lane and in their gardens, and they were smiling. Not the rictus smile of fear or the fixed smile of madness—a soft, pleasant, vacant smile, the smile of people watching something lovely just over your shoulder. An old man was watering a bed of flowers that had been dead so long they'd gone to gray lace. He watered them with great tenderness. Watering can, gray flowers, gentle smile.

"Hello," Edmund tried.

"Hello!" the old man said warmly. "Lovely day."

It was not a lovely day. It was the color of dishwater and getting worse.

"Could you tell us where we might find your headman?" Sera asked.

"Our what?"

"Your—the person in charge. The village elder."

The old man smiled his soft smile. "We don't need one of those."

A woman nearby laughed gently, as if he'd said something charming. She was holding a broom and sweeping a stretch of clean cobbles that was already clean, had perhaps always been clean, would be swept clean forever.

"When is the last time anyone came through here?" Edmund asked. "Traders? Wardens?"

"Oh, ages." The woman said it happily. "Everyone left, you know."

"Everyone left?"

"Mm. All our people went away." She beamed at them. "Isn't it nice weather?"

Edmund looked at Sera. Sera looked at Edmund. *All our people went away.* Said by a woman standing in her own doorway, wearing her own apron, sweeping her own step.

They moved down the lane slowly, and Edmund began, horribly, to understand. Every villager they spoke to was pleasant. Every villager was helpful in a vague, drifting way. And not one of them—not the baker standing in his empty bakery, not the children playing a game with no rules in the square, not the smith whose forge was cold—could say their own names.

"What do we call you?" Edmund asked the baker.

He considered the question with genuine, good-natured puzzlement, the way you might consider *what color is Wednesday?*

"I don't think that's mine," he said at last, apologetically. "I've been looking, though. I look every morning." He smiled. "I'll keep looking."

In the square, the children played their ruleless game, laughing at nothing, chasing nothing, and when Edmund knelt down and asked a small girl with mud on her chin what her name was, she leaned close, confiding, delighted, and whispered:

"I almost remembered it yesterday."

Then she ran off laughing, and Edmund stayed kneeling on the cobblestones of Harrowmere for a long moment, feeling something crack quietly behind their sternum.

Because here was the thing—the thing they could never say aloud in the guild, the thing they had locked under ledgers and careful invisibility for ten years: Edmund could hear what the beasts heard. And the beasts of Harrowmere, in the sheds and coops and one thin, starving hound tied by a well, were not silent because there was nothing to hear.

They were silent because they were grieving.

The hound lifted its head when Edmund passed, and its mind touched theirs, gentle and ruined: *the tall ones forgot themselves, friend-of-everyone, the tall ones forgot themselves and we cannot remind them, we never learned the words.*

"They can't remember," Edmund said aloud, before they could stop themselves. "The animals. They know something's missing but they never knew the words to begin with."

Sera stared at them. "You spoke to it."

"I—" Edmund's whole body went cold and hot at once. Ten years of hiding, and here on an empty road with no guild walls to hide behind, it came out of them like a splinter working loose. "Yes. I speak to beasts. I have since I was six. It's why I took the ledger job—to hide somewhere no one would ever ask me to talk to anything alive."

Sera kept staring. Then, unexpectedly, she sat down on the edge of the dry fountain in the square, right there in front of the smiling hollow villagers, and put her face in her hands, and laughed—not cruelly, but with a kind of exhausted relief.

"Thank the drowned gods," she said. "I thought I was going to have to spend another week pretending I believed you were just a very dedicated accountant."

"You *knew*?"

"Sera Voss has been many things, but stupid isn't one of them. You flinched every time a cart horse got whipped. You talked to the guild ravens. Out loud. In full sentences. You thought nobody noticed?" She dropped her hands and looked up at them, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "I noticed. I notice everything. It's the only skill I have left."

Edmund stood there in the middle of a stolen village, feeling the strangest thing: seen. Not caught. Seen.

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because you weren't ready to say anything." Sera shrugged. "People come out of hiding when they're ready. Rushing them just makes them dig deeper. Ask me how I know."

"How do you—"

"*Later.*" She stood, wincing, and nodded toward the far side of the square, where the thin hound still watched them. "First we figure out if these people can be helped. Can your friends tell us anything about what happened here?"

Edmund crouched by the hound. Its ribs pressed against its fur like barrel staves. *When,* Edmund asked it silently. *When did the tall ones forget?*

*Three nights past,* the hound answered. *The night the sky went blind. A walking thing came up the road, wearing a shape like a tall one, and it stopped at every door, and it asked each of them politely, 'May I?' And they said yes, because it was polite.*

Edmund's blood went to ice water. "It *asked*."

"What?" said Sera.

"It asked permission. At every door. And they said yes." Edmund stood slowly. "Sera, the Veil doesn't take. It *trades*. That's what Corvane was doing—that's what the broker wants—it's all trades. Which means these people gave their names away. Which means—"

"Which means somewhere there's a record," Sera finished, her eyes going sharp and hungry and alive for the first time in days. "A transaction. And transactions can be *reversed*."

For the first time since the stars went out, Edmund felt something that wasn't dread. Small, fragile, ridiculous—but real.

"We need to get to the capital," they said. "The archive. If there are records—"

"Three weeks' walk."

"Two, if we're limping efficiently."

* * *

They made camp that night in a shepherd's hut on the high moor, and Sera built a fire with the competence of someone who had slept outdoors for years, some of them voluntarily.

"Ward-theory," she said, tossing Edmund a strip of dried mutton. "Lesson three. Pay attention, this is the one that matters."

She drew in the ash at the fire's edge: a circle, then lines crossing it.

"Every ward is a promise with a price. You want a wall that says *nothing crosses*? Fine. But the wall needs feeding—intention, sacrifice, renewal. The border wards drank a drop of the king's blood every year at the Renewal Rite. Four hundred years of drops. And this year—" she drew a line straight through the circle, breaking it, "—no drop. Or rather, a drop that meant nothing, because the name attached to it had already been sold. Do you see? The king's binding-name was the anchor. Sell the anchor, and the whole ship drifts."

"So Corvane didn't just betray the city. He betrayed the *math*."

"He betrayed the math." Sera smiled grimly. "I like you, ledger-keeper. You make betrayal sound like bookkeeping."

"In my experience betrayal usually is bookkeeping. Just with worse margins."

She laughed—a real laugh, short and rusty—and then it faded, and she looked into the fire, and Edmund watched her decide to say something.

"I told you I notice everything," she said. "Here's what I noticed about myself, since we're trading truths. Fifteen years ago I signed the inspection report that cleared those wards. My signature, my seal, my word. When they failed, the tribunal needed someone to blame, and I was convenient—I'd been asking awkward questions about guild influence for years. So: disgraced, stripped, done." She turned her hands palm-up, scarred and burn-marked. "But here's the thing I can't stop chewing. I *did* clear them. I checked everything. Except I never checked whether the wards were being fed honestly, because that wasn't a warden's job—that was the guild's sacred trust. I trusted the ledger." Her jaw tightened. "I built my whole honor on trusting a ledger, and the ledger was lying."

Edmund was quiet a moment. Then: "I spent ten years making ledgers lie. Quietly. Small fictions, so the impossible inventory wouldn't raise questions. A bottled echo filed as 'novelty, glass.' A moth that remembers futures filed as 'specimen, common.'" They poked the fire. "I told myself I was keeping everyone safe. Really I was keeping *myself* safe. Same crime, different direction."

"Two liars," Sera said.

"On a road full of people who can't even manage it anymore."

They sat with that. The fire cracked. Somewhere down in the dark valley, Harrowmere's chimneys smoked on, and its people wandered their smiling rounds, and its hound grieved at the end of its rope.

"Do you think they can come back?" Edmund asked quietly. "The villagers. If we reverse the trade."

Sera was a long time answering. "Ward-theory says yes. Everything traded can be traded back, if you find the counter-price." She looked up at the starless sky, and her voice went low. "But I'll tell you what fifteen years of disgrace taught me, Edmund. Some prices you pay forward. You don't get the old thing back. You get a new thing, and you have to decide whether to love it."

Edmund thought of the name the stablehorse had spoken—their true name, the one no living soul should have known, read off them like a label in the dark. They thought of how it had felt to hear it: violated and, in some terrible corner of them, *relieved*. Someone finally saying the thing they'd been hiding.

"Yeah," Edmund said. "I'm starting to learn that."

* * *

The next village was called Bellbrook, or had been. The signpost at its edge still bore the letters B-E-L-L-B-R-O-O-K, but when Edmund asked a woman drawing water at the well what the village was called, she smiled her soft smile and said, "Is it?"

By Bellbrook the pattern was familiar enough that it no longer shocked, which was its own horror—horror becoming routine after only two villages, the way the mind sands its own edges down. What was new was the church.

Or what had been the church. It stood at the village center with its doors thrown open, and inside, where an altar should have been, the villagers had arranged hundreds of small objects: buttons, spoons, hair ribbons, clay marbles, a child's shoe. Things that might once have belonged to someone. Offerings, or evidence, or maybe just the instinct of people who had lost the words for themselves reaching for objects instead—*this cup was mine, this ribbon was mine, I think this hand was mine.*

At the altar's head, written over and over in charcoal on the whitewashed wall in a dozen different hands, was a single sentence:

**WE ARE THE PEOPLE WHO LIVE HERE**

Not names. Just that. A congregation of the unnamed, signing themselves as geography.

Sera stood before it with her arms crossed tight, and Edmund saw her throat working.

"My mother taught me ward-craft," she said abruptly. "Before the academy, before any of it. She was a hedge-warden, unlicensed, the kind they don't put in the histories. She used to say: *a person is the smallest ward there is, Sera. Guard yourself like a border town.*" She exhaled. "I thought it was just something mothers say."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was giving me the whole discipline in seven words, and I was too clever to listen." She reached out and touched the charcoal letters, gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "These people guarded their towns. Fences, militias, granaries. Nobody ever taught them the smallest ward was the one they'd need."

From the rafters above, a pigeon shifted and cooed, and its mind brushed Edmund's, dim and sorrowful: *the tall ones keep putting their names on the wall but the wall won't hold them, friend, the wall won't hold them.*

Edmund relayed it. Sera closed her eyes.

"We're going to fix this," she said. It wasn't hope in her voice. It was something harder than hope—vow-shaped. "I don't know how yet. But I want it written somewhere that Sera Voss said it first: *we are going to give these people back their names.*"

"You realize," Edmund said, "that according to every ledger I've ever kept, promises like that are the most expensive line item there is."

"Good thing we stole a ledger full of names, then." She turned from the wall, and for a moment, in the gray light through the church doors, she almost smiled. "Come on, accountant. Capital's three weeks away and we're doing it in two."

They camped that night in the church itself, among the offerings, because it was the only roofed building in Bellbrook whose occupants seemed to want company. In the small hours, Edmund woke to find Sera awake, feeding scraps of dried mutton to the village's one remaining cat, a scarred gray tom who had taken up residence on her bedroll as if he'd been assigned to her.

"He talks," Sera said, without preamble. "The cat. I can't hear him, obviously. But he keeps looking at the north road and then at me, very pointedly, like I'm being slow."

Edmund listened. The tom's mind was old and flinty and tired, and what it showed them made the cold come back into their bones.

*There is another walker on the north road,* the cat said. *Behind you. It walks at night and stops at doors. It is following the smell of paper.*

Edmund sat up slowly. "Sera. Something's following us. Following the *ledger*."

Outside, far down the black thread of the north road, something that wore a shape like a tall one paused at the edge of the firelight's reach—and, very politely, knocked on the church door.
