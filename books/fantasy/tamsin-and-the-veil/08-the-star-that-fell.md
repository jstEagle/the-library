## 8. The Star That Fell

Sera Voss had spent eleven years learning to read the land the way other people read letters: the lean of a fencepost, the color of frost on a south-facing stone, the particular quality of silence that meant something had died nearby. So when she saw the crater from half a mile off—a perfect bowl punched into the heather like a thumbprint in bread dough—she knew before Edmund said a word.

"That's not lightning," she said.

"No," Edmund agreed. "Lightning doesn't smell like this."

They had been walking north for six days, through country that grew stranger by the mile. The unremembered villages were behind them now, their smiling hollow residents left to whatever peace or purgatory they'd found. Sera still dreamed about them. A woman at a well who couldn't tell her own name but had offered Sera water anyway, because some part of her remembered how to be kind even after everything else was eaten. Sera thought about that more than she wanted to. She thought about how she'd once been the kind of warden who would have filed a report and ridden on.

Now she was a disgraced drunk tracking a silence across the north with a guild clerk who talked to animals, and the strangest part—the part that kept her up at night—was that she'd never felt more honest in her life.

The smell Edmund meant was ozone and something older underneath it, something like the air inside a mountain cave that hadn't been opened since the world was young. Cold iron. Burnt sugar. And beneath all of it, faintly, a scent Sera had no name for until she did:

"Starlight," she said, surprising herself. "It smells like starlight."

"You've smelled starlight?"

"I've stood on ward-towers my whole life, clerk. You get to know the neighbors." Even if the neighbors had never once looked down at her. Even if she'd spent years maintaining the great border wards without ever being permitted to touch their heart-threads, because a Voss with a drinking problem and a dead mentor didn't get trusted with anything that mattered. "Come on. Whatever's in that hole, I want to see it before dark."

Edmund hesitated at the crater's rim the way they hesitated at everything—like a person perpetually waiting for permission that had never come. Sera had noticed it in the first day. The clerk moved through the world apologizing for the space they took up. It made Sera's teeth ache, mostly because she recognized it. Two liars, she'd told them back in Vaelmoor, recognizing each other. She hadn't said the other true thing: two cowards, too. Though maybe that was changing. Edmund had spoken to a dying elk last week with their whole chest, gift blazing out of them like a lamp uncovered, and the sky hadn't fallen. Small mercies.

Sera slid down into the crater first, boots skidding on glassed earth. The heat still radiated up through her soles, days old but banked deep, like a forge left to sleep.

At the center stood a young woman.

She was facing away from them, arms crossed, looking up at a sky that held nothing—not cloud, not bird, not one single point of light where the stars should have been, though it was late afternoon and wouldn't be dark for hours. Her hair was white. Not old-woman white, not winter white: furnace white, the white of a thing that had burned so hot it had burned its color away. Her clothes were strange—robes that seemed woven from shadow and silver thread, scorched at the hem, and beneath the scorching her bare feet stood on ground that should have cooked them.

She didn't turn around.

"I heard you at the rim," the woman said. Her voice was odd. Not accented—layered. Like three people speaking in unison and almost, but not quite, matching pitch. "You walk loudly for someone trained to walk quietly."

"Warden training emphasizes other things," Sera said. "Like not falling into craters."

"You fell in anyway."

"I walked in. There's a difference. Falling implies I lost an argument with gravity. This was a deliberate choice."

That got a reaction—a twitch of the white head, not quite a turn. "You're joking. The sky is empty and you're making jokes."

"The sky being empty is exactly why I'm making jokes," Sera said. "The alternative is screaming, and I did my screaming back in Vaelmoor. Rationed supply." She let her hand rest near her knife, casual, a habit rather than a threat. "Who are you?"

The woman finally turned.

Sera had seen beautiful people before; the capital had been full of them, polished and lacquered and hollow as festival masks. This was different. The young woman's face was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, all edges and intent, and her eyes—

Her eyes were wrong. They were the deep violet-black of the space between stars, and when they caught the light they threw it back in pinpricks, dozens of them, tiny points wheeling slowly in each iris like distant constellations turning.

Sera had guarded wards built on starlight her entire career. She knew what she was looking at.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, hells."

"Yes," the woman said flatly. "I'm told that's the usual response."

* * *

Her name was Lyra. Just Lyra, she said, the way you'd say a word you'd only just learned and weren't sure fit yet. She sat on a chunk of fused glass at the crater's center while Sera shared out dried plums and hard cheese from her pack, and Edmund hovered at the edge of the firelight radiating the particular anxious energy of someone who wanted very badly to ask questions and was terrified of the answers.

"You're a constellation," Sera said. It wasn't a question. "A living one. The old texts mention them—wardens' lore, mostly. The Seven Sisters. The Hunter. We used to calibrate the northern array against—" She stopped. Recalculated. "Wait. *Lyra.*"

"The Harp. Yes." Lyra turned a plum over in her fingers without eating it, studying it like an artifact from a dead civilization. Which, Sera supposed, it was. "My sisters and I hung above the northern wards for four thousand years. We sang the anchor-notes. Every night, the same song, holding the border against things that crawl in from outside the pattern." Her layered voice went very quiet. "Then one night the Veil came for us."

"The Veil ate stars?" Edmund asked. Their voice came out thin.

"The Veil eats *names*. And we are nothing but names, little clerk—old ones, sung ones, names woven into the fabric of the sky itself. Merope went first. Then Celaeno. Then Electra, then Taygete, Maia, Alcyone." Each name landed like a stone dropped down a well; Sera counted the sisters silently and felt her stomach drop with every one. "I felt each of them go. Like notes cut out of a song mid-phrase. Like hands around my throat getting tighter and tighter, and then—" Lyra spread her arms at the crater, the scorched robes, the whole absurd catastrophe of herself. "Falling. Burning. Waking up in dirt with a body that hurts and a hunger I don't know how to feed and memories that keep—" 

She stopped. Her jaw worked.

"Keep what?" Sera asked gently.

"Leaking." The word came out vicious. "I woke up knowing ten thousand years of the sky's history. This morning I tried to remember our song—the anchor-song, the one I sang every night since before your species learned to farm—and I could only recall the first verse. By noon I'll probably have lost the second. By tomorrow—" She hurled the plum away into the dark. It struck the crater wall with a pathetic little thump. "By tomorrow I'll be a girl standing in a hole who can't remember why any of it matters. That's what the Veil does, isn't it? I've seen your villages. I know what's coming for me. It's eating me from the inside out, one memory at a time, and there is nothing—" her voice cracked across three pitches at once, human and harp-string and something vast and grieving underneath, "*nothing* I can do about it."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere on the moor above, a nightjar called, and Sera saw Edmund tilt their head toward it—listening, always listening—and saw them go pale.

"What?" Sera asked.

"It's the nightjar," Edmund said reluctantly. "It says... it says the crater woman smells like the thing that took the stars. Same scent. Faded. Like a coat someone wore once." They winced. "Sorry. I'm sorry. Beasts don't really do tact."

"No," Lyra said, and to Sera's surprise there was something almost like laughter buried in the layers of her voice, dry and bitter as old wine. "No, they're right. I'm wearing its leavings. It chewed my sisters and spat me out half-finished, and I'm going to sit here leaking memories into the dirt until there's nothing left but a hungry animal in a stolen body." She looked up, and those star-filled eyes fixed on Sera with a ferocity that made the hair on her arms stand up. "Unless."

"Unless," Sera repeated warily. In her experience, *unless* was the most expensive word in any language.

"You're wardens. Real ones—I can taste the ward-craft on you, both of you, even the clerk, especially the clerk, you're soaked in old bindings, what *are* you?" She shook her head before Edmund could answer, or fail to. "Doesn't matter. You're going north. I watched you walking for two days before I fell—you travel like people chasing something. The Veil is north. The king is north. My sisters' names are in whatever belly it wears for a stomach." She rose, and standing, she was taller than she'd seemed sitting, and the scorched hem of her robe stirred in a wind Sera couldn't feel. "Take me with you."

"Why would we?" Sera asked. Not cruelly. She genuinely wanted to hear the answer, because she'd learned on this road that the reasons people gave told you more than the reasons they had.

And Lyra—the star, the ancient thing in a girl's furious body—didn't give her a speech about destiny or power or the anchor-song. She wrapped her arms around herself, and for a moment all the edges went out of her voice, leaving something very young and very tired.

"Because everyone I have ever loved has been eaten," she said, "and I am losing the memory of their faces one hour at a time, and if I stay in this hole alone I will forget why I'm angry before I forget how to be angry, and then it will have won without ever having to look at me." Her chin came up. The defiance snapped back into place like a visor, grief wearing a fighting face, and Sera thought: *there she is. There's the four-thousand-year-old soldier.* "So I'm going to walk north at the thing that ate my family, and I'm going to make it *look at me*, and either I remember why it matters or I die remembering nothing, but I will not sit in a hole and leak. Are you going to take me, or do I follow you at a distance and annoy you for three hundred miles?"

"She'd do it," Edmund murmured. "I've known her a week. She'd absolutely do it."

"Thank you, clerk."

"They also say," Edmund added, tilting their head again toward some conversation Sera couldn't hear, "that the foxes on this moor have started avoiding the north road. All of them. They say the road 'smells like forgetting.'" They swallowed. "And that two nights ago, they heard singing from the direction of the capital. A chorus. Very many voices. Singing a coronation hymn."

Sera felt the cold slide down her spine and settle, familiar now, an old companion. The king. The rite. Whatever renewal or coronation or feeding was coming, it was coming soon.

"Well," she said, and slung her pack over her shoulder, and discovered that she was smiling, which was either madness or the first honest feeling she'd had in eleven years. "Then we'd better move fast. Welcome to the worst company in Vaelmoor, Lady Lyra. Try not to fall on anyone."

Lyra stepped out of the crater in a single bound that covered thirty feet and landed light as ash, and if her eyes were wet when she turned away, neither of them mentioned it.

"One condition," she said, falling into step beside them, chin high, voice steady except where it wasn't. "When we find it. When we find the thing that ate my sisters. I get to be the one who makes it listen."

Above them, the sky stayed black and empty all the way to the horizon—but far to the north, Sera could have sworn she saw a flicker. A pulse. Like something enormous, wearing a king's shadow, turning its many mouths toward three small figures on a dark road.

And beginning, softly, to sing.
