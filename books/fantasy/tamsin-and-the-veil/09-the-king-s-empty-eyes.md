## 9. The King's Empty Eyes

I had been human for nineteen days when I learned that cities have a smell.

Not the obvious smell — smoke and horse and the sour breath of ten thousand people who have all eaten the same bread. I mean underneath that. Vaelmoor had smelled of iron and old promises. The villages we'd passed through had smelled of nothing at all, which was worse, because nothing was what the Veil left behind.

Caldenhold, the capital, smelled of honey cakes.

That was the first wrong thing. We came over the last ridge at dawn, three travelers road-worn and suspicious as cats, and the wind carried up to us the smell of a festival. Warm sugar. Roasted chestnuts. Fresh-cut garlands of evergreen laid over doorways. Below us the city spilled down its hill like a dropped jewel box, all white walls and copper roofs catching a light that had no business existing, because there were still no stars at night and yet the sun rose every morning as if nothing in the sky had died.

"Someone's having a very good day," Sera said beside me. She had her hood up even though it wasn't raining, because Sera believed hoods were a personality.

"It's the Rite of Renewal," Edmund said quietly. They were doing the thing they did — going soft and faraway, listening to something none of us could hear. A crow on the fence post below tilted its head toward them, then away, like a servant acknowledging an order. "Every year. The whole city turns out for it. They love him."

"Love who?"

"The king." Edmund glanced at me, and I saw them decide, again, not to be afraid of me. It was a kindness, that deciding. Most people didn't bother. "Aldric. He's beloved. Twenty years on the throne and they say he hasn't aged a day."

"Say that again," I said.

Edmund blinked. "He hasn't aged—"

"No. The not-aging part." My hands had gone cold, which was absurd, because I was made of cold. I had been a constellation. I had hung in the dark above this world for longer than this kingdom had existed, and I remembered — dimly, the way you remember a dream from childhood — watching fires burn down here and thinking they were pretty and thinking nothing else about them at all. But some things a star does not forget, even wearing a body that forgets where it put its boots.

"Things that don't age," I said, "are being kept."

* * *

We came into Caldenhold through the Tanner's Gate, because Sera said guards looked at faces and tanners' districts were full of faces nobody wanted to look at. She said it with real affection. I was beginning to understand that Sera loved the ugly parts of things the way other people loved gardens.

The streets swallowed us. And everywhere, everywhere, was joy.

That is not an exaggeration and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the one thing I have left. I am losing memories by the hour — my sisters first, their names going soft in my mouth like fruit past its season, and now smaller things, the color of my mother's — no. Not my mother. Stars don't have mothers. See? That's how it works. It takes a memory and replaces it with a smoother one, a tidier one, until you can't tell which holes are yours.

So: precision. The people of Caldenhold were joyful. Perfectly, uniformly, terribly joyful. A baker laughed at his own joke and his apprentice laughed at the same moment with the same laugh. Two old women argued cheerfully about a ribbon and reached for it at the same instant and touched hands and smiled identical smiles. A child cried in a doorway and her mother knelt and wiped the tears and said *there now* in a voice like warmed milk, and the crying stopped mid-sob, cut off clean, like a door closing.

"Do you hear that?" I whispered.

Edmund nodded slowly. "The birds stopped singing an hour ago. All of them. They're sitting in the trees facing the palace."

"Facing the palace," Sera repeated. "Like an audience."

"Like witnesses," Edmund said.

And me — I stood in the middle of that honey-cake street with festival ribbons strung over my head, and I felt the oldest thing in me go tight and small, because I knew that posture. Birds facing a thing. Silence arranged around a thing like courtiers around a throne. I had seen it before, from very far up, on the night the constellation called the Seven Sisters went out one by one while the world below slept, and the darkness where they'd been did not stay empty.

It filled with something that ate names.

* * *

We found a room above a chandlery because Sera insisted on high ground and Edmund insisted on paying, which led to a brief negotiation in which Sera pointed out that Edmund had stolen the money from a corrupt guildmaster and Edmund pointed out, with the mild stubbornness of a person who has spent ten years hiding behind ledgers, that stolen money spends exactly as well as honest money and buys the same beds.

"The moral accounting can wait," Sera said finally, throwing a coin pouch on the counter. "Everything can wait. Everything always waits. That's the problem with everything."

The chandler's wife gave us a room and a plate of honey cakes — of course honey cakes, the whole city smelled of them — and told us, unprompted and glowing, about the Rite. How the king would walk the Processional Way at noon. How he would bless the harvest-wards. How he had done it every year of his reign and never once stumbled, never once aged, never once — she lowered her voice here, delighted rather than frightened, which was the most frightening part — *changed*.

"Blessed man," she said. "Touched by something holy, my gran used to say. Whatever it is that keeps him, we should all be so lucky."

I bit into a honey cake so I wouldn't say anything. It tasted like ash dressed in sugar. Or possibly it just tasted like honey cake and I am not a reliable narrator of cake anymore.

Upstairs, Sera barred the door and turned to face us, arms crossed, hood still up indoors like a woman daring the ceiling to comment.

"So. The Rite of Renewal." She said the words like she was spitting out gristle. "I've read fragments. Ward-theory texts, pre-collapse. Renewal rites aren't blessings. They're maintenance. Something gets renewed. Something gets *paid*."

"And the king walks the Processional Way," Edmund said slowly, "every year, without aging."

"Without changing," I corrected. "There's a difference. Aging is change. This is the opposite of aging. This is preservation." I sat down on the narrow bed and put my head in my hands, which was a human gesture I had learned recently and found disturbingly effective. "When the Veil took my sisters, it didn't destroy them. Do you understand? It *kept* them. I could feel them for weeks afterward — not gone, not dead. Held. Like flies in amber. Like songs caught in a bottle." I looked up. "Edmund. You cataloged a bottled echo once. In your guild."

Edmund went very still. "Yes."

"What if the king is a bottled echo? What if he has been, for twenty years, and the whole city throws him a parade for it?"

Nobody answered. Downstairs, the chandler's wife began humming. Through the floorboards we could hear it, sweet and steady, and then — I pressed my palm flat against the boards to be sure — the humming changed key, and somewhere across the street another voice picked up the same tune in the same key, and then another, and for four full seconds half the street hummed in perfect unison before scattering back into their separate lives like startled fish.

Sera looked at me. I looked at Edmund. Edmund, very calmly, took off their spectacles, cleaned them on their sleeve, and put them back on, which I had learned was what Edmund did instead of screaming.

"We're going to the rite," Edmund said.

"We absolutely should not go to the rite," Sera said.

"We're going to the rite."

"I hate that I agree with you," Sera said, and started re-buckling her sword belt.

* * *

The Processional Way was a river of people, and we were stones in it.

I want to tell you about the crowd, because the crowd is the horror, more than anything that happened after. There must have been forty thousand souls packed along the white avenue, and every single one of them was happy. Not the ordinary ragged happiness of humans, which is always shot through with worry and hunger and the memory of yesterday's argument. This was smooth. This was lacquered. Faces turned toward the palace like flowers toward sun, and the smiles on them were all the same smile, the way the letters in a printed book are all the same letter.

Only the edges of the crowd were real. Only the latecomers, the ones shoved to the back, the ones too poor or too busy or too stubborn to feel whatever the city was feeling — only they still had crooked, particular, imperfect faces. A fishwife swore at the press of bodies. A guard scratched his nose. A boy stole an apple and grinned around it, guilty and alive.

The center was a lake of glass.

"There," Edmund breathed, and pointed with their chin, because pointing with a hand in a crowd like this was asking to be noticed.

The palace doors opened, and King Aldric came out into the sunlight.

He was beautiful. That is the truth and I report it with fury. He was tall and golden and moved like water finding its level, and the crowd's roar rose up around him like a tide, and he smiled at them with such warmth that I felt it in my borrowed chest, felt my own treacherous heart lean toward him, and I understood in that instant how a city could love the thing that was eating it. He wasn't frightening. That was the trick. Nothing about him was frightening. He was the most comforting sight in the world, a good king in the sun, and the comfort was the cage, and the cage was lined with velvet, and the velvet was lined with teeth.

"He's radiant," Edmund whispered.

"He's vacant," I said.

Because I could see it. Maybe only I could see it — I had been light; I knew what lit-from-within looked like, and Aldric was not lit from within. He was lit from *around*. Something poured through him the way light pours through a stained-glass saint, and the glass itself — the man, the boy he had been, whoever Aldric had been before — was empty. His eyes were blue and kind and utterly unoccupied, windows in a house where no lamps burned, and behind them, deep behind them, something vast turned over in its sleep and looked out through his smile at forty thousand upturned faces.

And then he spoke.

*"My people."*

His mouth shaped the words. But the voice — the voice came from everywhere. From the king's throat, yes, but also from the bells, from the crowd's own chests, from the cobblestones under my feet, from the space between my ribs where a star used to live. It was one voice layered ten thousand times, a chorus pretending to be a soloist, and every person in that square heard it as their own dearest voice, their mother's voice, their own voice at its kindest.

I heard it as my sisters'.

All seven of them, speaking together, saying *my people*, and I nearly went to my knees in the trampled mud of the Processional Way.

"You know it," Edmund murmured. Their hand found my elbow, steadying. "Don't you. You know what that is."

"It's the same," I managed. "It's the same voice that was in the dark where my sisters used to be. It wore their voices to call to me. It wears his voice now." I watched the king raise his arms in blessing, watched forty thousand people surge with love, watched the birds on every rooftop sit motionless and silent, facing him, witnessing. "It doesn't possess him the way your stories say demons do. Demons fight for the wheel. This — it *inhabits*. It settles in like weather. I don't think there's a king in there anymore at all. I think there's a shape the Veil likes to wear, because a beloved king is the best coat it ever found."

The rite continued. The king blessed the harvest-wards — and I felt that, actually felt it, a shiver running through the ground as ancient ward-stones beneath the avenue drank his touch and glowed faintly, briefly, before dimming again like coals left untended. Sera sucked in a breath beside me.

"The wards are starving," she whispered. "Look at them. They're supposed to blaze. They're barely holding a spark. Whatever renewal is supposed to happen here — it isn't happening. It's failing. Has been failing for years."

"Because Corvane sold the name," Edmund said, so quietly only we could hear. "The sacrifice's name. The payment bounced, and nobody noticed, and the debt is coming due."

Above us, the sunless-sky-blue afternoon flickered. Just for a heartbeat — a stutter, like a candle in a draft — and in that heartbeat every shadow in the square stretched long and wrong, all pointing at the king, and the chorus-voice laughed softly, warmly, lovingly, in a register no human throat could make.

Then the sun steadied. The crowd cheered. The king blew a kiss to a child held up on her father's shoulders, and the child beamed, and the father wept with joy, and somewhere inside me a star that no longer existed screamed.

* * *

We got out of the square by inches, swept along with the dispersing crowd, and did not speak until we were three streets away in the shadow of a shuttered dye-house, where the smell of urine and mordant salt mercifully killed the honey cakes.

Sera slid down the wall until she was sitting, which Sera never did, and stared at nothing.

"Twenty years," she said. "Twenty years I served the border wards believing the king's magic flowed down from the capital and held us all together. I took the fall for wards that were already hollow, fed by a hollow king, and the whole time—" She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "The whole time the wellspring was a puppet."

"Not a puppet," I said. I crouched down in front of her. Losing my memories had taught me one thing worth knowing: say the true thing while you still have it. "A puppet fights the strings. He isn't fighting. But Sera — Edmund — listen to me, both of you, because I'm losing hours and I want this said while I'm still me enough to say it." I waited until they were both looking at me. "That thing in the square. I've met it before. Not up close — nothing meets it up close twice. But I know its appetite. It eats names, yes, selves, yes. But what it loves — what it *hunts* — is constellations. Patterns. Things made of many bright parts joined into one meaning. A kingdom is a constellation. Forty thousand people joined into one joy, one voice, one king — that's not its victory. That's its *meal*, being fattened."

Edmund had gone pale. "The city."

"The city. And beyond it, the realm. And it's patient, because stars are patient, and it has eaten stars before." I stood, and my knees were shaking, and I let them shake, because pretending otherwise was a human habit I hadn't acquired yet. "We came here to find out what we're fighting. Now we know. It's bigger than a corrupt guildmaster. It's bigger than broken wards. It is the thing that unmade my sisters, wearing the face everyone in this country loves best."

Silence, except for the distant, lacquered cheering still rolling out of the square.

Then Edmund straightened, and did the spectacle-cleaning thing, and said, in the mild, stubborn, ledger-keeper's voice that I had come, against all my stellar judgment, to trust more than any prayer:

"Then we need the royal archive. Tonight. Because if the binding runs on names, and the binding is failing, then somewhere in that palace there's a record of the original payment — and I am very, very good at records."

From the direction of the palace, faint and sweet, forty thousand voices began to sing the same song in perfect unison.

And under it, so low only I could hear it, the chorus laughed — and in the laughter, clear as a bell, I heard my sisters' names, spoken tenderly, like a promise it intended to keep.
