## 10. Ink and Old Promises

The royal archive smelled the way old promises smell: dust, tallow, and the faint sourness of ink that had outlived everyone who'd written it.

Edmund stood in the third gallery of the Undercroft, where the crown kept the records it preferred not to read aloud, and tried not to sneeze. A single lantern hung from a chain above the reading table, swaying whenever the building breathed—which, in Vaelmoor's capital these days, was more often than it should have. Buildings weren't supposed to breathe. Edmund had been making a list of things that weren't supposed to happen anymore, and the list had outgrown its page.

"Find anything?" Sera's voice drifted up from the gallery below, along with the scrape of her boots on stone. She moved through archives the way she moved through everything—like she expected them to attack her.

"I've found," Edmund called back, "that the royal archivists of the last two hundred years were either geniuses or cowards, and I suspect both."

"What makes you say that?"

"Because every document that might explain what the Veil *is* has been misfiled." Edmund lifted a ledger bound in cracked green leather, its spine stamped with a catalog number that belonged to a shelf four galleries away. "Consistently. For centuries. Misfiled into the same locked case marked *Miscellaneous, Agricultural*. Someone wanted these hard to find but impossible to lose."

Sera appeared at the top of the stairs, a candle stub in one hand and her warden's coat hanging off her like a borrowed opinion. Her hair was still wet from the cistern they'd washed in that morning. "Agricultural," she repeated flatly.

"There are three treatises on binding-craft filed between a survey of turnip yields and a complaint about goats."

"The goats' or about them?"

"It's unclear. The handwriting gives up halfway through."

Above them, faintly, came the sound of Lyra being furious at something. She had claimed the map room on the grounds that maps were "the closest thing this city has to stars," and had spent the morning cross-referencing constellations against parish boundaries with the intensity of someone settling a personal grudge. Every so often a drawer would slam, and a small shower of plaster would drift down through the ceiling grate like sad snow.

Edmund opened the green ledger and felt the hair on their arms rise.

The first page was written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. There was no title. There didn't need to be. The opening line did all the work:

*In the year of the First King's crowning, the thing beneath the sky was given a meal, and the meal was names.*

Edmund sat down slowly.

"Sera," they said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, the way sound goes when a room is listening. "Come look at this."

* * *

She read over their shoulder for a long time without touching anything, which was how Sera showed respect: by pretending she wasn't there.

The account was written by an unnamed scribe—the first king's own, judging by the intimate, exhausted tone—and it described the founding bargain in language that circled the truth like a horse circling a snake.

*The Veil cannot be killed,* the scribe wrote, *for it is not alive. It can only be fed. The First King offered it a sacrifice of names—not one name, but many, gathered from those who loved him freely given, and woven together into a single word of binding. The Veil ate, and slept, and the king set wards upon the borders of the realm like stitches in a wound.*

*But a wound stitched must be re-stitched. The binding weakens each generation, as all debts do. And so each generation must renew it: a new sacrifice of names, freely given, gathered by the king's own hand, spoken into the dark at the turning of the age.*

*Freely given. Mark this well, whoever reads after me. The Veil knows the difference between a gift and a theft. Feed it stolen names and it will only grow hungrier.*

Edmund turned pages with fingers that didn't feel entirely their own. The ledger continued through the reigns—each renewal recorded in a different hand, some neat, some hurried, one written by someone whose hands were clearly shaking. Names were never listed. That was the point. The sacrifice's names were spoken once, into the Veil itself, and then deliberately forgotten by everyone who knew them, so that no thief could ever steal what no memory held.

Except, apparently, the memory of whoever gathered them.

"Here," Sera said suddenly, tapping a passage near the end. "Read the marginal note."

Someone—a later archivist, by the ink—had scrawled in the margin beside the record of the last successful renewal, ninety years past:

*Renewal due again within the current reign. The gathering falls to the crown. Pray the crown remembers its duty.*

"And the current reign," Edmund said quietly, "is Aldric's."

They looked at each other.

"Aldric was bound young," Sera said. "Everyone says it. Bound before his majority, during the plague years. If the renewal was supposed to happen under his hand—" She stopped. Edmund watched her arrive at the same place they had. "He couldn't have done it. He was a child, and then he was a mouthpiece. So who gathered the names?"

"Maybe no one." Edmund flipped forward, looking for any record of a renewal in the last twenty years. The pages thinned. The entries became inventory lists, grain tariffs, a memo about the palace chimneys. Whoever should have been writing about the binding had stopped writing about the binding entirely, somewhere around the time a boy king's eyes went empty and radiant.

Or—

Edmund's stomach turned over with a slow, cold lurch.

Or someone *had* gathered the names. Someone had done the work, held the sacrifice ready, waited for the turning of the age. And then, somewhere between the gathering and the speaking, the names had gone *missing*.

Sold.

"You're thinking what I'm thinking," Sera said. It wasn't a question.

"Corvane." Saying the name aloud felt like pressing on a bruise. "He's been selling names to the Veil's agents for years. I watched him do it. I stole his ledger of it. What if the guild's trade didn't start with small names? What if, years ago, a hooded buyer came to Vaelmoor asking after something very specific, very valuable—and Corvane, being Corvane, saw profit instead of catastrophe?"

Sera was quiet for a moment. In the silence, Lyra slammed another drawer upstairs, and plaster sifted down.

"We need proof," she said finally. "Not suspicion. Proof. Because if we walk up to a warden-commander—or worse, if we walk up to whatever's left of the court—and say 'the guildmaster ate the kingdom's safety net,' they'll ask us how we know, and 'a horse told me' is going to go badly for you."

"It always does," Edmund said.

"But you have his ledger."

"I have his ledger." Edmund had carried it against their ribs for six days now, wrapped in oilcloth, like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn them. "But it records sales, not purposes. A name sold here, a name sold there. No entry says *this one was the sacrifice.*"

"Then we find the entry that does." Sera straightened, and something in her face shifted—that hard, hungry focus Edmund was beginning to recognize as the real Sera Voss, the one who existed underneath the disgrace and the drink. "Corvane kept two books. Every merchant does. The sales ledger tells us what he sold. Somewhere there's a purchase ledger, or a correspondence file, or—" her eyes went distant, running through a smuggler's logic Edmund could almost hear clicking into place, "—or a *receipt*. Nobody sells the most valuable thing they'll ever sell without keeping proof it was theirs to sell. Not because of pride. Because of fear. He'd want insurance against the buyer coming back and saying *you never owned that name at all.*"

"So we're looking for Corvane's paperwork."

"We are looking," Sera said, with the ghost of a smile, "for a goat-farmer's filing system in a turnip archive. Welcome to wardenship."

* * *

It took them until well past midnight.

Lyra came down eventually, drawn by the smell of the tallow candles and the promise of a mystery, and turned out to be terrifyingly useful. She couldn't read the old script—but she could *weigh* it. "This page is lying," she'd announce, passing her palm over a folio. "This one's true but boring. This one is true and *afraid*." She could apparently taste the age of ink the way Edmund tasted weather. When Edmund asked how, she shrugged and said, "Stars remember everything they've shone on. Some of it rubs off." Then she'd gone quiet in the particular way she went quiet when she remembered her sisters, and Edmund had learned not to fill those silences.

It was Lyra who found the case.

She stopped dead in the fourth gallery, near a shelf of tax rolls nobody had touched since before the plague, and said, "This one's *loud*."

"Loud," Edmund repeated.

"Old-loud. Like a bell that rang once and never got permission to stop." She pointed at a slim folio wedged behind the tax rolls, bound in plain grey leather with no marking at all—which, in a building where even the goats had a catalog number, was its own kind of shout.

Edmund drew it out. The leather was cool in a way the room wasn't. Inside, in Corvane's unmistakable hand—Edmund had spent ten years forging adjacent entries in that hand, and knew its little vanities, the way he crossed his sevens like he was stabbing them—was a single letter, copied fair and kept.

*To the honored agent of the quiet market, greetings.*

*You inquire after a certain gathering, held in trust by the crown's agents in Vaelmoor. I confirm the article exists. I confirm it is intact: nine names, freely given, gathered per the old rite by the late Gatherer, whose duties passed to my guild upon her death. I confirm I am empowered to negotiate.*

*I will be plain, since you value plainness. The article is worth more than gold to your principal, and worth nothing at all to anyone else, which places me at a disadvantage I do not enjoy. Name a price worthy of the risk I take in parting with it. I remind you that I know what happens to those who deal falsely with your principal, and I assume you know what happens to those who deal falsely with me.*

*Awaiting your figure,*

*C.G., Gilded Scale Guild, Vaelmoor*

Below it, in a different hand—a hand that made Edmund's skin crawl to look at, because the letters seemed to shift slightly when unobserved, like insects under cloth—was the reply:

*Your figure is accepted. Deliverance at the turning of the season. The principal notes, for your future guidance, that the article was never yours to sell. The principal forgives this, once. The principal does not forgive twice.*

*The debt is noted.*

Edmund read it three times. The words didn't get better with repetition.

"Nine names," Sera said softly. "Freely given. Gathered by 'the late Gatherer.'" She exhaled through her teeth. "There was a person whose whole office was holding the kingdom's ransom in her head. And when she died, the office passed to—" she tapped the letters C.G. with one finger, hard enough to sting, "—*him*. And he sold it. He sold the renewal. He sold the stitch in the world's wound for whatever they paid him, and then he sat back and waited for the wards to fail so he could profit off the panic too."

"That's why the renewal failed," Edmund said. Their voice sounded far away. "That's why the Veil woke hungry. It wasn't just that Aldric was bound and couldn't gather new names. The old names—the ones meant to carry the binding across this generation—were *eaten early*. Sold to the thing they were meant to bind. It didn't just miss its meal, Sera. It was handed its meal, and then the meal was taken away, and now it's—" Edmund searched for the image and found it in the guild stables, in the eyes of a mare separated too long from her foal. "Now it's come to collect."

The lantern swung. Somewhere above them, the building breathed again, a long structural sigh that no building should make.

Lyra had gone very still, her hand flat on the table. "The debt is noted," she repeated. "That's what it calls everything. Debts. My sisters weren't devoured. They were *collected*." Her voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than screaming. "It keeps accounts. It has always kept accounts."

"Then we keep better ones," Edmund said, and heard, to their surprise, steel in their own voice—the voice of someone who had cataloged impossible trades for ten years and finally understood what all of it had been training for. "We have his confession. We have the reply. We have the sales ledger. Three documents that together prove the guildmaster of Vaelmoor sold the binding of the realm to the thing currently wearing the king. That's not a story anymore. That's a case."

"Cases need courts," Sera said grimly. "Courts need to still exist. Have you seen the state of the capital's courts lately? Half the magistrates smile too much and don't blink."

"Then we don't need a court. We need witnesses. Loud ones." Edmund began wrapping the folio in oilcloth beside the sales ledger, hands steady now, heart hammering. "Beasts testify. Hundreds of them. Rats who watched the hooded buyers come and go. Ravens who heard Corvane's window conversations. If I stand in a public square and let every creature in this city speak through me—"

"If you do that, every faceless clerk in the capital will know exactly where you are within the hour," Sera said. "You'd be painting a target on yourself in letters a mile high."

"I know."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"I'm going to *want* to do it anyway," Edmund corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that first we get this evidence somewhere it can't be burned, eaten, or misfiled under agriculture."

It was at this point—because the universe, in Edmund's long experience, had the comic timing of a drunk jester—that the lantern went out.

Not guttered. Not blown. Went out, all at once, like a name being crossed off a list, and the darkness that rushed in was thicker than a cellar's, thick enough to chew.

Edmund heard Sera's knife leave its sheath. Heard Lyra's breath catch with a faint crackle, like a struck flint. And beneath it all, rising from the stairwell that led up to the archive's single door, came footsteps. Many footsteps. Perfectly synchronized. The sound of people who had all agreed, at some point, to stop being people.

And a voice—polite, papery, coming from everywhere at once:

*"Archives close at dusk. Your debt is noted."*
