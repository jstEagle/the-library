## 11. Hunted by Your Own Voice

Sera Voss had spent eleven years learning to read a city the way other people read weather. Vaelmoor taught you quickly or it killed you: which alleys drained before the rains came, which taverns watered their ale and which watered their patrons, which guards could be bribed with coin and which required flattery first. She had thought herself fluent.

She was not fluent in this.

The clerks had been in the city for three days now, and they moved wrong. Not suspiciously — that she could have handled. Suspicion was a language she spoke like a native. No, they moved *correctly*, each one walking at precisely the pace of an unremarkable man going about unremarkable business, and that was the tell. Nobody in Vaelmoor was unremarkable on purpose. Everyone here performed some small theater of hurry or laziness or grievance. The clerks did none of it. They were grey men in grey coats with faces that Sera's eyes kept sliding off of, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth.

"They're using the ledger," Edmund said.

They stood in the loft above a tannery — Sera's idea; nothing cleared a room faster than the smell of three hundred hides curing in lime — and below them, through the floorboards, the tanners worked and swore and sang badly, blessedly human noises. Edmund sat cross-legged among Sera's collection of ward-chalk and stolen maps, turning the ledger over in their hands as though it might bite. It probably wanted to.

"Of course they're using the ledger," Sera said. "It's a list of names sold to an entity that eats names. It's practically a dinner bell."

"No, I mean—" Edmund's fingers tightened on the cover. "I mean they're using *my* handwriting. Look."

They passed it over, open to a page near the middle. Sera had seen the ledger before, had helped steal it, had watched Edmund copy entries by candlelight with the grim efficiency of someone defusing a bomb. But she hadn't looked closely. Now she did, and her stomach dropped through six floors of tannery.

The entries continued past the point where Edmund had stopped copying. Same hand. Same careful slant, same little hooked serifs on the capital letters. Dates from this week. Names she didn't recognize, followed by prices, followed by a column headed simply *delivered*, with checkmarks in it.

"That's not possible," Sera said. "You wrote up to—"

"I know where I stopped." Edmund's voice was very quiet, the way it always went when the beast-speaker gift was doing something to them. Sera had learned to dread that quiet. "I stopped at the Aldermoor entry. This is three weeks past that. And it's my hand, Sera. It's not a forgery. Forgeries get the letterforms right and miss the rhythm. This has the rhythm." They swallowed. "Something is writing with my voice. Or my voice is writing for something."

Outside, distantly, a dog began to bark — then cut off mid-sound, the way beasts had been cutting off all week, and Sera felt the silence land on the city like a dropped blanket.

"Right," she said, because someone had to say something practical, and apparently it was always going to be her. "New rule. We don't touch the ledger anymore. We don't carry it. We don't look at it fondly. As far as I'm concerned it's a cursed object and we treat it like one."

"It's evidence," Edmund said. "It's the only proof of what Corvane did."

"It's bait," Sera said. "We are fish, and it is bait, and I have been a warden long enough to know how that story ends for the fish."

Edmund looked at her with that expression they got — the one that was half guilt and half stubbornness, like a mule deciding whether to feel bad about refusing to move. "You think I don't know that? Every name in there is a person who got eaten, Sera. Some of them are probably still *in* it. If there's a way to pull them back out—"

"Then we'll find it after we're not being hunted by faceless men who write in your handwriting." Sera took the ledger out of their hands, and it was heavier than paper had any right to be, and she hated it, and she wrapped it in oilcloth anyway because Edmund was right too, damn them both. "We hide it. We go north. We find Lyra's crater-constellation friends or whatever's left of the archive leads, and we solve the actual problem instead of dying in an alley over stationery."

Edmund almost smiled. "Stationery."

"I'm frightened," Sera said. "When I'm frightened I get precise. Ask anyone I've ever arrested."

* * *

The plan was simple, which should have been the first warning. Simple plans assumed a rational world, and the world had stopped being rational around the time the stars went out.

There was a woman named Odile who ran a barge down the Mirren canal, north toward the hill country, and who owed Sera a favor dating back to the incident with the smuggler and the bear, which Sera refused to discuss and Odile never stopped discussing. The barge left at dawn from the third lock. All they had to do was reach it unseen.

Which was why, at half past four in the morning, Sera found herself crouched in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery watching a grey-coated clerk stand motionless in the rain-slick street, facing the exact spot where she and Edmund needed to cross.

"He can't see us," Edmund whispered. "Can he?"

"He doesn't need to see us. He knows where we are. That's worse." Sera counted doors, rooflines, distances. Old habits. "He's standing at the crossing point. Not patrolling it. Standing. Like he's been told: they will come through here."

"Because the ledger told him?"

"Because the ledger is you, and you're predictable." She said it gently, mostly. "You're a creature of habit wearing a disguise. You always cross at the bridge-side because the other way smells like the glue works. You hum when you're nervous. You—"

"I do not hum."

"You hum. Badly. Something with a lot of minor keys. The point is—" Sera stopped. Because the clerk had turned his head.

Not toward them. Toward the canal. And then he did the thing that Sera would remember for the rest of her life, in fragments, usually at night: he opened his mouth, and where a face should have been there was simply more coat-collar, shadow folding into shadow, and from the place where his mouth wasn't, a voice came out.

It was Edmund's voice.

"*Sera,*" it said, warm and a little wry, pitched exactly the way Edmund sounded when they'd just thought of something clever. "*The other way. Through the glue works. Trust me.*"

Beside her, Edmund went rigid. "That's—" they started, and their actual voice cracked in the middle of the word, and the clerk-thing's borrowed voice smiled — Sera heard the smile, somehow, in the shape of the vowels — and said, in Edmund's voice again:

"*See? Told you.*"

"Don't," Sera breathed. "Whatever it says next, it's lying. It's fishing. It wants us to move so it can follow the sound."

But Edmund had gone pale in the pre-dawn grey, and when they spoke it was barely a whisper. "It knows about the glue works. I've never told anyone about the glue works. I haven't even *thought* about it out loud since—" They pressed the back of their hand against their mouth. "Sera, it's not guessing. It's reading me. The ledger isn't a list. It's a *tuning fork*. Everything I am, it can ring."

And here is the thing Sera would lie awake examining later, the thing she turned over like a coin worn smooth: in that moment, she made the calculation every warden makes and hates. Two of them. One exit worth taking. One of them was carrying the gift that the enemy could track through ink and memory, and one of them was carrying ten years of ward-craft and a professional relationship with running away.

She didn't decide. Her body decided, the way it had decided during the border collapse, during the gutter shrine, during every bad night of her disgrace. Her body put itself between Edmund and the street.

"Go," she said. "Glue works. Don't argue, you argue like a filing cabinet. Go north, get on Odile's barge, tell her the bear owes me. I'll meet you at the third lock."

"Sera—"

"*Go.*"

And Edmund — bless them, curse them, stubborn mule — went, but only after grabbing her sleeve and saying, fierce and fast, "Third lock. You swear it. Swear it on something you actually care about, not on your honor, your honor's a mess—"

"On the bear," Sera said. "I swear on the bear. Now run."

Edmund ran. Sera turned to face the street, drew her old warden's knife — ceremonial, honestly, more decoration than edge, but a knife is a knife is an argument — and stepped out into the open where the clerk-thing could hear her boots.

"Evening," she said loudly. "Lovely weather for impersonation."

Three more grey coats detached themselves from doorways she had already checked. Four of them. Fine. She'd fought worse odds with better weapons, though admittedly not recently and possibly never.

The nearest one opened its absent mouth and spoke in her own voice — her younger voice, the one from before the disgrace, bright with a confidence she barely remembered owning: "*Warden Voss. You're under no arrest. We'd just like to talk about the ledger.*"

"Yeah," Sera said, backing toward the mouth of the alley behind her, buying seconds, counting them like coins. "Everybody wants to talk about the ledger. Nobody wants to talk about the bear. Story of my life."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being captured by entities that eat names: it doesn't hurt. That's the horror of it. They pinned her arms — hands like cold paper — and one of them leaned its faceless face close, and it *read* her. Not searched. Read, the way you'd read a document, unhurried, thorough, occasionally pausing as if to enjoy a passage.

She felt it take things. Small things first. The name of her childhood street. The smell of her mother's kitchen — gone, just *gone*, a hole shaped like bread. Her first posting. Her first command. Each extraction left a smooth blank space, painless, tidy, like a tooth pulled by a skilled hand while you were distracted.

She fought it the only way she could: by talking.

"You know what your problem is?" she said, as they dragged her down the alley toward a waiting cart with no driver. "You're terrible at your jobs. Truly. I've audited border garrisons with better operational security than you lot. You let two civilians steal a ledger off the most compromised guildmaster in the realm. Corvane has been selling names out from under you for *years* and you didn't notice until a bookkeeper with a hobby noticed for you. If I still had my badge I'd write you all up."

The clerk holding her arm paused in its reading. In her own stolen voice, it said, curious: "*Why do you do that?*"

"Do what?"

"*Talk. When it changes nothing.*"

Sera laughed, and it came out ragged, and she was surprised to find it was real laughter, genuine, rising out of some deep stubborn seam in her that apparently even name-eating couldn't reach. "Because it's mine," she said. "The talking. You can take the streets and the kitchen and the badge, but every word out of my mouth is one I chose, and you can't unread a choice, can you? You eat names. A name is what other people call you. This—" she jerked her chin at her own moving mouth, "—this is what I call me. Different inventory. Hands off."

For a moment — she would swear this later, and Lyra would believe her, and Edmund would hold her hand and say *of course* — the clerks went still. All four of them. Like a choir losing its place.

Then the cart rolled up, and they loaded her in, and the last thing she saw was the dawn coming up grey over Vaelmoor's rooftops, and the last thing she thought was: *third lock. I swore on the bear.*

* * *

Edmund did not go to the third lock.

Sera knew this the way you know things without knowing how you know them, the way she'd known the wards were failing a full day before they fell — pattern recognition, the warden's disease. She knew it because the cart had gone perhaps four streets when the noise started behind them: first a dog, barking in that urgent, organized way dogs have when they're relaying information rather than expressing opinion. Then another. Then a horse screaming somewhere near the canal, high and furious, and — faintly, impossibly, carried on the morning air — a human voice shouting words that were not words to anyone but her, because she'd spent a week learning what Edmund sounded like when they were terrified and doing the terrifying thing anyway.

"Oh, you absolute *idiot*," Sera said, with enormous tenderness, to no one who could hear her.

The clerks reacted like a hive stung. Two of them peeled away, back toward the canal, coats fluttering wrong, and the cart lurched forward faster, and Sera sat in the dark of it doing arithmetic. Edmund alone: dead or eaten within the hour. Edmund plus whatever the beasts of Vaelmoor could throw between them and four faceless clerks: unknown, but the dogs had sounded *organized*, and there were a great many dogs in Vaelmoor, and every rat and raven and stablehorse for ten miles had spent a week whispering to a bookkeeper who listened.

And the mission — the barge, the north, the archive leads, the whole fragile thread of it — died right there, because Edmund had chosen her over it, and if she let that choice be wasted then loyalty was just a word people said at funerals.

The cart turned onto the processional road, heading — she guessed — for wherever the Veil kept its paperwork. There would be a building. There was always a building. Bureaucracies, even eldritch ones, needed roofs.

Sera tested the ropes on her wrists. Warden's knots — she'd tied a thousand of them, and whoever had tied these had learned from ledgers, not from living hands, and their tension was textbook and their security was theoretical. She worked a wrist bone loose. Then another.

Above the rooftops, where the stars should have been, the empty sky seemed to lean closer.

"Hold on, Ed," she muttered, and got her thumb free. "I swore on the bear."

Somewhere across the city, a thousand animals went silent at once — and then, all together, in one voice that no living person heard and every living person felt, they began to scream.
