## 12. The Bargaining Floor

The entrance to the Veil's market was, of all things, a bakery.

Edmund stood across the street from it for the better part of an hour, hood up, ledger strapped against their ribs beneath a coat two sizes too large, watching flour-dusted apprentices carry trays in and out. The shop sat at the bottom of a crooked lane in the capital's oldest quarter, wedged between a coffin-maker and a moneylender, its windows glowing with the honest amber light of fresh bread. A chalkboard by the door advertised honey rolls and seed loaves in cheerful, looping script.

It was the cheerfulness that gave it away. Nobody in this quarter could afford honey rolls.

Also, there were no rats anywhere near it. Edmund had spent ten years among caged animals; they had learned to read absence the way sailors read weather. Every rat within three streets had gone to ground, and the pigeons circled the bakery's chimney in wide, nervous arcs, refusing to land.

*Something under there,* a gull had told them from a rooftop, preening. *Something old and hungry and polite about it.*

Polite hunger was, Edmund reflected, exactly what they were walking into.

They crossed the street before their courage could finish cooling. The bell above the bakery door chimed—bright, domestic, obscene—and the warmth inside hit them like a wall: cinnamon, woodsmoke, the low murmur of ordinary commerce. A woman behind the counter smiled at them with too many teeth and asked what they'd like.

"Nothing baked," Edmund said, and set a copper coin on the counter, face down. Stamped on its reverse was a moth—the guild's mark for *sold, not traded*. Corvane had taught them that code without ever knowing he'd taught them anything at all. Ten years of watching him do business through a ledger's keyhole. It turned out betrayal made excellent study material.

The woman's smile didn't change, but something behind her eyes did—a shutter closing over a window. "Downstairs," she said. "Mind the third step. It lies."

The stairs went down much further than the building should have allowed. Edmund counted forty-one steps and stopped counting when the walls began to breathe.

Not metaphorically. The stone flexed, very slightly, in and out, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and the candle sconces burned with flames that leaned toward Edmund as they passed, curious as cats. By the twentieth step the air had changed flavor entirely: bread above, and below it something like ink, and beneath *that*, faint as a name half-remembered on waking, the smell of cold stars.

The market opened up around them all at once.

It was built inside what might once have been a cellar and might now be the inside of something's throat. Stalls spiraled down into darkness, lit by lanterns that gave off more shadow than light, and the merchants—Edmund made themselves look at them, made themselves count them like inventory—were mostly human-shaped. Mostly. Their faces were smooth where faces should have features, like eggs, like thumbs pressed into dough before baking, and yet somehow Edmund knew each one was smiling.

Faceless clerks. The things that had hunted them since the guild. The things that had taken Sera.

A stall sold bottled echoes—Edmund recognized the craft instantly, the same make as the one they'd cataloged a lifetime ago in chapter one of their own quiet life. Another sold secondhand regrets, decanted into clay jars and labeled in a handwriting that hurt to read. A third offered *names, lightly used, some wear on the vowels.*

And everywhere, threaded through the crowd of buyers—some human, some not, some wearing their humanity like ill-fitting coats—ran the sound of bargaining. Haggling. Laughter, even. That was the worst part. It sounded almost like the Gilded Scale on a market day. It sounded almost like home.

*You belong here,* whispered a voice that was many voices, coming from nowhere and everywhere, warm as fresh bread. *Look how well you fit.*

"I don't," Edmund said out loud, and kept walking.

* * *

The broker's stall sat at the exact center of the spiral, which meant it sat at the center of everything, which meant Edmund had to walk past every temptation in creation to reach it. They kept their eyes forward. They thought about Sera in a cell somewhere below or beyond or *between*, and that got them through the stall selling perfect lives, and the stall selling forgiveness, and the one—gods help them—that sold silence, guaranteed permanent, priced by weight of grief.

The broker was waiting.

It looked like a clerk who had studied humans from a great distance and gotten most of it right: gray robes, gray skin, spectacles it did not need perched on a nose it did not quite have. Its hands were folded on a counter of black glass, and on the counter lay a ledger.

Sera's ledger. Edmund knew it at fifty paces—knew the water-stain on the cover, the way the spine cracked when opened flat. Sera had carried it through six years of disgrace the way other people carried prayer beads.

"You're late," the broker said. Its voice was one voice, which was worse than the chorus. One voice meant it was bothering to be *individual* for you. "We expected you four days ago. We were starting to worry you'd done something sensible."

"I had to sell my horse." This was true. Edmund had sold their horse to a carter with kind eyes and lied to the beast about coming back, and the guilt of it still sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. "I'm told punctuality is less important than delivery."

"Mm." The broker opened Sera's ledger with one finger and turned it around so Edmund could see the page. Sera's name was written there in ink that moved, slow as oil. Not erased. *Held.* "Warden Voss is in excellent condition. Barely used. She argued the entire way down, which we always find refreshing. So." The egg-smooth face tilted. "Your offer."

Edmund unslung the coat, unwrapped the oilcloth, and laid the stolen ledger of sold names on the counter of black glass.

Two hundred and eleven names. Two hundred and eleven people Corvane had fed to the Veil's agents over nine years to keep the Gilded Scale prosperous—traders, rivals, a wardens' captain, a child who'd witnessed a delivery. Edmund had copied every entry in their own careful bookkeeper's hand, because the original was hidden somewhere only they would ever find it, and because a good trader never brings their whole stock to market.

"This," Edmund said, "for both of them. The warden and the ledger she carries. Clean trades, no residue, no follow-up. You know I know how to write a contract."

The broker looked at the ledger for a long time. Around them, the market seemed to lean in. Even the shadows cast by the shadows went still.

Then the broker laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. That was the horror of it. It was the warm, rueful laugh of a colleague hearing a junior's first offer, and it made Edmund's stomach drop through the breathing floor.

"Oh, *bookkeeper*," the broker said. "Sit. Please. Let's do this properly, since you've come all this way and clearly prepared." It produced two stools from nowhere. Edmund sat, because sitting was a thing traders did, and hating themselves for it. "Here is what your ledger is worth. Names freely given are currency. Names *stolen*—" it tapped the cover, "—are counterfeit. Oh, they spend. But every one of these will eventually claw its way back out of us, and we'll be two hundred and eleven names poorer, and you'll be long gone, and we will remember that. We always remember." It folded its hands again. "So. Counteroffer."

"I'm listening."

"The ledger stays yours. Keep it. Bury it. Publish it in the town square if you like—it won't matter after tomorrow night, but it will make you feel righteous, and righteousness is free." The broker leaned forward, and though it had no eyes, Edmund felt itself being *read*, thoroughly, the way they read a ledger line by line. "Bring us the king's binding-name instead. Freely given, verifiably his, delivered before the renewal rite concludes tomorrow midnight. Do that, and Warden Voss walks out of here whole, and you walk out with her, and this account between us closes forever."

The market roared softly, like a shell held to the ear.

"That's not a trade," Edmund said. Their voice came out steadier than they deserved. "That's me doing your work for you. The binding-name is the last thing keeping Aldric bound on *your* terms instead of no terms at all. You want it because it lets you renegotiate the whole contract."

"Yes," the broker agreed pleasantly. "That is generally why one trades."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the warden goes into the general stock." The broker gestured, gently, at the stalls spiraling away into the dark. "She has useful skills. Ward-craft ages well. And you go back up the stairs with your counterfeit ledger and your clean conscience, and in—" it consulted nothing at all, "—thirty-one hours, none of it will signify. The rite renews. The king remains ours. The city keeps smiling its hollow smile, and you keep hiding in your ledgers until there's nothing left of you to hide." A pause, delicately placed. "Unless you'd rather be stock yourself. We pay well for beast-speakers. Very rare. The last one we processed was four hundred years ago, and honestly, the quality has declined since."

Edmund sat very still.

This was the moment, they understood dimly, that the whole shape of their life had been bending toward. Ten years of silence. Ten years of making themselves small and legible and harmless, of cataloging impossible things so they'd never have to *be* one. And here was the floor of the world, and here was the choice, and it wasn't courage versus cowardice at all. It was arithmetic. It had always been arithmetic.

Sera, arguing the whole way down. Sera, who had looked at Edmund's gift—the thing Edmund hated most about themselves—and called it *the single most useful damn talent in the realm, so stop apologizing for it.* Sera, who was currently ink on a page in a monster's inventory.

There was no version of walking away that Edmund could live inside.

"Before the rite concludes," Edmund said. "Midnight tomorrow. The binding-name, freely given, verified. And Voss released to me at the door, not after. I don't deliver goods against goods still held."

"Half now, half after," said the broker. "She waits in our back room until delivery. Standard practice."

"Standard practice," Edmund repeated, tasting the words like spoiled milk. Then, because a good trader knows when to stop pushing: "Done."

They shook on it. The broker's hand was cool and dry and closed around Edmund's like a ledger snapping shut, and somewhere in the contact Edmund felt the contract write itself into them—a small cold signature behind the breastbone, countersigned.

*There it is,* they thought. *Ten years avoiding becoming Corvane, and it took me one evening.*

*You were always going to trade,* said the many-voiced whisper, kindly, terribly. *Everyone does, eventually. The only question is what.*

* * *

The broker walked them personally to the foot of the stairs—not courtesy, Edmund understood, but display. The merchants along the spiral turned their smooth faces as Edmund passed, and the whispers followed:

*A beast-speaker.*
*Working *with* us.*
*How the mighty.*

At the base of the stairs, the broker paused. "A word of professional advice, bookkeeper."

Edmund said nothing.

"The binding-name of Aldric the Ninth has been lost for thirty years. Even *we* haven't found it, and we have found everything else. So either you know something remarkable"—the smooth face managed, somehow, to convey a raised eyebrow—"or you intend to cheat us. And I want you to know, sincerely, that we respect both possibilities enormously. Cheating us is nearly always fatal, but it is *never* boring."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"One more thing." The broker's voice dropped, and for just a moment the one voice frayed at the edges and became the chorus underneath, vast and patient and old beyond the concept of patience. "*We took the warden because she was bait for you. We let you find the market because we wanted you here. Everything you have done since the wards fell, you have done on paths we paved.*" The chorus receded; the pleasant clerk returned. "But do come back at midnight. We'll have the kettle on."

Edmund climbed the forty-one steps. The third step lied; they stepped over it.

* * *

Outside, the bakery smelled of cinnamon and the street smelled of rain, and Edmund stood in the drizzle for a long moment simply breathing air that belonged to the sky. Above the rooftops, where stars should have been, there was only the flat black ceiling the Veil had hung over the world—but Lyra had sworn the darkness was thinner tonight. Thinner, and in one place, far north, faintly bruised with light. Her sisters, maybe. Or her sisters' graves.

*I lied to it,* Edmund realized, walking fast, hood down now, letting the rain find their face. *I shook its hand and signed its contract and I lied.*

Because they didn't have the binding-name. They had a rumor, a dead queen, a bottled echo locked in the Gilded Scale vaults that they'd cataloged years ago and never forgotten—*echo, royal provenance, contents: a woman's voice speaking a name, do not open, do not appraise*—and a plan so thin that hope barely clung to it.

But the broker was right about one thing. Edmund had learned trading from the best liar in Vaelmoor.

They just hadn't expected to become him.

Somewhere below the bakery, in a back room between the walls of the world, Sera Voss was waiting, arguing with the dark. Hold on, Edmund thought at her, uselessly, fiercely. One more day. I'm coming back with the worst bargain anyone ever struck—

—and then, ahead of them, at the mouth of the crooked lane, a rat stood up on its haunches in the rain and looked directly at them, which rats did not do, and said in a small urgent voice:

"*They're following you already. Three of them. Run.*"
