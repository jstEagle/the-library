## 13. Midnight in the Menagerie

The royal menagerie smelled of hay, old straw, and the particular melancholy of animals kept for looking at rather than loving.

Edmund crouched in the shadow of a peacock's abandoned pavilion—abandoned because the peacock had been eaten during the siege months, and no one had bothered to mention this to the pavilion—and tried to make themselves smaller than a ledger clerk had any right to be. Above them, the palace walls rose pale against a sky that still refused to hold stars. Below, past the sleeping gardens, the great doors of the throne room glowed faintly, as if something behind them were breathing light instead of air.

Three days since the broker's bargain. Three days of Sera's silence, Lyra's fading, and Edmund's own conscience chewing on itself like a rat in a grain sack.

*Bring me the king's binding-name,* the broker had said, in that voice like a library falling down stairs, *and your warden goes free.*

Edmund had lied and agreed. Now they were hiding among cages to figure out what they'd actually promised.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice from the darkness.

Edmund's heart performed a maneuver that would have gotten a lesser organ dismissed from service. Then they realized: the voice hadn't come through ears. It came the way all beast-speech came, warm and low, like a thought that wasn't quite yours.

"Doing what wrong?" Edmund whispered, aloud, out of habit.

"Hiding. You're breathing like a bellows and you keep checking over your left shoulder when anyone sensible would check the right." A shape unfolded from the straw beyond the pavilion—an enormous hound, gray-muzzled, one ear crumpled from some ancient argument with a boar. He regarded Edmund with eyes gone cloudy at the edges but sharp at the center. "Also, you smell like fear and cheap ink. The ink is new. Most of the frightened ones just smell of fear."

"I'm not—" Edmund stopped. There was no point lying to a hound. There was rarely any point lying to anything that could hear your heartbeat. "I'm not most of the frightened ones."

"No," the hound agreed. "You're the one who talks to us. The stablehorse told everyone about you, you know. After the night the stars went out. She's been insufferable. *I knew his true name before he did,* she says. As if that's an achievement. She also knows the name of every horse who's ever been stabled next to her, and she brings that up constantly."

Despite everything—the terror, the stolen ledger burning a hole through Edmund's coat like a coal, the memory of Sera's face as the clerks took her—a laugh escaped. Small, cracked, but real.

"What do I call you?" Edmund asked.

"Bramble," said the hound. "Though the king used to call me Old Man, which I resented at fourteen and find accurate now."

"The king." Edmund went very still. "You were his hunting hound."

"I was his *everything* hound, thank you. Hunting was merely the profession. Companionship was the calling." Bramble lowered himself onto the straw with the care of someone whose joints filed formal complaints each morning. "And you're here about him. Everyone who creeps into the menagerie at midnight is here about him. The falconer's boy cried behind the owl mews last week. A woman in warden's grays stood by the wolf pen for two hours and never said a word—she had a way of standing like the ground owed her money. You three travel together, yes? The star-girl smells like cold iron and grief."

"Sera," Edmund said. "Her name is Sera Voss. And Lyra. And they're—" *Captured and dying*, respectively. "They're counting on me."

"Mm." Bramble rested his chin on his paws. "Then ask your question, ledger-keeper. You've been circling it since you arrived. You circle questions the way herding dogs circle sheep. It's exhausting to watch."

Edmund sat down in the straw. It seemed only polite.

"They say King Aldric invited the Veil in," Edmund said. "That the binding wasn't a conquest but a... a door he opened himself. Is it true? Did he *choose* it?"

Bramble was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere across the menagerie, a nightjar called, and got no answer, because there were no other nightjars anymore.

"Yes," the hound said finally. "I was there. I'm always the one who's there. That's the curse of being a dog—we attend everything and are consulted about nothing."

* * *

"He was nine years old," Bramble began. "Nine, and already king, because his father died of the same plague that was eating the city. You remember the Gray Winter?"

Edmund did. Everyone did, though the remembering had gone strange lately—memories felt thinner these days, like cloth washed too many times. "They say it killed one in three."

"One in three of the living. Of the *named*, rather." Bramble's ear twitched. "Here is what the histories will never tell you, because the histories were written by people who needed the king to be either a saint or a monster, and the truth is he was neither. He was a boy who couldn't sleep.

"The plague didn't kill quickly. It unmade you slowly. First you forgot small things—where you'd put your keys, your neighbor's face. Then bigger things. Your mother's name. Your own. The healers called it the graying. By midwinter, whole streets of people were wandering around smiling at strangers, contented as cattle, because they'd forgotten there was anything to grieve."

Edmund's skin prickled. *Villages where residents have lost their own names and wander smiling.* They'd walked through three of them on the road north. They'd thought it was new damage.

"It's happened before," Edmund whispered.

"It happens every time the Veil gets hungry enough to reach through the wards," Bramble said. "But that winter, it reached far. The court physicians bled the king's advisors. The priests burned incense until the palace smelled like a funeral that had given up. Nothing worked. And then a man came—a quiet man, hooded, polite, with a voice like several voices agreeing with each other. He offered the regents a bargain. One name, freely given, of royal blood, and the Veil would bind itself to the crown instead of roaming free. Contained. Fed on schedule instead of feasting at random. The regents refused, of course. Regencies always refuse; it's practically their job description.

"So the boy did it himself."

Edmund stared. "He was nine."

"He was nine, and he had watched his father forget his mother's name three days before she died, and his mother forget *his* name the day after. He came down to the kennels at midnight—I remember because it was my turn to be lonely with him—and he sat on the straw and asked me whether a name hurt when it was taken." Bramble's voice had gone very soft, the way beast-speech could go soft, softer than any human throat allowed. "I told him the truth. I said I didn't know. Nobody had ever given one freely before. And he said, *'Well, somebody has to find out, and everyone else is too scared.'*

"That was Aldric. Even then. Not brave the way the ballads say—ballads think bravery is shouting. Brave the way a small animal is brave, which is to say: terrified, and doing the thing anyway, because someone has to and no one else will."

The straw rustled as Edmund shifted. Their eyes had adjusted to the dark, and now they could see the menagerie properly: the empty peacock pavilion, the wolf pen where Sera had once stood, the long row of cages, most vacant, a few occupied by creatures that watched the palace with the patient resentment of the overlooked.

"And the binding worked?" Edmund asked.

"For eleven years, beautifully. The plague stopped within the week. The Veil kept its word—it always keeps its word, that's the horror of it, it's scrupulous—and Aldric grew up radiant and beloved and increasingly hollow, like a house where someone lives but never turns on the lights. Every year on coronation day, the rite renews the binding. Every year, a little more of him goes into the keeping of the thing behind his eyes." Bramble sighed, a vast, hay-scented sound. "Dogs don't understand gods. But we understand our people. And I can tell you this: whatever sits on that throne now wears my boy like a coat. It waves with his hand. It smiles with his mouth. Last month it fed me from the table, exactly the way he used to, and I ate it, because I am a dog and a fool, and afterward I howled at a sky with no moon in it until the guards threatened to have me shot."

Edmund's throat ached. This was the trouble with the gift—everyone warned you about hearing terrible things, but nobody warned you about hearing *tender* things. The rats had told them about the name-eater walking north. The ravens had mapped its route in dead words. But no one had mentioned that the enemy might have a hound who loved him.

"You don't hate him," Edmund said. "The king. Even now."

"Hate him?" Bramble lifted his head. "Ledger-keeper, I have hated many things in a long life. Cats. Bathes. The specific guard who stepped on my tail in the Year of the Bad Harvest. But that boy gave up his *name*—the thing you people treat as though it were nailed to your souls—to save strangers who never learned what it cost. If the Veil is wearing him, then the Veil made the worst mistake of its long unlived life. Because it didn't just take Aldric. It took on everything Aldric was. And Aldric was stubborn, kind, and constitutionally incapable of leaving anyone behind." The old hound's clouded eyes fixed on Edmund. "Sound like anyone you know? You crossed half the realm and broke into a palace to buy back one disgraced warden. The brokers must have found you baffling."

"They found me *negotiable*," Edmund said bitterly. "That's worse. I sat across from that thing and bargained. I've spent ten years despising traders who put prices on living things, and the moment it mattered, I opened my ledger and started dealing."

"Mm." Bramble considered this. "When a sheepdog bites a wolf to protect the flock, do we call the dog a wolf?"

"That's not—"

"When a healer cuts a man open, do we call her a butcher?" The hound's tail thumped once, decisively, scattering straw. "You humans have this disease where you believe your worst moment is your truest one. It's nonsense. Your truest moments are the boring ones. Feeding someone. Keeping a promise nobody witnessed. Sitting in straw at midnight listening to an old dog ramble, when you could be running somewhere safe." He paused. "Speaking of which—you're going to do something reckless, aren't you? I can smell it. Reckless has a smell. Like lightning before it lands."

Edmund looked down at their hands. Ink-stained, straw-dusted, trembling slightly. Hands that had catalogued a bottled echo and a moth that remembered futures, and had never once, in ten years, done anything that would get them noticed.

"The broker wants the king's binding-name," Edmund said. "The name the boy-king gave the Veil. His true birth-name. With it, the binding can be unmade—or remade, or transferred, or any number of terrible things. I agreed to bring it. I lied about having it. But I think..." They swallowed. "I think the name might actually exist. Hidden somewhere. And if I find it, I don't know whether I'll use it to free him or destroy him or hand it over to save Sera, and every version of me that makes that choice feels like a different person wearing my coat."

Bramble was silent so long that Edmund wondered if the old dog had fallen asleep sitting up, which he understood was possible.

"There's a story," Bramble said at last, "that the first queen wrote the king's true name down before she died. Sealed it away, so no broker or bishop could ever dig it out of the boy. The court calls it a legend. But legends, in my experience, are just memories that outlived their witnesses." He rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and nodded toward the shadowed end of the menagerie, where a small door stood half-hidden behind the wolf pen. "The queen kept birds. Her aviary connects to the old record rooms beneath the east wing. The lock has been broken for years—palaces only guard the things they're afraid of, and nobody's afraid of a dead woman's birds."

Edmund stood. Their legs had gone numb and their heart was hammering, but underneath the fear, something else had kindled—that dangerous warmth, the feeling of a plan assembling itself out of scraps.

"Why are you helping me?" they asked. "You could bark. Bring the guards. You'd be a hero to whatever wears the king these days."

Bramble looked at Edmund for a long time, and then did something Edmund had never heard a beast do: he answered in almost-human cadence, slow and deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Because you hear us. Because someone has to remember my boy as he was, and soon there may be no one left who can. And because—" the old hound's voice caught, an odd hitch in beast-speech, like a song skipping— "because when they took him, they took the part of him that remembered *me*. Whatever you do with that name, ledger-keeper, whoever you decide to be when you use it—give him back. Even a piece. Even just the piece that knew his dog."

Edmund reached out and laid a hand on the great gray head. Bramble leaned into it, heavy and warm, the way dogs lean when they have decided, finally and completely, to trust you.

"I'll try," Edmund said. "I can't promise more than that."

"Nobody ever can," said Bramble. "That's what promises are *for*."

* * *

The little door behind the wolf pen opened without a sound, which should have been Edmund's first warning. Doors in palaces did not open silently unless someone wanted them to.

Inside, the aviary corridor stretched away into darkness, lined with empty perches furred with dust. Dead queen's birds, long flown or long dead. At the far end, where Bramble had promised, a stair descended toward the old record rooms—and beside the stair, someone had left a lamp burning.

Fresh oil. Recent flame.

Edmund froze. And then, drifting up from below, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that turned their blood to ice water:

Someone was humming. Down in the archive. Humming a tune Edmund knew intimately—the little counting-song guild clerks used to tally columns, the one Corvane had whistled every morning for ten years.

And beneath the humming, layered under it like a second voice inside one throat, something else was humming along.

Something with many mouths.
