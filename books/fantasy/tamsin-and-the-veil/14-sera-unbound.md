## 14. Sera, Unbound

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above a shuttered dye-works in the Tanners' Quarter, which meant it smelled like regret and old vinegar and was therefore the last place in Vaelmoor anyone would look for three fugitives and a falling star.

Sera Voss had been awake for two days when she finally stopped pretending to sleep.

She lay on a pallet stuffed with wool scraps that poked her through the blanket like small accusing fingers, staring at the rafters, listening to the city breathe around her. Somewhere below, a dog was dreaming—she could hear the muffled thump of its tail against a wall, the little whining sounds of whatever dogs dreamed about. Probably meat. Dogs were honest like that.

She envied the dog.

The door at the top of the stairs opened without a sound, which meant Edmund, because Lyra still hadn't learned how doors worked and Aldric—the king, *the actual king*, asleep on a straw tick six feet away like a man sleeping off a wedding—snored like a landslide.

"You're awake," Edmund said.

"I'm awake."

"You've been awake."

"I've been awake," Sera agreed.

Edmund crossed the room and sat down on the floor beside her pallet, back against the wall, ledger balanced on their knees out of pure habit even though there was nothing left in it worth counting. In the dark they were mostly a shape: narrow shoulders, ink-stained fingers, the careful stillness of someone who had spent ten years practicing not being noticed.

"Your hands are shaking," Edmund said.

"My hands are always shaking. It's called being hungover, and I've been hungover since I was twenty-three."

"It's been four days since you drank anything."

"Then I'm four days sober and my hands are shaking about it." She flexed her fingers in the dark, watched them tremble, and hated them a little. "Go back to sleep, bookkeeper."

"I don't sleep well in buildings where people have died."

Sera turned her head. "Nobody's died here."

"Not yet," Edmund said mildly. "But the tanner who owned this loft sold his name to a Veil broker two winters ago. The beasts told me. He walked out of his own house smiling and never came back, and the building has been waiting for him ever since. Buildings do that, you know. Wait. Like dogs."

Sera stared at the ceiling. "You say things like that the way other people talk about weather."

"Would you rather I said it dramatically?"

"I'd rather you let me sleep."

"You weren't sleeping. You were lying there doing arithmetic with your face."

"What does arithmetic with my face look like?"

"Like this." Edmund made an expression of profound grim concentration, eyebrows knotted, mouth pulled sideways, and it was such an accurate impression of Sera's own resting face that despite everything—a gutter shrine, a captured friend, a stolen ledger, a kingdom going quietly hollow at the edges—she laughed. A short, cracked, rusty laugh, but a real one.

"There it is," Edmund said softly.

"Don't get smug."

"Too late. This is me getting smug. Enjoy it. It may be the last smugness either of us gets."

They sat in silence for a while. Below them the dreaming dog thumped its tail. In the corner, Lyra slept curled around herself like a comma, and even in sleep she dimmed and brightened faintly, a lamp running low on oil, and every hour she lost something else—the name of a river, the color of a particular sunset, once, horribly, the word for *sister*.

"She asked me today what bread tasted like on the night sky," Edmund said. "I didn't know what to tell her."

"Tell her it tastes like cold. Cold and far away." Sera closed her eyes. "I used to stand watch on the wall in winter and eat hardtack under open stars. That's what it tastes like. Cold and far away and better than anything you'll ever eat indoors."

"That's very poetic for a disgraced warden."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly gin."

Edmund didn't laugh this time. Sera felt the silence change shape, the way air changes before a storm, and knew—because she had spent eleven years as a warden learning to read silences—that the bookkeeper had come up those stairs for a reason and was now working up the nerve.

"Just say it," Sera said.

Edmund was quiet a moment longer. Then, carefully, the way you set down something fragile:

"When we got you out of the broker's cellar, you talked. In your sleep, after. You kept saying *I checked them, I checked them twice.*"

Sera's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the dye-works, through the earth, all the way down to wherever guilty things go to hide.

"The wards," Edmund said. Not an accusation. Worse—an invitation. "The night they fell. You'd inspected them yourself, hadn't you? Three weeks before. You told me that, our first night, drunk at the shrine. You said you'd signed the inspection report yourself and you'd stake your life the wards were sound."

"They were sound." Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "That's the whole point, Edmund. That's the thing I haven't—I couldn't—" She sat up. The wool scraps bit into her palms and she welcomed it. "Do you know what happens to a warden who fails the border wards? There are exactly two options. Death, if you're lucky and the tribunal is feeling merciful. Or disgrace, which is slower. They take your commission, your pension, your name off the rolls. They let you live in the city you failed and watch everyone remember why you're nobody."

"I know," Edmund said quietly. "I've been nobody professionally for a decade. It's not so bad once you get good at it."

"It's terrible."

"It's terrible," Edmund agreed. "But it's survivable, which is more than the dead can say."

Sera laughed again, or something near it. Then the laugh died, because there was no more room in her for anything that wasn't the truth, and the truth had been sitting in her chest for four days like a swallowed stone.

"I went back," she said. "After. After the stars went out, after the tribunal, after everything. I broke into the ward-vaults under the old garrison—it took me two months to find a way in, because apparently I'm only competent at crimes—and I pulled the inspection records. Mine. The originals. The anchor-stones, the binding-threads, the resonance seals, all of it."

"And?"

"And they were perfect." She heard her own voice crack and didn't bother fixing it. "Every seal intact. Every thread true. The wards I inspected were the strongest border-craft built in two hundred years. I staked my life on them because they were *worth* staking a life on. So then explain it to me, bookkeeper, because I've done the sums a thousand times and they don't add up. If the wards were sound, how did they fall like wet paper the moment the stars blinked out?"

Edmund was very still. "How did they fall?"

"They didn't fall." The stone in her chest turned over, and out came the words she'd been carrying alone in the dark for four days, and saying them aloud was like lancing a wound—horrible and relieving at once. "They were *unpicked*. From the inside. Someone with full guild access went into the anchor-vaults in the weeks before the collapse and loosened the binding-threads just enough—not enough to trip any alarm, not enough for any inspector to catch, just enough that when the Veil pushed, the whole lattice would come apart clean. Deliberate. Patient. Professional." She looked at Edmund, and the dark between them felt very wide. "There are maybe six people in Vaelmoor with the access and the skill to unpick a border lattice without leaving a mark. And one of them is Guildmaster Corvane."

The name hung in the air of the dead tanner's loft.

"He's been selling names," Edmund said slowly. "For years. I stole his ledger. Hundreds of them. I thought—I assumed he started after the wards fell. Opportunism. Feeding the crisis."

"No." Sera shook her head, and now that the stone was out, the rest came easy, terrible and clear. "It's the other way around. Think like a trader, Edmund—you're better at it than I am. What did Corvane need to make his business work? The Veil doesn't buy from just anyone. It needs a door. It needs a city whose protections fail at exactly the right moment, in exactly the right way, so that its agents can walk in and start collecting. A crisis. But crises are unpredictable. Unless you *schedule* one."

Edmund's breath caught. "He sabotaged the wards first. To create the opening. The name-selling came after—he needed the Veil established before he could trade with it at scale."

"He didn't fail to protect Vaelmoor." Sera's hands had stopped shaking. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice rain stopping. "He *sold* it. Piece by piece. And when the wards came down, when the stars went out, when half the border garrisons were overrun—who did the tribunal blame? The warden who signed the last inspection. Me. Because that's how it was designed to work. Someone had to be the failure. Someone had to be the story everyone told, so no one would ever ask the real question." She heard her voice go low and even, the voice she'd used on the wall in winter, reporting to superiors who never listened. "Corvane didn't just betray the city. He built me into the betrayal. I'm not the warden who failed the wards. I'm the alibi."

The silence afterward was long. Below them, the dog had stopped dreaming; it barked once, at nothing, and settled.

When Edmund spoke, their voice was gentle in a way that made Sera want to put her head in her hands. "Why didn't you tell us? Four days, Sera. We've been running ourselves ragged and you've been carrying this alone."

"Because it changes nothing." She said it fast, before she could lose her nerve. "We already knew Corvane was rotten. We already have his ledger. Knowing he picked the locks himself instead of just profiting from them—that doesn't get the king free. It doesn't relight Lyra's stars. It doesn't give anyone back their names. It's just—" Her throat tightened. "It's just the difference between failing and being *made* to fail, and somehow that's worse, because if I'd simply failed, at least the failure would have been mine. At least I'd deserve it. Do you understand? Guilt is a burden, but it's a *burden*. You can carry it. It's yours. What Corvane did was steal even that. He took the one thing I had left—my own damn guilt—and it turns out it was never mine at all."

She expected pity. She braced for it, the way you brace for cold water.

Instead Edmund said, "So take it back."

Sera looked up.

"Not the guilt," Edmund said. "The purpose. Listen—if Corvane unpicked the wards, then he knows ward-craft better than anyone alive outside the old orders. Which means somewhere in that guildhall of his—in his offices, his vaults, his private records—is the complete map of what he did. Every thread he loosened, every seal he compromised, every anchor-stone he hollowed out. And if we can find that map, we can do more than expose him." Edmund's eyes had gone bright, the way they did over a ledger column that finally balanced. "We can *rebuild* it. The lattice isn't destroyed, Sera. It's unpicked. Unpicked means the pattern still exists underneath. It means it can be re-tied."

Sera sat very straight on her pallet of poking wool, and felt something shift in her chest, some load redistributing itself, guilt transmuting grain by grain into something with an edge and a direction.

"You're saying the wards can be restored."

"I'm saying you're the only person alive who's read the original inspections and lived to be angry about it. I'm saying Corvane built his crime to make you the ending of the story." Edmund smiled, thin and fierce. "Let's make you the middle instead."

From the corner, a sleepy voice said, "Are we planning a burglary? I'm excellent at burglaries. I once stole fire from a temple."

"Lyra," Sera said, "you should be asleep."

"I was asleep. You two have the conspiratorial voices of a pair of kettles." The fallen star sat up, hair floating faintly around her head as if she couldn't quite be bothered to obey gravity anymore, and looked between them with eyes that held less light each day but no less fury. "Whatever it is, I'm in. I owe a debt to a certain guildmaster regarding my sisters, and I pay my debts loudly."

"We need to get back into the Gilded Scale," Edmund said. "Into Corvane's private records. Which means getting past the guild's wards—which, incidentally, are the same craft as the border lattice, which means—"

"—which means I know exactly where he'd have hidden his own weaknesses," Sera finished, and heard her own voice come out steady and new. "Because he'd have used the same trick. Hide the flaw in plain sight. Blame the inspector."

She stood up. Her legs ached; her hands, she saw with distant satisfaction, had gone perfectly still. Eleven years she'd carried the weight of the fallen wards, and it turned out the weight had been counterfeit all along—a prop in another man's play. Fine. Let him keep his play. She had something better now.

She had a job.

"First light," Sera said, "we plan the burglary properly. Wardens are thorough. And Edmund—"

"Yes?"

"When we find his records, I want to read every page myself. Every thread he cut. I want to know his crime the way he never bothered to know mine." She looked toward the window, where the sky beyond the shutters was beginning, very faintly, to consider gray. "And then I want to tie every single one of them back."

Edmund grinned. Lyra cracked her knuckles, producing a sound like distant thunder, which was showing off and everyone knew it.

And then, from across the room, from the straw tick where the King of Vaelmoor had been sleeping like an ordinary exhausted man, came a sound that froze all three of them mid-motion.

Aldric was sitting up. His eyes were open—but not looking at them, not looking at anything in the room. Looking, somehow, *through* the walls, through the city, northward. And his mouth was moving, shaping words in a voice that was his and yet layered, one voice under many, the way a river is water over stones.

"*They gather,*" the king said, in the chorus-voice that was not entirely his own. "*The little lights in the dark. Tell them—we see them. Tell them—we know what they want most. And we are so very willing to give it.*"

His eyes closed. He toppled gently back onto the tick and began, horribly, to snore.

In the silence that followed, Lyra said, very quietly, "It knows we're coming."

Sera reached for her boots.
