## 15. The Chorus Speaks

I had been human for nineteen days when I learned that the worst part of having a body was not the hunger or the cold or the way my knees ached on the road north.

It was the waiting.

Stars do not wait. Stars burn on a schedule older than language, and if you had asked me—back when I was the Hunter's Bow, third star of the constellation they called the Seven Sisters, though there were never seven of us and we were not sisters until mortals decided we were—if you had asked me what waiting felt like, I would have said I didn't know.

Now I knew. Waiting felt like this: crouched in the rafters of a burned-out granary outside the capital with Edmund asleep against a grain sack and Sera pretending to sleep while she cleaned her boots for the fourth time that night, and me counting the hours until dawn by how much colder my fingers got.

"I can hear you thinking," Sera said, without looking up. "It sounds like grinding millstones."

"Good. Then you're not sleeping either."

"Nobody sleeps well three miles from a king who's wearing an eldritch horror like a coat." She set down one boot and picked up the other. "You'd think I'd be used to it. I wasn't even drunk when the wards fell, Lyra. That's the joke. The one night in ten years I was sober enough to see it happen, and I still got the blame."

"You didn't fail," I said. It was the fourth time I'd said it. It kept being true and it kept not helping.

"Mm." She worked at a buckle. "And you didn't fall out of the sky on purpose, and Edmund didn't spend ten years pretending to be furniture. We're a very innocent company we keep."

That was when the candles went out.

Not blew out—there was no wind in the granary, and the flames didn't gutter. They simply stopped being lit, all six of them at once, the way stars stop being stars: one moment burning, the next a small black absence where light used to be.

Edmund sat up so fast they cracked their head on a rafter beam. In the dark I heard them hiss something unrepeatable, and then, quieter, in the voice they used only for beasts and emergencies: "Something's here."

"Not something," said a voice from everywhere.

I knew that voice. I had heard it across a hundred thousand years of dark, back when I hung in the sky and watched it move beneath the world like a tide of hungry silk. It did not speak in one voice. It spoke in many—the way a chord is many notes—and every voice in it was slightly wrong, pitched just past where comfort lived.

The Veil.

* * *

The darkness organized itself. That is the only way I can describe it. The black between the dead candles began to have shape, suggestion, the outline of a figure seated across the granary floor as though it had always been sitting there, as though we were the ones who had arrived late to its appointment.

It wore no face. It wore the idea of a face, the way a rumor wears a name.

"Do not be alarmed," it said, in a chorus of grandmothers and drowned men and one voice that sounded exactly like mine would sound if I let myself cry. "I come with gifts."

"Gifts." Sera was on her feet, knife drawn, which was like drawing a spoon against the sea. "The last time your kind gave a gift, a boy-king ended up hollow as a drum."

"The king chose poorly." A ripple through the voices, almost amusement. "He asked to save everyone. Saving everyone is expensive. You three are wiser. You will ask for less."

"We're not asking for anything," Edmund said. Their voice shook, but they kept it level, and I remember feeling proud of them in a distant, starlike way—pride being one of the mortal feelings I had collected recently, along with hunger and the strange ache I got watching Edmund count things when they were nervous.

"You misunderstand." The shape leaned forward. "We are asking nothing. We are *offering*. Walk away. Turn south tomorrow morning. And each of you will have the thing you want most. Freely given. No ledger. No debt."

Silence. Even the rats in the walls had gone quiet, and I thought—I know now—that Edmund was listening to them too, and that the rats were afraid in a way that made my borrowed heart hurt.

"Sera Voss," the Veil said, and her name came out sounding like a verdict being reversed. "Warden. Disgraced. Blamed for wards you did not break, by men who did. Walk away, and wake tomorrow restored. Your rank returned. Your record clean. The city will cheer your name again. They will build a statue. Statues last longer than guilt."

I saw Sera's jaw tighten. I saw her knuckles whiten around the knife. And I saw—because I was made of light once and I know what flickering looks like—I saw the exact moment the offer landed somewhere soft and old inside her.

"That's not—" she started.

"It is everything," the Veil said gently. "It is the only thing. Honor is the wound you lick nightly, little warden. We have watched you. We watch everyone."

Then it turned, if a faceless thing can be said to turn, toward me.

* * *

"Lyra."

My name in its mouth sounded like a bell cracking.

"Third of the Seven. The Archer's Bow. You burned for four thousand years and fell in one night, and do you know why?" The voices softened, became almost kind, which was infinitely worse. "Because we ate your sisters first. One by one. We saved you for last because you were the brightest, and bright things deserve anticipation."

Every hair on my body stood up. My hands were shaking. I hated that they could shake; stars don't shake.

"Walk away," the Veil said, "and we will give them back. All five. Relit. Hung in their places. The sky made whole, and you among them—not flesh, not cold, not hungry, not *waiting*. You will burn again, Lyra. Forever. Isn't forever what you were made for?"

And here is the truth I have carried since that night, the truth I will carry until I burn out or don't: I wanted it.

I wanted it the way drowning things want air. Nineteen days of mud and hunger and forgetting—I had already lost the color of my second sister's light. I couldn't remember whether Maera sang or hummed. Every hour as a mortal, some piece of four thousand years drained out of me like water through cupped hands, and the Veil stood there offering me the bucket.

"Yes," I whispered.

The shape inclined its head, satisfied.

"No," I said.

The word came out of me like an arrow loosed from the bow I used to be. I hadn't decided to say it. Or rather, some part of me had decided long ago, in a crater, kneeling in scorched earth over the place where my sisters' light had been, and tonight-me simply caught up to her.

"No," I said again, louder, because the first one needed reinforcing. "You ate them. You don't get to sell them back to me. Their light isn't yours to give—it's theirs, and you *stole* it, and I would rather forget every one of their names honestly than remember them in a sky you built."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere in the chorus, faintly, I heard something that might have been disappointment—or appetite recalculating.

"Flesh forgets," the Veil said softly. "In a year you will not remember refusing us."

"Then I'll refuse you again next year," I said, "on principle."

* * *

It turned last to Edmund.

I want to record this precisely, because later—after everything—it mattered. The Veil regarded Edmund for a long time, the way a merchant regards a coin it suspects is counterfeit.

"Ledger-keeper," it said. "Beast-speaker. Ten years silent. Ten years pressing your truest self flat under guild paperwork like a flower in a book." The voices gentled into something horribly like sympathy. "Walk away, and wake tomorrow ordinary. No whispers in your head. No secrets splitting you in half. No gift. You could sit in a tavern and hear nothing but people. You could be *normal*, Edmund Galloway. Isn't normal the thing you've been killing yourself to fake?"

I looked at Edmund. In the dark I could barely see them, but I could feel them the way you feel weather coming.

They were quiet for a long time.

When they finally spoke, their voice was small and steady and broke my heart in two specific places.

"For about four seconds," Edmund said, "I wanted it more than anything in the world."

"Four seconds is enough," the Veil murmured.

"But here's the thing." Edmund stood up. I heard their knees pop; they'd been crouching too long, another mortal indignity I had not yet learned to find endearing, though I would. "You're offering to take away the only part of me that's ever been *true*. Everything else—the ledgers, the invisibility, the flinching—that was the costume. The hearing is me. And the beasts you've been eating names around? They're scared. They're scared and they're talking to me, and if I walk away, nobody hears them." A breath. "So no. Keep your normal. It was never on sale anyway."

The Veil was quiet.

Then, softly, in a voice that was almost tender and entirely terrible: "All three of you refused. But none of you refused *cleanly*. Do you understand? Desire is a door, and you have each shown us where you keep yours. We do not need your yes, little ones. We only need your wanting. Wanting opens from the inside."

The candles relit.

All six, at once, flames standing straight as soldiers, and the granary was empty of anything except the three of us and the smell of ozone and old snow.

* * *

For a while nobody moved.

Then Sera sheathed her knife with a hand that trembled only a little, and said, to the room in general, "Well. That was the most polite attempt to destroy my soul I've ever experienced."

"It offered me my sisters," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat. "I said yes first. Did you hear that? Before I said no, I said yes."

"Everyone says yes first," Edmund said quietly. They had gone to the window and were looking north, toward the capital, toward the throne where a hollow king slept with something ancient curled behind his eyes. "That's how it works. That's how it got Aldric, probably. He said yes before he understood the price." They turned. "It knew our wants because it's been reading us since the wards fell. Which means it knows what we're planning."

Sera went still. "The archive. The binding rite. If it knows—"

"It knows," Edmund said. "It just told us. *Wanting opens from the inside.* It doesn't need to catch us sneaking. It'll just make sure someone else gets to our pieces before we do." They paused, and I watched them arrive at the same conclusion I was arriving at, the way mortals do—slowly, painfully, out loud. "Someone in the city is already its. Someone who wants something badly enough to have left a door open."

"Corvane," Sera and I said together.

Edmund nodded once. "Corvane sold names to its agents for years. He's been feeding it since before the wards fell. And if it knows we're coming for the binding rite, then he knows too—which means tomorrow, when we walk into Vaelmoor, we won't be sneaking anywhere." They picked up their satchel, the stolen ledger of sold names inside it, and slung it across their shoulder with the air of someone accepting a sentence. "We need to reach him before the Veil does. Or after. Either way—"

"Either way," Sera said grimly, pulling on both boots at last, "we're going to have a conversation with the man who ruined my life."

Outside, far to the north, above the sleeping capital, the sky was clear and utterly, deliberately starless—a hole in the world shaped like ambition.

I looked at it, and at my own two shaking mortal hands, and found—to my enormous surprise—that I was smiling.

"Let it read my wanting," I said. "I want my sisters avenged. Let it choke on that."

Edmund blew out the candles—one by one, properly, the mortal way—and in the dark, the three of us started walking toward the city and whatever was waiting in it.
