## 16. Corvane's Reckoning

The Gilded Scale guildhall had always smelled of ink and tallow and the particular sourness of men who counted other people's money for a living. Tonight it smelled of smoke. Somewhere below us, Vaelmoor was burning its lanterns against a sky that still refused to give back its stars, and the smoke of ten thousand desperate hearths had risen into a low grey ceiling that made the whole city feel like a room with the lid closed.

I stood in the doorway of Corvane's counting room at the top of the guildhall tower, and I did not knock.

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane sat behind his great desk of black walnut, the one he'd bought from a bankrupt shipping magnate and never let anyone forget. He was writing. Of course he was writing. The world could end twice over and Corvane would still be entering figures in a ledger, because figures were the only prayer he knew.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said without looking up. "Or fled. Or arrested by now. You've been spectacularly inconvenient about all three."

"I've been busy," I said.

"Ah." He set down his pen with the care of a man laying down a knife he intended to pick up again. "Busy. Yes. Sera Voss told me you would come. Well—she didn't tell me, precisely. She screamed it through the door while my clerks were dragging her out. Very informative woman, our disgraced warden. Terrible at keeping secrets. Wonderful at making threats." He folded his hands. "She's alive, if that's why you've climbed all these stairs. For now. The Veil's brokers find her useful as collateral. They're sentimental that way, in the manner of tax collectors."

Something in my chest went tight and hot. I made myself breathe around it. *Later*, I told the heat. *You can burn later.*

"You sold them," I said. "The wards. Chapter and verse, Corvane. Sera told me. You cut them yourself so the city would panic, so the king's council would come begging to the guild for stability, and stability has a price, and the price is names."

Corvane smiled. It was not a cruel smile, which was somehow worse. It was the smile of a man who has done arithmetic no one else has bothered to do and found himself, reluctantly, correct.

"Sit down, Galloway."

"I'll stand."

"You always were stubborn about furniture." He rose and crossed to the window, and I saw then how old he had gotten since the stars went out—not older in years but older in some structural way, like a building whose foundation has quietly shifted. Below us, the city glimmered with its frightened little lights. "Do you know what I was doing the night the wards fell? Do you imagine I was celebrating?"

"You were trading," I said. "With something whose voice sounds like a crowd reading over your shoulder. I heard it. Through the floor. Through the walls. I've been hearing things through walls my entire life, Guildmaster. It's rather my specialty."

He turned. And here is the thing I had not prepared for: he looked *relieved*. Ten years I had hidden from this man, and in the moment I finally stood before him with the truth in my mouth, his shoulders dropped like a man setting down a load he'd carried up too many stairs.

"So it's true," he said softly. "The beasts talk to you. I wondered. All those years you spent in the stables 'checking inventory.' The rats that fled the granary three days before the flood. The hounds that wouldn't hunt on the day the magister died." He shook his head. "I thought you were simply lucky. Luck is cheaper to employ."

"Why?" The word came out rougher than I meant it to. "That's all I came for. Not justice, not a confession for the wardens' tribunal. Just—why. Give me an answer I can carry."

Corvane looked at me for a long moment. Then he gestured at the window, at the starless dark pressing against the glass like a face.

"Because I read the old contracts," he said. "All of them. Every binding, every renewal, every scrap the archive would sell me at three in the morning. And do you know what I learned, Galloway? I learned that the Veil cannot be killed. Cannot be warded away forever. Cannot be bargained with, because it doesn't want what we want—it wants *names*, the way a river wants downhill. The first king fed it a sacrifice of names and bought two hundred years. Each generation renews the payment. And this generation—" he spread his hands, "—this generation's designated sacrifice was a girl born in the Lowmarsh, name entered in the guild registry when she was six days old. A name I sold eleven years ago to a buyer who paid in trade-guarantees that kept four hundred families fed through the famine winters."

My stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

"You didn't know," I said. "When you sold it. You didn't know what her name was *for*."

"I knew names were currency. I didn't know they were *mortgage payments*." His voice cracked, just once, and he smoothed it flat again with visible effort. "By the time I understood, the name was gone. Eaten. And the renewal failed, and the wards failed, and the stars went out, and every road since then has led downhill. So yes. I serve it now. Because someone has to stand at the top of the stairs and decide who gets eaten first, and better a man who keeps books than a beast that doesn't."

"And the villages," I said. "The hollow people, smiling in the road with their names gone. Did you keep *books* on them too? Neat columns? Little sums?"

"They were going to be eaten regardless," he said, and his voice was steady, and that steadiness was the most horrifying thing in the room. "I merely chose the order. Order, Galloway, is the only mercy left. Someone must impose it, or the Veil takes everything at once, everywhere, screaming. I have delayed the screaming for eleven years. What have you done? Hidden in a stable and talked to horses."

And there it was. The blade under the coat, slid between my ribs by a man who genuinely believed he was performing surgery.

For a moment—one long, shameful moment—I almost believed him. That's the thing about Corvane nobody warns you about: he isn't a monster. Monsters are easy. He's a man who did the math and couldn't live with the answer unless he became the answer. I had spent a decade being invisible to avoid exactly that kind of arithmetic.

But I hadn't come alone.

* * *

They had been gathering for three days. I hadn't asked them to. That's the part I will tell the tribunal, if there ever is a tribunal, and mean every word: I did not ask.

Word travels strangely when you stop hiding. I had spent ten years perfecting silence, and in the space of a few weeks of speaking openly—to ravens on the north road, to the dying elk, to rats in the capital's gutters, to the king's old hunting hound asleep in the royal menagerie—the gift had become a kind of rumor among animals, passed nose to ear to wing. *There is one who listens. There is one who asks. Tell the listener.*

So when I stepped back from Corvane's window and opened the tower doors, the testimony was already waiting.

It began with the pigeons.

Vaelmoor's pigeons are nobody's favorites—they steal bread, they nest in the guildhall eaves, they have the manners of small feathered tax assessors—but they are everywhere, and they see everything, and on the night Corvane first met the hooded buyer on the loading dock, a roost of forty-seven of them had been sleeping six feet above the transaction.

Their voices arrived together, layered, the way a flock moves as one shape:

*"The man with many voices beneath the hood. We heard him say: the registry, the sixth-day entries, the Lowmarsh line. We heard the guildmaster say: done, and done, and done."*

Then the stable cats, who had watched clerks carry ledger-boxes down to the river barges at hours when honest clerks sleep:

*"Nine boxes. Sealed with black wax that moved like skin. The barges went north. The cats remember; cats forget nothing except gratitude."*

Then the dogs of the Lowmarsh itself, the ones who had belonged to the hollow families before their owners wandered smiling into the road:

*"We waited at the doors. They never came home. Their names were the last thing they said to us, and then even we began to lose the sound of them."*

And the horses—always the horses, my oldest confidants—a hundred strong across the city's stables, speaking in the low rolling voice of animals who measure time in seasons and betrayal in generations:

*"He fed the wards poison and called it maintenance. We felt the wards die. It felt like a bone breaking in a body too big to scream."*

Voice upon voice upon voice. Rats from the sewers where the brokers met. Ravens from the towers where Corvane's signals flew. A moth—yes, the moth, the one that remembers futures, still alive in its jar in the forbidden inventory, which I had liberated two nights prior and which now spoke in a whisper like turning pages: *"I saw this. I saw this room. I saw him look afraid. The future arrives punctually."*

Here is what I had not understood until that moment, standing in the doorway with the night air full of testimony: the gift was never a curse of hearing. It was a court. The beasts of Vaelmoor had been waiting ten years for a witness, and I had finally stopped pretending I wasn't one.

Corvane stood very still in the middle of his counting room, listening to hundreds of small voices describe his life in unflattering detail, and I watched the arithmetic finally fail him. Not the moral arithmetic—he'd abandoned that years ago—but the practical kind. The kind where you calculate whether anyone is watching.

"Who else can hear this," he said quietly.

"No one," I admitted. "Only me. Only ever only me."

"Then it proves nothing. It's a madman's chorus. You'll be laughed out of any tribunal in the realm."

"Probably," I agreed. "But I'm not taking it to a tribunal."

I crossed to his desk and set down the thing I had carried up all those stairs: the stolen ledger of sold names, open to the page with the Lowmarsh girl's sixth-day entry, and beside it a fresh sheet of paper, and a pen.

"Because the clerks are coming," I said. "In about four minutes. Sera Voss sent word before they took her—she'd already written to the Warden-General, months ago, a letter she kept in a gutter shrine because she trusted no building in this city. It names you. It dates you. And the brokers hunting us have been using *your* courier routes, which means the Warden-General's investigators have been reading your correspondence for a week." I tapped the ledger. "You taught me everything I know about bookkeeping, Guildmaster. Including the part where every crime leaves a column somewhere."

Below us, faintly, boots on the guildhall stairs. Many boots. Corvane heard them too; I watched him hear them.

"You could run," I said. "You have money enough to vanish. But you won't, because running means the Veil eats Vaelmoor unordered, and order is the only mercy you have left. Isn't it? That's the trap you built for yourself. You can't even save yourself, because saving yourself would be *messy*."

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as old ink, and it cracked halfway through into something that might have been weeping in a man with less spine and more honesty.

"Ten years," he said, "you stood in my counting rooms and I never once suspected. Do you know why? Because I assumed everyone was like me. I assumed anyone with a dangerous secret would sell it." He picked up the pen. Set it down. Picked it up again. "What do you want, Galloway? Truly. Name it and it's yours. Gold, passage, the guild itself—"

"The truth," I said. "Out loud. Where the clerks can hear it. That's all. That's the whole price."

"And then?"

"Then nothing. Then you go wherever tribunals send men like you. Justice, Corvane. Not satisfaction. I've learned they don't come bundled."

He looked at me for a long time. Down in the stairwell, the boots were close now, and among them I could hear—through stone, through oak, through ten years of walls—a familiar voice giving clipped orders, hoarse and furious and unmistakably sober for the first time since I'd known her. Sera had gotten loose. Of course she had. She was Sera.

Corvane straightened his cuffs. It was such a small, human gesture, such a *bookkeeper's* gesture, that my anger nearly broke apart with pity.

"When the Veil comes for this city," he said, "and it will, and soon—and there is no ledger, no order, no one deciding who is eaten first—remember that I tried to spare you the screaming. Remember that someone stood at the top of the stairs."

"I'll remember," I said. "That's rather my specialty too."

The doors burst open, and the Warden-General's clerks poured in with lanterns and writs, and Sera Voss pushed through them with a bruised jaw and a stolen guard's cloak and the fiercest grin I have ever seen on a living face, and Corvane Galloway's guildmaster—Aldous Corvane, master of the Gilded Scale, seller of names, keeper of order—looked at the lot of us and said, in a voice pitched perfectly to carry:

"I confess. Take the ledger. It's all there. Every name, every buyer, every date." And then, quieter, just to me, as they bound his wrists with the particular gentleness reserved for gentlemen: "The broker you're planning to bargain with—you are, don't insult us both—it will cheat you. It cheats everyone. Whatever it offers, whatever it asks for, check the fine print twice."

"That's the first honest advice you've given me in ten years."

"It's the only kind worth giving at the end." He paused at the door, flanked by clerks, and glanced back at the window, at the starless sky. "Galloway. When you find the binding-name—if you find it—there's an entry in the vault registry, year of the Grey Frost, catalogued under curiosities. A bottled echo. I never sold it. Some prices even I wouldn't take."

And they took him away down the stairs, and the tower was quiet, and the hundreds of small voices settled like snowfall.

Sera leaned against the doorframe beside me, wincing, grinning. "Well," she said. "That was the single most unsettling thing I've ever witnessed, and I once watched a man arm-wrestle a bear."

"He confessed."

"He confessed. To clerks. With witnesses." She shook her head slowly. "You know what the worst part is? Part of me wanted him to fight. Wanted him to deny it, snarl, make it *worth* something. Instead he just... balanced his accounts." She looked at me sideways. "How are you doing? Honestly. Ledger-honest."

I considered the question. My hands were shaking. My chest felt like a room after a fire—scorched, standing, strangely clean. Justice, it turns out, tastes like nothing at all. It's a receipt, not a meal.

"I think," I said, "we need to get to the guild vaults before the Warden-General seals them. Corvane mentioned a bottled echo. Year of the Grey Frost. And I think—" my heart was suddenly hammering, because the shape of it was assembling itself, the hound's memories and the archive pages and now this last reluctant gift from a damned man, "—I think it might be the echo of a queen."

Sera stared at me. Below us, the city glittered its frightened lights against the dark, and somewhere far to the north, something with a king's shadow was waiting for its coronation.

"The vaults," she said, pushing off the doorframe. "Now. Before someone else reads the fine print."

We ran.
