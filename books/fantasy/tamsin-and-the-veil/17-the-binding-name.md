## 17. The Binding-Name

Sera Voss had spent eleven years learning to read wards the way other people read weather, and in all that time she had never once seen a ward drawn on a napkin.

"Edmund," she said, staring at the scrap of cloth spread across the safehouse table between a heel of bread and a stub of candle, "you've just sketched the binding architecture of the monarchy on something that was used to wipe ale off someone's chin."

"It was a clean napkin," Edmund said, not looking up from their work. Their pencil moved with the quick, apologetic precision Sera had come to recognize as their natural state—everything they did seemed to be apologizing for taking up space while doing it beautifully anyway. "Mostly clean."

Lyra leaned over Edmund's shoulder, hair still smelling faintly of woodsmoke from the night's escape through the tannery district. "Explain it again. Slowly. I was a constellation for four thousand years; we didn't do paperwork."

"Right." Edmund tapped the napkin. "The Veil's broker wants the king's binding-name. We assumed that meant some word of power buried in the royal archive. It doesn't. The archive has copies, and copies are worthless—the Veil eats written names like a goat eats a love letter. What the broker wants is the *living* name. Aldric's birth-name. The one his mother spoke over him when he was three days old, before anyone wrote it down, before it became official. A name given in love instead of law."

"And where," Sera said, though her stomach had already begun to sink, because she could see where Edmund's pencil was circling, "does one find a name spoken once, sixty years ago, by a woman who has been dead for forty?"

Edmund looked up. They had ink on their cheek and exhaustion in their eyes and that particular stubborn set to their mouth that Sera was beginning to find, against every professional instinct she possessed, endearing.

"The first queen's memory," Edmund said. "Which is preserved. In a bottle."

* * *

The bottled echo sat, at this moment, in the Gilded Scale guild's deep vault, catalogued under Impossible Trades, entry number two hundred and six. Sera knew this because Edmund had told her about it weeks ago, back when it had been an amusing anecdote—a jar containing the last spoken words of a dead queen, sold to the guild by a grave-robber who hadn't known what he had, priced at twelve crowns because Guildmaster Corvane had assumed it was a fake.

Twelve crowns. For the only key to saving the kingdom. Sera had served in the border wardens long enough to know that the difference between salvation and catastrophe was usually somebody's bad arithmetic.

"So let me make sure I understand the plan," Lyra said. She was pacing, which she did when she was frightened, her bare feet leaving faint impressions of light on the floorboards that faded after a heartbeat—less light than she'd had even a week ago. She was dimming. Everyone could see it. Nobody said it. "We break into the guild. The guild where Corvane knows your face, keeps his faceless friends on retainer, and maintains a private army of clerks who can smell a stolen ledger at fifty paces."

"When you put it that way it sounds bad."

"I haven't put it any way yet. That was just the facts."

"We don't break in," Edmund said. "I walk in."

The room went quiet. Even the mice in the wall—Sera had noticed them days ago, and noticed how Edmund always left crumbs near the baseboard, and said nothing about either thing—seemed to stop moving.

"You're joking," Sera said.

"I'm not." Edmund folded the napkin with unnecessary care, creasing it twice, buying time. "Corvane hasn't reported me stolen. Think about it—he can't. If he tells the Veil's agents I'm missing, they'll ask why one of his own bookkeepers ran, and then they'll start asking what else he's been hiding, and Corvane's whole survival strategy is being too boring to investigate. As far as the guild knows, I've been out sick. Grief, maybe. Everyone thinks my cousin died in the starfall panic. I have a whole story. I practiced it in the mirror."

"You practiced a lie in the mirror," Sera said slowly, "and you're telling me this now, and I'm supposed to believe you're the same person who couldn't admit to hearing rats talk for ten years."

"People change." Edmund's mouth twitched. "It's been a very educational month."

* * *

They argued about it until midnight, which in practical terms meant Sera argued, Lyra offered increasingly unhelpful suggestions involving her remaining stellar light ("I could go in as a distraction—" "You could go in as a *sputtering candle*, Lyra"), and Edmund sat with their hands wrapped around a cooling cup of tea, calm as a stone in a stream, until Sera ran out of objections and had to admit, privately, in the locked room of her own chest, that the real objection wasn't tactical at all.

The real objection was that she'd already lost people to this fight. That the thought of walking Edmund to the guild doors and waiting outside, counting heartbeats, made her feel like a warden watching a ward-line flicker—knowing exactly how it failed, knowing there was nothing in her hands but chalk and prayer.

She didn't say that. Wardens didn't say things like that. Instead she said, "I'm coming with you as far as the delivery entrance. I know the guard rotations. Old habit."

Edmund looked at her for a moment too long, and Sera got the distinct, uncomfortable feeling that beasts weren't the only ones who could hear things unsaid.

"All right," Edmund said softly. "Old habit."

* * *

The Gilded Scale at dawn looked exactly as it always had, which was the cruelest part. Brass scales gleaming in the windows. The caged messenger-hawks drowsing on their perches. The smell of ink and animal and money, layered like sediment. Sera stood in the shadow of the delivery archway in a porter's coat two sizes too big for her, and watched Edmund—shoulders hunched, ledger-case in hand, the picture of a tired clerk coming in early—get waved through the side door by a guard who didn't even look up from his breakfast.

It took everything she had not to follow.

She counted instead. Warden's trick: count to three hundred, and if nothing has gone wrong yet, count to three hundred again. She was somewhere in the second thousand when a small grey shape detached itself from the gutter spout beside her and landed on her boot.

A rat. Grey, one ear notched, eyes black and bright and—she would have sworn to this later, under oath if necessary—*expectant*.

"I don't talk to rats," Sera told it.

The rat groomed its whiskers, unimpressed.

"I don't," she insisted. "That's Edmund's department. I'm the one with the chalk and the bad decisions."

The rat squeaked twice, sharp, urgent, and then did something no ordinary rat did: it ran three steps toward the delivery door, stopped, looked back at her, and ran three steps again. Come on. This way. Hurry.

Every instinct Sera owned went off at once. She glanced up and down the alley—empty—and then, feeling like a madwoman, like a drunk at a gutter shrine talking to vermin, she followed the rat.

It led her along the wall, through a gap in the coal store, down a stair she hadn't known existed behind the guild's old ice house, to a rusted door with a lock so old it was practically decorative. And there, crouched in the dark with a pry bar and a face pale as tallow, was Edmund.

"How—" Sera started.

"The mice heard the vault rotation changed," Edmund whispered. "New locks, new schedule, everything moved up because Corvane's nervous. My way in is closed. But there's an old service tunnel from the ice house, except the door's been barred from the inside for thirty years and I can't—" They stopped. Looked at her. Looked at the pry bar. "I was actually just about to give up and come get you."

"Uh-huh."

"I was!"

"Give me the bar, Edmund."

* * *

The deep vault smelled of cold stone and old paper and, beneath it, faintly, of animals—the ghost-scent of every creature that had ever been bought or sold or stored below the Gilded Scale. Sera felt it crawl up her arms like frost. Edmund felt it too; she saw them pause on the stairs, hand pressed flat to the wall, listening to whatever the stones remembered.

"They're not happy down here," Edmund murmured. "The walls. There were beasts kept in these rooms once. A long time."

"Can you hear them? Still?"

"Echoes. Like voices in a seashell." Edmund's jaw tightened. "Come on. Two hundred and six."

The Impossible Trades section occupied a low-ceilinged room lined with shelves, each item tagged, boxed, bottled, or—in one memorable case—muzzled. Sera tried not to look at the muzzled case. Entry numbers ran in faded ink along the shelf edges: 190, 195, 200...

205. A jar of fog that screamed when opened. (Sera decided firmly not to ask.)

206. A squat glass bottle, cork sealed with red wax, holding nothing visible at all. The tag read, in Edmund's own neat handwriting: *Bottled echo. Last words of Queen Maren, first of her name. Provenance dubious. Value: sentimental.*

"Sentimental," Sera breathed. "You priced the kingdom's survival at sentimental."

"I priced it honestly. At the time, that's what it was worth to me." Edmund lifted the bottle with both hands, the way you'd lift a sleeping child, and Sera watched their expression do something complicated—reverence and fear and a strange tenderness all at once. "A dying queen's last words. Do you know what most people's last words are? Names. Always names. The people they loved. And a queen who died protecting her son—" Edmund's thumb traced the wax seal. "Her last words will be his birth-name. Spoken with everything she had left. That's not a copy. That's the living thing. That's the name *as it was given*."

"And the broker will accept it?"

"The broker wants a name of equal weight to the binding. A mother's whole heart, poured into a single word?" Edmund tucked the bottle into their coat, against their chest, like a second heartbeat. "There isn't heavier currency in the world."

* * *

They almost made it out.

Later, Sera would replay those last fifty yards a hundred times, looking for the moment it turned, and never quite find it. Perhaps the new locks had reported tampering. Perhaps Corvane's clerks had simply gotten better at smelling wrongness. Either way, they were halfway up the ice-house stair when the lamplight above them sharpened, and a voice came down the steps—smooth, courteous, and wrong, a voice like many voices agreeing with themselves.

"There you are," it said. "Entry two hundred and six. We've been looking forward to meeting its custodian."

Sera shoved Edmund backward down the stairs and put herself between them and the light, chalk already in her hand, drawing a ward-circle in the air the way you'd draw a knife.

Three figures stood at the top of the stair. They wore clerk's coats and clerk's faces, and that was the horror of them—the faces were *almost* right, features arranged the way a child arranges a portrait, everything present and nothing connected. The middle one held out a hand.

"The ledger was a fair trade," it said. "This is a better one. Give us the bottle, and the warden walks free. You have our word."

"Your word," Sera said, "is a chorus reading from a script."

"Yes," the clerk agreed pleasantly. "But it's a very old script."

Below her, she heard Edmund breathing fast, heard the soft clink of glass against a button, and thought: *run, you fool, run, I can hold a doorway for ten seconds, that's what wardens are for—*

Then Edmund stepped up beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and held out the bottle.

And Sera's heart dropped through the floor—

—until she saw the mouse.

It sat on Edmund's palm, tiny and trembling and absolutely resolute, and Edmund said, in a clear voice that carried up the stairwell, "Here's your trade. One echo, freely given." And the mouse leapt from their hand, landed on the bottle's wax seal, and *bit down*.

What came out of the bottle was not sound. It was older than sound. It was a woman's whole final moment unfolding in the dark of the stairwell—warmth, and lamplight, and a weight in the arms, and a word spoken so full of love that the very stones of the guild, the stones that remembered cages, seemed to lean toward it. Sera felt it pass through her like sunlight through water, and she saw the clerks' borrowed faces *shudder*, because they were built to eat names and this was a name wrapped in something they could not digest.

The wax seal cracked. The echo rose, unfurled, filled the stairwell top to bottom—

—and then, gently, like a bird finding a window, it poured itself into the mouse.

The mouse glowed. Briefly, impossibly, it glowed like a lamp, like a star, like a queen's whole heart wearing a small grey body, and the clerks recoiled from the light with a sound like a page tearing, and fled upward into the dark.

Silence. Dust settling. The mouse sat on the step, glowing faintly, whiskers twitching.

"Well," Sera said, when she could speak. "I hope you know what you just did."

"I gave a dead queen a body," Edmund said shakily. "Sort of. A temporary one. A very small one." They knelt, and the mouse climbed onto their shoulder and settled next to their ear like it had lived there all its life, and when Edmund spoke again their voice cracked straight down the middle. "She says his name. She says it whenever I ask. She *wants* to. Forty years in a bottle, and all she wants is to say her son's name to someone who can carry it to him."

Lyra found them twenty minutes later at the safehouse, took one look at the glowing mouse, one look at their faces, and said, "All right, I want the whole story, and if anyone tells me it's 'sentimental' I'm setting something on fire."

Edmund laughed—that startled, disbelieving laugh Sera was beginning to collect like coins—and set the mouse on the table in the candlelight.

"Her name is Maren," Edmund said. "And she remembers everything. And tomorrow night, we take her son's true name to the bargaining floor—and pray to every dark star left in the sky that the broker keeps half the bargains he makes."

On the table, the little glow pulsed once, steady as a heartbeat, and Sera could have sworn she heard it—not words, not yet, but the shape of a word, waiting:

a mother's voice, saying her son's name, over and over, ready.
