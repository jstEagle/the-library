## 18. Throne of Many Mouths

The palace of Aldermere had been built to impress, and it did—impressively badly, in Edmund's opinion, for anyone who had ever spent a night listening to what lived beneath marble floors.

We came in through the kennels.

It had been the hound's idea, which felt fitting. *The servants' doors are warded,* he'd told us, ears flat as we crouched in the dark of his run three nights ago. *But the kennel gate is only ever spelled against dogs leaving. No one thinks a dog would want to leave.* He had said this with the weary dignity of an old soldier describing the stupidity of generals, and Sera had laughed for the first time since her rescue—a short, cracked sound, like a door opening on rusted hinges—and I had loved her a little for it.

Now the three of us moved through the under-halls of the palace: me, Sera, and Lyra, whose skin still held a faint shimmer, like moonlight caught in milk, though she complained it was fading by the hour. She was down to perhaps a quarter of her light. She had not told us this. I had heard it from the mice, who noticed everything and gossiped about all of it.

"Left at the wine cellar," Sera whispered, one hand trailing the wall. Her ward-sense read spells the way I read ledgers—in lines and weights and places where the numbers didn't add up. "There's a seam here. Old work. The Veil's agents patched over it but they patch like clerks, not masons."

"Insulting my former colleagues," I murmured. "Bold, two days before they try to kill us."

"They were never your colleagues. You were a bookkeeper."

"I was a bookkeeper *adjacent* to unspeakable evil. There's a difference. It's mostly the pay."

Lyra snorted. Even now, even walking toward the thing that had eaten her sisters, she found me funny, or pretended to, and I clung to that pretense like a rope over a drop.

The seam in the wall was real—a hairline crack where the original stone met newer spellwork, and through it the wards thinned to almost nothing. Sera pressed her palm flat and hummed three low notes, the way she'd taught me on the road north, and the crack widened into a gap just wide enough for a person who had spent ten years making themselves small.

I went first. Of course I went first. It was my name we were carrying, after all—or rather, my name we were *not* carrying, because the true plan, the mad plan, the plan I had agreed to at three in the morning beside a dying fire while Lyra counted her remaining memories aloud like coins, depended on no ledger, no stolen binding-name, no bargain kept.

It depended on me giving myself away.

Above us, muffled by six feet of stone, the coronation-renewal rite had already begun. I could feel it through the floor—not a sound exactly, but a pressure, like the moment before a sneeze stretched out forever. The whole palace was holding its breath. So was the city. So, I suspected, was the sky.

* * *

The service stair let out behind a tapestry depicting the First King's triumph over "the Hungry Dark," which was what the history books called the Veil when they bothered to call it anything at all. The tapestry showed the king radiant, sword raised, dark thing banished. It did not show the sacrifice. History books rarely do; they're written by people who prefer their ledgers balanced on paper alone.

Through the weave I could see the throne room.

I had expected darkness. What I saw instead was worse: light. Hundreds of candles, chandeliers blazing, the great windows thrown open to a starless sky—and filling the room, rank upon rank, the court of Vaelmoor, dressed in their finest, smiling their finest smiles. And before the throne, kneeling in a circle of white salt that gleamed like teeth, King Aldric, robed in silver, head bowed.

His shadow was wrong. That was the detail my eyes snagged on and refused to release. Everyone else's shadow fell away from the candlelight, long and ordinary. His pooled *toward* him, drinking the light, and it moved when he did not—a slow, tidal shifting, like something breathing under a blanket.

And above him, coalescing out of the air itself, hung the chorus.

I don't know how else to describe it. Faces—hundreds of them, faint as breath on glass, overlapping, drifting, each one a person who had once been named. Some wore expressions of terror frozen mid-scream. Some smiled. One, near the edge, looked so much like a young woman I'd seen in a village north of Harrow's Ford—the one who had forgotten her own name and wandered the lanes beaming—that my stomach turned over.

"That's it," Lyra breathed beside me. Her voice had gone strange, resonant, the way it did when her old nature pressed close to the surface. "That's what took them. My sisters. They're in there." Her hands were shaking. "They're *singing*."

She was right. Now that I listened—really listened, letting the gift off its leash for the first time in this place—I could hear it. Not words. Something older than words. A chord made of every voice the Veil had eaten, sustained for centuries, and underneath it, thin and exhausted, a single human voice trying to hold its own note.

Aldric. Still singing. After all these years, some part of him had never stopped.

"Edmund." Sera's hand on my shoulder. "Edmund, look at me. You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where your eyes go somewhere else and you come back with terrible news."

"The chorus," I said. "It's not a cage. It's a *choir*. He's been singing with it. Willingly, at first—" I thought of the hound's story, the boy-king and the plague, the desperate bargain. "He thought he was conducting. He doesn't know he's been swallowed."

"Then we remind him." Sera checked the knife at her belt, then the second knife, then—for reasons known only to her—the third. "Rite renews at midnight. We have maybe twenty minutes. Edmund, when you walk out there, every agent in this building will feel you. Your name is a lit torch in a dark room. Are you ready for that?"

I thought about it honestly, the way I used to audit accounts. Ten years of hiding. Ten years of being Edmund-the-invisible, Edmund-the-ledger, Edmund-who-never-met-your-eye-because-eye-contact-invites-questions. And on the other side of the scale: a horse saying my name in the dark. A stable full of beasts who knew me better than any human ever had. Sera laughing in a gutter shrine. Lyra calling me *funny*, as if that were a fact about the universe rather than a generous fiction.

"No," I said. "But I've been not-ready for ten years. It's practically a skill."

Sera grinned, quick and fierce. "Gods, I've missed terrible plans."

* * *

We went in through the east door, because the west door was guarded and because I have always believed that if you must do something insane, you should at least do it with good staging.

The effect was immediate and gratifying. Three hundred courtiers turned as one, their smiles flickering like candle flames in wind. The music—there *was* music, thin and sweet and wrong, played by musicians whose eyes were white as boiled eggs—stumbled to a halt.

And the broker stepped out from behind the throne.

I had last seen it in the market beneath Vaelmoor, hooded, its voice like many voices speaking in imperfect unison. Now it wore a clerk's robes of deep violet, and its face—it had made an effort, I'll give it that. A face assembled from consensus: pleasant, forgettable, the face of someone you'd swear you'd met but couldn't say where. Its mouth smiled a half-second after the rest of it.

"Ledger-keeper," it said, and the many voices harmonized on the word. "You've brought yourself. How efficient. And here I feared you'd try something clever."

"We did try something clever," I said. "This is it."

Behind me, Sera moved left. Lyra moved right. The choreography we'd rehearsed in a barn outside the city, marked out in flour on the floorboards while the farm cat supervised with visible contempt.

"You have the binding-name?" the broker asked.

"I have something better."

"Nothing is better than the binding-name. It is the lock's own tongue. It is—"

"It's a *scrap of paper*, is what it is. A word in a dead queen's memory. You want transactions? Fine. Here's mine." I walked forward, one step, two, into the open floor, into the sightline of the king and his pooling shadow. "You feed on names. Freely given names are the rarest meal you've ever tasted—you said so yourself, in the vaults, when you thought I wasn't listening. Rats hear everything, by the way. Terrible security, your market."

The broker's consensus-face did something complicated. Several of its voices muttered to each other.

"A freely given name," it said slowly, "of sufficient weight. Yes. That would... suffice. That would more than suffice." Greed, I discovered, sounds like a choir going slightly sharp. "Offer it, then. Here, before the rite completes, and the king goes free, and your warden walks out of our cells, and the star-child keeps what light remains. All parties satisfied. All accounts settled."

"All accounts settled," I repeated, and hated how much I sounded like Corvane in that moment, and forgave myself, because the alternative was freezing.

I opened my mouth to give it—to give away the name only a horse had spoken, the name my mother had whispered and no record held, the last private thing I owned—

And the broker moved.

Not toward me. Toward *Lyra*.

Later, I would understand it perfectly, in the way one understands a fraud only after the money is gone: it had never intended to honor the trade. Why should it? It was the Veil's broker. Contracts were for creatures with only one mouth. It had needed me to *begin* the offering—needed the name half-loosed from my lips, vulnerable, in the air—so it could take everything at once: my name, Lyra's fading light, Sera's life, the whole basket of goods, no payment rendered.

Its hand closed around Lyra's wrist, and the light in her skin guttered like a lamp in a draft.

"*No!*" The word tore out of me, and with it—uncontrolled, ungoverned, the gift flaring like a struck match—every beast within a mile of the palace screamed.

Dogs in the kennels. Horses in the royal stables. Ravens off the rooftops, rats in the walls, the palace cats, the doves in the courtyard cote, a bear kept for the autumn hunt—all of it, all those voices, pouring through me and out of me in a wave that hit the throne room like weather. Courtiers staggered. Candles bent sideways. The chorus above the throne rippled, and for one instant, hundreds of eaten faces turned toward the sound with something like longing.

In that instant of chaos, three things happened at once, and I have replayed them a thousand times since, and they always happen in the same order, and it is always too fast.

First: Sera reached the great doors—the ones the agents would come through—and planted herself before them, knives drawn, ward-craft blazing up her arms in blue-white lines like frost climbing a window. She had told me once, drunk, in the gutter shrine, that she'd failed her one great duty. She had also told me, sober, on the road, that she intended to die correcting the arithmetic. She braced her shoulder against the doors and began to sing the oldest ward-song there is, the one wardens aren't taught anymore because it isn't in any book: the one that says *not through me.*

Second: the doors shuddered. Then boomed. Faceless clerks on the other side—dozens of them, dozens, the whole ledger of the Veil's servants come to collect—and Sera held, and the frost-lines on her arms cracked, and she held.

Third: Lyra looked at me across the throne room, her wrist still in the broker's grip, her light down to embers, and she smiled.

It was not a brave smile. That's what I need you to understand, whoever reads this, whatever remains of this account. It was not the smile of a martyr. It was furious. It was the smile of someone who has been robbed and has decided, on the spot, to rob the robber.

"Edmund," she said, and her voice rang like a bell cracking, "tell my sisters I'm coming."

And she burned.

All that remained of her—months of stolen memories, the last hoarded light of a constellation that had wheeled over the world since before there were names for stars—she poured out in a single instant, a silent detonation of pure radiance that filled the throne room like water fills a cup. The broker recoiled, shrieking in forty voices. The chorus scattered like smoke in wind, faces dissolving, and in the whiteness I saw them clearly for one heartbeat: not monsters, not mouths, just people, blinking, bewildered, *free* for the length of a flash.

The light seared through my closed eyelids and printed the world in negative: black candles, black courtiers, a black king rising from his knees with his hands over his face, screaming—not the chorus's scream, not the many-voiced drone, but one cracked, human, terrified voice, a boy's voice, a voice that had been waiting thirty years to remember itself.

When my vision cleared, the room was dim. Half the candles dead. The chorus gone—not destroyed, I knew, nothing that old is destroyed, but *blinded*, staggering, its thousand eyes full of spots.

Lyra stood where she had stood, and she was—smaller. Dimmer. A girl-shaped absence with a few sparks still clinging to her like fireflies to a window. She swayed.

I caught her. Of course I caught her. She weighed almost nothing, the way stars weigh almost nothing, which is to say she weighed everything.

"Told you," she whispered, "I had a little left over. For emergencies."

"You absolute idiot," I said, and my voice broke on it, warm and wrecked. "You glorious, catastrophic idiot."

"Bookkeeper," she murmured, sagging against me, "you should see the other constellation."

Then the doors exploded inward, splinters and blue fire, and Sera flew backward across the marble, and the clerks came through the smoke in a silent tide, and above the broken throne the shadow of the king rose up off the floor, vast now, wounded, *hungry*, and turned its attention—its entire, ancient, starving attention—on me.

On the name still caught in my throat. Half-offered. Half-mine.

The broker's consensus-face reassembled itself out of the smoke, smiling its half-second-late smile, and every voice in it said, together, with terrible tenderness:

"Now, ledger-keeper. Let's conclude our transaction."
