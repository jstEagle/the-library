## 19. The Name I Was Given

The throne room doors were still ringing from where Sera had slammed them shut behind us.

She'd wedged her warden's pike through the iron handles with the economy of a woman who had done this before and expected to do it again, and then she'd turned around, spat blood onto marble that had never once been spat upon in three hundred years, and said, "You have until they get through that. Which is longer than you think, because I'm standing here."

"Sera—"

"Go." She didn't look at me. She was watching the doors the way a hawk watches a field. "I've spent ten years being blamed for a door I failed to hold. Let me hold one properly."

There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't waste her time, so I ran.

* * *

The throne room was not what I expected, though I don't know what I expected. Gold, perhaps. Darkness. Something grander than a room that looked, in the strange starless light, like any guildhall in Vaelmoor — long, cold, hung with banners whose colors I couldn't quite fix in my memory no matter how hard I stared. That was the Veil's touch, I understood then. The room was eating the details even as I looked at them. The banners were becoming *banners*. The floor was becoming *a floor*. Everything here was on its way to being nothing in particular.

And on the throne sat King Aldric, radiant and vacant, his mouth open, and out of his mouth came the chorus.

It was worse up close. From the square below it had sounded like one voice multiplied. Here, close enough to see the way his throat moved wrong — too many shapes for one throat — I could hear that it wasn't multiplication at all. It was *addition*. Voice after voice layered into his, each one a name that had been eaten, each one still trying to finish saying itself.

Lyra got there first. Of course she did. She had been running since the crater, since the moment she'd learned her sisters were inside that sound, and she crossed the throne room floor like something falling upward.

"You took them," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle like ice on a pond. "Vela. Mira. The Twins. You took them and you wore their light."

*We took what shone,* the chorus said, gentle as a mother, horrible as only gentle things can be when they are lying. *Come home, little sister. We left your place empty.*

"I know," Lyra said. "I know you did."

And she opened her hands, and burned.

I have seen fires. I grew up in a border city; fire is a neighbor everyone has. But this was not fire. This was the last of a constellation spending itself all at once — white light with a blue heart, light that cast shadows going the wrong direction, light that made the banners remember their colors for one glorious instant before the brilliance swallowed everything. Lyra screamed as she burned, and the scream was grief and fury and something underneath both that sounded almost like relief, and the chorus *shrieked*, a hundred stolen voices hitting a note they had never agreed on, and the king's body arched against the throne like a man waking from a nightmare he couldn't find the edge of.

"Edmund!" Sera's voice, from far away, from the doors. The boom of something enormous testing iron. "Whatever you're going to do — do it *now*!"

I ran up the steps of the dais, and the broker stepped out of the dark beside the throne.

I should have known it would be there. It had always been there, in every deal, in every ledger line, in Corvane's careful smile and the hooded buyer whose voice was many voices. It stood like a clerk at a counter that wasn't there, ink-stained fingers folded, its face a smooth absence wearing the suggestion of features the way fog wears the suggestion of a street.

*You brought me nothing,* it said. Not angry. Never angry. Disappointed, the way an accountant is disappointed. *The binding-name. You promised the binding-name.*

"I lied," I said. "I'm told that makes me a trader."

*It makes you food.* It extended one hand toward me, unhurried, and I felt my edges go soft — my name tugging at its roots, the word *Edmund* loosening from me like a knot someone else was untying. *But I am patient. I have eaten patience. Give me the king's name, and you may keep yours a while longer. Everyone sells eventually. You above all people know the price of everything.*

And here is the thing I will tell honestly, because the whole story deserves honesty: for one heartbeat, I considered it.

Not because I wanted to live. Because I was so *tired*. Ten years of hiding, months of running, Sera bleeding behind a door, Lyra burning herself hollow, and all of it balanced on whether I could produce a single word. One word, and it would stop. One word, and everyone could rest.

That is how it gets you. Not with hunger. With *mercy*.

Behind me, faintly, through stone and iron and the shrieking of a wounded constellation, I heard a horse.

Not any horse. The bay mare from the Gilded Scale stable, the one who had spoken my true name on the night the stars went out — the name no living person knew, the name my mother had carried to her grave rather than let the guild records touch it. Her voice came thin and clear across whatever distance beasts measure distance in, and she said it again. My name. My real one. And beneath it, rising like water finding its level, every other voice: rats in the archive walls, ravens on the roof slates, the old elk's ghost-note, the menagerie hound, the mice in the pantry, the pigeons, the oxen, the moths — hundreds of small hearts all beating the same syllable back to me, over and over, the way a congregation says amen.

They remembered me. When the Veil made the world forget details, the beasts held mine like a coal cupped in bare hands.

The broker's hand stopped.

*What are you doing?* it asked, and for the first time, the many-voiced calm developed a crack in it.

What was I doing. I was doing the thing I should have seen from the beginning, back in the Gilded Scale, cataloging impossible trades. Every deal I had ever watched Corvane make had the same flaw, the same rot at the center: they were all *payments*. A name given under threat. A name sold out of fear. A binding renewed by sacrifice, generation after generation, because the first king had believed the only language the Veil understood was debt.

But I had spent a decade listening to beasts. And beasts do not pay. Beasts *give* — a bird gives its song, a dog gives its vigil, a horse gives its back and its courage, freely, without invoice, and no one has ever managed to put that on a ledger.

"You want a name of equal weight," I said. "Fine. Here's one."

I let go.

I don't have better words for it, and I am a novelist's narrator, so believe me when I say I reached for better words and found none. I took the name the mare had spoken — the true one, the buried one, the one that weighed as much as a king's because it had been kept as carefully — and instead of clutching it, instead of trading it, I held it out the way you hold out bread to something starving.

*I give this to you,* I said, in the speech that needs no sound. *Freely. No debt. No bargain. It's yours. Keep it well.*

The broker recoiled.

That was the moment I understood, in the last second available for understanding things, why the Veil ate names and could never be satisfied. It was not a predator. Predators digest. It was a *hunger shaped like a clerk*, and hunger cannot process a gift. Every name it had ever taken had come with fear attached, and fear is a kind of payment, and payment creates debt, and debt creates more hunger, forever, the snake eating its own tail down through the centuries. But a gift — a thing given with love and no expectation —

A gift has no hook in it. Nothing to pull. Nothing to owe.

The Veil tried to swallow my name and found, at the center of it, no transaction. Only a person who had finally decided they were worth giving away.

The chorus stopped.

All of it. Every stolen voice, mid-syllable, and in the silence I heard them — really heard them — the eaten names, Vela and Mira and the Twins and thousands more, and they were not screaming anymore. They were sighing. The sound of a debt forgiven. The sound of a ledger closing.

Then the binding shattered.

It did not explode. It *cracked*, like river ice in spring, a great jagged seam of light running up through the throne, through the king, through the invisible architecture of a bargain a thousand years old — and along that seam poured everything the Veil had hoarded, every name coming home like birds at dusk. I felt them pass through me. Some of them laughed. One of them, high and bright and young, brushed my cheek on its way up and I knew without knowing how that it was one of Lyra's sisters, going ahead to relight the sky.

King Aldric woke the way drowning men wake: screaming, human, alive.

He came off the throne and fell, and I caught him, or he caught me, we went down together onto stones that were suddenly just stones, and he clutched the front of my coat with both hands and screamed and sobbed and said, in a voice that was only one voice, ragged and cracked and entirely his own:

"It's *dark*. Why is it — where — I've been *saying* things, I've been saying them for years, I couldn't stop—" He stopped. He looked at me. His eyes focused, and focused, and focused, hunting for my face the way a man hunts for a word on the tip of his tongue. "Who — I know you. Don't I? I know you."

"You do," I said. Or tried to.

Because something was happening to me.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. I had braced for tearing, for loss like a tooth pulled. Instead it was like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was carrying — my name lifting out of me gently, completely, the way mist lifts off a morning river, drawn up and away and *kept*, somewhere, by something that had finally been fed what it actually needed. I felt the shape of myself remain. My hands. My ache in the left knee. Ten years of ledgers and a lifetime of listening. All of it stayed.

Only the word went. The handle. The thing other people used to pick me up.

Somewhere beyond the doors, the pounding stopped. In its place: silence, and then Sera's voice, hoarse and wondering, calling my name — and getting, I could hear, nothing back. Calling it again. Hearing the room answer her instead of me.

Lyra knelt in a circle of scorched marble, dim now, ordinary, breathing hard, human as anyone. Above us, through windows that hadn't shown anything but black for months, the first star came back.

Then the second.

Then, all at once, like a city lighting at dusk, the sky remembered itself.

Aldric was still gripping my coat. He had gone quiet, tears cutting through the grime of a thousand ceremonies, staring at the place where my face should have resolved for him and didn't quite. "I can't—" he whispered. "I can't see you properly. But I remember — warmth. Someone warm was here. Someone warm did this."

"That'll do," I said, and my voice sounded fine to me, and I suspected it always would, and that no one would ever quite be sure they'd heard it.

The great doors gave way at last, iron groaning, and Sera stumbled through with her pike bent nearly double and blood on her grin, took one look at the broken throne, the weeping king, the relit sky pouring through the windows, and the space where a person should have been standing — and said, in the flattest voice I had ever heard her use:

"I held the door for *ten minutes*. What did you two do, renegotiate reality?"

"Yes," I said.

"Of course you did." She limped toward me, squinting, head tilting like a bird's. "Edmund?"

Nothing. Not even an echo. I felt her try twice more, feel the word slip off the world, and watched something complicated move through her face — grief and fury and, underneath, a fierce and terrible tenderness.

"Well," she said finally, sitting down heavily on the steps of the dais beside me, shoulder pressing against a shoulder she could no longer put a name to but refused, absolutely refused, to stop leaning on. "Somebody had better help me figure out what to call you."

Above us, the stars kept arriving. And from the stables far below, from the rooftops and the gutters and the walls of the ruined wards, faint but unmistakable, rising like dawn — every beast in the capital began to sing.
