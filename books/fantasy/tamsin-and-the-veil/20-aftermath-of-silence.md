## 20. Aftermath of Silence

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

Sera knew it was a Tuesday because she was sitting in the wrecked throne room with a bucket of water and a rag, scrubbing soot off the flagstones where the Veil had burned itself out of the world, and one of the palace clerks—a small, nervous man named Pemworth who had spent three days weeping into his ledgers—stuck his head through the broken doors and said, "Warden Voss? There's a star. Over the eastern wall. It's very small and I don't know if that's normal."

Sera dropped her rag and went out to look.

It hung low over the rooftops of the capital, a single pinprick of silver in a sky that had been black as spilled ink for eleven days. It wasn't much. It looked almost embarrassed to be there, like a guest arriving late to a funeral. But when Sera squinted at it, something in her chest unclenched for the first time since the night the wards shattered—something she hadn't realized she'd been holding.

"Is that normal?" Pemworth asked again.

"No," Sera said. "But neither are you, Pemworth, and here we all are."

By evening there were four stars. By morning, nine. They came back the way feeling returns to a numb limb—not all at once, but in slow, prickling waves, each one a small ache of returning light. The astronomers' tower, which had been silent since the Night Without Stars, began ringing its bells at odd hours, and nobody minded, because the bells meant *another one*, and people stood in the streets pointing upward like children, and some of them cried, and some of them laughed, and quite a lot did both at once, which Sera privately thought was the most honest way to greet anything.

She watched them come back from the palace battlements every dawn, cup of terrible tea in hand, counting. On the fourth day after the first return, Lyra came to stand beside her.

Lyra looked different. That was the thing nobody wanted to say out loud. When they'd pulled her out of the wreckage of the throne room, she had been blazing—literally blazing, her skin throwing off light like a banked fire, what remained of her stellar self flaring in triumph or fury or grief, Sera couldn't tell which. Now the blaze had settled into something quieter. She still glowed faintly, especially at dusk, a soft luminescence under her skin like moonlight caught in milk. But she was dimmer than she had been in the crater. Dimmer even than last week.

"You're counting wrong," Lyra said, nodding at the sky.

"I'm counting fine."

"You missed the one behind the smoke over the tannery district. The dim one. She's called Vethis." Lyra said it casually, but Sera heard the catch underneath. Every star that came back was one of Lyra's sisters, returned from being eaten—and every one that came back seemed to take something else out of Lyra when it left her body. As though she had been carrying them. As though she had been their lantern, these eleven days, holding what light she could in her mortal chest until the sky was ready to take it home.

"How many is that now?" Sera asked.

"Forty-one."

"And how many were there?"

Lyra didn't answer. She just looked at the sky with an expression Sera recognized from mirrors—the face of someone doing arithmetic they don't want finished.

"The healers say you're losing more than light," Sera said carefully. "The archivists too. Your memory. It's not coming back the way the stars are."

"I know what the healers say. I was there. I have excellent recall of things said this week." Lyra smiled, and it was mostly the old defiant smile, but there was a hairline crack running through it. "Ask me about anything before the Night Without Stars, though, and I'll give you a lovely blank look. I remember my sisters' names. I remember being a constellation. I remember falling. Everything between falling and the crater is—" she waved a hand, "—soup."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny. I've decided it's a little funny, because the alternative is sitting down in the middle of the battlement and not getting up, and I have plans today."

"What plans?"

"To stop glowing in front of the baker's boy. He proposed yesterday. He's nineteen and he thinks I'm a miracle, which, fair, but he proposed to the glow, not to me, and I need to be less miraculous by spring or that poor child is going to marry a lamp."

Sera laughed despite herself, and Lyra grinned, and for a moment they were just two women on a wall watching the sky mend itself, and Sera thought: *this*. This is what we burned the Veil for. Not the ceremony, not the songs the ballad-makers were already composing badly. This. Terrible tea and bad jokes on a cold morning, with the stars coming home one stubborn sister at a time.

* * *

The summons came on the twelfth day, delivered by a royal courier who found Sera in the guild stables, of all places, talking to a mule.

In her defense, the mule had started it.

His name was Barley, he belonged to a grain merchant, and he had been standing outside the rebuilt Gilded Scale looking mournful ever since the Veil fell. When Sera walked past, he had turned his great head and said, in the flat, patient voice of all mules everywhere, *"You're the one who listens."*

Sera had stopped dead in the street. She was not Edmund. Listening to beasts was not her gift; her gift had been ward-craft, and her gift had failed, and she had spent ten years and one spectacular disgrace learning exactly what that failure cost. But the mule had looked at her with one liquid brown eye and repeated, *"You're the one who listens,"* and something in Sera—something that had been lying face-down inside her since the gutter shrine—had rolled over and sat up.

So she talked to Barley about his sore hoof. And then about his merchant, who drank. And then, because word travels fast among beasts and faster among beasts who have recently been freed from a name-eating silence, about everything else. By the time the courier arrived, Sera had been standing in the straw for two hours while a queue formed: two cats, a raven with strong opinions about the palace kitchens, a milk cow named Duchess, and a very old dog who only wanted someone to hear the story of his whole life, which was long, and involved a lot of sticks.

"Warden Voss." The courier bowed. "Her Majesty's council requests your presence. There is a matter of your reinstatement."

Sera looked at the courier. Then at the dog, who thumped his tail once, encouragingly.

"Tell the council," she said, "I'll be along directly."

* * *

The council chamber smelled of ink and panic. Eleven days of interregnum had taught the capital's officials nothing if not urgency; the king was recovering—Aldric woke screaming and human, and every day since had been a slow, painful climb back into his own skull—but a kingdom doesn't run on a recovering king alone, and the machinery of Vaelmoor's government had seized up like a rusted hinge.

They offered her everything. Sera gave them credit for thoroughness, if nothing else.

Full reinstatement. Warden-Commander of the border garrisons. A formal pardon read aloud in the square, with the old charges struck from the record and an apology from the Crown, drafted in gold ink on vellum thick enough to stop a crossbow bolt. A house. A stipend. A title, if she wanted one—Lady Voss of somewhere green and pleasant that she'd never seen and would hate within a month.

Lord Chancellor Merric slid the papers across the table with the smooth confidence of a man who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

"All we ask," he said, "is that you accept publicly. The realm needs a story, Warden Voss. The wards failed, the stars went dark, and now they're returning. People want to believe someone held the line. You're the most convenient someone."

"The wards didn't fail," Sera said. "Corvane sabotaged them. You know that. It's in the report I filed."

"Yes, yes, the traitor guildmaster, very tragic, we're hanging him next week—" Merric waved this aside as though Corvane were a scheduling inconvenience. "But that's the *explanation*, Warden. What the people need is a *story*. And stories require heroes, and heroes require—" he smiled thinly, "—cooperation."

Sera picked up the pardon. Gold ink. Her own name written in letters tall enough to see from the back of a square. Ten years ago she would have wept for this document. Ten years ago, disgraced and drunk and blamed for a catastrophe she hadn't caused, she would have crawled across broken glass for it.

She set it back down.

"No," she said.

Merric blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"Not that kind of no. Well. Also that kind of no." Sera stood, and was pleased to note that her hands weren't shaking. "Here's my counteroffer, Chancellor. You keep your pardon. Pin it up in whatever room you keep the other lies. Instead, give me this: a charter. A new order of wardens. Small, at first. Unfashionable. We won't ride in formation and we won't wear shiny armor and half of us will spend our time crouched in ditches talking to foxes."

Merric's expression suggested he was wondering whether the woman had taken a knock during the battle. "Talking to foxes."

"Listening to beasts," Sera corrected. "That's the whole order, Chancellor. That's the entire charter. Wardens who listen. Because I'll tell you what I learned in the last month, and you can write it down: the Veil ate names, and the beasts kept theirs. Every rat in Vaelmoor remembered more than the city archive did. Every crow. Every stablehorse. The knowledge that saved this kingdom was sitting in the mouths of animals the whole time, and nobody listened because listening to beasts is what madwomen do in gutters." She tapped the table. "Never again. Give me the charter, fund it modestly, and stay out of our way. In exchange, the borders get guarded by people who can hear trouble coming before it arrives—which, given what's out past those borders, you should want very badly."

Silence around the table. Somewhere, a clerk stopped writing.

"And if we refuse?" Merric asked.

"Then I'll do it anyway, without funding, and in five years you'll be begging my wardens for intelligence reports, and I'll charge you." Sera smiled. "I learned bargaining from a friend of mine. She's very good."

* * *

They gave her the charter. Merric grumbled, but the king—thin, gray-faced, and startlingly clear-eyed for a man three weeks free of an ancient devourer—signed it himself, and looked Sera in the eye while he did it, and said only, "Thank you. For holding the door."

"For holding the door," Sera repeated. "That's one way to describe what Sera did. I'd have said 'nearly died against a wall of faceless clerks,' but yours is shorter."

Aldric almost smiled. It was clearly still an effort; his face kept forgetting how. "I'm told I owe several people thanks I can't properly give. One of them in particular." Something moved behind his eyes—grief, or its cousin. "Someone who isn't... quite findable anymore."

Sera's throat tightened. "Yeah," she said. "I know."

* * *

Lyra chose mortality on a Thursday, at sunset, on the same battlement where they'd counted the stars.

There were ninety-one of them now. Sera had stopped keeping exact count because Lyra did it better, but she knew the number was close, close enough that everyone in the capital had begun speaking of the sky in the present tense again.

"There's one left that matters," Lyra said. She was sitting on the parapet with her legs dangling over a hundred-foot drop, which no longer alarmed Sera because Lyra had explained, at length, that she'd spent ten thousand years as a pattern of light and gravity and heights were frankly beneath her dignity. "Vessarine. My youngest. She was eaten first, actually—before the rest of us, before anyone knew what was happening. If she comes back, the constellation's whole. If she comes back..."

"If," Sera said.

"If she comes back, I go with her. That's how it works. I'm the lantern, remember? When the last light goes home, the lantern goes dark." Lyra turned her hand over and watched the faint glow pulse in her palm. "Unless I let it go first. Unless I spend it. All of it, here, in this—" she gestured at herself, at the battlement, at the city smoking gently below, "—in this body. Then I'm mortal. Properly mortal. Cold feet, bad knees, death eventually, the whole humiliating package."

"And the alternative?"

"The alternative is going home. Being Vessarine's sister again. Being *seen* again, the way I was before, by everything, forever." Lyra was quiet a moment. "Do you know what I remember most clearly? From before? Not the burning. Not the eating. I remember being *looked at*. Millions of sailors and farmers and lonely children, every night, looking up. And none of them ever once wondered whether the stars got lonely back."

Sera said nothing. Below them, a cart rattled through the gate, and someone was singing badly, and the smell of bread came up from the bakeries, and the whole ordinary evening carried on.

"I met a mule named Barley," Sera said finally. "He thinks I'm interesting. Low bar, admittedly. But he listens back."

Lyra laughed—that real laugh, the one that had survived the crater and the soup-memory and the loss of ten thousand years. "You're a terrible saleswoman for mortality."

"I'm a warden. We undersell everything. It's policy."

Lyra looked at the sky a long moment. Ninety-one stars, and one empty place where a youngest sister should be. Then she closed her fist around her own light, and opened it, and let it go—not upward, but outward, into the stone of the battlement, into the air, into the warm dusk of the world, spending it like a coin she'd been saving all her life.

The glow faded from her skin. Just like that. She sat in the gathering dark looking entirely, ordinarily human, and shivered, and said, "Cold. Is it always cold?"

"Only until spring. And then it's muddy."

"Wonderful." Lyra wiped her eyes quickly, pretending it was the wind. "I want it noted that I chose this."

"Noted. Witnessed. I'll put it in the order's first ledger, right under 'Charter, Founding of.'"

"You founded an *order*?"

"Chapter and verse, star-girl. First meeting's tomorrow. Attendance is mandatory."

"I'm not a warden."

"You listen to me complain daily. You qualify."

* * *

The first meeting of the Order of the Listening Wardens—name pending, Sera insisted, because naming things was serious business and also because Edmund wasn't there to mock her choices, and somehow the mockery felt necessary to the process—consisted of seven people and one goat in the back room of the rebuilt Gilded Scale.

The guild had changed hands. With Corvane exposed and awaiting the hangman, the crown had seized the Gilded Scale, and to everyone's surprise—including, Sera suspected, her own—it had been Edmund's old ledgers that saved it. Pages and pages of impossible trades, meticulously catalogued: the bottled echo, the moth that remembered futures, the whole strange inventory of a hidden life. The new guildmaster, a broad-shouldered ex-smuggler named Otteline, had read them all twice and declared the place "too useful to burn," and now the impossible trades continued openly, above-board, taxed and regulated and infinitely happier for it.

Edmund's old desk stood in the corner. Nobody used it. Nobody had moved it.

The seven founding members were an unpromising lot: a falconer who'd lost an arm and gained, she claimed, the ability to hear hawks in her dreams; a shepherd's daughter who'd been talking to sheep her whole life and had only just learned this was unusual; a palace groom; a fishwife; two of Sera's old wardens, the only ones who hadn't denounced her during the disgrace, which she tried not to hold against the others because fear made people stupid; and the goat, whose name was Turnip and who had wandered in off the street and refused to leave, and whom the shepherd's daughter swore was saying something important.

"He says the northern passes are moving," the girl reported solemnly. "The stones. He says the stones in the high pass are walking downhill."

Sera wrote it down. That was the rule, the first rule, the only rule: *everything gets written down.* Names, testimonies, prophecies, complaints about oats. Because the Veil was dead—or sleeping, or scattered, and Sera trusted it the way you trust a snake you've only stunned—and the best defense against a thing that eats memory was ink.

When the meeting broke up, dusk had fallen, and the last star—Vessarine, Lyra's youngest—had still not appeared. Lyra stood in the doorway of the guildhall looking up at the one gap in the mended sky, mortal and cold and trying not to count the cost aloud.

"She might never come back," Sera said quietly, beside her.

"I know."

"And you chose this anyway."

"I know that too." Lyra bumped her shoulder. "You'd have done the same."

"I'd have charged the crown for it."

And that was when the beasts began.

It started with Turnip, bleating from the back room. Then the stable dogs along the street, one after another, then the rooks lifting off the guildhall roof in a black spiral, crying—not alarm, nothing like alarm. It was a sound Sera had heard once before, on the worst night of her life, when a stablehorse had spoken a true name into the dark. But this was different. This was dozens of voices, hundreds, the whole breathing city of beasts finding one word at the same moment and saying it together, over and over, gentle as rain:

*"Tamsin. Tamsin. Tamsin."*

Lyra grabbed her arm. "What are they—"

"I don't know." Sera's heart was hammering. "But I know someone who will."

Down the street, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure stood listening to the chorus with their head slightly tilted—as if hearing their own name for the very first time.
