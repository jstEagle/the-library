## 21. What the Beasts Call Me

The morning after the world was saved, I woke up in a hayloft above the Gilded Scale stables and could not remember my own name.

I want to be precise about that, because people kept getting it wrong in the weeks that followed. It wasn't that the memory had gone soft, like a word on the tip of the tongue. It wasn't grief-fog or exhaustion or the strange amnesia that follows a blow to the head. The name simply wasn't there. If I reached for it—and I reached for it often, those first days, the way a tongue worries the socket of a lost tooth—my mind slid off the place where it should have been and found nothing. Not an absence shaped like a name. Just absence.

I knew everything else. I knew I liked my tea bitter and my bread burnt at the edges. I knew Sera Voss snored like a collapsing granary. I knew Lyra still flinched when she looked up at a sky with only one star in it, and that the star was her, and that she hadn't decided yet whether that was a comfort or a wound. I knew Corvane was in custody, that King Aldric was human again and screaming some nights, and that the Veil was dead—or as close to dead as such things come—because I had fed it the only meal it could not swallow.

I had given it my name. Freely. As a gift.

And gifts, it turns out, are indigestible.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being unmade: the world does not notice.

I walked down into the guildyard that first morning expecting—hoping, maybe—that someone would gasp, would point, would say *there goes the person who saved us all*. Instead, the farrier nodded at me the way you nod at weather. The cook set out an extra plate without being asked, then frowned at it, puzzled by her own generosity. A clerk I had worked beside for ten years looked straight through me, smiled vaguely, and said, "Lovely morning, isn't it?" to the air slightly to my left of my face.

Sera found me standing in the middle of the yard holding a plate of eggs I had no appetite for.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and wait for the world to confirm you exist." She took the plate from my hands and ate an egg off it, which was her way of being gentle. "Stop it. You exist. I remember you."

"Do you?" I asked, and hated how small my voice came out.

She considered the question honestly, because Sera has never once lied to me when it mattered, even when lying would have been kinder. "Sort of," she said. "I remember there was someone. Someone who heard the horse say your name in the square, someone who stole a ledger, someone who held my hand through the bars of that cell and told me the rats were keeping watch over me." She frowned, reaching. "I can't see your face when I try. Isn't that strange? I know we sat across from each other on the road north for eleven days. I remember your boots. I remember you humming when you thought I was asleep. But your face is—" she made a gesture, opening her fingers like a bird releasing something, "—warm. That's all. You're a warm spot in my memory."

"A warm spot," I repeated.

"The best warm spot," she said firmly, and put the plate back in my hands, and that was that. Sera grieves the way she fights: briefly, completely, and then forward.

Lyra took it harder, in her way. She came to find me that afternoon, walking through the guildyard gate with the particular careful tread of someone who has recently learned how heavy a body is. When she saw me she stopped dead.

"Oh," she said, and her eyes went bright and furious, which is Lyra's version of tears. "Oh, that's not fair. That's *not fair*, you don't get to—" She crossed the yard in six strides and hugged me hard enough to crack a rib, possibly literally; she was still learning her own strength. "I remember you," she said into my shoulder. "Stars don't forget. We're made of remembering. Every light in the sky is just a memory burning." She pulled back and gripped my arms. "You're still in here." She tapped her own temple. "Bright as anything. So if everyone else is too blind to see you, that's their failure, not yours."

It was the kindest thing anyone said to me that month, and she delivered it like an accusation, which somehow made it kinder.

But it was the beasts who gave me back to myself. Of course it was.

* * *

I discovered it by accident, three days after the unbinding, when I went down to the stable to check on the old mare who had spoken my true name aloud in the square—the one whose voice had cracked my hiding open like an egg. I had been avoiding her, if I'm honest. She was the last creature alive who knew what I had been called, and I couldn't bear to stand in front of that knowledge.

She was waiting for me at her stall door.

"*There you are,*" she said, in the way horses speak—not words exactly, but meaning arriving whole, the way sunlight arrives through a window. "*We've been looking everywhere.*"

"We?" I said.

And then the stable answered. The draft horses, the plow oxen, the fat guild cat asleep in the tack room who had never once deigned to speak to me—all of them turned, all of them *looked* at me, and the meaning rolled through the building like warmth through floorboards: recognition. Complete, undamaged, detailed recognition.

They remembered everything. My name—not the sound of it, because the sound was eaten, but the shape of it, the weight of it, the way you remember the shape of a key you've thrown away. They remembered ten years of my listening to them through cage bars. They remembered every small kindness: the mouse I'd smuggled grain to during the lean winter, the raven whose broken wing I'd splinted with ledger-paper and glue, the dying elk on the north road whose last words I had carried faithfully to no one. They remembered the moment in the throne room when I had opened myself entirely and let the Veil drink.

"*Name-giver,*" the old mare said, and the other beasts took it up softly, a low rumble of it, the way thunder takes its time crossing a valley. "*That's what we call you now.*"

"Is that—" My throat closed. I tried again. "Is that enough? To have a name only beasts can hold?"

The guild cat opened one eye from the tack room. "*Foolish question,*" it said, in the tone cats reserve for foolish questions. "*A name that lives in a hundred thousand memories is worth more than one that lives in a ledger.*"

I laughed, and it came out wet, and the mare leaned her great head against my chest, and I stood in that stable and let myself be known.

* * *

The weeks that followed were the strangest of my life, and I include the week the stars went out.

People remembered me the way you remember a summer afternoon. Not as a person—a *feeling*. The cook started leaving food out for "whoever's been tidying the kitchen," though she could never catch them at it. The guild apprentices spoke of a helpful presence in the archive, someone who always seemed to know where a record had been misfiled. Sera's new warden-recruits reported that the training yard felt friendlier lately, warmer, though none of them could say why.

At first this hollowed me out. I will not pretend otherwise. There were nights I sat in the hayloft writing letters to people who would never read them—*Dear Master Corvane's clerk, you shared a desk with me for nine years*—and burning them in the stove because even the paper felt like shouting into a well. There was a terrible evening when I passed my own reflection in a shop window and startled, genuinely startled, because for half a heartbeat I did not expect anyone to be standing there.

But grief, I learned, and freedom are braided together more tightly than we like to admit. Every loss in that braid had a twin.

Nobody stared at me in the street. Nobody expected anything of me. I could sit in the Gilded Scale's common room—my common room, though the deed said otherwise—and listen to forty conversations at once, invisible, unburdened, free in a way I had spent a decade faking and never once achieved. All those years I had hidden behind ledgers, terrified of being seen, and now the hiding was done *for* me, and underneath the ache of it was something suspiciously like relief.

"I think you might be the luckiest person in Vaelmoor," Lyra told me one night. She was sitting on the guildhall roof—she liked high places, missed them, I think—and I had climbed up with two cups of tea because she was still learning that bodies need feeding. "You wanted to be no one. Most people never get to find out if they'd like it."

"And do I like it?"

She considered, starlight-for-one glinting in her hair. "Some days. Some days you cry in the pantry. I've heard you. The cat told me."

"The cat talks too much."

"The cat says you talk too much, in your head, and it gets very loud for everyone with ears." She sipped her tea. "Which is another way of saying you're not alone, Tamsin—"

"That's not my name yet," I said quickly.

"—yet," she agreed serenely, and would say nothing more about it, because Lyra had taken to calling me that—she'd heard it somewhere, she claimed, in the hum between stars, though I suspected she'd invented it herself and dressed it up in astronomy—and the name fit the way new boots fit: stiff, unfamiliar, but unmistakably mine once walked in.

* * *

King Aldric came to find me in the spring.

I should say: he tried. What actually happened was that he arrived at the Gilded Scale with two exhausted-looking guards, asked for "the person who freed me," and was met with the blank, polite faces of people trying to recall whether such a person existed. He described me. Height, roughly. Voice, warm. A habit of standing very still around animals. The guards suggested perhaps His Majesty meant the stablemaster. The stablemaster, questioned, said he'd worked alone for years, hadn't he?

I was in the yard at the time, mucking stalls, because mucking stalls is honest work and honest work was holding me together in those months. I watched the king walk past the stable doors, pause, and turn his head like a compass needle finding north.

He was thinner than at the coronation-rite, and older, and there were lines around his eyes that had not been carved by any entity, only by ordinary sleepless human nights. But his eyes themselves were his own again—brown, tired, *present*—and when they found me across the yard, they did not slide off. They landed. Stayed.

"You," he said.

The guards looked where he was looking and saw, presumably, a stablehand and a pile of straw.

"You're the one," Aldric said, coming closer, and there was something almost desperate in it. "In the throne room. There was someone—I remember a voice. I remember being so far down in the dark, and something enormous eating everything I was, and then a voice saying—" he closed his eyes, searching, "—saying *take mine instead*. Or something like it. I've asked everyone. My own mother doesn't remember anyone like you. But I remember."

He stopped in front of me. This close, I could see his hands shaking, and I understood suddenly that beneath the gratitude was terror—the terror of a man who had been possessed for thirty years and could no longer fully trust his own memories, who needed one witness, one confirmation, that the voice in the dark had been real.

"It was real," I said quietly.

His breath caught. "You *are*—"

"I'm the one," I said. "Or I was. The thing that ate my name ate most of me along with it, as far as people are concerned. You see a stablehand. Everyone sees a stablehand. Or nothing at all. It varies."

"But I see *you*," he said, wonderingly. And then, softer, with the dawning grief of a kind man doing arithmetic: "You gave it your name. For me. For all of us. And now you're—" he gestured helplessly at the yard, the guards staring through me, the whole world's polite blindness, "—nobody."

"Nobody," I agreed. And then, because the truth deserved its full weight and also its full lightness: "It's better than it sounds. The beasts remember me perfectly. Every rat in Vaelmoor knows my name, or whatever rats keep instead of names. I'm apparently quite famous among the pigeons."

Aldric laughed—a startled, cracked, entirely human laugh—and pressed the heel of his hand to his eyes for a moment. "I owe you a kingdom," he managed.

"You owe me a stablehand's wage and a hot meal," I said. "The kingdom was already mine. I live in it."

He insisted on the meal. We ate it in the stable, of all places, because he asked to meet the mare who had spoken my name in the square—he remembered that too, the horse's voice cutting through the chorus like a knife—and she regarded him for a long moment before pronouncing him, to his visible relief, "*mended, mostly.*"

Before he left, he stood in the stable door with the evening light behind him and said, "I'll find a way to give it back. Your name. There must be a way. I'll spend the rest of my reign looking if I have to."

"Don't," I said.

He blinked.

"I mean it, Majesty. Don't look. Here's the thing I've learned that maybe only I could learn: a name given away isn't a name lost. Mine bought something. It's *in* something. It's woven into the fact that you wake up screaming but you wake up *yourself*, that Lyra gets to grow old and stubborn, that Sera's wardens listen to beasts now. My name is doing work. Let it work." I shrugged, and found that I meant it all the way down. "Besides. I'm going to choose a new one. I've been practicing."

"Practicing," he repeated, and something in his face eased—the desperate thing settling, the debt rearranging itself into something a man could carry. "What kind of person practices being named?"

"Someone who's finally doing it on purpose," I said.

He bowed to me—actually bowed, king to stablehand, in front of his baffled guards—and left. At the gate he paused, looked back, and said, so quietly I nearly missed it: "Thank you, whoever you are. Thank you, whoever you choose to be."

* * *

That night I climbed to the hayloft and took out the new ledger I'd bought from the stationer who always overcharged me because he couldn't remember having shortchanged me before.

The beasts had gathered below—the mare, the drafts, the oxen, the cat draped insolently over the stall door, and beyond the walls, faint as tide, the wider chorus of Vaelmoor: dogs and doves and dock-rats, all the small listeners of the city, attentive. Word travels fast among the named-less.

I dipped my pen. On the first page, under the heading I had chosen—*Tamsin and the Veil: a true account, so that it may never be eaten*—I wrote my first line. Then I paused, pen hovering, and listened to the stable breathing below me, a hundred warm shapes who knew me utterly and wanted nothing from me except that I keep going.

"*Well?*" the old mare prompted, gentle as falling snow. "*Are you going to introduce yourself, or shall we do it for you?*"

I smiled, and set the pen to the page, and began:

"My name is Tamsin. I was born under a different name, in a year the beasts still remember, and this is the story of how I lost the one and chose the other—"

Below me, soft as rain beginning, every beast in the stable murmured the new name at once, tasting it, learning it, making room for it in their long memories. And far off across the sleeping city, from the direction of the river and the rooftops and the palace kennels, came answering voices—one, then dozens, then more than I could count—rolling toward me through the dark like a tide coming home:

*Welcome, Tamsin. Welcome home.*
