## 22. Tamsin and the Veil

The new guildhall smelled of sawdust and fresh ink and, faintly, of goat.

That last one was not in the plans, Edmund was fairly sure. The architects had drawn up a beautiful building—tall windows to let the light in, wide counters so no one had to lean over ledgers like conspirators, a reading room with actual chairs instead of the old backbreaking stools Corvane had always insisted built character. But the goats had opinions about architecture, and the goats had won a small courtyard of their own on the east side, and now the whole place carried their particular musk like a signature on every page.

Edmund found they didn't mind. A guild that smelled of goats was a guild that could be trusted, roughly speaking, to smell of something true.

They sat at the long counter in the morning light with a brand-new ledger open before them. It was a lovely thing: cream pages, a spine stitched by hand, corners bound in green leather because the bookbinder had run out of black and Edmund had said *green is fine* before realizing they meant it. The first page was still blank. That was the trouble. Every blank first page was a small dare.

Behind them, the reading room filled slowly with the morning's odd assortment—the Gilded Scale had changed its membership somewhat since the fall. There were still traders, yes, honest ones mostly, though honesty in Vaelmoor remained a matter of degree rather than kind. But there were also wardens now, three of Sera's listeners with their ink-stained cuffs and their habit of tilting their heads at empty air. There was a woman who traded only in bottled echoes and insisted on labeling each one by hand. There was a boy of perhaps twelve who had arrived one day with a moth on his shoulder and no other credentials whatsoever, and whom nobody had thought to turn away, because the moth remembered futures and the boy remembered everything else.

And there were the beasts. That was new too. The old guild had kept its animals caged in the cellar, inventory to be appraised and sold. The new guild kept a door open to the courtyard, and the animals came and went as they pleased, which meant that most mornings Edmund's ledger was reviewed by an audience of pigeons, one extremely judgmental heron, and whatever the goats felt like sending up as delegates.

"Page one," said the heron, from the windowsill. "Still blank. Still blank. Coward."

"I'm savoring it," Edmund said.

"You've been savoring it for nine days." The heron preened. "In the future I remember, you finish it today. Also you spill tea on page forty. Just so you know."

"That's not how remembering futures works, and you know it."

"The future remembers what it likes," said the heron serenely, which was the sort of thing herons said when they didn't want to be argued with.

Edmund dipped the pen. Hesitated. The trouble was not the story—they knew the story better than they had ever known anything, having spent the last months telling it over and over: to Sera, to Lyra, to Aldric, to the assembled beasts of Vaelmoor who had heard it in fragments and demanded the whole. The trouble was the name at the top of the page. Because a ledger needed a keeper's name, and Edmund did not have one anymore, and this was both the wound and the gift, depending on the hour.

Most hours it was the gift. Some hours—gray ones, rainy ones, the hour after waking—it was the wound.

They had tried living with the blank space. For a while they'd signed documents with a small drawing of a moth, which the guild had found charming and the tax assessor had found infuriating. They had tried *the Keeper*, which made them sound like a character in someone else's ballad. They had tried nothing at all, which worked fine with people—people looked straight through the gap where a name should be and smiled anyway, warm and vague, the way you smile at sunlight through a window—but it did not work with paper. Paper wanted names. Paper had always been honest that way.

A shadow fell across the counter. Edmund looked up into the face of a large brown mare who had pushed open the courtyard door with the confidence of someone who owned the building.

"You're doing the thing," the mare said. "Where you stare at the page like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money. Emotionally."

"Nine days," the mare said. She was called Buttercup, which she resented, and she had been the first beast to speak to Edmund after the Veil fell, standing in the ruins of the old stable with the stars coming back one by one overhead. *You gave your name away,* she'd said then, wonderingly, *and you're still here.* It had been the first time anyone had told Edmund they were still here, and Edmund had cried into her mane for a length of time they did not discuss in polite company.

"I'm choosing carefully," Edmund said. "It's not every day a person gets to pick."

"No," Buttercup agreed, gentler than her tone suggested. "Most of us get handed ours in a stable and never asked if it fits."

Outside, Vaelmoor went about its morning. From the tall windows Edmund could see the market stalls going up, the wardens' new tower—Sera's tower, though she'd deny it—rising pale stone against a sky that had held its stars faithfully for a full turning of the seasons now. People moved below without looking up, which was right and proper. Cities should be allowed to forget their crises at a reasonable pace. Vaelmoor had been forgetting enthusiastically: already there were songs about the Night Without Stars that got the details wrong in comforting ways, and children played a game called *Wardens and Chorus* in which the rules made no sense but everyone got to shout.

Nobody in those songs mentioned the person who had fed their own name to the dark. Not because they were ungrateful. Because they couldn't. Edmund had watched it happen a hundred times now—a friend's eyes sliding off them mid-sentence, a warm impression remaining where a person had been, the way you remember a fire's heat after the fire. Sera remembered best. Sera had fought for every scrap of it, had written things down in her own stubborn hand—*E. likes honey in tea, E. hums when counting, E. once lied to a duke with a straight face and apologized to his horse afterward*—and read them aloud on bad days like a litany. Even so, some mornings Sera greeted Edmund in the courtyard with the polite wariness of a stranger, and by afternoon the litany had done its work and she'd be arguing with them about ward-theory again, and Edmund had learned to hold both greetings gently, the stranger and the friend, because both were Sera being brave.

The beasts remembered perfectly. That was the strange arithmetic of the sacrifice: the Veil ate the name out of human memory, and the name took root in animal memory instead, growing there like a seed in dark soil. Every creature in ten miles knew exactly who Edmund was. The rats had composed a song about it. It was not a good song. It was mostly about bread.

Edmund looked down at the blank page again.

"Write the story," Buttercup said. "Start there. The name will come when it comes. Names are herd animals. They follow the writing."

"That's not—"

"Page forty," the heron reminded them from the sill. "Tea."

Edmund laughed, and dipped the pen, and wrote:

*A ledger of the Gilded Scale, kept by no one in particular.*

Then, because the pen had warmed to its work:

*Once there was a bookkeeper in the city of Vaelmoor who could hear what beasts were saying, and told no one, and thought this was the same thing as being safe.*

The words came easier after that. They came the way water finds a channel it has cut before. Edmund wrote about the impossible trades—the bottled echo, the moth that remembered futures—and about the silence of the beasts on the night the wards began to fail, a silence only one person in ten miles had noticed, and how loneliness can masquerade as diligence for years. They wrote about Corvane, without hatred, which had taken practice; about the hooded buyer whose voice was many voices; about the horse in the panic-dark speaking a name no living soul knew, and the cold that had walked up Edmund's spine on hearing it, the terrible honor of being *read*.

They wrote about Sera finding her drunk in a gutter shrine and recognizing, in the wreck of her, another liar keeping faith with something broken. They wrote about villages of smiling hollow people, and about a young woman standing in a crater with starlight going out of her by the hour, furious and grieving and refusing both. They wrote about the king's vacant radiance, and the hound in the menagerie who remembered a boy-king choosing damnation to save his people from plague, and how compassion complicates every enemy worth fighting.

They wrote about the bargaining floor, and about becoming, for one necessary hour, the kind of trader they had always despised. They wrote about the chorus offering each of them their heart's desire—honor, stars, normalcy—and about how nearly each of them had taken it, and how the nearly mattered more than the refusal, because courage that costs nothing is just posture.

They wrote about the throne room. Their pen slowed there. Some parts of the story lived closer to the bone than others: Sera holding the door alone against the faceless clerks, laughing like a woman finally using her whole self; Lyra burning the last of her stellar light in one white blaze that blinded a chorus of mouths; the broker's betrayal arriving precisely on schedule, because brokers are the most reliable creatures in any world.

And then the last part. The name given freely—not sold, not traded, not paid, *given*—which the Veil, that great devourer of taken things, could not digest. You cannot eat a gift. It goes down wrong. It stays in you and becomes yours instead of the other way around. Edmund had understood this only in the moment of doing it, standing before the throne of many mouths with the king screaming himself back into his own body, and even now they could not entirely explain it, except that the Veil had been built out of theft, and a gift was the one coin not in its currency.

When they finished that passage, the reading room had gone quiet. The pigeons had gathered on the counter rail. The judgmental heron stood at the page's edge with its head cocked, and for once it said nothing at all, which from a heron was a eulogy and a toast combined.

Edmund flexed their writing hand. Page forty waited, innocent, unsullied. They moved the tea deliberately to the far side of the desk, purely out of spite toward the future.

* * *

The name came three days later, as names do: sideways, unannounced, in the middle of something else.

Edmund was in the vaults beneath the new guildhall, cataloging the recovered inventory—the things Corvane had hoarded, the things the Veil's clerks had dropped in their flight, the strange residue of a decade of dirty trade. Most of it had been returned or destroyed. One shelf remained of items with no clear owner, and among them, wrapped in oilcloth, sat a familiar shape: a bottle, stoppered with wax, holding a curl of grey mist that moved like breath.

The bottled echo. The very first impossible thing Edmund had ever cataloged, back when they were still pretending to be only a bookkeeper. It had gone missing during the flight from the guild, and Edmund had assumed it eaten or sold, and here it was, patient as all echoes are.

They unstopped it, more from ritual than curiosity.

The echo sighed out into the vault air and hung there, and it was Edmund's own voice, ten years younger, reading aloud from an inventory list in the flat careful tone of someone hiding in plain sight: *"—one bottled echo, provenance unknown, value negotiable—"*

Younger-Edmund paused in the recording, and then, thinking themselves alone, added softly: *"One day I'll write down the real ledger. Everything. All of it. Someone should keep the true accounts."*

The echo faded. The vault was silent except for the drip of the aquifer far below, and the breathing of a rat on a barrel who had followed Edmund down and had, per rat custom, opinions.

"Well," Edmund said aloud, to the vault, to the echo, to the decade between. "That's the name, isn't it. It was never going to be a word anyone gave me. It was going to be the thing I do."

The rat squeaked agreement, or possibly requested bread.

*Tamsin.* Not a name inherited or imposed. A name like a ledger: a promise to keep true accounts. To write things down so they could never be eaten. To be, permanently and on purpose, the person who remembers out loud.

Edmund climbed the vault stairs two at a time, which they had not done in years, and went straight to the counter, and opened the green-bound ledger to its first page, and above the story they had already written, in letters large enough that even a vague warm impression of a person would leave a mark, they signed:

*Tamsin. Keeper of the true accounts.*

The heron looked at it for a long moment. "Hm," it said. "In one of the futures I remember, you pick something worse."

"Thank you."

"Don't mention it. Page forty, incidentally, remains threatened."

* * *

Word traveled the way it always did in Vaelmoor: fast, slightly wrong, and eventually to the right ears. Sera came by that evening with two cups of tea and the expression she wore when she'd rehearsed something. She set the cups down, looked at the ledger, looked at the name, and said, "Tamsin," testing it, and then again, firmer, committing it to whatever corner of her memory still fought for every scrap: "Tamsin. Good. It suits you. It sounds like someone who'd apologize to a duke's horse."

"You remember that?"

"I wrote it down." She tapped her temple. "I keep the true accounts too, these days. Somebody has to, in case you spill tea on yours."

Lyra arrived at dusk, walking up from the harbor where she'd spent the day teaching sailors to navigate by the rekindled constellations—dimmer than before, fewer than before, hers now rather than heaven's, and she wore the diminishment like a medal. She read the name off the page and grinned. "Tamsin. I knew a Tamsin once. Terrible singer. Excellent liar. Best friend I ever made in a crater." She hugged them hard enough to creak ribs, and whispered, "It fits," and that was all, and it was enough.

Even Aldric came, in plain clothes, with two nervous guards he dismissed at the door. The king stood in the reading room among the pigeons and the goats' delegates and looked at Edmund with the particular soft focus of a man seeing warmth where a person should be, and he said, "I'm told I owe someone a debt I can't properly see. I'm told she—or he, or they—gave away the one thing that can't be bought back." He frowned gently, reaching for it, and lost it, and smiled instead. "Whoever they are, I hope they know the kingdom says thank you."

"They know," Edmund said.

"Good." Aldric hesitated. "You have a kind voice. Has anyone ever told you that? Like someone reading aloud near a fire." And he bowed—to the room, to the warmth, to the ledger—and left, and Edmund sat for a while with their heart doing something complicated and quiet.

Buttercup put her head over the counter rail. "Well, Keeper. Story's written. Name's chosen. What's next on the true accounts?"

Edmund looked at the ledger—at the finished tale, safe in ink, beyond any appetite—and then past it, at all the blank pages waiting behind it like snowfield. Somewhere north, villages of smiling hollow people were slowly remembering themselves, name by name, as the Veil's grip rotted off them. Somewhere in the capital, Sera's new order of listening wardens was drafting its first oath. Somewhere in the courtyard, the goats were eating the architect's original plans, which everyone agreed was an improvement.

"Next," Edmund said, dipping the pen, "we start volume two."

And from the courtyard, the stable, the rooftops, the rafters, the riverbank, the cellar where the last caged things had long ago been freed—from every throat and burrow and branch in Vaelmoor, in one voice, at one moment, as if the whole city's beasts had been waiting with held breath for exactly this—

*Welcome home, Tamsin.*
