## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees woke before I did, as they always did, and by the time I came down the crooked stairs with my boots half-laced, the garden was already humming with the low, contented sound of a thousand small workers going about their business. Morning light came down into the valley soft and gray-gold, caught in the mist that pooled between the slopes like milk poured over stone, and everything smelled of wet earth and rosemary and the faint, sweet rot of last autumn's leaves composting at the edge of the bean rows.

I stopped at the door and breathed it in. I always stopped. It was a habit I didn't remember forming, which was true of most of my habits, if I was honest about it — and I tried not to be, most days.

"Gideon!" Maren's voice carried from the herb shed, cracked and sharp as a blackbird's call. "If you're standing in the doorway admiring the weather, you can admire it while carrying water."

"I'm admiring the bees," I called back.

"The bees don't need your admiration. They need their syrup topped off and their queens checked, and so does the east bed need weeding before the feverfew chokes itself to death out of sheer stubbornness." A pause, then, grudging: "But they are good bees this year."

They were good bees. That was the thing about the valley — even its failures were gentle ones. The hives sat along the south wall of Maren's cottage where the sun gathered all day, five white boxes gone silver-gray with age, their paint peeling like old skin. I lifted the lid on the nearest one and the hum deepened, warm and alive against my palms through the frame, and I stood there a while with smoke curling from the tin pail at my feet, doing nothing but listening.

You could live a whole life inside that sound. Some part of me had decided, long ago, that I would.

* * *

Maren was in the shed when I brought her the first combs of the season, bent over her worktable with her spectacles pushed up into her wild white hair, grinding something that smelled bitter and green. The shed was the only room in the cottage that stayed dark even at noon; bundles hung from every rafter — yarrow, wormwood, vervain, things whose names she'd taught me and things whose names she refused to give — and the air was thick enough to chew.

"Set them there." She didn't look up. "And tell me what you see."

I looked at the combs. "Capped honey, mostly. One patch of drone brood going soft in the corner — chalkbrood, maybe. Third hive from the left is queenless again."

"Mm. And what do you feel?"

That was the question she asked every time, and every time I gave the same answer, because it was the only honest one. "Nothing."

"Nothing," she repeated. She set down her pestle and finally turned those pale eyes on me — eyes the color of watered milk, too light for a face so brown and seamed. "You hold the product of six thousand living creatures in your hands, child, and you feel nothing?"

"They're bees, Maren. Not a spell."

"Everything is a spell, if you're built right." She took the comb from me, and her fingers brushed mine — dry, papery, deliberate. She held them there a moment longer than she needed to. Whatever she was looking for, she didn't find it, or she found exactly what she expected, because her mouth went flat and she turned back to her mortar. "Well. Built wrong isn't the worst fate. Go weed the feverfew."

I went. But I stood outside the shed door for a moment first, in the strip of sunlight between the eaves, and pressed my palm flat against the wood the way I sometimes did when I thought she wasn't watching. The shed was the closest thing to magic our household contained. Not because anything in it worked — nothing in it ever worked, not properly — but because Maren treated it like it might. She'd mutter over her charms the way other people prayed, tie knots in red thread with the gravity of a woman defusing a trap, and hang the finished amulets in the window to cure in moonlight that never seemed to do anything at all.

Hedge-witchery, she called it. Small craft for small needs. And in the valley, small was all it could ever be.

* * *

Here is the truth of Hollowmere Valley, the truth every child raised here learned before they learned their letters: magic cannot cross the tree line.

Not won't. Cannot. The valley sat in the cup of the mountains like water in a bowl, ringed with pine and birch and larch, and somewhere along that ragged green hem — nobody knew exactly where, because nobody who tested it could ever quite bring themselves to step past — the world simply changed. Inside, charms curdled, blessings slid off like rain off wax, and the few gifted folk who'd wandered in over the years spoke of feeling deafened, blinded, hollowed out, as though someone had reached into their chests and scooped something essential away. Outside — well. Nobody from the valley went outside to ask. There was no reason to. The valley had everything: good soil, sweet water, a mild climate the mountains guarded like jealous dogs, and a silence so complete you could hear your own heart change tempo when you lay still enough.

We were, all of us together, the safest people in the world. And safety, I was learning, has a taste. It tastes like bread you've eaten every day of your life. Nourishing. Forgettable.

I weeded the feverfew. My hands knew the work without me — pinch below the node, twist, pull, shake the soil back — and while they worked, the rest of me drifted, the way it always drifted, toward the tree line. You could see it from the east bed, a dark unbroken seam stitched across the upper slopes, and beyond it the peaks went blue and then white and then into sky. I used to imagine, when I was smaller, that the forest was holding its breath. That if you listened hard enough at dusk you could hear the whole valley exhale.

I didn't remember arriving here. That was the strange thing I never let myself think about directly, the thought I stepped around the way you step around a hole in the floor. Everyone else in Hollowmere had a before — the miller had been a soldier, Widow Anselm had come over the pass as a bride, Maren herself had stories of cities and courts she told only when deep in her cups. But me? My life began in this garden, at perhaps seven years old, with dirt under my nails and Maren's hand on my shoulder and not one single memory behind it. When I asked — and I had asked, once, twice, a handful of times across the years — Maren said the same thing every time, in the same tired voice: *fever, child. Bad fever. It eats the early years sometimes.*

Fever. As if that explained why I couldn't remember my mother's face. As if that explained why, some nights, I woke with a word on my tongue in a language I'd never been taught, or reached for a name that dissolved like sugar in rain.

Restless safety. That was what I had. A life like a held breath.

* * *

Midday, I brewed the week's charms.

This was ritual more than craft, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said it. Maren laid out the materials on the kitchen table — red thread, iron nails, wax, dried rue, a stoppered vial of well water she swore had been drawn under a full moon by her own grandmother — and walked me through each step with the patience of a woman teaching scripture she no longer believed in but recited anyway, because the words were load-bearing.

"Wax first. Warm it in your hands, not the fire — your hands know things your head doesn't." Her hands closed over mine, shaping the lump of beeswax. "Now speak the need into it. Quietly. A charm is a secret you tell an object."

"What need?" I asked. It was the same question every week.

"Safe passage. It's always safe passage." Something flickered across her face, there and gone, like a fish turning under dark water. "Speak it."

I spoke it. *Safe passage,* into the warming wax, feeling foolish, feeling the word land nowhere, echo off nothing. In the stories Maren told of the outer world, charms were living things — they warmed, they pulled, they whispered back. Mine just sat there, a gray lump slowly cooling into indifference. By evening it would be hung above the door with all the others, a row of little dead moons, and we would both pretend not to notice that not one of them had ever done a thing.

Except.

There was the matter of the knot. Every charm I made developed one — a single loop in the binding thread that tightened on its own overnight, no matter how loosely I tied it, no matter how carefully I anchored the ends. Maren had examined it a hundred times with her magnifying glass and her frowns. *Slipped tension,* she said. *Damp air.* But damp air didn't explain why the knot only appeared on my charms, or why, the one time I'd left a finished piece on her table instead of hanging it myself, the thread had stayed smooth as a hymn.

I didn't mention the knot anymore. Neither did she. It sat between us like the fourth chair at a table set for three.

Today, though, when I tied off the binding thread and set the charm aside, I watched it. Nothing. Smooth thread, slack and ordinary. And yet when I glanced back ten minutes later, mid-chore, there it was — the loop, snug and neat as a bowline, as if the thread had dreamed it into being while I wasn't looking.

I picked it up. Held it to the window. The wax was warm. Not fire-warm, not sun-through-glass warm. Hand-warm. Warm the way a thing is warm when it has just been held by someone who loves it.

I put it down very carefully and did not think about it, in the loud, deliberate way of someone thinking about nothing whatsoever.

* * *

Evening came down the valley walls like slow honey, gilding the birches first, then the rooftops, then the garden. I fed the hens, mended a hinge, argued with the queenless hive's new tenants about the merits of a introduced queen versus their doomed laying workers, and ate supper with Maren in the kitchen while the fire ticked and the row of dead charms swayed gently above the door in a draft I couldn't feel.

She was quiet tonight. Quieter than her usual quiet, which was already a considerable quantity. She kept glancing at the window — not the charm-window, but the small one over the sink that faced north, toward the high shoulder of the valley where the tree line ran closest to the cottage. The light was failing there. Shadows pooled under the pines like spilled ink.

"You're expecting someone," I said.

"I'm expecting nothing." She cut her bread too hard; crumbs jumped. "Eat your stew."

"You've checked that window four times since the kettle went on."

"And you count my glances now? You've grown cheeky." But there was no heat in it, and her hand, when she reached for the salt, wasn't steady. "It's nothing. Old bones ache before weather. Old eyes play tricks at dusk. Eat."

I ate. The stew was barley and late leeks and a rabbit I'd snared that morning, and it was good, and I tasted almost none of it, because I was watching her watch the window, and the shape of her worry was starting to look familiar in a way I couldn't name. Like a word in that unremembered language, sitting just behind my teeth.

When the dishes were done she stopped me at the stairs, one hand on my arm, her grip surprisingly strong.

"Gideon." My name in her mouth sounded different tonight. Heavier. "Tomorrow we sort the attic. All of it. Every box, every trunk. It's past time, and I'm not getting younger, and if a cart comes for my things someday I'll not have strangers pawing through my linens finding things that would take explaining."

"The attic? You've kept me out of that attic since I could walk."

"And tomorrow you'll wish I'd kept you out another twenty years." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. They were fixed on me with an expression I'd only seen once before — years ago, the morning after I'd sleepwalked to the tree line and stood there until dawn, facing the forest, and she'd found me and dragged me home and never once spoken of it. Fear. Old, practiced, patient fear, the kind that has been waiting a long time for a date it dreads.

"Is something coming, Maren?"

"No." Too fast. Then, softer, into the space between us: "No, child. Nothing comes into the valley. That's the whole point of the valley."

She climbed the stairs ahead of me, candle in hand, and paused at her door.

"Sleep well," she said. "And Gideon — if you dream tonight, don't follow it."

I lay awake a long while after the house went dark, listening to the bees settle, the wind test the shutters, the valley breathe its vast held breath around me. Above the door, unseen in the darkness, fourteen dead charms hung in their careful row, and somewhere among them one small lump of wax stayed warm as a held hand, keeping a secret I hadn't known I'd told it.

Outside, far up the northern slope, at the exact place where the last pine gave way to bare rock, something moved. Just once. Just at the edge of what the eye will admit to seeing. Then the night folded shut again, and the valley forgot it, the way the valley forgets everything.

Tomorrow, the attic.

I fell asleep telling myself it was only dust and old linens up there, and for the first time in a year of nights, I dreamed — and in the dream, someone was calling a name that was almost mine.
