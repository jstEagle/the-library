## 2. A Date Written in Ink

The books arrived on the back of a mule that had no business being alive.

Gideon heard it before they saw it — the wet rattle of its breathing at the gate, the creak of leather panniers swollen with cargo — and by the time they reached the yard, the beast had already laid itself down in the dust as though it intended to die there and considered the matter settled. Its flanks heaved. Its eyes had the flat, patient look of an animal that had walked farther than any animal should.

"Maren!" Gideon called toward the herb shed. "There's a mule at the gate and I think it's given up on living."

Maren came out wiping her hands on her apron, and when she saw the creature she stopped so abruptly that Gideon walked into her back. For a long moment neither of them moved. The wind stirred the bee-balm along the fence row, filling the air with a sweetness that suddenly felt too loud.

"Whose is it?" Maren asked quietly.

"There's no mark on it. No brand." Gideon circled it carefully, the way one circles anything that might bite or might simply be dying. "But look at the packs. They're sealed with wax."

The wax was black, stamped with a sigil neither of them recognized: a circle broken by a single vertical line, like an eye that had been sewn shut. Maren's jaw tightened in a way Gideon had learned to read over eleven years of apprenticeship. It was the look she wore when a fever took a child despite every charm, when the frost came early and ate the orchard. The look of a woman losing an argument with the world.

"Well," Maren said at last, briskly, in the voice she used to bury fear under work. "We won't let good paper rot. Get the knife. We'll cut the seals and see what a dead man thought was worth carrying up a mountain."

"You don't know he's dead."

"No one sends a mule like this who means to follow it." She was already unbuckling the first pannier. "Help me, child. Books are heavy, and grief is heavier, and we've only two backs between us."

They carried the library inside over the course of the afternoon — forty-odd volumes wrapped in oilcloth, packed in wool shavings, each one cool to the touch in a way that made Gideon's fingers itch. Cool like river stones. Cool like the space under the bed where they'd hidden as a small child from storms they could no longer remember. The sender, whoever he had been, had packed them the way one packs things meant to survive a flood.

Maren stacked them in the back room, the little stone chamber where she kept the things she did not explain: jars of honey dark as tar, a birdcage with no door, a mirror turned to face the wall. She did not invite Gideon to help shelve them. She did not, in fact, say much of anything for the rest of the evening, and went to bed early, leaving a pot of barley stew on the banked fire and instructions about the bees that were twice as detailed as usual, as though she were compensating for something unsaid with an excess of said.

Gideon ate alone. Washed the bowls alone. Lay awake listening to the valley breathe — the wind coming down off the peaks in its long slow exhalation, the bees asleep in their skeps, the creek muttering to itself the way creeks do when no one listens — and felt the restlessness come up in them like water rising through chalk.

It was always this way after something arrived from outside. A peddler's cart, a letter, a dead man's mule. The valley held them the way a cupped hand holds water, gently and completely, and Gideon loved it and resented it in equal measure, the way one loves and resents a room one has never been allowed to leave. Eleven years. They had been brought here at six years old, or so Maren said, an orphan out of the plague country beyond the pass, and in eleven years Gideon had never once crossed the tree line. Not because they were forbidden. Because the valley did not permit it — or rather, because something in Gideon went quiet and cold whenever they walked toward the boundary, some instinct older than memory turning them back like a hand on the chest.

Magic died at the tree line. Everyone knew that. It was why the valley existed, why people had fled into it during the wars, why nothing enchanted could follow you past the last birch. Inside, the world was only the world: honest dirt, honest weather, honest death. Maren's charms worked poorly here, half-hearted things that eased a headache and failed against a fever, and Gideon's worked worse. They had been trained in hedge-craft since they could hold a mortar and pestle, and in eleven years they had managed exactly one charm that functioned as intended — a simple warming knot, tied in red thread, that kept tea hot for an hour. They still carried it. It was the closest thing they had to proof that they were more than what they appeared.

*And what do you appear as?* they asked themselves, staring at the rafters. *A gardener. A beekeeper's boy. Nobody.*

It was safer to be nobody. Maren said so often enough. But nobody, Gideon was beginning to suspect, was a kind of costume, and costumes chafe.

Sleep would not come. So, near midnight, Gideon rose, dressed, took the lamp, and went to the back room.

They told themselves it was practical. New stock needed cataloguing; Maren's eyes were bad at night; there was no harm in sorting paper. The lie was comfortable enough to walk on. They set the lamp on the table, unwrapped the first bundle, and began.

Most of the books were what they expected: herbals, formularies, almanacs bound in cracked calfskin, the ordinary furniture of a working cunning-man's shelf. One was a bestiary with the illustrations scraped out — deliberately, thoroughly, every creature reduced to a blank rectangle of parchment, which raised the hair on Gideon's arms and which they chose not to think about. One was a book of hours with all its prayers intact but every saint's name inked over in a careful, patient hand.

The ledger was at the bottom of the third bundle, wrapped separately, in cloth rather than oilcloth, as if it had been packed last and handled most.

It was larger than the others, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, with brass corners greened by age. There was no title. When Gideon opened it, the spine crackled like a step on frozen grass, and the smell that rose out of it was not the smell of old paper. Old paper smells of vanilla and dust and gentle decay. This smelled of iron and cold cellars and, beneath that, faintly, of blood left too long in a wooden bowl.

The first page bore a date in faded brown ink: a hundred years gone, spring of a year whose name Gideon didn't know. Below it, in the same hand, a line of script:

*Recorded as witnessed. Corrected as required.*

Then columns. Dates down the left margin, entries beside them, some a word, some a page. Gideon turned slowly, reading without understanding, the way one reads a language dreamed rather than learned. *The wolf crosses water. The crown eats. The key is forged and hidden.* Entries marked with symbols — a moon, a tree, a closed eye — recurring in patterns that almost resolved and then slid apart, like a face seen through running water.

It was a prophecy ledger. Gideon knew the type only from Maren's warnings: books kept by seers who wrote down what came to them and revised what failed to arrive. Dangerous things, Maren said. A prophecy isn't a window, it's a hook. You read it and it reads you back.

Gideon should have put it down then. They were raised better than this; they had been raised specifically, meticulously, for eleven years, to put such things down.

They turned the page instead.

The handwriting changed. Still the same ledger, same century-old ink, but the entries grew longer, more urgent, crowding the margins, letters slanting as though written by lamplight in a hurry or in fear. And then, near the middle of the book, a full page given over not to words but to a drawing.

A face.

Gideon's lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

It was done in fine crosshatched strokes, the work of someone who drew often and well: a young person's face, three-quarter view, unfinished at the collar as though the artist had run out of time or nerve. Short dark hair pushed back from the forehead. A nose with a bump on the bridge where — Gideon's hand rose involuntarily to their own nose, to the bump acquired falling out of the plum tree at nine. Eyes wide-set, heavy-lidded, watchful. A mouth that looked, even in ink, even in profile, like it was holding something back.

Beneath the portrait, two lines. The first was a name, or part of one, blotted out so thoroughly the paper had thinned: a hole in the shape of a word.

The second line was a date.

Gideon read it once. Read it again. Their lips moved around it silently, testing it, the way one tests a fruit that might be sweet or might be poison, and found it familiar in the worst possible way, because it was tonight. Not a night like tonight. Not a night in some other century wearing tonight's clothes. The day, the month, the season — all of it arranged with terrible precision into the present tense. Tonight. This night. The night the mule had come. The night Maren had gone pale and gone to bed early and told them, twice, unnecessarily, how to bank the fire.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, the bees slept. Somewhere far off, the creek went on muttering its one-word sermon, and Gideon stood in a cold stone room holding a hundred-year-old book that had drawn their face before they were born, and felt curiosity and dread rise together in their chest like twin flames sharing one wick — and dread, they discovered, burned hotter.

*Corrected as required,* the first page had said.

Who corrected a prophecy? And what did it mean, that the correction had waited a hundred years to land on an orphan in a forgotten valley, on the one night of the year that —

Footstep. Floorboard. The house's particular grammar of weight and wood, and Gideon knew it instantly: Maren, awake, crossing the kitchen.

Gideon had perhaps four seconds. Long enough to close the ledger. Not nearly long enough to decide whether to hide it, and some stubborn, fatal part of them — the part that had walked to the tree line a hundred times and turned back a hundred times and hated it — decided nothing at all. They simply stood there, lamp lit, book open, guilty as sunrise.

Maren filled the doorway in her nightdress and shawl, silver hair loose, and Gideon watched her take in the scene: the open bundles, the disturbed dust, the ledger. Watched her face do something Gideon had never seen it do in eleven years. It didn't go white. It went *absent* — drained of expression entirely, emptied like a room cleared in haste, and somehow that was far worse.

"Where," Maren said, and her voice was steady, which was its own kind of terrifying, "did you get that."

"It came with the mule. It was in the bottom of — Maren, there's a picture. In the book. There's a picture of—"

"I know what's in the book." She crossed the room and took the ledger from Gideon's hands, and her hands were not steady at all. She looked at the portrait for a long moment. Then at the date. Then she closed the book with exaggerated gentleness, the way one closes a door on a sleeping thing one does not wish to wake, and set it on the table, and put both her palms flat upon its cover as though it might otherwise stand up and walk.

"Gideon." Her voice had changed now, dropped its steadiness, gone rough. "Listen to me very carefully. Tomorrow we will talk. Tomorrow I will tell you things I swore I'd carry to my grave, and you may hate me for them, and you'll have the right. But tonight — tonight you will go to your room, and you will bar the door, and you will not come out until I call you. Not for sounds. Not for voices. Not if you hear me calling."

"Not if I hear *you* calling?"

"That is precisely what I said." She was already gathering the other books, stacking them with shaking hands, not looking at Gideon. "The valley keeps things out, child. That's its whole nature and its whole mercy. But a book is a door, and doors are funny things, and this one was carried across the tree line tonight, which means either the valley is weakening—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again, softer. "Please. Go to your room."

Gideon went. But at the threshold they stopped, because there was a question lodged in them sideways, sharp as a fishbone, and it would not swallow and it would not spit out.

"The face in the book," they said. "Whose is it?"

Maren stood among the stacked books with the lamp behind her, and for a moment her shadow on the wall seemed taller than any shadow had a right to be, thin and branching, like a tree in winter.

"You know whose it is," she said. "That's the whole horror of it. You've known since you opened the cover. Now go, and bolt your door, and whatever knocks tonight — *let it knock.*"

Gideon barred the door. Sat on the bed with the warming knot in their fist, the red thread biting into their palm, listening to the house settle and the wind descend the mountain and, beneath it all, very faintly, very patiently, something at the edge of hearing turning pages.

Outside, the moon was rising. It was not quite full, and it was not quite anything yet — but it was counting, Gideon thought. It had been counting for a hundred years.

And tomorrow, Maren would finally say out loud the thing the whole valley had been built to keep unsaid.
