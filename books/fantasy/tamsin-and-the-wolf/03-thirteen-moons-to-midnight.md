## 3. Thirteen Moons to Midnight

Maren had kept the ledger at the bottom of the chest for thirty-one years, beneath the winter wool and the dried lavender and a false floor of cedar slats she had planed herself, and in all that time she had told herself the same lie every autumn when she lifted the lid to air it: *not yet. There is no need yet. Perhaps there will never be a need.*

She had believed it, mostly. Belief is a thing you can grow, like yarrow, if you tend it daily and refuse to look too closely at its roots.

Now Gideon was asleep in the loft, or pretending to be, and the kitchen smelled of beeswax and burnt rosemary — Maren had let the smudge stick catch fire entirely, her hands unsteady for the first time since the fever year — and the prophecy ledger lay open on the scrubbed pine table between the salt crock and the lamp, its vellum gone the color of old teeth.

She had not wanted to open it. She had opened it anyway, because the alternative was worse: waking tomorrow with the same date on the same page and telling herself again that ink could fade if you ignored it long enough.

The sketch was still there. Of course it was there. A face in three-quarter view, done in a fine nervous hand by someone who had never met the person they were drawing but had seen them anyway, the way seers see — through water, through smoke, through the wrong end of time. The jaw was softer in the drawing. The brows were heavier. But it was Gideon's face, unmistakably, rendered a hundred years before Gideon drew their first breath, and beneath it, in faded iron-gall ink, the date.

Tonight's date. The moon's thirteenth turn of the waning season.

Maren pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood bit back. Outside, the valley did what the valley always did after dark: nothing. No owl called, because owls were creatures of habit and habit required a world that remembered from one hour to the next, and the valley did not remember. That was its whole purpose. That was why she had chosen it, out of all the hidden places in a wide and hungry world, as the place to raise a child who must never be found.

*The valley that forgets.* The hedge-folk had other names for it, less kind ones. Maren thought of it as the only place magic could not follow, which meant it was also the only place where certain kinds of magic could not be *seen*. A child born outside the craft, tethered to no ley, written into no book of blood — such a child cast no shadow in the sight of witches and kings alike. Here, Gideon had grown up invisible on purpose. Here, Maren's charms curdled in the jar and her salves worked no better than goose fat, and she had learned to practice medicine instead of magic, honest craft, sore backs and fevers and babies pulled squalling into lamplight. It had been a fair trade. It had been a penance. She had never decided which.

And now the ledger said tonight, and somewhere beyond the tree line the world that remembered was turning toward them like a slow wheel of knives.

She heard the loft ladder creak before she saw Gideon's bare feet on the rungs.

"You're burning something," Gideon said, voice thick with sleep, and then their eyes found the open book and went sharp and awake all at once, the way they always did around anything with pages. "That's not one of your herbals."

"No," Maren said. "It isn't."

Gideon came to the table and stood over the ledger, and Maren watched them read their own face, watched the recognition move through them like weather — first confusion, then the quick animal denial, then the terrible settling, the way a body settles when it realizes the ground has been moving under it all along.

"That's me," Gideon said quietly. Not a question. They touched the page with one fingertip, careful, the way you touch a sleeping snake. "This is my face. In a book older than you are."

"Older than my grandmother," Maren said. "Sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Sit down anyway." She poured honeyed milk without asking whether they wanted it, because some rituals hold a body together when nothing else does, and pushed the cup across the wood. "You'll want your hands warm for this. Trust me."

Gideon sat. They wrapped both hands around the cup and did not drink, and Maren took the chair opposite, the good chair, the one she'd carved the winter Gideon turned nine and wouldn't stop asking where they'd come from, and she looked at the child she had stolen and saved and lied to for sixteen years, and she began.

* * *

"The man who wrote this was called Brother Ellery," she said. "He was a scribe at the Pale Court, back when the palace still had a roof and the king still had a mind. He wasn't a prophet by trade. He was a copyist. But he had the sight the way some people have a limp — it didn't stop him walking, it just made everything hurt."

Gideon said nothing. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

"Ellery wrote down what came to him, and most of it was rubbish — who'd cheat at cards, which well would go sour. But once a generation, sometimes twice, something large comes through, something with weight enough to bend a life around it, and he wrote those down clean. Three of them, in this ledger. Three lines, and then he stopped writing prophecies altogether and started writing prayers, which tells you what he thought of his own gift."

Maren turned the pages with a care that was almost reverence, past columns of cramped script, to the last leaf bearing writing. She read aloud, slowly, because the words deserved to be heard in the open air at least once before they finished their work:

"*A king shall wear a crown that wears him back. It will drink his years and speak with his mouth, and none who love him will know his voice from its own.*"

She paused. Gideon's knuckles had gone white around the cup.

"*A wolf shall carry the last true blood of the fallen line, and the crown will hunt the wolf to the last mile of the world, for the wolf's death is the crown's coronation.*"

"And the third," Gideon said. Their voice had gone very flat, very careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening for the crack.

Maren turned the page to the sketch. To the face. To the date.

"*And a key shall be born outside the craft,*" she read, "*nameless, shadowless, unbought by any god — and the key alone shall turn in the lock, and the lock alone shall know the key, and the price of turning shall be paid in what the key remembers.*"

The silence afterward had texture, like felt, like snowfall. Somewhere out in the dark the bees shifted in the hive, a sound like a held breath.

"Born outside the craft," Gideon repeated. "That's — everyone's born outside the craft. Most people don't have any magic."

"Not most people," Maren said. "Most people are simply empty of it. You're different. You're a hole in the weave, Gideon. I've known it since the night I found you. Magic doesn't pass through you; it doesn't even try. Witches can't scry you. Curses slide off you like rain off wax. And things that hunt by magic—" she made herself say it plainly, because plainness was a kindness now, "-things that hunt by magic cannot find you at all."

Gideon set the cup down. Their hand was shaking, and they looked at it as though it belonged to someone else. "You knew. All this time. You knew what I was, and you never—"

"I knew you were safe here," Maren said. "That's what I knew. Everything else was a story I hoped would stay a story."

"But the date. Tonight. You've had this book how long?"

"Thirty-one years."

"And you never thought to mention that my face was in it?"

"I thought about mentioning it every single day," Maren said, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it crack, because Gideon had earned the sound of the truth breaking. "And every day I chose the lie, because the lie kept you fed and asleep and laughing at my bad jokes, and the truth would have put a hook in you. Prophecy is a hook, child. You don't hang it on the wall and admire it. It hangs *you*. The moment you know your own page, you start walking toward it. Every step you take to escape it is a step along it. I have watched it happen. I have *done* it happen."

"So you decided for me." Gideon was standing now, though Maren couldn't remember them rising. "Sixteen years. My whole life, you decided what I got to know about myself. Where I came from. Who my parents were. Why I'm — whatever I am. You just — you cut those pages out and burned them and handed me a garden and some bees and called it a life."

"Yes," Maren said.

The word landed between them like a stone in a well. Gideon stared at her, waiting for the apology, the justification, the flood of explanation, and Maren gave them none of it, because there wasn't one. There was only the choice, and she would make it again, and they both knew it.

"I found you in the ash of a burning house," she said at last, quieter. "Three days' ride east of here, past the tree line, in a village that had been visited by men wearing gray cloaks and crown-forged iron. Everyone in that house was dead except a baby who didn't cry, who didn't startle, who lay in the middle of all that horror looking up at me with eyes like the space between stars. And I knew what you were before I picked you up, because I'd read this ledger, and I knew what the men in gray cloaks were hunting, and I knew that if I left you there, the world would find you and use you up the way crowns use everything up."

"Maren—"

"Let me finish. I've had thirty-one years to finish and I never let myself, so let me have five minutes now." Her hands were trembling; she folded them together and squeezed until the trembling had nowhere to go. "I carried you across the boundary and the moment we crossed, you began to cry — the first sound you'd made, the midwife in me knew it, the first sound of your whole life — and I understood then what you were worth and what it would cost. So I stayed. I buried my craft at the tree line like a dog buries a bone it means to come back for, and I raised you in the one place on this earth where nothing could see you. Not witches. Not kings. Not fate itself, I prayed. I taught you herbs instead of hexes. I taught you to brew charms that fail, so you'd never wonder why your real power never woke. I made you small, Gideon. I made you invisible. I did it every day for sixteen years, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I am not sorry, and I am sorry — both, at once, forever. That's the truth of it. There isn't a cleaner version."

Gideon had turned away, toward the window, toward the black glass of it and the garden beyond, invisible in the dark. When they spoke, their voice was low and rough.

"The prophecy says the price is memory. What the key remembers."

"Yes."

"What do I remember, Maren? What is there to take?" They turned back, and their eyes were wet and furious. "A garden. Some bees. You. That's all I have. If the key turns tonight, it takes the only life I've ever—"

"It won't turn tonight," Maren said. "Do you hear me? It doesn't have to. Prophecy is a road, not a rope. Ellery wrote what he saw from where he stood, and where he stood was a hundred years ago, and things change between the seeing and the arriving. Kings die. Wolves hide. Keys stay in drawers." She crossed the room and took Gideon's face in her two hands, the way she had when they were small and feverish, thumbs on the cheekbones, and she made her voice into the voice she'd used over sickbeds for forty years, the voice that had talked children back from the far shore. "Tomorrow we burn this ledger. Tomorrow we grind the ashes into the compost with the rest of what the garden forgets. You will stay indoors, you will keep away from the windows, and in a week this will be a story we tell each other when we're old. Do you understand me?"

Gideon didn't answer. Gideon was looking past her shoulder, at the window, and their expression had changed — not fear exactly, something older than fear, the look of an animal that hears a sound outside its den and cannot name it.

"Maren," they whispered. "What's that at the tree line?"

She turned. Beyond the black ranks of the garden, past the fence posts and the bee hives and the last of Maren's failing wards, the valley's edge showed as a paleness between the trunks — the boundary itself, the seam where the remembering world ended. And against that pale seam, low and lurching, something was moving. Something large. Something leaving a dark trail behind it that caught the starlight.

Something that fell, at the edge of the grass, and did not get up.

Maren's heart went cold and clear, the way it did over a dying patient, every useless feeling burned away and only the work remaining. She was already reaching for her cloak, already blowing out the lamp.

"Stay inside," she said. "Lock the door behind me."

But even as she said it, she knew — with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading signs, in tea leaves and in faces and in the last leaf of a dead scribe's book — that the thirteenth moon had already begun to turn, and it had not come for Gideon at all.

Not yet.

It had sent something else ahead of it, bleeding across her garden path, and whatever it was, it was dying at the exact spot where the world forgot — and Maren, curse her soft old heart, was going to go out and save it.
