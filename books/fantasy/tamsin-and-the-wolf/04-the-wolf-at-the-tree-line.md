## 4. The Wolf at the Tree Line

The valley had a way of making time feel like honey — slow, golden, and faintly sticky at the edges of everything.

I felt it most in the afternoons, when Maren went down to the village to trade herbs for salt and gossip, and I was left alone with the garden and the bees and the long green silence that pressed against the windows like something patient. That day the light came slantwise through the smoke-hole of the cottage, laying stripes across the floorboards, and I lay on my back counting them because there was nothing else worth counting.

Seventeen years old, and my whole world measured four miles across.

Not that anyone had ever said so out loud. But I'd walked the boundary enough times to know its shape. You could go as far as the orchard, the millpond, the standing stone with its lichen-eaten carvings no one could read. You could climb the sheep paths almost to the top of the western ridge. And then you reached the tree line — an unremarkable line of birch and pine, thinning toward the pass — and something in your chest simply turned you around. Not fear. Nothing so dramatic. Just a gentle, absolute conviction that there was nothing up there worth seeing, and your feet would carry you home before you'd decided anything at all.

Maren called it *the valley's kindness*. She said it the way other people said *the Lord's mercy*, with her eyes fixed somewhere over my shoulder.

I called it a fence, and I hated it the way you can only hate a thing that has never once hurt you.

* * *

That evening I was at the hives when the light began to fail.

There were six of them, white-boxed and humming along the south wall of the garden, and they were the closest thing I had to magic that worked. Everything else failed at some invisible threshold — the charms I braided from rowan and red thread, the fever-salves, the little knotted blessings Maren taught me to whisper over jars of honey. They all came out *almost*. Almost sweet. Almost warm. Almost what they were meant to be. Maren said hedge-craft was like that everywhere, that true magic was rare as a blue moon, but I'd seen her work, once, when she thought I wasn't looking: she'd held a dying sparrow in her cupped hands and the air around her had gone gold as cider, and the bird had flown.

My hands never went gold. My hands only smelled of beeswax.

So I tended the bees, who asked nothing of me but steadiness, and they gave me their low brown song, and for a while the restlessness in me went quiet. The hive nearest the wall had a queen I liked — a big amber thing I'd named Sorrel — and I was leaning close to hear her, cheek nearly against the warm wood, when the bees changed their note.

Anyone else would have missed it. It wasn't anger, exactly. It was attention. Six thousand small bodies turning, all at once, toward the tree line, the way a congregation turns toward a door.

I straightened. The evening had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had died completely, which was wrong — the wind never died in the valley, it just wandered. The birches at the edge of the garden stood absolutely still, every leaf hanging like a held breath.

And then I heard it. A sound I knew only from Maren's stories and the butcher's yard: something heavy dragging itself through brush, with wet, labored breathing underneath.

I should have gone inside. Maren had rules, and the first rule was *nothing comes over the tree line, and if something tries, you let the valley do its work.* The valley's kindness. The fence. Whatever crossed that line got turned around, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, and sent wandering back into the dark where it belonged.

But the sound didn't turn around. It kept coming — closer, and closer, and then not coming at all, and the silence afterward was worse than the noise.

I told myself I was going to fetch Maren. I told myself that right up until I was halfway across the fallow field, the cold grass soaking my skirts, the last grey light showing me the tree line ahead and something dark crumpled against it like a dropped coat.

It was a wolf.

Not a dog wearing wolfishness, not one of the shaggy hill-hounds the shepherds cursed. A wolf, proper and enormous, its coat the black of deep water, lying on its side in the bracken with its ribs going like bellows. Even from ten feet away I could see why it hadn't moved. There was blood — too much of it, black in the dusk — welling from three long wounds along its flank, and its hind leg was bent in a way legs don't bend, and there was iron in it. I could smell the iron. A hot, wrong smell, like a forge left untended, and beneath it something stranger: a sweetness, like crushed fennel, that made the back of my teeth ache.

An arrow stood in its shoulder. Fletched in white.

I knew about the arrows in white. Every child in the valley did, though we were never told why. *If you ever see a man in grey with a white-fletched arrow,* Maren had said once, her voice gone flat and strange, *you come indoors, Gideon. You bar the door. You do not look at his face.*

The wolf's eye found me.

I have been looked at by many creatures — cats, horses, the miller's ill-tempered goose — and none of them looked at me the way that wolf did. It wasn't animal wariness. It was recognition, sharp and horrified, the look of someone caught naked in a doorway. Its lip lifted off teeth longer than my thumb, and the growl that came out of it was less a sound than a vibration, something I felt in my sternum, in the roots of my teeth, in the ground under my knees.

"Easy," I said, which was absurd, and we both knew it.

The wolf tried to rise. Its ruined leg folded, and it went down hard, and the sound it made then undid me — not a howl, nothing so grand, just a small punched-out whine, quickly strangled, the sound of something determined not to show how much it hurt. I knew that sound. I had made versions of it my whole life, quietly, behind the cottage door, whenever Maren asked if I was happy here.

Here is the thing about the tree line. I had tested it a hundred times, and it had always turned me gently around. But kneeling there in the bracken, ten feet from the bleeding wolf, I realized the line ran between us. The wolf lay on the far side of it, in the world where magic lived, and I stood on ours, in the world where nothing worked, and the wolf could no more cross to me than I could cross to it. Every time it dragged itself an inch toward help, some invisible kindness turned it back, and I watched it happen — watched its body simply refuse, watched confusion and terror flood its eyes as the ground itself betrayed it.

It was drowning on dry land.

"No," I said aloud, to nobody, to the valley. "No, you don't get to be kind right now."

Later I would try to explain this to myself and fail. All I knew was that the wolf's eye had hold of something in me and would not let go, and that the blood spreading under it was very dark and very fast, and that whatever the valley thought it was protecting me from, it was not protecting *this.*

I stepped over the line.

Nothing happened. That was the first miracle, though I didn't know it was a miracle; I'd never managed it before, and the valley let me pass as if I'd merely stepped over a root. The air changed on my skin — warmer by a degree, or thicker, like wading into bathwater — and the ache in my back teeth sharpened until I tasted copper, and the birches overhead seemed to lean in. Magic. I was standing in magic the way you stand in a river, and it pushed at me, curious, testing, finding nothing to grip.

There was no time to wonder about that. The wolf lunged as I knelt — teeth snapping shut an inch from my wrist, close enough that I felt the wind of it — and then it collapsed again, trembling, spent.

"I'm not going to hurt you," I said. "You're going to hurt me, probably, and I'm going to do it anyway."

It took three tries to get my arms under it. It weighed as much as a yearling calf and fought me the whole way, weakly, furiously, its hot breath against my neck, its claws leaving bright lines down my forearms that I wouldn't notice until later. The blood soaked through my apron immediately, shockingly warm. The iron-and-fennel smell filled my head. Somewhere behind us, far off toward the pass, I thought I heard a whistle — two notes, high and deliberate, like a man calling a dog — and the wolf heard it too, and went rigid in my arms, and stopped fighting me entirely.

We crossed the line together, and the moment we did, the weight doubled. Not the wolf's weight. Something else — the valley taking stock of what I'd brought in, perhaps, or the world reasserting its ordinary physics. I staggered, fell to one knee in our own grass, heaved the wolf the last few feet over the boundary stones, and collapsed beside it, gasping, my heart slamming, my hands slick and black to the wrist.

The whistle sounded again. Closer. And with it, faintly, voices — two men, maybe three, speaking in the flat clipped accents of the capital, words I couldn't make out but whose tone I understood perfectly. They were not calling a lost dog. They were tracking wounded prey, and they were annoyed.

The wolf dragged itself upright. I watched it plant its good legs, shake its great dark head, and fix its eyes on the tree line where the voices came from — and then it looked at me, and something passed across its face that I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe. Gratitude is too soft a word. It was more like a debt being signed.

Then it screamed.

There's no other word for it. The sound tore out of it and broke apart mid-air, and the breaking was the worst part, because the scream became two sounds braided together — wolf and something else, something with vowels — and the body followed the voice. Bones moved that have no business moving. Fur receded like water running backward. The spine lengthened, the jaw shortened, the great dark pelt drew inward and upward, and I fell back on my hands in the wet grass and stared at a woman.

She was younger than me by a year, maybe two. Black-haired, cropped short and matted with blood. Her flank — where the wolf's wounds had been — was torn open in three long gashes across her ribs, and the arrow was still in her shoulder, and she was naked and shaking and furious, and her eyes were the same eyes, amber shot through with green, and they had not softened at all.

"Don't," she rasped, before I could speak. Her voice was rough as a file. "Don't scream. Don't run. Don't *pray*." She got one arm under herself, swaying, grey-lipped. "If you scream, they'll hear, and then everyone in this precious little graveyard dies with me."

"A graveyard," I repeated stupidly. Blood was still running between my fingers. My forearms stung where her claws had opened them, and the sting was oddly grounding, proof I hadn't dreamed any of this.

"You live in a valley that eats magic." She said it like an accusation, like I'd built the fence myself. "Do you know what that makes this place? Do you know what it means that I couldn't cross your *line*?" She laughed, and it cost her; the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought blood to her lips. "It means nothing in. Nothing out. It means you're safe, and safety is just a slower way of—"

The whistle cut her off. Two notes, much closer now, at the very edge of the birches. She went silent as the grave, and I saw her start to drag herself back toward the line — back toward the hunters, back toward death, because anywhere was better than being cornered here, in a cage with a stranger.

I caught her arm.

She bared her teeth at me — and even human, even half-dead, the gesture was wholly wolf, and my stomach dropped through the earth. "Let go."

"There's a cellar," I said. Words tumbling, stupid, fast. "Under the cottage. Maren keeps it warded — warded against everything, she says, though nothing works here, so maybe that means it works double, I don't — there's a hatch under the honey racks, and the village won't talk, they never talk, they barely *look* at each other—"

"Gideon!"

Maren's voice, from the garden path, sharp as a snapped wire. I turned. She stood at the gate with her market basket still on her arm, onions rolling loose at her feet where she'd dropped them, and her face — I had never seen Maren's face do what it was doing. It had gone the color of candle wax, and she was looking past me, past the bleeding woman, at the tree line, at the white-fletched arrow, and her lips were moving around a word I couldn't hear.

The woman's hand closed on my wrist — hard, desperate, her blood and mine mingling warm between us.

"Hunters," she whispered. "Crown-sent. They'll burn this valley to find me. Your choice, hedge-girl: hide me, or hand me over. But choose *fast.*"

Through the birches, unhurried now, certain, came the crunch of boots.
