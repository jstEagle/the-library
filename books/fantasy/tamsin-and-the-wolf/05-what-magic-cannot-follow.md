## 5. What Magic Cannot Follow

I woke to the smell of beeswax and crushed mint, and for one merciful moment I did not remember where I was or what had chased me.

Then the pain arrived, and memory with it.

I was lying on a narrow bed in a low-ceilinged room that smelled of dried herbs and old smoke. A quilt the color of rust covered me, heavy as a hand pressing down. Through a window no wider than my forearm came light — real sunlight, unfiltered by iron or spellwork, falling across a floor of packed earth swept clean. Somewhere outside, bees droned in numbers I had never heard gathered in one place. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A sound like the valley itself humming.

I tried to sit up and my side tore open its complaint. The wound the Sentinels' pike had given me ran from beneath my ribs to my hip, stitched now with black thread and slathered in something that burned cold instead of hot. Someone had stripped off my ruined shirt and dressed me in a linen shift that smelled of lavender and of someone else's life.

*The wolf*, I thought. *They saw the wolf.*

Panic is an old companion. It came up my throat like bile, and I bit it down the way I'd learned to bite everything down, because a princess who panics dies, and a wolf who panics dies faster, and I had been both for long enough now that the biting was nearly automatic.

The door opened without a knock.

The apprentice stood there with a basin steaming in their hands. Up close, in daylight, they were stranger than they'd been at the tree line — thin as a winter branch, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that kept moving, cataloguing, never settling on anything longer than a breath. They looked at me the way people look at weather coming over a ridge. Wary. Measuring. Already deciding where to stand when it breaks.

"You're awake," they said. "Good. Maren said if you woke before sundown I should change the dressing."

"I can dress my own wounds."

"Of course you can." They set the basin down anyway, close enough to be an insult or a courtesy; I couldn't tell which. "You bled through the first two. Maren did the stitching. She says you'll keep the scar."

"Maren," I repeated. "That's the witch."

"That's the hedge-witch." A flicker of something — loyalty, maybe, or just habit — crossed their face. "She doesn't like being called the other thing."

"And what do you like being called? You dragged me across a boundary I couldn't cross myself. That's not nothing. I should know your name before I owe you my life."

Something tightened around their mouth. "Gideon," they said, as if the name cost them. "And you don't owe me anything. I didn't do it to be owed."

"No," I agreed. "You did it because you couldn't watch something die. There's a word for people like that. It's usually followed by a grave."

* * *

Maren came at dusk.

She was older than any woman I had ever seen and moved like she resented every year of it, shoulders squared against her own age as though it were an enemy at the gate. She carried a lamp and a bowl of broth and set both down with the economy of someone who had long ago stopped wasting motion on kindness.

"Eat," she said. "Then we talk. In that order, because you'll want your strength for arguing, and I'll want you too tired to win."

I ate. The broth was venison and nettle and something bitter underneath, and I could feel it doing its work, knitting heat back into limbs the cold forest had emptied. When the bowl was empty she took it, sat down on the room's only stool, and looked at me with eyes the gray of old pewter.

"Tamsin," she said. "Last of House Vael. Your mother wore silver bells in her hair at her wedding, and your father wore a crown he hadn't earned yet. Am I close?"

My hand went to my belt before I remembered I had no belt, no knife, no ring — nothing but the shift and the stitches and the scar. "Who told you that name?"

"No one living." Her voice was flat as slate. "I knew your grandmother. I brewed the fever-medicine that let your father live long enough to be crowned. And I have spent thirty years in this valley watching the tree line, waiting for the day his crown sent something through it after me." She leaned forward. "It finally did. It just wasn't looking for me."

Outside, the bees had gone quiet. The silence felt deliberate, like a held breath.

"Tell me what you know about crowns," Maren said.

"I know mine was stolen before I was born."

"I mean *the* crown. The Iron-and-Holly. The one Aldric wears." She said his name without title, and something in me bristled and then, traitorously, softened, because no one had said my father's bare name in years — everyone else said *the King*, or *His Radiance*, or nothing at all, in the careful way people speak of the dead.

"What about it?"

"It's older than the kingdom. Older than the mountain. It was made by people who understood that power wants a shape, and if you give it one, it will fill it." She turned the lamp wick down with one finger. "Every king who has worn it has fed it. A little at first. Years, then memories, then the parts of themselves that loved anything more than ruling. It eats slowly, so the eating feels like growing. By the time a wearer notices what's missing, the missing parts don't miss themselves."

"You're saying my father is being eaten."

"I'm saying your father has been dinner for eleven years, girl, and the worst part—" she met my eyes, and I hated her for what came next, and I needed her to say it anyway— "is that some nights, he sets the table himself."

I was on my feet before I knew I'd moved, and the stitches pulled fire up my side, and I didn't care. "You don't know him. You knew his *mother's* medicine. You've been hiding in this hole smelling of bees while he—" My voice cracked and I despised the crack. "He still writes to me. He still remembers my name. He held my mother while she died and he *wept*, and whatever your crown-eating story is, it didn't stop him from—"

"From what?" Maren asked quietly. "From sending hunters through three provinces to kill his only remaining heir? From burning the village at Ferrow's Bend because a tinker there sold a charm with a wolf worked into it? From wearing iron forged from the same ore as the crown, so that his own Sentinels can't disobey him even if they wanted to?" She didn't raise her voice. She never raised it, I would learn. "Tamsin. Grief makes liars of us all. Yours is just better rehearsed."

I sat back down because my legs made the decision for me. The fire in my side beat in time with my pulse. Somewhere beyond the wall, Gideon was very carefully not listening, which meant they were absolutely listening, and I found I didn't have the strength left to mind.

"The thirteenth moon," I said at last. "Is that part of your story too?"

Maren went still in a way that told me I'd struck closer than she wanted. "Where did you hear that phrase?"

"My nurse sang it. Before she was dismissed. Before my mother died." I wrapped my arms around my knees, and the shift was thin and the room was cold, but I refused to shiver where the witch could see. "*Thirteen turns and the line runs dry. Thirteen turns and the key comes by.* I thought it was a lullaby. I thought a lot of things were lullabies."

For a long moment Maren looked at me, and behind the pewter of her eyes something moved — pity, or fear, or the particular exhaustion of a woman who has carried a secret until it grew teeth.

"It was a prophecy," she said. "Written down a hundred years before your father was crowned, by a seer who went mad writing it, and it names your bloodline's end at the turn of the thirteenth moon since the binding. Which falls—" she glanced toward the window, toward the thinning sickle of the moon above the trees— "soon. Sooner than I'd hoped. Sooner than anyone hoped."

"Then my father wins either way," I said. "If the crown eats him, the line ends hollow. If the prophecy's true, the line ends dead. Either way, House Vael is a footnote."

"No," Maren said. "Either way, House Vael is a *keyhole*. And keys, girl, are not the same as doors." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took the lamp with her. At the door she paused. "Sleep. Heal. And when you're well enough to be angry again — and you will be, anger's the last thing grief lets go of — come find me. There's a book you need to see. And an apprentice who needs to see it more."

* * *

Gideon found me at dawn, sitting on the step outside the cottage with my side bandaged and my pride still bleeding.

They stood at the edge of the garden among the bee-hives, keeping the distance of someone used to being kept away from, and for a while neither of us spoke. The valley lay green and gold around us, absurdly peaceful, the mountains ringing it like a cupped hand. Magic could not follow me here. I had felt it the moment I crossed — the crown's iron hook in my chest going slack, the constant low hum of being *hunted* dropping away like a burden set down. This place was a blind spot in the world's eye.

"Why did you drag me over?" I asked finally. "You didn't know me. You risked whatever this is—" I gestured at the valley, the garden, the small safe life leaking out of every window— "for a bleeding stranger who happened to have fur."

Gideon crouched down and pulled a weed from between the bean rows with unnecessary force. "Maren says magic can't cross the tree line. That things that are hunted can't be found here." A pause. "But wounded things can be carried."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." They looked up at me then, and their eyes were the color of river water over stone — hard to see the bottom of. "I don't remember why I do half the things I do. Maren says I hit my head as a child. I've stopped asking." They stood, dusting soil from their knees. "You're the first person I've ever pulled *into* this place instead of watching something stay out. It seemed important. Don't ask me why."

"Everything with you is 'don't ask me why.'"

"Everything with everyone is 'don't ask me why.' Most people are just better at pretending otherwise." They hesitated, and then, grudgingly, like paying a debt: "Your wolf form. When I dragged you over the line, you were lighter than you looked. Like carrying something full of wind instead of blood." A beat. "It didn't frighten me. I want you to know that. Whatever you are — it didn't frighten me."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, rusty, closer to a sob than I'd allowed myself in years. "Well. That makes one of us."

* * *

Three days I stayed in the valley, and each day the moon above the tree line thinned like a coin worn smooth.

On the third night I dreamed of my father. He sat in the great hall at Vaelmoor with the crown blazing on his head, and he was smiling at me with my mother's smile — a smile he'd borrowed somewhere along the way and never given back — and when I reached for his hand, the fingers closed on mine like a trap, and beneath the skin of his wrist I could see the iron shining through, branching like veins, like roots, like something planted long ago and patiently grown.

I woke with the taste of metal in my mouth and Gideon shaking my shoulder, and outside the window the sky was the bruised purple of false dawn, and Maren was standing at the tree line with her shawl clutched around her, staring north as though she could see all the way to the capital.

"They've found the trail," she said, without turning. "Charm-work on the boundary stones. Old work, stolen work — the kind that shouldn't function past the tree line, and yet." She spat into the grass. "Someone gave the hunters a way in. Which means someone knows what sleeps in this valley, and has decided it's time to wake it."

Gideon went white beside me. "The ledger," they whispered. "Maren — the date in the ledger. It's tonight."

And I did not know what ledger they meant, or what date, or why the hedge-witch's face had gone the gray of old ash — but I knew the feeling crawling up my spine, the one that had lived there since the night my mother died, the one that said: *the trap is already sprung, child. You were simply the last to be told.*

The moon hung over the eastern ridge, one night shy of dark, and I thought of my father wearing his crown in a hall a hundred leagues away, and I thought: *thirteen turns.*

We were almost out of turns.
