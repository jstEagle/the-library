## 6. Leaving the Garden

The bees knew first.

I was at the hives when it happened, sleeves rolled to the elbow, smoke curling from the tin smoker in my hand, and every bee in the long row of white boxes went silent at once. Not calm-silent, not the drowsy hum of a warm afternoon. Silent the way a room goes silent when someone has just said something unforgivable.

I stood with my hand half-raised and listened to the valley hold its breath.

"Maren," I said, though she was all the way up at the house, though she couldn't possibly have heard me over the distance and the rosemary and the low stone wall. I said it anyway, the way you call out to someone in a dream, knowing the dream won't answer.

The light had changed too. It was late afternoon, high summer, the hour when the sun comes slantwise through the western pines and turns the whole valley the color of cider — but the light had gone thin instead, watery, as if someone had diluted it. The mountains that ringed us on every side looked closer than they should have. Sharper. As if the valley were drawing itself in like a snail into its shell.

I left the smoker on the hive stand. I don't remember deciding to run; my legs simply decided for me, and I let them, because in six years of living inside the tree line I had learned one rule above all others: when the bees go quiet, you go inside.

Tamsin met me halfway up the garden path.

She'd been three days among us by then — three days of broth and bandages and furious, grudging convalescence in Maren's spare room — and she moved like a convalescent no longer. She came down the slope with her dark hair loose and her eyes fixed on the tree line, and when she saw me she didn't slow.

"They're here." Her voice was flat as a blade laid on a table. "I felt it in my teeth. Crown-iron, coming through the north pass."

"That's impossible." I heard how young my voice sounded, how much it wanted to be told otherwise. "Nothing crosses. Nothing *can* cross. You said—"

"I said magic can't follow me in." She caught my arm, and her grip was stronger than her wound should have allowed, and there was blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers anymore but hadn't been washed out either. "But they're not carrying magic, Gideon. They're carrying a *key*. A stolen charm, hedge-made, cheap and ugly — the kind of thing that opens doors that were never meant to open. Someone gave them the way in."

"Who would—" But even as I asked it, some cold, quiet part of me already knew the shape of the answer. Charms like that weren't sold in markets. They were made by people who understood boundaries. People who understood *this* boundary.

People like Maren.

I didn't say it. Tamsin saw it land anyway, whatever crossed my face, and her grip tightened once — not comfort, exactly. Acknowledgment.

"Inside," she said. "Now."

* * *

Maren was in the kitchen, and she had turned it into a fortress.

Every jar of dried herbs had come down from the rafters. Every charm I'd ever brewed that never quite worked — the fever-knots, the salt rings, the little wax seals stamped with symbols I'd copied without understanding — lay in rows across the table like soldiers awaiting orders. Maren stood among them with her sleeves pinned back and her gray hair escaping its braid, and she was grinding something in the mortar with a rhythm so steady it frightened me more than shouting would have. Steady meant deliberate. Deliberate meant she had known this could happen.

"You felt it," she said without looking up. Not a question.

"The bees went quiet," I said.

"The bees always know first. Bees live on the edge of things." She set down the mortar and finally looked at me, and what was in her face made my stomach drop through the floorboards. Not fear. Something older than fear. Resignation that had been sitting in her for years, maybe decades, waiting for this afternoon to arrive. "How many, Tamsin?"

"Four. Maybe five. They'll reach the wall by dusk."

"Dusk." Maren nodded slowly, as if dusk were an old acquaintance she'd been expecting. "Then we have until dusk. Gideon — sit down."

"I can help. I can grind, I can—"

"*Sit.*"

I sat. The chair scraped against stone, loud in the quiet kitchen, and outside the window the light continued to thin like watered milk.

Maren came around the table and took both my hands in hers. Her palms were rough as bark and warm as bread, and I realized, with a strange distant grief, that I could count on one hand the number of times she had touched me like this — deliberately, with meaning, rather than handing me a basket or steadying a ladder. Six years under her roof, and we had loved each other in the language of chores and silence and soup left covered on the stove. We had never needed words. Now she was going to use them, and I could see it cost her.

"What's coming through that pass isn't looking for Tamsin alone," she said. "They've had word of a wolf heir hiding in a valley where magic dies. And they've had word of something else. Someone else." Her thumbs pressed hard against my knuckles. "A nameless key. Born outside magic. Raised where the crown's eye cannot see."

"Tamsin told me about the prophecy," I said. "Thirteen moons. The king bound to his crown. The wolf heir. And the key." My mouth had gone dry. "That's a story, Maren. That's ink in a hundred-year-old book."

"It's ink that drew you out of this garden three days ago to drag a bleeding wolf across a boundary that has killed men for trying less." She said it gently, which was worse. "Child, nothing about you is a story. That's precisely the problem. Stories are made of magic, and you are not."

Outside, far off, a crow started up — then another, then a whole ragged argument of them, lifting from the north pines in a black spill against the sky. Tamsin went to the window. When she turned back, her face had gone wolf-pale, the color of the moon through cloud.

"They're past the pass," she said. "In the trees."

* * *

We spent the last hour of daylight preparing, and it was the strangest hour of my life, because everything we did was ordinary. Maren banked the fire. She wrapped bread and cheese and a jar of honey in cloth. She put my good cloak over my shoulders and fastened the clasp herself, her fingers steady at my throat, and I stood there like a child being dressed for winter while the world ended quietly around us.

"You're coming with us," I said. It wasn't a question either. "You're coming through the boundary."

"No, child."

"Maren—"

"There are things a hedge-witch is for." She smiled, and it was the smile she wore when a fever broke or a calf was pulled safe or the first crocus came up through snow — the smile that meant: *this is the work, and the work is good.* "This valley has kept you invisible for sixteen years because I wove it so, thread by thread, year by year. That weaving doesn't unravel itself. Someone has to stay behind and hold the knot while you slip loose. Otherwise they follow you out, and everything I've done is for nothing."

"Hold the knot *how*?"

She didn't answer. She went to the shelf above the hearth and took down the ledger — the prophecy book, the one with my own face sketched in faded ink beside tonight's date — and pressed it into my hands. Its cover was cracked leather, cool and patient as a stone in shade.

"You take this," she said. "Whatever happens, whatever they tell you, whatever the crown offers — you keep this book, and you read it, and you believe the parts that frighten you most. Those are the true ones."

My eyes were burning. "I'm not leaving you."

"Gideon." She cupped my face in both hands then, and her eyes were bright and dry and absolutely certain, and I understood that she had rehearsed this moment for sixteen years in the privacy of her own heart, and that no argument I owned could stand against it. "I have been your mother in every way that matters except the one that hurts. Let me do the part that hurts. It's the only part I have left to give."

Somewhere up the valley, a sound like a branch breaking — but wrong, too loud, too green. Then another. The sound of the tree line being persuaded to open.

Tamsin appeared in the doorway, pale, jaw set. "The wall's breached. Two of them through, maybe three. We have minutes."

* * *

We ran through the garden.

It was the last time I would ever see it, though I didn't fully know that yet — some part of me refused to know it — and so I did the thing you do when you cannot bear a thing: I memorized it without admitting I was memorizing. The bee boxes with their white paint peeling. The rosemary gone woody and silver. The bean poles leaning like tired dancers. The little stone wall I'd built myself at eleven, crooked on its north side because I'd been crooked at eleven too. All of it gilded now in the last copper light, all of it utterly silent, the bees still holding their terrible breath.

Maren ran ahead of us, and I marveled even through the terror at how fast she could move, this old woman who shuffled to market and complained about her knees. She ran like someone who had run this path before in her mind ten thousand times.

The boundary showed itself as we approached — I had never seen it before, though I had lived beside it all my life. It hung between the last of the apple trees and the first of the wild pines like heat-shimmer off a summer road, a faint vertical trembling in the air, and beyond it the forest looked darker than forest should look, deeper, *hungrier*, full of a color I had no name for.

And standing before it, planted in the path like a fence post given flesh, was a man in gray.

He was younger than I expected. That was the worst of it — he had a young face, a decent face, the kind of face that helps old women across streets, and he held a small iron rod tipped with a sliver of glass that glowed faintly, sickly, like a dying ember. The stolen charm. Behind him, deeper in the trees, shapes moved. More of them.

"Old woman," he called, and his voice was almost apologetic. "Step aside. We only want the wolf."

"You want what the crown wants," Maren said, "and the crown wants everything. Step aside yourself, boy, before you learn what a hedge-witch keeps in her garden."

He raised the rod. The glass sliver flared.

What happened next happened fast, and I have replayed it a thousand times since, and it has never once played the same way twice. Maren flung out her arms, and the garden answered. Not dramatically — no lightning, no roaring wind. The rosemary simply *rose*, a wave of green and silver scent so thick it hit like a wall. The bees came off their hives in a single golden thunderclap, forty thousand wings speaking with one voice. The stone wall I'd built straightened itself, squared itself, became a rampart. Every charm I had ever brewed and failed — every dead fever-knot and broken salt ring — lit up along the fence posts like lanterns remembering what light was for.

Because they had never failed. I understood it in one blinding flash, even as Tamsin seized my wrist and hauled me toward the shimmering line. They had never worked because *here* they had nothing to work *with* — and Maren had kept me brewing them anyway for sixteen years, filling them, charging them, storing them in the rafters like jars of lightning, against exactly this evening.

"GO!" Maren screamed, and her voice cracked down the middle, and the garden roared.

* * *

Tamsin hit the boundary first, pulling me after her, and I want to tell you what crossing felt like, and I am going to fail, because there are no words in the valley's language for it.

It was like surfacing from deep water. It was like the first breath after a fever breaks. It was like walking out of a dim room into noon — no, it was like learning that the dim room had been a *cell*, and the noon had been there all along, everywhere, waiting patiently for me for sixteen years.

Magic flooded in.

It came through my skin, my breath, the roots of my hair. It tasted of iron and honey and cold pine and something underneath all of those, something vast and old and *attentive*, as if the whole world had been holding its breath along with the bees and had just, at last, exhaled my name. My hands glowed. I could see my own veins beneath the skin, threaded with faint gold light, and I could feel every growing thing within a mile — the pines drinking, the moss climbing, a fox's heart hammering in its den — and it wasn't overwhelming, that was the terrible wonderful thing, it fit, it *fit me*, like a key sliding into a lock that had been cut for it.

Behind us, the garden screamed. Not the garden — Maren, or the charms, or both, a sound of green things burning bright and going dark. Tamsin spun toward it and I grabbed her, and she was shaking, and I was shaking, and neither of us turned around, because turning around would have meant stopping, and stopping would have meant dying, and Maren had bought our running with everything she had.

"Don't look," I whispered, to her or to myself, I don't know.

"She held the knot," Tamsin said hoarsely. "Gideon — she *held* it. They can't follow. Look."

I looked back, just once, just enough. The shimmering wall stood whole behind us, brighter than before, sealed like a healed wound. On the near side of it, the gray man and his companions milled like ants at the foot of glass. And through the veil, faint as a reflection in deep water, I saw the garden again for one last instant — the bee boxes, the crooked wall, the figure of an old woman standing amid drifting golden light, arms still raised, head bowed, alive, unbroken, *holding* —

and then the pines closed around us and the valley was gone.

* * *

We walked until full dark, because stopping felt like drowning. Tamsin found us a hollow beneath a granite overhang where a spring seeped cold and clean from the rock, and there we finally sat, side by side, two fugitives and a book, while the real night came down around us — a night full of sounds I had never heard, because sounds in the valley had always been only sounds, and now each one arrived with its meaning attached, like fruit on a stem.

I could feel the owl above us thinking about mice. I could feel the spring thinking about the sea. I could feel Tamsin beside me, a banked fire of a presence, wolf and girl braided together in one skin, and when she glanced at me I knew she could feel me feeling her, and for once neither of us looked away.

"You felt it," she said quietly. "When you crossed."

"I felt everything."

"That's what they'll have felt too. The crown. Whatever wears it." She pulled her knees up, chin resting on them, and stared into the dark trees. "A key that size, turning all at once? Gideon, you didn't cross a border tonight. You rang a bell that can be heard in the capital."

The words settled over me like snowfall. Somewhere behind us, a valley sealed itself around an old woman and her bees. Somewhere ahead, a king sat wearing a parasite for a crown, and it had just heard my name — not my name, but my *shape*, the shape of me, ringing out across a hundred miles of dark country.

I opened the ledger on my knees. The pages rustled in a wind I couldn't feel. And there, in ink a century old, beside a date that had stopped being tomorrow and become today:

*a nameless key, born outside magic, shall turn where the thirteenth moon turns.*

I closed the book and held it against my chest, over my heart, where the gold light still faintly pulsed.

"We walk tonight," I said. "While it's dark. While we're small."

Tamsin looked at me — at the glow under my skin, at the tears I wasn't bothering to hide, at whatever new thing I had become in the space of one crossing — and something in her fierce, grieving face shifted, softened, settled. Not trust. Not yet. But the decision to travel beside me a while longer, which is where all trust begins.

"Small," she agreed, rising, offering me her hand. "For now."

Above the black pines, the moon was rising — one turn thinner than the month before, waning toward its thirteenth, and it followed us down the mountain road like a pale eye that had finally, after sixteen years, found what it was looking for.
