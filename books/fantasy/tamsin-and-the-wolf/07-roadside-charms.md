## 7. Roadside Charms

The valley let us go easier than I expected.

I had braced for it to fight — old places like that hold what they keep the way a fist holds a stone — but the moment Gideon and I crossed the tree line and looked back, there was nothing to look back at. Mist had swallowed the whole of it: the garden, Maren's crooked chimney smoke, the bees still working their last hives of autumn. As if the valley had simply closed its hand and walked away.

Gideon stood in the wet grass with their arms wrapped around themselves, shivering, though the morning was mild.

"You feel it," I said. Not a question. I could see it in the way they held their head, tilted slightly, like a deer listening to something past hearing.

"Feel what?"

"The magic." I said the word carefully. In the valley it had been a dead thing to them, a word for charms that never quite worked, teas that only half-cured. Out here it was weather. It moved through the grass in slow silver ripples that anyone raised outside could read as easily as print. "It's everywhere now. You're standing in it."

They turned their hands over, palms up, as though expecting to catch some of it like rain. "It feels like," they began, then stopped. "Like the moment before a sneeze. All over. Constantly."

I laughed before I could stop myself — a short, rusty sound. I hadn't laughed since the tree line, since the hunters' arrows, since the blood on my side had soaked through my shirt and Gideon's hands had pressed against it without asking permission. "That's the most honest description of magic I've ever heard."

We walked east, toward the capital, because there was nowhere else to go. The prophecy ledger rode in Gideon's satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, and my mother's ring rode on its chain around my neck where I'd reclaimed it from the mud, and between those two small weights we carried everything either of us owned.

* * *

By the second day I knew we couldn't outrun the patrols on foot, so I taught Gideon instead.

Not because I wanted to. Because I had watched them sleep the first night — someone had to keep watch, and wolves don't need much — and I had seen the way their fingers twitched in dreams, tracing shapes in the air. Sigils. Old ones. The kind hedge-witches scribble on doorframes against bad luck, except Gideon drew them in their sleep with a fluency that made the hair on my arms stand up.

Maren had taught them, supposedly. Maren, who lived in a valley where magic went to die, who brewed charms that "never quite worked." Either Maren was the worst teacher in the history of craft, or she had been teaching with one hand tied behind her back and both eyes on the door.

"Show me your shielding charm," I said, on the morning of the third day, in the lee of a fallen wall where an old shepherd's hut had given up and become a hill of stones. We could hear the patrol road below us — boots, and the particular jingle of crown-forged iron that I would know in my sleep until I died.

"I don't really have one." Gideon crouched beside me, breath fogging. "Maren said shields were vanity. She said if you need a shield you've already made three mistakes."

"Maren sounds wise and insufferable."

"She is. Both."

"Good. Then this will be familiar." I picked up a pebble and set it on the flat stone between us. "A shield isn't vanity out here. It's the difference between walking into a town and being dragged into one. Watch."

I laid two fingers on the pebble and breathed out the way my mother had taught me when I was six years old, kneeling in a palace nursery that smelled of cedar and candlewax, back when the crown was just a heavy beautiful thing my father wore on feast days. *Small circle. Small cost.* The air above the pebble thickened, bent light the way water bends it, and when I flicked a twig at it, the twig slid off as if the pebble had grown a skin of glass.

Gideon watched with the expression I was beginning to know — not wonder exactly, but recognition. Like someone hearing a song they'd forgotten they knew.

"Now you."

"I told you, I don't—"

"Gideon." I kept my voice level. "You crossed a boundary that kills magic by dragging a bleeding wolf through it, and the wolf lived. You've been brewing charms in a dead zone for years and some of them half-worked, which should be impossible. Whatever Maren taught you, she taught you more than she admitted. Try."

They picked up a pebble of their own. Held it. Closed their eyes.

And the air *folded*.

Not bent — folded, clean as a page turning. A shell of shimmer snapped into being around the pebble so tight and so bright that the little stone seemed to sit inside a drop of water, and when I threw my twig, it didn't slide off. It *shattered*. Splinters hissed away across the stones, and the shield didn't even ripple.

Neither of us spoke for a moment. Somewhere down the slope, the patrol jingled past, oblivious.

"That's," I said finally, "a very good shield."

"It felt easy." Gideon opened their eyes and stared at their own hands as if the hands belonged to a stranger. "Tamsin, it felt *easy*. Is that wrong? It shouldn't be easy, should it?"

"No," I said quietly. "It shouldn't."

* * *

We fell into a rhythm after that, the two of us, and I distrusted how quickly it came.

Walk at dawn, hide at dusk. Travel the shepherd paths and drover roads where patrols were thin. Gideon learned to read the land the way I did — not by scent, that was mine alone, but by the small grammar of broken twigs and disturbed moss. In exchange I taught them craft in stolen lessons: how to draw a ward that would sour milk in a pursuer's flask, how to whisper a quieting charm over their own boot-steps, how to bank a fire so it gave heat without light.

Every lesson went the same way.

My wards took me a full breath to raise. Gideon's rose before they finished thinking them. My quieting charm softened footsteps; theirs erased them entirely, so completely that twice I lost track of them myself and had to fight down the animal panic of turning to find empty road. My fire-banking trick was a trick. When Gideon did it, the flames lay down like hounds and *stayed*.

"You're doing something extra," I accused them on the fifth night, watching a cookfire burn low and obedient in its ring of stones. "Some technique Maren taught you. Some hedge-witch secret."

"There's no secret." They poked the fire with a stick; it flared politely and settled again. "I do exactly what you show me. It just—" They searched for the word. "It listens. Everything listens to me out here. Didn't you say magic was weather? Well, it's been raining on me for five days and I'm starting to drown."

I should have said something sensible. Something about control, about caution, about the fact that power that comes too easily comes with a price tag hidden somewhere, because in my experience it always does.

Instead I heard myself say, "Show me the light charm."

"The what?"

"Light. Every apprentice learns it first. Candle-flame in the palm. My father's court mage taught it to children at Midwinter — it's a party trick, honestly, a flame the size of a thumbnail that doesn't burn." I held out my own hand and demonstrated: a small gold blossom of fire opened above my palm, warm and harmless, guttering in the breeze. "Now you."

Gideon cupped their hand the way I had. Drew the sigil — I watched them draw it, a simple three-stroke figure any village child could manage.

The clearing lit up like noon.

Flame stood off their palm a forearm high, white at the heart, throwing our shadows huge and trembling against the dark trees. Moths came staggering out of the night toward it. The heat of it touched my face from three feet away, and Gideon stared into it with their mouth open, and the fire did not burn them, did not even redden their skin, burned the way a star burns, which is to say with a patience that frightened me more than any hunger could.

"Put it out," I whispered.

"How?"

"*Think* it smaller!"

They thought it smaller. It shrank — reluctantly, like a creature asked to leave a warm bed — down to a candle-flame, down to a spark, down to nothing. Darkness rushed back in. For a long moment the only light was the cookfire and the moon coming up over the ridge, one night short of full.

"Tamsin," Gideon said, in a very small voice. "What am I?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for five days, the one the ledger answered and I hadn't, the one whose answer sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

I'm not a liar by nature. Wolves can't afford the habit; our noses are too good. But I looked at Gideon's face — pale, firelit, genuinely afraid — and I understood suddenly why Maren had spent years lying to them, and why she'd done it with love.

"I don't know," I said. Which was true. What I didn't say was: *but the prophecy does, and the prophecy says you were born to be spent.*

"Some things are written before we're ready to read them," I said instead. "When we reach somewhere safe, we'll read the ledger properly. All of it. Together. Until then—" I nodded at their hand. "Smaller. Always start smaller than you think you need. Promise me."

"I promise." They flexed their fingers, looking at them. "Tamsin? Thank you. For teaching me. Most people would have just used me and run."

"Most people haven't pulled a wolf out of a death-boundary with their bare hands." I fed a branch to the fire, more to occupy my mouth than anything. "Besides. You're the first person in months who's looked at me and seen Tamsin instead of the heir. Or the wolf. It's—" I stopped, surprised by my own honesty. "It's nice."

They smiled at me across the fire, tired and crooked and real, and I felt something in my chest loosen that had been clenched since the night the Sentinels came to my mother's grave.

Wary partnership, I had called it, in the privacy of my own head. Friendship, the word whispered back, treacherous as all soft words are. You cannot afford friends when you're carrying a prophecy and a bloodline and a price on your head. I knew that. I knew it the way I knew the taste of iron.

I let myself have it anyway.

* * *

The trouble found us on the seventh day, because trouble always knows when you've started to hope.

We had come down out of the hills onto the river road, where the going was easy and the danger doubled — easy roads carry patrols the way rivers carry silt. Gideon had grown confident with the quieting charm, enough that I'd let them lead while I ranged ahead in wolf-shape for an hour, running the wind, reading the country. It was good to be four-legged. It was better to come back and find a human shape waiting for me at a milestone, arms crossed, trying not to smile at the state I made shifting back.

"We should reach the ferry by tomorrow midday," Gideon said, marking our map — Maren's map, smuggled out in the satchel with the ledger. "There's a market town past it. Wren said—"

They stopped. Their head came up, sharp, the way mine did.

I heard it a heartbeat later: hooves. Not one horse. Six, maybe eight, moving fast on the road behind us, and beneath the hoofbeats that sound I would know in my sleep until I died — the jingle of crown-forged iron.

"Off the road," I breathed. "Now."

We dove for the alder brake along the riverbank, down through mud and dead nettles, and pressed ourselves flat among the roots as the patrol thundered past. Through the leaves I counted them: eight riders, grey cloaks, and every one of them wearing a collar of black metal that drank the afternoon light. Crown-iron. Not ordinary Sentinels, then. Bound men. Men the crown wore the way my father wore it, little parasites on little thrones.

They reined up fifty yards downstream. One of them dismounted and crouched over the mud of the road, and even at this distance I saw him lift something on the point of his knife.

Hair. Long and brown. Gideon's hair had caught on alder bark when we dove through.

"They have a tracker-charm," I whispered. My heart had gone slow and cold, the way it did before a hunt — or during one, depending which end of it you stood on. "Bound iron finds what it's fed. That's your hair. In a minute he'll know which way we ran."

Gideon's face had gone white, but their voice, when it came, was steady. "The river. If we cross, the water breaks scent-tracks. Maren said—"

"Maren said right." I was already pulling the ledger-satchel higher on Gideon's shoulder, checking the strap, checking their hood, doing with my hands what I couldn't do with words, which was tell them that whatever came next I intended for us both to survive it. "Deep water, then the far bank, then we stop being two travelers and become pilgrims, because nobody hunts pilgrims during Moonfast season. Can you swim?"

"I grew up in a valley with a river through it. Yes."

"Good." I looked at the water — wide, brown, fast with autumn rain — and then at Gideon, this impossible person Maren had hidden from the world, whose spells listened to them like devoted hounds, whose face was inked in a book a century old. "On three, we go in together. And Gideon?"

"Yeah?"

"When we're across — I want to try something. Your magic listens to you. Mine listens to me. I've never seen what happens when two people listen to each other." I bared my teeth, which in wolf-country is a grin. "Whatever the crown sends next, I think it's time it learned what it's up against."

Downstream, the tracker straightened up, turned, and pointed — directly at our alder brake.

"One," I said.
