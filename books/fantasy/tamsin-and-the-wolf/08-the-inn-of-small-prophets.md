## 8. The Inn of Small Prophets

The inn announced itself before we saw it: a smell of woodsmoke and wet wool rolling down the road like a welcome, and then a light — one yellow window burning in the blue dusk the way a single candle burns in a cathedral, small and stubborn and certain of itself.

Tamsin stopped at the bend in the road and put out an arm across my chest, which was becoming a habit of hers, and not one I minded as much as I pretended to.

"The Inn of Small Prophets," she said. "Named for the fortune-tellers who work the pilgrim road. They're mostly frauds."

"Mostly?"

"Mostly." She said it the way she said most things, flat and forward-facing, but her eyes were doing that thing they did when she was counting exits. "We need beds, bread, and to be nobody. Can you be nobody?"

I thought about the valley, about eleven years of being nobody on purpose, and almost laughed. "It's the one thing I'm qualified for."

* * *

Inside, the inn was all low beams and louder voices, a long room crowded with carters and pilgrims and two fur-traders who smelled like they'd been wearing their wares longer than their shirts. The fire was enormous and slightly too hot, the way fires are when you've been cold for three days on the road. My hands ached toward it before I'd decided anything.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with flour to her elbows and a voice that could have been heard from the road without any window's help. She took Tamsin's coin — the last of the honest silver — and gave us a room under the eaves and a look that said she'd already decided we were trouble and had priced that in.

"Supper's stew," she said. "Bread's extra. Trouble's double."

"We're not trouble," Tamsin said.

"No," the innkeeper agreed pleasantly. "You're *quiet*. Quiet's worse." And she went back to her dough.

We ate at the end of a bench near the wall, backs to it, the way Tamsin always sat. The stew was barley and turnip and something that might once have aspired to mutton, and it was the best thing I had ever tasted, because I was nineteen days out of Maren's garden and everything still tasted like freedom, even turnip.

I had the ledger open on my knees under the table. I'd taken to reading it the way other people chewed their nails — constantly, compulsively, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something kinder if I worried them enough. They never did. *A key born outside magic. A wolf heir. A king bound.* Tonight's date had come and gone three weeks ago in a chapel crypt I didn't want to think about, and the ledger had simply turned its page and kept writing, patient as winter.

"You're doing the face," Tamsin said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're reading something that's reading you back."

I closed the book. "There's a woman by the fire," I said, because I'd been trying not to say it for an hour. "She hasn't ordered anything. She hasn't spoken to anyone. She's just sitting there with her hands out like she's warming them, except she keeps turning them over. Palms up. Like she's waiting for someone to put something in them."

Tamsin didn't turn around. That was another habit of hers: never looking directly at a thing she wanted to see. "Describe her."

"Old. Grey braid wrapped twice around her head. A shawl with stars on it, real embroidered ones, not painted. And her eyes—" I hesitated. "Her eyes are closed. Have been since we came in. But she turned her head when you walked past. Followed you. With her ears, I suppose."

"A seer," Tamsin said, and there was iron in it now. "On the pilgrim road. Of course. Leave it alone, Gideon."

"I haven't done anything."

"You're holding your spoon upside down and you haven't blinked in a minute. That's what you look like when you're about to do something."

I looked down. The spoon was indeed upside down. "How do you know all my faces after three weeks?"

"Because you make so few of them around me." She said it lightly, but it landed somewhere beneath my ribs and stayed there, warm, and I busied myself with the stew so she couldn't see where it had landed.

Across the room, the old woman turned both palms slowly upward again, an offering to no one, or to everyone, or — I understood with a cold slide down my spine — to me.

* * *

I would like to tell you I resisted. That I finished my supper like a sensible person and went up to bed. But there is a kind of pull that lives in prophecy the way gravity lives in stone, and I had been falling toward this woman since the moment I saw her empty hands. Perhaps since the moment I opened a century-old ledger and found my own face inked inside it. Perhaps since the moment Maren first set me down in a garden where magic couldn't follow, and called it safety.

I crossed the room. Behind me I heard Tamsin exhale through her nose, the specific exhale of a wolf deciding not to bite anyone yet.

Up close, the seer smelled of juniper and old paper. Her shawl's embroidered stars caught the firelight and seemed to hold it a half-second too long. Her eyes stayed closed, lids smooth as river stones, and when I sat down across from her she smiled like she'd been expecting me since before I was born.

"There you are," she said. "Late, though. You were meant to come to me years ago. Someone moved the road."

My mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed kindly. "You wouldn't. Hands, child. Give me your hands."

Every sensible thread in me said no. Every thread tied to the ledger said yes faster than I could weigh them. I put my hands in hers.

They were dry and light as moth wings, and they were shaking — not with age, I realized. With something closer to fear. Her thumbs found the lines of my palm and traced them the way a blind woman reads, and I watched her face while she read mine, and I watched the kindness drain out of it like water from a cracked bowl.

"Hm," she said.

"Hm?" I said, in a voice I barely recognized as mine.

"Hm," she said again, and turned my hand over, and turned it back, as though checking whether she'd mislaid something. "That's odd. That's very—"

And then she recoiled.

There is no gentler word for it. She dropped my hands as if they'd burned her, shoved her chair back with a screech that cut the whole room's noise, and pressed her knotted fingers against her mouth. Around us, conversations faltered. The innkeeper looked up from her dough. Somewhere a dog began to bark at nothing.

"What?" I whispered. "What did you see?"

The old woman's blind eyes came open — and they were white, milk-pale, terrible, aimed at some middle distance where I did not exist. "Pages," she said. "Your pages are already written. All of them. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I read futures, child. Threads. Maybe-ness. Everyone comes to me full of maybe. You—" her breath hitched. "You are a finished book. Bound. Inked. Dried. There is nothing in you left unwritten, and the last page is tonight, and the page after that is blank, and blank pages in a bound book mean only one thing—"

"Stop." It wasn't my voice. It was Tamsin's, low and level, and she was suddenly between us, one hand flat on the table, the other resting easy and terrible at her hip. "The reading's over."

"You don't know what she is," the seer breathed.

"I know exactly what she is," Tamsin said, "because I've read the same book you have. And I know what happens to seers who talk about it in rooms full of strangers on roads watched by crown-sentinels." She leaned in, and even I felt the pressure of her, the wolf just under the skin of her, the heir of a deposed line wearing a borrowed coat. "So here is what happens instead. You forget our faces. You forget you ever touched these hands. And in exchange, you get to keep every one of your own pages unwritten."

The seer's white gaze slid to Tamsin and softened into something like pity. "Wolf-child. You can't buy what's already sold."

"Watch me."

Tamsin reached into her collar and drew out the ring.

I had seen it only once before, the night we camped in the birchwood, when she'd held it to the moonlight and told me whose it was — her mother's, the queen who died in the coup, the last true thing Tamsin owned from a life that had ended in blood. Silver worked into the shape of a running wolf, with a moonstone set where its heart should be, and the stone had a faint inner weather to it, clouds moving behind clouds.

"This buys silence," Tamsin said. "All of it. Permanent."

The seer's hands hovered over the ring without touching it, the way people hover over holy things. "Do you know what you're offering? This is royal work. Old royal work. There's maybe three rings like it left in the world and the crown has been hunting them for twenty years to melt them down."

"Then it's worth a great deal of silence."

"It's worth more than silence." The seer's voice dropped until I had to lean in to catch it, and her milk-white eyes found mine again, and whatever she saw there made her flinch a second time. "Girl. Listen to me, because I'll only say this once and then I'll take the ring and be mercifully unable to help you further. A finished book isn't a cage. Understand? Written isn't the same as doomed. But whoever wrote you wrote you *thoroughly* — every chapter, every turn, down to the ink drying on the final line. And a book that complete has an author, and authors of that kind do not leave loose ends." Her trembling hand closed at last over the ring, and the moonstone seemed to dim, or perhaps to hide. "Someone spent a great deal of magic making sure of you. Ask yourself what they were so afraid you'd choose freely."

The fire popped. The room's noise crept back in around us, conversations resuming, the dog giving up, the ordinary world reasserting itself as though nothing had happened. The seer tucked the ring away into her shawl with the tenderness of a woman burying a friend, stood, and shuffled toward the stairs without another word.

I sat very still. My palms, where she'd held them, felt strange — lighter, somehow, as if she'd read something off them that wasn't ink at all but absence.

"Gideon." Tamsin sat down across from me, close, her knee against mine under the table. "Look at me."

I looked at her. Firelight, grey eyes, the small scar through her eyebrow I still didn't know the story of.

"She's wrong," Tamsin said. "Or she's right, and it doesn't matter, because I've seen you choose things. You chose to drag a bleeding stranger over a boundary that could have killed you. Nobody wrote that. You chose to come find me in the cells. Nobody wrote that either, because I was there, and I'm telling you, you hesitated, and hesitation isn't in books. Hesitation is free will having a bad night."

Despite everything, despite the cold still sitting in my chest, I huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to be true. Comfort's incidental." She turned her mug in her hands, and for a moment she looked younger, and sadder, and more like the girl who had lost a mother and a throne and a name. "I shouldn't have given her the ring."

"Tamsin—"

"No. Let me say it, because I'll only manage it once." She stared into the mug. "Everything I have left of her fits in one hand. And I gave it away in a heartbeat, because it was yours to spend, apparently. Your safety. Your silence. Everything costs me nothing lately except the things I actually care about, and I keep paying anyway, and I don't—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "Don't make me regret it by getting yourself written to death. That's all. That's the whole speech."

For a while neither of us spoke. The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, and we both tracked it, and it was only a carter going to bed.

"Tamsin," I said finally. "What did she mean — *the last page is tonight*?"

Tamsin's jaw tightened. "Seers talk in riddles to sound expensive. Tonight is nothing. Tonight is stew and a bed and an early start."

But she said it too fast, and she wouldn't meet my eyes, and I remembered — with the slow, sick certainty of a trap closing — that I hadn't actually checked the ledger since noon.

* * *

I checked it in the eaves-room, by candlelight, while Tamsin barred the door and pretended not to watch.

The pages turned the way they always did, soft and eager, as if the book had been waiting for my hands. Past the crypt. Past the thirteenth moon's eclipse, which was behind us now, which was *supposed* to be behind us, which the ledger itself had named as the ending —

And there, under tomorrow's date, in fresh black ink that gleamed wet when the candlelight caught it:

*A key born outside magic comes to the Inn of Small Prophets, where a blind woman reads what cannot be unread.*

Below it, a sketch. Not my face this time.

An inn. This inn, down to the crooked chimney. And drawn small in the corner window, two figures — one standing, one seated — and beside the seated figure, a single word in the ledger's cramped antique hand:

*Witness.*

The candle guttered. Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard the front door open, and the innkeeper's voice rise in greeting — warm, welcoming, the voice of a woman admitting a guest on a cold night.

Then it stopped.

Mid-word. Mid-breath. Stopped the way a clock stops.

And in the silence that followed, Tamsin went very still by the door, nostrils flaring, and said the four worst words I had ever heard her say:

"Iron. Crown-forged. Many."
