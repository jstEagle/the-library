## 9. Crown-Sentinels

The road to the capital ran through country that remembered being wild.

Tamsin felt it in her bones the way she felt weather coming—old forests gone to pasture, hedgerows grown feral where farmers had stopped tending them, and beneath it all a hum of magic like blood in the ears. Three days out from the valley, the world was thick with craft again: charms nailed over doorways, blessing-knots in the fence wire, a shrine at every crossroads with its candle stubs burned down to pools of wax. Magic had never left this land. It had only been waiting for someone worth following.

She walked half a pace ahead of Gideon, as she always did now, though neither of them had agreed to it. It had simply happened somewhere on the second day, the way habits happen, the way a pack forms without ceremony. She scouted. Gideon followed. The ledger rode against Gideon's chest inside their coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and Tamsin caught herself glancing back at it the way sailors glance at storm clouds.

"You keep looking at me like I'm about to sprout a second head," Gideon said.

"I'm looking at the book."

"I'm carrying the book."

"Which is why I'm looking at you." Tamsin stepped over a fallen gate, its hinges rusted to lace. "That thing has gotten us hunted across half a province. I'd feel better if it were strapped to a mule."

"There are no mules."

"Then I'd feel better if you walked slower and complained more. You're too quiet for a hedge-witch. Hedge-witches mutter. They talk to their herbs. You walk like someone trying not to leave footprints."

Gideon considered this with that maddening, level patience they brought to everything, as if the whole world were a garden bed that only needed steady hands. "Maybe I learned it in the valley," they said. "When you spend your whole life hiding, you get good at it."

Tamsin said nothing. The words landed closer to the bone than Gideon could know. *When you spend your whole life hiding.* Yes. She knew the shape of that life from the inside—the crouched years after her mother's death, when she'd slept in ditches and stolen bread and taught herself to be small even in wolf form, because a wolf that drew eyes did not live long. She had run from the palace thinking herself the last creature alive who understood what it cost to be invisible on purpose.

Then she'd watched this gangly apprentice drag a bleeding stranger across a boundary that should have killed them both, and understood she'd been wrong.

*Don't,* she told herself, the same word she'd been using for three days. *Don't get attached. Don't make another person into a reason to hesitate.* Her father's face floated up behind her eyes—what remained of it—and she pushed it down where it lived, in the cellar of her chest, behind a door she kept locked with both hands.

The afternoon was going copper when they came to the ford.

It wasn't much of one—a river gone shallow and braided with autumn, willows leaning over gravel bars, the water talking to itself in a hundred small voices. On the far bank the road climbed toward a ridge crowned with pines, and beyond the pines, if the map Wren had scratched for them was true, lay the river town of Harrowgate and the last safe beds before the capital's reach began in earnest.

Tamsin stopped at the water's edge and let her senses open.

This was the part of herself she trusted most, the part no crown-forged iron could counterfeit: the animal attention that read a landscape the way scholars read texts. Wind from the northwest, carrying woodsmoke and wet stone and—her nostrils flared—horse. Not near. Upwind, which meant the horses hadn't smelled them yet. But horse meant riders, and riders on this road, this close to the capital's long arm, meant—

"Gideon." Her voice came out flat and low, the voice she used for warnings. "How many charms do you have left?"

"Three salt rounds, two hush-knots, and a light-spell Maren would be ashamed of." Gideon was already reaching into their coat. "Why?"

"Because we're about to have company."

* * *

They came off the ridge at dusk, and there were six of them.

Tamsin counted them from the shadow of a drystone wall, Gideon pressed beside her, and what she saw turned her mouth dry. These were not the bounty-men who had chased her through the spring provinces—greedy, sloppy, armed with silver-tipped crossbows and bad information. These moved like soldiers. They dismounted in sequence, two holding the horses while four advanced on foot in a staggered line, and even at forty yards she could hear the sound their armor made.

Not the ring of steel. A kind of *hum*. A low, hungry note, like a finger run around the rim of a glass.

Crown-forged iron.

Her grandmother had described it once, in the years before the fall, when Tamsin had been small enough to sit under the table while the exiles talked politics over sour wine. *The crown eats a king,* her grandmother had said, *and what it excretes is iron. Never touch it bare-handed. It doesn't cut you. It unmakes the craft in your blood. A shifter caught in crown-iron stays caught.*

Six Sentinels. Four advancing, two at the horses. Full dark coming down over the ford like a lid.

"We go around," Gideon whispered. "Through the willows, we can—"

"They'll hear the water. And look at the far bank." Tamsin nodded toward the ridge road, where two of the horses stood saddled and ready. "They're not patrolling. They're waiting. Someone told them we'd come through the ford tonight."

Gideon went very still. "Wren?"

"No. Wren owes me a life-debt; they wouldn't sell me twice." Though doubt gnawed at her even as she said it—everyone sold everyone, eventually, in the country her father ruled. "The seer, maybe. At the inn. Silence bought with a ring isn't silence bought forever."

"So we fight."

"So we *don't*." Tamsin turned and took Gideon by the shoulders, and felt how thin they were, how warm, how stubbornly alive. "Listen to me. Crown-iron unmakes shifters. If it touches me while I'm changed, I don't come back—I stay whatever shape it catches me in, or worse, nothing at all. So I can't fight them. But they're not here for you. They think you're luggage. A hedge-witch with a book." She pulled the ledger from Gideon's coat before they could stop her, wrapped it tight, and shoved it back against their chest. "You take the willows downstream. There's a fisherman's path along the bank; I smelled woodsmoke from a hut. Get to Harrowgate. Find Wren. Don't wait for me."

"No," Gideon said. Simply, immediately, the way they refused everything—extra food, extra sleep, extra distance between them. "No, we go together."

"Gideon—"

"I dragged you across a boundary that should have killed us both. You don't get to hand me my own lesson back with different words." Their hands came up and gripped her wrists, and their eyes in the dying light were fierce and frightened and absolutely immovable. "I spent nineteen years being hidden, Tamsin. Being *kept*. I am done watching people I—" They stopped. Swallowed the word whole. "I'm done watching people get taken because of me."

Something cracked in Tamsin's chest, hairline and fatal, right down the middle of all her resolutions.

*Don't get attached.*

Too late. Had been too late since the tree line.

"Then here's what happens," she said, and heard her own voice drop into the register she used for commands she couldn't afford to repeat. "We split at the willows anyway—but not to run. I draw them. All six. I'm fast, and in the dark, in the reeds, they can't corner me unless they net me, and nets aren't crown-iron. I lead them upriver, circle back through the pine stand, and meet you at the fisherman's hut by moonrise. If I'm not there by full dark tomorrow—"

"I'll wait."

"If I'm not there by full dark tomorrow, you take the book to the ruins beneath the old palace, and you finish this, and you don't come looking for me. Swear it."

"I won't swear that."

"Swear it or I don't shift, and then we both die stupidly at a ford, and the crown wins, and your whole life in that valley was for nothing." She said it cruelly, deliberately, because cruelty was the only lever that moved Gideon when kindness failed. "Swear."

The pause lasted three heartbeats. Then Gideon said, "Moonrise tomorrow," in a voice like a knife laid flat on a table, and Tamsin chose to believe it was agreement.

* * *

It went wrong in ninety seconds.

Later—much later, in a cell that smelled of straw and iron, she would replay those seconds until they wore grooves in her, hunting the moment she could have chosen differently. The moment was always the same. It was the moment she looked back.

The plan worked exactly as plans do, which is to say it worked until it met the world. She went into the reeds and shed her skin the way she'd done ten thousand times, the change rolling through her like cold water, spine lengthening, senses blooming wide and silver. Wolf. The night opened up for her—every heartbeat in the grass, every breath of wind, the six soldiers' sweat sharp with fear under their discipline. Good. Fear was useful. Fear slowed hands.

She broke cover on the upstream side, gave them one glimpse of grey movement, one snarl pitched to carry, and the line folded toward her exactly as she'd known it would. Soldiers chase what frightens them. She led them along the bank, quick and untouchable, weaving the willow trunks, letting them taste failure. Behind her, downstream, she heard the small splash of Gideon entering the shallows, and something in her chest unclenched a fraction.

Then the net came out of nowhere.

Not thrown—*cast*, the way a charm is cast, and she understood a half-second too late that these were not ordinary soldiers. The net was woven with threads of dull grey metal spun fine as hair, and it fell over her like a spider's work, and the world *screamed*.

Crown-iron didn't burn. That was the horror of it. It simply reached into her and began to *unpick*. Her shifted body frayed at the edges like rope under strain. She felt the wolf-shape start to slide, felt her human bones trying to surface through it, felt the two shapes grinding against each other like millstones with her soul caught between. She went down in the mud and gravel and bit through the netting, and the iron filled her mouth with a taste like coins and grave-dirt, and still she fought, because fighting was the only prayer she knew.

Four of them held her down. She broke a man's arm with a twist of her neck. She raked a helmet loose and saw a young face underneath, terrified, human, and hated him and pitied him in the same breath. The humming of the iron grew louder, filling her skull, and underneath it—she would swear this later, and no one would believe her—underneath it she heard the crown itself. Laughing. It knew her blood. It had eaten her father; it recognized the flavor of his daughter.

And then, through the roar, she heard Gideon shouting her name.

*No.* The word tore through her worse than the iron. *Run. Run, you stubborn, brave, impossible fool—*

They came out of the willows with a salt round blazing in each fist, white fire spilling over their knuckles, hurling charms with a precision no hedge-witch had any business possessing. One Sentinel went down clutching eyes seared blind. Another staggered as a hush-knot sealed his shout inside his throat. And for one absurd, blazing moment Tamsin thought—*they might actually—*

The sergeant moved.

He was older than the others, grey at the temples, and he didn't reach for Gideon at all. He simply turned, unhurried, and drove an iron-cored gauntlet into the net, into *her*, into the place where her ribs met her heart, and the crown-iron sang through her whole body like a struck bell.

The change shattered. She collapsed into her human shape mid-struggle, naked and gasping in the mud, her limbs suddenly heavy as drowned timber, her craft guttering out of her like a lamp out of oil. The net cinched. Chains followed—she heard them, felt their cold, and knew that touch would mean the same unraveling.

"Gideon," she rasped. Blood in her throat. "Go. *Go.*"

But Gideon had stopped. Ten yards away, lit by their own failing charm-fire, they stood frozen in the exact posture Tamsin had seen once before—at the tree line, the instant before they'd chosen to drag a dying stranger across a boundary. The posture of a person deciding to break themselves open for someone else.

"Don't," Tamsin whispered. "Don't you dare—"

The sergeant raised a fist, and light leapt from it—not the Sentinels' craft but something older, something with the crown's hunger in it, a snare of golden threads that flew across the ford and closed around Gideon like a lily closing at dusk. Gideon went down. The ledger skidded free of their coat, spinning across the gravel, fetching up against a stone at the water's edge, its oilcloth wrapping torn, its ancient pages drinking the dark.

And Tamsin, chained and kneeling in the mud of a nameless ford, did the only thing left to her.

She spoke.

Not loudly. The iron had taken most of her strength, but it had not taken her voice, and her voice was the one thing in all the world that still carried her father's blood before the crown had soured it. She used the high speech of the old court, the language of kings, and she put her whole ruined inheritance into it:

"Hear me. I am Tamsin, daughter of Aldric, heir of the ember throne. Touch that witch and the prophecy dies unwritten—and your master's crown comes off his head in a box. You want the key? Then the key walks free, and I go with you, and everyone gets paid."

Silence. The river talking. The Sentinels looking at one another.

The sergeant studied her for a long moment, and then Gideon, and then the ledger lying in the shallows, and Tamsin watched him do the arithmetic of a career man weighing prophecies against promotions.

"The witch keeps the book," he said at last. "The wolf comes with us." He glanced down at her, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "His Majesty will want you alive, my lady. What he'll want *done* with you—" He shrugged. "That's above my station."

They bound her wrists with iron thread. They threw a cloak over her shoulders, and she clutched it around herself and tried to memorize everything at once: the ford, the pines, the fisherman's hut downstream with its thread of woodsmoke, and Gideon—Gideon on their knees in the gravel, held by golden light, watching her with an expression that Tamsin knew would follow her into whatever cell awaited. Guilt. Fury. Resolve gathering behind them like a storm deciding which coast to ruin.

"Moonrise tomorrow," Gideon called, voice cracking. "Tamsin. *Moonrise.*"

She smiled at them with blood on her teeth, because it was the only gift she had left to give.

"Harrogate," she said. "Find Wren. And Gideon—" The chains hauled her up; she let them. "Whatever's written in that book about you—it hasn't happened yet. Make them wrong. Promise me you'll make them *wrong*."

The net closed over her head, and the crown-iron sang, and the last thing Tamsin saw of the free world was Gideon lunging forward against the golden snare, screaming her name into the dark—and the ledger, sodden and defiant, glowing faintly in the shallows like a coal refusing to die.

* * *

They took her north through the night, hooded, chained to a saddle, the iron humming against her skin like a hive of patient wasps. Somewhere behind them, she told herself, Gideon was cutting loose. Finding the hut. Finding Wren. Turning guilt into something with edges.

She held onto that the way drowning people hold driftwood. Because she had heard the sergeant's orders before the hood came down, and one sentence of them had lodged under her ribs like a splinter:

*"Straight to the palace dungeons. His Majesty's asked for the wolf-daughter by name—and he's asked twice. Once in his own voice."*

A pause. The sound of a man choosing his words around something he didn't understand.

*"Once in the other one."*
