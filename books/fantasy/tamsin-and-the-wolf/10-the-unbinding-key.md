## 10. The Unbinding Key

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Tamsin's breathing evened out in the loft above.

Gideon sat at the table with the prophecy ledger open before them, and the inn around them had gone quiet in that particular way old buildings go quiet — not silent, but listening. The beams ticked as they cooled. Somewhere below the floorboards, a cellar of bottled cider breathed its slow apple-sweet breath through a gap in the stone. Rain came against the shutters in long, patient strokes, like fingers testing a lock.

They should have slept. They had walked since dawn, and their feet still ached with the memory of it, and tomorrow — if Wren's directions held — there would be another road, longer and more dangerous than this one. But the ledger lay open under their hands the way a wound lies open under a bandage, and Gideon could not stop looking.

*The key shall be born outside magic,* the old script read, *and to the valley of forgetting shall the wolf carry them.*

Outside magic. Born outside it. The words had seemed strange when Tamsin first read them aloud on the road, strange the way a stranger's coat is strange — noticeable, but belonging to someone else. Now, alone, with only the fire and the rain for company, the coat fit. And Gideon was beginning to understand that it had always fit, and that everyone but them had known.

They turned back to the earlier pages. The hand that had written the prophecies was careful, clerkish, a scribe's hand — but every few pages, cramped into margins and gutters and once, daringly, across the bottom of an illustration of a burning crown, a different hand appeared. Smaller. Angrier. The ink was browner, older, as though the writer had been in a hurry or weeping or both.

Gideon fetched Maren's little brass reading-glass from their pack and bent close.

The first marginal note sat beside the verse about the thirteenth moon:

*Not thirteen moons. Thirteen turnings. The moon does not count; it is counted.*

Beside the verse about the king bound to his crown:

*Aldric is the third. The first wore it willingly. The second wore it too long. There will not be a fourth.*

And beside the line that had made Tamsin go white as birchbark — *the nameless key shall pay what cannot be refused* — the brown-inked hand had written, in letters pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the page:

*TOLL. Ask what the toll IS, you fools. Ask what a thing born outside magic can give that magic itself will accept. Not blood. Not years. Magic has no hunger for those. It takes only what it cannot make.*

Gideon read the last sentence four times. *It takes only what it cannot make.*

Magic could make light. It could make wolves out of women and women out of wolves; it could make crowns that lived and charms that lied and gardens grow in defiance of frost. What couldn't it make?

The answer arrived not as a thought but as a coldness, starting behind the sternum and spreading outward like water finding the cracks in a floor.

Memory.

Magic could not make memory. Memory belonged to the one who remembered. It was the one possession that could not be forged, borrowed, or conjured — a coin minted nowhere but inside a living mind. A thing born outside magic, raised where magic could not follow, would have spent a whole life accumulating exactly the currency magic could not print.

Gideon set down the reading-glass very carefully, because their hands had begun to shake, and they did not want to hear the glass rattle against the wood.

"No," they said aloud, to the fire, to the rain. "No, that isn't—"

But the ledger was already turning under their fingers, past the prophecies proper, into the last pages — pages Tamsin had never seen, because Gideon had kept the book close since the tree line, the way you keep a wound close. The final leaves were different paper, softer, cheaper, and filled edge to edge with the brown-inked hand. Not prophecy anymore. Confession.

*She brought the child to me at midwinter,* it began. *The last unbinding's daughter, or son — she would not say which, and I did not ask. She said the child must be raised where the crown cannot smell them. She said the valley forgets, and so the valley hides.*

Gideon's mouth went dry. *She.* The pronoun sat on the page like a lit coal.

*I asked her what the toll would be, when the time came. She said she did not know. I believe she lied. I believe she knew exactly, and that is why her hands shook when she handed the child over, and why she would not come inside but stood at my door in the snow like a woman at a graveside.*

*I have worked out the arithmetic since. The unbinding requires memory — a life's worth of it, paid all at once into the crown's hunger until the parasite starves and lets go. The key pays everything. Every face, every name, every summer. They walk away from the binding free, and hollow, and they do not know what they have lost, which is the cruelest mercy in the design. Whoever built this riddle built it so the key would not even mourn themselves.*

*Unless someone remembers for them.*

Gideon stopped reading. The fire popped. Upstairs, Tamsin shifted in her sleep, and the floorboard creaked once, and then the inn resumed its listening.

Their whole life had been a room with one door, and they were standing in front of it now, and the door was open, and they were afraid of the draft coming through.

Because here was the thing — here was the small, obscene, undeniable thing: Gideon could not remember their childhood.

Oh, they had explanations. Everyone in the valley had explanations. The fever, Maren said, when Gideon was seven and first noticed the gap and asked. A bad fever that burned the early years out of you like a candle burning down past its holder. It happened. It was sad. Eat your porridge.

And Gideon had believed it, because believing it was easier than noticing how carefully Maren changed the subject, how her eyes went to the window whenever the past came up, how the valley itself seemed to lean around Gideon like people leaning around a drunk at a funeral.

They tried now, deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to learn its shape. Their earliest memory was the garden — must be, surely — the smell of bee-balm and turned earth, Maren's hands showing theirs how to tie a bean vine without strangling it. But when they reached for what came *before* the garden, there was nothing. Not fog, not shadow, not a locked door. Nothing. A blank with clean edges, like a page torn out rather than left unreadable. Fevers left wreckage. This left nothing at all.

Fever didn't tear cleanly. Only scissors tore cleanly.

"Mother," Gideon whispered — the word they had always used for Maren, half-habit, half-hope — and then laughed, a small terrible sound, because the woman who took them in had taken them in the way a soldier takes a wounded bird off a battlefield: knowing full well what it was being saved *for*.

No. That wasn't fair. Even as the bitterness rose, Gideon heard the ledger's own testimony against it. *Her hands shook. She stood at my door like a woman at a graveside.* Maren had not delivered them to the slaughter. Maren had hidden them — in the one place on earth where the prophecy's clock could not find them, where magic died at the tree line and the crown's long nose could not follow the scent. Twenty years, nearly. Two decades of charms that never quite worked, of bees and bread and boredom, of a safety so complete it had felt like a cage — and it *had* been a cage, but cages keep wolves out as well as birds in.

Maren hadn't raised a sacrifice. She'd raised a hiding place, and put a person inside it, and loved them, and prayed — hedge-witches don't pray, but Maren had, Gideon saw it now, in every ward scratched over every doorframe, in every lesson about staying indoors, in every flinch at travelers' news from the capital — she had prayed the date would pass them by.

And then Gideon had gone and found the ledger. Had read their own face in a century-old book. Had walked out of the valley with a wolf at their side, straight toward the altar they'd been built upon.

The fire had sunk to embers. Gideon fed it two splits of birch and watched the flames climb, and made themselves keep reading, because the alternative was sitting in the dark with the arithmetic unfinished, and they had never in their life been able to leave a sum unresolved.

*There is a way,* the brown-inked hand wrote, near the end. *I found it in the third year of searching, and I have told no one, and I will write it here only because paper keeps secrets better than people. The toll need not be paid whole. Memory divides. A life is not one coin but ten thousand, and the unbinding asks only that the sum be met. If the key pays less than everything, the binding loosens by that much and no more — but if the key pays enough, if they pay deep, past the recent years into the root-years, the crown will take the oldest memories first, for it is greedy the way fire is greedy and eats the driest wood first.*

*Which means a clever key might choose what to lose. Might feed it the years they can least afford to keep — or the ones they can least afford to lose — and live on around the gap, as people do, as I have seen people do.*

*Or two might share the toll. I do not know if it is possible. I do not know what happens when two purses are poured into one hunger. But the crown is a single appetite, and appetites do not count coins, only weight.*

Two.

Gideon closed the book.

The rain had softened to a drizzle. Through the shutter-crack, far off over the black hills, a smear of lighter dark marked where the clouds were thinning — the waning moon, three turns gone now, ten left, falling toward its thirteenth like a stone toward the bottom of a well.

They thought of Maren's face the day the ledger was found — the pallor, the order to stay indoors, the way her hands had done that thing they did, smoothing her apron over and over as if the fabric were a wound she could press closed. All those years of apron-smoothing. All those years of a woman standing guard over a secret that was eating her alive, and Gideon had called it fussiness. Had resented it, some days. Had dreamed, some nights, of a mother who explained things, who trusted them, who let them cross the tree line just once —

—and Maren had known, Gideon understood now, that the wanting itself was dangerous. That curiosity was the leash the prophecy held. That the moment Gideon wondered hard enough about who they were, the valley would lose its grip on them, because the valley could hide a person but it could not hide a question.

*You were never meant to read that book,* Maren had said, white-lipped. And she had been right, and she had also been wrong, because the book had been waiting a hundred years, and books written by desperate people are patient in a way that mothers cannot afford to be.

Gideon got up, quietly, and climbed the ladder to the loft.

Tamsin lay on her side facing the wall, one arm flung out, her breathing deep and even. In sleep the anger went out of her face and left something younger, something that looked — Gideon realized with a jolt — like grief wearing a girl's skin. The heir of a dead house, hunted by her father's own iron, carrying a prophecy that ended her bloodline in ten turns of the moon. She had told them all of it on the road in that flat, furious voice she used for unbearable things, and Gideon had listened, and had not understood until this hour that they were both running from the same book.

If the toll could be split — if the brown-inked stranger's arithmetic held — then Tamsin need never know what Gideon had decided while she slept. Or rather: Tamsin would know, afterward, and would grieve, and would be alive, and her father would be alive, and the crown would be ash, and somewhere in Gideon's head there would be a hole shaped like a valley, and they would not even miss it, because you cannot miss what you do not remember.

That was the trick of it. That was the filthy genius of the design. The sacrifice cost nothing to the one who paid it — after. Only before. Only now, tonight, in this hour, did anyone have to feel the weight of it, and by morning Gideon could simply choose not to tell, and the choice would vanish along with everything else, tidy as a swept floor.

They stood in the dark of the loft a long time, watching Tamsin breathe.

Then they climbed back down, took up the pen and the bottle of ink they'd bought in the last town, and opened the ledger to its final blank page. If someone had to remember what was about to be given away, let it be paper. Paper kept secrets better than people. Paper, apparently, also kept promises.

They wrote fast, before courage failed:

*To whoever finds this — to me, most likely, if any of me survives the paying —*

*My name is Gideon Crane. I was raised in a valley that forgets, by a witch named Maren, who did not steal my childhood. She buried it to keep it safe, the way you bury seed. I am going to spend it now, and I am choosing this, and I want it written down that I chose it, because after tonight no one will be left who knows that I ever had anything to choose with.*

*Ten moons. One crown. Two purses, if the old man's sums hold true.*

*Some dates are endings. Let this one be mine, so that hers —*

The pen hovered. Outside, the drizzle had stopped altogether, and through the shutter-crack the clouds had torn open, and the waning moon looked in at them, three-quarters gone, counting itself down.

Gideon finished the line.

*— hers can be a beginning.*

They blotted the page, closed the ledger, and sat with their hands flat on the cover, feeling the whole shape of their life rearrange itself around them like furniture moved in the dark. In the morning they would wake Tamsin. In the morning they would point them both south, toward the capital, toward the Moonfast crowds and the ruined palace beneath them, toward the cell where a wolf waited in iron.

In the morning, they would lie to her, gently, and hate themselves for it, and do it anyway.

The ember-light guttered. The moon slid westward, one night closer to its thirteenth turn, and Gideon Crane sat alone with a book of prophecies and the last full night of their own remembering, and did not sleep at all.
