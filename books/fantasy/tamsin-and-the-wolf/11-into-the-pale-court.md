## 11. Into the Pale Court

The road to the capital was paved with the bones of old pilgrimages, and by the third day Gideon had learned to read them. White stones, worn smooth, set into the earth in a line that wandered like a drunkard's thought—each one a marker where the faithful had once knelt on their way to the Moonfast, each one polished by a century of knees and prayers. Now the stones were cracked, and weeds grew through the mortar, and no one knelt anymore except to pick a pocket or check a boot sole for wear.

Gideon walked beside Wren and tried not to think about Tamsin.

It didn't work. It never worked. Tamsin was there in every silence, in the space at Gideon's left shoulder where she should have been walking, tall and scowling and alive. Two days since the Sentinels had taken her. Two days of sleeping in ditches with the ledger pressed against their ribs like a second heartbeat, of waking at every snap of twig convinced it was crown-forged iron coming for them too.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

"Doing what."

"The face. The one where you're chewing on your own guilt like it's gristle." Wren was small and quick and moved the way water moves, finding every gap in the crowd, in conversation, in a person's attention. Their hair was cropped close and dyed the color of river mud, and they wore three coats layered against the autumn cold, all of them stolen, none of them matching. "Save it. Guilt's a fine spice but a terrible meal. You'll want your strength for the city."

"I'm not hungry."

"Liar. Everyone's hungry going into Vespergate." Wren said the name the way other people said *storm* or *fire*—with respect, and a little relish. They crested the hill ahead of Gideon, and then stopped, and for a moment even Wren's restless hands went still.

The capital lay below in the bowl of the valley's sister: a gray sprawl of slate roofs and smoking chimneys, ringed by walls the color of old teeth, and rising from its heart the pale bulk of the old palace—ruined, half-collapsed, its broken towers silhouetted against a sky already bruising toward evening. And above it, above everything, hung the moon. Nearly full. Three nights short of its thirteenth turn.

Even at this distance, Gideon felt it. A pull behind the eyes, faint as a hook set in soft flesh. The same pull that had dragged them out of the valley in the first place, that had made Maren go white as whey and order them indoors. *Stay inside, love. Whatever you hear, whatever you see—stay inside.*

They hadn't stayed inside. Look where staying inside had gotten Tamsin.

"There it is," Wren said softly. "Vespergate. City of bells and knives. You'll love it or it'll eat you, and honestly, coin says both."

* * *

They came through the South Gate at dusk, in a river of festival traffic so dense that the guards had given up checking faces and settled for checking purses—which, Gideon noted, Wren had anticipated by carrying nothing worth stealing and looking bored enough to be poor. Gideon carried the ledger sewn into the lining of their coat, flat against their spine, and every time a guard's hand brushed near it their heart performed a small violent drumroll.

The disguise had been Wren's idea, and Wren's craft. Charm-sellers were everywhere before a Moonfast—hedge-folk come to town to sell luck-strings and fever-knots to the superstitious, licensed by nothing, tolerated by everyone because even Sentinels wanted a charm against bad weather. Gideon wore Maren's old traveling cloak, faded indigo, hemmed with the protective knotwork that any hedge-witch would recognize and any fraud would fake badly. At their belt hung a tray of charms they'd made on the road: braided threads, knotted cords, little bundles of dried herbs wrapped in waxed linen.

Here was the joke of it, the bitter joke Gideon kept turning over like a stone in the pocket: the charms were real. Or as real as anything they'd ever made in the valley, which was to say, mostly not. But out here, past the tree line, past the boundary where magic died—out here their hands remembered things they'd never been taught. The knots tightened themselves true. The herbs hummed faintly when Gideon held them. On the road, Wren had bought a headache-charm as a joke and reported, with genuine unease, that their headache had vanished and hadn't returned in two days.

"You're a walking scandal," Wren had said. "Do you know what charm-sellers would pay for your fingers? Do you know what the *crown* would pay?"

So now Gideon walked into Vespergate wearing their own strangeness as a costume, selling small magics to a city that didn't know what it was buying.

Inside the walls, the festival had already begun. Lanterns hung from every eave and balcony, hundreds of them, paper and horn and glass, glowing amber and rose against the deepening blue. The streets ran thick with people—shopkeepers hauling out barrels of spiced wine, children running underfoot with moon-masks pushed up on their heads, a woman on a balcony scattering bread to the crowd while a priest below shouted the old blessing, *the moon wanes, the moon returns, what is spent shall be restored.* Drums somewhere, many drums, a heartbeat under everything.

And over it all, threaded through the lantern-light and the wine-smell and the drumbeat, something else. Something Gideon felt in their teeth. A wrongness, thin as a wire. The same sensation they'd had at the tree line the day the wolf collapsed—but inverted, magnified, spread across an entire city like oil across water.

"What is that?" they asked.

Wren's grin flickered and died. "You feel it too? Huh." They glanced up, quick, at the ruined palace looming over the rooftops. "That's the Crown, love. Everyone feels it this close to the thirteenth turn. They just don't know that's what they're feeling. They call it festival-fever. Tempers run hot, crowds go strange, the guard doubles its watch. Every Moonfast it's worse. This year—" Wren drew a finger across their throat, delicately. "This year I'm betting on riots."

* * *

Wren's safehouse was a garret above a dye-shop in the Tanner's Quarter, reached by a staircase that smelled of urine and vinegar and creaked in six distinct dialects of complaint. The room itself was surprisingly comfortable: a pallet, a stove, a window overlooking a courtyard full of hanging dyed cloths that turned the light red and violet and gold.

From beneath a floorboard, Wren produced maps. Real ones, inked on good paper, marked in a hand that was neat despite the fingers that had drawn it being quick as birds.

"Now," Wren said, spreading the largest across the floor and weighting its corners with candle stubs. "Lesson one of Vespergate: the city is a body, and we are going to walk it like surgeons. Here—" a tap, "—the Moonfast market, where you'll set up your tray tomorrow and sell charms and look harmless. Here, the Sentinel barracks; avoid. Here, the chapel of the Pale Court—the old royal chapel, deconsecrated a hundred years back when the palace fell. Underneath it are the cells where they keep political prisoners. That's where they'll have her."

Gideon looked at the little square Wren indicated. It seemed impossibly far away, impossibly well-guarded, impossibly *underground*. "How do you know they have her there? They could have taken her anywhere."

"Because I know Sentinels," Wren said. "Crown-forged iron doesn't travel well; it has to stay near the source, near the palace. And because yesterday morning a tinker I know saw a covered wagon come off the processional road and stop at the chapel, and the men unloading it walked like men carrying something heavy and cursed, which iron is." Wren looked up, and for the first time since the tree line, their face held no performance at all. Just a sharp, plain seriousness. "She pulled me out of the Merrow Canal when I was eleven. I'd picked the wrong purse—a river-gang purse—and they were going to drown me slow, as a lesson. She came out of the water wearing teeth and gave them a different lesson." Wren shrugged, the motion too casual to be honest. "I owe a life-debt. In my trade, that's the only currency that can't be counterfeited. So we get her out. Whatever it costs."

"And if it costs more than we have?"

Wren smiled again, and it was the smile of someone standing on a ledge deciding to enjoy the view. "Then we spend it anyway. Now. Lesson two."

* * *

They worked until midnight, and Gideon learned more about the city in one night of Wren's talk than they had learned about the whole world in seventeen years in the valley.

Which gate the Sentinels watched hardest (the Processional). Which they watched least (the Drowned Gate, because it opened onto a sewer). How the festival would unfold—three nights of it, building toward the eclipse on the third, when the moon would turn dark at midnight and every soul in Vespergate would look up at once. How the guard changed at the chapel, how the cells were reached by a stair behind the vestry, how the vestry door had a lock that was old work, pre-palace, the kind Wren could open "with a hairpin and opinions."

"And you?" Wren asked, late, when the candles had burned down to pools. "What's your part in this grand surgery?"

Gideon was quiet a moment. They had been turning something over all evening, feeling it grow heavier and more certain, the way dough rises in a warm room.

"The ledger says the key goes to the king," they said finally. "Not sneaks to the king. Not is smuggled to the king. The marginalia—Maren's handwriting, a hundred years old, in the margins—it says *the key walks in the front door.*" They touched their spine, where the book lay hidden. "I've been thinking about what that means. And I think it means I'm not supposed to sneak anywhere. I think I'm supposed to be seen."

Wren stared at them. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"Probably both."

"No, wait." Wren sat forward, eyes catching the candlelight, and Gideon watched the idea take hold of them the way wind takes a sail. "Wait. The Moonfast market. Hundreds of charm-sellers. Thousands of strangers. Everyone masked or half-masked after dark, everyone jostling, everyone looking up at the moon instead of at each other. If you're going to be seen, that's where you're seen from. And if the Sentinels are hunting a runaway apprentice and a wolf-blood—" they tapped the map, "—they'll be watching the roads and the gates and the taverns. Nobody watches a charm-stall twice."

"You want me to set up a stall directly under their noses."

"I want you to set up a stall directly under their noses and *sell them things*, Gideon. Sell a Sentinel a luck-knot. Let him hold it in his iron-gloved hand and feel it hum and not know why his skin prickles. Be so obvious you're invisible. That's the oldest trick in my trade and it has never once failed me." Wren leaned back, satisfied. "Fear makes you hide. Audacity makes them hide—from *you*. Trust me. I've been audacious professionally for nine years."

Gideon laughed—a startled, rusty sound, the first in two days—and was surprised by it, and surprised by how much it loosened something in their chest. Fear was still there. It had been there since the tree line, a cold stone swallowed whole. But sitting in the lamplight with this quick, strange, loyal stranger, the fear shifted shape. It became fuel. It became the thing that made their hands steady instead of shake.

"All right," they said. "Audacity."

"Audacity," Wren agreed solemnly, and poured the last of the dye-shop's terrible plum brandy into two chipped cups. "To the wolf in the cellar. May she be furious when we find her. Furious means alive."

They drank. Outside, the drums went on, and the moon climbed, fat and pale and nearly done, dragging at the tide of Gideon's blood like a hand in the dark.

* * *

The next evening, the Moonfast market was a sea of lanterns and noise, and Gideon stood in the middle of it behind a folding table, selling magic.

It had taken an hour to learn the rhythm: the patter, the flattery, the little theater of wrapping a charm while telling its story. Wren had coached them—*smile with your voice, not just your face; never let them see you count the coin*—but most of it came strangely easy, as though some buried version of Gideon had always known how to stand in a crowd and be believed. Maren's voice lived in their patter without their permission. *A charm is a promise you make with your hands, love. Make it kindly.*

By full dark they had sold out the fever-knots and were down to the last luck-strings, and the crowd around the stall had grown thick and festive and slightly drunk, and the wire-wrongness in the air had grown thicker too. Twice Gideon had felt it spike—once when a drunkard stumbled into the table and flew into a sudden inexplicable rage, biting the man who tried to help him up; once when the drums stopped for a full minute and the whole market seemed to hold its breath, thousands of faces tilting upward toward the waning moon like flowers toward a dying sun.

And once—this was the moment Gideon would remember, later, in the dark under the earth—once when a Sentinel stopped at the stall.

He was big, the way furniture is big, in black-lacquered armor with the crown's sigil stamped at the shoulder. His gauntlets were dull gray metal that drank the lantern-light and gave none back, and Gideon knew without being told: crown-forged iron. The same stuff that had closed around Tamsin's wrists.

Their heart slammed. Their hands, trained by two days of terror and one night of Wren, did not.

"Charm-seller." His voice was bored gravel. "Papers."

"Street license, honored one." Gideon produced the scrap Wren had forged, seals and all, and kept their eyes respectfully lowered, and kept their breathing even, and felt the ledger's weight against their spine like a hand pressing them upright.

He examined it too long. Then he picked up a luck-string from the table—turned it over in those gray gauntlets, the braid Gideon had knotted on the roadside with words they barely knew they knew.

Gideon felt the charm wake against his iron. Felt it like a struck bell, like a shout. Waited for the gauntlet to close, for the shout to become a cry of *witch*, for the market to turn and stare—

The Sentinel frowned. Shook his head slightly, as a man does clearing a ringing ear. Set the string down.

"Festival trash," he said, and moved on into the crowd.

Gideon let out a breath that shook only a little. Around them the market roared on, oblivious. Above the rooftops the ruined palace stood black against the stars, and high over its broken towers the moon hung, one night from its last turn, pulling at everything—pulling at the crowd, pulling at the wire-wrongness in the air, pulling hardest of all at the thing sewn into the lining of a charm-seller's coat.

*Tomorrow,* Gideon thought. *Tomorrow night, the eclipse. Tomorrow night, the cells.*

They were wrapping the last luck-string for a gap-toothed child with a copper coin when Wren materialized at their elbow, moving through the crowd like a fish through reeds. Their face was carefully blank, which on Wren meant catastrophe.

"Gideon." Low, under the drumbeat. "Change of plans. We don't have till tomorrow."

Gideon's hands went still on the string. "What happened."

"Went to case the chapel vestry, like we said." Wren's eyes flicked side to side, quick, professional. "There's activity. Wagons. Iron. They're moving prisoners tonight—all of them, down into the old palace itself, before the eclipse. Word among the watch is the Crown wants its enemies *close* for the thirteenth turn. Wants them where it can—" Wren's throat worked. "Where it can feed."

The wire-wrongness in the air spiked, sudden and vast, and down the market row a dozen lanterns guttered at once, and somewhere a woman began to scream, and the screaming spread like fire in dry grass.

Gideon folded the luck-string into the child's fist and closed the small fingers over it.

"Wren," they said, and heard their own voice come out level and cold and entirely unfamiliar, "how fast can you get us into the palace?"
