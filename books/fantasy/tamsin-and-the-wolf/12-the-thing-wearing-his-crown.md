## 12. The Thing Wearing His Crown

They came for her at the hour when the dungeons forgot the sun.

Tamsin knew the hour by sound alone — three days in a cell without windows had taught her to read time the way wolves read weather, through pressure and instinct and the small betrayals of stone. The fortress above her breathed differently at night. The guards' boots slowed. The rats grew bold along the walls, their claws ticking against the flagstones like rain on a roof. And somewhere far overhead, the Moonfast bells began their slow rehearsal for the festival's final turn — thirteen nights of ringing, and this was the twelfth, and tomorrow the moon would go dark as a closed eye.

Her shoulder had stopped bleeding. That was the first mercy. The second was that they had not taken her wolf from her; whatever iron they'd forged into the manacles, it muted her shifting but did not smother it. She could feel the animal beneath her skin like a banked fire, patient, furious, waiting. *Hold*, she told it. *Hold until there is something worth burning.*

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Two Sentinels entered, their crown-forged irons humming that wrong note she had learned to hate — a frequency that lived behind the teeth, that made her fillings ache and her thoughts slide sideways. Between them stood a third figure, a clerkish man with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic posture of someone who delivered bad news for a living.

"Princess Tamsin," he said, and flinched at his own word. "Forgive me. The prisoner Tamsin. You're wanted above."

"Above" was doing a great deal of work in that sentence. She looked past him down the corridor, where torchlight failed into blackness, and said nothing. Three days of silence had become a discipline. Every question she refused to answer was a small stone she kept, and she was building something with them, though she didn't yet know what.

"It isn't an interrogation," the clerk said, wringing his hands. "It's — His Majesty requests your presence."

Something went through her then, cold and quick as a fish under ice. She had imagined many things about this dungeon: interrogations, tortures, the long dull machinery of a crown that ate its wearers grinding her down for information she didn't have. She had not imagined being *invited*.

"He knows I'm here," she said. Her voice came out rusted from disuse.

"The King knows everything that happens beneath the palace," the clerk said, and there was something in his tone — not reverence, not fear exactly. Something closer to the way villagers spoke of floods. "He has known since you crossed the city wall."

* * *

They took her up through the bones of the old palace.

She had grown up in these halls, before the fall, before exile, before her mother died with the family name still sweet on her lips, and the ruins remembered her even if no one else did. The staircases were the same ones she had raced down as a child, her slippers slapping marble, her father's laughter chasing her — except now the marble was cracked and furred with pale mold, and the laughter was nine years dead, and the only thing chasing anyone down these stairs was the smell: wet stone and old smoke and, underneath it all, something sweeter and far worse. A smell like flowers left too long in a closed room. Like honey poured over rot.

The crown-sent iron at her wrists sang its sick note louder as they climbed. She understood why when they passed the first window. Below the ruined palace, the capital blazed with Moonfast — ten thousand lanterns, rivers of candlelight, the whole city dressed in white and silver for the thirteenth turning. And rising out of that sea of light, visible even from here, even through glass filmed with grime, was the new palace on its hill. Her father's palace. The one he had built with the crown already whispering in his ear, its spires shaped like thorns, its windows dark.

Light bent toward it. She could see it plainly from this angle — the lantern-glow below leaning, very slightly, the way grass leans toward a thing that feeds on it.

"You'll want to compose yourself," the clerk murmured, not unkindly. "His Majesty is not... He has changed, since the last time you saw him."

"I saw him eight months ago," Tamsin said. "At my mother's grave. He wept. He held my hand. He told me to run, and I was fool enough to think it was love talking."

The clerk said nothing. But his silence had a shape, and the shape of it was pity, and she hated him for it the whole rest of the climb.

* * *

The throne room of the old palace had been her father's council chamber once, back when the monarchy answered to something besides its own hunger. They had cleared it since. The long table was gone, the maps, the chairs — all of it stripped away, leaving a vast empty hall where her footsteps echoed like someone else's. Torches burned in brackets along the walls, but their light seemed to lose its nerve halfway to the ceiling, and the dark up there had texture, had weight, like the inside of a throat.

He sat at the far end, on the steps of the old dais rather than the throne itself — the throne had been broken, she saw, its seat split down the middle as if by an axe or a bite — and for one impossible moment, seeing him sitting there small and slumped in the torchlight, she was nine years old again and he was waiting up for her after she'd climbed the orchard wall past bedtime.

"Papa," she said, before she could stop herself.

His head came up.

And the moment broke.

It was his face. That was the horror of it, the thing she would carry for the rest of her life however long that turned out to be: it was *his face*, every line of it, the heavy brows she'd inherited, the scar through his left eyebrow from the boar hunt when she was six, the deep-set eyes that crinkled when he laughed. Nothing had been replaced. Nothing had been remade. And yet the face was wrong the way a portrait is wrong, the way a mask of someone beloved is wrong — all the features present and accounted for, and none of them moving quite together. His smile arrived a half-second before his eyes agreed to it. When he blinked, it was both eyes and then, a heartbeat later, one more blink, just the left, like a stutter in the machinery.

"Tamsin." Her name in his voice. His voice exactly — the low gravel of it, the northern vowels — and yet pitched strangely, evenly, without any of the rises and falls that made speech human. Every word landed with the same weight. "My daughter. My girl. Come here. Let me look at you."

She did not come here. Her feet stayed planted on the cold floor while the Sentinels withdrew to the walls, and the clerk melted away into the dark, and it was just the two of them in the throat of the ruined palace, and the sweet rot-smell was stronger here, so strong she could taste it at the back of her tongue.

"You have her eyes," he said, tilting his head. The motion was smooth, fluid, *too* fluid, like water wearing a man's shape. "Elira's eyes. Do you know, I had forgotten what color they were. Isn't that strange? I had to be reminded." He smiled, and the smile was warm, and the warmth reached nowhere. "There are so many things I had to be reminded of. But I remember you. I remember everything about you, Tamsin. I remember the song you used to sing to the kitchen cats. I remember how you cried when the foal died, and wouldn't eat for two days, and your mother said *she gets that from you*. I remember—"

"Stop," Tamsin said.

"—the way you looked at me," he continued, gentle, relentless, "when I taught you to hold a blade. As if I'd hung the moon. As if I were worth—"

"*Stop.*"

He stopped. And then he laughed, and the laugh was the worst thing yet, because it was her father's laugh, the real one, the one that had filled their house in the north — and it came out of that wrong face with perfect fidelity, note for note, and meant nothing at all. It was a recording. It was a lure. It was the anglerfish's little light, bobbing in the dark water, shaped exactly like the thing you loved most.

"You're angry," he said, with the tone of a man observing weather. "That's reasonable. Anger is a form of love, did you know that? Love with the direction reversed. I've had a great deal of time to study love, sitting here. It's remarkable material. So *pliable*."

"That's not my father," Tamsin said to the room, to the torches, to whatever listened from the textured dark above. "I don't know what you are. But he is not here, and I will not sit at your table and be fed his voice."

The thing wearing her father considered this. Its head cocked — his head, his gray-streaked hair falling across his forehead the way it always had — and for a moment the performance dropped, and what looked out through those familiar eyes was old. Old beyond bearing. Old the way the space between stars is old. Not evil, precisely. Evil would have been easier. This was hunger, pure and patient and utterly without malice, the hunger of a thing that had never once needed to consider its food's feelings.

"Clever girl," it said, in his voice. "Elira was clever too. Stubborn. She fought me for nine years, you know. Right up to the end. Such stamina. Such *love*." It rose from the steps, unfolding joint by joint, and walked toward her, and its gait was almost right, almost a man's walk, with just enough glide in it to raise every hair on her body. "Do you know what she said to me, at the end? At the grave, when you thought I sent you away out of mercy?" It stopped an arm's length from her. Close enough that she could see the fine detail of the wrongness: his pupils did not contract in the torchlight. They stayed wide and fixed and dark, drinking. "She said, *whatever wears your face next, keep it away from our daughter.*"

Tamsin's breath left her in a thin stream. Beneath her skin, the wolf slammed against the iron's mute, over and over, silent screaming.

"So here is my dilemma," the thing said warmly, reasonably, her father's mouth shaping each careful word. "I could wear him for you forever. I have his memories — oh, all of them, every one, I drank them slowly over nine years like a long meal. I can give you back every birthday, every lullaby, every time he carried you on his shoulders through the snow. I can be so much *more* him than he ever was. He was tired, Tamsin. He was frightened. He made mistakes. I never make mistakes. I am the best of him, distilled. I am everything you ever loved about your father, perfected, and I will never die, and I will never leave, and I will never—" the voice softened, dipped, found the exact register he had used when she was small and fevered, "—never let anything hurt you ever again."

"No," Tamsin whispered.

"There is another option, of course." It spread its hands — his hands, the burn scar across the right palm from the forge, she had watched that scar happen, she had held the cold cloth to it — in a gesture of terrible openness. "You refuse me. You fight me. And I take him off the shelf entirely, and you spend the rest of your life wondering which parts of tonight were the mask and which were the man underneath, still in here, still watching, still—" it leaned close, and its breath smelled of honey and graves, "—*hoping*."

The blow of it landed somewhere deeper than flesh. Because that was the trap, she realized. That was the true architecture of the thing. Not the iron, not the hunters, not the prophecy. This. It kept the man alive inside itself the way a spider keeps a wrapped fly, and it offered him up in pieces, and every piece was real, and every piece was poison, because hope was the hook it fed on. Somewhere in the crown's long history, it had learned that grief could be resisted, but hope — hope opened the door from the inside.

"You want me to hope," she said. Her voice shook. She let it. There was no shame in shaking; the wolf shook before it struck. "You feed on it."

"I feed on everything," the thing said pleasantly. "But hope is *sustaining*."

"Then starve."

She spat at its feet. It looked down at the small wet mark on the stone with an expression of genuine, almost tender interest — and then her father's face lifted, and the smile that spread across it was not his at all, was nothing human, was the smile of a mouth deciding what it wanted.

"Oh, Tamsin," it said. "Your friend. The little hedge-witch with the ledger. Did you truly believe the valley could hide either of you?"

Ice, then fire. "If you touch Gideon—"

"Gideon." It tasted the name. "Such a strange child. Made, not born. Do you know what they are? Do you know what was done to make them?" It leaned closer still, and its voice dropped to the register of shared secrets, her father's conspiratorial murmur, the one he'd used to tell her ghost stories by the fire. "Because *I* know. I helped write them, in a manner of speaking. And tomorrow night, when the moon turns her thirteenth page, that child is going to walk into this room and open me like a letter. Unless, of course..."

It straightened. Clasped its hands behind its back. Became, horribly, convincingly, a king again — weary, dignified, burdened, every inch the father who had once ruled justly from the throne it now squatted beside like a wasp in a rotted apple.

"Unless you convince them not to come," it said. "One letter, Tamsin. One small lie, written in your own hand. Tell the key to stay home. Do that, and I'll show you where I keep what's left of your father. Alive. Whole. Recoverable, perhaps, in time, with care. Refuse..." It sighed, and the sigh was Aldric's sigh, worn soft with use. "Refuse, and tomorrow night everyone pays full price. The witch-child. The city. Him. You."

The torches guttered, all together, as if the room had inhaled.

Above them, muffled by miles of stone, the Moonfast bells rang out the twelfth midnight. One turn left. Tamsin stood in the throat of her childhood home, wrists in crown-iron, her father's face smiling at her from inside the thing that wore it, and she thought of Gideon — stubborn, terrified, brave Gideon, walking toward this room with a book of prophecies and no idea what waited here — and she understood, finally, completely, the shape of the choice her mother had died refusing.

"I'll need paper," she said.

The thing's smile widened, and behind it, deep in the dark of those fixed and drinking eyes, something that might have been a man screamed without any sound at all.
