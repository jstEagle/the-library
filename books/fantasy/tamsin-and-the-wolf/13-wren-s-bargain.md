## 13. Wren's Bargain

Wren had stolen a great many things in their nineteen years — purses, rings, a bishop's seal, once an entire cart of candied plums while the carter wept into his ale — but they had never before stolen a person out of a palace. They were finding the work agreed with them.

The Moonfast festival roared overhead like a river in flood. Through the grate of the drainage channel, Wren could hear it: bells and screaming braided together, drums that had stopped keeping time, somewhere a crowd chanting a word that might have been *mercy* or might have been *moon* and no longer knew the difference. The crown's hunger leaked down through the stones like damp. Wren felt it in their teeth, a low ache, an appetite with no name attached.

They pressed their back to the wet brick and counted heartbeats until the patrol passed. Four Sentinels, iron-shod, walking in that too-perfect rhythm the crown-forged armor gave them. Wren had watched Sentinels move for years from the safe side of market squares. They walked like men who had forgotten they were men. It was the forgetting that frightened Wren most — not the iron, not the cruelty, but the way a person could be worn hollow by something they loved.

Which was, when Wren thought about it honestly, rather close to home.

"Clear," they whispered, and hauled Gideon up out of the dark.

Gideon came up dripping, pale as candle tallow, clutching the prophecy ledger against their chest like a shield made of paper. Their hedge-witch charm-seller disguise had survived the climb through the undercity about as well as Wren's patience had: one sleeve torn, face smudged, eyes enormous. But their hands were steady. That surprised Wren. Most people who'd spent three days running from crown-sentinels had hands that shook.

"You said cells," Gideon whispered. "You didn't say *under a chapel*."

"You said *Tamsin*," Wren answered. "I said I'd get you to her. The geography was never part of the bargain."

It wasn't quite true. The geography had been very much part of the bargain, because the geography was the hard part, and Wren had spent two nights learning it: the chapel of Saint Vess above, deconsecrated and used now for storing festival wine; beneath it, the old royal crypts, and beneath *those*, the punishment cells the Pale Court pretended not to use. Two staircases, one guarded door, and a lock so old it predated the crown itself — which meant, thank every small god, that it predated crown-iron, and could be picked by someone with quick fingers and no scruples.

Wren had both.

"This way." They moved along the channel wall, bent double, breath shallow. The air tasted of wine-vinegar and old stone and, faintly, of ozone — the smell Wren had learned to dread, the smell the crown left on things it touched. "There's a grate at the end. Beyond it, the crypt stairs. Don't touch anything silver."

"Why not?"

"Because everything silver down here is listening."

Gideon gave them a look — the particular look of someone deciding whether the person guiding them through a haunted dungeon is mad or merely honest — and followed.

* * *

Wren owed Tamsin a life-debt, and life-debts among thieves were not the airy sentimental things ballads made of them. They were ledgers. Tamsin had pulled Wren out of the Serpent's Reach canal with a hook through their shoulder and a fever coming on, had sat three nights feeding broth into a stranger because the stranger had once lifted a purse off the wrong man and paid for it. She hadn't asked what Wren had done to deserve the knife. She'd just asked if they could swim, and when Wren said no, she'd gone in after them without taking off her boots.

You did not repay that with gratitude. Gratitude was cheap. You repaid it with service, and you kept serving until the ledger balanced, and if it never quite balanced, well — some debts were better carried than paid.

But Wren would be lying, later, if they claimed it was only the debt.

Three days ago, in the smuggler's loft behind the tannery, Wren had watched this same bedraggled apprentice spread the prophecy ledger open on a barrel head and read aloud the marginalia in a voice that got smaller with every line. *The key shall pay in memory. What is given cannot be kept. What is unbound cannot be re-bound.* And then Gideon had looked up, and Wren had seen the arithmetic happening behind their eyes — the terrible, quiet arithmetic of a person calculating exactly how much of themselves they were worth.

Wren knew that arithmetic. Wren had done it at eleven years old, standing over their mother's body in a room that smelled of the same ozone, doing sums about what a child was worth to a crown that ate its people slowly and called it love.

So yes. The debt brought them this far. The rest was something else, something Wren refused to name because naming things gave them power over you, and Wren had spent a lifetime making sure nothing had power over them except locks and locks could be picked.

"The grate," they breathed now, working the knife-blade into the hinge pins. "When I say go, you go. No heroics. If a Sentinel finds us, you run and I talk, because I can talk my way past anything and you—" they glanced at the ledger, at Gideon's white knuckles, "—you are apparently the only person alive who matters, which means you don't get to be brave."

"That's not fair."

"Nothing down here is fair. That's how you know you're in the right place."

The last pin slid free. The grate swung silent on oiled leather Wren had wedged into the hinges an hour ago, because preparation was the whole religion of staying alive. Beyond lay the crypt stairs, descending into cold, and at the bottom, faint as a heartbeat, lamplight.

They went down.

* * *

The cells beneath the chapel were older than the palace, older than the city walls, cut from rock that remembered being a mountain. Wren had bribed a turnkey — a sad, soft man named Odo who drank himself brave every evening and slept like the dead between the ninth bell and midnight — and so the corridor to the deep cells stood open, lit by a single lamp guttering in its niche.

Cell after cell, empty. The Pale Court did not keep prisoners long. Prisoners were questions, and questions were dangerous, and the crown preferred its answers already given.

Then, at the corridor's end: a sound. Low, rhythmic, deliberate. Someone counting.

Wren pressed a finger to their lips even though Gideon needed no telling. They crept forward, and there — through the barred window set into the cell door — was Tamsin.

She sat cross-legged on the stone floor, wrists bound in front of her with cord that shimmered faintly wrong, crown-touched cord, the kind that bit. Her lip was split. There was a bruise climbing her jaw like ivy. And she was counting — under her breath, steady as a tide — *one, two, three, four* — and Wren understood suddenly that she was counting moons. Counting the turns she'd promised herself she'd survive. Keeping time the way a wolf keeps time, by the sky, by the blood, by the thing inside her that refused to lie down.

"Tamsin." Gideon's voice cracked on the second syllable.

Her head came up fast, wolf-fast, and for one heartbeat her eyes were wild and yellow-rimmed — then they found Gideon's face through the bars and everything in her went still, the way a forest goes still, the way a held breath goes still.

"You're supposed to be in the valley," she said.

"I'm supposed to be a lot of places." Gideon dropped to their knees at the door, fingers threading through the bars toward hers. "I'm supposed to be sorting Maren's books. I'm supposed to be anyone else. Here I am anyway."

Tamsin laughed — a broken, wonderful sound — and pressed her bound hands to Gideon's through the gap. "You absolute fool. You beautiful, doomed fool."

"I brought a plan."

"You brought a *book*."

"It's a very good book."

Wren leaned against the wall and let them have the moment, arms crossed, watching the lamp flame bend in a draft that shouldn't exist underground. This was the part Wren was good at: standing slightly outside the light, seeing the whole shape of a thing. And the shape of this thing was simple and awful. Gideon loved her. Had loved her since a roadside and a shared cloak and a lesson in charms, probably, or maybe since the tree line, maybe since the first moment a bleeding wolf looked up at them with human fury in its eyes. And Gideon was going to pay for that love with the only coin they had.

Memory. The journey. All of it.

Wren had read the marginalia twice more after Gideon fell asleep that night in the loft. *What is given cannot be kept.* Not *what is lost*. Given. As though memory were a gift you handed over willingly, and perhaps it was, and perhaps that was the cruelest part — that the unbinding wouldn't take anything Gideon didn't choose to give.

And Gideon would choose it. Wren had known it within a day of meeting them. People who grew up invisible chose, in the end, to make themselves into gifts. Wren recognized the shape because they'd worn it themselves, once, before learning better.

Which was why, when the reunion finally wound down and Gideon turned to outline the plan — the eclipse, the throne room, the thirteenth turn — Wren pushed off the wall and said:

"Before you get to the noble part. We need to discuss payment."

Tamsin's eyes sharpened. "Payment? You're helping us because—"

"Because of the canal, yes, I know what I'm helping you because of. But this is separate." Wren crouched down to Gideon's level, and made their voice flat and businesslike, the voice of the market stall and the counted coin, because the other voice — the one that wanted to say *please don't do this, please let me carry it instead* — was a voice that had no place in negotiations. "After the unbinding. When you've paid your toll. You'll wake up remembering Tamsin's face and nothing else. Not the road. Not the inn. Not the seer, not the sentinels, not the cellar where we planned all this. Not me."

Gideon went very still. "That's the price. Yes."

"So here's my price." Wren held up one finger. "I keep it."

"Keep—"

"The story. All of it. Every day from the tree line to the throne room, written down, witnessed, kept safe. You pay your memories to the crown, fine. But memories aren't the only way a thing stays real. Stories do it too, if someone carries them properly." Wren heard their own voice go rougher than intended and hated it, and kept going. "I'm a thief. Stealing is what I do. So I'll steal your story right out from under the crown's nose, and I'll hold it, and one day — if you ever want it back — I'll read it to you. Word for word. And you'll get to meet yourself again."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, the festival screamed, and the lamp flame shivered.

"You'd do that?" Gideon asked quietly. "Carry something that heavy, for someone you've known five days?"

"I've carried heavier for people I've known less." Wren shrugged, and found that the shrug didn't come off right, that their shoulders wouldn't perform indifference. "Besides. It's not charity. It's a bargain. You get your story kept; I get—" and here Wren faltered, because what did Wren get? What was the honest answer?

Wren got to matter. Wren got to be, for once in their grubby mercenary life, the keeper of something instead of the taker. Wren got to stand in front of the arithmetic — *how much is a person worth* — and write a different sum underneath it.

"I get to see it through," they said finally. "Nobody's ever trusted me with the ending before."

Tamsin was watching Wren through the bars with an expression Wren couldn't read — something old and aching, the look of someone recognizing a familiar hunger in a stranger's face. "Wren," she said slowly. "When this is done. When the crown is ash. Whatever happens after — you know you're not getting rid of us. Right? Debts don't work that way. Once they're paid, what's left isn't debt anymore."

"What's left, then?"

"Family," Tamsin said simply. "If you want it. Chosen kind. The kind nobody prophesies."

Wren looked away first. Looked at the lock instead, because locks were easier. "Ask me again when we're not all about to die," they muttered, and took out their picks.

* * *

The lock on Tamsin's cell was old iron, pre-crown, and it surrendered to Wren in forty seconds flat — the easiest theft of the night, and somehow the sweetest. The cord binding Tamsin's wrists was another matter. Crown-touched stuff smoked faintly when Wren's knife touched it, and Tamsin hissed through her teeth, and in the end it was Gideon who solved it: a small charm, murmured half-under-breath, hedge-witch simple, and the cord slackened like a snake falling asleep and slid away.

Gideon stared at their own hands. "That worked too well again."

"Everything you do works too well," Tamsin said, flexing her freed fingers. Then, softer: "Save it. Save all of it. You'll need every drop tomorrow night."

Tomorrow night. The words settled over the three of them in the dark corridor like snowfall. The thirteenth turn. The eclipse. The ruined palace below the living one, where a king sat wearing a parasite and begging, they said, for death.

Wren shouldered their pack and checked the corridor — empty, the lamp burning low, Odo snoring somewhere beyond the turning stair. Time to move. Time to get the wolf-heir out of the crypt and the key hidden until moonrise, and then—

Above them, muffled by many floors of stone, the festival bells began to ring wrong. Not the measured Moonfast peal. A clanging, arrhythmic, panicked — the sound of a city discovering something in itself it did not want to know. The ozone smell thickened. The lamp flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

Tamsin lifted her head, nostrils flaring, and her eyes went yellow at the rims.

"It's starting," she said. "The crown's hungry tonight. It's leaking into the streets." She looked at Gideon, and Wren saw the fear in her face transmute, mid-breath, into something harder and cleaner. "We run for the ruins now. Tonight. Before it finishes eating the city and comes looking for dessert."

Wren exhaled, tightened the straps on their pack, and thought: *well. So much for the careful plan.*

"So," they said, pulling their hood up. "Anyone fancy stealing a revolution?"
