## 14. Cell Beneath the Chapel

The chapel above me had been dead for a hundred years, but its bones still remembered how to pray.

I knew that because I could hear them. Not the stones themselves—though the palace ruins groaned and settled like an old man turning in his sleep—but the people moving through them. Moonfast pilgrims, hundreds of them, come to the ruined capital to burn their small offerings under the waning moon. Their footsteps crossed the broken flagstones overhead in rhythms like a litany: *step, pause, step-step, pause.* Somewhere above, a bell with no clapper swung in the wind and made a sound like a held breath.

I sat in the dark beneath it all, in a cell that had once been a priest's, judging by the rotted cassock still hanging on its hook, and I listened to the city pray to a moon that was almost gone.

Thirteen nights. That was what Wren had told me before vanishing back into the festival crowds with my coin purse and half my dignity. Thirteen nights until the eclipse, when the last sliver of the thirteenth moon would slide into shadow and the crown's hunger would be at its weakest—and mine, if the ledger's marginalia spoke true, at its most useful.

*The key pays in memory. The key pays in self.*

I had read those words so many times the ink had begun to feel less like ink and more like scar tissue.

The cell was cold in the way that stone is always cold—not biting, just patient, as though it could wait longer than any flesh could endure waiting. A grate near the floor, rust-eaten and wide enough for a hand, opened onto the corridor that ran beneath the old chapel. Beyond it, darkness. Beyond *that*, according to Wren's scratched map on my forearm—inked there because paper could be taken from me, skin could not—the cells where they kept the crown's enemies.

Where they kept her.

I had not slept. Sleep required believing the world would still be shaped the same when I woke, and I no longer trusted the world's hands.

So when the footsteps came down the corridor—uneven, favoring the left leg, dragging slightly on the third step—I knew them before I let myself know them. Some things the body recognizes ahead of the heart, the way a dog knows thunder before the sky splits.

"Tamsin," I said, to the dark, to the grate, to whatever gods presided over ruined chapels and ruined plans. "Tamsin."

The footsteps stopped.

For a long moment there was nothing. Then a sound I will carry past every memory they take from me: a low exhale, half laugh, half wound, the sound of someone who has been holding a breath since the Sentinels took her on the road and has only now been given permission to release it.

"Gideon." Her voice came through the grate roughened, lower than I remembered, scraped raw by whatever they did in the cells of the Pale Court. "Tell me you're not really here. Tell me I've finally gone mad and this is the kindest hallucination they've given me."

"I'm here." I pressed my palm flat against the cold iron. "Wren got me into the undercroft. I've been here two days. I've been—" My voice cracked, and I hated it, and I let it crack anyway. "I've been listening to your footsteps for two days without knowing they were yours."

"You counted my steps?"

"Left leg drags on the third one."

A pause. Then, closer, her breath against the grate, warm in all that cold. "You absolute hedge-witch fool. You came *back*. Into the capital. During Moonfast. With your face on a wanted notice and your name in a prophecy book."

"My name isn't in the prophecy book." I smiled at the dark, and she couldn't see it, and somehow that made it easier to say. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

We talked the way prisoners talk—in fragments, in the spaces between the boots of passing guards, in whispers pressed so close to the grate our lips nearly touched iron. She told me about the cells: the crown-forged manacles that drank warmth from the skin, the questions asked in a voice that wasn't quite her father's, the way the thing wearing King Aldric had looked at her with his eyes and smiled with his mouth and said, *daughter*, in a tone that had never once belonged to him.

"They're going to bring me before him again," she said. "Before the eclipse. They want me to witness something. The crown likes witnesses—it likes an audience the way a fire likes wind." A bitter breath. "I think they mean to make me watch it finish eating him. Or make him watch it eat me. It hasn't decided which order it prefers."

"Then we go tomorrow night. Eclipse night. Before they decide."

Through the grate, in the thin light that leaked from some distant torch, I saw her fingers curl around the bars. Wolf-strong fingers, scarred across the knuckles from a life of fighting for a throne she didn't want.

"And how exactly," she said, very quietly, in the voice she used when she was trying not to shout, "do we do that, Gideon? You have a book of marginalia and a stolen key to the undercroft. They have an army wearing iron forged from the same curse we're trying to break."

"I have the unbinding." I said it plainly, because dressing it up would have been a lie, and I was done lying to her. "The ledger says the key can unbind the king from the crown. It says the toll is memory. All of it. Everything the key is, everything the key remembers, poured out to starve the parasite loose. That's why Maren hid me in the valley. That's why she erased—" I stopped. Started again. "That's why she raised me the way she did. I was never meant to be found. I was meant to be *spent*. Once. Completely."

The silence that followed was long enough that I heard the bell overhead swing twice, breathless, in the wind.

"No," Tamsin said.

"It's not a request, Tamsin. It's arithmetic. The unbinding needs—"

"*No.*" The word came through the grate like a blow, and somewhere down the corridor a rat went silent. Her breathing had changed—I could hear the wolf in it now, close beneath the skin, the way it always was when she was frightened and refused to call it fear. "You don't get to stand in the dark and tell me the price is *you*. Not after the road. Not after the inn, and the seer, and the Sentinels, and every mile where the only reason I kept walking was—" She stopped herself. I heard her jaw set. I heard her choose her next words like weapons laid carefully on a table. "I have watched one parent be devoured already. I am not standing in a ruined chapel trading one for the other."

"The alternative is your father dies wearing it. The alternative is the crown finds another head."

"Then we find another way."

"There *is* no other way. The marginalia is clear. The key pays in memory or the key doesn't pay at all, and if the key doesn't pay, then in three generations there's another child born outside magic, raised in a garden, taught charms that don't work, and handed to a crown like a coin to a—" My voice broke again. I pressed my forehead against the grate, against the cold, and said the smaller truth underneath the large one. "Tamsin. I grew up invisible on purpose. I spent nineteen years being careful in a valley that forgets. If this is what I'm for—if all of it, the hiding, the hedges, the whole gray careful life—if it buys your father free and breaks that thing, then it's the first thing I'll ever have done that mattered. Let it matter."

Her hand found mine through the grate.

That was the moment I nearly lost myself—not to dread, not to the prophecy, but to the simple unbearable warmth of her fingers laced through mine, wolf-warm, trembling in a way her voice never allowed itself to tremble.

"Listen to me," she whispered. "You listen to me, Gideon of nowhere, keeper of bees and failed charms. I have spent my whole life being told what my blood is *for*. Heir. Weapon. End of a line written down a century before I was born. And I swore, in the cells of this place, in the dark, that I would never ask anyone else to pay a price written in someone else's book." Her grip tightened. "So we split it. Whatever the toll is, we split it. You are not spending yourself alone while I stand by and watch. I'd sooner let the crown keep me."

"That's not how the magic works. The key is singular. The prophecy—"

"Then we make it work differently. Your Maren taught you charms that bend rules, didn't she? Brews that almost work? Well." And I heard it then, the tenderness hardening, cooling, setting like steel quenched in cold water: her grief becoming purpose, her fear becoming plan. "Almost is where we start. We find the seam in the spell. There's always a seam—you taught me that on the road, remember? Every binding has one, because everything made can be unmade along its own making."

I closed my eyes. Above us the pilgrims prayed; below us the prisoner planned; between us, threaded through a rusted grate, a bargain was forming that no ledger had predicted.

"If we split it," I said slowly, feeling my way through the thought like a hand along a wall, "the toll comes out of both of us. Unevenly. The key carries the channel—that doesn't change. But if you anchor it, if you hold the king steady while I open the unbinding, part of the hunger might feed on you instead. Memories. Maybe years. Maybe—" I couldn't say it. "Maybe things I can't predict."

"Then predict them anyway. You're the charm-brewer. Brew me the worst case and I'll drink it."

And so we planned. There in the dark, whispering through iron, we built it piece by piece: Wren would unlock her cell at moonset. The eclipse would begin as the last sliver of the thirteenth moon went dark, and we would have the length of that darkness—perhaps half an hour, perhaps less—to reach the throne room where Aldric sat bound. I would open the unbinding. She would hold him. We would pay together, unevenly, impossibly, along a seam we could only hope existed.

"And afterward?" I asked, when the plan was built and there was nothing left but the saying of it. "Afterward, if it works. If we're both still—afterward. What then?"

She was quiet a long time. When she answered, her thumb was moving slowly over my knuckles, back and forth, like someone memorizing a page they expected to lose.

"Then we walk out of this city," she said, "and we keep walking, and wherever we stop, we plant a garden. And if neither of us remembers why the garden matters—" her voice caught, steadied, caught again, "—we'll grow it anyway. Some things live deeper than memory. Bees know that. You told me."

"I told you that?"

"You told me that. On the road. You were covered in honey and insufferable about it."

I laughed—actually laughed, there in the cell beneath the dead chapel, and the sound went up through the stones and joined the praying of the pilgrims, and for one moment the whole ruined palace felt less like a tomb and more like a place where living things had gathered.

Then the torchlight at the end of the corridor shifted. Boots. Two guards, maybe three, coming down the passage on their rounds, and Tamsin's hand slipped from mine like water closing over a dropped stone.

"Eclipse is tomorrow," she breathed through the grate, already retreating into the dark of her own cell. "Moonset. Wren. The seam. Don't you dare spend yourself without me, Gideon. Swear it."

"I swear," I whispered. "On the garden."

The boots passed. The dark settled back like snowfall. And far above, the bell with no clapper swung in the wind, holding its breath—

while beneath the chapel, in the oldest cell of the Pale Court, something that wore a king began, softly, to sing.
