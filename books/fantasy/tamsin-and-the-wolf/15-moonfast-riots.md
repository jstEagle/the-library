## 15. Moonfast Riots

The city had been holding its breath for three days, and on the night of the thirteenth moon it finally exhaled wrong.

Tamsin felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes like the moment before a storm breaks over high country, that flat metallic taste in the air that her mother had called *wolf-weather*. She stood at a shuttered window of Wren's cousin's loft above the tannery row, watching the Moonfast crowds gather in the great square below, and every instinct she owned was telling her to run.

"They're lighting the lanterns," Gideon said beside her. Their voice was quiet, but their hands were not; they kept turning the prophecy ledger over and over, as if the answers might migrate to a different page if they held it long enough. "That's early. The rite isn't until the eclipse."

"Everything's early tonight." Tamsin pressed her palm flat against the cool wood of the windowsill. Beneath the smell of tanning lime and smoke, beneath the honey-and-clove sweetness of festival bread drifting up from the square, there was something else. Something old and wet and hungry, like the breath of a cave. "Can't you feel it?"

Gideon went still. That was the thing about them that still unsettled her, after all these weeks on the road — how completely they could stop, body and thought together, like a held note. When they spoke again, their voice had changed. Flatter. Certain.

"It's leaking."

* * *

She had explained it to them once, on the road past Harrow Fen, sitting hip to hip on a cold milestone while patrols passed on the far side of the reeds. *The crown doesn't just wear him,* she'd said. *It feeds. And what it can't take from him anymore, it takes from everything around him. The court stopped feeling anything years ago. That's not grief, Gideon. That's an appetite.*

Now the appetite had reached the city.

Below them, the first lantern rose — a paper globe the size of a barrel, gilded with wishes written in ink that would burn away by morning. Tradition said you wrote what you wanted to release: a dead father's name, a broken vow, a fear. Ten thousand lanterns would rise at the eclipse and the sky would fill with small burning sorrows, and for one night the city was supposed to feel lighter.

But the crowd wasn't writing sorrows tonight. Tamsin could see them from above — little knots of people huddled around scribes who charged a copper a letter, and even at this distance she could read the change in their posture. They weren't bowed over their wishes. They were leaning toward each other. Shoulders tight. Heads close. The particular geometry of people about to do something they'll be ashamed of by morning.

"Gideon." She caught their wrist. "Look at the lanterns."

The first one had risen free of the crowd and was climbing through the dark — and then it turned. Not drifted. *Turned*, deliberate as a hawk, and began to descend back into the square, trailing sparks, and where its light fell the crowd flinched away from it and then, horribly, leaned into it, faces upturned, mouths open, drinking the glow like water.

"That's not wind," Gideon whispered.

"No." Her mouth had gone dry. "It's hungry."

* * *

They were down the stairs and into the street before the second lantern fell.

Wren met them at the door of the tannery yard, already moving, already talking, that quick ferret energy of theirs sharpened to a point. "You felt it too, then. Whole street's gone strange. Old Marta tried to give away her entire stock of bread and then screamed at a child for taking it. There are men fighting over wish-paper. Wish-*paper*, Tamsin. It's worth nothing." Wren's eyes flicked between them, and for once the pickpocket's grin was nowhere in evidence. "And the Sentinels have pulled back to the palace. All of them. Like something called them home."

"The crown is calling everything home," Tamsin said. "It's going to feed."

"Then we go now." Gideon shouldered their charm-seller's pack, the one full of charms that worked far too well, and looked at the two of them with a steadiness that Tamsin had learned to trust more than almost anything in her life. "While everyone's looking at the sky."

They made it four streets.

* * *

The riot began, as far as Tamsin could ever piece together afterward, somewhere near the fountain of the Seven Sisters — a woman screaming that someone had stolen her wish, or her husband, or her name; accounts differed, and most witnesses disagreed about whether there had been any woman at all. What Tamsin knew was what her body knew: the crowd around them changed texture all at once, the way a pack changes when the alpha's mood turns, and then the whole street was running and shoving and no one was running *toward* anything.

A man went down beside her under six pairs of feet and she hauled him up by his collar without stopping, shoved him into a doorway, kept moving. Behind her, glass broke. Ahead, a wine merchant's cart had overturned and people were on their knees in the spillage, lapping at it, actually *lapping*, and Tamsin's stomach turned over and over.

"Tamsin!" Gideon's hand found hers out of the chaos — they always found hers, some hedge-witch sense of hers, some wolf sense of hers, she'd never asked which. Their face was pale, smudged with soot from a fallen lantern, but their eyes were doing that thing again. Going flat. Going clear. "The crown's hunger — it's not making them angry. Look. It's making them *want*. Everything they've ever wanted, all at once, and none of it reachable. That's why they're breaking things."

"So we get off the streets before we want something stupid."

"I already want something stupid." But Gideon didn't say what, and pulled her left instead, toward the river road, and Tamsin filed the words away in the place where she kept things she couldn't afford to look at yet.

* * *

They ran.

The city came apart around them in slow, terrible beauty. Lanterns rose and turned and descended like burning birds coming home to roost, and wherever their gold light touched, the frenzy bloomed — a knot of dancers whirling in a burning market stall, a choir singing at the top of a bell tower with voices cracking, lovers weeping in doorways, a whole family digging in the ruins of their own cellar with bare hands, laughing, laughing, looking for something that had never been buried there. The crown's hunger poured through the streets like floodwater finding every low place, filling every want to bursting and then cracking it open.

And through it all, Tamsin ran with a clarity she hadn't felt since her mother died. Because this — this was what her father had been living inside for eleven years. This was the weather of him. Every feast day at court where the servants' eyes went dull, every council session where grown men wept without knowing why, every year her mother had spent standing between Aldric and the world like a dam against exactly this. Her mother had known. Her mother had died knowing, and the crown had eaten her grief for dessert.

"This is what we're unbinding," she said to Gideon as they cut down the tanners' stair toward the river, the old palace ruins rising black against the eclipsing moon ahead of them. Not a question. A vow.

Gideon squeezed her hand and said nothing, and somehow that was better than any answer.

* * *

The river road was emptier — the frenzy had a gravity, pulling everyone toward the square and the falling lanterns and the palace beyond, and for a few blessed minutes they moved fast through the dark with only the screams behind them. Then the bridge.

Four Sentinels stood at the near end of it, crown-forged iron dull even in lantern-light, and Tamsin's heart dropped through her ribs — except they weren't watching the fugitives. They were facing the water, motionless, and as Tamsin slowed she saw why. The river itself was glowing faintly, a sick gold thread running along the current, flowing *toward* the palace. Toward the crown. Everything the city wanted tonight was being drawn downstream, and the Sentinels stood guard over the current like priests at an altar.

"We can't cross here," Gideon breathed.

"We don't need the bridge." Tamsin pointed upstream, to where the old mill leat crossed the river on a footbridge so narrow and rotten that no Sentinel had bothered with it in a century. "Wren showed me. Come on."

They doubled back through a cooper's yard, over a wall slick with rain, Gideon's grip sure on her hand the whole way — and Tamsin realized, with a jolt, that the fear had gone out of her. Somewhere between the falling lanterns and the river of wanting, panic had burned down to coals, and the coals were steady, and she was walking toward her father's prison with a clear head and a key at her side, and there was nothing in the world the crown could offer her that was worth more than this.

* * *

The mill leat bridge held. Barely. Halfway across, a plank cracked under Tamsin's boot and she went down to one knee, and below her the golden current slid past, and for one moment — one long, sweet, poisoned moment — she wanted.

She wanted her mother's voice. She wanted the nursery at the summer palace, and the smell of cedar, and her father lifting her onto his shoulders before any of it, before the crown, before the council, before the word *heir* became a weight instead of a promise. She wanted it so badly her teeth ached, and the river seemed to pause beneath her, attentive, offering.

*Tamsin.* Gideon's hand, closing hard around hers. Not pulling. Just there. Warm and callused and real, smelling of beeswax and woodsmoke and Maren's garden, of everything magic had never managed to touch.

The wanting broke like fever. She got up. They crossed.

On the far bank, shaking, she laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh. "It offered me my childhood."

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. You said my name." She looked at Gideon then, really looked, at this person the prophecy had written into a century-old book, this key born outside magic, this runaway apprentice who had crossed a boundary that kills and come out the other side still gentle. "That's how we beat it, isn't she? It offers us what we've lost. And we hold on to what we have."

Gideon's expression did something complicated and quick, there in the dark — a flicker of pain, gone before she could name it. "Something like that," they said. "Come on. The eclipse is starting."

* * *

Above the ruined palace, the moon was dying.

It hung fat and amber over the broken towers, and the earth's shadow was eating it bite by bite, and the whole city behind them had gone silent in a way that was worse than the screaming — ten thousand people standing in the dark, looking up, waiting to be filled. The last unshadowed sliver of moon gleamed like a coin at the bottom of a well.

The old palace crouched at the river's bend, roofless, windowless, its throne room open to the sky — the kings of the old line had built it that way on purpose, Tamsin's mother had told her once, so that the crown could see the moon it answered to. So that someone would always be able to look up and remember what the crown was for. Her ancestors had trusted the sky to keep watch. The sky had watched eleven years of her father's devouring and done nothing.

Tonight it would watch something else.

At the shattered gates, Tamsin stopped and drew a breath that tasted of stone and river and ending. Below them, down the long broken stair, gold light pulsed in the dark like a heartbeat — the crown, awake, waiting, its hunger finally given a feast worthy of it. And somewhere down there, wearing her father's face, the thing that had eaten her childhood sat on a throne of rubble and listened.

"He knows we're coming," she said quietly.

"Yes," Gideon said.

"He's going to offer me everything I've lost down there. He's going to offer you—" She stopped. Gideon's face in the dying moonlight was unreadable, and had been unreadable, she realized suddenly, since the river. Since before the river. Since the inn, maybe. Since the seer recoiled from their palm. "Gideon. What is it going to offer you?"

For a moment, silence, and the eclipse ate another sliver of moon, and the gold pulse below beat faster, like a heart scenting blood.

"The same thing it offered me at the tree line," Gideon said at last, and their voice was steady, and their hand, when it found hers, was not. "Everything I forgot. Come on, Tamsin. We're losing the moon."

They went down the broken stair together, into the dark, toward the heartbeat of gold — and behind them, far across the city, the last sliver of moon slipped beneath the shadow, and the thirteenth turn began.
