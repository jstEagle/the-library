## 16. The Ruined Palace

The riots sounded different from below.

Above us, Moonfast had torn itself open—bells clanging out of rhythm, the shriek of glass, the wet animal roar of a city forgetting its own name. But down the throat of the old palace, the noise arrived muffled and distant, like weather happening in another country. What replaced it was silence of a stranger kind: stone breathing, if stone could breathe. Cold air moving through corridors that had not held a living guest in twenty years, carrying the smell of wet ash and something sweeter underneath, something like honey left too long in the sun until it turned to rot.

Tamsin went first. Of course she did. She had shifted at the mouth of the ruins—a choice, I understood now, not an instinct—and moved through the dark on four silent paws, her grey coat drinking what little light leaked down from the burning city above. I followed with Maren's ledger pressed to my chest inside my coat, its pages warm against my ribs as though the book had a heartbeat of its own and was frightened too.

We descended by staircases that had forgotten they were staircases. Whole flights had collapsed into rubble we climbed like scree; others hung suspended, defying their own brokenness, and Tamsin would not set paw on those. We went around, through halls where faded banners still hung from walls gone soft with damp—the wolf-and-crown of Aldric's house, stitched by hands two generations dead. In one chamber, a child's wooden horse lay on its side in a drift of plaster dust, and I found myself kneeling before I knew I'd moved, brushing the dust from its painted eyes.

*Don't,* I told myself. *You'll want every memory you find tonight. That's how it gets you.*

But that was getting ahead of things. Then, there was only the descent, and the dark, and the growing certainty—rising in me like water in a sealed cellar—that whatever waited at the bottom already knew we were coming.

* * *

The throne room announced itself by temperature.

The air turned warm. Not comfortable-warm—the warmth of a body, of breath, of a great sleeping animal's exhalation. After hours of cold stone it landed on my skin like a hand. Tamsin stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her haunches, and every hair along her spine lifted, a dark ridge of hackles silhouetted against a light that should not have existed.

Because there was light. Faint, gold, pulsing—coming from ahead, around a final corner, in slow rhythm. Bright. Dark. Bright. Dark.

Like breathing. Like a heartbeat slowed almost to stopping.

*Tamsin.* I said her name without sound, only shaping it, and she looked back at me with her amber eyes, and in them I saw something I had never seen in her face before, not even when the Sentinels took her, not even bleeding at the tree line.

She was afraid for someone else.

She shifted back—bones sliding, fur receding, the small violence of it done quietly, without the cry she usually made—and stood before me as a woman again, naked and shaking, and I pulled off my coat and put it around her shoulders because my hands needed something to do or they would have been useless forever.

"He's in there," she whispered. "My father."

"I know."

"You don't. You can't." Her voice cracked and she hated it, I could see her hating it. "I saw him in the cells. I told you what spoke through him. But this is the throne room, Gideon. This is where it's strongest. Whatever's left of him—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Whatever's left of him is smaller here."

"Then we go to the smallest part," I said. "That's the part that can still be saved."

She looked at me for a long moment. In the pulsing gold light her face was half-lit, half-erased, and I thought: *this is what the crown does. It shows you people half-finished and calls it wholeness.* Then she nodded once, took my hand—her grip cold, then warming, then gripping hard enough to hurt, which was its own kind of promise—and we stepped around the corner together.

* * *

The throne room of the old palace had been beautiful once. I could tell the way you can tell a face was beautiful under ruin: the proportions were still there, the tall windows now boarded with rotted planks, the vaulted ceiling furred with soot, the floor a mosaic of wolves running through silver grass, most of the tiles pried up or shattered. Rubble lay heaped against the dais like snowdrifts. Weeds had forced their way through the mosaic and grown pale and spindly in the dark, reaching toward the light they could never reach.

And on the dais, beneath the one window whose boards had fallen away, so that a single bar of moonlight fell across him like a blade—

King Aldric sat on his throne.

He was not what I expected, which was a monster. He was worse. He was a man.

Gaunt, yes—hollowed at the cheeks, the tendons of his neck standing out like bridge cables—but recognizably, unbearably human. He wore the rags of royal clothes, gold thread gone grey. His hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, fingers curled slightly, the way a man's hands rest when he has given up holding anything. And rising from his brow, rooted there—I could see the roots, dark veins threading back into his skull, into his temples, down behind his ears like the branches of an inverted tree—

The crown.

It did not look like metal anymore. It looked like antler, like coral, like something grown rather than forged, and it pulsed with the same slow gold light that had guided us down, bright and dark, bright and dark, keeping time with nothing so simple as a heart.

"Aldric," Tamsin breathed.

His head came up. And for one moment—one suspended, shining moment—it was only a father looking at his daughter. His eyes found her across all that ruined distance and filled with tears, and his mouth shaped her name without sound, *Tamsin, Tamsin*, and his open hands turned over as if to reach for her, as if she were six years old and running to him across a summer lawn.

Then the light behind his eyes changed.

It didn't dim. It *rearranged*. The grief in his face stayed exactly where it was, every line preserved, but it stopped meaning anything—the way a mask means nothing, however perfectly it copies a face. His mouth kept smiling. His eyes kept weeping. And the voice that came out of him was his voice, warm and deep and gentle, and it belonged to none of it.

"The wolf-child," it said fondly. "And the key. Come all this way. Come down into the dark, both of you, like good little moths."

"Don't talk to her," I said.

My voice surprised me. It came out level, and low, and certain, and I felt Tamsin glance at me sideways, because that was not the voice she knew either. Something about the room had reached into me and found a register I hadn't used before.

The crown laughed through Aldric's mouth. It was a horrible sound precisely because it was a kind one.

"Oh, but I've been waiting to talk to *you*, Gideon Crane." My name in that warm dead voice, syllable by syllable, like coins counted out. "Do you know how long I have waited? A hundred years your ledger sleeps. A hundred years the key grows in its garden, among the bees, among the little charms that never work—" Aldric's head tilted, and the crown-light swelled. "Did you truly believe that was accident? Did you think the valley chose *you*?"

"I know why I was born," I said. "I read the marginalia. I know what the key pays."

"Mmm. Words on a page." The ruined king spread his hands, palms still up, an usher's gesture, a host's gesture. "Words are so thin, aren't they? Let me show you something heavier."

* * *

It did not enter my mind. I want to be clear about that, because for the rest of my life—for whatever life I have—I will need to be clear about that. It opened a door, and memory walked through on its own.

The valley. Not as I knew it, but as I had known it once, long ago, before Maren, before the garden, before the bees.

A woman's hands braiding my hair. Smoke-smell and juniper. A song in a language I did not know I knew until I heard it again inside my own skull, and my whole body shook with recognition. A door. A door with iron hinges and a charm nailed above the lintel, and a woman's voice saying, *stay inside the boundary, little key, stay where it cannot follow, and I will come back for you when the moon turns*—

Maren. Younger than I had ever seen her, laughing, lifting me—

Gone.

The vision snapped shut like a trap, and I was on my knees on the broken mosaic and did not remember falling. Tamsin was beside me, snarling at the throne, and the crown was watching me with Aldric's wet and smiling eyes, and it let the silence stretch just long enough for the ache to bloom fully before it spoke again.

"There it is," it said softly. "There's the toll. Every memory you feed me, gone forever—the valley, the witch, the bees, the whole small safe world that made you. That is the price of unbinding, little key. That is what the last one paid, and look how well it served her." A pause, delicate as a knife. "But I am generous. I am older than generosity. So hear my offer."

Aldric rose from the throne.

It was terrible to watch—not the movement itself, which was smooth, but the way the body resisted it even as it obeyed, the way his knees trembled under a weight no one else could see. He descended three steps of the dais and stopped, and the moonlight through the broken window cut across his chest, and the crown blazed.

"Walk away," the crown said, through the mouth of the man it was eating. "Turn around. Climb back up into your rioting city and take your wolf and go east, go anywhere, and I will give them back. All of them. The valley entire. Her laugh. The song. The door with the iron hinges. Every year she stole from you, returned with interest—you will remember your childhood better than you remember yesterday. You will lose nothing. No one will die tonight. The king keeps his crown, the prophecy keeps its date, and you keep—" the voice gentled further, became almost tender, became unbearable— "*you keep yourself.*"

The throne room was very quiet. Somewhere far above, the riot had gone strangely still, as if the whole city were holding its breath along with me.

"And if I don't walk away?" I asked.

"Then you pay," the crown said simply. "All of it. Everything she gave you. Everything you are. And the worst part, little key—the part I will enjoy—is that you won't know what you lost. You'll stand in the ashes of your own making and feel a shape where your life used to be, and you will never once be able to mourn it properly, because mourning requires remembering." Aldric's ruined smile widened. "So. Choose. Your memories, or my freedom. It is such a small thing, really. Such a small thing to carry a kingdom on."

And here is what I will never tell anyone, what I barely admit to myself: for a moment—a real moment, seconds long, golden and warm as the light on the dais—I wanted to say yes.

Not because I was weak. Because I was tired. Because I had crossed a country on the strength of a book that bore my face, and slept in ditches, and watched Tamsin taken in chains, and carried guilt like a stone in my belly, and all of it, all of it, might simply end if I turned around and walked back up into the ordinary world with my bees and my garden and my mother's laughter restored to me like a gift I'd never known I'd lost. The crown did not tempt me with power. It tempted me with *home*. With Maren's kitchen. With safety. With the ache of being invisible on purpose, finally explained and finally ended.

I felt Tamsin's hand close over mine.

She didn't speak. She didn't argue. She just held on, her palm rough and warm, and I understood that she knew—knew exactly what was being offered, knew how much it cost to refuse—and that she would stand there holding my hand until the mountains wore down if that was what I needed.

I looked past the crown, past the parasite wearing love like a mask, to the man behind it. To the smallest part.

Aldric's eyes—his true eyes, buried deep beneath the gold—were fixed on his daughter's hand in mine. And his lips moved. No sound came out. The crown was not spending its voice on him. But I read the shapes of the words, and they were not *walk away*, and they were not *choose*, and they were not any word the parasite would ever say.

They were: *"Kill me."*

His hands, still open on the air between us, curled slowly into fists. His jaw clenched. The crown-light flared, startled, furious, and for one instant the whole golden glow guttered like a candle in wind as the man inside it spent the last of his strength fighting from within.

"No," Tamsin whispered. She had seen it too. "No, Papa—"

*"Kill me,"* his mouth said again. *"Please. Before it finishes."*

The crown seized him. I watched it happen—watched the light flood back into his eyes and his spine straighten and his fists relax finger by finger, compelled, until he stood slack and smiling again, a puppet rehung on its strings. But it was too late. We had seen. The smallest part had spoken, and it had begged not for freedom.

It had begged for death.

"Well," said the crown lightly, through its stolen mouth, as if nothing had happened at all. "Have you decided, little key? The moon is nearly at its turning. The eclipse begins within the hour. I would hate for you to run out of time to make the clever choice."

I got to my feet.

My legs were shaking. My eyes were wet. Somewhere inside me the vision of Maren's young laughing face was already fading, and I knew—with a clarity that felt like falling—that whether I walked away or stayed, I had already begun to pay.

"Not yet," I said. "But soon. And when I do, crown, I'm going to offer you something you haven't thought of."

Aldric's head tilted. The gold light pulsed, curious. Behind it, deep down, a father's eyes watched his daughter and the hedge-witch's apprentice stand together in the ruins of his kingdom, and waited, and hoped.

"There is always a third path," I said. "Maren taught me that. Charms never quite work the way they're written. Neither do prophecies. Neither do *you*."

The crown smiled its warm dead smile.

And above us, through the one broken window, the moon began, at last, to darken.
