## 17. What Maren Erased

The crown did not speak so much as *pour*.

Gideon had braced for a voice, something with teeth in it, the way the ledger's marginalia had described the parasite: *a hunger that learned to sing*. Instead there was a warmth spreading through their skull like honey off a hot spoon, and inside the warmth, memories. Not theirs. Then—worse—*theirs*.

They were kneeling on the broken flagstones of the ruined throne room, palms flat against cold stone, and Aldric was a slumped shape ten feet away, half of him still his own, and Tamsin was shouting their name from somewhere behind, and none of it mattered, because Gideon was four years old and standing in a garden that was not Maren's.

The garden was older. Wilder. Bees moved through it in slow gold rivers, and a woman knelt at its heart with her hands buried to the wrist in soil that glowed faintly, like embers under ash. The woman turned. Her face was—

Gideon knew that face. They had grown up watching it across the breakfast table, across the herb-drying racks, across twenty years of small silences. But younger. Unlined. And weeping.

*Mother.*

No—not mother. The memory corrected itself even as Gideon reached for it, the way a dream corrects itself when you look too hard at a door. A mother-figure. An unbinding-witch named Sorrel, who had held them as an infant over a stone basin while the last king's crown burned away into smoke, and who had paid for that freedom with—

With what? The memory flinched sideways. Gideon chased it.

*Paid with her own name,* the crown murmured, generous now, a poisoner offering wine. *She unbound King Edvard and forgot she had ever loved anyone at all. She stood in her own garden among her own bees and felt nothing but weather. So she made a bargain with the ones who came after. She would raise a child for the next unbinding. A child born outside magic, a key cut to fit the lock. And when the thirteenth moon came, the key would pay the whole toll—all of it, every year, every love—and the witch would go free.*

The throne room tilted. Gideon's hands were still flat on the stone; they could feel the cracks in it, the old scorch-marks where Edvard's crown had died, and beneath all of it a pulse, slow and vast, the crown's heartbeat moving through the palace like blood through a body.

*Tamsin.* Their voice came out cracked. "Tamsin, don't let me—"

"I'm here." Her hand closed over their shoulder, warm and strong and shaking. Wolf-warm. "I'm here, Gid. Whatever it's showing you—it lies."

"It doesn't lie." Gideon heard themselves say it and hated how calm it sounded. "That's the worst part. It doesn't have to."

Because the fragments kept coming, and they fit. They fit like a key fits a lock, which was, Gideon thought with a wild, floating hilarity, rather the point.

A kitchen in a house that wasn't the valley house. A woman with Sorrel's face packing a single bag with hands that trembled. A child—*them*, small, dark-haired, clutching a carved wooden bee—asking where they were going. The woman crouching down, pressing her forehead to theirs, and saying words Gideon had carried for twenty years without knowing they were a memory:

*"You won't remember this. That's the kindest thing I can do for you. One day you'll hate me for it, and I hope you live long enough to."*

Then the tree line. Then the valley swallowing them like water swallows a dropped stone. Then Maren—younger Maren, Maren before the grey came into her braid—standing at the boundary with her arms crossed and her jaw set, looking at the child delivered to her doorstep like a debt collector looks at a ledger.

And Maren's voice, from a night years later, low and fierce, overheard through a wall: *"I didn't agree to spend a person, Sorrel. I agreed to hide one. If the moon ever comes due, I'll stand between that child and the whole sky before I hand them over."*

Gideon came back to themselves gasping, as if surfacing from deep water. The throne room reassembled around them: the shattered dais, the ribs of the roof open to the eclipsing moon, Aldric breathing in wet rattles at the edge of hearing, the crown on the floor between them all, no longer worn but not dead either, its iron filigree crawling slowly, patiently, like a thing that had learned time was on its side.

It had shown them everything. That was the temptation, Gideon realized. Not gold, not power. It had opened its fist and poured out the one thing they had ached for their entire hidden, careful, invisible life: *where they came from.*

"Take it back," Gideon whispered.

"Gideon—" Tamsin started.

"No. Listen to me." They turned to face her, and saw their own reflection in her eyes: pale, tear-streaked, lit silver by the dying moon. "It offered me my memories if I walk away. It showed me them first so I'd know what I'd be refusing. That's the trap. That's always been the trap." They laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and thin. "Maren erased my childhood to keep me from being spent. And the crown just handed it back to me like a gift, because it knows I'll pay anything to keep it."

The eclipse was nearly total. The light in the throne room had gone the colour of old pewter, and in that light the crown looked almost beautiful, which Gideon supposed was how it had gotten kings to put it on for three hundred years.

"The toll is memory," they said. "That's the mechanism. Sorrel paid with hers. I was made to pay with mine. All of it—the valley, Maren, the bees, the books, you—" their voice caught on *you* and they pushed through it, "—everything, fed into the unbinding until the lock comes apart. That's the price written in the margin. One soul's whole remembering."

"Then we find another way," Tamsin said flatly. "We break it. We burn it. Wren said—"

"Wren isn't here, and the marginalia is clear, and I've read it eleven times." Gideon got to their feet. Their legs held. That surprised them. "But listen. Listen. The toll is *memory*. Not a soul. Not a life. Memory. And a toll," they said, and here something steadied in them, something that felt less like courage than like a carpenter picking up a familiar tool, "is a price. And a price can be *split*."

Tamsin went very still. Wolves went still like that, Gideon was learning—stillness as prelude.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"*No.*" Louder now, echoing off the broken vaulting. Aldric stirred at the sound of her voice, some drowned remnant of him turning toward his daughter like a plant toward light. "You are not going to carve pieces off yourself to feed that thing. You've already lost your childhood once. You don't get to lose it twice. You don't get to lose *any* of it. I just found you, Gideon. I'm not—I will not stand here and watch you pay for my father's crown with your *life*."

"My life isn't the price. My remembering is." Gideon stepped closer, close enough to see the eclipse reflected twice in her eyes, two small dark moons. "And here's the arithmetic, Tamsin, so hear all of it before you argue. Sorrel paid alone and it hollowed her so badly she built a child to do it next. If I pay alone, same ending. But the unbinding doesn't care whose memory it eats. It cares only that the toll is paid in full. In *full*." They held up two hands, cupped, as if weighing something invisible. "Half from me. Half from someone bound to the crown by blood. Someone whose memories of it are poison anyway."

Understanding hit Tamsin like an arrow. She stepped back. "You want me to forget my father."

"I want us both to survive." Gideon's voice gentled. "You'll lose the worst years of him. The parasite wearing his face, the dungeon, the man who begged us to kill him. I'll lose—" and here the grief rose in them, huge and patient, and they let it rise instead of fighting it, because Maren's voice was in their head now, *one day you'll hate me for it, and I hope you live long enough to*, "—the valley. Maren. The garden. Twenty years of being safe. That's my half. It's more than half, honestly. I did the sums. But it leaves me *me*. It leaves me you. It leaves whatever we build after."

"And you'll wake up tomorrow and not know why you trust me," Tamsin said. Her voice had gone rough. "You won't know why you're walking beside me on some road with no idea where you're going."

"No," Gideon agreed. "But I'll know I chose to. There'll be a letter, maybe. Wren will keep the story—that was the bargain. And somewhere in me there'll be a shape where the valley used to be, and I'll know it was full of something worth paying." They smiled, and found, to their astonishment, that the smile was real. "Sorrel spent herself and called it kindness. Maren hid me and called it safety. Both of them decided for me. This is the first thing in my whole life I get to decide *as* me. I'd like it to be the one thing nobody else gets to choose."

The eclipse reached totality. The moon went black-ringed, a coin of ash, and the crown began to sing—a thin, high, hungry note that set Gideon's teeth aching and made the dust on the flagstones dance in little rings.

Tamsin looked at her father. Aldric looked back—or the ruin of him did, one eye human and drowning, one eye already gone to iron. And whatever passed across her face in that moment, Gideon thought later, was the bravest thing they ever witnessed: not fury, not refusal, but a wolf's grief finally lying down.

"He taught me to fish," she said quietly. "Before. He taught me to fish on the Vell, and he laughed when I fell in, and I want to keep that. Tell me the splitting keeps that."

"The unbinding takes what's nearest the wound," Gideon said. "The crown-years. The rot. What came before should hold—if we aim it. And I'll aim it. I'm the key. Keys know their own locks."

Tamsin closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had settled in her, the way snow settles after wind.

"All right," she said. "Split the toll. But you're writing that letter to yourself tonight, before we start, so morning-you knows who to thank." A ghost of her old sharpness came back into her mouth. "And Gideon—if you forget me entirely, I'm dragging you back to the valley myself and making Maren fix it, and she'll lecture us both for a decade."

"She would at that." Gideon's throat tightened. "Gods, she would."

The crown sang louder. The black moon hung over the broken roof like an open mouth. And Gideon Fairweather—runaway apprentice, hedge-witch's ward, nameless key, born outside magic to be spent once—took one breath, then another, and knelt at the edge of the dais with their hands open and their heart, for the first time in their life, entirely their own.

"Tamsin," they said. "Hold him steady. I'll begin."

* * *

The unbinding took memory the way fire takes kindling—fastest where it was driest, greediest where it was oldest.

Gideon fed it the valley first, deliberately, holding each piece up to the flame with a name attached, a funeral rite for the living. *The smell of beeswax in the autumn shed. Maren's hands, cracked and capable, wrapping a poultice. The taste of well-water on a hot afternoon. The particular silence of a place where magic cannot follow, and the ache of hiding inside it.*

Each memory went golden as it left, unravelling into threads of light that streamed toward the crown, and the crown screamed—not the singing note now but a true scream, iron in agony, its filigree thrashing like a netted animal. Across the dais, Aldric arched and shuddered, and Tamsin held him, arms locked around the wreck of her father, feeding the flame her own tithe: the dungeons, the parasite's voice, the years of watching a stranger wear her father's love like a mask.

Gideon felt the valley go in stages. First the edges—the road in, the tree line, the sense of the boundary like a held breath. Then the middle years, soft things, daily things, going out one by one like candles pinched by careful fingers. They wept throughout and did not stop working, and understood, distantly, that this too was a gift from Maren: twenty years of learning to work through grief without letting it spill the tincture.

Last of all, at the very centre of the flame, they placed the woman with Sorrel's face and the woman with Maren's braid, and pressed their forehead, briefly, to the cold stone, the way a child presses her forehead to her mother's.

"Thank you," Gideon said, to both of them, to neither. "I hope I live long enough."

The crown burst.

Not into flame—into *light*, white and total, and into a sound like every bell in the world rung at once and then silenced forever. The parasite tore free of Aldric in a writhing ribbon of shadow and iron-light, shrieked once at the black moon above, and came apart into nothing, into less than nothing, into a smell of hot metal and a silence so profound that Gideon heard, quite clearly, an owl outside the ruined walls, going about its ordinary night.

Then the moon slid free of the earth's shadow, and silver came flooding back into the world.

Gideon woke on the flagstones with Tamsin's face above them, streaked with ash and tears, and felt—nothing missing. Nothing missing at all. Only a great warm emptiness where the valley had been, a shape like a footprint in snow, and the absolute certainty, bone-deep and unexplainable, that the woman leaning over them was the most important person in the world.

"Why," Gideon began, and their voice was rust. "Why do I know your face?"

Tamsin laughed—a broken, joyful, disbelieving sound—and pulled them upright, and held on.

"Because," she said, "you paid for me. Come on. Let's go see if my father remembers how to be one."
