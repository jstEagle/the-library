## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon began to die at the stroke of midnight, and Tamsin felt it in her teeth.

She had shifted twice on the descent into the ruined palace—once to carry Gideon down the collapsed stair where the stones had fallen like teeth from a broken jaw, and once more in the antechamber when the crown's hunger had rolled over them like a cold tide, making her hackles rise and her human throat too small for the growl building in it. Now she stood in the old throne room with her father's hands in hers, and she was neither wolf nor woman but something between: a daughter holding steady while the world came apart.

Aldric sat slumped in the throne that had been his grandfather's, his grandfather's before him, back to the days when this hall had rung with music instead of drips and the whisper of dust. The eclipse light came through the shattered vault above in a thin silver thread, falling across his face like a blade laid flat. He was so thin. When had he become so thin? She remembered him lifting her onto his shoulders at the harvest fair, his laugh rolling out over the crowd like a bell, and the man in the throne was a sketch of that king, an etching left out in the rain.

"Tamsin." His voice was gravel under snow. "You should not have come."

"I know." She squeezed his hands. His fingers were ice. "I never do what I should."

Behind her, Gideon moved through the dark with the ledger open, reading by the light of three small flames they'd coaxed from nothing—no flint, no tinder, just will and words, because Gideon's craft had always worked too well, because Gideon had never been ordinary, because everything about them had been written a hundred years before they were born. The flames threw their shadows huge against the walls, among the faded banners of a house that would end tonight or be reborn.

"The circle's drawn," Gideon said. Their voice was steady. Too steady. Tamsin knew that steadiness; she wore it herself like armor. Underneath it, their heart was hammering—she could hear it, wolf-sharp, rabbit-fast. "Tamsin, whenever you're ready."

"I'm ready," Aldric said.

"Not you," Tamsin said softly. "Never you again. That's rather the point."

* * *

They had argued about it in the cell beneath the chapel, two nights ago, with iron bars between them and the crown's sentinels pacing overhead. Gideon had laid out the toll plainly, the way they laid out everything—like a charm to be brewed, ingredients measured, cost counted. *The unbinding needs memory. A life's worth of it. Mine.*

"And I told you no," Tamsin said now, as the eclipse deepened and the silver thread of light thinned toward nothing.

"You told me no," Gideon agreed. They knelt at the edge of the chalk circle, chalk mixed with ash and salt and, Tamsin now understood, the ground-up binding of a century-old prophecy. "And then I asked you what you'd pay instead, and you didn't have an answer."

"I'd give my memories of *you*—"

"—and then who holds your father steady? Who does he trust enough to look at while the crown tears loose?" Gideon shook their head. In the flame-light their face was calm and young and terribly certain. "It has to be both of us anyway. That's the third path. Half the toll from each. We split it, we survive it, and the crown gets exactly enough to choke on."

"Half a life's memory is still half a life."

"Yes." Gideon looked up at her then, and their eyes were wet, and they were smiling. "But it's half a life *chosen*. Nobody wrote that part. Not even the ledger."

Tamsin wanted to argue. She had been arguing with fate since the day her mother died, since the day the hunters first caught her scent, since the day a wounded wolf dragged itself to a valley's edge and found a pair of hands gentle enough to pull it through. But the moon was dying above them, and there was no time left for anything but what they had chosen.

"Hold him," she said, and her voice broke only a little. "Whatever happens. Hold him."

* * *

The ritual began with names.

Gideon spoke theirs first—*Gideon Crane, born outside magic, made inside it, key and lock and hand upon the door*—and the words went out into the ruined hall like ripples across black water. Then Tamsin spoke her own, and her bloodline's, all the way back: *Tamsin, daughter of Aldric, daughter of Elowen, heir of a house crowned and uncrowned and crowned again, last leaf on a broken branch.* Her father wept at Elowen's name. The crown drank the tears before they reached his chin. She saw it happen—the silver filigree flexing like the gullet of some pale bird—and hatred rose in her so hot and clean that for one moment she was entirely wolf, entirely fang, entirely certain.

Then Gideon began the unbinding, and there was no room left for anything but witness.

"It asks for memory," Gideon said, and their voice had changed—deeper, doubled, as though something older than either of them spoke through their mouth. "So we feed it memory. We feed it the valley."

"No," Aldric gasped. "Child, whatever it's asking—"

"Hush, Papa," Tamsin said, and pressed his hands between hers, and held on.

The eclipse reached its fullness. The moon went dark as a closed eye, and in that darkness the throne room filled with a light that came from nowhere—from everywhere—from the place beneath the world where magic pooled like groundwater. And Gideon opened their hands and began to remember aloud.

"I am standing in Maren's garden. It is morning. The bees are waking, and the light comes through the apple trees in coins..."

Tamsin heard it happen. She couldn't see it—memory has no color, no shape—but she felt it leave Gideon the way heat leaves a stone at dusk. Each sentence took something. Each image, offered up freely, dimmed somewhere behind their eyes. The garden. The bees. The smell of Maren's charms brewing wrong, always slightly wrong, chamomile and something burnt. The tree line where magic died. The first morning, the second, the thousandth, all the safe gray mornings of a childhood spent hiding in plain sight—

"Gideon," Tamsin whispered. "It's taking too much."

"It's taking what I'm giving." Gideon's voice frayed at the edges. "That's different. That's everything."

The crown began to scream.

There was no other word for it. No mouth, no lungs, and yet the sound tore out of the silver circlet like metal being unmade, like a nail pulled from living wood, high and grinding and endless. The banners on the walls stirred without wind. Dust fell from the vault in silver threads. Aldric arched in the throne, every muscle locking, and the parasite that had worn him for thirteen years felt, for the first time, the fingers prying at its roots.

*Tamsin—* her father's voice, inside her skull or inside her heart, she couldn't tell—*it hurts, it hurts, let me keep it, let me keep it, I'll be good—*

"That's not you talking," she said, and her voice came out low and rough and did not shake. "That's the thing wearing you. Papa. Look at me. Look at *me*."

His eyes found hers. For one instant—one blessed, unbearable instant—the fog cleared, and behind it was her father, whole and terrified and *his*, and he said, in a voice as small as a boy's: *"Don't let it take me with it."*

"Never," Tamsin said. "Hold on. We're paying, not you. Do you hear me? We're paying. You're only letting go."

And Gideon kept remembering.

* * *

They fed it the valley piece by piece, and Tamsin held her father's hands and watched the toll being paid and thought: *this is what love costs, and this is what love is worth, and I would do it again, I would do it twice.*

She listened to a life she had never known described in a voice growing quieter and quieter. The hedge-witch Maren, fierce and flour-dusted, teaching charms that never quite worked because magic couldn't cross the tree line—but teaching them anyway, teaching patience, teaching care. The taste of honey straight from the comb. The particular silence of a valley that forgot everyone who entered it, and how that silence could feel like safety and like a cage in the same breath. A book found in a box. A date written in ink. A face sketched on old paper that turned out to be your own.

"That's enough," Tamsin said, when Gideon swayed. "Gideon. That's enough—it's loosening—I can feel it—"

"One more," Gideon whispered. Their eyes had gone distant, unfocused, a person looking inward at shelves already emptying. "One more. The most important one. It wants the most important one."

"Don't—" Tamsin started, because she knew, she *knew* what the most important memory in Gideon's life was, and it wasn't the valley at all.

But Gideon was already speaking.

"A wolf at the tree line," they said. "Wounded. Bleeding. It looks at me like I'm the enemy and like I'm the answer, both at once. I drag it over the boundary and it becomes a girl with furious eyes, and I think—"

Their voice stopped. Not faltered—*stopped*, like a clock with the pin drawn.

"I think..." Gideon frowned. The flames around them guttered. "I think... there was someone. There was someone on the road. There was a road. Why can't I—"

"*Enough!*" Tamsin roared, and the word came out half-wolf, shaking dust from the rafters, and she wrenched one hand free of her father's grip and seized Gideon's wrist. "The unbinding doesn't get to choose. *We* choose. You've paid. Gideon—you've paid, look at me, you've *paid*—"

And the crown came off.

Not lifted—*torn*. It ripped free of Aldric's brow with a sound like the world's worst grief, screaming that grinding metal scream, and rose into the air above the throne trailing threads of silver and shadow, thrashing, reaching, grasping for the heads it had worn for a century. Tamsin threw herself over her father. Gideon's remaining flame flared white. And the parasite, starving, unfed, unworn, curled in on itself like a burning scroll and came apart into sparks and smoke and a smell like lightning-struck sand, and the darkness of the eclipse broke, and the moon's rim reappeared above the shattered vault, thin and bright as a healing wound.

Silence.

Real silence, for the first time in thirteen years. Tamsin hadn't known how loud the crown had been until it stopped—a pressure behind her eyes she'd mistaken for the shape of the world.

"Papa?"

Aldric breathed. It was a terrible, ragged, wonderful sound. He sagged in the throne like a puppet with cut strings, and Tamsin caught him, and his head lolled against her shoulder, and he weighed almost nothing, and he was *warm*, warm the way the dead are not, warm the way fathers are.

"Tamsin," he murmured. His voice was hollowed out, an echo of an echo, but it was his. Only his. "Your mother used to sing. Doesn't... doesn't someone used to sing?"

"We'll find the song," Tamsin said, and held him, and wept without shame into his ruined hair. "We'll find all of it. Every piece. I promise."

* * *

Gideon was sitting where they had knelt, very still, in the ring of dead chalk.

Tamsin crossed the throne room to them slowly, her father's breath evening against her neck, dread rising in her throat like floodwater. She had seen what the toll took. She had heard it stop mid-sentence, mid-*name*, and she was afraid—not of what Gideon had lost, but of what Gideon might have lost *last*.

"Gideon?"

They looked up. Their eyes were red-rimmed and confused and, underneath the confusion, kind—the same kindness that had dragged a bleeding wolf across a boundary line, wherever that line was, whoever had done the dragging.

"Hello," Gideon said politely. Then, after a moment, tilting their head: "Do I know you?"

The question landed like a knife between Tamsin's ribs. She knelt in front of them, slow, the way you approach a frightened animal, and searched their face. The garden was gone. The bees, the valley, Maren—all of it, paid out into the dark. And the road, the inn, the seer, the cells, the plan, the promise—

But Gideon's gaze caught on her face and stayed there, snagged like cloth on a nail. Their brow furrowed. Something worked behind their eyes, some deep current that the toll had not managed to dam.

"I don't remember you," Gideon said slowly. "I don't remember anything. But—" They raised a hand, hesitated, touched their own chest as if checking whether the ache lived there. "But looking at you feels like coming home. Is that strange? That feels strange."

Tamsin laughed. It came out cracked and wet and more honest than any prayer she'd ever spoken.

"No," she said, and took their hand. "No, that's not strange at all. My name is Tamsin. And I'm going to tell you a story—about a garden, and a wolf, and a date written in ink. It's a long story. We have time."

Above them, through the broken vault, the moon climbed free of the earth's shadow, whole and silver and beginning again.

In the corner of the throne room, forgotten in the ash where the crown had burned, the prophecy ledger lay open—and its final page, blank for a hundred years, had begun, faintly, to fill with ink.
