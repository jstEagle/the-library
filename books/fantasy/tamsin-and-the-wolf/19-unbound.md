## 19. Unbound

The valley went first.

I had thought I would feel it leave — that memory would tear like a bandage, like bark stripped from a living tree — but it did not. It drained. That was the cruelty of it. The unbinding did not rip anything from me; it simply opened my hands and let the water run out, and I stood there feeling the level fall and could not even say what shape the vessel was.

The bees came first. Not the idea of bees — the bees themselves, the particular weight of them on my palms in high summer, the way Maren's hives hummed a half-tone lower when rain was coming, the sting on my left wrist when I was nine or ten or some age I would never be able to name again. The smell of the garden after cutting. The exact green of the gate. Maren's voice saying *Gideon, mind the combs*, and then Maren's voice at all, and then the word *Maren* becoming a sound with nothing behind it, a door opening onto a wall.

I fed it all into the circle beneath the eclipsed moon.

Tamsin knelt across from me with her father's head cradled in her lap, her hands pressed to his temples, holding him steady the way you hold a frightened animal through a storm — not restraining, just present, just *here*. King Aldric's eyes were open. Half of him was still his own; I could see that now, see the man underneath the thing, the way you can see the riverbed through floodwater. His mouth moved. No sound came out. But Tamsin nodded as though he'd spoken, and said, "I know. I know. Hold on."

The crown screamed.

I do not have another word for it. Metal does not scream — but this metal did, a sound that came less through my ears than through my teeth, my bones, the wet architecture of my inner ear. The throne room of the ruined palace had no roof; the eclipse sat above us like a lid drawn over the moon, a ring of dying light, and every shadow in the chamber leaned toward the crown as if pulled by a tide.

"Keep going," Tamsin said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Whatever it shows you — keep going."

It showed me everything.

That was the last toll, I think. Or the first payment returned. The crown, in its death-throes, tried to buy me. It opened its hoard like a miser forced at knifepoint, and there — there it all was: a woman with Maren's hands but a younger face, running through snow with an infant wrapped against her chest. A door in a hillside. A name spoken over a cradle that was not Gideon, something older, something that ended in a vowel the valley's air had worn away. A hearth. A lullaby in a language I suddenly knew and would forget knowing. The moment before the tree line, the moment magic let go of me like a hand opening —

*You could keep this,* the crown said, in a voice like coins poured on stone. *All of it. Walk away. Let the old king rot wearing me. You were born to be spent once, little key. Refuse your purpose. Take back what is yours.*

And grief — real grief, the kind with weight — because for one whole breath I wanted it. I wanted the garden and the bees and the woman who had bled herself hollow to hide me. I wanted the name that ended in the vowel. I wanted to be a person with a past instead of a person with a hole.

Then I looked at Tamsin.

She was crying without any of the noise of crying. Tears ran down her face and dropped onto her father's cheek and she did not wipe them away or speak of them; she simply held him and watched me with those amber eyes that had been wolf-gold the first night I saw her at the tree line, bleeding and furious and alive, and she did not ask me for anything. She had refused, in the cell beneath the chapel, to let me pay the full toll. She had argued until her voice cracked. And now, with everything hanging on it, she asked for nothing at all. She only looked at me as though I were already worth whatever it cost.

"No," I said to the crown. "Take the valley. Take all of it."

*Foolish—*

"You were made to bind," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, calm, the calm I'd only ever felt in Maren's garden among the hives. "I was made to unbind. We end tonight, both of us, doing exactly what we were made for. The difference is that I chose it."

I opened my hands wider.

The valley left me.

* * *

There is a strange mercy in forgetting pain along with everything else. I remember — I will always remember, because this part survived, this part happened outside the valley of myself — the sensation of emptiness. Of standing in a room where furniture had been and finding nothing but clean floorboards and pale rectangles on the walls where pictures used to hang. The eclipse-light dimmed. The screaming climbed to a pitch beyond hearing and kept climbing, became pressure, became silence so total my ears rang with it.

Aldric convulsed once, hard, arching against his daughter's arms.

"Hold," Tamsin whispered — to him, to herself, maybe to me. "Hold, Papa. Almost. Almost."

The crown tore free.

Not lifted — *tore*, the way a root tears from soil, bringing up dark threads of something that might have been flesh and might have been older than flesh. It rose above the broken throne trailing smoke and light and a century of stolen years, and for one instant I saw it plainly: not gold, not anymore, not for a long time. A thing coiled in the shape of a circlet, hungry and ancient and, at the very end, afraid.

It burned.

Fire with no heat, white and silent, consuming it from the inside out, and the parasite made a sound at the last — small, almost human, almost pitiful — and came apart into ash that drifted upward instead of down, up toward the ringed and darkened moon, and was gone.

The silence afterward was enormous.

I don't remember falling, but I must have fallen, because the next thing I knew the floor was cold against my cheek and someone was saying my name.

"Gideon. Gideon. Look at me."

I opened my eyes.

A face hovered over mine, lit by the returning edge of the moon sliding free of the earth's shadow — silver light spilling back into the ruined palace inch by inch, catching on dust and ash and the tear-tracks drying on her cheeks. Amber eyes. A strong jaw. A scar through one eyebrow I didn't know the story of.

She was the most important person in the world.

I knew that the way you know fire is hot or winter is cold — instantly, totally, below thought. And I knew absolutely nothing else about her. Not her name. Not how we'd come to be lying on the stones of a ruined throne room with a gaunt grey-haired man breathing raggedly beside us. Not why my chest ached like a house with everyone gone out of it.

"I—" My voice came out ruined. I swallowed and tried again. "I'm sorry. Do I know you?"

Something crossed her face. I watched it happen — watched the blow land and be absorbed, watched her choose, in the space of a single breath, what to do with it. Later I would understand how much that breath contained. In that moment I only saw her jaw set and her eyes stay soft.

"Yes," she said. "You know me."

"I don't remember."

"I know." She took my hand. Her grip was warm and calloused and certain, and my hand fit inside it like a key — no. Like something else. Like a hand fitting into a hand. "That's all right. I'll remember enough for both of us. For now — can you sit? Can you walk?"

Behind her, the old man stirred. He raised one hand to his bare head, slowly, as though expecting to find the weight still there, and touched only his own thin hair, and began — quietly, terribly — to weep. Not the weeping of a king. The weeping of a man waking from a hundred-year nightmare to find the dream had eaten most of him and left the rest to count the cost.

"Tamsin?" he rasped.

Her whole body changed. Every wall in her came down at once, and she turned and gathered him up, this hollowed stranger who was somehow her father, and held him the way I imagined — without knowing why I imagined it — she had once been held as a child.

"I'm here," she said into his hair. "I've got you. It's over. It's finally over."

The moon cleared the shadow entirely, and full silver light flooded the throne room, and somewhere far above us the city of the capital began to make noise — bells, voices, the confused and joyful roar of ten thousand people waking from the crown's long fever. But that was all distant, all later. In the circle of light around the broken throne there was only the three of us, alive, unbound, and beginning.

* * *

They tell me things. They have been telling me things for two days, patiently, in fragments, watching my face each time for signs of breaking.

The woman is called Tamsin. She is — was — a princess, though she says the word like it tastes of iron. The old man is Aldric, her father, a king no longer, though the paperwork of that has yet to catch up with the truth of it. There was a crown. There is no longer a crown. There was a prophecy, a ledger, a journey I apparently made through festival riots and Sentinel ambushes and an inn where a blind seer read my palm and recoiled. There is a pickpocket named Wren who has appointed himself keeper of my story and tells it back to me in installments, embellished shamelessly, with himself cast as increasingly heroic.

And there is a valley.

I know this only because they told me. A valley where magic cannot follow, where a hedge-witch named Maren hid me for — they aren't sure how long. Years. Most of a life. When I try to reach for it, my mind slides off, the way a thumb slides off wet glass. I remember nothing. Worse than nothing: I remember the *shape* of remembering, the ache of a room with pale rectangles on its walls, and no furniture ever coming back.

Maren. I say the name sometimes, testing it. It means nothing and it means everything. Tamsin says I wept the first time I heard it, sitting on a pallet in Wren's garret, and did not know why, and wept harder for not knowing.

Here is the strange thing, though. Here is the thing I keep turning over like a stone found on a road.

I am not unhappy.

Grief sits in me, yes — a low constant ache, a phantom limb itching where the valley used to be. Some mornings I wake with my face wet and no memory of dreaming. When Wren described the garden — he pieced it together from things I'd said on the road, he tells me, the bees and the charms and the gate — I had to go stand at the window for a long while, looking at nothing.

But underneath the ache, something else. Something quiet and sure as a pulse.

Because here is what survived. The toll took the valley, took Maren's face and the bees and the gate and the name-that-ended-in-a-vowel. It did not take what the valley made. It did not take the steadiness in my hands when I work a charm — and my charms work now, gloriously, terrifyingly well, magic flooding into me the moment I crossed that boundary I cannot remember crossing. It did not take the habit of noticing things: which herbs bitter, which clouds promise rain, which silences are peaceful and which are hiding wolves.

And it did not take her.

Tamsin comes to my window every morning. I know her footsteps on the stair before I hear them — know them, the way I knew her face mattered before I knew why. She brings bread and news and her father when he's strong enough, and she tells me pieces of our story, and when she gets to the parts where I was brave she looks embarrassed on my behalf, and when she gets to the parts where she was captured she goes grim and short, and when she gets to the cell beneath the chapel — where I supposedly pressed my fingers to the grate and promised her I'd find a third path, splitting the toll, refusing both the crown's bargain and the prophecy's arithmetic — she stops talking altogether for a while.

Yesterday she stopped, and then she said: "Do you want me to stop telling it? I could stop. If it hurts."

"It doesn't hurt," I said, and found it was true. "It's like hearing about someone else. Someone I'd have liked to know."

"She'd have liked you too," Tamsin said. "She *did*." And then, quieter, looking at her hands: "I miss her. Is that mad? You're right here."

But it isn't mad. I understand it completely. She lost me too — not to death, which at least ends, but to a door that stays shut. She walks beside a person who wears my face and holds my hand and laughs at her jokes, and every day she grieves the one who made that journey with her, the one who fed a valley into a circle of light and chose her with the last clear piece of a self that no longer exists.

So I have decided something. I decided it this morning, watching her come up the street below my window with bread under her arm and her father leaning on her shoulder, both of them squinting in the sun like creatures newly emerged from underground — which, I suppose, they are.

I cannot give her back who I was. But I can become someone worth the price. I can learn the story Wren keeps and make it true again going forward. I can be the person she grieves and the person beside her, both at once, until the seam between them heals over.

She reached my door. I heard her pause on the landing — I always hear her pause, one breath, gathering herself, the way you do before entering a room where someone you love is grieving something you can't fix.

I got up and opened the door before she could knock.

She blinked. Then she smiled — that crooked, guarded smile that I already knew, that I knew the first morning, that I will know every morning after — and held out the bread.

"Thought you might be hungry," she said.

"I'm always hungry," I said. "Apparently that survived too."

She laughed, and behind her Aldric smiled his hollow, healing smile, and down in the street Wren was already shouting something about the council convening at noon, about decisions to be made, about a kingdom with no crown and what on earth they intended to do with it.

Tamsin stepped inside. She put her hand in mine, familiar as breathing, and I closed my fingers around it and found — as I find every time — that it was enough. More than enough.

It was a beginning.
