## 20. A Kingdom Without a Crown

The council chamber still smelled of smoke.

Not the sweet, resinous smoke of the Moonfast braziers, nor the acrid stink of the crown's burning—that smell had finally scrubbed out of the stone after three days of rain and open windows—but something older, something that had lived in the walls of the palace since before Aldric's grandfather wore the iron. Tamsin stood at the long table where her father had once held court and breathed it in, and thought: *this is what a kingdom smells like when it has been holding its breath for a hundred years.*

She had not wanted to come back to this room. She had said as much, twice, to Gideon on the walk from the infirmary wing, and both times Gideon had squeezed her hand and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. Some rooms you do not enter because you want to. You enter them because they are waiting for you, and kingdoms are patient in the way of old animals.

The council sat along either side of the table—nine of them now, where there had been twelve before the riots. Chancellor Vess, gray and precise, with ink stains on her fingers that never quite washed away. Lord Halloway of the river provinces, whose face had gone soft with grief since his son was found alive in the flood-cellars, having spent two days pulling strangers out of the water. Mother Ilsa of the chapel, who had hidden three families beneath her floor during the frenzy and would not speak of it, though her hands still trembled when she poured. And Wren, sitting at the far end, too small for their chair, too still, watching everything with those quick dark eyes that missed nothing and gave back less.

Tamsin's father sat at the head of the table.

He looked smaller than she remembered. That was the cruelest part—not the hollows under his eyes, not the way his hands shook when he lifted his cup, not even the silver streak that had gone through his hair overnight like frost claiming a field. The cruelty was in how *small* he seemed. For all her life, King Aldric had been a mountain. Even after her mother died, even as the crown ate him year by year and his love curdled into decree, he had filled every room he stood in. Now the crown was gone—melted, or nearly—and what remained was just a man in a plain wool tunic, blinking in the light like something newly dug out of the earth.

Which, Tamsin supposed, was exactly what he was.

"Let us begin," Vess said, and unrolled the document before her with the crisp snap of someone who had rehearsed this moment in front of a mirror. "By unanimous consent of the surviving council, we address the matter of succession."

Tamsin felt every eye turn to her. She kept her gaze on the window, where the morning fog was burning off the lower city, and the bell towers stood sharp against the pale sky.

"The throne is empty," Vess continued. "Aldric, son of Maren—" a ripple went through the room at the name; Tamsin herself had only learned it two days ago, and hearing it spoken aloud in this chamber made her jaw tighten— "has abdicated. The crown is destroyed. By law and by custom, the succession falls to the bloodline of the old kings." A pause, deliberate as a knife laid down. "To you, Tamsin."

*There it is,* she thought. *The trap with velvet lining.*

She had known it was coming. She had felt it building for days, in the way the council members looked at her in the corridors—in the way the servants bowed a half-beat too low, in the way the guards outside her door had doubled without anyone telling them to. Hope was a tide, and it had been rising in this city since the night the sky split open over the ruined palace, and tides do not care what they drown.

"I have an answer," Tamsin said.

Vess inclined her head. "We await it."

Tamsin pushed back her chair and stood. Her legs were steady. She noticed that, and was grateful for it, because there had been a time—not so long ago, though it felt like another life, another body—when standing in front of powerful people had meant running from them.

"You want me to take the crown," she said. "Or rather, you want me to take the *throne*, since there is no crown anymore. You want a queen because a kingdom without one feels like a ship without ballast, and I understand that. I do. I grew up on stories about this table. My mother used to tell me that someday I'd sit at its head, and I believed her, and then she died, and then my father stopped being my father, and I ran so far from this room that I forgot the color of its wood." She laid her palm flat against the table, against the grain worn smooth by a century of royal hands. "So believe me when I say I know exactly what I'm refusing."

"Tamsin—" her father began.

"No," she said, gently. "You've done your part, Da. You paid more than any of us. Let me finish mine."

Aldric closed his mouth. His eyes were wet. He did not wipe them, and she loved him for that—for the small human dignity of letting himself be seen weeping in front of his council, when for twenty years he would sooner have cut off his hand.

"The crown didn't fall on his head by accident," Tamsin said, turning back to the council. "It was forged to be worn. It was made hungry on purpose, by people who thought a king should be more than a man, and every person who sat in that chair and let it happen—they chose it. They chose hunger over humanity, power over people, again and again, until the thing wearing my father's face wasn't wearing anything else underneath." She let that sit. Let the silence do its work. "And now you're asking me to sit in the same chair. Not the same crown—the same *chair*. The same idea. That one person should hold all of it, because that's how it's always been done."

"The alternative is chaos," Lord Halloway said quietly. It was not a challenge; it was a fear, honestly offered, and Tamsin respected him for it.

"The alternative," she said, "is what we already saw in the streets three nights ago. When the crown's hunger leaked into the city, what happened? Did the people wait for a king to save them? No. They pulled each other out of the floods. They broke down the doors of the granaries and shared what they found. Mother Ilsa hid strangers under her floorboards. A pickpocket carried messages through a rioting city because he'd decided someone was worth saving." She nodded down the table at Wren, who went very still. "That's what a kingdom actually is. It isn't the chair. It never was the chair. It's the hands."

Vess's pen had stopped moving. "Then what would you have us do?"

"Build something that doesn't need saving by one person," Tamsin said. "A council that answers to the districts, not to the palace. Courts that a farmer can actually reach. And—" here she paused, because the next part mattered most, and she had rehearsed it in front of a mirror too, though she'd never have admitted it— "a record. Someone whose whole office is remembering. Not a historian behind a wall—a chronicler who walks the streets, who writes down what the council does and what it costs and who pays. So that no one ever again can say they didn't know. So that the next time someone offers this city a hungry crown, there's a book somewhere with the whole story in it, in ink that doesn't fade."

"And who would fill such an office?" Vess asked.

Tamsin smiled. It surprised her, arriving on her face like weather.

"Someone who owes a debt," she said, "and pays it better than anyone I've ever known. Someone who kept a story safe when keeping it could have gotten them killed. Someone who knows every roof, cellar, and gutter in this city, and every lie told in them." She turned fully toward the end of the table. "Wren. Would you be our chronicler?"

For a moment, Wren did not move. Then they blinked, slow, like a cat deciding whether to wake, and Tamsin watched something pass across their face—fear first, then disbelief, then a kind of fierce, startled joy that they tried to smother and could not.

"Me," Wren said. Not a question. An echo, testing the weight of it.

"You," Tamsin said. "Unless you'd rather keep picking pockets."

Wren considered this with visible seriousness. "The hours were better."

"Wren."

"I'll do it." They stood up in their too-big chair, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they pushed through it the way they pushed through everything—headfirst, grinning, terrified. "I'll do it. But I want it written down that I asked for a coat with real buttons, and nobody listened."

Laughter moved around the table like a lamp being lit, room by room. Even Aldric laughed—a rough, rusty sound, as if the mechanism of it needed oiling after years of disuse—and Tamsin felt something in her chest unlock that she had not known was locked.

* * *

They melted the crown at noon.

Not in some private forge behind locked doors—Tamsin had insisted on that, over Vess's mild objections and Halloway's stronger ones. If the thing was going to die, the city would watch it die. Fear festers in the dark, and this city had been afraid for a hundred years.

The great square before the chapel was packed shoulder to shoulder. Tamsin stood on the chapel steps beside her father, and Gideon stood on her other side, close enough that their shoulders touched, and below them the crowd stretched out like a dark sea holding its breath.

The forge had been built at the center of the square, a squat brick furnace with a crucible glowing at its heart. Two smiths attended it—an old woman named Brey who had hammered plowshares through three wars, and her grandson, who kept looking at the crowd as if he couldn't quite believe they were all there for him.

Aldric stepped forward alone.

He carried the crown in both hands. It had been cleaned of ash, but no amount of cleaning could make it look like anything but what it was: a ring of black iron worked into the shapes of thorns, heavy as sin, ugly as truth. In the daylight, with no head beneath it, it looked almost pitiful. Almost small.

Tamsin knew better. She had seen what it looked like when it was full.

Her father raised it above his head, and the crowd went silent in a wave, from the steps outward, until the only sound in the square was the wind off the mountains and the groan of the furnace.

"For ninety years," Aldric said, and his voice carried because the heralds had taught him how, long ago, before the crown took his gentleness, "this city has been ruled by a hunger. It wore my face for the last twenty of them. Some of you loved me anyway. Some of you feared me. Both were reasonable." A ragged breath of laughter moved through the crowd, disbelieving. "I cannot give back what was taken. I cannot undo the decrees or raise the ones the Sentinels killed. But I can do this."

He walked to the furnace. He stood before it for a long moment, and Tamsin saw his arms tremble—not from the weight of the iron, she thought, but from the weight of letting go—and then he set the crown into the crucible.

The scream was brief.

Tamsin had heard it before, on the night of the unbinding, and she had hoped never to hear it again—that high, grinding shriek, like a nail dragged across the inside of the skull. The crowd heard it too. She watched mothers pull children close, watched grown men go white, watched the whole sea of people flinch as one body. And then the scream thinned, and thinned, and became only the sound of iron becoming liquid, and the orange glow of the crucible swallowed the last of the black.

Brey worked the bellows. Her grandson stirred the melt with an iron rod, and sparks climbed into the cold air like reversed snow. It took an hour. Nobody left. They stood in the square in the winter light and watched a hundred years of hunger boil down to a dull orange soup, and Tamsin stood among them and felt the strangest thing: grief. Real grief, for the thing that had eaten her father, because hating it had been the spine that held her upright for so long, and now the spine was molten, and she would have to learn to stand some other way.

When it was done, Brey poured the melt into a mold her grandson had carved from river clay—a shape Tamsin had approved without quite understanding why, until she saw it: a bell. A simple bell, wide-mouthed and plain, with no ornamentation at all except a single line of lettering around its lip, which the smiths had copied from Gideon's careful handwriting:

*SOME DATES ARE BEGINNINGS.*

They hung it in the chapel tower that evening. When the rope was pulled for the first time, the note that came out was low and clear and strange—not the bright alarm of a warning bell, not the solemn tolling of a funeral bell, but something between. Something that sounded, Tamsin thought, like a door opening onto a road.

The crowd in the square below listened, and then, slowly, they began to sing.

* * *

Later—much later, when the singing had faded and the lanterns had been lit and the council had dispersed to argue happily about district maps—Tamsin found her father in the garden behind the chapel.

He was kneeling in the dirt. Actually kneeling, sleeves rolled, pressing bulbs into the cold earth with his bare hands, and when Tamsin sat down on the step beside the bed he did not stop or straighten or perform anything at all. He just kept planting.

"Your mother used to grow nightbells here," he said. "Did I ever tell you that?"

"No."

"She said they only opened at dusk. Said some things won't bloom for an audience." He patted soil over a bulb, moved to the next hole. "I tore the garden out my third year wearing it. The crown didn't like things that bloomed without permission."

Tamsin watched his hands work. Steady now. Steadier than they'd been in the infirmary, steadier than they'd been at the table. There was a hollow where the parasite had been—she could see it when he paused, a flicker behind his eyes, a man listening for a voice that wasn't there anymore—but the hollow was filling, slowly, the way a well fills when the poison is drawn off. With dirt. With bulbs. With small, patient work.

"The council will want you as an advisor," she said. "Vess, especially. She thinks you remember things the rest of us don't."

"I remember plenty." He sat back on his heels and looked at her, and his eyes were his own—tired, and sad, and his. "Mostly I remember that I have a daughter who refused a throne today, and I have never in my life been prouder of anything, including anything I did while wearing a crown."

"Da—"

"No. Let an old man plant his flowers." He smiled, and it was crooked, and it was hers, the smile from before, dredged up from wherever he had kept it all these years. "Go on. Your Gideon's waiting at the gate. Been waiting near an hour, by the look of it, pretending to read that ledger."

Tamsin stood. At the garden gate she paused, and turned back.

"What will you grow?" she asked. "Now that it's yours again."

Aldric considered the dark beds, the turned earth, the whole small world of it.

"Something that opens at dusk," he said. "Something that blooms whether anyone watches or not."

She left him to it.

Gideon was at the gate, exactly where her father had said, the prophecy ledger open in their hands though the light was nearly gone. They looked up as she came, and smiled, and the smile was warm and easy and utterly without history—because that was the price, the toll, the valley they had fed to the unbinding. Gideon remembered her face but not why it mattered. Remembered the journey in fragments, in Wren's retellings, in the chronicle that was even now being copied out in a borrowed coat with real buttons.

And it was enough. It had turned out to be enough, and Tamsin still marveled at that every day—the grace of it, the strange arithmetic of loss.

"Well?" Gideon said, closing the book. "Do I get to know how it went? Wren says I'm not allowed to read the chapter until it's finished."

"It went," Tamsin said. "No crown. A bell instead. Wren's got an office now, right over the granary—says the view is terrible and the rent is free, which is the best deal they've ever had."

"And your father?"

"Planting flowers."

Gideon nodded slowly, taking this in, filing it somewhere behind those clear gray eyes that held so much and knew so little of what they held. Then they tilted their head toward the gate, toward the city beyond, toward the road that led out of it and away.

"Come on, then," Gideon said. "Walk me somewhere. Somewhere I've never been—which shouldn't be hard, given the circumstances."

Tamsin laughed, and took their hand, and together they passed under the gate and out into the lamplit streets, while overhead the new bell rang the hour in its low, clear, beginning sort of voice—and far to the north, past the mountains, past the tree line, in a valley that had forgotten them both, a hedge-witch's garden waited under the stars for a letter that was already written, and sealed, and tucked inside a coat pocket, ready for the road.
