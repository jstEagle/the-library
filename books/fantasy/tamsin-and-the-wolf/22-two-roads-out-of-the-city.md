## 22. Two Roads Out of the City

The bell rang at dawn.

They had melted the crown three days past, and still the sound of it hung in the city like weather—every hour, on the hour, the new bell in the old chapel tower tolling a note that was not quite bronze and not quite silver, something stranger, sweeter, forged from a parasite's iron and blessed by a council that did not yet know what it had unmade. Gideon woke to it in the room above Wren's cousin's bakery, lying still beneath a blanket that smelled of flour and woodsmoke, and listened to the note fade, and thought: *that was the last one. That was the last morning I wake up not knowing.*

Because today they left.

The knowledge sat in their chest like a swallowed coal—not pain, exactly, but heat, a live and restless thing. They rose without waking Tamsin, who slept the way wolves sleep, curled tight with her back to the wall and one hand loose near her throat, and dressed in the clothes Maren's letter had come wrapped in no longer mattered, and went down the narrow stairs into the pre-dawn dark of the kitchen.

Wren was there, of course. Wren was always there, it seemed, materializing in doorways like a rumor, small and quick-eyed and smelling faintly of other people's pockets. They sat cross-legged on the counter beside a bowl of rising dough, turning the prophecy ledger over in their hands—the old ledger, the century-old book with Gideon's face sketched inside it in faded ink, the date that had fallen tonight a hundred years before Gideon was born.

"You're up early," Gideon said.

"I don't sleep much since the riots." Wren said it lightly, but their thumb traced the ledger's cracked spine the way other people touched charms. "Too many bells. Too many *names*." They looked up. "I finished it. The chronicle."

Gideon's hands went still around the cup of water they'd poured. Since the night Wren had read the story aloud—all of it, every chapter of the journey Gideon had paid away—they had been careful not to think too long about what was missing. The valley lived in them now only as a shape: green, quiet, ringed by trees where magic died. A garden. Bees. An old woman's voice saying *stay indoors*. Everything else was a hole shaped like a life.

"Read me the last page," Gideon said. "Again."

Wren hopped down from the counter. They opened the ledger to pages that had not existed a month ago—pages written in Wren's sharp, slanting hand, ink still faintly shiny—and read:

"*And the key, having paid, remembered nothing of the place that made them. But the wolf remembered for them both, and the chronicler wrote it down, and this is the truth of it: that the valley was called Fairweather Hollow, that the hedge-witch who raised the key was named Maren, that she grew lavender along the south wall and talked to her bees and lied beautifully to keep one child safe. That the child's name—*"

Wren stopped.

"That part you know," they said quietly.

Gideon knew it. It was the one thing the unbinding had left them, or perhaps the one thing they had refused to pay: their own name, Gideon, and the knowledge that somewhere beyond the tree line an old woman was waiting to learn whether the spending of a life had been worth the keeping of it.

"I'm going to write to her," Gideon said. "From the road. I don't know if I'll send it."

"Maren would want the letter either way." Wren closed the ledger and held it out. "Take it."

"It's yours. You carried it."

"And now I'm done carrying it." Wren's grin flashed, quick and crooked as ever, but their eyes were wet. "You're going to new country. Somebody has to stay here and make sure the council doesn't melt anything else important. Besides—" they tapped the cover, "—a chronicle isn't finished until its subject reads it aloud on a road somewhere. That's tradition. I just invented it."

Gideon took the book. It weighed less than it should have. It weighed more than they could say.

Upstairs, floorboards creaked. Tamsin came down the stairs with her boots in one hand and her hair still loose from sleep, wearing the shirt Gideon had mended with stitches that were, thanks to a month of roadside practice, almost straight. She paused on the last step and looked at the two of them in the grey kitchen light—at Gideon holding the ledger, at Wren perched on the counter like a gargoyle with a good heart—and something in her face went soft and fierce all at once.

"Leaving without waking me," she said. "Some things never change."

"We weren't leaving yet."

"You were thinking about it loudly enough to wake the dead." She crossed the kitchen, pressed her forehead briefly to Gideon's—a habit now, a greeting, a small ceremony neither of them had ever discussed—and then turned and pulled Wren off the counter into a hug that lifted the pickpocket clean off their feet. Wren squawked. Tamsin held on anyway.

"Chronicler of the realm," she said into Wren's hair. "Try not to steal the crown jewels. There aren't any anymore, but try."

"No promises."

* * *

They walked out through the Moon Gate at first light, when the city was all long shadows and the smell of bread and river water, and the streets were full of people who looked at Tamsin and did not look away. That was the strangest thing, Gideon thought—the looking. For a hundred years her family had been a story told in whispers, the deposed line, the mad king, the crown that ate its wearers. Now she walked through the capital bare-faced and people stopped in doorways to watch her pass, and some of them bowed, and one old woman pressed a sprig of dried lavender into Gideon's hand and said, "For the road, key-bearer," and Gideon could not explain to her why the smell of lavender made their eyes sting.

Beyond the gate, the road ran west through orchards heavy with late fruit, and the world opened the way it always did outside walls—vast and indifferent and lit gold at the edges. Gideon breathed it in. Magic moved in everything here; they felt it now the way they felt temperature, a low hum in the grass, a shimmer in the air above the road. A month ago the feeling had been overwhelming, terrifying, a flood after a lifetime of drought. Now it was simply the texture of being alive. Their little spells worked. Their charms held. When they brushed a sleeping bird's nest in a hedgerow and murmured the mending-word Maren must have taught them once, in another life, the broken twig knitted whole.

Tamsin watched them do it with that expression she wore sometimes—pride and grief sharing a chair.

"You're getting better," she said.

"You're a terrible teacher. You said so yourself."

"I'm an excellent teacher. You're an alarming student. Most apprentices take years to learn the mending-word." She walked a few steps in silence, boots crunching on the packed earth. "Maren taught you faster than that, I'd wager."

The name landed gently now, most days. Gideon turned the lavender sprig over in their fingers. "I keep trying to remember her voice. I can't. I remember that there *was* a voice. I remember—" they searched for it, the way they did every morning, reaching into the hole— "I remember being safe. That's all. Just... safe, and bored, and restless, and wishing something would happen." They laughed, short and rueful. "Be careful what you wish for."

"You wished for a wolf, a prophecy, and a revolution?"

"I wished for *something to happen*. The universe has poor aim."

Tamsin laughed too, and the sound startled a pheasant out of the hedge, and for a while they walked without talking, which was its own kind of language now, the comfortable silence of two people who had seen each other at their worst and stayed.

Near midday they crested a hill and looked back. The capital lay behind them in the haze, white-walled, chimney-smoked, the chapel tower rising above it all with the new bell catching the sun. Somewhere under that tower was a dungeon where a hollowed man was learning, slowly, how to be a father again—learning it badly, in letters he sent that Tamsin read and answered and read again. Somewhere in the city was Wren, already writing. Somewhere far north, past mountains Gideon had crossed in a memory they no longer owned, was a valley with a garden going wild for lack of tending.

"Do you miss it?" Tamsin asked, following their gaze. "The city. The—before."

"The before?" Gideon considered it honestly, because Tamsin deserved honesty and had paid for it in blood. "I miss things I can't name. It's like—there's music missing from songs. I hum along and there's a gap where the melody should be." They looked down at the ledger in their satchel. "But Wren gave the melody back. On paper, at least. And the gap—" they touched their chest, "—the gap is mine. I paid for it. I'd pay it again."

"I know," Tamsin said. "That's the worst part. That you would."

She said it without bitterness. She had argued, in the dark under the ruined palace, with the eclipse eating the moon overhead—argued until her voice broke—that Gideon should not pay at all, that her father had worn his crown long enough, that no prophecy got to spend Gideon's memories like coin. And Gideon had found the third path anyway, the split toll, the answer no page had predicted: half the valley's memory given, the king freed, the parasite burned. Some costs you couldn't dodge. But you could choose *how* you carried them, and who carried them with you.

That was the whole lesson, maybe. That was what the ledger had never managed to write.

* * *

They made camp that night in a stand of birches beside a stream, and Tamsin built a fire with the ease of someone raised in exile camps, and Gideon brewed tea in a dented pot and doctored it with honey bought from a roadside farmer. The moon came up thin and new—the first turn of a fresh count, thirteen moons until nothing at all, because prophecies, Wren had written, are debts that die when paid.

Tamsin shifted after supper. Not all the way—she saved the full change for open country—but enough: ears, eyes, the faint silvering of her hands in the firelight, the wolf coming close to the surface the way it did when she felt safe. Gideon had learned to read it. Safe, for Tamsin, had been a rare country for a long time.

"New country," she said, watching the flames. "That's what you told Wren. Where are we actually going?"

"I don't know." Gideon stretched their legs toward the fire. "Somewhere with a garden. Somewhere the soil's good and nobody's heard of prophecies. Somewhere we can get bees."

"Bees."

"Maren kept bees." The certainty arrived whole, sourceless, one of the strange gifts the hole in their memory left behind—facts without stories attached. "I'm sure of it. I don't remember it, but I'm sure. And I want—" they stopped, surprised by the size of the wanting, "—I want to build the thing I can't remember. Does that make sense? Plant the lavender. Keep the bees. Learn the craft properly, from the roots, not just the emergency bits you taught me on the road."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Firelight moved in her wolf-gold eyes.

"My mother had a house," she said at last. "Before the deposition. Before any of it. In the western hills, near the coast. It'll have gone to ruin—twenty years of ruin—but the land will still be there. Land doesn't get deposed." She turned her hands over, silvered palms up. "I haven't been back. I couldn't. It was hers, and then it was grief, and grief and I have an understanding: we keep our distance." She looked up. "But if I went with a gardener. If I went to *build* something instead of mourn something."

"Then we'd need seeds," Gideon said. "And a roof that doesn't leak. And probably a very patient neighbor."

"And bees."

"And bees."

They smiled at each other across the fire, tentative and enormous, two exiles drawing a map out of nothing. It wasn't a plan yet. It was the seed of one, and seeds were Gideon's trade, whatever else had been taken.

Later, wrapped in blankets with the fire banked low, Gideon took out the ledger one last time and read the final lines by ember-light. Wren's handwriting, steady and sure:

"*They left the city at dawn on the day after the bell was hung, two travelers on the west road with a book, a mended shirt, and between them the sum total of everything worth keeping: a name, a debt forgiven, and a home not yet built. The prophecy ended where all prophecies end—in the past tense. What came next belonged to no ink but their own.*"

Below it, in smaller letters, Wren had added a postscript:

"*P.S. — The chronicler notes, for the record, that the key asked to hear the story twice, and laughed in the same places both times, and cried in the same places both times, which proves that some things survive any toll. The chronicler further notes that the wolf let the key sleep against her shoulder on the third night of the road, and pretended afterward that it hadn't happened, and that the chronicler saw, and is putting it in the book anyway, because history owes the truth to somebody.*"

Gideon closed the book and pressed it flat against their chest and, in the dark, leaned against Tamsin's shoulder, and Tamsin—who had read over their shoulder, because of course she had—said nothing at all, which was its own confession, and the fire ticked, and the stream ran, and somewhere east behind them a bell tolled midnight in a note that was not quite bronze and not quite silver, and was, despite everything, beautiful.

Tomorrow the road bent west, toward hills and salt wind and a house with twenty years of ruin in it, toward lavender and bees and a family being built out of choice instead of fate. Tonight there was a fire, and a shoulder, and a book full of a self they were learning to carry.

In a century-old ledger, beneath a sketch of their own face, a date had once waited like a trap. Gideon understood now that it had never been a trap at all. Dates are only doors. Some close. Some swing wide onto country no one has mapped, and you walk through with your whole heart in your mouth and your whole life in your arms, and you find, standing on the far side in the morning light—

some dates are beginnings.
