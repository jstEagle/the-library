## 1. The Taster's Portion

The arsenic went down easier with honey.

Ada Montrose had learned that in her first year, at fourteen, when old Mistress Corvel still ran the taster's bench and believed in teaching through terror. *Sweetness is a vehicle, girl. Poison rides best in pleasure. That's why kings die of dessert and never of gruel.* Ada had swallowed the lesson along with the laced cake, and the small burning knowledge of it, and had kept both ever since.

Now she sat alone in the back room of the magistrate's kitchens, candle guttering, an open ledger before her and a plate of honey cakes at her elbow. One was dosed. She knew which one. She always knew which one.

She ate it slowly, the way the ritual demanded—three bites, chewed thoroughly, then wait. The Choir's rules for tasters were older than the guild itself: eat the portion, count to three hundred, feel for the bloom of heat in the belly or the prickle at the gums. If you were still counting at three hundred, the table was clean. If you weren't, well. The ledger would record it, and someone else would inherit your column.

Ada counted. Around forty she felt the familiar warmth spread under her sternum—not poison's kiss but its opposite, the deep dull immunity that thirty years of daily doses had built into her blood like mortar into a wall. Her body was a ledger too, she thought. Every entry written in suffering, every balance paid. Arsenic, hemlock, nightshade, the gray powder from the eastern trade they called widow's salt. She carried them all, and none of them carried her.

At two hundred she picked up the quill again.

*Entry 447,* she wrote in the margin, small and neat. *Magistrate Ollen's table. Honey cakes, almond glaze. Dosed by cook's boy—sloppy, visible crystals if you looked. Cleared.*

The ledger itself was not hers to write in, strictly speaking. It was the Choir's great book, the one that lived chained to the master's desk upstairs, and what Ada held was only her copy—but hers was the true one, because Ada made it so. The guild traded in impossible things: bottled grief, borrowed luck, second chances sold on installment. Every trade generated debt, and every debt needed recording, and the masters who signed the contracts were too grand to keep their own arithmetic straight. So they kept Ada. Thirty years of double entries, cross-checked nightly, memorized in columns until the numbers lived behind her eyes like faces.

She turned the page. Candlelight caught the ink and made it gleam like something wet.

*One grief, bottled, sold to Lady Vess of the Salt Quarter. Repayment: one favor, unspecified, callable at need.* Paid, thank the quiet gods—or rather, paid before the quiet gods stopped listening, three hundred years ago, when the prayers of the world began falling into silence like coins into a well that had no bottom. The Choir had flourished in that silence. When gods don't answer, people will buy their miracles retail.

*One luck, borrowed, seven years' term, debtor: the Harbormaster's office.* Outstanding. Everything was outstanding lately. The free cities had been borrowing against tomorrow for so long that tomorrow had become a creditor with a long memory and no mercy.

Ada worked through the columns methodically, mouthing the figures as she went, committing them deeper. This was the part of the ritual no one paid her for—the memorization—and it was the part she'd never once skipped. A taster who could be poisoned was replaceable. A taster who knew every debt in the Choir's books was something else entirely. Not indispensable; Ada had spent thirty years making sure no one found her indispensable, because indispensable people got noticed, and noticed people got used up. But *informed*. Quietly, invisibly informed. It was the closest thing to power she permitted herself.

Outside, the city murmured its evening murmur. Cart wheels on wet cobbles. A drunk singing three streets over. The magistrate's household settling toward supper, unaware that the woman in the back room had just eaten their death and found it wanting.

At three hundred, Ada finished the cake. Clean. She brushed crumbs from her fingers, blotted the entry, and allowed herself the last unpoisoned cake as wages.

It was while she chewed that she heard the voice.

"Please," said someone in the yard beyond the window. "Please, I know You're there. I know You can hear me. Please let my boy breathe through the night."

A woman's voice, cracked and worn smooth with repetition—the voice of someone who had prayed the same prayer so many times it had gone past meaning into habit, the way a stair goes smooth in the middle. Prayers like that were everywhere in the free cities. They were furniture. People prayed the way they swept floors: daily, hopelessly, without expectation. Three hundred years of silence taught you to pray with your eyes closed so you wouldn't have to watch nothing happen.

Ada didn't look up. She licked honey from her thumb and reached for the next ledger page.

And the yard-voice answered.

Not the woman. Something else—a sound like a great door opening very far away, like wind moving through a house the size of the sky, and out of it came words, actual words, spoken aloud in a language Ada's bones understood before her ears did:

***He will breathe.***

The honey cake turned to stone in Ada's mouth.

She sat perfectly still. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. In the yard, the praying woman made a sound Ada had never heard a human being make—not a sob, not a laugh, but some third thing between them, the noise of a person whose whole architecture of despair collapses in a single instant and leaves them standing in open air.

Then footsteps, running, joyful, fading toward the poor quarter. Then nothing.

Ada swallowed the stone. She looked at the ledger. She looked at the candle. She got up, went to the window, and looked out at the ordinary dark yard with its rain barrel and its stacked firewood, as though the air might still hold the shape of what had passed through it.

Nothing. Just the city, breathing. Just the world, going about its business, entirely unaware that three hundred years of silence had ended in a magistrate's backyard between one bite of cake and the next.

*No one else noticed,* she thought. *The whole world shifted and no one else noticed.*

But that wasn't quite true, and Ada's mind, which kept accounts whether she asked it to or not, began entering the discrepancy. The Choir's oldest contracts—all those impossible things sold across three centuries of godless silence—had been written with clauses. Contingency clauses. *Should the gods resume answering,* the ancient boilerplate read, *all terms revert. All debts come due.* She had copied those clauses a thousand times and thought them legal fossils, dead language from a dead era, the way a scribe copies words about dragons without believing in dragons.

Every contract in her ledgers had a clause like that. Every single one.

Ada sat back down. Her hands, she noted distantly, were steady—thirty years of swallowing poison taught a body composure if it taught nothing else. She turned to the front of her copy, to the flyleaf where the founding entries lived in her mother's handwriting, faded brown, older than she was. Her mother had been the Choir's chief auditor before her, and her mother's mother before that. Three generations of Montrose women keeping the guild's true books. Ada had inherited the work like a crooked spine.

She had read this flyleaf a hundred times. She read it now with new eyes, and there—at the bottom, in cramped script she'd always taken for a household inventory—she saw it properly for the first time:

*One soul, Montrose line. Sold to the Undercourt. Payment received in full.*

Her sister's name followed. Her dead sister, dead these twenty years, dead of a fever that had burned through her in four days while Ada, immune to everything, could do nothing at all.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above her, the guildhouse creaked like a ship.

Ada did not cry. Crying was an expense, and Ada balanced everything. But her hand, when she set it flat on the page, was cold as a creditor's heart, and beneath the fear—beneath the vast, tilting realization that the gods were awake and every debt in three hundred years of bookkeeping was about to come due at once—there bloomed something hotter and more dangerous.

Because if the contracts reverted, all of them, then the Undercourt's claim had activated too. And the Undercourt, the court beneath the mountain, the Soul-King who collected souls as interest—that court did not send letters. It sent collectors.

Ada closed the ledger. She pinched out the candle. And in the dark, tasting honey and arsenic and the ghost of her own name, she began—to her own horror—to calculate.

* * *

Upstairs, the guildmaster's bell rang once, sharp, the signal for emergency assembly. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the mountain like smoke from a lit fuse, came the sound of the Undercourt's horn.
