## 2. Entries Outstanding

The knocking started before dawn.

Ada knew the difference between a debtor's knock and an honest one. Honest knocks were apologetic—two soft raps, a pause, as if the knocker hoped no one would answer. Debtors knocked like the building owed *them* something. This was that kind of knock, multiplied by twenty fists on every door of the guildhouse at once, and beneath it, rising through the floorboards, a sound she had never heard in nine years of keeping the Choir's books: people praying in the street.

Not whispering. Not the embarrassed mumble of a woman asking Saint Vellum to let her son's fever break. Full-voiced prayer, addressed by name, with expectation.

*The gods are answering,* someone shouted below her window. *The gods are answering and I am OWED.*

Ada sat up in the dark, still tasting yesterday's arsenic—a faint almond ghost at the back of her throat, her body's way of filing a complaint it had long since learned to ignore—and felt the world tilt under her like a table with one short leg.

She dressed quickly. Not because she wanted to face what was coming, but because she had learned long ago that dread does not improve with waiting. It only compounds.

* * *

By full light, the guildhouse courtyard looked like a market day for desperation.

They were everywhere. The fishwife whose husband had been lost off the Sere coast eleven years ago—the Choir had sold her a promise of his return, one borrowed miracle, payable on faith. The twin brothers who'd split a second chance between them and now demanded the other half. A man Ada recognized only from the ledgers, entry 4,417: *one name, held in trust*, who stood in the middle of the crowd opening and closing his mouth like a landed fish, trying out syllables he hadn't been permitted to own.

And they were all shouting the same thing: the gods had come back. Old ones. Nameless ones. The kind the Choir's founding charters called *the original creditors.*

A rain-priestess Ada didn't know was standing on the fountain's rim, arms raised, preaching to anyone who'd listen: "Three hundred years they were silent! Three hundred years we paid interest to men instead! Now the accounts are open again—every contract wakes, every debt comes due!"

"Due *to whom?*" Ada called out, before she could stop herself.

The crowd turned. She was standing on the guildhouse steps in her plain grey dress with ink stains on her fingers, and she felt, as she always did, the peculiar discomfort of being perceived. She had built her whole life on being overlooked—the poison-taster nobody remembered, the bookkeeper nobody questioned, the Montrose nobody counted. Visibility was a tax she had never budgeted for.

"You're the ledger-keeper," the rain-priestess said, descending. Water beaded on her skin though the morning was dry. "You keep the true books."

"I keep *copies*. The master keeps—" Ada stopped. The master's chambers had been dark when she passed them. His bed unslept. His strongbox open and empty except for a single note in his slanted hand: *Gone to ground. You have the keys. Forgive me.* Two sentences, and the second one a lie, because forgiveness wasn't his to request anymore than the keys had been his alone to abandon.

"The master keeps nothing this morning," Ada finished. "I keep everything."

The crowd made a sound—not quite a cheer, not quite a growl. The sound of people deciding whether you were their advocate or their obstacle.

"Then read us our accounts," said the fishwife, pushing forward. "Read us what we're owed."

And there it was, the word that changed everything. Not what they *owed*. What they were *owed*. Because if the old gods answered prayers—if the dormant contracts woke—then the whole architecture of the Choir inverted overnight. Every miracle sold, every luck borrowed, every grief bottled and resold: those weren't just transactions anymore. They were liabilities. The Choir had spent three centuries collecting on promises the original creditors might now enforce themselves.

Ada looked out at two hundred faces and understood, with the cold clarity that arsenic had taught her, that she was standing inside a collapsing ledger.

"Inside," she said. "All of you who hold contracts. Inside, in order, and I'll read every entry."

It was madness. It would take days. But the alternative was a mob, and mobs don't wait for arithmetic.

* * *

They came through the counting room in a river.

Ada sat at the long table with the great ledger open before her, and the debtors filed past, and she read to them—entry numbers, terms, balances outstanding—and with each entry she felt the strangeness of it deepen. Because the entries were *changing*.

Entry 2,204, the fishwife's husband: the ink had darkened overnight, the way ink does when paper is damp, except the room was dry as bone. Where it had read *payable on faith*, it now read *payable on demand*.

Entry 3,990, bottled grief, vintage unspecified: the seal notation had altered from *held in vault* to *claimed by originator*.

Every page she turned showed the same transformation, as if some vast hand were amending the Choir's books from the other side of the paper. The old gods weren't just answering prayers. They were auditing.

"Your husband," Ada told the fishwife carefully, "is listed as returnable. Immediately. By the party that holds him."

"Which is who?"

Ada checked the counterparty line. It was blank. It had always been blank—she'd assumed it meant the Choir itself. Now she suspected it had always meant *whoever answers*. And last night, someone had answered.

"Go home," she said. "Light a candle. Say his name."

"That's it?"

"That's what the entry says. I don't write the terms. I only keep them." She almost believed it herself.

Hour after hour. The smuggler with the contraband luck—he'd bought forty years' worth of good fortune in stolen lots and now found it all called due for repossession, and he wept at the table like a child. The man owed his own name, entry 4,417, who signed his receipt with an X because he legally couldn't do otherwise, and then watched the X blur and reform into letters as the ink woke: *Corvin*. He said it aloud once, quietly, like tasting something, and then said it again louder, and then walked out into the courtyard shouting it at the sky, and the sky, Ada would have sworn, leaned down a little to listen.

It was near dusk when the crowd thinned enough that she dared turn to the back of the ledger. The historical entries. The deep pages, brittle as autumn leaves, where the founding contracts lived.

She told herself she was looking for precedent—for any record of what happened the last time the gods woke, three centuries ago. That was good bookkeeping. Due diligence. That was all it was.

The Montrose name caught her eye the way a nail catches a hem.

There were Montroses throughout the early volumes—her family had been Choir people since before the Choir had its charter, tasters and treasurers, keepers of other people's poisons and other people's promises. She'd known that much. Her mother had taught her figures on these very tables; her mother's handwriting was all over the mid-century volumes, precise and upright, each numeral a small act of integrity.

Her mother's hand appeared on the page before her now. Dated thirty-one years ago. The year her sister died.

Ada's own heartbeat went strange and loud.

The entry was short. Contract entries usually were; the horror was always in the brevity. It read:

*Received into escrow, per agreement: one soul, Montrose line, minor, deceased. Forwarded same day to the Undercourt, against debts outstanding. Consideration rendered. Account closed.*

Below it, in the margin, smaller, in the same hand, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the page:

*Forgive me. There was no other collateral. They would have taken both.*

Both.

Ada set down the pen she hadn't realized she was holding. Her hands were perfectly steady—they were always steady; that was her profession, steadiness under poison—and that steadiness felt like the most obscene thing in the world.

Her sister. Wren, who'd died at seven of the fever that took half the city's children that winter. Ada had been four. She had one memory: Wren holding her up to see the swallows nesting under the eaves, saying *they come back every year, Addie, remember that, they always come back.*

Sold. Not taken by god or grave—sold. Her mother had pawned Wren's soul to the court beneath the mountain to cover a family debt, and had kept the Choir's books afterward for thirty years with that entry three shelves away, and had taught little Ada her numerals within arm's reach of it, and had died without ever—

Without ever what? Telling her? Asking forgiveness? The marginal note said *forgive me*, but notes in margins aren't confessions. They're bookkeeping. Even remorse, in this house, got filed.

Ada became aware, distantly, that the rain-priestess was speaking to her. That the counting room had gone quiet. That everyone remaining—maybe thirty debtors, too desperate or too stubborn to leave—was watching her stand motionless over an open book with tears running down a face that showed no other expression at all.

"Ledger-keeper?" the priestess said gently. "What does your book say?"

What did it say. It said her family's history was a transaction she had never consented to. It said the dead could be collateral and the living could be billed. It said that somewhere beneath the mountain, in a court that weighed souls like coin, her sister had been sitting in escrow for thirty-one years while Ada swallowed poison daily and called it a career.

"It says," Ada heard herself reply, in the flat clear voice she used for reading terms aloud, "that the Choir owes a Montrose. And I intend to collect."

She closed the ledger. The sound of it was very final, very small, like a door.

Outside, far to the north, past the city walls, thunder rolled from a direction that had no clouds—and the rain-priestess turned her head toward it sharply, water rising in beads along her arms like the air itself sweating.

"That's not weather," she said. "That's the mountain. Something under it is waking up. And ledger-keeper—" she looked back at Ada, and her eyes were the grey of floodwater, "—whatever you just swore to collect, I think it heard you."
