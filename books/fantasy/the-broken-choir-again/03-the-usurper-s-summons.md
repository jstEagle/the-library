## 3. The Usurper's Summons

The summons arrived the way Ada's poisons did: dissolved in something sweet.

A courier in imperial grey brought it to the guildhouse at dawn, a wafer of pressed honey sealed with black wax, and by the time the guildmaster's clerk carried it up to her room, Ada had already read the words bleeding through the paper. She held it over the candle anyway, watching for the second script that sometimes surfaced under heat. There it was, faint as breath on glass:

*Bring the ledgers. Bring them yourself.*

No signature. No seal beyond the wax. The usurper's chancery had learned that from the Choir, she supposed—how to say a thing twice, once for the record and once for the victim.

She ate the wafer. It was good honey, mountain thyme, and beneath it a bitter thread of something meant to mark her—to let whoever was watching know whether she'd swallowed and where the swallow went. She let it sit on her tongue a moment longer than necessary before she took it down, because there was no point pretending she hadn't noticed. Noticing was all she had ever been permitted to do.

Downstairs, the guildhouse had stopped being a guildhouse overnight. It had become a wound with people pressing against it.

They filled the entry hall and spilled down the steps into Mire Street: debtors who'd felt their contracts wake in the night like animals turning over in a barn. A woman whose bottled grief had begun to weep through the glass. A man who'd borrowed luck twenty years ago and now found it demanding to be repaid *in kind*, whatever that meant when luck came home. The rain-priestess Sef, whom Ada had never liked and never trusted and who nevertheless stood at the door like a hinge, deciding who entered.

"The master?" one of them asked, and no one answered, because Master Corvane had left before dawn with two saddlebags and the guild's traveling seal, and everyone knew what that meant. The rat knows the water is coming. The rat does not warn the other rats.

Ada climbed past them all to the counting room, locked the door, and opened the ledger to the page she had not slept for thinking about.

*One soul, Montrose line, sold to the Undercourt.* Her mother's hand. Her mother's exact hand—the small hooked fives, the way she crossed her t's like little altars. Ada had spent three days telling herself it was forgery, that some debtor had slipped an entry into the family book as a joke too cruel to be funny. But she had audited her mother's handwriting the way other people audited accounts, line by line across forty years of entries, and there was no lie in it. Her mother had sold a soul to the court beneath the mountain, and the entry said nothing about which Montrose.

There was only one candidate. Ada had been born breathing wrong, sickly, half-here; her sister Liss had been born loud and golden and certain the world would hold her gently. If you were going to sell a soul, you'd sell the one that seemed least likely to miss it.

Or the one that seemed most likely to be worth something later.

"Montrose." Sef's voice through the door, flat as slate. "Riders on the causeway. Imperial grey. Six of them."

Ada closed the ledger. She looked around the counting room—at the shelves of bottled grief, the drawers of borrowed luck, the strongbox where second chances were kept in salt—and understood, with the same clarity she brought to a poisoned dish, that none of it would survive the week. The question was only whether she would be standing near it when it burned.

She put the ledger inside her coat, against her ribs, where she'd carried contraband before, and went down to open the door herself.

* * *

Captain Veyra had been an inquisitor for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned exactly one thing worth learning: every summons was a trap, and the only variable was who it was set for.

She rode at the head of six riders through the free city of Vhalen's Mouth, past shuttered temples and shrines people had lit again in defiance of a hundred years of law—little clay dishes of oil on windowsills, old names whispered over bread. The old gods were answering prayers. Veyra had prayed once herself, three nights ago, alone in her quarters, the first time since she was a girl. She had asked nothing. She had simply said the name her grandmother used to say, to see if anything would happen.

Something had happened. She still didn't know what. It had felt like being *read*.

"Captain." Sergeant Ollis, riding at her left, young enough to still believe the Empire was a shape rather than a hunger. "The writ says seize the ledgers and arrest the masters."

"The writ says that," Veyra agreed.

"And?"

"And the masters fled last night. I have it on good authority." She had it on her own authority; she'd paid a beggar outside the guildhouse to watch the doors, which was technically a misuse of funds and practically the only honest work she did anymore. "So we'll take what's there and ask questions of whoever stayed. Questions are cheaper than arrests. Arrests require paperwork, and paperwork goes to the Chancery, and everything that goes to the Chancery goes to him."

Ollis didn't say *the usurper*. Nobody said it. Eleven years ago he'd been General Aurel, the hero of the Pass, and then he'd been Regent, and then he'd simply been the throne wearing a man, and language had quietly rearranged itself around him the way water rearranges itself around a dropped stone.

Veyra had read the file on the Choir twice. It was thin, which was itself suspicious—eleven years of reports and the sum total was *they trade in impossible things, they keep books, they pay their taxes*. No one paid their taxes that cleanly. And now the usurper wanted those books personally, not copies, not summaries, *the ledgers themselves*, and he wanted them brought to the capital by the person who kept them, and he had issued the summons the same week the gods came back.

She could do arithmetic. She'd been doing it for eleven years, subtracting pieces of herself—one friend here, a lover there, the girl who'd joined the inquisitors believing law was a lamp instead of a leash—and the remainder was getting small.

The guildhouse door opened before they knocked. That was the first surprise. The second was the woman in the doorway: small, grey-dressed, unremarkable in the way of furniture, with ink stains on her fingers and eyes that did the thing Veyra hated most in a witness, which was *wait*.

"You're expected," the woman said. "Come in before the street sees. Half of them owe us money and the other half are owed, and neither group needs to watch this."

"And you are?"

"Nobody," the woman said. "I keep the books."

* * *

The counting room smelled of beeswax and cold iron. Ada served the captain tea out of habit and watched her not drink it, and respected her for it, and hated her a little for the respect.

"Master Corvane fled," Ada said. "You know that already, or you wouldn't be sitting so comfortably."

"I know he left. Whether he fled depends on what happens next." Veyra set down the untouched cup. "I'm Captain Veyra. I have a writ for the ledgers."

"I know who you are." Everyone in the trade knew Veyra—the inquisitor who closed cases without burning buildings, which in the current Empire made her either the best of them or the most dangerous. "And I'll give you the ledgers. All of them. On one condition."

Inquisitors, in Ada's experience, reacted to conditions one of two ways: outrage or curiosity. Veyra's face did neither. It did arithmetic. Ada found she liked her immediately and entirely distrusted her, which was, she reflected, probably how people felt about her.

"Condition," Veyra repeated.

"You read them. You're famous for reading things properly—I've heard the clerks talk. Read every entry, every contract, every collateral note. Then tell me honestly—" and here Ada leaned forward, and let herself be visible, fully visible, for the first time in her adult life, "—do these books prove the free cities traffic with forbidden gods? Or do they prove something else?"

Silence. Downstairs, the debtors murmured like weather.

"Why would you want me to read them?" Veyra said slowly. "If there's something in them, reading them condemns you."

"If there's something in them, someone has to read them eventually, and I would very much prefer it be someone who can count." Ada stood, went to the shelf, and began lifting down ledgers—decades of them, spines cracked, corners soft with handling. "Your emperor wants proof we traffic with forbidden gods. Fine. Here is thirty years of traffic, itemized. Bottled grief, borrowed luck, second chances. Sold, bought, pawned, redeemed. Look for the gods in it. Look hard. And while you're looking, Captain, look at the *counterparties*. Who sells to us. Who buys. Whose seals appear on the collateral notes."

Veyra had gone very still, the stillness of a woman hearing a floorboard creak somewhere below her.

"You're saying the books implicate the crown."

"I'm saying a ledger doesn't care who it implicates. That's the whole virtue of a ledger." Ada stacked the volumes in front of her. "I keep these books. I have kept them since I was nineteen. Every entry is true, including the ones that will hang me. Can your emperor say the same of his?"

"That's treason."

"That's bookkeeping." Ada sat back down. "You have a choice, Captain. Take the ledgers to the capital unread, deliver them to a man who will burn them after extracting whatever serves him, and go back to closing cases until the day you close one he doesn't like. Or sit in this room for however long it takes and actually read them, knowing that what you find may cost you your commission, your pension, and quite possibly your neck."

"And why," Veyra said, "would I choose the second?"

"Because you haven't drunk the tea," Ada said, "and you counted the exits when you came in, and you asked about the writ's wording instead of waving it. Because you suspect the summons is a trap, and you're right—it is—but not for me." She tapped the top ledger. "He wants these books because the gods came back and debts woke up, Captain. *All* debts. Including his own. He bought his crown from somewhere, and somewhere keeps records too."

For a long moment Veyra didn't move. Then she pulled off her gauntlets, finger by finger, and laid them on the table beside the tea like a woman setting down weapons—or picking them up.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "And if I find what you think I'll find, Montrose—what's your name? Your actual name."

"Ada."

"Then, Ada, you should know that in eleven years I have never once gone home clean. Whatever's in these books, when I find it, I don't get to unfind it. Are you prepared to have done that to me?"

"No," Ada said honestly. "But I'm prepared to do it anyway. That's rather the theme of my life lately."

Veyra almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn at the edges, but it was real. She drew the first ledger toward her, opened it, and began to read.

It took her four hours to reach the middle entries. It took her five more to reach the page in her late employer's predecessor's hand that stopped her cold—a collateral note, sealed with the black wax of the Undercourt, listing souls received *against the purchase price of a crown*.

Veyra read it three times. Then she looked up at Ada, who had been watching her the whole time with the patience of poison.

"How many copies of this exist?" Veyra asked.

"One," Ada said. "That one."

"Then we have a problem," said Captain Veyra, "because in nine days I am required to report to the capital. And in ten, someone will come looking for both of us."
