## 4. Debtors' Vigil

Master Corvane was gone by dawn.

I knew it the way I knew most things—by what was missing. His chair stood empty behind the counting desk, his cup still warm, his seal ring left in the center of the blotter like a tip for a servant. Beside it, a note in his small, hurried hand: *The ledgers are yours. So is everything they cost.*

No apology. No instruction. Just that, and the smell of his fear-sweat soaked into the upholstery.

Three hundred years the Choir had survived by being nothing—a rumor with a ledger, a guild that owned no building it couldn't abandon, no face it couldn't forget. We traded in impossible things: bottled grief, borrowed luck, second chances. And we had endured every purge, every inquisition, every reforming magistrate because no one could find us to burn us. Corvane had understood that. Corvane had taught me that. My whole career had been built on the principle that the safest place in any room is slightly outside it.

He'd run anyway.

I sat in his chair—it didn't fit me; chairs never did—and pulled the ledgers toward me across the desk. Forty-one volumes. I had memorized most of them years ago, back when memorizing seemed like a clever trick and not a survival skill. But knowing the entries wasn't the same as owning them. Owning them meant the debtors would come to *me* now. Owning them meant when Captain Veyra's writ arrived—and it would arrive—it would bear my name where Corvane's had been.

Outside the guildhouse window, Ashfall was already wrong. The bells of the low quarter rang without being struck—I'd verified this at four in the morning, standing in the belfry with my hand on a rope nobody had pulled. Prayers were being answered in the streets. A fishwife's curse had come true so thoroughly that three men were dead and the fourth was selling tickets. The old gods were awake, and every contract in my ledgers had clauses written for exactly this contingency, clauses drafted by lawyers who'd been dust for two centuries, clauses that said, in effect: *when the gods resume answering, all obligations accelerate.*

All of them. At once.

I opened the first ledger to the fresh entries and began to count the coming storm.

* * *

They came at dusk, as I'd known they would—not to the front door, which was bricked up, but through the coal chute, down the cellar stairs, into the tally room where the Choir had always done its real work. I'd sent word through channels so old they barely qualified as channels: a chalk mark on the smuggler's dock, a hymn hummed off-key outside the rain-priestess's shrine, a coin of the old mint left in the palm of a man who collected such things.

They came because there was nowhere else. That, or because of me. I genuinely couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Brannick came first, of course—the smuggler, though he hadn't moved cargo in years, only debts. He filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley, gray-bearded, salt-scared hands, eyes that had looked at too many horizons to be impressed by mine. He'd owed the Choir a favor since before I joined it, and he'd never once asked what the favor bought him. He just set a lantern on the tally table and said, "You look terrible, Montrose."

"I've been eating arsenic," I said. "It agrees with me better than most people."

"Corvane?"

"Gone."

"Figured." He spat, ceremonially, in the direction of the stairs. "Rats know when the ship's honest."

Sera Vane arrived second, dripping even indoors, the way she always was—rain-priestess of a god who'd been silent for three hundred years until Tuesday, and who had apparently spent the silence catching up on correspondence. Her vestments were patched river-cotton, her hair full of grit, and when she looked at me her eyes had the flat shine of someone who'd been crying and resented it. "My god answered me," she said, without greeting. "Do you understand what that means? Three hundred years of prayers going into a hole, and now—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Now the hole answers back, and it remembers everything I asked for. Everything."

"Then you know why I called you," I said.

"I know why you *think* you called me." She sat down hard. "I'm not sure the gods agree."

Last came Wren Halloway, who was owed his own name—which sounds like poetry until you've seen the ledger entry. *One name, 'Wren,' pawned by debtor's father, 40 years past, interest accruing.* He'd been called Boy, then Lad, then Hey-You, then a series of work-names that changed with each employer, and he had come to the Choir five years ago with a fistful of stolen coins and a question: how much to buy myself back? The answer had been more than he'd ever earn honestly, so he'd been earning dishonestly ever since, running errands for the guild, sleeping in our cellars, learning our ciphers. He was twenty-six and had never once been called anything he chose.

He took the seat farthest from the lantern, as always. "Is it true Veyra has the writ?" he asked. "Captain of the Inquisition? They say she burned the Tallow Street chapter house with everyone inside."

"They say a lot of things," Brannick said. "Half of them are true, which is worse."

"The writ is real," I said. "It names the Choir as traffickers with forbidden gods. It names the ledgers as evidence. It does not yet name me, because Corvane fled before anyone thought to ask who kept the books." I let that land. "Give it a day."

* * *

I laid it out for them the way I lay out a poisoned table: precisely, with everything labeled.

"The gods are answering prayers. Every dormant contract in these ledgers activates on divine response—that's clause nine, subsections one through eleven, and yes, I've read them all, twice, in Latin and in the vulgar tongue. Which means every soul pledged, every luck borrowed, every grief bottled against future payment, comes due now. The Choir can't pay. The Choir is a filing system, not a treasury. And the Undercourt—" I turned the ledger around so they could see the entry in my mother's hand, the ink browned with thirty years, the letters pressed deep enough to tear the page. *One soul, Montrose line, sold to the Undercourt.* "The Undercourt collects."

"That's your sister," Sera said quietly. Not a question. She'd known. Of course she'd known; rain-priestesses hear things water won't carry.

"My sister," I agreed. "Sold before I was born. Held beneath the mountain for forty years, accruing interest, and now that the old gods are awake, the court beneath it will want to finalize its holdings." I closed the book. "So here is the choice, and I'll thank you not to pretend it's a hard one, because it isn't. Run. Tonight. Brannick has ships—or had, last I checked. Sera, your god is answering prayers; go somewhere with a high water table and ask for a new life. Wren, take whatever name you like, no one will know the difference. The writ comes for the ledgers and whoever holds them. That's me. It doesn't have to be you."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above us, the bells rang again, unstruck.

"You rehearsed that," Brannick said.

"Every word."

"Good speech. Wrong audience." He leaned forward, and the lantern light caught the old scar across his knuckles. "You think I came through a coal chute at dusk because of a chalk mark? Girl, I've been watching you poison yourself for magistrates for six years. Watching you keep double books so the small debtors got forgiven and the fat ones paid double. You think none of us noticed?" He shook his head slowly. "You've been the only fixed point in this whole rotting guild since Corvane started drinking. We don't follow the Choir, Ada. We follow you. We just never told you, because you'd have gotten strange about it."

"I am *strange about it* right now," I managed.

"Aye," he said. "There it is."

Sera was looking at her own hands. "When my god finally answered," she said, "do you know what it said? First thing, after three hundred years? It said: *ask the keeper of accounts; she knows what is owed.*" She laughed, wet and unhappy. "I assumed it meant Corvane. But Corvane ran. And you're still here."

Wren said nothing for a long moment. Then, in a voice like something unwrapped from too many layers: "If we run, I stay nameless. If we do this—whatever this is—maybe I get to be somebody at the end of it. Even if the somebody dies under a mountain." He shrugged. "That's more than running buys."

I looked at the three of them—at the smuggler who'd anchored himself to me without my consent, the priestess whose god had named me in his first words in three centuries, the boy buying his name back coin by coin—and felt something I had spent thirty-one years successfully avoiding: being *known*. It was unbearable. It was also, I noticed with clinical detachment, the first time in my life I hadn't wanted to leave a room.

"There's a way in," I said, because if I stopped talking I'd do something embarrassing, like thank them. "Drowned mine-shafts under Mount Ceren. The Undercourt floods its borders but keeps the old workings open—water remembers, and Sera's god owes the deep courts favors older than the mountain. And once inside, I don't need an army. I need access to their ledgers."

"Their ledgers," Brannick repeated.

"Every court keeps books. Kings, gods, guilds—even the ones that pretend otherwise. And I have read enough fraudulent accounts to know fraud when I audit it." I tapped the entry in my mother's hand. "My mother sold one soul. I intend to find out how many times she sold it, and to whom, and whether the court beneath the mountain has been charging interest on goods it never legally held."

Sera smiled then, slow and terrible, the smile of someone whose god had woken up hungry. "You're going to audit hell."

"I'm going to *invoice* it," I said. "Hell can wait for payment."

Brannick laughed—one bark, surprised out of him—and started gathering the lantern and the charts of the old mine-works he'd absolutely brought with him despite claiming ignorance of my plan. Wren blew out the candle stub he'd been holding like a talisman. And Sera stood, water pooling under her feet from nowhere, and said the thing that made the hair rise on my arms:

"They're already watching the mountain, Ada. My god showed me. There's a woman in usurper's black camped at the treeline with a writ and a company of soldiers, and she's waiting for someone to go in." She met my eyes. "She's waiting for *you*."

I picked up the ledgers—all forty-one volumes—and felt their weight settle across my shoulders like a yoke I'd been fitted for since birth.

"Then let's not keep the captain waiting," I said.
