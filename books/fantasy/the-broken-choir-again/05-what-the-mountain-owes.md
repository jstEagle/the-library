## 5. What the Mountain Owes

The guildhouse smelled of beeswax and old paper, and beneath both, faintly, of poison.

Captain Veyra noticed it the moment her boots crossed the threshold. Twenty years in the inquisitor's service had trained her nose as well as her eye; she could smell a lie in a room the way other women smelled rain coming. This lie was chemical. Arsenic, maybe. Something sweeter laid over it, like rot dressed in perfume.

"Touch nothing," she told her clerks, and then, because she was honest with herself if no one else: "Touch what you must. Catalogue everything."

They had come at dawn with writs and lanterns and forty soldiers, expecting resistance. They found an empty chair still warm. The guildmaster—Corvus Dain, master of the Choir of the free cities, keeper of debts no court would honor—had fled sometime in the night, leaving behind his ledgers, his seal, and a cup of tea gone cold on his desk. Veyra touched the cup. Lukewarm. He'd run in a hurry, or he'd wanted them to think so.

She sent her clerks to the counting room and took the private study for herself. That was where the real books would be. There were always real books behind the real books; she had learned that from her father, who had kept two sets of accounts for his ferry business until the tax men hanged him for the smaller one.

The study was small and mean for a man of Dain's reputation. A desk scarred with knife-marks. A window overlooking the canal, painted over from the inside. Shelves of ledgers bound in grey leather, each spine numbered in a hand so precise it looked printed. She pulled the first one down and opened it, and felt something shift in the room, as though the house itself had been waiting for someone to look.

*Entry the first,* said the flyleaf, in that same printed hand. *Let every debt be written true, for the old gods are waking, and they keep accounts of their own.*

Veyra read on.

She had expected smuggling manifests. Stolen relics. The usual traffic of a guild that traded in impossible things—bottled grief, borrowed luck, second chances. Her writ from the usurper had been specific: find proof that the free cities trafficked with forbidden gods, seize all records, hang the principals. Clean work. The kind of work she did well.

What she found instead was arithmetic.

Every entry had a debtor and a creditor, a sum and a term, a rate of interest rendered not in coin but in years, memories, luck, love. A fisherman who had borrowed three days of fair weather and repaid with the memory of his drowned son's face. A widow who had sold her grief by the bottle, ounce by ounce, until she could stand at the funeral dry-eyed and call it healing. A man who had pawned his own name and walked the city ever since as a stranger even to himself.

It was ugly. It was also, Veyra realized somewhere past midnight, the most honest bookkeeping she had ever seen. No rounding. No concealment. Every cruelty entered in full, priced fairly, witnessed twice. The Choir did not pretend its trade was anything but what it was. Her own government could not say the same.

She worked through the night, and near dawn, in the third ledger, in a different hand—older, shakier, pressed hard into the page as though the writer feared the ink might refuse to hold it—she found the Montrose entry.

*One soul, Montrose line, sold to the Undercourt. Received in payment: passage safe across the border wars, three children kept from conscription, and one debt forgiven that should have beggared us. Signed under duress of circumstance. Witnessed by the mountain.*

Veyra sat back. The candle had burned to a stub. Somewhere below, a clerk coughed.

A soul sold to the Undercourt. To the court beneath the mountain, the one the old stories named and the new law forbade naming—the court of the Soul-King, who weighed petitioners like coin and collected souls as interest. The usurper's edicts were clear: the Undercourt was superstition, a fable told to frighten children and excuse bad harvests. Yet here it was, in ink, in a ledger kept by the most careful bookkeeper in the free cities, dated thirty years back.

And there was more. Beneath the entry, in the margin, someone—Dain, or whoever had kept these books before him—had noted the follow-up with the dispassion of a man recording rainfall:

*Note: Undercourt confirms receipt. Interest accrues. See Crown Ledger, ref. Ashfall.*

*Crown Ledger.* Veyra turned pages with hands that had gone very steady, the way her hands always went steady when the ground began to move. There was a cross-reference index at the back of the volume, and under *Ashfall* she found it: a second ledger, thinner, bound in black, locked in the bottom drawer of the desk. The key was in the inkwell. Of course it was. Dain had left everything exactly where a careful searcher would find it, and Veyra was beginning to understand that this too was a message.

She unlocked the drawer and drew out the black ledger, and the smell of it was different—colder, like the air off a glacier. The entries inside were not in Dain's hand at all. They were older. Some were in scripts she did not know, and some were in no script at all, characters that seemed to rearrange themselves when she looked away and settle again, sullenly, when she looked back.

But the recent pages were legible. The recent pages were in a fine, aristocratic hand she recognized, because she had seen it on a hundred execution warrants.

The usurper's own.

*Received of the Undercourt: one crown, forged before the flood, provenance of the deep places. Given in collateral: souls of the field of Ashfall, being four hundred and eleven, terms of redemption unstated.*

Veyra read it three times. Four hundred and eleven souls. She knew the field of Ashfall. Everyone did—it was the battlefield where the old king had made his last stand, where the usurper's armies had won their throne. The dead of Ashfall were martyrs in the official histories, heroes of the founding, mourned in annual ceremony with black banners and tolling bells.

Mourned. Not buried. *Collateral.*

She thought of the ceremonies. She had stood at them, in dress uniform, sword drawn in salute while the priests sang the names of the honored dead. Had any of those names been on this page? She turned back, and yes—there, in the long list, a name she knew personally. Sergeant Ellery Vance, who had served under her first command, who had died holding the ford at Ashfall so the rest of the company could retreat. Veyra had carried the news to his mother herself. She remembered the woman's face. She remembered promising the death had meant something.

His soul was in a drawer beneath a mountain, held against a crown's good behavior.

Veyra closed the ledger. Opened it again. Closed it. Her reflection stared back at her from the window glass, grey-faced in the pre-dawn light, and she made herself look at it.

Here was the shape of it, plain as a balance sheet: the law she had served for twenty years, the law that sent her to burn guildhouses and hang god-traders, was itself the largest debtor in the book. The usurper had bought his throne from the very court his edicts forbade. Every order she had ever executed in the name of suppressing the old powers had been, in truth, collections enforcement—a man protecting his collateral. And now the old gods were answering prayers again, which meant the contracts were live again, which meant the Soul-King would come looking for what he was owed.

Which meant the summons that had brought her here was never about the Choir at all.

She thought of the writ in her breast pocket, the usurper's seal warm against her ribs. *Find proof the free cities traffic with forbidden gods.* She had found it. Just not in the direction the seal intended. The proof ran uphill, all the way to the palace, all the way to the head it sat upon.

Her clerks would be waking soon. They would catalogue the grey ledgers and write their reports, and the reports would go up the chain, and somewhere between here and the capital the truth would be trimmed and folded and filed until only the useful lies remained. That was how the machine worked. She had been a gear in it for twenty years and had called the turning purpose.

Veyra wrapped the black ledger in oilcloth and put it inside her coat, against the writ, seal to seal. Two documents, pressed together like opposing counsel. One of them was going to have to burn.

She blew out the candle. In the grey light, the guildhouse looked less like a den of thieves than like a courthouse after hours—dusty, tired, waiting for someone to reopen the dockets. On the desk, beside the cold cup of tea, lay a single sheet of paper she had missed, or that had perhaps chosen its moment. A note, unsigned, in the printed hand from the flyleaf:

*If you are reading this, Captain, you have found the Crown Ledger, and you are now the most dangerous person in the free cities. The guildmaster sends his regrets. The ledgers are yours. So is the debt.*

*P.S. — Ask yourself why a man who flees leaves his books behind. Some evidence is meant to travel.*

Veyra read it twice and laughed, once, without humor. Dain hadn't fled. Dain had *delivered*. The whole abandoned guildhouse was a package addressed to whoever came looking with honest eyes—and the usurper had sent her, because she was thorough, because she was loyal, because loyalty was the one corruption no one ever checked for.

Outside, the canal mist was burning off, and far to the east, past the rooftops, she could see the road. Dust on it. Columns of dust. The vanguard was moving early, and moving fast, and the orders in her pocket said that when it arrived, she was to fire the guildhouse and everything in it.

Everything in it except what she now carried against her heart.

Veyra settled her sword belt, squared her shoulders, and gave the orders that would send her clerks home ahead of schedule. Then she stood alone in the empty study, listening to the distant drums of an army marching on a lie, and understood that she had already chosen. She just hadn't yet admitted it out loud.

There was, she reflected, a name for people who burned evidence for the crown. And another name, an older one, for people who kept faith with the dead.

Sergeant Ellery Vance had held a ford so others could cross. It occurred to her, with terrible clarity, that she was standing in a doorway of much the same kind—and that somewhere beneath the mountain, in a cold drawer in a colder court, he was waiting to see what she would do.

She picked up the oilcloth bundle, and went to find a horse.
