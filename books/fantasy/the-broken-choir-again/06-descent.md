## 6. Descent

The rain-priestess's name was Sef, and she smelled like a storm about to happen.

"You'll want to breathe shallow," she said, standing at the mouth of the old Adit Nine, where the mountain's throat opened into the dark. "The water down there remembers being rain. It gets sentimental."

Ada adjusted the pack on her shoulders. In it: three days of bread, a hooded lantern, a copy of her mother's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and a lead vial she had bought from a smuggler named Corvo for more money than she'd earned in two years of tasting poison. The vial was empty. It would not stay empty, if the gods were kind.

"And you?" Ada asked. "Why come with me? Your debt was discharged. I checked. Twice."

Sef smiled, and lightning flickered somewhere behind her eyes, or perhaps just behind them in the mine-shaft, a trick of Sef's lantern. "The rain doesn't keep accounts, taster. It only falls on everyone equally. That's the whole theology." She turned toward the dark. "Besides. Someone has to teach you how to drown properly."

* * *

The shafts had been drowned for sixty years, ever since the Vein Flood took the lower workings and the mining companies wrote off everything below the third level as a loss. The Choir, naturally, had never written anything off. Impossible things kept their value underground.

They waded in to their thighs, then their waists, then—where the tunnel dipped beneath a fallen beam—they swam, Sef pulling Ada along by the wrist with a grip like river current.

"Breathe now," Sef murmured, and pressed her palm flat against Ada's sternum. "Small sips. The water will hold you if you let it."

Ada did not believe this. Ada believed in arsenic and antimony and the precise weight of honey cake before a magistrate's dinner. But she breathed as instructed, and when the black water closed over her head it did feel—briefly, treacherously—like being held.

They surfaced in a cathedral of stone. The lantern found veins of ore running through the walls like handwriting, and water falling somewhere in the dark with the patience of centuries.

"How far?" Ada asked. Her voice came back at her three times.

"Down is not a distance here," Sef said. "It's a direction of intent. Keep walking and want it enough."

Ada wanted it. She thought of the entry in her mother's hand—*One soul, Montrose line, sold to the Undercourt*—and the way the ink had browned with age but never faded, as if the ledger itself refused to let it go. She thought of Liss, who had died at nine years old of a fever that should have taken anyone, and who had instead been collected. Sold decades ago by a mother who could not pay her own debts any other way.

Ada had spent her whole life making herself small so that no one would look too closely at what she carried. Now she was walking into the one court in the world where everything she carried would be weighed.

Good, she thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. Let them weigh me.

* * *

They walked for what felt like a day and might have been an hour. The tunnels changed character the deeper they went—the rough-hewn stone giving way to something smoother, then to something that was not stone at all but compressed silence, walls that swallowed the sound of their footsteps and gave nothing back.

"Stop talking," Sef said quietly. "We're in the ledger now."

Ada understood what she meant when the first petitioner passed them going up: a man made mostly of shadow and unpaid promises, climbing the flooded stairs with the dogged patience of someone who had been walking for a very long time. He did not see them. Petitioners descending rarely saw each other; grief is a private country.

Then the dark ahead turned silver, and they stepped out onto a gallery overlooking the court.

It was vast beyond sense—a cavern where the ceiling should have been, there was only depth going up, studded with lights that might have been souls or might have been stars or might have been both, since down here the distinction was apparently negotiable. At the center stood a set of scales the size of a ship's mast, and upon those scales petitioners were being weighed. Not judged. Weighed. A clerk-thing with too many hands recorded the results in a book that Ada's professional eye estimated at forty thousand pages.

And on a throne of fused coinage sat the Soul-King.

He was smaller than she expected. That was the first obscenity of him—not his crown of black iron, not the chains of contracts that draped from his shoulders like ermine, but his ordinariness. He looked like a magistrate. He looked like every man who had ever signed something without reading it, elevated to eternity.

"Wait here," Sef said. "I'm rain. I don't have standing."

"I don't have standing either."

"No," Sef agreed. "But you have collateral. Go be worth something."

* * *

The floor of the court was cold and smooth as a ledger page. Ada crossed it alone, her wet boots printing small dark marks that a clerk-thing followed behind her, blotting them up with something like distaste.

When she reached the foot of the throne, the Soul-King looked at her the way she imagined a moneylender looks at a stranger's coat—assessing fabric, assessing lining, assessing what could be gotten.

"A petitioner," he said. His voice was dry paper. "Above ground they tell stories about me. Fire and teeth. And still they come down, one after another, wanting things."

"I don't want a thing," Ada said. "I want an entry corrected."

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Interest, maybe. Or appetite.

"Speak."

"My name is Ada Montrose. My mother sold my sister's soul to this court thirty-one years ago. Entry seven hundred twelve, Montrose line, in a hand I'd know anywhere because I learned my letters copying it." She unwrapped the oilcloth and held up the ledger. "I've audited your intake records against my family's accounts. I'm prepared to discuss terms for the soul's release."

The Soul-King laughed. It was not a kind sound, but it was not unkind either—it was the sound of a man who had heard ten thousand petitions open with bravado and enjoyed counting how many ended there.

"Terms," he said. "You come into my court with a mortal book and speak to me of terms. What could you possibly offer that I do not already own? I hold the mortgages on kingdoms, taster. I hold the usurper's crown in escrow. What are you?"

Ada's heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt. She let it. Fear was just another currency; she had spent her life handling substances that could kill her, and the trick was always the same—you did not pretend the danger away, you priced it in.

"I'm proof against every poison known," she said. "Arsenic, antimony, belladonna, the grey powders, the sweet ones. Twenty years of daily doses. My body is a ledger of survived toxins, and there is no other like it in creation. You deal in impossible things, Your Majesty. So do I. I am one."

The scales above them shifted, though no one stood upon them. The clerk-things went still, all their many hands paused mid-entry.

"Immunity," the Soul-King said slowly, tasting the word. "A body that cannot be poisoned. Do you know what that's worth below?"

"I know exactly what it's worth," Ada said. "That's why I'm offering it as collateral. My sister's soul for my body's service. You weigh petitioners like coin—so weigh me. Put me on the scale against her entry and see which side falls."

"And if you lose?"

"Then you get both of us," Ada said. "Principal and interest. I've read your contracts, Your Majesty. I know how you write them."

For a long moment the Soul-King said nothing. Above them, the false stars burned. Somewhere in the deep water, rain that remembered being rain waited patiently for a priestess who had promised to wait.

Then the king raised one ordinary hand, and the great scales descended, and a clerk-thing gestured Ada toward the pan with all its hands at once.

"Then let us do arithmetic," the Soul-King said, and his voice had gone hungry. "Step up, taster. Let's see what twenty years of poison has made you worth."

Ada stepped onto the scale—and felt the whole court lean in to read her weight.
