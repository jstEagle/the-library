## 7. The Fraudulent Ledger

The Soul-King's counting house had no windows, and it did not need them. Light came from the ledgers themselves—every open book on every shelf shed a faint glow, the color of the soul recorded inside. Gold for the contented. Blue for the grieving. A sickly green, in one corner cabinet, that Ada decided not to ask about.

She stood on a floor of polished slate before a desk the size of a barge, and behind the desk sat the Soul-King.

He was smaller than she'd expected. That was the first fraud, she thought. All that terror, all those prayers ground out over centuries by people begging the mountain not to notice them, and the thing they feared was a narrow man in a grey coat with ink-stained fingers and eyes like two drops of ledger-light. He looked like what he was: an accountant. The most dangerous kind of king.

"You offered your body as collateral," he said. His voice was dry paper turning. "An immunity to poison. Curious collateral. Worthless to me dead, useless to me alive. You have brought me nothing."

"I've brought you an audit," Ada said.

Somewhere in the dark of the shelves, a book glowed a little brighter. She chose to take that as encouragement.

The Soul-King folded his hands. "The Undercourt does not submit to audit."

"Everything submits to audit," Ada said. "That's the whole point of keeping books. If you didn't want them read, you'd have burned them. Instead you've kept perfect records for three thousand years." She set her satchel on the desk and began unpacking it with the unhurried care of a woman laying out instruments. "Which means you're bound by them. Not by law—the law is whatever you say it is, down here. By arithmetic. Numbers don't flatter kings. They only balance or they don't."

"And if they don't?"

"Then you've been holding stolen goods," Ada said, "and I imagine a king who collects souls as interest cannot afford to be caught holding stolen goods. Your whole court runs on the principle that a debt honestly incurred must be honored. Break that principle once, publicly, and every soul in these shelves has grounds to walk out."

The silence that followed had weight. In it, Ada heard the faint sound she'd been hearing since she entered the court and had been pretending not to hear: a whispering from the shelves, thousands of souls murmuring in their bindings like sleepers talking in their dreams. Listening.

"Show me," said the Soul-King.

* * *

He gave her a clerk's stool, which she took as either courtesy or contempt, and he gave her the Montrose file, which she took as surrender he hadn't yet admitted to.

The contract was beautiful. That was her first professional assessment, and she hated how much she admired it. Vellum that drank light. Her mother's handwriting—younger than Ada had ever known it, the letters still learning their shapes—and beneath it, the seal of the Undercourt pressed deep enough to bite through three pages.

*One soul, Montrose line, second daughter. Consideration rendered: passage through the famine winter, five years' harvest, and the mending of a broken marriage vow.*

Second daughter. Not eldest. Not "the child born after."

Ada laid the contract flat and made herself read it four times without letting her hands shake. Grief wanted the room; she posted it at the door like an unwelcome creditor. Later. There would be time to feel this later, if there was a later, and grief could wait its turn because grief, unlike debt, kept no interest.

"Read the consideration clause aloud," the Soul-King said.

"Passage through the famine winter," Ada recited. "Five years' harvest. The mending of a broken marriage vow." She looked up. "My mother wasn't selling my sister. She was buying the family's survival. I know how that reads down here—I've read ten thousand contracts just like it upstairs. Desperate people, fair terms, freely signed." She turned the vellum over. "But a contract is only as good as the seller's title. And my mother did not own what she sold."

"She was the mother. Mothers hold their children's souls in trust until majority. Standard doctrine."

"Standard doctrine," Ada agreed, "in courts where trust means the holder can't dispose of the asset for personal gain. But my mother had already disposed of it. Before this contract. Before the famine winter." She drew a folded paper from her coat—the one she'd carried down drowned mine-shafts, wrapped in oilcloth, drier than any document had a right to be. "Do you know what else answers prayers now that the old gods are back? Their records do. I asked the rain-priestess to petition the river-god for every vow ever sworn at his ford. He answered. The gods keep books too, Your Majesty. They just don't charge interest."

She unfolded the paper and smoothed it on the desk beside the contract. Two documents, side by side. Two dates.

"This is a pawn-ticket," Ada said. "Sworn at the river-god's ford, witnessed by his current, sealed in his memory—which is now, conveniently, available testimony. Dated eleven months before my mother signed yours. It reads: *One soul, Montrose line, second daughter, pledged to the River Beneath the Ford against repayment of a debt of bread.*"

The Soul-King did not move. But every book on the eastern wall brightened at once, a ripple of gold and blue running down the shelves like wind through wheat, and Ada understood that the whispering had stopped. Every soul in the room was listening now.

"Pledged," the Soul-King said carefully, "is not sold."

"No," Ada agreed. "Pledged is better for me. Pledged means she never had clear title to sell. You can't sell what you've already pawned; you can only sell what you still hold. My mother held nothing but a redemption right—a promise that the soul would come back to her if the bread-debt was repaid. And here—" she tapped the pawn-ticket's final line— "*Redemption price: one honest apology, spoken at the ford, by the debtor or her blood.* Which was never paid. So the redemption right lapsed into the river's holding by default, per the terms written here, in plain language, by a god who apparently writes clearer contracts than you do."

Something flickered across the Soul-King's face. It might have been anger. It might, Ada thought with a strange lurch, have been delight. She had met men like him above ground—masters of guilds, magistrates, tax-collectors—who were bored by everything except the moment someone found a real flaw in a real document. The flaw was the only thing that made the work worth doing.

"Continue," he said.

"The consequence is simple. On the date of your contract, my mother possessed no title to transfer. She purported to sell a soul that was already held in pledge elsewhere. Your court accepted delivery of stolen goods." She let that sit. "You didn't steal her. I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have. You bought in good faith—or as close to good faith as this court gets. But possession in good faith doesn't create title. Ask any magistrate in the free cities. Ask your own ledgers, which is the same thing. Somewhere in this room is the book that says so."

"It does," the Soul-King said quietly.

The whispering surged.

He rose—he was shorter standing than sitting, which felt like the last small fraud being corrected—and walked to a shelf near the center of the room, and took down a book that glowed no color at all, a grey book, a book of rules rather than souls. He laid it open on the desk and turned it toward her without a word.

Ada read. *No instrument conveys more than the conveyor holds. Goods taken without title remain goods of the true owner, wheresoever found, and shall be restored upon proof, costs borne by the holder.*

"Costs borne by the holder," she repeated. "Meaning you pay the redemption price. The apology at the ford. Or its equivalent value, assessed by—"

"By me," the Soul-King said. "I assess all values. It is the one power I have left that matters." He looked at her for a long moment, and the ledger-light in his eyes did something she had never seen a creditor's eyes do: it dimmed, the way a man's eyes dim when he sets down a weight. "Three thousand years I have weighed petitioners like coin. Do you know how many have audited me?"

"How many?"

"One." He closed the grey book. "You are the first person in the history of this court to come down here and check my work."

* * *

They released Wren's soul at the third hour of the court's night, which was indistinguishable from every other hour, in a ceremony that consisted almost entirely of paperwork.

Ada had expected—she admitted it to herself now—thunder. Chains breaking. Some vast groan of the mountain. Instead a clerk brought a lead vial the size of a thumb, glowing a soft stubborn gold, and the Soul-King signed a release in triplicate, and the shelves along the eastern wall sighed all together, a sound like a congregation sitting down.

"Your sister," the Soul-King said, "was never supposed to be here. I want that recorded. The fault lies with a woman who sold what she didn't own to save what she couldn't save otherwise, and with a court that checked nothing, because no one had ever asked us to." He held out the vial. "Take her up. Let her choose what she becomes. That is the one term of release I add of my own authority: no one may bind her again. Not a god. Not a guild. Not a sister."

"Not a sister," Ada repeated, and took the vial. It was warm. Of course it was warm. "I'll write it in my ledger tonight. First entry on a fresh page."

"One more thing." The Soul-King stood very straight, a narrow man in a grey coat holding himself like a man about to say something expensive. "The usurper's armies reach your city in two days. And the crown he wears—" something moved behind his eyes, quick and cold, the accountant surfacing again— "was purchased from this court. Collateralized. I hold the receipts, and the payments stopped eight years ago, and I do not forgive debts, even to kings. Especially to kings."

Ada went still. "You're telling me the war upstairs—"

"I'm telling you," the Soul-King said, "that when you surface, you will find the world already burning. And I am telling you where the fire's first spark was purchased." He turned back to his desk, already reaching for the next file, an accountant to the last. "Go. Climb fast. And Montrose—if anyone asks how you beat me, tell them the truth. Tell them it was arithmetic."

She climbed. Through the drowned shafts, past the rain-priestess waiting at the waterline with her lantern lit, up and up through stone that smelled of smoke now instead of water, and when at last Ada broke the surface into night air, the sky over the free cities was the color of the green-lit books in the corner cabinet.

The city was burning. And in her hand, warm as a heartbeat, her sister waited to be born.
