## 8. Armies at the Gates

Veyra had burned a hundred buildings in her career, and she knew the arithmetic of fire the way other soldiers knew drill. Wind from the east. Tile roofs, dry after a summer of withheld rain. The guildhouse would go up like a struck match and take the block with it, and by morning there would be nothing left to audit.

The order sat in her gauntlet, sealed with the usurper's black wax. *Burn the Choir's records. Leave no ledger standing.*

She had read it four times on the ride down from the ridge, and each time her eye snagged on the same word. *Records.* Not the debtors. Not the people. The usurper did not fear what the Choir knew about the city's griefs and gambles. He feared what it knew about him.

She dismounted in the guildhouse square, where the last of the debtors' vigil still smoldered in ash rings—candle stubs, prayer-scraps, the residue of desperate hope. Her squad waited at the edge of the square, torches lit, faces carefully blank. They had heard the rumors too. Everyone had. The vanguard had breached the river gate at dawn, and the free cities were falling one bell-tower at a time.

"Captain," said Sergeant Ollis, "the master's chamber is through there. The strongroom's already been cracked."

"I'll see to it myself."

Inside, the guildhouse smelled of dust and abandoned tea. Papers everywhere—the debris of a guild that had fled faster than it could tidy itself. Veyra climbed to the master's study and found the strongroom open, its iron door hanging like a jaw.

The ledgers were gone. All but one shelf, and that shelf held something else: a locked case stamped with the usurper's own seal, left behind when the guildmaster ran. She broke it with her knife.

Collateral receipts. Dozens of them. Souls, listed like sacks of grain, pledged against loans—and every loan signed by the same hand she saluted every morning. The crown on the usurper's head was not inherited. It was *held*, mortgaged to the court beneath the mountain, and the payments were overdue.

Her loyalty had been cracking for weeks, hairline fractures she'd refused to name. Now it went all at once, quiet as ice giving way.

She thought of the summons she'd carried, the trap she'd suspected was set for her too. She thought of the law she'd enforced for twenty years, which turned out to be an unpaid bill.

Ollis appeared in the doorway. "Captain? The torches are ready."

Veyra looked at the receipts in her hands. Then she looked at the brazier.

"Change of orders," she said. "We burn these." She held up the case. "All of them. Every receipt the usurper ever signed. And then we ride north, and we tell the free cities exactly what their conqueror owes."

Ollis stared at her. Behind him, the squad shifted, uncertain.

"That's treason," he said.

"Yes." She fed the first receipt to the coals and watched the Soul-King's seal curl and blacken. "It is. Anyone who wants no part of it can walk now, and I'll say nothing."

No one walked.

* * *

Ada came up out of the mountain into a sky the color of a bruise.

For six days she had walked in the dark—drowned shafts, flooded galleries, the rain-priestess Sef murmuring prayers to water that remembered being sky. For six days she had carried the lead vial against her sternum, warm as a second heart. Her sister's soul. Sold before Ada was born, pawned by a mother who had double-crossed even the dead, and won back with nothing but arithmetic and nerve.

Now she stood at the mine's collapsed mouth above the valley, and the valley was burning.

Smoke stood over the free cities in columns, straight and terrible in the windless air. Bells were ringing—not the measured alarm of drills but the ragged overlapping panic of towers sounding without coordination. Somewhere below, the usurper's vanguard was feeding on the streets she'd grown up invisible in.

"We're too late," Sef said softly.

"No." Ada touched the vial through her shirt. "We're exactly on time. There's just more audience than I planned."

They descended through terraced vineyards gone to ash, past a shrine where someone had smashed the old god's face off the stone and someone else, later, had laid fresh flowers anyway. Ada catalogued the damage the way she catalogued everything: entries, amounts, dates. The usurper was running up a debt the size of a war, and wars always came due.

By the time they reached the lower city, night had fallen, and the fires had organized themselves into a glow you could read by. Soldiers moved through the streets in the usurper's black-and-gold, nailing proclamations to doors: *By order of His Radiant Majesty, all traffic with forbidden gods ends this day. The Guild of the Choir is dissolved. Its officers will be taken.*

*Its officers.* Meaning her. The guildmaster had fled north with the ledgers, leaving Ada the blame and, as it turned out, the purpose.

An alley opened onto the guildhouse square, and Ada stopped so suddenly Sef walked into her back.

The guildhouse was not burning. It should have been—she could see the imperial squad, torches, the order being read aloud—but instead a woman in an inquisitor's uniform was feeding papers into a brazier while her own soldiers formed a ring around her, facing outward, as if protecting her from something.

From the army they'd just betrayed.

"The inquisitor," Sef breathed. "That's Veyra. That's the usurper's own hound."

"I know who she is." Ada had memorized the woman's name months ago, filed under *threats, active*. And here she was, burning collateral receipts by the light of a brazier, committing treason in full view of her squad.

Two women, Ada thought, choosing the same crime in the same hour, on opposite sides of the same square, neither knowing the other existed until tonight.

She should have slipped away. Invisibility had kept her alive for forty years; it was the one discipline she'd never broken, not even for arsenic. But her sister's soul was warm against her chest, and somewhere in the burning city there were people who needed to see what was possible, and Ada Montrose was done being a footnote in other people's ledgers.

She stepped into the square.

Sef made a small sound of protest. A soldier turned, hand going to his sword, and then Veyra looked up from the brazier and their eyes met across thirty feet of ash and ember.

"You're the accountant," Veyra said. Not a question. "Montrose. The one who went under the mountain."

"And you're the inquisitor burning her master's paper." Ada walked forward, unhurried, until the firelight reached her face. "I'd ask why, but I've seen his receipts. You found the same entry I did."

"The crown." Veyra's mouth twisted. "Bought and paid for with souls. Twenty years I enforced his law, and his law is a lien."

Behind her, the squad watched in silence—soldiers deciding, moment by moment, whose side of history they were standing on. Beyond the square, boots rang on cobbles. More troops coming. Minutes, perhaps less.

"They'll hang you for this," Ada said.

"They'll try." Veyra closed the empty case. "What's in the vial?"

"My sister."

Something moved behind the inquisitor's eyes—recalculation, the look of a woman revising a ledger she'd thought balanced. "Then we have very little time, and a great deal to do. The usurper's whole claim rests on those receipts proving the Choir traffics with forbidden gods. Burn the Choir, burn the proof, own the story." She nodded at the brazier. "But if the receipts surface—if the city sees *his* signature next to the Soul-King's—"

"—the story burns him instead." Ada allowed herself, for the first time in her life, a smile that showed teeth. "I kept double books for twenty years, Captain. I know exactly what happens when the hidden column comes to light."

Boots in the street now, close. A sergeant's voice shouting orders.

Veyra drew her sword—not toward Ada, but toward the square's entrance, planting herself between the approaching troops and the woman holding a soul in a lead vial. Her squad, after a breath that lasted a year, drew theirs too.

"Well, accountant," Veyra said. "You wanted to be visible. Here's your chance."

Ada lifted the vial high, letting the firelight catch the lead, and raised her voice over the bells and the burning and the drums of the advancing army—a voice that had whispered over dinner tables for forty years, pitched now to fill a square.

"People of the free cities! Come and see what your king owes!"

And the crowd, hiding in doorways and cellars all down the burning street, began to come out and see.
