## 9. The Broken Choir Sings

The city was burning from the river up.

I came out of the mountain with soot in my teeth and my sister's soul in a lead vial against my sternum, and the first thing I saw was the free cities' skyline wearing a crown of smoke. The usurper's fire-ships had done their work on the docks. The granaries were next—I could tell by the direction of the wind and the color of the glow, a greasy orange I'd learned to read the way other people read weather. Somewhere in that light was the guildhouse. Somewhere in that light was everything I'd spent thirty years keeping invisible.

Sefa stood beside me at the mine-mouth, her rain-priestess robes gray with tunnel dust, and she looked at the burning city the way a physician looks at a wound: assessing, not despairing.

"The aqueducts still hold," she said. "I can feel the water moving under us. They haven't cut it yet."

"Then we go in through the water," I said.

She almost smiled. "You've changed, taster."

"I've been audited." I touched the vial through my coat. "Let's move."

* * *

We came up through the cistern beneath the Temple Ward, Sefa's drowned tunnels giving us a road no army thought to guard, and surfaced into a city that had stopped running and started standing. That's the only way I can describe it. For three days the usurper's vanguard had expected the free cities to do what free cities always did—bargain, scatter, pay. Instead the streets were full of people who had decided, all at once and without anyone ordering it, that they were done being entries in someone else's ledger.

They'd gathered at the Fountain of Nine Voices, the old plaza where the Choir had held its auctions back when auctions were honest work. The fountain had run dry for a century; now it ran black with ash-fall, and around it stood thousands—dockworkers with boat-hooks, the rain-priestesses in their soaked gray ranks, debtors I knew by name from my ledgers, faces I'd only ever seen as handwriting. And at the edge of them, sword drawn but pointed at the ground, stood Captain Veyra of the usurper's own inquisition, her white cloak scorched along one hem, holding a satchel that I would later learn had contained every collateral receipt the usurper possessed—and contained nothing now but ash.

She saw me come out of the temple shadows with the vial in my hand, and something in her face unclenched.

"You're alive," she said. It wasn't relief exactly. It was more like a ledger balancing.

"Barely. You?"

"I committed treason about an hour ago." She nodded at the satchel. "I'm told it gets easier."

"It doesn't," said the smuggler Corvo, appearing at my elbow with the man-who-was-owed-his-name—Tam, he was calling himself now, saying it out loud every few minutes like a man testing new boots—"but it does get simpler."

I looked at the crowd. Thousands of faces, and every one of them turning toward me, and every one of them seeing me—not the invisible woman, not the taster who swallowed poison so magistrates could eat safely, not the clerk behind the curtain. Me. My hands shook. Thirty years of practice at being overlooked, undone by one walk through a plaza.

"They're waiting," Sefa murmured. "The gods are waiting. Can't you feel it? The air's been thick since sundown. Like a held breath."

I could feel it. The old gods had been answering prayers for weeks now—small answers, private ones, a coin found, a fever broken—but here, over this square, the pressure of them was enormous, like the moment before the first drop of a storm that has crossed an entire desert to find you.

I climbed the dry rim of the fountain. My voice, when I found it, was the voice I used for reading ledger entries aloud: flat, precise, carrying.

"My name is Ada Montrose. I kept the Choir's books." A murmur went through the crowd—the books, the hated books, the books that owned half of them. "I'm not going to apologize for that tonight. There isn't time, and apologies are just debts with better manners. But I will tell you what I found when I audited the court beneath the mountain, because you deserve to know what your kings and guildmasters have been trading in while you slept."

I told them. All of it. The soul-collateral, the fraudulent contracts, the usurper's crown bought with souls as surety, the entry in my mother's hand that had sold my own sister before Wren was old enough to know what a soul was. I told them how the Soul-King's own bookkeeping had condemned him—how precision, that cold little virtue I'd built my whole life on, had done what armies couldn't.

"And this," I said, and drew out the vial, "is what fraud returns when it's caught. One soul, Montrose line. Stolen twice—once by my mother, once by the court—and owed back to no one but herself."

The plaza went silent except for the fire, far off, and the water moving under our feet.

"Wren," I said. "Come home."

I pulled the stopper.

* * *

I don't have words for what came out of the vial, and I am a woman whose profession is words, so understand what it costs me to admit that. It wasn't light, exactly. It was more like the memory of light—the way a room remembers a window after dark, the way a name feels in the mouth of someone who loved you. It rose above the fountain and hung there, and the whole crowd breathed in at once, and the ash-fall stopped midair.

And the old gods answered.

Not one god. All of them—or as many as the free cities had ever whispered to, crowding into that square like relatives at a door. The dry fountain spoke with nine voices, as its name had promised and no one had believed for a hundred years. The rain-priestess's god came down in the water mains and sang up through every grate and gutter. The god of small mercies sat down on the fountain rim next to a child and asked her name. Tam's god—the god of names, the one who'd let his be sold—stood in front of him and gave it back with interest: three more names, futures he might have had, laid at his feet like folded coats.

And into me, into the space where Wren's soul had lived beside my heartbeat for the whole descent, poured an answer to a prayer I hadn't known I was still praying.

*Ada,* said the fountain, in a voice I knew better than my mother's, better than my own. *You did the arithmetic.*

"You taught me to count," I said, and my voice broke on the second word, and I let it break, in front of everyone, visible, *visible*, and discovered that breaking in public is not the same as falling.

*The Choir is broken,* the gods said together, nine voices and ninety, a chord that cracked cobbles. *Broken things can be restrung. What will you make of it?*

I looked out at the crowd—at Corvo, who'd never once left a crewmate behind; at Sefa, dripping and radiant; at Veyra, standing witness in the usurper's uniform she was about to stop wearing forever; at thousands of people who had spent their lives as entries, as collateral, as interest accrued on other people's bargains.

"Not a guild," I said. "No more ledgers of what people owe. A court instead—a court of appeals, open at any hour, where anyone can stand where I'm standing and be heard. No price of entry. No collateral. The powerless heard first." I lifted the empty vial. "The Choir died owing all of you. Let it spend its death paying up."

The sound that came off that plaza—I felt it in my ribs, in my teeth, in the poison-proofed marrow of me. Not a cheer. A chord. Ten thousand voices finding each other, and underneath them the gods singing harmony, and underneath *that* the water in the aqueducts keeping time.

Veyra mounted the fountain rim beside me. She stripped off the white cloak of the usurper's inquisition, folded it once—it deserved folding, even traitor cloth—and set it on the stone.

"I burned the receipts," she told the crowd, in the parade-ground voice that made usurper's soldiers stand straighter. "Every proof of the crown's collateral. And I'll swear to it before any tribunal this city cares to convene. The law I served was itself an unpaid debt. I'm done collecting for creditors." She looked at me. "If your court needs a witness with an ugly memory for detail, taster, I'm available."

"Sworn testimony starts tomorrow," I said. "Tonight, sing."

And they did. Gods and debtors and defected captains, the whole broken choir of the free cities, singing over the fires while the fires burned themselves out against walls that held, because Sefa's water was moving, and Tam's name was his again, and somewhere above the ash-hung dark a soul that had been sold twice was learning, note by note, the shape of her sister's face.

* * *

Near midnight, when the fires had shrunk to embers and the plaza had thinned to the stubborn and the sleepless, Wren came to me.

Half-real—that's the truth of it, and I'll write the truth or nothing. She stood in the fountain's dry basin with the moon coming through her in places, translucent at the edges like paper held to a lamp, and she was twenty-two years old forever, the age she'd been when Mother sold her, and she was looking at her hands, turning them over, delighted.

"I can hold things," she said. She picked up a pebble. It stayed picked up. "Ada. Look. I can *hold* things."

"You always could," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it, and we laughed until it turned into the other thing, the crying thing, and then we sat on the fountain rim with our shoulders touching—solid, warm, real enough—and watched the embers.

"The gods said you'd need time," I said. "To become. However much it takes."

"I've got time." She bumped my shoulder with hers. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Nobody owns my time anymore."

Down in the plaza, Veyra was teaching dockworkers how to form a witness chain for the tribunals. Corvo was arguing theology with a god of small mercies and, from the sound of it, winning points on procedure. Sefa stood at the fountain's edge with her palms flat on the stone, listening to the water, smiling.

Tomorrow there would be tribunals, and the usurper's main army still two days out, and a court of appeals to build from nothing but an empty vial and a bad reputation. Tomorrow I would sit down with fresh paper and begin.

But tonight my dead sister sat beside me in the ash-light, learning to be alive, and the broken choir sang, and for the first time in my life I was not invisible, and it did not hurt at all.

"Sing something," Wren said. "You used to. Before."

"I was six."

"I remember everything now. It's very inconvenient." She nudged me again. "Sing."

So I did—badly, quietly, just for her—and above us the old gods leaned in to listen, and below us the city that refused to burn began, entry by entry, to learn what it was owed.
