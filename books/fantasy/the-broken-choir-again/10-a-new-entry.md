## 10. A New Entry

The paper was the first thing I changed.

For thirty years I had written in ledgers bound in black calfskin, their pages ruled in red, because red was the Choir's color and red was the color of what bled out of people when you took something from them. The new book I had made myself, in a bindery on Tallow Street that still smelled of glue and winter apples. The cover was undyed linen, soft as an old shirt. The pages were unruled.

I sat at the same desk where my mother had once sold her daughters' futures without telling them, and I opened the book to its first page, and for a long time I wrote nothing at all.

Outside the window, Sableharrow was rebuilding. That was the only word for it—rebuilding, not recovering, as if the city had decided that fire was simply a season like any other and now it was over. Carpenters' hammers kept a rhythm up and down the lane. Someone had strung lanterns between the gutted guildhouse and the chandler's shop across the way, so that even the ruins looked festive after dark. The old gods had answered publicly, in front of three hundred witnesses, and the city had responded the way cities do: with arguments, with sects, with a dozen competing theories scrawled on walls in charcoal, and underneath all of it, stubbornly, with hope. People prayed openly now. They prayed badly, greedily, wonderfully. They prayed like people who had just remembered they were allowed.

And they came to us.

Not to the Choir—the Choir was dead, its master fled, its name burned along with half of Tallow Street. They came to the Court of Appeals, which was what Veyra had called it, half-mocking, the night she stood witness against the usurper with the collar of his own inquisitors still around her throat, and the name had stuck because names do that when they're true. We had no guildhall yet. We had the ground floor of the chandler's shop, two tables, a rain-priestess who heard petitions about water rights and water griefs both, a smuggler who could find anyone in the city and was learning, slowly, to be found himself, and a man named Oren who had been owed his own name for forty years and had taken it back in chapter nine of this story, so to speak, and now answered to nothing else.

We had me. And we had the ledger—or rather, we didn't have one, which was the point, which was the whole revolution of it, small as revolutions go: a blank book.

* * *

My sister came down the stairs the way she always did now, one hand on the rail, testing each step like ice on a pond.

Wren had been dead for thirty years and alive for four months, and neither state fit her quite right. She was real the way early morning is real—you believed in her completely while looking directly at her, and if you glanced away too long you had to check. Her outline softened at the edges in candlelight. She ate everything I put in front of her and tasted none of it; food, she said, was a habit she was building from the outside in. She slept in the room that had been hers before our mother sold her, and sometimes I heard her walking in it at night, touching the walls, confirming them.

But she laughed. That was the thing nobody tells you about souls come home from under mountains: they laugh, and the laugh is entirely theirs, earned nowhere, inherited from nothing, and it fills a room like water filling a dry boat.

"You've been staring at that page since noon," she said, pulling a stool up beside my chair. "I counted. You've sharpened the same pen four times."

"It has to be right."

"It's a blank page, Ada."

"That's why it has to be right."

She rested her chin on her hand and watched me the way she used to watch me practice swallowing poison, back when we were girls and she thought I was apprenticed to some ordinary trade. Wren had never known what I did for the Choir. She'd died before I rose high enough for family to matter, and then she'd been collateral for longer than she'd been a person, and there was a version of this reunion—I'd imagined it often enough in the mountain's dark—where she woke furious at me for every year I hadn't come for her sooner. She wasn't. I still don't know how she isn't. Some arithmetic even I can't perform.

"Show me," she said.

So I told her, because I had told her already and she liked hearing it again, the way you reread a book that saved your life. How I had sat in the Soul-King's counting hall with his ledger open between us, a book older than the free cities, and found the flaw. Our mother had pawned Wren's soul twice—once to the Undercourt, once, years earlier and secretly, to a river-god whose cult had gone extinct before the debt could ever be called. Two claims on one soul. A fraudulent entry by any tradition of bookkeeping, mortal or divine. The Soul-King was many things—vast, patient, terrible in the way a glacier is terrible—but he was, above all, exact. He could not hold stolen goods. He could not keep a contract void at its root. His own bookkeeping bound him tighter than any chain, and I had known it would, because I had spent my whole life among people who mistook power for accuracy and never once considered that accuracy might be its own kind of power.

"You beat a god with fractions," Wren said, delighted, as she was delighted every time.

"I beat him with his own rules. There's a difference. Fractions are honest."

"And now?" She touched the blank page, leaving no mark, though I half expected her finger to pass through. It never did. "What goes here? What are you writing first?"

* * *

Here is what I had been circling all day, the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

Every ledger I had ever kept began the same way. *Name. Debt. Terms.* Three columns, red-ruled, and beneath them the sum of what a person lacked. I had memorized thousands of entries. I could recite the griefs and lucks and second chances of a whole district the way priests recite psalms. And somewhere in those thirty years—later than I should have, later than Wren's death, later than any excuse I can offer—I had understood that the ledger was never a record. It was a lens. Write a man down as what he owes, and everyone who reads him will see a debtor, including, eventually, the man himself.

So the first page could not have columns. It could not have terms. It could not ask, in any form, what anyone lacked.

It had to ask what they were owed.

That sounds simple. It took me four months to arrive at it, and I arrived at it the way most truths arrive: through other people's mouths. Through the rain-priestess, who ruled that a widow's petition for her drowned son's name deserved the same gravity as a petition for rain. Through Oren, who stood in our rough court and said, "I don't want compensation. I want the years back," and taught me that some debts can't be paid, only acknowledged—and that acknowledgment, done properly, publicly, in ink, is not nothing. Through Veyra, who came to us after the trial with her resignation letter folded in her pocket like a knife she'd decided not to use, and who now kept the only column I permitted: a record of wrongs the old systems had committed and never corrected, so that no one could tell us, ever again, that the past was settled.

Through Wren herself, most of all. Because the first thing I had written in any book since the fire was not an entry at all but a single line at the top of the page, and I had been staring at it since noon:

*Owed: to Wren Montrose, thirty years.*

Thirty years. What does that even mean? No court could return them. No god had offered. The Soul-King, exact as he was, had released her soul and closed the account and considered the matter balanced, because to him it was—principal returned, interest forgiven, ledger clean. But a life is not principal. The weddings she missed and the winters she missed and the smell of bread she missed and the sound of our mother's voice before it went hard—those were not line items. They were simply gone, and the only honest entry was the one that said so, plainly, without pretending anything could settle it.

*Owed: to Wren Montrose, thirty years. Status: unpaid. Standing: acknowledged.*

That was the format. That was the whole invention. Not payment—acknowledgment. A book that said: we see what was taken from you, we cannot give it back, and we refuse to pretend otherwise, and you are not crazy for grieving it, and you are not a debtor for having lost.

"Ada." Wren's voice pulled me back. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm deciding," I said, "whether the first entry should be yours or someone else's."

"Whose else?"

"There's a boy on Cooper's Lane. Eight years old. His father was pressed into the usurper's army and died at the gates, and the army owes him a body to bury and the city owes him a father and nobody keeps books on boys like him. Nobody ever did. That's rather the point of me now." I turned the pen in my fingers. "But you were first. You were always going to be first. I just—" I stopped, and started again, more honestly than I'd meant to. "I want to get it right because it's you. And I want to get it right because whatever I write here becomes the shape of every page after. First entries are load-bearing, Wren. Ask any accountant."

Wren looked at the page for a long moment. Then she reached over and turned my hand so the pen pointed at the paper instead of at my own wrist—an old gesture; she'd done it since we were children, when I worried things too tightly.

"Then write mine," she said, "and write it true. All of it. Even the parts where I'm still learning to be alive. Even the part where I eat bread and taste nothing." She smiled, and the candle caught the edge of her, and for a moment her outline held steady as anyone's. "If the book is going to say what people are owed, it should also say what they're owed *now*. Not just the old debts. Today's ones. Mine is today's. I'm owed breakfasts that taste like something. I'm owed a life I haven't rehearsed. Put that down. Put down that I'm owed a future, and that my sister is the one holding the pen, and that I trust her with it."

My eyes did something inconvenient. I let them.

I wrote it. All of it, in my small precise hand, the hand that had audited a god: her name, her losses, her standing—*acknowledged*—and then, at the bottom, in the space where the old ledgers had kept the running total of what a person lacked, I wrote the running total of what she was owed, and left the figure open. Unfinished. To be added to, page by page, year by year, as long as she lived and I kept the book.

Wren read it twice. "There," she said softly. "That's a proper inheritance."

* * *

Below us, someone knocked on the chandler's door—a small knock, hesitant, the knock of a person who has never been listened to and doesn't expect to start tonight. I heard the rain-priestess open it, heard a woman's voice begin, halting, to explain what she had lost and what she needed and how no one, in forty years of asking, had ever written it down.

I capped the ink. Wren squeezed my shoulder and went to put the kettle on, because hospitality was another habit she was building from the outside in, and because she had decided, with her characteristic quiet ferocity, that no petitioner would ever sit in our court unwarmed.

Before I went down, I looked once more at the first page of the first honest book I had ever kept.

At the very top, above Wren's entry, above everything, I had written the title. It had come to me weeks ago, whole, and I had resisted it because it sounded too large for two tables and a kettle. But titles are like first entries: load-bearing.

*The Ledger of What Is Owed.*

Downstairs, the knock came again, braver this time. I picked up the pen, blew out the candle I no longer needed—the morning light through the window was enough, and I was done working in the dark—and went to answer.
