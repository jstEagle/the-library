## 1. Salt on the Pass

The wind came off the high shoulder of Thornfell like a blade drawn slow across a whetstone, and Yara Eriksen stood in it and let it test them, as it tested everything that climbed this far.

Below, the smugglers' road bent through a fold of grey stone—a road no map admitted to, because maps were things that got people hanged. It was barely a road at all. A goat track widened by ten generations of feet that preferred not to be counted, stitched along the mountainside with cairns that only looked like cairns. To anyone who didn't know, the path simply ended at the third marker and the mountain swallowed the rest. To those who knew—and knowing was the whole of the trade—the third cairn leaned west, and west meant *alive*, and east meant the drop into Wolf's Gullet where the snow never quite finished burying what fell there.

Yara knew. Yara had known for ten years, which was longer than most people survived knowing anything in Thornfell.

They shifted the salt pack higher on their shoulders and breathed. Salt was good cargo—heavy, honest, wanted everywhere and taxed nowhere if you were clever about the routes. Salt didn't spoil, didn't rattle, didn't beg questions at checkpoints. A mule train of salt could pass for a pilgrim's baggage if you dressed it right, and Yara had dressed it very right indeed: faded shrine-cloths over the sacks, wax seals of Saint Vellin's chapel pressed into the knots, a pilgrim's staff with a hollow core holding nothing more dangerous than dried plums. Pilgrims went up the mountain to weep at the old shrines. Nobody searched grief.

Behind them, the little caravan waited in the wind-shadow of an outcrop: four mules, two hired hands from Greyhollow who knew better than to talk, and Doss, the caravan-master, a squat man whose face had been weathered into something between a fist and a map of itself.

"Storm's coming down the north face," Doss said, not looking at the sky but at Yara, because Doss had learned long ago that Yara's face told him about weather before the sky did. "You feel it?"

"I feel it." Yara did—not in any way they would ever say aloud, but the mountain spoke to them in small pressures, a hum beneath the hearing, the way a struck bell keeps its note in the metal after the sound is gone. The storm was still hours away, but its weight was already leaning on the stone. "We make the Hollow Stair before it breaks, or we don't make anything."

Doss grunted and turned to shout the order in the low, clipped cant of the roads—*up and quiet, up and quiet*—and the mules put their ears back and obeyed, because even animals understood the grammar of this place.

They climbed.

Yara walked point, as always, though their legs ached in the cold and their thirty-one years sat in their knees like wet sand. Point was not a reward. Point was a sentence the road passed on anyone who could read it, and Yara could read it the way priests read scripture—every scree slope annotated, every frozen waterfall footnoted with the year it last killed someone. There was a shelf of rock two miles on where the ice grew sideways in the wind and you crossed it flat on your belly, arms spread, trusting the mountain to hold you the way you'd trust a stranger with your purse. There was a defile where the echo lied, throwing voices backward so that a shout ahead sounded like it came behind, and more than one fleeing rider had wheeled his horse straight off the edge listening to it. Yara had watched that happen once, nine years ago, and had said nothing then either.

Saying nothing was the discipline. Saying nothing was the whole shape of their life, worn smooth as a river stone by ten years of wearing.

Because Yara Eriksen had been born with a voice that could unmake things.

Not loudly. Not dramatically—that was the first lie everyone believed about swordspeakers, fed by ballads where heroes bellowed and castles fell. No. The gift lived in precision, in the finding of the single true note inside any made thing—the seam where the smith's hammer had joined iron to iron, the hinge-point where a lock's will met its obedience, the knot in a rope where intention ended and mere holding began. Find the note, speak it, and the thing remembered it was once separate parts, and agreed, quietly, to be so again.

Steel. Stone. Memory. The old texts said memory too, and the old texts had not been lying, which was rather the problem.

Yara hadn't spoken a full word of the gift in three years, four months, and some days they tried not to count. Small things, though. Small things were the salt in the wound that kept it from closing. A rusted padlock here. A snare trap found on the road, sprung with a breath instead of a stick, so the trapper would think the wind did it. Once, memorably, a wagon.

They came around the shoulder of the mountain an hour past midday and saw the wagon, and Yara's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution, because it was *that kind* of wagon.

It sat wedged in the narrows where the road pinched between a wall of black stone and a fall of ice-slick scree—one of the crown's customs wagons, iron-banded, the king's blue-and-gold painted on its side with the cheerful optimism of paint applied in a warm capital city. Its rear axle had found the one hole in the world, and the wheel had gone into it to the hub, and now the wagon listed like a drunk against the rock wall while its driver—a customs man in a sheepskin coat, red-faced with fury—cursed at the mules and the axle and the mountain generally, and two soldiers stood by with muskets slung and hands in armpits, doing the particular nothing that soldiers do when there is work but none of it theirs.

And chained to the wagon's tailboard, under a tarp that had slipped half off, was a second wagon. Smaller. Seized goods, by the look of the lead seals stamped across its door—grey wax, the crown's mark, the little ritual humiliation the customs men loved: *your property is now evidence, and evidence goes nowhere.*

Yara stopped the caravan with a raised hand and stood in the wind, doing arithmetic.

The narrows were thirty feet wide. The crown wagon blocked twenty of them. There was a bypass—a scramble up through the scree that cost two hours and risked a mule's leg—but the storm was coming down the north face faster than it should, and two hours was a margin they did not have. And beyond all that, simple, stupid pride sat in Yara's chest like a hot coal: these were *their* roads. Ten years of learning them, bleeding on them, burying friends in them. The crown's wagons came up here maybe twice a season, blundering along the official pass with their painted wheels and their sealed doors, and mostly they turned back at the ice shelf like sensible invaders. This one had gotten ambitious.

Ambition, on Thornfell, was how you died.

"Wait here," Yara said to Doss.

Doss looked at the wagon, then at Yara, and his weathered face did something complicated. He knew what Yara was. Not the word for it—he'd never said the word, wouldn't have dared—but he'd seen enough over the years to have built himself a careful ignorance, the kind smugglers construct around useful people the way you bank a fire. He nodded once and turned his back, ostentatiously studying the sky.

Yara walked down into the narrows alone.

The customs driver saw them coming and squared up, glad of an audience for his fury. "Road's closed! Axle's broke, we're here till the repair crew comes from Fort Brase, so turn your circus around and—" He squinted. "You're not on the toll ledger. Where's your papers?"

"Pilgrims," Yara said mildly, and let the accent do its work—the flat vowels of the lowland chapels, the humble stoop. They held up the staff. "Saint Vellin's. We go up to weep."

"Weep somewhere else. Road's closed."

"So I see." Yara looked at the sunken wheel, at the axle, at the wagon's lean against the wall, and felt the familiar ache open in their chest—the ache that was the closest thing they had left to hunger. Because the truth was, and they hated the truth, that part of them *wanted* this. Wanted the problem. Wanted the excuse. Ten years of silence and the gift sat in them like water behind a dam, and every small mercy—a sprung snare, a loosened knot—was a crack let into the wall, and each crack made the next one easier, and Yara knew exactly where that road ended because they had walked among the ruins of the people who'd taken it all the way.

But the storm was real. The margin was real. And a mule with a broken leg on the scree bypass was a dead mule and possibly a dead hand, and Yara had spent ten years keeping their people alive and intended to keep doing it.

"You'll never shift it without the crew," the driver was saying. "Axle's seated. Whole wagon's bound up tight as a drum. Could stand here a month."

"A month," Yara repeated. "In this cold."

"That's the crown's problem, not yours."

*No,* Yara thought. *It's mine. Everything on this road is mine. That's the oath of it, unwritten, the only vow I've kept.*

They stepped closer to the wagon, close enough to lay a palm flat against the iron banding, and the driver opened his mouth to object, and Yara looked at him—just looked, with the particular stillness that ten years of silence builds in a person—and whatever he saw in their face made him close his mouth again.

Under the glove, under the skin, Yara listened.

This was the part no ballad sang: the listening. The gift was nine parts hearing to one part voice. Iron has a voice—it hums, faintly, at the frequency of its forging, every piece of it remembering the fire that shaped it and the hammer that persuaded it. A wagon in distress was a chorus of complaint: the axle groaning at its seat, the bands straining, the wheel rim singing its thin note of pressure against stone. And threaded through all of it, the *seam-note*—the one true tone where the axle met the hub, the place where two pieces of the world had been made to hold each other and were now, in their iron hearts, begging to stop.

Yara found it. It took four heartbeats. It had once taken them four minutes; the sense atrophies without use, like a language going rusty on the tongue, but it does not leave. It does not ever leave. That was the cruelty of it. You could starve the gift for a decade and it would sit in you patient as a seed, waiting for rain.

The soldiers had drifted closer, curious. The driver frowned. The wind came down the pass and carried the smell of snow.

Yara breathed in. Held it. And spoke—

One syllable. Barely voiced. More exhale than word, pitched under the wind, aimed through the palm and into the iron like a needle slipped into a lock. The syllable had no meaning in any living tongue; it was older than meaning, a fragment of the founding cadence, the one scrap of the Choir's great music that Yara's mentor had trusted to a child's memory on the last night she had a voice to trust anything to. *Find the seam. Speak it home. Let the made thing remember being parts.*

The wagon sighed.

There was no other word for it. The whole structure exhaled—a soft metallic breath, almost human—and the axle slid free of its seat with a sound like a coin dropping into deep water, and the wheel rose out of its hole, and the wagon settled level on the road with a gentleness that no broken thing had any business possessing.

Silence in the narrows. Even the mules back up the line had gone still.

The driver stared at his wagon. Then at Yara. His face went through several weathers—confusion, suspicion, the beginning of fear—and landed, blessedly, on greed. "Well," he said slowly. "Well, then. Lucky. Ice must've shifted. Must've shifted the whole seat, that's—" He was building his story as he spoke, the way men do when the alternative story is unthinkable. "That's lucky."

"Lucky," Yara agreed.

The soldiers weren't building stories. The younger one, a boy with chapped cheeks, was staring at Yara's gloved hand where it still rested on the iron band, and his eyes had the particular shine of someone assembling a memory he doesn't yet know will matter. Yara lifted the hand away, casually, a pilgrim patting a wagon for luck, and smiled the empty smile of the devout.

"Road's clear now," they said. "Saint Vellin is kind to weepers."

They turned and walked back up the line, and behind them the customs men shouted the mules forward, and the great blue-and-gold wagon rolled on toward Fort Brase carrying its cargo of confusion, and Yara kept their pace even and their breathing even and did not look back at the boy with the shining eyes.

*He'll tell it wrong,* they told themselves. *By the time it reaches a tavern it'll be a saint's miracle, or a trick of the ice, or nothing at all. Stories wear out on the road like boot leather.*

It was true. It was also, they knew, not entirely true, and the not-entirely-true part of it walked beside them the rest of the day like a second shadow.

* *

They made the Hollow Stair as the light died, and camped in the lee of the great cairn called the Old Woman, and the storm arrived on schedule, arriving being the one thing in Thornfell that kept its appointments. Snow came down in the wind like the mountain shaking out a grey cloak, and they huddled the mules in the crescent of the stones and lit a fire in the dug pit that generations of smugglers had cut into the rock, screened from above, invisible from anywhere that mattered.

Doss brought Yara a tin cup of tea boiled from pine needles and sat beside them without asking, which was his way of talking.

"That soldier," he said at last. "The young one."

"He saw nothing."

"Mm." Doss drank. "I once saw nothing, years back. A woman on the Fen road, unchained a bear trap with her bare hand, no blood, trap just... gave up. Told myself I saw nothing for six years. Then the purges came through Lowmarsh and I saw her name on a list, and I thought, well, I saw nothing, and she burned anyway, and I'd drunk with her." He drank again. "Seeing nothing doesn't save anybody. It just saves you having to feel it."

Yara looked into the fire. The flames did their ordinary beautiful lying, orange and gold, promising warmth the altitude wouldn't honor.

"What would you have me do?" they asked quietly. "Sing about it?"

Doss flinched. Actually flinched, glancing at the hired hands wrapped in their blankets across the pit, and Yara felt the old shame move through them, familiar as arthritis. Ten years, and they could still frighten a friend with a figure of speech.

"No," Doss said. "No, I know what you'd do. You'd do what you did today. Small thing. Quiet thing. Enough to get us over the pass and not enough to get us noticed." He turned the cup in his hands. "I just wonder sometimes what it costs you. Doing it small. Like—like a singer made to hum. Only ever hum."

Yara said nothing. The wind filled the silence with snow.

Because that was the truth of it, and Doss had come nearer to it in one drunken fireside sentence than anyone had in ten years: Yara Eriksen was a singer made to hum. Had been born—or made, or cursed, the theology was disputed—into an order that had sung whole cities into being, and had survived that order's fall by shrinking the song down, down, down, to a whisper, to a syllable, to a padlock giving up, until the gift was less a power than a habit, less a habit than an itch, and the itch was the loneliest companion a person ever had.

Somewhere out there, other survivors might be humming too. Yara had never dared find out. Finding out was how the Inquisition found *you*.

"The storm breaks by dawn," Yara said instead. "We're over the Stair by noon, into Greyhollow by dark. Salt sells, we eat, we take the winter contract with the Kestrels if it's offered, and we are nobody, Doss. We are nobodies all winter. That's the plan. That's always the plan."

"And when the plan stops working?"

Yara looked at him.

"Every plan stops working eventually," Doss said, unbothered, watching the fire. "Man my age knows that. Question's only whether you're ready when it does." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and shuffled off toward the mules, and left Yara alone with the tea and the wind and the itch behind the breastbone, humming its one forbidden note.

Far below, in the valley towns, in the market squares, in the warm rooms where decisions were made by men who had never crossed a pass in winter, something else was already moving. Papers were being copied. Criers were being paid. A hunt was being given its shape and its seal.

Yara didn't know that yet. But the mountain knew—the mountain always knew first—and all that night, as the storm screamed over the Old Woman's cairn, the stone hummed under Yara's sleeping body in a register they had not heard since the burning years, and they woke twice with their heart slamming and could not say why, and did not sleep again.
