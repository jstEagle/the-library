## 2. The Proclamation

Greyhollow smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and the particular sourness of a market town that had learned to keep its eyes down. Yara came through the south gate with the morning traffic—drovers, charcoal-burners, two sisters hauling sledges of turnips—and let the crowd's noise close over them like water over a stone.

They had dressed for it: a carter's oilskin gone grey at the shoulders, boots patched twice, a face arranged into the mild blankness of someone thinking about nothing but the price of grain. Ten years of practice had made the arrangement nearly effortless. Nearly. There was always a moment, passing under any gate tower, when Yara's throat tightened around the silence they carried there, as if the guards might hear the unsung words packed behind their teeth.

No one looked at them. No one ever did. That was the whole art of it.

The market square was fuller than a Tuesday warranted. Yara noticed that before they noticed anything else—the way the stalls had been pushed back from the well, the way the town's few constables stood in a line with their pikes held too carefully, the way people clustered in knots and then drifted apart again, murmuring. A platform had been built against the tithe-barn, raw planking still bright from the saw. On it stood three figures in the black-and-silver of the crown, and behind them, hung from the barn's beam, a banner Yara had not seen since childhood and had hoped never to see again.

A silver bell on a field of black. The sigil of the Office of Resonance.

Yara's hands went still on the strap of their pack. Around them the crowd did the same thing their body wanted to do—stopped, collectively, the way a flock stops when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"Crown business," called one of the constables, a young man whose voice cracked on the second word. "All persons to attend the reading."

They attended. There was no choice in it worth mentioning. Yara let themselves be pressed forward with the turnip-women and the drovers until they stood perhaps thirty feet from the platform, anonymous among the hats and hoods, close enough to see the crier's breath fogging, far enough to be nobody.

The crier was a thin man with a voice trained to carry across courtyards, and he unrolled his proclamation with the ceremony of a priest handling relics.

"By order of His Majesty's Inquisitor-General, and under seal of the Regency Council—" the king had been failing for years; everyone knew the council ruled; no one said so "—let it be known throughout the marches of Thornfell and all lands adjoining."

He paused. Somewhere near the fountain a baby fussed and was hushed with terrible gentleness.

"Whereas the art called swordspeech is an abomination before crown and gods; whereas its practitioners have brought war, ruin, and the unmaking of lawful property upon the realm; whereas the peace of two hundred years is held sacred—"

*Two hundred years.* Yara kept their face smooth. The peace was held by something, certainly. They had never believed it was held by law.

"—it is hereby proclaimed that every person gifted in the resonant arts shall present themselves at any crown office within forty days, to be registered, examined, and assigned."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through barley. *Assigned.* The word had a soft shape and a hard meaning, and everyone heard both.

"Those who fail to register," the crier continued, and here his trained voice faltered just slightly, just once, before steadying, "shall be adjudged enemies of the realm. Any person harboring, concealing, or aiding such an enemy shall share their judgment. Informants will be rewarded from the confiscation of the condemned."

He read it twice more, as proclamations were read, and each time the words landed a little heavier, the way rain settles into cloth. Yara listened to all three readings because listening was survival now, because every clause mattered, because *forty days* was either generous or insulting depending entirely on how far you had to run.

Forty days. Yara had been running for ten years and had gotten nowhere except alive.

* * *

They stayed for the demonstration. Everyone did; leaving would have been worse than watching.

One of the black-and-silver officers—a woman with a narrow face and the patient manner of a schoolmistress—brought forward a long iron frame set with coils of pale metal that Yara recognized with a lurch they had not permitted themselves in a decade. A resonance cage. Smaller than the ones the Choir had used, cruder, meant not to hold a voice but to pull one out.

"This device," the schoolmistress-officer announced, "detects resonance. It does not harm. It merely listens." She smiled at the crowd, and it was almost a kind smile. "You have nothing to fear if you have nothing to hide."

She walked it along the front rank of the crowd, holding it like a lantern. It hummed faintly, a sound Yara felt in their molars more than heard. Farmers flinched from it. A boy laughed nervously and was glared at by his mother into silence. The cage passed a fishmonger, a beggar, a pair of twins—and hummed on, toneless, finding nothing, because most people were empty rooms as far as song was concerned.

It passed within six feet of Yara.

Yara had spent ten years learning to be quiet—not silent, which was easy, but quiet in the way a banked fire is quiet, the gift folded down small and cold and buried so deep beneath layers of deliberate dullness that even a listening instrument found only ash. They breathed shallow. They thought about turnips. They let their heartbeat slow the way they'd taught it to, and the cage hummed past them flat and dead as a dropped rope.

The officer reached the end of the row and turned back. And that was when the humming changed.

It was very slight at first—a thickening in the note, a warmth. The officer stopped beside an old woman standing near the fountain with a basket of dyed yarns, blues and madder-reds, the colors of half the blankets in Thornfell. She was small and round-shouldered, wrapped in a shawl that had been mended more times than it had been itself, and she was humming.

Yara heard it clearly across the square, and their blood went cold, because they knew the tune.

Three notes, rising, then the fourth falling away unresolved. A cadence. Not a song anyone sang anymore—it hadn't been sung aloud in ten years—but a fragment of the Choir's evening office, the phrase that once closed the day in every cloister from the coast to the passes. Children raised in the choir schools carried it in their bones the way they carried their own names. This woman must have been a child of the orders, or a servant of them, or simply old enough to remember when the towers still rang.

She wasn't singing it as magic. She was singing it the way you hum while choosing yarn, without thought, the way a widow talks to a husband long dead. Three notes rising, the fourth falling away. Her hands moved over her basket. Her eyes were somewhere fifty years distant.

"Hold," said the officer.

The old woman looked up, puzzled, the tune dying in her open mouth. For one heartbeat the square was absolutely still, and Yara saw the exact moment understanding arrived in the woman's face—saw it come slowly, like frost crossing a window, because she was old enough to understand faster than she could pretend not to.

"I was only—" she began.

"Name?" said the officer.

"Maren. Maren Ostrow. I've lived in Greyhollow sixty years, my son is a cooper on—"

"You were sounding a forbidden cadence."

"It's a lullaby." The old woman's voice rose, cracking. "My mother sang it to me. It's a lullaby, it's nothing, it's—"

The constables took her arms. She didn't fight, exactly; she struggled the way old women struggle, twisting, dropping her basket, spilling blue and red yarn into the mud while the crowd watched and the yarn bled color into the water between the cobbles. Her shawl came loose. Underneath, at her collarbone, Yara saw it: a small faded mark, a scar in the shape of a tuning fork struck through with a line. An oblate's brand. She had been a lay sister of some minor house, sworn to service, probably never gifted at all—probably couldn't have unmade a butter knife with a lifetime of trying.

It didn't matter. The cadence was enough. The cadence had always been enough.

"They're killing us for remembering," the old woman cried, and her voice went up and broke, and for one horrible instant it caught on the fourth note, the falling one, and the whole square felt it—a pressure behind the eyes, a grief with no name, the ghost of an office of evensong that had ended ten years ago in fire.

Then the constables had her at the wagon, and the moment passed, and Greyhollow exhaled and looked at its shoes.

Yara stood in the crowd with their heart slamming and did nothing.

That was the thing they would carry up the mountain that night, the thing that would sit down inside them and make a home: they could have done something. One word, pitched true, and the pikes in the constables' hands would have come apart into rust and sand. One word and the resonance cage would have been a pile of dead wire. One word and Maren Ostrow would have walked out of that square and vanished onto roads only Yara knew, and the crown could have posted its proclamations on trees for all the good they'd do.

One word. And then the hunt would have turned, instantly and forever, onto the carter in the grey oilskin standing thirty feet away, and every soul in this square would have become a witness or an accomplice, and the forty days would have become four, and the mountains would have grown too small to hide in.

So Yara did the arithmetic they had done ten thousand times, the arithmetic that always came out the same, and watched the wagon door shut on the old woman's crying, and told themselves it was wisdom.

It tasted like the mud the yarn was bleeding into.

* * *

The market resumed. That was the worst part, or Yara supposed it was the best part, depending on what you thought towns were for. Stalls reopened. The turnip-women argued prices. Someone laughed near the fountain, and the laugh had a guilty edge to it, and no one remarked on the dark stain where the yarn had lain, though everyone stepped around it.

Yara bought what they'd come for—salt, a whetstone, a twist of dried plum for the road—with coin counted out in small amounts, haggled over convincingly. Their hands were steady. Their hands were always steady; it was the rest of them that shook somewhere underneath, in a place no instrument could listen to.

At the salt-seller's stall, an old man leaned close under cover of the transaction and said, very quietly, not looking at them:

"Heard the Inquisitor-General himself is coming to the marches. Vael. They say he's got a list already. Names." He weighed out the salt with exaggerated care. "They say the list has names on it that nobody's spoken in years."

"Sounds like talk," Yara said.

"Everything's talk till it isn't." He pushed the little sack across. "Safe roads, carter."

Yara nodded and moved on, and the words followed them through the crowd like a dog they couldn't shake off. *Vael.* The name had crossed the passes ahead of the proclamation, as such names do, traveling the smugglers' roads faster than any official post. An Inquisitor-General who had risen not by burning speakers but by *finding* them—by being, the stories said, impossible to lie to, impossible to hide from, a man who treated mercy and cruelty as tools in the same drawer and chose between them with a clerk's neatness.

Yara had filed the name away weeks ago as one files a storm warning. Now the storm had a date on it. Forty days.

They finished their trading. They bought, after a visible hesitation that cost them something real, a length of blue yarn from a stall whose owner wouldn't meet anyone's eye. They didn't know why they bought it. They told themselves it was for mending, which was a lie, and knew it was a lie, and paid for it anyway.

* * *

Greyhollow emptied of them by mid-afternoon, the way Yara had learned to leave places: unhurried, unremarkable, taking the eastward road out of sight of the gate before cutting north on a drover's path that dwindled into a sheep track and then into no track at all. By the time the town was a smudge of smoke behind the shoulder of the fell, Yara had stopped performing. The carter's slouch fell away. The mild face fell away. What remained walked fast through the bracken with the blue yarn wound tight around one fist, and did not look back.

The guilt came with altitude. It always did. Down in the towns there was noise enough to drown it, but up here the mountain was quiet in the old way, the resonant way, and every step seemed to ask the question Yara had been refusing for ten years.

*You could have. You could have, and you counted instead.*

"And the counting was right," Yara said aloud, to the wind, to no one. Their voice sounded strange after a day of disguise—too much itself. "One old woman against everything. Against the roads, against the child I used to be, against—" They stopped. Against what? They had never finished that sentence in ten years. Against the promise. Against the oath. Against the burned faces behind the eyelids whenever they let them close too long.

The cave shrine was three hours' climb above the pass, tucked behind a waterfall frozen mid-fall into a curtain of blue ice, and Yara reached it as the light went copper and then grey. Inside, the air smelled of stone and old smoke and, faintly, of the tallow they'd left here last time, and the time before. Ten years of small visits. Ten years of keeping a grave that had no body in it, because the Choir's bodies had been ash on the wind and its graves were everywhere and nowhere.

Yara knelt. Set out the salt, absurdly, as an offering—salt for memory, salt for tears, the oldest currency of grief. Unwound the blue yarn and laid it across the flat stone that served as an altar, one long spiral of it, bright against the grey.

"For Maren Ostrow," they said. "Whoever she was. Whatever she was. She remembered, and they took her for it, and I bought her yarn and walked away."

The cave said nothing. Caves are honest that way.

Yara sat back on their heels in the gathering dark, and the fear finally caught up with the guilt, overtook it, swallowed it whole. Because it hadn't been the proclamation that frightened them, or the cage, or even the wagon door closing. It was the old woman's face in the instant before the constables reached her—that flicker of recognition, of *calculation*, the same arithmetic Yara had just performed, playing across a stranger's features. She had known what her humming would cost. She had hummed anyway.

And Yara had spent ten years telling themselves that silence was survival, that the gift was a debt better left unpaid, that the world had voted with fire and the vote was final. But the crown's men had just dragged a harmless grandmother off a market square for three notes of a lullaby, and the mathematics of hiding had shifted underneath them like scree. There was no longer any amount of quiet that would be quiet enough. The hunt was not looking for the guilty. It was looking for the remembering, and everyone remembered something.

Somewhere below, miles down in the dark of the valley, a wolf began to sing—one long note, rising, and Yara's whole body flinched at the shape of it, the way it climbed and climbed and refused to resolve.

Tomorrow they would go down to the high shrine proper, the one under the glacier, and do what they did every year on this season's edge: speak the names of the dead into the stone, all ninety-one of them, and let the mountain hold the memory their mouth was not allowed to. It was a small rite. A private treason. It had been enough, once.

Tonight, sitting in the cold with a dead woman's yarn bright on the altar-stone, Yara understood that it was not going to be enough anymore. Something was coming down the passes behind them—had been coming for ten years, maybe, or two hundred—and it wore a silver bell on black, and it had a list, and the list was longer than names.

Far off across the valley, the wolf's note finally fell away into silence.

Yara sat alone in the dark and, for the first time in a decade, hummed the fourth note after it.
