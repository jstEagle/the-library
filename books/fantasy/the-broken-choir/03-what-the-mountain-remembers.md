## 3. What the Mountain Remembers

The storm came down off the high crags two days after the wagon, and Yara went to ground the way they always did when the sky turned white: up, not down. Down meant valleys, and valleys meant people, and people meant questions Yara had spent ten years learning never to answer.

The cave shrine sat above the tree line on the shoulder of Mount Cael, three hours' climb from the last honest road and six from the nearest dishonest one. It had no name on any map. The smugglers who used the Thornfell passes called it the Whisper Hole and avoided it, which was precisely why Yara loved it. Superstition was a better lock than any door.

Yara reached it as the light failed, snow crusting in their lashes, breath tearing in rags. The cave mouth was a low black seam in the rock, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and Yara pressed their palm flat against the ice and whispered the old word—not a swordspeaker's word, nothing that would unmake anything, just a word of warmth, of *welcome*—and felt the mountain answer with a faint pulse under their hand, like a heartbeat slowed to stone's pace.

Inside, the cold was different. Older. The kind of cold that kept things instead of killing them.

Yara lit no fire at first. They knelt in the dark, letting their eyes adjust, letting the mountain settle around them like an old coat. The shrine proper was a shallow alcove at the back, and in it, carved into the living rock by hands long turned to dust, stood nine pillars no taller than a child. Each pillar bore a single vertical groove, worn smooth by centuries of fingertips. Each pillar had once held a name.

There were no names now. There hadn't been for ten years. The Choir of the Nine Voices had been struck from every record, every gravestone, every memory the crown could reach—and the crown's reach into memory had been long indeed.

But mountains forget slowly. And so do those who survive them.

Yara unwrapped the bundle from their pack with the care of someone handling relics, because that is what it was: a twist of grey wool containing nine small river stones, each one rubbed smooth, each one carried from a different water. They had gathered them over years, one per voice, walking rivers Yara had never walked with the people whose names the stones stood in for. It was a poor substitute for graves. It was all there was.

They set the stones in their places before the pillars, one by one, touching each groove as they went.

"Vessa of the High Water." The first stone clicked against rock. "You sang the deep harmonies. You laughed through your nose when you found something funny, which was everything."

"Corim Underhill." Click. "You could hold a note longer than a winter night and complained about it the whole time."

"Tamsin. Brannoc's Tamsin." Yara's throat tightened around the name, but they said it anyway. That was the rule. Every name, every year, spoken aloud or not spoken at all. "You were the kindest of us and the bravest, and both of those things killed you."

Stone by stone. Voice by voice. The cave drank the words and gave back silence, and the silence was not empty—it was the particular silence of listening, the silence Yara had been raised inside, the silence of a choir drawing breath.

When the ninth stone was set, Yara sat back on their heels and looked at the little assembly of them, grey and wet-gleaming in the dark, and felt the familiar grief rise in them like floodwater. Ten years, and it had not dulled. Ten years, and they would not let it. Forgetting was what the crown wanted. Forgetting was the whole architecture of the thing.

"I'm still hiding," Yara told the stones. "I know. I know what you'd say, Vessa. You'd say a voice kept in a jar isn't a voice at all. You'd say it with your stupid beautiful certainty and I'd want to strangle you." A breath fogged the air. "But you're dead, and I'm not, and being alive has required certain compromises."

The stones said nothing. Stones are honest that way.

Yara built a small fire then, tucked into a hollow the old singers had cut for exactly this purpose, and fed it scraps of dry juniper until the smoke rose thin and sweet. Firelight climbed the pillars and made shadows dance in the grooves, and for a moment—just a moment—the shrine looked almost as it must have looked when it was full: nine voices warming themselves, trading songs, arguing about tuning while the snow came down outside like the world's softest erasure.

And because the fire was warm, and the dark was kind, and no living soul was within twenty miles, Yara let the memory come.

* * *

It had been autumn, the year of the burning. Yara was nineteen and thought themselves invincible, which is the natural condition of nineteen-year-olds who can unmake steel with a whisper.

The Choir's sanctuary at Hollowmere had been hidden the way all their sanctuaries were hidden—in plain sight, disguised as a poorhouse, its true chapel sunk beneath the floor where the beggars slept. Yara remembered the smell of that place better than any face: tallow and wet wool and, underneath, the faint mineral tang of worked resonance, the smell of songs too large for ordinary air.

Cantor Maerin had found Yara half-starved on the Salt Road at fourteen, a feral child with a gift they didn't understand and a temper to match. She had been small and round and utterly unafraid, with hands like a farmer's and a voice that could have shamed a cathedral. She taught Yara the first law before she taught them anything else:

*"A swordspeaker's power lives in restraint. Unbinding is easy. Any fool with the gift can unmake. What we swear is to keep the world whole—to speak only what must be spoken, and pay the cost of our own silence. Swear it, girl, or walk away now, because the oath is not a leash. It is a spine."*

Yara had sworn. They had all sworn, kneeling in the under-chapel with Maerin's hand heavy on their head, and the oath had gone into them like iron into a forge-fire, binding gift to discipline, power to conscience. It was why the Choir's speakers could walk through armies untouched. It was also, Yara understood only much later, why they died so easily when the soldiers came. An oath-bound speaker will not unmake a man who is only following orders. Even if the orders are to kill her.

The purge began on a Tuesday. Yara remembered that absurd detail—the ordinariness of it. Market day. Maerin had sent Yara out for flour and apples, and Yara had come back to find the poorhouse surrounded by the king's men in their grey-and-crimson, and the air above the building shimmering with something wrong.

Resonance cages. The crown had learned to cage song.

"They knew," Vessa gasped, catching Yara's arm in the alley behind the tannery, dragging them into shadow. Her face was grey. "Yara, they *knew*. Someone sold us. Someone—"

She never finished. A soldier rounded the corner and Vessa did the only thing an oath-bound speaker could do with a sword coming at a child of the Choir: she stepped between. Yara watched the blade take her through the chest and heard, impossibly, the last note leave her—a high, clear tone that cracked the soldier's sword in his fist and made him scream—but the note was small, restrained, obedient even in death, and it saved no one.

Yara ran. That was the truth of it, the truth they had never set before these nine stones in all their years of remembrance: they ran, because Maerin's voice was in their skull saying *survive, survive, the memory must survive*, and because running was the one order an oath could not forbid.

They fled down through the under-chapel as the resonance cages closed overhead and the screaming started. The sanctuary's founding stones still hummed around them—the Choir's oldest songs, sung into the bedrock by the first voices two hundred years ago—and Yara had pressed their palms to the humming stone and begged it, *begged it*, to open the way it opened for the founders.

It had. Whether because the stone pitied them, or because Maerin had already paid some price Yara never learned, a seam had opened in the floor—a crack of daylight and river-smell—and Yara had dropped through it into the dark beneath Hollowmere as the world above filled with fire.

They should have kept running. Instead, from the mouth of the escape tunnel, Yara had looked back.

The sanctuary burned above them, and through the flames, on the steps, Cantor Maerin stood with her arms open and her chin lifted, singing.

Not fighting. Not unmaking the soldiers, though Yara had seen her unmake a landslide once with less effort than most women spend on bread. She stood in the fire and sang the *Lament for the Founding*—the oldest song, the true one, the one about the stolen hymn—and her voice rose over the roar of the burning roof-beams like a lighthouse over a drowning sea, and every soldier who heard it stopped moving. Just stopped. Stood in the firelight with their weapons hanging forgotten, faces slack, mouths open, hearing something they had no right to hear and would spend the rest of their lives failing to remember.

Then the resonance cage snapped shut around her, and the song cut off mid-phrase, and the soldiers woke, and Yara saw no more because they were running again, down and down into the earth, with the Lament unfinished in their ears and Maerin's face seared into the backs of their eyes forever.

They said afterward that she burned. Yara knew better. What burned was the building. What the cage took was worse—it took her *voice*, drew it out of her the way the Inquisition's engines drew marrow from bone, and bottled it. Somewhere in the capital, Yara had always believed, in some vault beneath the palace, Cantor Maerin's last song sat in a glass vessel, owned. Displayed, perhaps. Sung, perhaps, by men who had no idea what they were holding.

That was the night the Choir ended. That was the night Yara Eriksen stopped speaking anything larger than a whisper, buried the gift under ten years of smuggling routes and false names, and began the long penance of staying alive.

* * *

The fire had burned low. Yara fed it another branch and watched the sparks climb toward the cave ceiling, where ancient soot from ancient fires made a black sky full of black stars.

"You'd be ashamed of me," they said to the stones. "Or you'd understand. I've never decided which."

Vessa would have been ashamed. Corim would have understood. Tamsin would have taken their hand and said nothing at all, which was its own answer.

The truth Yara could not speak anywhere else was this: the hiding had worked. Ten years of silence, and the Hunt had passed them by, and the gift sat coiled and quiet as a sleeping adder, and no one had died of it. Wasn't that worth something? Wasn't survival itself a kind of fidelity? The memory lived. Yara carried it, whole, every name, every song, every crime—and someday, someday, perhaps, there would be a moment when speaking it would save more than it destroyed, and until then the oath held, and the silence held, and the mountain kept its secrets.

It was a good argument. Yara had been making it for a decade.

So why, then, did the silence feel heavier this winter? Why did the whispers in the lowland towns have a new edge—*registrations, proclamations, the Inquisitor-General himself come north*? Why had the seized wagon on the pass two days ago carried crown seals Yara didn't recognize, and guards who searched with a thoroughness that looked less like routine and more like doctrine?

Something was changing below the snow line. Something was hunting.

Yara pulled their cloak tight and stared into the coals, and the coals stared back with Maerin's fire in them, and for the first time in ten years Yara wondered—not feared, exactly, but wondered—whether the mountain's patience and their own were about to run out together.

Far below, muffled by miles of rock and snow, a sound reached the shrine that had no business reaching it: a bell. Greyhollow's market bell, ringing at dawn when it should have been silent, ringing wrong—too fast, too many strokes, the old alarm pattern the mountain folk still used for fire and invasion.

Yara stood, heart hammering, and the nine stones gleamed in the dying firelight like nine small closed eyes.

Whatever was coming, it had arrived.
