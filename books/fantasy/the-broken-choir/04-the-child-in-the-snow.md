## 4. The Child in the Snow

The pass had a name once—Vethmar's Saddle, after some long-dead shepherd who'd supposedly driven a thousand sheep across it in a single whiteout—but the smugglers called it the Needle's Eye, because it was narrow and cold and it took something from everyone who went through. Yara Eriksen had crossed it forty-one times in ten years. They knew its moods the way other people knew the faces of their children: the way the wind funneled through the rock throat at dusk, how the drifts built against the eastern wall until they swallowed a man to his shoulders, where the ice grew black and honest and where it lay thin as a lie over running water.

They were crossing it now with a pack of salted eel, two bolts of Greyhollow linen, and a letter sewn into their coat lining that they had not read and did not want to. Routine work. The kind of work that kept a person invisible.

The storm had come up out of the north an hour past noon, the kind of storm Thornfell threw like a gauntlet, daring travelers to pick it up. Yara had considered sheltering in the hollow below the third cairn until it passed. But there was a window on this stretch—the ice bridge at the far lip held firm only while the cold held deep—and so they'd pressed on, hood down, breath freezing in the wool across their mouth, counting steps the way they counted everything: without letting the numbers mean anything.

That was when they heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. The Needle's Eye did not permit sound; it ate everything above a whisper and gave back only wind. What Yara heard was underneath the wind—a thinness in it, a wrongness, like a flute note played under a waterfall. Their body knew what it was before their mind consented to name it. Resonance. Faint, ragged, and coming from somewhere off the trail to the east, down among the boulders where nothing lived and no one had any business being.

Yara stopped. Stood very still. Told themselves, in the flat practical voice they used for such things, that it could be ice shifting. That it could be a bird. That there were no birds in a whiteout, and ice did not sing in harmonics, and they knew both of these facts perfectly well.

*Keep walking,* said the voice of ten years' discipline. *You are a salt-and-linen courier with bad knees and no history. Whatever is singing in the snow is not your song.*

Yara kept walking. They made it eleven steps.

On the twelfth, the thinness in the wind broke—cracked like a cup dropped on stone—and became, for half a heartbeat, unmistakably a child's voice, humming something old, before it cut off into silence that felt worse than the sound.

Yara stood in the driving white and hated themselves with a thoroughness that would have impressed their old teachers. Then they turned east, off the trail, into the drifts.

* * *

They found her the way one finds anything in a blizzard: by nearly falling over her.

She was wedged into the lee of a split boulder, curled small, half-buried already, snow building over her like the mountain trying gently to reclaim her. A child—eight years old, perhaps nine, though it was hard to tell with the cold having gotten into her face. Dark hair plastered wet against her cheeks. No coat worth the name; a wool dress gone stiff with frost, boots that had been good boots once, for someone else. Her lips were the color of slate. And she was humming again—that broken little thread of melody, barely more than breath, the way a dying fire hums.

Yara knew the tune.

That was the thing that stopped them, hand halfway extended, heart slamming against their ribs like something caged. It wasn't just any old air. It was a fragment of the Vespers of Unbinding—one of the Choir's evening songs, the ones sung at the close of the day's work when the towers still stood and the world still had towers in it worth singing to. Yara had not heard it spoken aloud in ten years. Had not permitted themselves to hum it even alone, even in the deepest cave, even drunk. And here it came out of a frozen child like water out of a cracked jug, unbidden, unguarded, wrong in every particular of time and key and yet recognizably, horribly itself.

"Hey." Yara knelt, pulled their hood back so she could see a face, kept their voice low and rough and ordinary. "Hey. Look at me."

The girl's eyes opened. They were gray, unfocused, rimmed with ice. She looked at Yara the way people look at things in dreams—without surprise, without fear, as if a stranger kneeling in a blizzard were simply the next thing that happened.

"You're loud," she said. Her voice was a thread about to snap.

"I'm—" Yara blinked snow from their lashes. "I'm what?"

"Loud." She frowned faintly, as if the word cost her. "Inside. You're all... bells. But wrapped up. Wrapped up tight." Her eyes drifted closed again. "Like a bell in a box."

Every instinct Yara owned screamed at once, a chorus of old fears: *leave her, leave her, the Hunt will be following her like wolves follow blood, every hour you carry her you carry your own pyre.* Ten years of careful silence, of the small gray life, of never touching the gift, never speaking of the Choir, never once looking back at the smoke of what they'd been—all of it said *leave her.*

Yara shrugged off their outer coat and wrapped it around her instead.

"There we are," they said, and their voice came out steadier than they deserved. "Up you come. Arms around my neck. Don't argue, I've carried heavier and complained less."

She weighed almost nothing. That was the detail that undid them, later—they knew it even then, gathering her up, feeling the bird-lightness of her, the terrible economy of a body burning its own walls for heat. She hooked her chin over Yara's shoulder. Against their ear, through the wind, they felt rather than heard her murmur:

"Don't open the box."

"What?"

But she was already sliding toward sleep, which was death's front porch in cold like this, and Yara did the only useful thing, which was to walk, fast, back toward the trail, then north along it to the hollow below the third cairn where there was wood cached under stone and a fire could be coaxed in the lee of the rock. They walked with the girl's frozen cheek against their neck and her small fists knotted in their shirt, and they did not let themselves think about bells or boxes or songs. They thought about footing. They thought about distance. They thought about anything except the fact that they had spent ten years learning to be no one, and had thrown it away in twelve steps of snow.

* * *

The hollow was a smuggler's cache and a poor man's sanctuary: a scoop in the mountainside roofed by a leaning slab of granite, floored with pine boughs gone brown and fragrant, a fire ring of blackened stones, dry wood sealed in oilcloth beneath the cairn. Yara got her out of the wet dress—clinical, quick, eyes averted, wrapping her in every dry thing they owned including the spare shirt they'd been saving against their own need—and got her between their knees inside the sleeping roll, back against their chest, sharing heat the old hard way. The fire caught on the third match. Outside, the storm leaned its whole weight against the mountain and found the granite unmoved.

For a long time there was nothing but the work of warmth: rubbing her hands, chafing life back into her fingers, pressing warmed stones wrapped in cloth against her feet, feeding the fire, watching her breathing go from shallow flutter to something deeper. Frostbite had taken the tips of two toes and maybe part of one ear; Yara treated what they could with snow-melt and the grease from their kit, and told themselves the rest was beyond their skill and always had been.

It was while heaping stones near the fire that Yara saw the scar.

It sat over her heart—or where a heart would be, just left of the sternum, high enough to show above any neckline, low enough to hide under a pendant. A seal scar. Yara knew seals; they wore the memory of one in their own shoulder blade, a brand the size of a coin that ached whenever they came near another speaker, aching now, aching badly, a low hot throb like a tooth gone wrong. But theirs was a binding of restraint, an oath made visible. This was different. This was large—it covered the whole of her small chest in fine silver lines, a lattice like frost on glass, like the branches of some tree seen from very far above. And at its center, where a trunk might rise, the lines converged into a shape Yara recognized and had hoped never to see again outside of nightmares: the Choir's mark. The seven-voiced star.

Someone had sealed this child. Not bound—sealed. Closed her up like a letter, like a vault, like a tomb. And whatever was written inside her was written deep, because the lattice was old work, patient work, done by hands that knew exactly what they were doing.

Yara sat back on their heels and looked at the sleeping girl and felt the ground shift under ten years of certainty.

Because there was only one person alive who worked seals like that. One person whose lattice-work Yara had seen before, once, from across a burning courtyard, on the bodies of the dead. The Last Cantor. Maerlin Dusksong, who had walked into the flames at Hollow Abbey carrying the Choir's memory in her own flesh rather than let the crown have it—and who had been eight months dead before Yara ever learned her name.

And yet here was her signature, drawn in silver on a living child.

"No," Yara said aloud, to the fire, to the storm, to nobody. "No, that's not possible."

The girl stirred. Her eyes opened—clearer now, focused, fixed on Yara with an unsettling steadiness for someone so recently a block of ice.

"It's possible," she said.

Yara went very still. "What did you say?"

"You said it's not possible." She frowned, the small crease of a child annoyed at being misunderstood. "But it is. I don't know how I know that. I don't know—" and here her voice changed, thinned, went frightened in the sudden way of children who discover a hole where something should be, "—I don't know lots of things. I can't find my name. I keep reaching for it and it's like... like a door painted on a wall."

Something in Yara's chest folded in on itself. They knew that feeling. They had spent a decade cultivating a smaller version of it, a door they chose not to open. Hers had been taken. Or given away. Or paid, like a fare, like a toll.

"Do you remember anything?" Yara asked, keeping their voice gentle, keeping their hands busy with the fire so their face could do what it liked. "Before the snow?"

She thought about it seriously, the way children think, with her whole body. "Warm," she said at last. "A room with warm floors. Someone braiding my hair and getting it wrong and laughing. Singing. Lots of singing." Her brow furrowed deeper. "And running. Cold. Men with..." She stopped. Her small hands had come up and gripped the edge of the sleeping roll, knuckles white. "Men with quiet feet. Who didn't breathe right. Who listened with their hands."

Hounds of Quiet. The phrase rose in Yara's mind unasked, unwelcome—the Hunt's trackers, deaf from birth or deafened young, trained to feel resonance through their palms, through their breastbones, through the soles of their feet. Men who couldn't hear a scream and could feel a whispered word at fifty paces. If men with quiet feet had been chasing this girl across the high passes in winter, then whoever had sent them wanted her very badly, and the crown's reach was longer than Yara had let themselves believe.

"Hey." Yara reached out and eased her fingers loose from the blanket, held her small cold hand in both of theirs. "Look at me. You're safe tonight. Tonight, I promise. Tomorrow we'll figure out tomorrow."

She studied them with those gray eyes, and then she did the thing that finished it, the thing Yara would think about for years afterward: she scooted across the bedroll, put her head against Yara's ribs, right over the ache in the shoulder-blade's echo, and sighed.

"You're sad," she said. "Underneath. Like the bell in the box is cracked."

"The bell's fine," Yara said, too quickly.

"It's cracked," she said, with the mild insistence of a child stating weather. "Cracked bells ring truer anyway. That's what—" She stopped. Blinked. Her face went smooth and puzzled, a page wiped blank mid-sentence. "That's what somebody said. I don't know who."

Outside, the wind screamed down the Needle's Eye and found nothing to answer it. Inside, the fire popped and settled. Yara sat with a stranger's child asleep against their chest, a seal scar silvering her skin in the firelight, a stolen song still ghosting through their memory, and understood—with the calm clarity that arrives only after the point where panic would be useful—that the careful gray life was over. Not threatened. Over. You did not find a sealed vessel of the Choir on a frozen pass and walk away from her. Whatever she carried, the crown had already decided it was worth killing for, and things worth killing for had a way of ending up in the middle of your road whether you took them up or not.

They should have felt terror. Mostly they felt tired, and underneath the tired, shamefully, a small coal of something that had lain cold for ten years and was now, treacherously, beginning to glow.

Purpose. That was the word for it. They pushed it down like a fire that must not be seen from the road.

* * *

She slept through the night. Yara did not.

They sat up feeding the fire and doing arithmetic they didn't want to do. The girl had come over the passes from the west—from Lowmarsh country, or beyond it, from the direction of the capital itself, which meant she had traveled weeks in winter, which meant someone had planned her route, provisioned her, pointed her at the mountains. Smuggler roads. *Their* roads. Which meant someone in the trade had moved her, and sooner or later that someone would come looking, or the Hunt would squeeze him until he sang. Either way, threads led here now. Threads led to Yara.

Near dawn, the storm broke apart all at once, the way mountain storms do, leaving a sky scoured clean and blue and brutally bright. Yara stepped out of the hollow into light that hurt, and looked south along the backtrail, and saw, far down in the white vastness of the pass, a line of dark shapes moving with the unhurried precision of creatures that had lost a scent and were circling back to find it.

Not riders. Walkers. Broad-shouldered, bare-headed in the cold, moving in a spread line, heads bowed—not searching with their eyes.

Listening with their hands.

Yara stepped back into the hollow's shadow, heart hammering, and looked at the sleeping child, and did the last of the arithmetic. There was one place within reach where a fugitive could be hidden from the crown's own instruments: Lowmarsh, three days south, where the wet ground drank resonance and the folk had long memories and longer grudges. Where, God help them both, Brannoc lived.

Brannoc, who hadn't forgiven Yara in ten years and had every reason never to.

The girl murmured in her sleep. Yara crouched, shook her shoulder gently.

"Time to wake up, little one. We've got to move."

Her eyes opened, clear and calm, and she said, in a voice not entirely her own—older, rounded with an accent dead these hundred years:

*"The road remembers what the walker forgets."*

Then she blinked, and she was a frightened eight-year-old again, asking who was going to carry her.

Yara looked south, toward the marshes, toward Brannoc, toward every debt they had fled a decade to escape, and began packing the camp.
