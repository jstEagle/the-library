## 5. Inquisitor-General Vael

The rain had followed him down from the high passes for three days, as if the mountains themselves were reluctant to let him go, and Inquisitor-General Corvus Vael found this flattering rather than tiresome. Things that resisted him were things that mattered.

He rode into Greyhollow at dusk with a retinue of twelve—deliberately modest, deliberately visible—and paused his grey mare in the market square long enough to be seen. That was the first instrument of his trade: not the iron, not the resonance engines locked in their padded cases behind him, but simply the sight of him. A tall man in dove-grey wool with silver at the collar, unhurried, pleasant-faced, his hair going white at the temples in a way that suggested wisdom rather than age. He smiled at the fishwife who stared too long. He tipped his hat to the crier, who had gone hoarse shouting the Proclamation and now stood gaping at its living embodiment.

*Let them look,* he thought. *Let them memorize the face and tell each other he is not so terrible after all.* Fear was a blunt tool. Curiosity was a scalpel.

The innkeeper at the Drowned Bell nearly dropped a tray when Vael requested two rooms—the good one facing the square, and a second, smaller one "for my clerks," though he traveled with no clerks. The second room would remain empty and unlocked, and by morning half the town would know it, and someone with something to sell or something to confess would come knocking on it before the week was out. They always did. People imagined informants were made by torture. In truth they were made by empty rooms and the unbearable weight of secrets carried alone.

"Supper, m'lord?" the innkeeper stammered.

"Whatever the kitchen has finished cooking," Vael said warmly. "I dislike making anyone work late on my account." And he watched the man's shoulders drop an inch, watched the small gratitude bloom, and catalogued it. By tomorrow the whole street would hear that the Inquisitor-General was courteous. Courteous men got invited indoors. Courteous men got told things over fences and across tables. The butchers of history had always been preceded by the charming ones; it was only that no one remembered the charming ones afterward, which was precisely the point.

He ate a modest supper of river trout and brown bread, complimented the cook sincerely—he never wasted a compliment he did not mean, because false praise curdled—and retired early. Not to sleep. To work.

In the lamplight of the good room, he opened the slim leather folio that held everything the Crown Hunt knew about Greyhollow, which was almost nothing, and began the patient labor of turning almost-nothing into enough.

Three arrests already made under the Proclamation. A cooper's son who had been heard whistling a cadence in thirds—a child's trick, probably learned from a grandmother, probably meaningless. An old woman named Maren Ostrow, taken yesterday from the wool-seller's stall for humming what witnesses called "the deep song," whatever that was. And a third, a drover who had fled into the Thornfell passes rather than submit to registration, which was itself a confession worth more than any humming.

Vael read Maren Ostrow's name twice and felt the particular quickening he had learned, over twenty years, to trust more than any hound or engine. *The deep song.* The peasants always reached for the same vocabulary without knowing why. Depth. Weight. Something old moving underneath the floorboards of the world. They heard a fragment of the Choir's cadences in a lullaby their great-grandmothers had sung without understanding, and some animal part of them recognized it as water recognizes the sea, and they reached for words like *deep* because they had no others.

He closed the folio and sat for a while with his hands folded, listening to the rain.

They think I am a butcher, he reflected—not bitterly, but with the detached interest of a man considering how a portrait resembles him. Even his own riders thought it, the ones who whispered about the Quiet Room in the Greyfall cells. Let them. Butchers burned the gift wherever they found it, and two centuries of butchery had brought the realm to the edge of a cliff so sheer that only three men alive knew it was there. Vael was not a butcher. A butcher destroyed. What Vael intended was closer to *harvest*—though even that word was too agricultural, too gentle, suggesting wheat that grew back season upon season. No. What the Choir had been, what the gift was, could not be regrown once cut. It was more like mining. Like cutting gems. Each voice, once extracted, was gone from the world forever—but it would be *kept*. Preserved. Put to use.

And the realm would live.

He blew out the lamp and slept, briefly and well, the sleep of a man who has never once doubted that his work was mercy.

* * *

The Greyhollow jail was built into the foot of the old watchtower, and it smelled of wet stone, tallow, and the particular sourness of human fear given time to ferment. Vael descended the stairs slowly, letting his boots announce him, and nodded pleasantly to the turnkey, a heavy-jawed man named Dolm whose hands shook slightly as he worked the keys.

"You'll want to wait outside," Vael told him gently. "What happens next is Crown business, and men have been known to see things through the bars that troubled their sleep after."

Dolm left with unseemly haste. Good. Fewer witnesses meant fewer rumors, and the rumors that spread best were the ones with no witnesses at all.

Maren Ostrow occupied the second cell. She was smaller than her file photograph had suggested, a sparrow of a woman with wool-comber's knuckles and eyes that had gone flat and careful since her arrest. She sat on the bench with her hands folded, and she did not look up when he entered. She had been expecting him. Everyone was always expecting him eventually; it was the waiting that broke most of them before he ever spoke.

"Good morning, Mistress Ostrow," Vael said, settling onto the stool across the bars as if visiting a neighbor. "I hope they've fed you adequately. I'm told the porridge here is an insult to oats."

Nothing. Her jaw tightened, that was all.

"I'll come directly to it, then, since you're clearly a woman who dislikes wasting time." He produced the file, though he did not open it. "You were heard on the fourth of this month, at the wool-seller's stall, humming a melody that three separate witnesses describe as 'the deep song.' You declined to explain yourself to the town sergeant. You've declined to explain yourself to anyone. Which is your right, under the old statutes—" he let the word *old* do its quiet work, "—but not under the Proclamation. Under the Proclamation, silence is itself an answer."

"They said I hummed," she said at last. Her voice was dry as chaff. "Half the market hums. It's not a crime to hum."

"No," Vael agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Humming is not the crime, Mistress Ostrow. Humming is merely the symptom." He rose, crossed to the padded case his riders had carried down from the pass, and unlatched it with the reverence of a priest opening a reliquary. Inside, nested in wool, lay the Resonance Engine—or rather, the heart of one: a sphere of black glass the size of a fist, veined with fine silver wire, cold enough that frost breathed off its surface even in the damp cell air. Around it, coiled like sleeping serpents, the cables and the crown of thin needles that completed the circuit.

"Do you know what this is?"

Her flat eyes flicked to it despite herself. "Devil's work."

"The devil gets blamed for a great many clever things," Vael said. "This is the work of the Choir itself, or near enough—their own principles, turned to a purpose they never imagined. It doesn't hurt you. I want you to understand that before anything else, because everyone believes it will hurt, and the believing is worse than the thing." He lifted the sphere free and cradled it. "It listens. That is all it does. It listens to the songs stored in the deepest rooms of you—the ones you don't even know you carry, the ones your mother's mother sang into your blood before you had ears. And it plays them back to me. Nothing is damaged. Nothing is destroyed. You will walk out of this room with every memory you came in with."

"That's a lie," she whispered. "Everyone says the Quiet Room takes things."

"Everyone says a great many things." He set the sphere on the stool between them, where its darkness seemed to gather the cell's poor light into itself. "Now. Here is what I know, Mistress Ostrow, and you may correct me where I err. You are sixty-one years old. You were born in Highfen, on the edge of the Thornfells. Your grandmother came from further up the mountain—from a village that no longer appears on any map, because it was one of the singing villages, before the Purges. And somewhere between her and you, a melody survived. Not the whole of it. A thread of it. Perhaps you learned it as a weaving-song. Perhaps as a lament. You may not even remember learning it. But when you hum it, the wool-seller's dog lies down flat, and the fire burns low and blue, and the woman at the next stall forgets, for a moment, what she was angry about."

For the first time, fear moved visibly through the old woman—her folded hands pressed whitely together. "I never done anything," she said. "Whatever it does, I never *meant*—"

"I know," Vael said, and his voice went soft and true, because this was the moment that mattered, the hinge on which everything turned. "That is exactly what I believe. You never meant anything. You are not a criminal, Mistress Ostrow. You are a *vessel* who was never told what she carries. And the Crown has spent two hundred years burning vessels, and I am here to tell you that I find it a stupid, wasteful crime, and that I intend to end it."

She stared at him. Suspicion, hope, terror, all fighting for the same narrow face. He gave her time. Patience was the other half of charm; charm opened the door, patience walked through it.

"What happens to me?" she asked finally. "If I let it listen."

"You will sleep, deeply, for perhaps an hour. You will dream—I won't pretend otherwise; the dreams can be strange. And when you wake, you will be moved south to Greyfall, comfortably, in a covered wagon, where you will be housed and fed and cared for better than you have been since you were a girl. There are others like you there. Others who carry threads." He smiled, warm as banked fire. "You will not be alone anymore, Mistress Ostrow. Think about what that means, for a woman who has spent a lifetime humming a song no one else knows."

It took another hour of gentle talk before she nodded. It always took an hour. He fitted the needle-crown to her temples himself, checking each contact twice, murmuring reassurances in the tone of a barber discussing weather, and when he activated the engine the black glass began, very softly, to sing.

Not with sound, exactly. With *presence*. The cell filled with a pressure like the moment before lightning, and Maren Ostrow gasped, and then her lips parted and a voice came out of her that was not hers—younger, deeper, layered, a fragment of harmony that had slept in her blood for three generations. It lasted less than a minute. It was, Vael thought, watching the sphere drink it in with its slow pulse of silver light, one thread of the Lamentation of the Founding. Possibly the third movement. Possibly unique.

When it ended, the old woman slumped, weeping without knowing why, and Vael removed the crown and held her hand until the tears passed.

"There," he said gently. "You see? Still whole. Still yourself."

"It was my gran's voice," she whispered. "I heard my gran."

"Yes," Vael said. "You did. And now she won't be lost. None of it will be lost ever again." And the extraordinary thing—the thing he had learned long ago to rely upon—was that he meant it. Every word. That was what the butchers had never understood, what made him necessary: the gift could not be harvested by cruelty. Cruelty made the deep rooms slam shut. Only genuine tenderness, genuinely felt, pried them open.

He recorded the extraction in his folio—date, source, probable movement, quality: *minor fragment, third movement, degraded but recoverable*—and sent word to the sergeant that Mistress Ostrow was to be fed well and treated courteously until the wagon came.

Then he sat alone in the cell with the cooling engine and allowed himself, briefly, the arithmetic that governed his life.

Two hundred and eleven years since the last king's men had cut the Choir down and stolen its founding song to feed the sustaining hymn that held the realm's wards, its roads, its very law together. Two hundred and eleven years of borrowing breath. And the hymn was failing—he had seen the ledgers, had heard the fraying at the edges of the Festival chorus, had measured the decay in instruments far finer than this little glass heart. Within a decade, perhaps less, the song would fail entirely, and everything built upon it would come down, and millions would die in the collapse.

Unless the song was rebuilt. Unless every scattered thread—every grandmother's lullaby, every weaving-song, every humming in every market—was gathered, extracted, preserved, and rewoven into a new foundation. The gift could not be permitted to run wild in the world; wild song had started the war that nearly ended everything. But neither could it be destroyed. It had to be *owned*. Collected. Held safely in vaults and engines, sung by machinery and bound servants, forever.

A choir with no singers. A hymn with no mouths. Order without the danger of voices.

It was the only mercy that lasted. He believed that completely, and the completeness frightened even him sometimes, in the hour before dawn.

* * *

The cooper's son proved worthless—an hour in the engine yielded nothing but a tavern round and a girl's name, and Vael released him with a warning and a coin, because releasing people was cheaper than feeding them and kinder than hanging them and made the next prisoner easier to open.

But it was the turnkey, Dolm, who brought him the evening's real prize, sidling into the empty second room at the Drowned Bell exactly as Vael had predicted, twisting his cap in his fists.

"Beg pardon, m'lord General. There's talk. Down the docks and up at the pass-road both." The man swallowed. "Talk of a child. Come down out of the Thornfells ahead of the snow, or been seen to, anyway. A girl, eight, nine years old. And the peddler swears—swears on his mother—that when she cried, the ice on the ford *sang back at her*."

Vael set down his pen very carefully.

"A seal scar," Dolm added, because he had saved the best for last, hoping to be paid for it twice. "Over the heart, the peddler says. Seen it when her coat tore. Round scar, like a brand, but old. Old as her."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere outside, the rain had stopped, and the silence of the mountains pressed against the windows like a listening ear.

A sealed child. A living vessel with the doors locked from the inside—which meant a Cantor had done the sealing, and there had been no Cantors for ten years, not since the last purge, not since the night the swordspeakers had burned. Which meant the child had been sealed *during* the fall. Which meant she carried something worth sealing. Something someone had died to hide inside an eight-year-old heart.

"Thank you, Dolm," Vael said, rising, and his smile was as warm and pleasant as it had ever been, and Dolm would remember afterward, all his life, how the warmth of it had suddenly made him cold. "You've been more helpful than you know. Sergeant Brannic will see you're paid."

He stood a long while at the window after the turnkey had gone, looking north toward the black shoulder of the Thornfells, where snow was already closing the passes.

*A child in the mountains,* he thought, *with a song locked in her chest, and winter coming on.*

Somewhere up there, if the peddler's tale was true, the last loose end of the old war was freezing in the dark. And Corvus Vael had never in his life left a valuable thing uncollected.

He rang the bell for the innkeeper and ordered horses saddled for dawn.
