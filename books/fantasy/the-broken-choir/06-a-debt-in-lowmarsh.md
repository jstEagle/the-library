## 6. A Debt in Lowmarsh

The marsh took three days to forgive them for entering.

Yara had learned its moods long ago, back when the Choir still had a name worth saying aloud—back when Brannoc used to walk these boardwalks with his sleeves rolled and his hands smelling of yarrow and river-clay, laughing at Yara's horror of the mud. Now the boardwalks were rotten in places, and the laughter was gone from the man, if not entirely from the place. The marsh remembered him. It remembered everything. That was the trouble with Lowmarsh: nothing here ever dried out enough to forget.

Merrin rode on Yara's back, wrapped in a smuggler's oilcloth, her cheek pressed between their shoulder blades. She had not spoken since the pass. Not since the hounds—or rather, since the silence where the hounds should have been, which was worse. She weighed almost nothing. Children who did not eat weighed almost nothing, and this one picked at food like a bird deciding whether the hand that offered it was a trap.

"Is it far?" she asked at last, her voice small against the wind off the reed-beds.

"Far enough." Yara shifted her weight higher. "Hold on. The boards lie about themselves."

It was true. Half the walkways of Lowmarsh were honest timber laid by honest smugglers three generations back; the other half were traps, illusions of planking stretched over black water deep enough to swallow a wagon and keep it. The clans maintained them with a system of knots tied to the rails—a red cord meant *sound*, a grey cord meant *rot*, no cord at all meant *do not be a fool*. Yara read the cords without thinking, the way they once had read the resonance-lines in steel, and tried not to feel how much easier that old reading had been. How much sweeter. How much more like falling.

*You do not use it,* they reminded themselves, as they had reminded themselves every day for ten years. *You are a person who walks. You are a person who carries things and pays for things and says please. You are nobody's sword.*

The word *sword* sat wrong in their head all the same. It always had. The Choir had called them swordspeakers because their voices could unmake what was made—steel, stone, memory, oath—but Yara had never felt like a blade. Blades were simple. They wanted one thing. Yara wanted, mostly, to be left alone, and even that want had failed them now, because there was a child breathing against their spine whose heart carried a scar shaped like a seal, and because somewhere behind them the crown's Inquisitor-General was asking questions in Greyhollow with a voice like warm honey poured over cold iron.

They came into Lowmarsh proper at dusk, when the fen-lights rose—pale green wisps over the water that the clans swore were the breath of drowned gods and everyone else knew were swamp-gas, and both explanations were allowed because neither hurt anyone. The village was less a village than an argument between water and wood: stilt-houses leaning together like conspirators, rope bridges sagging under strings of drying fish, smoke from peat fires lying flat along the water so the whole settlement seemed to float in a grey veil. Somewhere a woman was singing a work-song to keep eels from the nets, low and repetitive, and Yara flinched before they could stop themselves—at the melody, at the way sound moved through the wet air, at everything.

"You know someone here," Merrin said. Not a question. Children who had lost their names became unnervingly good at statements.

"I owe someone here," Yara said. "That's different, and worse."

* * *

Brannoc lived at the end of the longest walkway, in a house that had given up pretending to stand straight. It listed eastward over the water like a man leaning to look at his own reflection and not liking what he saw. There was a herb-drying rack on the porch, empty except for dust. There was a chair, and on the chair was Brannoc.

He had been handsome once, in the broad, calm way of people whose hands could mend anything. He was forty-five now and looked sixty, grey coming into his beard early, his big frame gone soft in the middle and hollow at the shoulders, as though the important parts of him had been scooped out and only the packaging remained. A jug sat by his right hand. Another jug waited under the chair, because Brannoc planned ahead the way he had once planned surgeries—one step past disaster.

He looked up at the creak of the boards, and Yara watched recognition move across his face in stages: first the blankness of drink, then the flicker of memory, then something harder and colder than either.

"No," he said.

"Hello to you too, Brannoc."

"No." He said it louder, waving them off with a hand that shook. "Whatever it is. Whatever you're selling or running from or carrying. No. You don't get to come down my walkway after ten years and—" His eyes went past Yara then, to the small face peeking over their shoulder, and whatever he was going to say died in his throat. Yara watched him look at Merrin and saw the healer wake up in him, briefly, like a lamp turned down but not out. Then he smothered it deliberately, the way he'd learned to smother everything. "No," he said again, quieter, and it sounded like grief wearing anger's coat.

"We need a room. Food. And your eyes."

"My eyes are drunk."

"Your eyes have never been drunk in your life. You drink specifically so you can say they are."

Brannoc laughed, and it was the worst sound Yara had heard in years, because underneath the laugh was the old warmth, and underneath the warmth was the wound. "Get inside before the neighbors count your faces," he said. "And leave the child on the porch. I mean it, Yara. Leave her outside."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to say things I don't want her to hear, and because if she comes in smelling like what she smells like, I'll have to think about things I've spent ten years not thinking about, and I am very, very good at not thinking now. It's my one remaining discipline." He hauled himself up, swayed, caught the doorframe. "Fish stew's on the hook. If you're feeding her, feed her first. Children heal from the stomach inward. Everything else is vanity."

* * *

Merrin ate three bowls of fish stew on the porch in the fen-light, sitting cross-legged with the oilcloth around her shoulders like a small grey queen holding court over the water. Yara watched her through the window while Brannoc sat across the table and did not look at them.

"She's maybe eight," Yara said.

"I can see how old she is."

"There's a seal-scar over her heart. Old work. Deliberate. Someone sealed something into her and burned the door shut behind it."

Brannoc's jug stopped halfway to his mouth. For a moment—the length of a heartbeat, no more—he was entirely sober, and Yara saw the man he had been: the Choir's finest diagnostician, who could put his palm flat on a dying singer's chest and tell you which note of her soul had gone silent. Then the moment passed and he drank, and set the jug down too hard.

"And you thought," he said, "that you would bring that thing to me. To my house. In my marsh."

"She has no name. No memories. She woke up in the snow on Coldspring Pass with a seal on her chest and Hunt hounds behind her, and she took my hand like she'd known me her whole life, and I didn't know what else to do."

"You could have left her."

"Yes," Yara said. "I could have. I've been telling myself that every day since. It hasn't taken."

Brannoc stared at them across ten years of silence. The peat fire popped. Out on the water, the eel-song woman had stopped singing, and the quiet that followed had texture, like wool.

"Do you remember," Brannoc said at last, "the last thing you said to me? Before the abbey? Before everything?"

Yara did. They had spent ten years carefully not remembering it, and it surfaced now whole, the way drowned things surface when you disturb the water above them.

*"If it goes wrong,"* Yara had said, *"don't come looking. Don't sing for me. Don't spend yourself on me."*

And Brannoc had sung for them anyway. Had spent himself. When the purge came down on the Hollow Abbey and the Choir burned, it was Brannoc who had held the escape-tunnel open with a healing-song pushed backward, wrongly, terribly, singing mending into a collapsing wall until the effort cooked something in his gift that had never healed right since. Yara had crawled out of the smoke with their mentor's ashes in their hair and Brannoc's last true song still ringing in their bones, and Brannoc had crawled out with hands that trembled and a voice that could no longer mend a papercut, and they had looked at each other in the reeds for exactly as long as it took to understand that each of them was the other's worst memory.

Then Yara had walked away, because staying meant being seen, and being seen meant being followed, and being followed meant more dead singers. They had told themselves it was wisdom. Brannoc had told himself other things, most of them from jugs.

"You said don't come looking," Brannoc said now. "So I didn't. Do you know what that's like, Yara? Everyone I loved was in that abbey. Every hand I ever trained beside. And the one person who walked out alive told me not to look for them, and I obeyed, because obedience was the whole disease, wasn't it? We were oath-bound. We were bound. And I lay in this house for a year listening to my own hands shake and thinking: I kept the oath. I kept the oath and it cost me the Choir, and it cost me my craft, and it bought me nothing. Nothing. The oath was a leash and we all knelt to it and they killed us anyway."

"The oath saved some of us."

"It saved *you*." He said it without heat, which was worse. "It saved the one who ran."

Yara took that the way you take a blow you've known is coming: by letting it through instead of blocking it. "Yes," they said. "It did. And I've hated myself efficiently for a decade, Brannoc, so you needn't labor the point. But there's a child on your porch with a seal carved by someone who knew the Last Cantor's work, and the Inquisitor-General of the realm is pulling memories out of prisoners' heads with a machine in Greyhollow, and I cannot read that seal, and you can't either—but you can look at her without killing her, which is more than I can promise myself, because every time I touch the edges of that scar with my gift it *pulls*, and I don't know what it pulls toward."

Silence. The fire settled. Somewhere out in the dark a marsh-bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and gave up.

"A favor," Brannoc said finally. "You always were quick to pay. It's how you avoid owing."

"What do you want?"

"I want you to listen without defending yourself. Can you do that? One minute of it. It may be physically beyond you."

Yara folded their hands and said nothing, which was, they suspected, the correct answer, because something in Brannoc's face loosened a fraction.

"When you left," he said, "I told myself you were protecting us. That's what you told me, and I drank it down like everything else. But I've had ten years, Yara, and the truth is you weren't protecting us. You were protecting the secret. Us, the survivors—we were just the people the secret happened to live inside. You cut us loose like a rope with blood on it. And I understand why. I do. I've turned it over ten thousand nights and I understand why, and understanding it has not made it hurt less, and I have decided to stop waiting for it to hurt less." He leaned forward. "So here is my price. Not gold. Not a favor I can hold over you. My price is this: you sit there while I say that I loved the Choir, and I loved the people in it, and losing them broke me in a way your leaving finished, and none of it—not any of it—was my fault, and none of it was yours either, and we were both too proud and too frightened to say that sentence to each other while there was still anyone alive to say it to."

The words hung in the smoky air. Yara found that their throat had closed, and that blinking was suddenly a complicated activity requiring management.

"That's it?" they managed. "That's the price?"

"That's the price. Cheap, isn't it. Ten years I could have had it for free if either of us had knocked." Brannoc wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, roughly, as if angry at it. "Now. The child. Bring her in, and Yara—if that seal is what I think it is, if it's founding-memory, Cantor-work—then keeping her under this roof isn't concealment. It's a target with a roof. You understand that. Anything that matters, the Hunt will find. The question is only whether it finds her somewhere with friends or somewhere alone."

"With friends," Yara said. "This time with friends."

Brannoc looked at them for a long moment, and then he got up, unsteadily, and opened the door, and the fen-light came in green and pale around the small silhouette of a girl who had stopped eating and was watching the doorway with an expression far too old for her face.

"Come in, little one," Brannoc said, and his voice cracked only slightly on the second word. "Let's have a look at you."

* * *

He examined her by lamplight at the kitchen table, and Yara stood by the wall and watched the transformation they had hoped for and dreaded in equal measure. Because Brannoc drunk was a ruin, but Brannoc working was Brannoc entire—the trembling steadied when his hands had purpose, the fog burned off his eyes, and within minutes he was murmuring the old clinical cadences under his breath, *breathe for me, little one, there's a girl, good, again*, and Merrin, starved for gentleness the way she was starved for food, bloomed under it like a plant finding sun.

Then he drew back her collar and saw the scar, and went absolutely still.

Yara had seen it before, of course—an oval mark over the sternum, pale as old candle-wax, fine as engraving. Up close, in lamplight, it resolved into lines. Not random lines. Script. A script Yara half-recognized and could not read, spiraling inward toward the center of the seal like text written on the inside of a closing door.

"Oh," Brannoc said softly. "Oh, you foolish, brave old man."

"Who?" Yara asked. "Whose work?"

But Brannoc wasn't answering. He had placed two fingers flat on the scar, feather-light, the way he had once touched dying singers, and his lips were moving—no sound, only the shapes of words, counting something, measuring something—and Yara saw the color drain out of his face degree by degree until he looked like the fen-light itself.

"Brannoc."

"This isn't a lock," he whispered. "Yara. This isn't a cage. Seals keep things *in*. This—" His fingers hovered over the spiral. "This is a *keepsake*. Someone wrapped a memory up the way you wrap a coal in ash to carry fire through the night. Someone trusted this child to deliver it." He looked up, and his eyes were clear and terrified and, for the first time in ten years, fully awake. "Do you know what the founding memory is? What actually happened, at the beginning, before the hymn, before the crown? Because I do. We all knew pieces. And if she's carrying the whole of it—"

He stopped. Outside, far off across the black water, a dog began to bark—not the lazy alarm of a marsh-dog at eels, but a hard, rhythmic, purposeful barking, and beneath it, faint as a finger drawn on a drumhead, Yara felt something they had hoped never to feel again.

Resonance. Moving through the water. Moving toward them.

Someone was singing on the marsh road.
