## 7. The Seal Scar

Brannoc had not touched a patient in four years, and the first thing his hands did when they closed around Merrin's wrist was shake.

He covered it by reaching for the jug on the shelf, pouring a measure of barley spirit into a clay cup, and drinking half of it before he let himself look at her again. The girl sat on the edge of his cot with her boots dangling, too short to reach the floor, watching him with the grave patience of a child who had learned that adults were weather—unpredictable, occasionally dangerous, best endured quietly.

"You're the healer," she said. Not a question. Yara had told her, then, on the road up from Lowmarsh. Told her enough to sit in a drunkard's smoky loft and wait to be poked at like a market lamb.

"I was," Brannoc said. "Once."

"What are you now?"

He almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a cough instead, wet and ugly in his chest. "A man with a still and no customers who can pay." He set the cup down. "Now. Yara says you don't remember your name."

"Merrin. They gave me that one." She said it simply, without grief, which was somehow worse. "I remember snow. And a door. And—" Her brow creased, a small furrow like a plow-line across a field. "And singing. But I don't know any songs."

"Everyone knows songs."

"Not the way I mean it." She looked down at her own hands, small and chapped, folded in her lap like a good little congregation of one. "It's more like the songs know me. And they're waiting for something."

The fire popped in the grate. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters of the Lowmarsh loft, and somewhere below, the marsh water lapped against pilings with its endless patient tongue. Brannoc found he could not move. His hands, which had once set bones and drawn fevers and closed the eyes of the dying with a steadiness that had made old Cantor Ilvane call him *the calmest pair of hands in the Choir*, would not obey him now.

*The Choir.* He hadn't let himself think the word whole in ten years. It always came out broken, the way a man avoids stepping on a grave.

"Yara tells me you have a scar," he managed. "Over your heart."

Merrin nodded. Without being asked again—without ceremony, without the coyness children were taught—she pulled the collar of her rough wool shirt aside.

Brannoc had prepared himself for many things. A burn. A brand. Some crude smuggler's mark or Inquisitorial seal, the kind they pressed into flesh to tag property or mark the suspected. He had prepared himself to be angry about it, because anger was easier than what actually happened, which was this:

He knew the hand that had made it.

The scar sat over the girl's sternum, low, just above the soft hollow where a child's heartbeat lived loudest. It was the size of a coin—a crown-piece, he thought, and nearly laughed again at the irony—but it was not a burn and not a brand. It was a *seal*. The skin there was smooth as river glass, faintly iridescent when the firelight crossed it, and arranged within it, so fine you'd need a healer's trained eye or a lover's intimacy to see them, were lines. Threads. The ghost of script in no alphabet used anywhere south of the mountains, curving inward toward the center like ripples closing over a dropped stone.

He knew it because he had watched it done. Once. Only once, in all his years of service, and the memory of it had followed him into every bottle since.

"Can I touch it?" he asked. His voice came out strange. Far away.

Merrin considered him with those gray eyes, old as winter pools, and nodded.

His fingers, when they finally settled over the scar, felt nothing—and that was the wrongness of it, the horror of it. Skin should be warm. Flesh should answer flesh. But under his fingertips the scar was neither warm nor cold, neither raised nor sunken. It was *silent*. Ten years he had spent drowning his own gift in grain spirits until barely an echo remained, and even his ruined senses could feel the absence radiating off that small circle of skin like cold coming off a lake at dusk. Something was in there. Something vast, folded down and down and down into a space smaller than a coin, held shut by work so fine it made his eyes sting.

"Oh, you foolish, brilliant old man," Brannoc whispered. "What did you do?"

"That's what I asked," said Yara from the doorway.

He hadn't heard the swordspeaker come in. Of course he hadn't; Yara moved like the roads themselves, quiet and crooked and arriving where least expected. They stood with their back against the doorframe, arms crossed, snowmelt dripping from their cloak onto his floorboards, watching him touch the girl's chest with an expression Brannoc couldn't read—or perhaps could read too well, because it was his own expression reflected back at him. Fear wearing patience as a mask.

"Well?" Yara said. "You've seen enough scars in your time. Is it what I think it is?"

Brannoc withdrew his hand slowly, as if from a sleeping animal. He walked to the window instead, needing distance, needing to look at something that wasn't a child's collarbone. Below, Lowmarsh lay steaming under a thin sun, all rot-boards and reed-roofs and the brown gleam of channels. Somewhere a woman was beating a rug. Ordinary sounds. The world insisting on itself.

"The last night of the Choir," he said, because Yara deserved the truth and because saying it aloud might make it smaller, "there were maybe forty of us left in the abbey. Everyone else had run or burned. And Cantor Ilvane gathered the youngest of us in the undercroft—you weren't there, Yara, you'd already gone to the passes—and he told us the war was lost, but the memory didn't have to be." He turned. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The founding memory. The *first song*. What the Choir was made to carry, generation to generation, voice to voice, from before the kingdom had a name. Every speaker carries a fragment. That's what we are—that's what we *were*. Living pages. And Ilvane—"

"He sealed one," Yara said. "Into a child."

"Into a vessel." Brannoc shook his head. "No. Into a *library*. Look at the work, if you dare to look. That's not a hiding place, it's an archive. Layered. Warded. Built to hold a fragment of the founding memory intact through—" he gestured helplessly at the girl, at the decade between, at everything "—through whatever came after. Through purges. Through hunts. Through *time*, Yara. He meant it to survive us."

The silence stretched. Merrin, to her credit, had pulled her shirt straight and sat swinging her feet, listening to them discuss her like a parcel with the untroubled air of someone who had long ago stopped expecting to understand the adults around her.

"How do you know it's the founding memory?" Yara asked quietly. "Could be anything. Could be a laundry list."

"Because of how it's sealed." Brannoc came back to the cot and crouched, bringing himself level with the girl, and pointed—not touching—at the fine lines within the scar. "See how the script turns inward? All toward the center? That's a cantor's lock. We used it for our most sacred holdings. The outer rings are wards—the ones that keep the Hunt's hounds confused, that keep dream-readers out, that kept it hidden through ten years of a crown that would burn this child alive to get at what she carries. But the innermost ring—" his finger traced the air above the smallest circle, "—that's not a ward. That's a *keyhole*. And there is exactly one key cut to fit it."

"Which is?"

"A full voice. An oath-bound voice, sung at the pitch of grief." Brannoc looked up at Yara. "Which makes you either the worst person to have found this child, or the only one."

Yara's face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all, which was its usual trick. "Ilvane's dead," they said. "I watched him burn."

"I know what you watched." Brannoc rose, knees cracking, and went to pour himself another measure of the barley spirit. His hand was steady now. It was always steady once the decision was made; it was only the waiting that shook him. "But dead men leave instructions. He spent his last hour in that undercroft telling four terrified apprentices what would come. He said: *one day a vessel will find a voice, or a voice will find a vessel, and when it happens you will know it by the scar and by the silence, and you will have a choice.*" He drank. "I thought he was raving. I thought it was the fever talking. I took my fragment and I ran into the marshes and I drowned it in drink and I told myself the old man died mad."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at his finest work sitting on my cot asking me what songs are." Brannoc set the cup down harder than he meant to. "And now there are criers in every market town between here and the capital offering silver for speakers' heads, and an Inquisitor-General in Greyhollow with machines that pull memories out of people like teeth, and the girl is sitting in the one loft in Thornfell every deaf hound in the crown's service is going to sniff out eventually, because Yara—" he stepped close, close enough to see the exhaustion banked behind the swordspeaker's eyes like coals under ash, "—because whatever else she is, she is *loud*. Sealed, yes. Silent, yes. But things that loud don't stay quiet forever. Cracks happen. Thaws happen. And when this seal opens—if it opens—it won't whisper."

Merrin had stopped swinging her feet.

"I can hear you," she said.

Both of them turned. She wasn't looking at them; she was looking at the fire, at the middle distance, at some country only she could see.

"When you talk about the inside of me," she said carefully, "it gets warm. Right here." She pressed two fingers below her collarbone, over the scar. "Like something turning over in its sleep. And just now, when you said *keyhole*—" her brow furrowed again, deeper, "—I heard music. Just a little. Three notes. I've never heard them before and I know them better than my own name, except I don't have a name, so I suppose that's not saying much."

Brannoc felt the floor tilt gently beneath him, though he knew it was only the marsh wind rocking the loft on its pilings, the way it had rocked for sixty years.

Three notes. He knew three notes that could live in a child's ear like that. Every Choir apprentice learned them before they learned letters: the opening phrase of the *Cantus Primus*, the first song, the one the histories said the first king had begged the singers for and been refused, and had taken anyway, and had built a throne upon. Two hundred years the crown had sung its sustaining hymn from stolen notes, and two hundred years the true song had slept in living vessels scattered and hunted and burned, until—

Until one was sitting on his cot, humming.

Because she was humming. Barely audible, tuneless, the way children hum without knowing it, three rising notes over and over, and the fire flared green for half a heartbeat, and the jug on the shelf rang softly in sympathy, and outside the window every gull on the Lowmarsh pilings went silent at once.

Then it passed. The fire burned orange. The gulls resumed their quarrels. Merrin blinked and looked around as if waking.

"Was that—" she began.

"Eat something," Brannoc said roughly, and pushed the bread and hard cheese across the table to her, because his hands needed to do something ordinary before they did what they were going to do next. "Eat. Both of you. You look like a pair of scarecrows that lost an argument with the crows."

While the girl ate—carefully, thoroughly, with the disciplined hunger of a child who had known real want—Brannoc stood at the window with his back to the room and made himself think clearly for the first time in four years. It hurt, like walking on a foot that had gone to sleep.

If the seal cracked naturally, the fragment would leak out in dreams, in fragments, in fits of power the girl couldn't control and couldn't hide. The Hunt would find her within a season. If Vael's machines got near her—if that charming butcher laid his extraction device over that scar—he wouldn't just learn what she carried. He'd take it, whole, and hand the founding memory to the men who had spent two centuries singing lies with it. And if the seal opened fully, uncontrolled—

Ilvane had described that too, in the undercroft, in his burnt-out voice. *A founding memory released all at once into an unready mind burns the mind away like frost off a spring field.*

There was one other way. One controlled way. One voice in all the world shaped to fit that innermost ring.

He turned around. Yara was watching him over the rim of a bowl of broth, and Brannoc saw that the swordspeaker already knew—had probably known since the pass, since the moment they pulled a nameless child out of the snow and felt the silence of her heart-scar through their own ruined gift. Knew, and had carried it up the mountain to a drunk in a marsh loft because carrying it alone had become impossible.

"Don't say it," Yara said.

"Somebody has to say it."

"Don't." The word came out flat and final as a door slamming. "She's a child. She's *hiding*. The whole point—the entire point—was that nothing touches her until she's grown, until she chooses, until—"

"Until what?" Brannoc asked, not unkindly. "Until the crown's hounds choose for her? Until Vael's machine chooses? Yara. Listen to yourself. There is no *until*. There is only *before* and *after*, and we are standing in the last hours of *before*."

On the cot, Merrin had stopped eating. She looked from one of them to the other, bread halfway to her mouth, and Brannoc watched understanding arrive in her face—not the full understanding, thank every merciful silence, but its shadow. The shape of being discussed. The shape of being a thing with a purpose that other people argued over.

"I'm not going anywhere with anyone," she announced, with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old, "until somebody tells me what's inside me."

The fire crackled. The wind rocked the loft. And far to the east, though none of them could know it yet, in a stone cell beneath the assizes of Greyhollow, an Inquisitor-General was leaning over an old woman's trembling body with a machine of brass and bone, listening—for the first time in his career—to a song he did not recognize, and smiling.

Brannoc looked at the girl, and at the silent coin of skin above her heart, and at the friend he had betrayed a decade ago and never apologized for, and heard himself say the words he had sworn in the ashes of the abbey never to say:

"It's the beginning of everything," he told her. "And I think, child, that it chose you back."
