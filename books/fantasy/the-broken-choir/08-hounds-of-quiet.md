## 8. Hounds of Quiet

The marshes began where the road gave up pretending.

Yara felt the change underfoot before they saw it—the packed clay of the smugglers' path softening into something that sucked at their boots with each step, greedy and patient, as though the ground itself had learned to wait. Mist lay over the reeds in long gray banners, and the air smelled of rot and iron and the particular cold sweetness of standing water that had forgotten how to move.

Behind them, Merrin stumbled and caught herself on Yara's pack strap for the hundredth time that day.

"Tell me again why we can't take the road," the girl said. Her voice had gone hoarse from two days of walking, but she hadn't complained once until now, which Yara privately counted as a kind of courage most grown soldiers never managed.

"Because roads have ears," Yara said. "And eyes. And men with lists."

"And this place has... frogs."

"Frogs," Yara agreed gravely, "and mud that swallows horses whole, and fog thick enough to hide a cathedral. The Ferrywoman's people call it the Nine Bends' little sister. Nobody official comes here because nobody official can find anything here, including themselves." They paused, listening—not with their ears, though they were good ears, but with that other sense they kept folded away like a knife wrapped in wool. "We'll cross tonight. By morning we'll be on the far side, and the Hunt will be chasing mist."

Merrin looked out at the drowning landscape with an expression Yara was beginning to know well: the look of a child deciding whether to be afraid or fascinated, and choosing fascination as an act of will. "Do you really think they're still following us?"

Yara did not answer immediately, because the honest answer was *yes, and closer than I'd like*, and because lying to Merrin had begun to sit wrong in their chest, like a stone swallowed sideways.

Three days ago, in the market at Lowmarsh, a fishwife had grabbed her daughter's arm and stared at Merrin across the crowd with an expression that wasn't curiosity. It was recognition—or the shape of it, the way a dog recognizes a scent it's been trained to. She hadn't shouted. She'd simply turned and walked quickly toward the garrison house, and Yara had paid for their bread, taken Merrin's hand, and left by the back way while the bell above the chandler's shop was still swinging.

Since then, Yara had felt it. Not heard—*felt*. A pressure at the edge of perception, faint and rhythmic, like a pulse heard through a wall. The Hunt was not using dogs. Dogs were useless against someone who could walk silent and mask her trail with ash and stream water. The Inquisitor-General was smarter than that.

The Hunt was using listeners.

* * *

They made camp on a hummock of higher ground just past full dark, a knuckle of land crowded with blackthorn, surrounded on three sides by channels of black water. Yara built no fire. They shared dried fish and a heel of hard bread, and Merrin chewed slowly, watching the mist move between the reeds like slow animals.

"They're called the Hounds of Quiet," Yara said, when the silence had gone on long enough that not saying it would be worse. "You should know what's coming. The Hunt's trackers aren't men with dogs. They're deaf men."

Merrin frowned. "Deaf? How do deaf men hunt anyone?"

"That's exactly what makes them dangerous." Yara turned the fish bone over in their fingers. "Most people make noise without knowing it. Footfalls. Breath. But there's another kind of noise, quieter than sound. Everything alive hums, Merrin. Blood moving. Nerves firing. A heart beating is a drum; a thought is a whisper. Most of us can't feel any of it. But men born deaf—who've never had ordinary hearing to lean on—sometimes the world teaches them to listen with their skin instead. The Inquisition finds them young. Trains them. Teaches them to feel resonance the way you'd feel heat from a fire."

"Resonance." Merrin said the word carefully, tasting it. "Like... singing?"

Yara's hands went still. "Why do you say that?"

"I don't know." The girl shrugged, but her eyes had gone distant, fixed on some middle distance where nothing was visible except mist. "It feels like the right word. Like when Brannoc touched my scar and went all strange. He didn't hear anything. He *felt* something. I saw his face."

*Brannoc,* Yara thought, and the name pulled at them like a hook set deep. Three days since they'd left him in Lowmarsh, drunk and furious and hiding them anyway, and every hour since, Yara had been doing arithmetic about what Vael's interrogators could pull out of a man who'd sworn he'd forgotten everything worth remembering. Brannoc had promised he'd keep the girl out of his songs. But promises were thin armor against the machines the Inquisition used.

"You felt him feeling you," Yara said aloud. "That's what the Hounds do. Except they're hunting. And you—" they hesitated, then made themselves finish, because Merrin deserved the truth even when it was ugly, "you are the loudest quiet thing in these mountains. That seal on your chest isn't just a scar. It's a held note. Ten years of a note, held without breaking. To a man who listens with his skin, you must shine like a bonfire in a cellar."

Merrin put her hand flat over her sternum, over the pale branching mark beneath her shirt. "Can they feel it now? Tonight?"

"Not yet. Water helps—it damps the hum, blurs you into the marsh's own noise. That's why we came this way. But if they've picked up your thread..." Yara looked out at the fog. "We keep moving. We don't stop until the far ridge."

So they didn't stop. They waded channel after channel, Yara counting bends by landmarks Brannoc had once described in one of his rare sober hours—a lightning-split alder, a cairn of white stones, a drowned fence row that marked some farmer's claim from a century ago. The water rose from ankle-deep to knee-deep to, in one crossing, up to Yara's waist with Merrin clinging to their shoulders, both of them gasping at the cold. The moon came and went behind ragged cloud. Somewhere a bittern boomed, a sound like a giant swallowing.

It was near the fourth bend, in the deepest throat of the marsh, that Yara felt the pressure change.

It came the way weather comes—first a stillness, then a weight. The faint rhythmic pulse they'd been tracking for days sharpened, resolved, became *directional*. Not one listener. Three. Moving in a spread line, fanning through the reeds to the south and east, closing on the hummock country like fingers closing on a coin.

"Merrin." Yara kept their voice below a breath. "Don't ask questions. Do exactly what I do."

The girl read their face and nodded, lips pressed white.

They left the water. It was counterintuitive—every instinct said the channels were cover—but Yara had spent ten years learning how hunters worked, and listeners hunted differently than eyes did. Open water rang. Every splash echoed along the surface tension like a struck bell. Reeds, dead bracken, peat bog that swallowed each footfall and gave nothing back—that was where a body's hum got muffled, diffused, lost among the marsh's own slow noise.

They moved north through the blackthorn tangle, bent double, Yara setting each foot down heel-first with the deliberate care of a man walking on sleeping children. Behind them, the pressure grew. Then—worse—it *changed*. One of the three sources stopped advancing and began to swing wide, circling east, cutting the angle toward the ridge. Toward the only dry ground for miles.

They know the terrain too, Yara thought grimly. Or they have maps, and maps don't get tired.

An hour past midnight, crouched in a stand of dead reeds with water seeping through every seam of their clothing, Yara saw the first of them.

He came out of the fog like something the marsh had dreamed: a tall man in oiled leather, bareheaded, moving with a strange rolling grace. His eyes were open but wrong—unfocused, aimed at nothing, seeing nothing. His head tilted constantly, minutely, the way a bird's does. At his hip hung no sword but a long slender rod tipped with a tuning-fork of dull silver, and every few steps he would touch the fork to his own wrist, and Yara, watching, felt the faintest shiver pass through the air like the memory of a bell.

A Hound of Quiet. Yara had seen one only once before, ten years ago, from a hiding place, on the worst night of their life. This one was younger. His movements were less certain. He was maybe twenty-five years old, and he had been stolen as a child and remade into an instrument, and none of that mattered at all, because forty paces behind him came two more, and behind them, faint lantern-glow and the clink of armed men who could see.

The lead Hound stopped.

His head turned, slow as a weathervane, until his blind gaze pointed directly at the reed stand where Yara and Merrin crouched.

He raised one hand. Fist closed. The signal carried down the line without a word, without a sound—and that was the horror of it, Yara thought, the whole hunt conducted in perfect silence, a murder arranged entirely in the language of the deaf.

There was no running. The nearest channel was thirty yards of open ringing water. The ridge was a mile, and the circling Hound held its flank. Yara ran the geometry twice and got the same answer both times: caught.

Which left one thing, and Yara had sworn, ten years ago over a burned body, never to do it again.

*Merrin,* they breathed, so softly it was hardly voice at all. *When I stand up, you run for the split alder. North-northwest. Don't look back. Don't stop.*

*What are you going to—*

*Run.*

Yara rose out of the reeds.

For one heartbeat the Hound's blank face showed nothing, and then it showed everything—the tilt of his head sharpening, his nostrils flaring, his whole body orienting on Yara like a compass needle finding its pole. He felt them. Of course he felt them. A swordspeaker's hum was a bonfire to these men, oath-bound or not, and Yara had just stood up inside their senses like a sun rising.

The Hound's hand went to the silver fork at his belt—to signal, to summon the soldiers with their lanterns and their crossbows—and Yara spoke.

One word. The word for *unbind*, in the old tongue, pitched low, shaped with the precise breath-control that ten years of silence had kept sharp as a whetstone. The fork at the man's belt came apart. Not broke—*came apart*, its metal forgetting, for one glorious instant, that it had ever been forged, the silver crumbling to gray sand that hissed into the water.

The Hound staggered. And then he did the thing Yara had not planned for, the thing that would live behind Yara's eyes for years afterward: he smiled. A wide, wondering, almost joyful smile, as if he had finally, after a lifetime of silence, heard something worth hearing. He reached for the horn at his collar—the backup signal, the one that needed no ears, a whistle that would cut through fog and marsh and sleep itself—

Yara closed the distance in six strides.

They had meant to strike him senseless. That was the plan, the whole plan, the only plan: the edge of the hand, the temple, a man asleep in the mud for six hours. But the marsh mud betrayed them both—one slick step, a lurch—and the blow that was meant to stun came down angled and hard on the hinge of the man's jaw, and his neck turned with a crack like green wood splitting, and he dropped.

He did not get up.

The silence afterward was enormous. It filled the marsh, filled Yara's skull, louder than any bell. The other Hounds had frozen—Yara could feel them, two points of straining attention in the fog, their quarry's hum suddenly spiked with something they had never been trained to read. Grief, perhaps. Or fear. Even instruments, it seemed, could be frightened.

Then the lanterns began to move, and the moment for grief was over.

* * *

What followed was not a fight. Fights had rules, exchanges, a shape. What followed was ten years of buried knowledge pouring out of Yara like water from a breached dam, and Yara hating every second of it even as their body performed with terrible fluency.

They unmade the soldiers' weapons one at a time, a whispered syllable per piece, feeling each unbinding like a small tearing behind the eyes—pikes sagging into soft rods, crossbow strings drifting apart into loose fiber, a sword turning to red dust in its owner's grip. The men shouted, then stopped shouting, because shouting brought answers they couldn't survive. Yara moved through the fog and let the fog do half the work, striking throats and knees and the soft places behind ears, taking a slash across the forearm that they barely felt, putting men down into the sucking mud with a thoroughness that left no room for mercy and no time for it either.

At some point Merrin reached the split alder. Yara knew it without seeing, felt the girl's small bright hum pause there, trembling, obedient, waiting—and something in that obedience hurt worse than the blade-slash, worse than anything.

It ended the way such things end: suddenly, with the fog full of groaning men and floating lanterns and no one left standing who wanted to stand. Seven soldiers down. Two Hounds fled east into the marsh, their hums receding like retreating tide. And the third—

The third lay at Yara's feet in the black water, his blind eyes open to the sky, his wondering smile still on his face.

Yara knelt beside him. Pressed two fingers to his throat, knowing already, needing to know anyway. Nothing. The marsh lapped gently at his shoulders, rearranging itself around him, indifferent.

Ten years. Ten years of silence, of ash-masking trails and walking soft and biting down on every word that might betray them, and it had taken Yara less than four minutes to kill seven men, and the knowledge had come back not rusty but *clean*, oiled, eager, like a sword kept wrapped in wool and wax against exactly this day. Their hands were steady. That was the worst part. Their breathing was even. Some part of them had done this work so long and so well that it had never actually left, had only waited, patient as the marsh, and now it stood up inside them and stretched and looked around with interest.

*I'm sorry,* Yara said to the dead man, in the old tongue, the word for sorrow that also meant *debt*. It was the first time in ten years they had spoken it to anyone but the wind.

Merrin found them there. She came out of the reeds with her chin up and her fists clenched, having run exactly as far as ordered and not one step farther, and she looked at the bodies in the water and at Yara kneeling among them, bloody to the elbow, and Yara braced for the scream, the recoil, the child's terror that would be no more than fair.

Instead Merrin waded forward and put her small hand on Yara's shoulder, right over the slash, gentle as bandaging.

"He was smiling," she whispered. "Before. Why was he smiling?"

"Because he heard us," Yara said. Their voice came out sanded flat. "Because for one moment, he heard what he'd been listening for his whole life. And I killed him for it."

Merrin was quiet a moment. Then, with the terrible practicality of children: "Was it you, or was it them? Them making you?"

Yara looked at her—at the seal scar showing pale at her collar, at the eyes that asked questions no eight-year-old should have to carry—and understood, with a lurch, that the question was not childish at all. It was the oldest question the gift had ever asked. *Who made you promise? Who made you kill? Where does the power end and the wielder begin?*

"The Inquisitor-General sent them," Yara said, standing, joints protesting, blood cooling on their arm. "But I swung. Remember that, Merrin. Whatever anyone tells you about destiny and gifts and chosen ones—remember that somebody always swings."

They gathered what the dead could spare: a waterskin, a flint, a coil of oiled cord, the broken Hound's silver fork-sand, which Yara pocketed with a superstitious care they didn't examine. Then they took the girl's hand and turned north, away from the lantern-lit water, toward the ridge and the long dark beyond it.

They walked until the sky went gray. And it was only when the first light showed them the far hills, and safety of a kind, that Yara allowed themselves to think the thought that had been waiting all night at the bottom of them:

Seven men dead, and the Hunt would not mourn quietly. Vael would learn what had happened at the Nine Bends within a day. He would learn that the last swordspeaker had used the gift openly, lethally, to protect a sealed child—and every report would confirm what the Inquisitor-General had surely already suspected.

The girl was not just a fugitive. She was the prize. And Yara Eriksen had just shown the whole machinery of the crown exactly where, and how fiercely, she was guarded.

Behind them, far off across the marsh, a whistle sounded—thin, clear, carrying over the water. Not pursuit. Assembly. The Hunt, gathering its dead, and counting.

Yara did not look back. But they walked faster.
