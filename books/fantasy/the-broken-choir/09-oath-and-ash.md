## 9. Oath and Ash

The cave they found that night was not one of the shrine-caves—Yara had been careful to walk them past those, careful as they had been careful for ten years—but a smuggler's hollow two bends below the Widow's Tooth, a place where the mountain had cracked itself open long ago and never bothered to heal. Someone had built a fire-ring there once, stones blackened and stacked with the particular patience of people who meant to come back. Smugglers' courtesy. You left the ring standing; you left dry wood under the stone lip; you left no other trace. The roads of Thornfell were held together by such small kindnesses between strangers who would never meet.

Yara built the fire small. Small fires warmed without announcing, and the wind at this height came down off the snowfields in long cold breaths that made even a modest flame shiver and lean. Merrin sat close to it, knees drawn up, hands out, watching the coals with an attention that Yara was beginning to find unsettling in its stillness. Children fidgeted. Children asked for food and then asked again. This child ate what she was given, thanked her in a voice like a bell rung softly, and then went back to watching things—the fire, the dark mouth of the cave, Yara's own face—with the patient gravity of someone much older wearing a child's skin.

She had slept on the walk up from Lowmarsh. Actually slept, mid-stride almost, leaning against Yara's side while Yara kept moving because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant feeling the hounds' deaths in her hands again. Three days now since the marsh. Her knuckles had healed clean; Brannoc's salves worked as well as ever, when Brannoc could be persuaded to open his door and not simply drink himself into the next province. But Yara's hands remembered. In the quiet moments—in the pauses between one step and the next—they remembered the weight of the hatchet, the give of it, how little resistance a man's body offered if you struck where the training told you to strike.

Ten years of silence, undone in one night of marsh-water and screaming.

"You're doing the thing again," Merrin said.

Yara blinked. "What thing?"

"The thing where your eyes go somewhere else and your jaw gets tight." Merrin did not look away from the fire. "Brannoc does it too. Before he drinks."

"I'm not going to drink."

"I didn't say you were." A pause. The fire popped. "Are you going to tell me? About the oath? You said you would, when we were somewhere safe."

This was somewhere safe, or as near to safe as the world offered anymore: a hole in a mountain that appeared on no map, behind them three days of hard walking and a dead Hunt tracker buried under a cairn of marsh stones that would fool no one who looked closely. Safe was a relative country, and they were only ever visitors there.

"Safe," Yara said, "is a word I stopped trusting around the time I learned to read proclamations." They fed another branch to the fire. "But yes. I said I would. And you've earned it, asking nothing else for three days, which is more restraint than most grown men I've smuggled salt with."

Merrin turned then, finally, and looked at them fully, and Yara felt the look like a hand laid flat against the sternum. There it was again—that quality in the girl's attention, that listening-with-the-whole-body quality that Yara had spent a decade trying to forget and could not, because it was the first thing the Choir taught and the last thing it lost. *We listen before we speak,* Maren Sable had said, on a morning very much like this one, in a shrine very much unlike this one. *Because the world is always already singing, little wren. Our work is only to add our note without breaking the song.*

"Tell me about the oath," Merrin said.

So Yara told her.

* * *

"It begins with water," Yara said. "Cold water. Mountain water, the kind that comes straight off the ice and tastes of stone. You kneel in it—it comes to here—" they touched their own collarbone, "—and it is so cold that your body wants to scream, and the first lesson of the whole life is that you don't. Not because anyone forbids it. Because you listen first, and the water has something to say, and screaming is just noise drowning out the world."

Merrin's head tilted. "How old were you?"

"Six." Yara smiled, and was surprised to find the smile didn't hurt. "I stood in that water and I thought I would die of it, and my mentor—her name was Maren Sable, and she was the finest speaker of our generation, and she is ash now, and I'll come to that—Maren stood beside me in the same water and said, 'Good. Now you know what your body wants. The oath is learning what it may have.'"

"The oath is about wanting?"

"The oath is about everything," Yara said. "But it starts there, yes. Listen. I'll give it to you properly, the way it was given to me, because some things rot when you paraphrase them."

They sat forward. The firelight moved on their face, and for a moment—only a moment—they let themselves feel it, the old cadence rising in the chest like water finding its level, and they spoke the words in the speaking-voice rather than the singing-voice, which was permitted, which was the whole point:

*"Voice is a blade, and a blade must sleep.*
*I will not speak steel against flesh.*
*I will not speak memory against mind.*
*I will not unmake what I have not first understood.*
*I will hold my silence dearer than my anger,*
*and my anger dearer than my ease,*
*and the song dearer than all of them.*
*What I break, I break with grief.*
*What I keep, I keep with joy.*
*And when the last note fails, let it fail in me last."*

The words settled into the cave the way stones settle into water. Merrin had gone very still—not her usual stillness, which was watchful, but a deeper thing, a held-breath thing. And Yara watched her and felt the first cold thread of dread, because he knew that stillness. He had seen it in novices hearing the oath for the first time. He had seen it in the mirror, at six years old, dripping mountain water, understanding without understanding.

"That's it?" Merrin whispered. "That's the whole oath?"

"That's the heart of it. There are longer forms. There are commentaries that would fill this cave. Maren used to say the oath was a house with a thousand rooms and you lived in one room at a time, and each year they gave you a new key." Yara poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "But the shape of it is simple. We could do things, Merrin. Things you've seen me do—a whisper to loosen an iron lock, a word to make a seized wagon forget it was ever bound. And things you haven't seen, and won't, if I have anything to say about it. A swordspeaker with full voice can unmake steel. Can unmake memory. Can reach into a man's mind and take from it the memory of his mother's face, and leave him smiling, because we can choose what he feels about the loss. Do you understand what that means?"

Merrin thought about it with her whole face, the way children do. "It means you could make people love you."

The words landed harder than any blow Yara had taken in the marsh. "Yes," they said quietly. "It means exactly that. And so the oath exists. Every gift we had was bound inside the oath like a blade in a scabbard. Power bound to restraint. Not power *and* restraint—bound. The binding *was* the power. An unbound voice isn't stronger, it's just louder, and loudness is the death of music. Maren said—" and here Yara's voice caught, and they let it catch, because Merrin deserved to hear that too, "—Maren said the Choir fell the day we started believing the oath was a cage instead of a tuning. That we mistook the instrument for the prisoner."

"What happened to the Choir?"

"You know some of it. Everyone knows the shape of it, even if the crown sands down the edges. Ten years ago the Inquisition burned the shrines and put the speakers to the sword or the pyre, and the realm called it justice, and the war that nearly ended everything before that was blamed on us. On the gift." Yara looked into the fire. "I was seventeen. I was on a messaging run in the low passes when the purges began—too far away, too slow coming back. When I reached the shrine at Vaelmoor, it was burning. Maren was still inside. She had stayed. She could have unmade every sword that came up that road with a single sustained note, and she stayed, and she sang the *Lament for the Unfinished* while they burned her, because the oath says the song is dearer than all of them, and she believed the realm needed to hear the lament more than it needed her alive."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the cave's mouth.

"She chose it," Merrin said. It wasn't quite a question.

"She chose it. Or she believed she chose it, which may be the same thing or may be the saddest difference in the world." Yara wrapped their arms around their knees. "I ran. That's my part in the great tragedy—I ran, and I hid, and I took a name that wasn't mine and a trade that keeps me moving, and for ten years I have kept the oath perfectly, because keeping it perfectly required nothing of me but silence. No temptation. No choice. Just absence." They laughed, short and bitter. "You want to know the truth of oaths, Merrin? They're easy to keep in hiding. It's the living that tests them."

Merrin was quiet for a long time. The fire burned down a little; Yara added wood, mechanically, watching the girl rather than the flames. He could see the question forming in her before she spoke—he could see it the way you see weather building over a ridge—and some part of him, some old and frightened part, wanted to fill the silence with anything, with smuggling stories, with songs, with lies.

He let the silence stand. Listening first. It was the first thing the Choir taught.

Then Merrin asked it.

"Who made you promise?"

* * *

The question went into Yara like a splinter going into soft wood—clean, deep, and immediately invisible, working its way toward somewhere vital.

"Who—" Yara stopped. Started again. "The order. The Choir made the oath. Every speaker swore it."

"But who *made* it?" Merrin pressed, and there was nothing childish in her voice now; there was something ancient moving behind her eyes, something that made the hairs on Yara's arms rise. "Someone wrote it. Someone decided that people with the gift should have to promise not to use it. Who were they? Were they gifted too? Did they swear it themselves?"

"They—" Yara reached for the answer the way you reach for a stair in the dark and find nothing there. "The First Cantors. The founding generation. The oath comes down from them, from the founding—"

"From the founding," Merrin repeated softly. And her hand drifted up, without her seeming to notice it, to the scar over her heart, and her fingers rested on it the way you rest fingers on a door you're not sure you want to open. "The founding that I dream about. Towers. Ten thousand voices. And underneath the singing, underneath all of it, there's something else. Something that sounds like—" She frowned, struggling. "Like a lock being built. Like people hammering a lock onto something that was singing free."

Yara's mouth had gone dry. "Merrin. What else do you remember?"

"Nothing. Only that." She looked up, and the ancient thing behind her eyes receded, and she was eight years old again and tired and far from home. "Only that the oath sounds beautiful, but it also sounds like a wall. And walls are built by somebody, aren't they? Somebody wanted the singers quiet. Somebody *needed* them quiet." She hugged her knees. "You keep the oath like it's holy. But holy things are still made by people. My mam used to say that. Holy things are made by people, and people can be wrong, or wicked, or both."

"Merrin—"

"Who made you promise?" she asked again, gentler now, and somehow that was worse. "Did you ever ask? Did anyone? Or did you all just stand in the cold water and say the words because everyone before you said the words?"

Yara opened their mouth to say *the First Cantors*, to say *the founding*, to say *it is the oldest law of the gift*—and found, to their genuine horror, that they did not know. Not truly. They knew the oath's words the way they knew their own heartbeat, but its origin was a haze of reverence, of *it has always been thus*, of commentaries citing older commentaries citing texts lost in the fall. The Choir had venerated the oath the way the crown venerated its hymn—and Yara had seen tonight, in a market square, what happened to people who hummed forbidden cadences, and had begun to understand what a venerated thing could be used to hide.

A lock being built. Hammered onto something that was singing free.

"I don't know," Yara said at last, and the admission cost more than the killing in the marsh had. "I don't know who made the oath, or why, or whether they were right. I know it kept me alive. I know it kept the gift from becoming a whip. And I know that I have never once, in twenty-seven years, thought to ask your question until you asked it for me."

Merrin nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she had already suspected, and Yara wondered—not for the first time—what else was sealed behind that scar, waiting.

"Maybe that's why I have it," she said, touching the seal scar. "Maybe whoever locked this into me wanted someone to ask questions later. When it was safe to ask."

"Or maybe," Yara said quietly, "they wanted someone to *open* it later. And I'm beginning to think those might be different people entirely."

The fire burned low. Merrin curled up in the bedroll Yara spread for her near the warmth, and within minutes her breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of true exhaustion—but her lips kept moving faintly after sleep took her, shaping syllables in a tongue Yara half-recognized and could not name, and her hand stayed pressed over her heart as though holding a door shut from the inside.

Yara sat awake long after, feeding the fire, turning the splinter-question in the wound of them. *Who made you promise.* Outside, the wind shifted, and for a moment—surely only the wind, surely only exhaustion—it carried something down from the high passes that sounded almost like distant voices, many voices, singing in harmony a song Yara had sworn never to sing.

By dawn, Yara had made a decision they did not yet say aloud, even to themselves: they were going to need answers. And the only person left alive who might have them was a drunk healer in Lowmarsh who owed Yara a debt—and hated them for it.

They broke camp before the sun cleared the ridge, and turned south, back toward Lowmarsh, back toward Brannoc, back toward everything the oath had told them to leave buried.
