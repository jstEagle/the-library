## 10. The Gilded Choir Loft

The choir loft above the Chancellery was gilded the way a wound is bandaged.

Inquisitor-General Corvus Vael had walked beneath it every morning for eleven years—the long gallery connecting his offices to the Chancellery's private wing—and every morning he had told himself the same small lie: that the gold leaf overhead was ornament, nothing more, a flourish of an age that had loved display. It took a man of his particular discipline to hold a lie that size in place for eleven years. He was proud of it, in the way a surgeon is proud of a steady hand. The lie held because he made it hold.

Today it would not hold any longer.

He climbed the narrow stair behind the tapestry of the Concord at Aldenmere—that famous weaving where the first king and the last Cantor stood hand in hand before the assembled Choir, all of them smiling in wool and silver thread—and paused, as he always did, at the top step. The loft opened before him: a vaulted chamber the length of a cathedral nave, its ribs sheathed in gold, its windows of pale glass that turned afternoon light into something like honey. And along both walls, tier upon tier, the resonance pipes—no, that was the word the engineers used, and it was a lie too. They were throats. Two hundred bronze throats, each one tuned to a note no living singer could produce alone, each one fed by channels cut into the living rock below, channels that ran, so the old plans showed, all the way down to the cells where voices went when their owners were finished with them.

Vael stood in the doorway and let the hum find him.

It always found him first in the sternum, then in the teeth, then somewhere behind the eyes where memory kept its house. The sustaining hymn did not have a melody you could whistle. It was not music in any sense a fiddler in Greyhollow would recognize. It was a chord that had been sounding for two hundred years without pause—a chord with no beginning written into it, no resolution, no rest. The realm breathed to it. Contracts signed under it held. Borders drawn along its harmonics stayed drawn. A king's word carried weight in proportion to how well the hymn carried him, and every coronation since Aldenmere had been, at its heart, a tuning ceremony: the new sovereign placed beneath the great chord until his voice learned to ride it, until his lies acquired the texture of truth.

*We do not rule the realm,* Vael thought, walking the length of the loft with his boots silent on the gilded floor. *The chord does. We are merely the hands that keep it fed.*

And today he had come to tell the Chancellor that the feeding had failed.

Ives was already there, of course. Chancellor Edran Ives was a man who arrived early to everything, on the theory that punctuality was a form of ownership—if you were in the room first, the room belonged to you. He stood at the far end of the loft beneath the master pipe, a slight, neat figure in grey silk, his hands folded behind his back, his head tilted as though listening to a lover rather than a machine built from the bones of a murdered order.

"You've read my report," Vael said.

"I've read your report." Ives did not turn. "I found it alarmist."

"It was accurate."

"Accuracy is a quality reports have." Now Ives turned, and Vael met the Chancellor's eyes—pale, quick, unhurried eyes, the eyes of a man who had never once in forty years of power raised his voice. "Truth is a quality actions have. Your report says the hymn is failing. My instruments say the hymn is *drifting*. These are very different words, Inquisitor-General, and only one of them requires me to wake the Privy Council at midnight."

"Then let us use precise words." Vael crossed to the instrument table against the north wall, where his own devices waited among the Chancellor's—his resonance forks wrapped in velvet, his wax cylinders, and the thing his technicians called the Listener, a brass hemisphere studded with hair-fine needles that could read a sung memory off the air itself, or out of a skull, given time and the subject's cooperation. He set his report on the table between them like a man laying down a card he knew would win. "Page four. The fundamental."

Ives looked. Vael watched him look, and watched the flicker arrive—the smallest possible contraction around the eyes, gone in half a heartbeat. Eleven years of reading faces in interrogation rooms had made Vael fluent in a language most men didn't know they spoke. The Chancellor had seen page four before. He had simply hoped Vael hadn't understood it.

"The fundamental has dropped a quarter-tone," Ives said mildly. "Over two centuries. That is not failure. That is settling. Cathedrals settle. Mountains settle."

"Cathedrals are not alive." Vael pulled the velvet from a tuning fork and struck it softly against the table's edge. The note rang, thin and clean, and the great chord overhead answered it—and here it was, the thing he had verified seventeen times in seventeen sealed rooms, the thing that had cost him three sleepless months and, if he was honest, a portion of his faith: the hymn answered *flat*. Not by much. By exactly the amount his instruments said. By enough. "Listen. Strike any true tone in this room and the chord bends toward it and misses. Do you know what that means, Chancellor? It means the hymn is no longer self-correcting. A living song corrects. A dying one drifts. This one has been drifting since before either of us was born, and the rate is accelerating. At current decay—" he tapped page four— "the sustaining harmonic fails entirely within the year. Perhaps less. Winters shorten it; I can show you the correlation. Cold stresses the bronze. The realm will feel it by midwinter, whether we announce it or not."

"And what does the realm feel, precisely?" Ives asked, and there it was—not mockery, worse, patience. "A sour note? Some farmers' contracts go soft? You speak of failure as though the sky falls. The hymn sustains, Corvus. It does not *hold up* anything. Remove it and the stones stay where they are."

"Do they." Vael struck the fork again, let it die. "Then explain Greyhollow to me. Explain why a market town on the far side of Thornfell produced three unregistered resonants in a single season. Explain why the hounds are pulling handlers toward children instead of hiding adults. Explain why, in the last two years, the gift has begun surfacing in people who have no blood-claim to it at all—bakers, ferrymen, a shepherd woman who mended her neighbor's shattered wrist by humming over it and remembers none of it afterward." He stepped closer. "The hymn is failing, Chancellor, and as it fails, what it was built to suppress is coming back up through the floor. The old gift isn't dead. It was never dead. It was *pressed down*, pressed flat under two hundred years of borrowed breath, and now the weight is lifting and it is rising everywhere at once, in everyone, untrained, unsanctified, unregistered. You may call that drifting. I call it a tide."

For a long moment the only sound was the chord, vast and patient, filling the gilded vault the way water fills a hull.

"You have been to the archives," Ives said at last. It was not a question, and something in his voice shifted—the patience cooling into something older and harder. "The restricted shelves. I signed those permissions myself, two years ago, because I believed you were the kind of man who could look at the founding documents and remain useful. I am beginning to wonder."

"I looked," Vael said. "That was the problem, wasn't it. You wanted a man who could look without seeing."

"I wanted a man who understood that some truths are load-bearing." Ives moved at last, walking slowly down the tiered aisle between the bronze throats, his fingertips trailing the rail. "You want the history, Corvus? Very well. Here is the history as it actually was, since you've earned it. Two hundred years ago this realm was a patchwork of singing lords and oath-bound speakers tearing each other apart with voices that could unmake steel and memory alike. You've read the war records. Three generations of it. Cities that forgot their own names. Whole valleys that woke up one morning loving their neighbors and could not say why, and hated themselves for it, and burned their own granaries out of shame. The Choir did not prevent that world. The Choir *was* that world—ten thousand gifted voices, each certain of its own righteousness, each able to rewrite the minds of anyone within earshot. The first king did not steal the song. He *ended* it. He took the Choir's founding harmony—the one harmony powerful enough to bind all the others—and he bound it into this loft, into this chord, and he used it to make lying expensive and borders permanent and memory reliable. Every peace treaty since Aldenmere rests on that foundation. Every inheritance. Every grave that stays a grave." He stopped and faced Vael fully. "Yes. The singers died. Yes, the manner of it was ugly—I have read the uglier archives too, don't flatter yourself that you've found something I haven't. But ask yourself what the alternative was. Ask yourself what happens in six months if that chord stops. Not the farmers' wrists, Corvus. The treaties. The oaths. The *agreement* that reality is a shared project. You think you fear the gift returning? Wait until you see a realm full of ordinary people discovering that their memories are negotiable and their neighbors' gratitude can be sung into them. That is what the first king saw. That is what he built this to prevent. Call it theft if it helps you sleep. I call it triage."

Vael had prepared for anger. He had prepared for denial, for threat, even—for the possibility, which he had privately ranked likeliest, that Ives already knew everything Vael knew and more. What he had not prepared for was the Chancellor's fluency. The speech had the worn smoothness of a thing rehearsed across decades, across conversations with other men who had climbed these stairs and read page four and needed talking down. Vael understood, with a cold settling in his chest, that he was not the first Inquisitor-General to stand in this loft and hear the chord run flat. He was merely the latest in a line of men who had been shown the abyss and handed a shovel.

"How many of you know?" he asked quietly. "How many Chancellors have stood where you're standing?"

"All of them worth the name." Ives smiled faintly. "It is the second duty of the office. The first is keeping the hymn fed. Which brings us to the actual reason you requested this audience, since we both know it wasn't the fundamental. You want the girl."

Vael did not answer at once. Outside the honey-colored windows, the capital lay in its evening haze—rooftops and spires and the distant glitter of the Hymnal Festival scaffolds going up in the great square, workmen no larger than ants raising the stages from which, in three weeks, the realm would sing its borrowed song aloud and call it gratitude.

"A child surfaced in Thornfell," Vael said. "Eight years old. Carries a seal scar over the heart—old work, pre-purge technique, and not fieldwork. The mark of the Last Cantor himself, if my readers are calibrated correctly. She has no memory of her name. My hunt believes she is traveling with someone. The descriptions are... suggestive." He let that hang. "A fragment of the founding memory, sealed in a living vessel, walking free through my mountains while the chord that replaced it runs flat. Do you understand what she is, Chancellor? She is not a fugitive. She is a *spare part*. If the hymn dies, the founding song does not have to die with it. It exists, whole, in that child's chest. Which means the crown has a choice it has not had in two centuries: we can fall with our stolen song—or we can finish the theft properly. Take the original. Bind it fresh. Begin the count again."

Something moved behind Ives's pale eyes then, and Vael, who had spent eleven years learning to read the small contractions of the human face, saw it and wished he hadn't: not relief, not greed, but *recognition*—the look of a man hearing his own secret plan spoken back to him by another mouth.

"Yes," Ives said softly. "That is the choice. Though you should know, Inquisitor-General, that the binding you're describing cannot be done to a vessel against her will. The old rites required consent—it was the Choir's one law, and it is physics, not ethics. A sealed memory opens only for a willing voice." He turned back to the great pipe, to the chord that had been dying patiently above their heads for two hundred years while the realm sang its praises underneath. "So we will need her willing. Or we will need to make her believe she is. See to it, Corvus. Whatever it costs. Whatever it looks like. Bring me the child before midwinter, and bring her *unbroken*—and if you must break things on the way, break anything else."

Vael bowed, because the architecture of eleven years required it, and turned for the stair. At the top step he paused, as he always did, and looked back once at the gilded vault—at the two hundred bronze throats holding their long flat note over a kingdom of sleepers—and felt, for the first time in his disciplined life, the lie slip its moorings entirely.

He descended past the tapestry of the Concord at Aldenmere, where the first king and the last Cantor smiled their woven smiles, hand in hand.

By morning he had ordered two things: a fast courier to Greyhollow, doubling the reward for the child and her protector—

—and a private search, under his own seal, of the restricted archives, for everything ever written about a swordspeaker named Yara Eriksen.

* * *

The courier rode east through the night. In Greyhollow, in a cell that smelled of wet straw, an old healer named Brannoc was dreaming of fire, and did not yet know that by week's end he would be asked to trade his silence for a child's life.

And in the gilded loft, alone now, Chancellor Edran Ives stood beneath the failing chord with his head tilted, listening the way a miser listens to a coin he suspects is clipped—and began, quietly, to plan for a realm with only one voice left in it.
