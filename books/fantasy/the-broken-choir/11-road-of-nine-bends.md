## 11. Road of Nine Bends

The road earned its name honestly. Nine times it bent away from the river and nine times it bent back, and between each bend lay a stretch of ground so narrow that two carts could not pass and a rider could not turn. The smugglers who kept it called it the Needle's Thread, because it stitched together territories that would otherwise have nothing to do with one another, and because anyone who traveled it was being sewn into something they might not be able to get out of.

Yara had walked it eleven times before. They knew which stones shifted underfoot after rain, which overhangs sheltered watchers, which bends held a firepit still warm from the last caravan of contraband salt or unlicensed iron. What they did not know—what no map could tell them—was whether the Ferrywoman had heard about Greyhollow yet, and what she would decide that news was worth.

Merrin walked close behind them, close enough that Yara could hear the child's breath, quick and shallow in the cold. Three days out from Brannoc's low room above the tannery, and the girl had not once complained. She saved her questions instead, hoarding them like a miser hoards coin, and spent them at dusk when the light made lying easier.

"Is she real?" Merrin asked now, as the road bent for the fourth time and the river showed itself again below, grey-green and patient. "The Ferrywoman. Brannoc crossed himself when he said her name, and he wasn't even drunk yet."

"She's real." Yara tested the next stretch of path with their boot heel before trusting it. Loose shale, frost-slicked. "Whether she's a woman is another question."

"What is she, then?"

"A fence," Yara said. "Not of goods. Of secrets. She buys them, sells them, trades them, holds them in bond. Somewhere on this river there is a ledger with everything anyone in Thornfell has ever wanted hidden, and she can read it."

"Can you read it?"

Yara glanced back. Merrin's face was pinched with cold, her seal scar hidden under a scarf wound twice around her throat, but her eyes were sharp as a knife left out overnight. *Brannoc's eyes*, Yara thought, and then corrected themselves: no, older than that. Eyes like that had looked out of faces in the Choir lofts, in the years before the burning. The gift did not only live in voices. It lived in attention.

"No one reads her ledger," Yara said. "That's rather the point of her."

* * *

They camped that night in the hollow beneath the sixth bend, where an old smuggler cache dug into the hillside still held its straw pallet and its soot-blackened kettle. Yara built no fire. Fire drew eyes, and eyes on the Road of Nine Bends belonged to someone, and everyone on this road belonged eventually to the Ferrywoman.

Merrin slept badly. Yara sat awake beside her and listened to the child's dreams move through her body—the small twitches, the murmurs, the way her fingers curled sometimes as if plucking at strings no one else could see. Twice she spoke aloud in a language Yara had not heard since the abbey burned, since before that, since the lofts, and both times the sound of it went through Yara like a fishhook set behind the breastbone.

The old tongue. Not learned. *Remembered.* A girl with no name and no past, carrying words that had been dead for ten years and buried deeper than that.

Yara pulled their cloak tighter and thought about the bargain ahead, and hated every part of it.

Because here was the truth Yara had not said to Brannoc, had not said to Merrin, had barely admitted to the dark: you did not cross the Ferrywoman's water by paying coin. Coin she had in barrels. You paid in true things. A secret told whole, surrendered forever, entered into the ledger and sold to whoever came asking after. That was her toll. That was how she had survived every purge, every hunt, every change of crown—because half the realm owed her silence and the other half paid her rent on theirs.

And Yara Eriksen, last of the swordspeakers, was walking toward her carrying the single most dangerous secret in the world, wrapped in an eight-year-old child with a scar over her heart.

There would be no crossing without payment. And there was no true thing Yara could give that would not burn them all.

Unless.

Yara looked at the sleeping child, at the small mouth moving around dead syllables, and began, very quietly, in the dark, to build a lie.

It had to be built well. That was the cruelty of it—a lie offered to the Ferrywoman had to be load-bearing, had to have true bones inside it, or she would feel the hollowness the moment it crossed her palm. She had a gift herself, though she'd have flayed anyone who called it that: she could taste falsehood the way a good cook tastes salt. Not magic. Worse than magic. Craft, honed over decades, fed on decades.

So the lie needed a spine of truth. Yara lay back against the cold stone and assembled it piece by piece, the way they'd once assembled oaths, and fell asleep near dawn with the shape of it finished and the shame of it already settling in.

* * *

The Ferrywoman's crossing announced itself by absence. For half a mile the road ran silent—no bird song, no squirrel chatter, nothing—and then the trees opened onto a stretch of black water too wide to swim and too deep to ford, with a flat-bottomed ferry moored at a dock of blackened pilings, and a house beyond it that seemed less built than accumulated: driftwood and ship-timber and wagon-boards and, Yara had always suspected, at least one chapel door, all fused together into something that leaned but had never fallen.

She stood on the dock waiting for them, which meant she had known they were coming, which meant someone on the road had already sold their passage, which meant the price had just gone up.

She was small. That was always the first shock, for those who expected a giantess of secrets. Barely taller than Merrin, wrapped in layered grey wool, her face a network of fine lines like cracks in old glaze, her eyes pale and lidless as a fish's. Her hands were bare despite the cold, and they were the hands of someone who had counted everything in the world at least twice.

"Yara Eriksen." Her voice was dry paper. "Eleven crossings. Twelve, if you make it today. I had begun to think you'd retired."

"I tried," Yara said. "The world objected."

"The world does that." The pale eyes slid down to Merrin, and Yara felt the look like a hand laid flat on their chest—not pushing, just weighing. "And this is?"

"My daughter."

The word came out smooth. Too smooth. Yara watched the Ferrywoman hear it, watched her taste it, and saw the faint crease at the corner of her mouth that meant: *interesting.*

"Your daughter," she repeated. "How domestic. How sudden. Ten years alone on the high roads and now a child appears at your side in the worst winter in a decade." She turned, robes whispering, and started up the dock. "Come. The water won't hold still all day, and neither will I."

The ferry was poled by a boy of perhaps fifteen who never once looked at either of them, which Yara knew from experience meant he was one of hers—raised in the house of secrets, deaf in the ordinary way or trained deaf in the useful one. The river took the boat with terrible gentleness. Halfway across, the Ferrywoman sat down on a coil of rope and folded her hands.

"Now," she said. "You want the far bank, and the road beyond it, and my silence about having seen you. That's three things. I sell nothing in bundles, but I'll bundle for old customers. The price is one true memory. Told whole. Told here. Mine to keep, mine to sell if the buyer's right comes along."

"You know why we're running," Yara said. "You've heard about Greyhollow."

"I've heard about a great many things." She smiled without warmth. "I've heard the Inquisitor-General has taken rooms in the Saltcellar and pays his informants in pardons. I've heard he has a device that drinks memories out of people's heads like whey through cheesecloth. I've heard he collected four singers off the market square and two of them died screaming and one of them sang something under the extraction that made his own hounds lie down and whimper." Her fish-pale eyes glittered. "I've heard a great deal, Yara Eriksen. What I haven't heard is what you're carrying. And that gap in my ledger is beginning to itch."

Merrin's hand found Yara's sleeve. Small fingers, gripping hard.

"One memory," the Ferrywoman said again. "True. Or you swim."

* * *

Later, Yara would take the lie apart in their mind a hundred times, examining each joint, each seam, wondering where it had begun to breathe on its own. But in the moment, standing on the swaying deck with the black water underneath and the pale eyes waiting, there was only the telling.

Yara gave her the truth first. That was the craft of it—you led with truth until the listener's ear was open, and then you let the false thing slip in wearing the truth's coat.

"Ten years ago I was in the Choir loft at Hollowmere," Yara said. "You know that. Everyone who keeps a ledger knows that. I was apprentice to Cantor Ilse Varn, and when the soldiers came I ran, and she burned, and I have carried that for ten years like a stone in the belly." The words were true, and saying them hurt in the old familiar way, and Yara let the hurt show, because the hurt was the currency. "Here is the memory you're buying. The night before the raid, Ilse Varn came to me and gave me something to keep. Not a relic. Not a document. A name. She said: *if I die, find the one this name belongs to, and tell them the Choir forgives them.*"

The Ferrywoman had gone very still. Even the river seemed to quiet.

"And the name?" she asked.

Here it was. Here was the hinge on which everything turned.

"Sera Kestrel," Yara said.

The name dropped into the silence like a coin into a well. It was a good name for the purpose—it existed, it had weight, it pointed somewhere real. Sera Kestrel, clan-mother of the Kestrels, was known to every soul in Thornfell, and known to be old enough, and ambitious enough, and secretive enough, that a connection to the fallen Choir was exactly the kind of secret the Ferrywoman's ledger existed to hold. It was unverifiable. It was delicious. It was poison dressed as wine.

And it was a lie. Ilse Varn had died screaming Yara's own name, cursing the day she'd taught a swordspeaker to read music, and the only thing she'd ever given Yara was a scar across the palm and a debt of grief that would outlive them both.

But the Ferrywoman didn't know that. Couldn't know it. No one alive knew it except Yara and the dead.

"Sera Kestrel," the Ferrywoman repeated slowly, tasting each syllable. "The clan-mother."

"The Choir forgives her," Yara said. "That was the message. Whatever she did—or didn't do—Ilse wanted her to know it was forgiven. I never delivered it. I was afraid of the clans, afraid of the roads, afraid of being seen. Ten years I've carried a dead woman's forgiveness in my pocket like a coward." Yara met the pale eyes and did not blink. "Now it's yours. Sell it to her if you like. Sell it to the Inquisitor-General. It will buy me a crossing either way."

For a long moment the Ferrywoman said nothing. The boy poled. The water muttered against the hull.

Then she laughed—a short, dry sound, like a stick snapping.

"Oh, that's *good*," she said. "That is genuinely good. Do you know how long I've wondered about Sera Kestrel? Twenty years I've watched that woman build a smuggling empire out of swamp and stubbornness, and there has always been something under it, some ballast I couldn't see. Forgiveness from the Choir. From Ilse Varn herself." She shook her head, almost admiring. "The buyers will fight over this."

*Then it's done*, Yara thought, and felt the shame arrive on schedule, cold and complete. *You've just handed a knife to the cleverest woman in the mountains and pointed it at your only ally.*

Because that was the other edge of the lie, the edge Yara had lain awake sharpening in the dark: if the Ferrywoman sold the secret to Sera Kestrel herself—as she inevitably would, because the best secrets come home to roost—then Sera would learn that Yara Eriksen had traded her name across the black water. And Sera Kestrel did not forgive. Sera Kestrel collected debts, with interest, in blood or gold or service, whichever bled slowest.

Yara was spending the future to buy the present. It was the oldest transaction in the world, and it never once came cheap.

The ferry grounded against the far pilings. The Ferrywoman rose, brushed off her grey skirts, and fixed Yara with the fish-pale stare one last time.

"We're settled, then. Your crossing is paid, my silence is yours, and the road beyond is open to you for nine days before the snows close it." She paused. "One thing more. Free of charge, because I liked your mother's memory, and because I am feeling generous, and because generosity is cheaper than loyalty."

"What?"

"There are men on the road behind you." She said it lightly, the way one remarks on weather. "Six of them. They don't ride like smugglers and they don't smell like smugglers. They walk with their heads tilted, listening to the ground." Her gaze flicked, brief and unreadable, to Merrin—to the scarf, to the small hand still knotted in Yara's sleeve—and something moved behind the pale eyes, some recalculation, some entry being made in a ledger no one would ever audit.

"They're a day behind you now," the Ferrywoman said. "They won't be tomorrow. Run well, Yara Eriksen. And give my regards to your daughter."

The word *daughter* landed like a needle. Yara took Merrin's hand and stepped onto the far bank without looking back, and only when the black water lay between them and the leaning house did the child speak.

"That wasn't true," Merrin said quietly. "What you told her. About the name. I don't know how I know, but I know. It tasted wrong."

Yara looked down at her—at the too-old eyes, at the scarf hiding the scar, at the small face already learning the weight of what adults do to survive—and felt the full cost of the crossing come due all at once, not in coin, not in secrets, but in this: the first person to trust them in ten years had just watched them lie, and known it, and filed it away.

"No," Yara said. "It wasn't true."

Behind them, faint across the water, the Ferrywoman was laughing again.

Ahead, up the frozen switchbacks, the road climbed toward the high passes—and somewhere on it, a day back and closing, six men walked with their heads tilted, listening to the ground.
