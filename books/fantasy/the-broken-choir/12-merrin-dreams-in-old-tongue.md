## 12. Merrin Dreams in Old Tongue

The towers came first.

They always came first, before the voices, before the light. Merrin stood in a courtyard of white stone that was warm under her bare feet even though there was snow on the mountains all around, and the towers rose up and up and up until she had to lean her whole head back to see where they ended, and they didn't end. They went up into the sky like fingers pointing at something only they could see. Bells hung in them — no, not bells. She couldn't say what hung in them. Things that were shaped like bells but made of glass, or of frozen singing, and when the wind moved through them they didn't ring, they *sang*, each one a different note, and all the notes together made a sound like the inside of a word too big to fit in her mouth.

She knew this place. Not the way you know a place you've been, with your feet-memory and your eyes-memory. She knew it the way you know your own name, except she didn't know her own name, so maybe it was the other way around. Maybe the place knew *her*.

"Hello?" she called, because in dreams you can call out and not be afraid of what answers.

Ten thousand voices answered.

Not one voice saying ten thousand things. Ten thousand voices saying one thing, together, the way a hundred birds at dawn make one morning. The sound came from everywhere — from the towers, from the stones under her feet, from inside her own chest, which was strange and wonderful and a little bit frightening, like being hugged by something enormous. The word they sang had no shape she could hold, but she felt its meaning settle over her like a blanket: *welcome home, welcome home, welcome home.*

"I'm not home," Merrin said politely, because even in dreams you should be polite to ten thousand voices. "I'm on a mountain with Yara. We're going south."

The voices went quiet. All at once, the way candles go out when someone claps. And in the quiet she heard something else underneath, something that had been there the whole time and she'd only just noticed it, the way you notice a smell after the bread is done baking. A wrongness. A hole in the song. Somewhere in the middle of all those towers there was an empty space shaped exactly like a voice, and the emptiness was *singing too*, and its song was thin and borrowed and cold, like wearing someone else's coat.

Merrin woke up.

* * *

The cave was dark and smelled of woodsmoke and old stone and, faintly, of Yara, who slept sitting up against the far wall with their sword across their knees even in sleep, which Merrin thought must be very tiring but also very smart. The fire had burned down to orange coals. Outside, wind worried at the mouth of the cave like a dog wanting to be let in.

Her heart was still beating fast from the dream. She put her hand flat on her chest, over the scar — the shiny patch of skin over her heart that never itched and never hurt and never sweated, smooth as the inside of a seashell. In the dream, sometimes, she could almost remember how she got it. There had been hands. Kind hands. Someone singing over her, low and sad, like a mother singing to a child who is already asleep. *Forget, forget, forget,* the song said, *until it is safe, until it is time.* And then the hands had pressed something into her chest that wasn't a thing at all but a memory, a whole memory, folded up small as a seed.

She didn't remember any of that while she was awake. She remembered it the way you remember a story someone told you about yourself. But her hand knew the scar, and the scar knew the dream, and between them they had an understanding that left Merrin standing outside of it, knocking.

She got up quietly and went to sit near the coals, holding her hands out. Her breath made little ghosts in the firelight.

And then, without deciding to, she began to hum.

It wasn't a tune she knew. It wasn't a tune anyone knew — she was sure of that, sure the way you're sure the sky is up. It was four notes, then five, then seven, rising and folding back on themselves, and as she hummed them the coals seemed to brighten, just a little, just at the edges, as if the fire were listening. The ache behind her eyes — the headache she'd carried since the bridge, since the arrow, since the bad night — loosened its grip by one finger's worth.

"Merrin."

Yara's voice. Quiet, but with edges on it. Merrin turned. Yara hadn't moved — still against the wall, sword still across knees — but their eyes were open and fixed on her, and in the coal-light they looked the way the sky looks before hail.

"You were humming," Yara said.

"Was I?" Merrin touched her lips, surprised. "I don't know that one."

"What were the words?"

"There weren't any words." She frowned, trying to catch the tail of the melody before it slipped away. "Just... it goes *up* here, and then it turns, like a stair that turns—" and she hummed it again, three notes of it, before Yara said, sharp and fast:

"Don't."

The word cracked through the cave like ice giving way. Merrin stopped. The coals dimmed. Yara closed their eyes for a moment, and when they opened them again the edges were gone, smoothed over, but Merrin had seen them, the way you see the mountain before fog comes down.

"That song," Yara said, more gently now, "where did you hear it?"

"Nowhere. I told you. It's just — it comes out. Like a sneeze but with notes." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Is it a bad song?"

Yara was quiet for a long time. The wind filled the silence, pushing at the cave mouth, giving up, pushing again.

"It's an old song," Yara said at last. "Older than me. Older than the roads we walk. Go back to sleep, Merrin. We have a long climb tomorrow."

Merrin lay down on the bedroll and pulled the blanket to her chin. But she lay awake a while, listening to Yara not sleeping either, and she thought: *they're afraid of my sneeze.* Which was a funny thought, and would have been funnier if Yara's fear didn't come off them like heat off a stone.

She dreamed again before morning. This time there were no towers. There was only a hall full of people, thousands of them, standing shoulder to shoulder in robes of every color, and every one of them was looking at her, and every one of them was mouthing the same silent word, and though there was no sound she knew exactly what the word was, the way you know your own hand in the dark.

The word was *remember.*

* * *

In the morning they climbed.

The pass south of the shrine was narrow and white, the kind of road Yara called a smuggler's blessing and Merrin called a smuggler's punishment, because it went along a shelf of rock with a drop on one side deep enough to swallow a house and a wall of ice on the other, and the wind came down the funnel of it hard enough to push her sideways. Yara walked in front and set the pace and never once looked nervous, which Merrin appreciated, because she was nervous enough for both of them and possibly for the mountain too.

They stopped at midday in the lee of a boulder, chewing dried plums and hard cheese. Merrin sat with her back against the stone and watched the clouds tear themselves apart on the peaks across the valley.

"Yara," she said. "What was the Choir?"

She had asked before. She kept a little count of it, somewhere inside: this was the fourth time. The first two times Yara had said *an old story, nothing to do with us.* The third time Yara had said nothing at all for so long that Merrin had given up and gone to look at lichen.

This time Yara chewed slowly, swallowed, and looked out at the valley instead of at her.

"Why do you ask now?"

"Because I dreamed about it."

Yara's jaw tightened. "You dreamed about towers," they said. "White towers. With glass bells."

Merrin stared. "How did you—"

"Because everyone who ever sang with them dreamed about the towers. Every one of us, every night, for years after. They said it was the towers dreaming *us*." Yara brushed crumbs from their knee with unnecessary force. "The Choir was an order. Singers. Their songs could do — things. Mend, break, bind, unbind. They kept the peace for a thousand years, and then there was a war, and then there weren't any more Choir." They stood, brushing snow from their seat. "That's the whole story. Eat your plum."

"That's not the whole story," Merrin said. "Stories don't end in the middle. Even I know that, and I don't know anything."

For a moment — just a moment — Yara looked at her with an expression Merrin had no name for. It wasn't anger. It was closer to the face you make when you find a door in a wall you'd lived beside all your life and never noticed, and the door is open, and something is looking out.

"No," Yara said quietly. "They don't." And they shouldered the pack and started walking, and that was the end of the fourth answer, which was somehow both the least and the most of any of them.

They walked. The shelf narrowed. The wind screamed itself hoarse. And somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, with the drop yawning beside her and her mittens aching with cold, Merrin began to sing.

She didn't mean to. That was the terrible, wonderful truth of it — it simply rose in her, the way water rises in a spring, and came out of her mouth in the old language she did not know she knew. The words were round and heavy and sweet, syllables that rolled like river stones, and she understood every single one of them the way you understand warmth, without translating, and she could not have told anyone what any of them meant.

*Vael-ith, vael-ith, sorren kal —*

The ice wall beside them shivered.

Not fell. *Shivered* — a tremor running up its blue-green face like a shiver up a horse's hide, and a sheet of loose snow slid off the top of it and hissed down into the drop below, and above them, high on the cut slope, a load of powder let go and whispered down across the path behind them, sealing the road they'd come by under two feet of fresh snow.

Merrin stopped singing. The last note hung in the cold a heartbeat longer than notes should hang.

Yara had gone very still, one arm thrown out across her chest, holding her back from the edge. They were looking at the ice wall. Then at the buried road behind. Then at her.

"You stopped the slide," Yara said. Not a question. "Or you started it. Either way—" They stopped. Started again. "Do you know what you just sang?"

"No," Merrin said honestly. Her heart was hammering, but not with fear — with something bigger, something that didn't fit right in an eight-year-old chest. "But I know what it *did*. It told the mountain to be careful." She paused. "It told the mountain *please*."

Yara took off their hood, which they never did in the wind, and stood there letting the cold hit their face, and Merrin understood that they were doing it to feel something other than what they were feeling.

"We camp early tonight," Yara said finally, pulling the hood back up. "Off the shelf, down in the pines. And Merrin—" They crouched to her level, and this close she could see how tired their eyes were, tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking. "If the songs start coming during the day, when we're on the road, where people can hear — you squeeze my hand. Twice. Can you do that?"

"Twice," Merrin said. "What happens if I sing where people can hear?"

Yara looked past her, down the valley, toward the south, where somewhere beyond the white peaks the world went gray and smoky and full of men who hunted children for the sounds in their blood.

"Then everything changes," Yara said, "and none of us gets to choose how."

* * *

They made camp in the pines as the light went bruise-colored, in a hollow where the trees grew thick enough to hold the wind off. Yara built a small fire, small even by Yara's standards, and cooked barley with salt pork, and Merrin ate two bowls because climbing makes you hungry and singing, apparently, makes you hungrier.

Afterward Yara sat sharpening the sword, which was what Yara did instead of talking, and Merrin sat picking pine needles out of her mitten seams, which was what she did instead of asking questions, although she was saving up questions the way other children saved up buttons.

"Yara," she said, spending one. "When you were little. Did you dream about the towers too?"

The whetstone paused. Resumed. Paused again.

"Yes."

"Were they nice dreams?"

"They were the best dreams I ever had." Yara said it flatly, to the sword, to the fire. "That's what nobody tells you. That's the trap of it. You spend your whole life afterward trying to get back into a dream, and meanwhile you're awake, and awake is where everyone gets hurt." They sheathed the sword and looked at her across the flames. "Go to sleep, Merrin."

But sleep, when it came, brought the towers again, and this time the dream was different.

This time the courtyard was empty. No voices. No song. Just the white stone and the impossible towers and the terrible silence, and in the middle of the courtyard stood a figure in gray robes with its back to her, and she knew — the way she knew the towers, the way she knew the scar — that this was the one who had sung over her, the one with the kind hands, the one who had folded the seed of memory into her chest and said *forget until it is safe.*

The figure turned. Its face was —

Its face was —

Merrin woke with a cry, sitting bolt upright, the name of the face already dissolving like sugar in hot water, and found Yara crouched beside her with a hand on her shoulder and the other hand on the hilt of the sword, eyes raking the treeline.

"What is it? What did you see?"

"A person," Merrin gasped. "In my dream. From before. They sealed me, they — Yara, I almost saw their face, I almost *remembered*—"

Something crossed Yara's face then, quick as a fish under ice — hope and horror in the same instant, fighting each other, and horror winning by a nose.

"Merrin." Yara's grip on her shoulder tightened, gentle but firm. "Listen to me very carefully. If the memories start coming back — not dreams, memories, real ones, wide-awake ones — you tell me *immediately*. Before you tell anyone. Before you hum a note. Do you understand? Because there are people looking for what's inside you, and some of them want to kill it, and some of them want to take it out of you and use it, and neither of those things will be gentle."

Merrin nodded. Her eyes were stinging. "Why does everyone want what's in my head? I don't even want it."

Yara was quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"Because," Yara said at last, and their voice was low and rough, "it's the truest thing left in the world. And true things are dangerous to people who built everything on lies."

Merrin lay back down. She pulled the blanket up. She squeezed her eyes shut and tried to think about plums, about pine needles, about anything small and safe.

But as she drifted, half-dreaming, she heard herself murmur — aloud, in the old rolling language, in a voice that was hers and yet much older than hers — a single sentence she perfectly understood and could not translate:

*The seed remembers the tree.*

Across the fire, Yara went pale as the snow on the pass.
