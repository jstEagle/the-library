## 13. The Tollhouse Trap

The tollhouse at Miller's Bend had been a smuggler's friend for thirty years, and Yara had known it for ten of those. Old Dagny kept the ledger, took her cut, and asked no questions that couldn't be answered with coin. The road narrowed there between the river and the cliff, funneling all traffic—honest and otherwise—past her shuttered window, and in exchange for a modest percentage she let certain wagons pass unweighed, certain riders pass unnamed.

Yara saw the smoke before they saw the building.

Not hearth-smoke. Hearth-smoke rose lazy and grey in the still morning air, smelling of peat and porridge. This smoke stood straight up from the chimney in a thin black column, disciplined as a soldier's spine, and it smelled of pitch and wet ash—the smell of a fire built fast and hot to burn something quickly. Papers, maybe. Or a body's clothing.

Yara reined the mule to a stop where the road bent around a stand of black pines, well out of sight of the tollhouse yard, and held up a hand. Behind them, Merrin stopped too, huddled in Brannoc's oversized coat with the hood pulled forward over her hair. The girl had learned silence well in three weeks on the road. She didn't ask questions anymore. She just watched Yara's face the way small animals watch the treeline, reading danger in the set of a jaw.

"Stay here," Yara said quietly. "If I don't come back by the time you count two hundred breaths, take the mule down the drover's path. You remember it?"

"The one that smells like sheep."

"The one that smells like sheep." Yara almost smiled. Almost. "Two hundred breaths, Merrin. Not two hundred and one."

Merrin nodded once, solemn as a little grave marker, and Yara left her crouched among the pines and went forward alone, low along the riverbank where the willows grew thick enough to swallow a walking figure whole.

The yard was wrong. That was the first thing. Ten years of living half-invisible taught a person to read a place the way a musician reads a score, noticing the notes that shouldn't be there, and the yard at Miller's Bend was full of them. Dagny's old dog wasn't sleeping on the step. The water trough had been moved two feet from its usual spot, leaving a pale rectangle of dry ground like a scar. And the tollhouse door stood open, though the morning was cold enough to make an open door an act of either stupidity or compulsion.

Three horses tied at the rail. Big-boned, deep-chested, shod heavy. Hunt stock. Yara knew it the way they knew their own heartbeat—by the way the animals stood, patient and hard-used, bred for long pursuit rather than short glory. Their tack was oiled dark, unadorned. No regimental colors. The Hunt didn't wear colors. The Hunt wore nothing that could be remembered afterward.

*They're ahead of us,* Yara thought, and the thought landed in the chest like a stone dropped down a well. *They've been ahead of us since Lowmarsh.*

Because that was the arithmetic that had been quietly assembling itself for days, the sum Yara had refused to finish: the hounds in the marsh had found their trail too easily. The ferry crossing at Nine Bends had been watched but not watched well—as if the watchers didn't need to watch closely because they already knew where the quarry would surface next. Someone had fed the Hunt their route. Or someone had simply been better than Yara at thinking like a hunted thing.

And now the Hunt sat in Dagny's tollhouse, drinking Dagny's tea, waiting for a woman and a child to come walking up the river road because the river road was the only road.

Yara pressed back into the willows and made themselves breathe. Fear was a tool like any other; it had a handle, if you knew where to grip it. Grip the fear. Turn it over. Look at what it showed you.

It showed this: three horses, not six. A squad was four riders minimum, usually five. So either some were mounted and ranging, or—

The door of the tollhouse opened and Dagny came out, and Yara's heart clenched and then unclenched in the space of a single breath, because the old woman walked stiffly but walked free, escorted by a man in a plain grey riding coat who held her elbow with terrible courtesy. He was saying something to her. She was shaking her head. Then he released her, and she went back inside without looking at the yard, and the man turned and scanned the tree line across the river with the unhurried thoroughness of a farmer checking his fields after a storm.

Yara knew that face. Not the face itself—they'd never met—but the *kind* of it. Vael's officers all had it: the pleasant blankness of men who had been emptied of everything except purpose. This one was younger than most, with soft hands and a hard mouth, and he carried his sword the way a scholar carries a book he has memorized but never loved.

Four more riders came out of the trees on the far side of the yard, materializing from shadows Yara would have sworn were empty. Five total, then. A full squad. They'd split—one party visible, one party ghosting the approaches—and the visible party was the lure. The whole tollhouse was a hook baited with routine, and Yara had very nearly swallowed it, because routine was the most dangerous thing a hiding person could love.

*Walk away.* The voice of caution, the voice that had kept them alive for ten years, spoke clearly and reasonably. *Back away through the willows, take the drover's path, put the mountains between you and this place. You've done it before. You can do it again.*

But Merrin was two hundred breaths away, counting, trusting, and the drover's path emerged onto the open hillside a mile east—open ground, and five riders on Hunt stock would run down a mule and a child before the child finished being frightened. And behind them, south, the way they'd come, the marshes were crawling. Yara had felt it for days like a toothache: the net drawing closed, strand by patient strand, until there was no direction left that wasn't watched.

Which meant the Hunt had finally made its mistake. It had assumed Yara Eriksen would do what Yara Eriksen always did. Run. Hide. Fold themselves smaller and smaller until they disappeared.

The officer in the grey coat gave one last look at the willows—directly at the stretch Yara occupied, though the light was wrong for seeing and the willows were thick—and then he said something to his men, and two of them swung into their saddles and rode west along the river road. Toward the pines. Toward the bend. Toward the drover's path.

Toward Merrin.

Later, Yara would try to reconstruct the moment honestly, would try to find the space where choice lived, and would fail. There was no deliberation. There was no weighing. There was only the sight of those two riders moving at a walk toward a hooded child among the pines, and then there was Yara standing up out of the willows with the morning sun full on their face, in the open, in the middle of the road, doing the one thing they had sworn on burning flesh never to do.

"Here," Yara said, and their voice carried across the water with a strange, clean clarity, as if the air itself had leaned in to listen. "I'm what you're looking for."

Everything happened very slowly after that, the way things happen when a life changes shape. The officer turned. The remaining riders reached for their weapons—not hurriedly, not alarmed, because alarm was not in their training; they moved with the smooth economy of men who had done this many times to many people, and the memory of all those other times sat on their faces like a comfortable habit. One of them smiled. That was the worst part, Yara thought distantly. The smiling. As if this were harvest work, familiar and satisfying.

"Yara Eriksen," the officer said. "Inquisitor-General Vael sends his regards. He hoped you'd come quietly. He does so prefer the gifted alive."

"You have me mistaken for someone who intends to be taken."

"No," the officer said, and drew his sword, and the sound of it—a clean silver ring off the steel, echoed a half-beat later by four more rings as the others followed—went through Yara like a bell through the body. "I don't think you are mistaken for anything at all."

Five blades. Five men in grey. A tollhouse full of witnesses behind its shuttered window, and somewhere in the pines a child counting breaths.

Yara had spent ten years keeping the gift locked below the waterline of their life, a drowned bell that must never ring. They had whispered single syllables at seized wagon-hinges and felt sick for hours after. They had let injustice pass on the roads of Thornfell because passing it was the price of survival, and they had told themselves the price was honorable, or at least affordable, and some mornings they had almost believed it.

Now the lock was open, and the bell was swinging, and Yara understood—with the strange lucidity that comes in the last instant before a fall—that they had missed it. Gods forgive them, they had *missed* it.

The first word was not a word. That was the thing no chronicle ever got right, because chronicles were written by people who had only heard swordspeakers from a distance, and from a distance the gift sounded like language. Up close it was older than language. It was the sound a thing makes when it remembers being unmade—when iron recalls the furnace, when the smith's hammer is undone backward into ore and intention. Yara opened their mouth and the sound came up from somewhere beneath the ribs, beneath the diaphragm, from the place where the oath had been knotted ten years ago, and it was not loud. It barely disturbed the air. But every blade in the yard heard it.

The officer's sword came apart.

Not broke—*came apart*, the way a chord comes apart when the singer stops sustaining it. The steel sighed, softened, lost its agreement with itself. For one impossible moment it hung in the air as a shape without substance, a sword-shaped absence, and then it fell in a rain of red-grey dust that the wind took and scattered over the river. The scabbard followed, leather remembering it had once been hide. The buckle remembered it had been ore.

The officer stared at his empty hand. His face did something complicated—fear arriving late, like a guest who'd missed the invitation.

The second rider got his blade halfway raised. Yara sang the second note, lower, and the man's sword died in its sheath, and his dagger died in his boot, and the buckles of his saddle girth dissolved so that his horse sidestepped and left him standing in the stirrups holding nothing. The third and fourth went together, one word with two edges, and their weapons sloughed off them like winter ice off a branch in springtime. The fifth rider—the one who had smiled—actually managed a swing, a real swing, a good arc that would have opened Yara from collar to hip had it connected.

It passed through empty air, because Yara had already stepped inside it, close enough to see the pores of the man's skin, close enough to speak softly, and softly was exactly how they spoke:

"*Sleep, iron.*"

The sword unraveled in mid-arc. The rider stumbled through the falling dust of it, caught himself, and stood there holding a hilt whose blade was now a drift of powder running out between his fingers, and his smile was gone, and what replaced it was the naked face of a man discovering that the world contained something his training had no shelf for.

Silence. River-sound. A crow somewhere upstream, complaining.

Yara stood in the middle of the yard with the gift still singing faintly in their blood, and the feeling that rose in them was so beautiful and so terrible that they would spend years failing to describe it. It was *rightness*. That was the horror of it. Ten years of silence, and the gift fit them like a hand fits a glove worn since childhood—every note landing true, the power rising smooth and eager, asking for more, offering more. Somewhere in the unraveling of those five swords there had been a moment—half a heartbeat, no longer—when Yara had wanted to keep going. To sing the next note higher. To feel what the words could do to armor, to gates, to walls, to the men inside the armor and behind the gates.

Half a heartbeat. And in that half-heartbeat they had understood, finally and completely, why the Choir had burned, and why the oath existed, and why their mentor had chosen the fire rather than break hers.

The officer found his voice. "Gods above," he whispered. He was backing away, hands open and empty, and there was no method in him now, no charm, just a young man in a grey coat whose sword had become dust in front of his eyes. "You're supposed to be the last of them. You're supposed to be *nothing*—"

"I am nothing," Yara said, and heard how their own voice shook. "That was the entire point."

Then the shuttered window of the tollhouse banged open, and Dagny looked out.

The old woman's face was pale as tallow. Her eyes went from the dust on the ground to Yara standing in the open road, and Yara watched the knowledge arrive in her—the recognition, the recalculation, ten years of polite blindness collapsing into sight. Dagny had taken Yara's coin for a decade. Dagny had weighed Yara's wagons and never once looked too closely at the woman who paid in exact change and never hummed. Now she looked. Now she saw.

"It's her," Dagny said, not to anyone, to the room behind her. "Saints preserve us, it's the Eriksen woman. It's a *swordspeaker*."

Behind her, in the dim interior of the tollhouse, Yara could see faces. A carter who'd been waiting out the search. Two drovers sharing a morning jug. A boy, maybe twelve, holding his father's hand. Five pairs of eyes, wide in the gloom, and every one of them now looking at Yara with the expression Yara had spent ten years making sure no human face ever wore in their direction.

Not hatred. Worse than hatred. *Recognition.* The look people give a storm, a plague-ship, a thing that belongs to the old stories and has no business standing in a muddy yard wearing a traveler's cloak.

"They saw," the officer said. He had recovered enough to understand it too, and understanding had put the method back into his voice, cold and precise as a surgeon's probe. "You understand what that means, Eriksen? Every soul in this room will swear it before nightfall. By tomorrow there'll be a broadside with your face on it in every market from here to Greyfall. The Inquisitor-General won't send five riders next time. He'll send fifty. He'll send the hounds and the quiet-men and whatever else he keeps in his cellars." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing comfortable in it. "You've just made yourself the most valuable creature in the Seven Reaches. Congratulations."

From the pines, faint but clear in the still air, came a child's voice, counting: "*One hundred and ninety-eight. One hundred and ninety-nine—*"

The officer's head turned toward the sound. Yara saw him make the calculation—child, witness, leverage—and saw the order form behind his eyes before it ever reached his lips.

Yara moved without deciding to move. Three strides put them between the officer and the tree line, and when they spoke again the gift was in it, not as a weapon but as weight, the way a bell's note fills a church:

"Your weapons are dust. Your horses are a mile away and lame in the near fore, if I'm any judge. And if you take one step toward that child, I will show you what else a swordspeaker can unmake besides steel." A pause. "I would genuinely rather not. Please believe that. It's the truest thing I've said in ten years."

The officer looked at Yara for a long moment. Whatever he saw in their face seemed to satisfy some professional instinct, because he stepped back, and raised one hand, and his disarmed squad—dusty, empty-handed, hollow-eyed—began to withdraw up the road at a walk, not a run, because running would have been an admission.

At the edge of the yard he stopped and turned.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the General was right about you. He said you'd break eventually. He said the kind ones always do." He touched his bare head in something that was almost a salute. "He also said you'd hate it. He was right about that too, I think."

Then they were gone, five men on foot fading up the river road, and the yard was quiet, and the crow was still complaining, and the dust of five swords lay scattered on the mud like the residue of a dream.

Merrin came out of the pines at a run and crashed into Yara's legs, arms locking around their waist, and Yara held her and discovered their hands were shaking and their eyes were wet and their chest was full of something enormous and unspeakable that was equal parts joy and grief.

"You came back," Merrin said into the cloak. "Before two hundred."

"I came back."

"What happened to them? Their swords went all—" she made a gesture, fingers opening like a flower, like a dissolution, "—like that."

Yara crouched to her level. Behind her, the tollhouse door opened and the witnesses came out one by one onto the step—the carter, the drovers, the boy clutching his father—all of them watching, none of them approaching, and Yara knew that every mile from here to Greyfall would now be traveled under eyes, that the invisible decade was over, that the price of five saved seconds and one child's safety would be paid in full and with interest.

"Merrin," Yara said quietly, "we need to talk about what I am. All of it. Before anyone else tells you first."

But even as the girl tilted her head, listening, Yara was looking past her—at Dagny, already hurrying inside, already reaching for paper and ink, because the old woman's survival depended on speed now, on being the first to report, and Yara could not even blame her for it.

By nightfall, Yara knew, the mountains would know.

By week's end, so would the crown.
