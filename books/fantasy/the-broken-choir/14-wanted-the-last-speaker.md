## 14. Wanted: The Last Speaker

*By order of the Crown Hunt, and under the seal of the Inquisitor-General:*

*Let it be known throughout the Thornfell reaches and all lands watered by the Grey that the fugitive styled YARA ERIKSEN, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, is wanted alive for questioning in matters touching the security of the realm.*

*Description: of middle years, unremarkable countenance, hair the color of wet slate, a scar through the left brow. Speaks with the flat vowels of the coast though claiming mountain kinship. Walks softly. Trust nothing soft.*

*REWARD: Five hundred crowns, a pardon for all debts and crimes save murder, and a house-grant in the lowlands, to any who deliver this person to a Crown officer. Two hundred crowns for information leading to the same.*

*The bearer of the gift is dangerous. Do not listen to them sing. Do not ask them questions you do not wish answered. Report sightings to any rider of the Hunt.*

*—posted at every tollhouse, chapel door, and market post from Coldspring to the Salt Pass*

* * *

In Greyhollow, the broadside went up on a grey morning beside the notice of a lost mule, and by noon it had been read aloud so many times that the words had worn smooth as river stones.

"Five hundred crowns," said Widow Cassel, who sold eels at the corner by the well, to anyone within earshot. "For one person. You could buy the whole of Lowmarsh for five hundred crowns, and here they're spending it on one thin swordspeaker."

"They say she unmade six men's weapons at the tollhouse," said the farrier's boy, who had heard it from his cousin, who had heard it from a drover, who had been there or near enough. "Just *unmade* them. Swords turning to rust and rainwater in their scabbards. The riders' hands still shaking three days after."

"It was four men," said the drover himself, drinking ale he had not paid for because everyone wanted him to keep talking. "And it wasn't rust. It was worse than rust. It was like the steel forgot it had ever been steel. I watched a blade come apart like sugar in warm water, and the man holding it just stood there looking at his empty hand like a man who's forgotten why he walked into a room." He drank. "I've seen war. I never saw anything like that."

"And now they want her *alive*," said the schoolmaster, who liked to be precise. "That's the part that sits wrong with me. If she's so dangerous, why not kill her? Why pay more for her breathing?"

Nobody had an answer for that, but everybody had a theory, and the theories were passed around the well and the tavern and the chapel steps like coins of small denomination, each one slightly clipped.

"She knows something."

"She stole something."

"The Inquisitor wants to study her. They say he has machines down in the cells that drink memories out of your head like whey through cheesecloth."

"They say the old woman they took in autumn — the one who hummed — they say when they brought her out of the cells she didn't know her own daughter's face. Just sat there humming, except now she hummed the *crown's* hymn, over and over, like something had been written into her."

"That's a lie."

"That's what my cousin's wife heard from the laundress who works the palace laundry, and the laundress has no reason to lie about humming."

What nobody said aloud, in Greyhollow, was the thing that was said quietly, in kitchens, behind doors, in the pauses between sentences: that some of them had heard the old cadences from their grandmothers. That a lullaby was only a lullaby until someone told you it was forbidden, and then you lay awake at night hearing it in your own mouth, wondering what else lived in you unbidden. That when the criers read the proclamation and the crowd had murmured its fear, there had been — here and there, quickly smothered — a different sound underneath. Something almost like longing.

* * *

At the Salt Pass, the broadside nailed to the customs post flapped in a wind that smelled of snow, and a Kestrel clan courier named Dagny read it twice, memorized it, and rode hard for the ferry crossing where Sera Kestrel kept her winter court.

Sera listened without expression while Dagny recited it, then took the paper herself and held it close to the brazier's light.

"Wet slate hair," she said. "Scar through the left brow. That's accurate. Whoever wrote this has seen her up close." She turned the page over. "No artist's likeness. Interesting."

"They're printing them in Greyfall," Dagny said. "A likeness is coming. The Ferrywoman says a rider came down from the capital two days ago carrying a sealed case, and whatever was in it went straight to the Hunt camp at Coldspring."

"A likeness." Sera fed the broadside to the fire and watched it curl. "You can't arrest a likeness. But you can make every honest soul in the mountains look at it over their breakfast until the face becomes an itch they have to scratch." She looked up. "Where is she now?"

"No one knows. That's the point of her, isn't it?"

"That is indeed the point of her." Sera sat back. "We gave her passage and we gave her silence, and we named our price, and she hasn't paid it yet. Until she does, she's not ours. But hear me, Dagny — if the Hunt comes asking the Kestrels about roads, we know no roads. We are simple folk who move wool and whiskey and occasionally other things, and we have never in our lives seen a woman with a scar through her left brow."

"And if they show us the likeness?"

Sera smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Then we will squint at it and say she looks like someone's aunt in Millbrook, and isn't it strange how all these swordswomen look alike."

* * *

In the tavern at Lowmarsh, where the river fog came in through walls that had never quite been weatherproof, the talk ran darker, because Lowmarsh had lost people to the Hunt already.

Brannoc's name came up the way names do in small places — casually, and then not casually at all.

"The healer?" said the ferryman. "Old Brannoc? He's half-pickled most days. What would the Hunt want with him?"

"He knew her. Everyone knows he knew her. He was Choir, before. Before it burned."

"He was a *healer*. He set my brother's leg after the rockslide and charged me nothing but a bottle, and the leg healed straighter than the other one, which I always thought was showing off."

"That's what I'm saying. Showing off. These gifted ones can't help it. And now there's a price on his friend's head and he's sitting right there in the marsh like a lure on a line."

"You think they'd take him?"

"I think," said the first speaker, lowering his voice, which in Lowmarsh meant leaning closer because the fog ate whispers, "that two nights ago there were riders at the tollhouse asking after him. Asking how much he drinks. Asking whether he'd fight. Asking if anyone would miss him."

The tavern went quiet in the particular way of small places, where quiet is a whole conversation.

Because that was the arithmetic nobody wanted to do aloud: that Brannoc was known, and loved a little, and drunk often, and easy to find; and that the crown did not need Yara Eriksen to walk into a trap of its making when it could simply build the trap out of everything she might care about, and wait.

* * *

Brannoc, in fact, was doing what he always did on grey evenings, which was sitting in his surgery with a bottle he pretended was medicinal and a memory he made no such pretense about.

He had heard the broadside read aloud in the market that morning — a crier with a good voice, wasted on bad news — and he had stood in the crowd with his hood up and his heart going like a hammer in a bell tower, listening to his own description of Yara read out to forty indifferent faces. Wet slate hair. Scar through the left brow. *Trust nothing soft.*

He had almost laughed. Almost. Because whoever wrote it had gotten her exactly right and understood her not at all, and that, Brannoc thought, was the whole history of the Choir in miniature.

He poured another measure and looked at his hands. Healer's hands, once. They had trembled even before the drink, in the years since, but they remembered things hands should not remember: the weight of a Cantor's staff, the pressure of sealing wax and song against a child's sternum, the exact temperature of Mentor Ellin's forehead the day she burned and he did not.

*They'll come for me,* he thought, with the clarity that drink sometimes brought instead of sleep. *Not because I matter. Because I'm the hook, and she's the fish, and Vael is patient the way winter is patient.*

He considered running. He had a boat. He had the marshes, which he knew better than the men who patrolled them, having healed half of them and buried two. He could be in the Kestrel country in three days, or across the border in five, and live out his years as some nameless herb-man in a place where no one sang anything at all.

He looked at the shelf above his desk, at the row of jars and the small clay whistle he had not touched in ten years, and he thought about a girl of eight with a scar over her heart and no name, and a stubborn, frightened, magnificent fool walking her through blizzards toward a truth the size of a kingdom.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, and was surprised at how steady his voice was. "I'm done running ahead of you, Ellin. One of us should stand somewhere."

He finished the bottle, because tomorrow he would need a clear head and clear heads were easier to come by after a bad night's sleep than after sobriety abruptly abandoned, and he banked the fire, and he slept in his chair with the whistle in his hand like a talisman, which was foolish, and he knew it was foolish, and he did it anyway.

* * *

They came at dawn, which showed a certain professional respect.

There were eight of them, and they did not kick the door. They knocked. That was the thing about the Inquisitor-General's people that unsettled Lowmarsh more than violence would have: the courtesy. The knock. The officer at the door with his hat in his hands like a man calling on a widow, saying, "Master Brannoc? We'd like a word about a mutual acquaintance," while six others moved quietly around the back of the house through the fog.

Brannoc opened the door himself, dressed, shaved, sober-faced, the whistle left in plain sight on the desk where a careless man would leave it.

"About time," he said. "I've been expecting you since the broadside went up. Your crier has a lovely voice, by the way. Has anyone told him he's wasting it?"

The officer blinked. Whatever script he had prepared evidently did not include this. "You'll come with us."

"I'll walk, too. My knees are fifty-eight years old and they don't run." Brannoc stepped out into the fog, looked once at the marsh — at the grey water and the grey reeds and the grey sky, all of it beautiful and none of it his — and raised his voice so the neighbors peering from doorways could hear: "Somebody feed my cat! There's dried fish in the blue crock!"

There was no cat. There had not been a cat in nine years. But the neighbors would tell the story of it for a month, and in the telling, the story would travel, and stories traveled faster than riders along the smugglers' roads, which was the entire reason he had said it.

They bound his wrists with cord, not iron — iron, the Hunt had learned, rang against certain voices — and put him in a wagon, and the wagon rolled east toward Coldspring, where the Inquisitor-General kept his tents and his quiet machines and his endless, courteous patience.

* * *

Vael received the report that evening by lamplight, reading it twice, as he read everything twice.

*Subject taken without resistance. Subject verbal, cooperative in manner, evasive in substance. Subject requested, unprompted, that his cat be fed.*

The Inquisitor-General set the paper down and looked at the map pinned to the tent wall — the Thornfells stitched with red thread, the roads, the passes, the little inked crown marking the shrine in the high country that his scholars insisted had been a Choir site.

"There's no cat," said Captain Ilsabet, from the tent flap.

"No," Vael agreed. "There is no cat."

"He wanted us to know he expected us. He wanted the neighbors to hear it. He's sending a message."

"Yes. He's telling her he didn't run." Vael rose and stood before the map, hands clasped behind his back, a lean and courteous silhouette against the lamplight. "Which means he believes she'll come for him. Which means either he knows her better than I do—" he tilted his head, considering, "—or he's lying to her the way he lied to my extraction team today."

Ilsabet frowned. "Lied how? The machine pulled nothing. He just... hummed at it. The resonance readers say it was like trying to drink from a mirror."

"Oh, he gave us something." Vael touched the map, lightly, over the marsh country. "He gave us a memory. A false one, beautifully made — I could feel the seams, barely. A memory of the Choir's fall in which the speakers scattered north, toward the Kestrel highlands, in the spring. He sang it to the device willingly, the way a man feeds a stray dog so it will follow him home." He smiled, and it was a gentle smile, and his officers had learned to be afraid of it. "So. Either the old man is protecting a route that runs north — in which case she runs south, and he knows I'll guess that, in which case perhaps she runs north after all. Or the memory is true and he is cleverer than a decade of drink suggests. Or—" he paused, savoring it, "—he is telling me exactly what he wants me to believe, and the truth is somewhere no one has looked, because everyone is looking at the liar."

"Where do we search, sir?"

"All of it." Vael took a pin between finger and thumb. "But we do it slowly, and we do it loudly, and we let every village from here to the capital know that the healer sits in our cells, comfortable, unharmed, *waiting* — because bait that looks like bait catches nothing, and bait that looks like mercy catches everything." He pressed the pin into the map at Coldspring, then another at the shrine, then a third, after a moment's thought, at the road to Greyfall itself. "Post the likeness tomorrow. Every market, every chapel, every mile-stone. Let her face look down from every wall in the mountains like a saint in reverse."

"And if she comes for the prisoner?"

Vael turned from the map, and for a moment the lamplight caught something in his face that was not cruelty and not kindness but the pure, cold pleasure of a man watching a machine of his own design begin, at last, to turn.

"Then, Captain," he said, "we shall finally meet the last speaker of the Choir. And I promise you — I will be very, very polite."

Far to the west, past the marshes and the ferry and the nine bends of the hidden road, in a hollow beneath a storm-worn ridge where a small fire burned low, Yara Eriksen woke suddenly in the dark with Merrin asleep against their shoulder — woke with the absolute, wordless certainty, the way one wakes to a sound already ended, that somewhere behind them a door had closed, and a friend was on the wrong side of it.
