## 15. The Ferrywoman's Price Called Due

The rain had followed them for two days, thin and needling, the kind that did not fall so much as hang in the air waiting to be breathed. Yara came down out of the high pines with Merrin's small hand locked in their own and the road opening below them like a gray tongue: the Road of Nine Bends, or what remained of it where it met the water.

The Vess lay wide and slow at the bottom of the valley, brown as old tea, carrying whole trees on its back without effort. Mist stood on it in columns. And moored at the near bank, exactly where the Ferrywoman's runners had said she would be, sat the barge — a long black hull with a cabin like a coffin stood upright, lanterns burning behind horn panes the color of drowned men's teeth.

Yara stopped at the tree line and looked at it for a long time.

They had paid her once already. Eleven days ago, at the first bend, Yara had given the Ferrywoman a memory: the taste of their mother's bread, the sound of her humming, the shape of the kitchen where both had lived. A true memory, freely given, torn out clean. It was the toll for passage through smuggler territory, and Yara had paid it because the alternative was the open king's road, and the king's road meant the Hunt.

But there had been a second thing asked of them, later, at the fifth bend, when the Hunt's hounds had come close and Yara had needed the ferry again — needed it desperately, with Merrin shivering and the resonance-hounds feeling their way through the dark toward the child's sealed heart. The Ferrywoman had named a price then too, and Yara had lied to her. Had offered up a memory that was not a memory at all but a construction: a false scene, built from fragments, of a village burning and a brother dying in it. It was a lie stitched from real thread, which was the only kind of lie that ever worked on anyone who dealt in truth.

Yara had known even then, walking away across the water, that it might not hold. The Ferrywoman trafficked in secrets the way other merchants trafficked in salt, and no one stayed in that trade for forty years by failing to know when they were being robbed.

"Is that her?" Merrin whispered. She had pulled her hood back despite the rain. Her seal scar showed pale at her collarbone, a thin silver line over her heart like a crack in porcelain.

"That's her."

"She looks like a church."

Yara almost smiled. "She'd take that as a compliment. Stay close. Say nothing unless she asks you something directly. If she asks you something directly—" Yara crouched, took the girl's shoulders, made their eyes meet. "Lie badly. On purpose. She'll find a deliberate lie charming. She'll find a good one insulting."

"Why?"

"Because a bad lie says you respect her. A good one says you think she's stupid." Yara straightened. "And nobody robs the Ferrywoman twice."

* * *

The gangplank was slick with rain and older than the war. Yara crossed it first, boots ringing on wet wood, and the cabin door opened before they reached it — opened inward on warmth and lamplight and the smell of pipe smoke and dried eels and something else underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off deep ice.

The Ferrywoman sat behind a table that had once been a ship's door laid flat on barrels. She was small. That was always the shock of her, every time: everyone expected a giant, a hag, a woman carved from river stone, and instead there was this neat, compact person with white hair cropped short as a soldier's and hands folded on the wood like a woman at prayer. Her eyes were the color of the Vess in flood.

"You look tired, swordspeaker," she said. Her voice was dry paper. "Sit. Both of you. The girl can have the stool. I don't bite children. Adults, occasionally."

Merrin climbed onto the stool with the careful solemnity of a child entering a temple. Yara remained standing.

"You knew," Yara said.

The Ferrywoman poured three cups of something dark from a dented pot without asking. "I knew before you finished saying it. Sit down, Yara Eriksen. You'll want your legs under you for this conversation."

Yara sat. The tea tasted of pine resin and iron.

"The burning village," the Ferrywoman said, folding her hands again. "The brother. Ash on the wind, a name half-spoken, all that lovely grief. You gave it to me at the fifth bend with tears standing in your eyes, and I took it, and I thanked you, and I let you cross." She sipped. "And then I held it up to the light, the way I hold everything up to the light, and do you know what I saw?"

"I imagine you're going to tell me."

"A seam." She held up one finger. "One seam. Grief is never seamless, swordspeaker. Real memories have loose threads everywhere — smells that don't belong, faces that change, doors that were painted a different color. Yours was too well-made. Too tidy. Somebody had built it rather than lived it." Her eyes glittered. "And there are perhaps four people alive who could build a memory well enough to fool me for even an hour. I've been wondering for eleven days which of them you were."

Yara set down the cup. Their mouth had gone dry. "You let us cross anyway."

"I let you cross because I was curious how far you'd go. And because the Hunt was close, and I dislike the Hunt more than I dislike being robbed." She leaned forward. "But curiosity is not charity, Yara Eriksen. Debts on my water are debts. You knew my law when you stepped aboard the first time. One true memory, freely given, for passage. You gave me a forgery. That makes you not a customer but a thief, and I have drowned thieves in this river for less than what you stole."

Merrin had gone very still on her stool. Yara felt the girl's fear like a change in air pressure — and beneath it, faintly, horribly, felt something else. The seal scar warming. The child's gift stirring toward the Ferrywoman the way a compass needle stirs toward north.

"No," Yara said quietly, to the girl, and Merrin looked down at her hands.

"What was that?" the Ferrywoman asked pleasantly.

"Nothing. A word to the child." Yara kept their voice level. "Name your price."

"Oh, I will." The Ferrywoman rose, unhurried, and went to the cabin's rear wall, where dozens of small objects hung on pegs and nails: keys, coins, buttons, a bird's skull, a lock of hair tied with red string, a glass eye. Each one, Yara knew, was a memory someone had paid, kept here in physical form because the Ferrywoman trusted objects more than heads. Objects didn't lie about what they held. "You owe me one true memory of weight equal to what you attempted to steal. Not equal in size. Equal in weight. My scales, my measure."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take the girl instead."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Yara heard them land all the way down.

"The crown wants her," the Ferrywoman went on, turning back, and now her pleasantness had gone out of her voice entirely, leaving something flat and old. "The Inquisitor-General has offered ten thousand crowns for a child with a scar over her heart, delivered breathing. Do you know what ten thousand crowns buys on these rivers? Every boat. Every bend. Every pair of eyes from the source to the sea. I could retire a queen." She tilted her head. "So. The memory, or the child. Choose quickly; the rain is rising and I have cargo waiting."

Yara's mind moved fast and cold, sorting options the way they'd once sorted sword-forms. Fight: the Ferrywoman had six crew somewhere on this barge, and Yara was strong but not six-strong with a child to shield. Flee: the far bank was marsh, and the marsh was where the hounds had nearly taken them once already. Pay: pay with what? Every true memory of weight was a load-bearing wall in the house of who Yara was. Take away enough walls and nothing stood.

There was the night their mentor burned. There was the oath itself, spoken word for word. There was the face of the first person Yara had ever unbound steel in front of, and the look on it. There was—

No. Not those. Those were the last of them, and the Ferrywoman would know their worth, and after they were gone Yara would be a hollow coat walking around holding a child's hand.

"There's a third option," Yara said.

"There's always a third option. It's usually worse."

"You keep the debt," Yara said. "Open-ended. Standing. I come back and pay it when I have a memory to spare — when this is done, when the girl is safe, when I'm free to cut something loose. You know who I am. You know what I am. My word has been good on these roads for ten years, and you know it, and that's worth more than any single memory, because I'll still be swimming in true things long after tonight."

The Ferrywoman looked at them for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, dry sound, like a stick breaking.

"Do you hear yourself? 'When this is done.' Swordspeaker. Everyone who says 'when this is done' dies before it's done, or wishes they had." She shook her head slowly. "You want credit. From me. From the woman whose entire power rests on the fact that all accounts settle on the water, tonight, in cash." She walked to the wall of tokens and touched the bird's skull gently, the way another woman might touch a grandchild's cheek. "Do you know why I deal in memories and not gold? Gold can be counterfeited. Memories can only be stolen. And you—" she turned, and her flood-colored eyes were terrible in the lamplight, "—stole from me. So no. No credit. No standing debt. No third option. The memory of true weight, or the girl. I've been patient. Patience is expensive."

The seal scar was warm now. Yara could feel it without touching the girl, a faint heat in the air between them like a banked coal. Merrin's breath had gone quick and shallow. And Yara understood, with a sick lurch, that the child was beginning to answer the threat — that some sleeping thing under her breastbone had heard the word *take* and was waking to object.

If it woke fully, here, in this cabin, on this water — the Ferrywoman would get far more than she bargained for. So might the whole valley.

"Then we're done," Yara said, and stood.

"Swordspeaker—"

"We're done." Yara lifted Merrin off the stool and set her behind them. "I won't pay you a true memory, because the ones I have left are the last beams holding up my roof, and I won't give you the girl, because she isn't mine to sell and never was. And I won't fight you, because you've been fairer to me than most, and I don't kill old women over a ledger."

"Careful," the Ferrywoman said softly.

"But I won't leave you my debt either." Yara backed toward the door, drawing Merrin with them, and the cold mineral smell of the cabin seemed to sharpen, the way air does before lightning. "You wanted payment in fire and truth. Here's your truth: whatever you think you know about the girl, whatever the crown told you she is — it's less than half. And the half you don't know will eat boats like yours for breakfast if anyone tries to collect her. Keep your water. I'll keep my road."

"You are making an enemy of the only neutral party left in these mountains," the Ferrywoman said, and for the first time something moved under her voice — not anger. Regret. "I liked you, Yara Eriksen. That was a mistake I rarely make."

"I know," Yara said. "Me too."

They went out onto the deck. Rain hammered the planks. Behind them the cabin door stayed open, lamplight spilling yellow across the wet wood, and the Ferrywoman's voice followed them, dry and clear:

"Every ferry on the Vess is mine, swordspeaker. Every crossing, every bend, every bridge from Greyhollow to the delta. You've just made nine hundred miles of river into a wall." A pause. "Unless."

Yara stopped. Hated themselves for stopping.

"Unless?" Merrin whispered against their side.

"Unless you pay before you reach the far bank," the Ferrywoman called. "My law is my law. But my law ends at the waterline. Cross, and be free of me forever — and hunted on every river in Thornfell until the day the Hunt takes you, which, at current speed, will be soon."

Yara looked at the gangplank. Then past it, to the far bank — and to the bridge.

It was the only bridge for twenty miles: an old smuggler span, rope and timber, thrown across the narrows where the Vess ran fast and white between stone jaws. It predated the war. Smugglers had maintained it for generations because bridges, unlike ferries, belonged to whoever held the banks, and the clans had always held the banks. It was the one piece of crossing in the valley the Ferrywoman did not own — the one loophole in her empire, kept in repair by mutual agreement because even she had found it useful.

Yara looked at it for a long moment. Then at the lanterns of the barge. Then at the girl pressed against their side, shaking, her seal scar glowing faintly now through her wet shirt like a coal wrapped in cloth.

"Merrin," Yara said quietly. "Can you run?"

"Yes."

"Run."

They went down the gangplank together at a dead sprint, boots slamming wet boards, and behind them the Ferrywoman's voice rose — not in fury, but in something worse, something almost sorrowful: "So be it! The water remembers, swordspeaker! The water remembers everything!"

The bridge swayed under them as they crossed, rope rails slick, planks drumming, the white water roaring below through the stone jaws. Halfway across, Yara risked one glance back.

Lanterns were moving on the barge. Figures. And at the rail, small and neat and dry under the rain as though the rain refused to touch her, the Ferrywoman stood watching — not following, not sending anyone. Just watching, the way one watches a debt walk away that one intends to collect with interest.

Or the way one watches a fuse burn.

Yara understood one heartbeat too late.

"Off the bridge!" they shouted, and hauled Merrin the last ten feet, and hit the far bank rolling as the first arrow took the near anchorage — not aimed at them, never aimed at them. Aimed at the ropes.

The Ferrywoman's crew had brought firepots. Of course they had. The bridge was the loophole; the loophole was an insult; and insults on the Vess were settled the same way everything was settled on the Vess. Flame caught the soaked hemp with a hiss and then a roar, and the span sagged, groaned, and folded into the narrows like a knee giving way, timbers cartwheeling white water, the fire hissing to steam as the river swallowed its first meal in a century.

Yara lay in the mud on the far bank with Merrin clutched to their chest and watched the last burning section drift downstream past the black barge, past the woman at the rail, who raised one hand — not in farewell. In accounting.

Nine hundred miles of river, closed. The last neutral ally in the mountains, converted to an enemy with one match. And ahead of them, east and south, the capital road, the Hunt, the net — and nowhere left to hide that someone hadn't sold.

Merrin was crying silently, the way children cry when they've learned crying costs extra.

"It's all right," Yara said, and heard how the lie sounded, and said it again anyway, because some lies were load-bearing too. "We didn't need the river. We never needed the river."

Behind them, across the roaring narrows, the Ferrywoman's lanterns turned downstream, toward Greyhollow. Toward the Hunt. Toward whoever paid best for news of a swordspeaker and a marked child.

Toward the Inquisitor-General, who paid in crowns, and crowns, unlike memories, could not be forged.

Yara got up, lifted Merrin onto their hip — she was lighter than she should have been, too many days of hard road — and turned east into the rain, toward the high passes and the long cold climb, carrying the last secret in Thornfell on one hip and the ashes of every bridge behind them.

By morning, Yara knew, every smuggler from the pass to the delta would know what they had done at the narrows. By evening, so would Vael.

The race was no longer to stay hidden.

The race was to reach the capital before the story did.
