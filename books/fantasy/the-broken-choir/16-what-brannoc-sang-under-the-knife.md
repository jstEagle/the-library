## 16. What Brannoc Sang Under the Knife

They took his boots first.

Brannoc had expected the manacles, the hood, the long jolting ride in a wagon that smelled of wet canvas and old blood. He had even expected the boots—there was a story about that, an old Choir story about a healer in the second war who hid a resonance shard in his heel and sang half a garrison to sleep with it. The Inquisitors' manuals remembered everything the Choir had ever taught them, which was the bitter joke of it all: the crown's hunters were the Choir's best students.

So when the guard knelt and pulled his boots off with careful, almost reverent hands, Brannoc almost laughed.

"Something amusing?" the guard asked. He was young. They were always young.

"Just thinking," Brannoc said, "that I'd have hidden it in my hat."

The guard did not smile. The cell door closed behind him with a sound like a held breath, and Brannoc was alone in the dark with the cold of the stone coming up through his stockinged feet and the memory of Merrin's small hand slipping out of his as they'd taken him—*run, girl, run, don't look back, don't you dare look back*—and he sat down against the wall and let himself shake for exactly as long as he allowed himself to shake, which was until his breath stopped hitching, and not one moment more.

He was sixty-one years old. He had been a healer of the Choir of the Nine Towers before the Choir was ash, and he had been a drunk of Lowmarsh after, and both of those men knew something the young guard did not: that fear was a tide, and tides could be waited out.

The cell was small, dry, and windowless. A slot in the door. A bucket. A bench bolted to the wall so it could not be burned or broken or used. Someone had thought about this room. Someone had thought about it the way a surgeon thinks about a body on the table—with respect, and with no love whatsoever.

Brannoc closed his eyes and began, silently, to count his heartbeats. It was the oldest discipline there was, older than the Choir, older than the towers: *the body is a road, walk it slowly.* He counted four hundred beats before the slot scraped open and a bowl of barley soup came through, and another six hundred before the door itself opened and two guards in grey said, "Up. The General will see you now."

* * *

He had imagined Vael, these past three days, as a monster. It was the sensible thing to do. Monsters were easier to prepare for; monsters announced themselves.

The Inquisitor-General of the Crown Hunt turned out to be a lean, silver-templed man of perhaps fifty, dressed not in the black-and-crimson of the Hunt but in plain charcoal wool, sitting behind a table in a warm room lit by a fire and two good lamps, rising from his chair when Brannoc was brought in, and offering—of all things—a chair.

"Healer Brannoc," Vael said. "Please. Sit. Your feet are bare and the floor is stone; I won't have you standing on ceremony or cold flagstones either."

The room was nothing like a torture chamber. That was the first thing wrong with it. There were books along one wall, actual books, spines cracked with use. There was a decanter of wine and two glasses. There was a brazier, and beside the brazier, on a stand of black iron, a device Brannoc recognized so violently that his stomach dropped as if the floor had opened under him.

It looked like a harp that had been argued with. A frame of silvered bone—no, not bone, he corrected himself, *chitin*, from the deep-cave moths the Choir had once farmed in the dark—and strung across it, instead of gut, a single long filament of drawn glass, fine as spider silk, humming faintly in the warmth of the room. At its base, a horn of hammered copper flared outward like a lily. Like a throat.

A *throatcatcher*. Last of its kind, probably. The Choir had made eleven. The Choir had made them to record dying songs—the last words of masters, sung into the glass so the melody would outlive the flesh. He had stood beside one at forty years old and watched old Mother Cass sing her death-hymn into it, and the glass had caught her voice like frost catches a fern, and everyone in the room had wept, and it had been holy.

Someone had taken the holiest instrument he had ever known and turned it into a knife.

"You're looking at my little treasure," Vael said, following his gaze. He said it without embarrassment. "Yes. It's one of theirs. I imagine you know what it does better than I do—I've only read about it." He poured the wine. "I've always found that the people who built things understand them best. Shall we begin?"

"Begin what?" Brannoc took the chair because his legs advised it. He took the wine too, because he was who he was, and because refusing gifts from Vael would tell Vael more than accepting them.

"The truth," Vael said pleasantly. "About the child."

* * *

Here is what Brannoc knew, and had known since the moment the throatcatcher's copper horn was wheeled to within arm's reach of him: he was going to lose.

Not might. *Was.* He had spent ten years drowning the gift in grain spirit precisely so that no one could pull it out of him, and it had worked—he was nearly deaf to resonance now, a singer gone hoarse, a bell full of cracks—but the throatcatcher did not need his cooperation the way an interrogator did. It listened to the body. To the small involuntary harmonies of memory itself, the way a remembered voice still moved the muscles of the throat. Ten years of drink had muddied his instrument, but mud was not silence. Given time, given heat, given pain, the glass would find the song underneath.

And the song underneath was Yara. And the song underneath *that* was a child with a seal scar over her heart carrying the founding memory of the world.

So he was going to lose, and the only question left—the only freedom remaining to a man chained to a chair in a warm room with good wine—was *what* he lost *with*.

Vael began gently, as Brannoc had known he would. Questions about Lowmarsh. About the smuggling roads, offered almost apologetically, as though asking a favor. About the winter, the passes, the traffic of fugitives. Brannoc answered in the accent of the drunk he had played for a decade: vague, sullen, self-pitying, a man whose memories had gone soft at the edges from the bottle. It cost him something real to perform that man again—to put the mask of his own shame back on—but the mask fit; he had worn it so long it had grown to the bone.

Then, an hour in, over the second glass of wine, Vael set down his own untouched cup, folded his hands, and said, with the mildness of a man discussing weather:

"I'm going to be honest with you, Healer, because I think you're a man who respects honesty. The device works. You know it works. We can do this the kind way, where you talk and I listen and perhaps in a year, if you've been useful, there's a farm somewhere and a pardon. Or we can do it the other way." He glanced at the throatcatcher almost fondly. "The other way takes longer, and you'll remember less of your life afterward, and I'll still get what I came for. But I want you to understand that I take no pleasure in it. What I take pleasure in—" and here his voice changed, just slightly, warmed, the way a stove warms a room, "—is the music itself. I've heard three speakers sing into that glass. Do you know what I learned? That the gift isn't power. Everyone thinks it's power. It isn't. It's *memory*. Perfect memory, carried in sound. The crown has spent two hundred years telling everyone the swordspeakers were weapons. I'm the only man in Greyfall who knows they were libraries."

Brannoc kept his face still, but inside, something went very cold and very clear.

Because that was not the speech of a butcher. That was the speech of a collector. And a collector, unlike a butcher, could not be fed lies about small things. A collector would hear a false note the way a jeweler hears paste.

*Good*, thought the man who had once been a healer of the Nine Towers. *Then we shall give him no false notes. We shall give him a whole true-seeming song.*

* * *

They strapped him into the chair beside the brazier at what he judged to be past midnight. The restraints were padded. That detail undid him more than cruelty would have; someone had *upholstered* this thing, had thought about comfort, had understood that the body must be kept calm for the glass to hear clearly. The throatcatcher's horn was brought close—not touching, never touching, hovering a handspan from his sternum like a snake tasting air—and the glass filament above it began, very softly, to sing.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt like being *remembered*. Like walking through your own house in the dark and finding every lamp already lit, every drawer open, every letter you'd ever written laid out on the table for a guest to read. His mother's kitchen. The smell of the infirmary soap. The first corpse he'd ever washed, a drowned boy, fourteen. The taste of cheap spirits, decade upon decade of it, laid out in amber sequence. The fall of the towers—he flinched, and the glass sang louder, hungry, and Vael said quietly, "Let it come. Fighting it only tears things. Let it come, Healer. I only want the winter. Only the last few months. Tell me about the pass. Tell me about the child."

And here is what Brannoc did, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, while the glass drank the light out of his memories:

He remembered that the throatcatcher did not record thoughts. It recorded *song*—the body's music, the harmonics of what a person truly believed had happened. It could not be lied to with words.

But it had never once, in all the history of the Choir, been tested against a master of the healing cadences. Against a man whose entire art was the shaping of what bodies believed.

You could not lie to the glass. But you could *sing to it*. And every healer of the Nine Towers learned, in their third year, the discipline the masters called *the borrowed wound*: how to lay a memory into a patient's flesh that was not theirs—a pain remembered, a fever patterned, a grief shaped and placed—so completely that the body lived it as truth. It was forbidden. It was the deepest taboo of his order, worse than killing, because it remade a person from the inside. He had sworn, at nineteen, with his hand on the tower stone, never to use it on anyone unwilling.

The glass hovered at his chest, drinking.

*Forgive me,* he said, to the tower stone, to Mother Cass, to the drowned boy, to whatever was left of the Choir to hear it. *Forgive me. I am about to break the last law I have left.*

And Brannoc, last healer of the Nine Towers, began to compose.

* * *

He gave it to the glass in pieces, the way a cook feeds a fire.

A road—true, a real road, the Salt Road above Greyhollow, because true bones make false flesh believable. Snow on it—true; there had been snow. A night, a waystation, a fire. And into that true scaffold he wove the borrowed memory, singing it silently through the cage of his own ribs, laying it into his own body with the terrible intimate craft of his art, until his flesh believed it, until his heartbeat believed it, until the small muscles of his throat shaped the harmony of a thing that had never happened:

*A woman. Tall, grey-eyed, sword at her hip—Yara Eriksen, yes, let the glass have her name, let it have her face, better they hunt a name than a shadow. She comes to his door in Lowmarsh with a child. But the child is not what they think. The child is dying—no seal scar, no founding memory, nothing but a wasting sickness of the lungs, the same fever that took the marsh villages two winters back. Yara begs. Brannoc refuses—too dangerous, too hunted—and Yara leaves the girl anyway, and the girl dies in his care nine days later, small and light as a bird, and he buried her under the alders behind the chapel in Lowmarsh, and he can show them the grave, oh, he can show them the grave, dig it up, weigh the little bones.*

He wove grief into it—real grief, harvested honestly from the true deaths he had known, from the purge, from the burning of Mother Cass, from every patient he had ever failed—and the grief was the mortar that held the lie together. The glass drank it and trembled and drank deeper.

And then, the masterpiece, the part that cost him most: he let the glass find his love for Yara. Not hidden. *Found*—he loosened his grip and let it pour out, ten years of guilt and loyalty and the old bright days in the towers when Yara had been a furious brilliant student and he had been the one who bandaged her hands after she practiced too long. Let Vael see a broken old man's love for a friend he believes dead or damned. Let Vael see the confession underneath: *she brought me a dying child and I could not save her, and I hate her a little for leaving her, and I loved her anyway, and she is gone now, gone north over the Kestrel passes alone, grieving, armed, dangerous, and utterly empty-handed.*

The girl died. The girl was buried. Yara fled alone. Three clean sentences, sung in flesh and glass, and every note of them false except the snow.

Sweat ran down Brannoc's temples. The effort of the borrowed wound was enormous—it was like lifting a house with his mind—and somewhere past the second hour his nose began to bleed, a thin thread of red over his lip, and Vael leaned forward with genuine concern and said, "Stop. Stop, Healer, breathe," and Brannoc laughed, a cracked wet sound, and let his head fall back.

"There," he whispered. "Take it. Take all of it. It's yours. It was never any use to me."

* * *

Dawn came grey through a high slit window Brannoc hadn't noticed in the dark. Vael stood at the brazier, turning the throatcatcher's copper horn slowly in his hands, listening—we assumed listening—to the ghost-song caught in the glass. His face was unreadable, which frightened Brannoc more than anger would have.

"A grave under the alders," Vael said at last. "Behind the chapel in Lowmarsh."

"If you dig it up," Brannoc rasped, "you'll find what's left of a child who deserved better than both of us."

"And Eriksen went north. Alone. After burying her."

"After burying her." Brannoc let his voice crack on it, and did not have to work hard; some of the grief in it was real, just aimed at the wrong grave.

Vael was quiet a long moment. Then he crossed the room, and to Brannoc's astonishment, unstrapped the restraints himself, gentle as a nurse, and stood looking down at him with something that was almost—almost—respect.

"Do you know," Vael said, "in twenty years of this work, I have extracted perhaps four hundred songs. And every one of them was true. Every single one. People cannot help it—the glass doesn't take words, it takes *belief*." He set the device down on its iron stand with a soft click. "Which means one of two things, Healer. Either you are telling me the truth and a child is dead in the marsh mud. Or you are the finest singer I have ever encountered, and you have just done the impossible, and I should burn this room to the ground and start again with pliers."

The silence stretched. Brannoc's heart slammed against his ribs, and he made his face into the face of a defeated old man, which was not difficult, because defeat was the costume and it fit him perfectly.

Vael studied him. Then he sighed, and it sounded almost disappointed, almost lonely.

"But pliers are so crude," he said, "and you'd only sing me another beautiful lie, wouldn't you? So here is what happens instead. You stay in a comfortable cell. You get your boots back. And I send riders to Lowmarsh to dig under the alders." He smiled then, thin and courteous and terrible. "If there's a grave, Healer, you've bought yourself a farm. If there isn't—"

He paused at the door.

"—then I'll know exactly what you are. And I'll come back, and I'll bring the glass, and we'll see whether a man can keep singing lies with his hands broken. Rest well. Truly. You've earned the rest, whichever sort of man you turn out to be."

The door closed. The lock turned. Brannoc sat alone in the grey light with blood dried on his lip and his soul hollowed out like a gourd, and he began, very quietly, to laugh—because it had worked, it had *worked*, the lie was planted and growing, and every rider Vael sent to Lowmarsh was a rider not hunting the frozen passes, and every day spent digging under the alders was a day Yara and the living, breathing, sealed-memory child gained on the road—

—north. Toward the Hollow Abbey. Toward the one place in all the world where the seal on that child's heart might crack wide open, and Brannoc, laughing his silent broken laugh in the dawn-lit cell, prayed to the ash of the Nine Towers with everything left in him:

*Let the abbey hold them safe. And God forgive me—let it hold them better than I did.*
