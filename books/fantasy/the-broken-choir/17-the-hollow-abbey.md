## 17. The Hollow Abbey

The abbey announced itself before they saw it: a smell of old ash and wet stone riding the wind down the valley like a warning whispered from one hilltop to the next.

Yara had been avoiding this place for ten years. They had mapped their smuggler's roads with the care of a man laying out his own grave, and every route they'd ever drawn bent around the Vale of Corrowen the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. There were reasons. Good ones. The kind of reasons that kept a person alive and hollow at the same time.

But the snow had come early and come mean, and Merrin's cough had gone from a child's rattle to something deeper, wetter, that Yara heard in the dark of their shared fire and could not unhear. The passes east were closed. The low road was crawling with Hunt riders since Coldspring — since the arrow, since the girl's fever had begun to burn strange. And between them and any honest shelter lay only one roof wide enough to hide in and forgotten enough to be empty.

The Choir's own roof.

"We can't go there," Merrin said. She said it without knowing why she said it. Yara had learned to watch for that in her — the sentences that arrived from somewhere below memory, delivered in a voice too flat for an eight-year-old. "It's sad there."

"It's a ruin," Yara said. "Ruins are just stone that stopped being useful. Nothing sadder about it than about us."

"That's not—" Merrin pressed her mittened hands against her chest, over the scar she never let anyone look at anymore. Her brow furrowed, and then, as it always did when the seal stirred, the furrow smoothed away as if a hand had passed over it. Whatever she'd almost remembered went back to sleep. "I'm cold," she said instead.

"I know," Yara said. "Come on."

* * *

They came down through the birch trees at dusk, when the light went the color of old bone, and the abbey rose out of the snowfield like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down here to die and been too proud to finish.

Yara remembered it whole. That was the cruelty of it. They remembered the bell tower wearing its copper skin green as riverweed; now the tower stood stripped to grey bone, half-fallen, its bell long ago hauled away by men who thought metal mattered more than what the metal had been for. They remembered the cloister garth where novices grew herbs in neat beds, singing to the thyme because thyme sang back if you were patient. Now the garth was a field of drifted snow, and beneath it, Yara knew, the herbs still grew wild and unsung, bitter and small.

The great doors had burned in the first night of the purge. What remained was a gap in the wall shaped like a doorway, edges charred black even after all these years, as though the stone itself refused to forget.

"Stay behind me," Yara said, and stepped through into the dark.

Inside, the nave opened up like the inside of a throat. Snow had found its way through the broken roof in places, lying in pale drifts down the center aisle where processions had once walked. The pews remained — that was the thing Yara had not prepared for, though they should have known. Everything else had been taken or toppled, but the pews stood in their ranks, row upon row of them, scorched along their upper edges where the fire had swept through and then, inexplicably, died. As if the fire had sung its way down the aisle and run out of breath.

Hundreds of seats. All of them empty. All of them facing the place where the altar had been, which was now just a scarred plinth holding up nothing but shadow and thirty years of dust.

Merrin walked past Yara without waiting, straight down the center aisle, small boots printing the snow, and stopped three rows from the front, and turned around slowly, looking up at the broken vaulting.

"This is where they sat," she said.

"Merrin—"

"The tall people." She frowned, trying to hold something that slid through her fingers like meltwater. "There were so many of them. They were all talking at once but it wasn't loud, it was—" Her face went slack. The frown faded. She blinked at Yara with ordinary eyes, an ordinary lost child's eyes, and asked, "Can we make a fire? I'm really cold."

"Yes," Yara said, and their own voice came out rougher than they intended. "Yes, we can."

* * *

They made camp in the sacristy off the north transept, a small stone room whose door still hung on leather hinges gone stiff as iron. Yara got a fire going in the corner where the hearth had been, using wood prised from a pew — sacrilege, probably, but the gods of the Choir had been dead a long time and would not begrudge a freezing child a warm seat.

Merrin slept badly. Yara sat beside her and listened to the fever move in her breathing, and fed the fire, and did not sleep at all.

This was the room. Not this exact room — the abbey at Corrowen had been a daughter house, a place of training, of quiet summers — but rooms like it. Yara had been seventeen the last time they'd slept in a sacristy, on a pallet that smelled of cedar and lamp oil, while Master Ellery Voss sat cross-legged on the floor across from them teaching the third discipline of unbinding.

*A sword is a promise someone made to hurt you,* Voss had said, turning a captured Huntsman's blade over in his knotted hands. *Every promise can be unmade. But you must know the shape of the promise first. Listen to it. Every forged thing remembers the moment of its making. Your gift is not destruction, little wren. It is testimony.*

Then the fires had come, and Voss had stood in the burning doorway of this very nave with his arms spread wide, singing the doors shut behind Yara with a word that cost him everything, and Yara had run into the mountains carrying nothing but a name that was no longer safe and a voice that would never again be raised above a whisper.

Ten years. Ten years of salt roads and false names and a power folded up small and sewn into silence like a knife wrapped in wool. They had told themselves it was wisdom. Sitting here, in the ash-smell of the only home the gift had ever had, Yara admitted what it actually was: fear, wearing wisdom's coat.

Merrin whimpered in her sleep. Her hands curled against her chest. And in the firelight, Yara saw the scar over her heart shift — not bleed, not open, but *move*, the sealed lines of it flexing like something breathing under a lid.

The fever was doing more than burning her. Something in her was thawing, and the arrow-wound had hastened it, and every mile they walked toward the capital walked her closer to whatever she carried. Yara had known this, in the way one knows a debt is coming due. Knowing and being ready were different countries.

Outside, the wind moved through the broken clerestory windows and made a sound. Yara went very still, listening, because it was not quite the sound of wind.

It was almost a chord.

*Old house,* they thought. *Old, grieving house.* Stone sang when wind worked it; every mason knew that. It meant nothing.

They made themselves believe it meant nothing until dawn.

* * *

Morning came white and silent. Merrin woke clearer-headed, hungry, demanding food with the imperiousness of the genuinely recovering, and Yara counted it a mercy and gave her the last of the smoked trout and half their own portion besides.

It was Merrin who wanted to explore. Of course it was. Children are drawn to ruins the way water is drawn downhill, and this child most of all — she walked the nave with her head tipped back, trailing her mitten along the scorched pew-ends, and Yara followed two steps behind with a hand near her shoulder and dread sitting in their stomach like a swallowed stone.

She stopped at the front, before the bare plinth of the altar.

"They stood here," she said. "When they sang it the first time."

"What did they sing?" Yara asked. Carefully. The way you speak to a bird you don't want to startle off a branch.

Merrin's mouth opened. Closed. Her small face worked, effort visible in it like a labor, like a woman pushing at a door far too heavy for her. "A... a giving song," she managed. "You give it away and it comes back bigger. That's the whole — that's the whole point, that's what makes it *work*, you have to give it—" And then the slackness took her again, sweet and terrible, and she looked up at Yara blankly and said, "Why are there no windows?"

"There were windows," Yara said quietly. "Glass. Colored. The soldiers broke them."

"Why?"

"Because they were told to."

Merrin considered this with the flat pragmatism of children. "That's a bad reason."

"It's the reason most things get broken."

But the girl had already moved on, crouching at the base of the plinth, and Yara saw her go rigid — saw her whole small body lock like a snared animal's — and heard her make a sound that was not a word in any language Yara knew, and yet Yara's bones understood it. Understood it the way a struck bell understands the bell next to it.

"Merrin." Yara dropped to their knees beside her. "Look at me. Look at me, little wren."

Her eyes were open but she was somewhere else. Her lips moved. Sound came out of her in fragments, and the fragments were not the common tongue.

Yara knew the language. Had sworn, like every survivor, never to speak it aloud again where walls might have ears. The Cantor's Tongue, the old books called it — the speech the founding voices had used, pitched to carry meaning in the harmonics rather than the words, so that a sentence sung by ten throats meant something none of the ten intended alone. Hearing it come out of an eight-year-old in a burnt abbey was like watching a corpse sit up and ask after the weather.

Merrin's voice cracked and steadied, cracked and steadied, finding pitches no child should find. The wind outside picked up. In the broken clerestory, the chord Yara had heard in the night sounded again, closer now, answering her — or she answering it, Yara could no longer tell which way the current ran.

And the fragments resolved. Not all at once, and not cleanly — pieces, shards, a mosaic assembling itself in the air between them:

*"...and the ninth verse is the king's verse, remember, the ninth belongs to the crown..."*

*"...no — no, sister, listen — he has taken the giving out of it, he has cut the return, he sings it *to himself*, a song given to no one is a song that eats..."*

*"...two hundred years, they said it would last two hundred years, and we are the collateral, we are the surety, our voices are the coin..."*

*"...hide it. Hide the whole of it in the smallest vessel. A child forgets. A child survives. Let her carry it until the world is ready to hear—"*

Merrin collapsed sideways. Yara caught her before her head struck the stone.

For a long moment the abbey was silent except for the wind, and the wind was only wind again, spent, ordinary. The girl in Yara's arms breathed fast and shallow, her skin hot as a hearthstone, and the scar over her heart had changed — Yara could see it even through her shirt, the sealed lines no longer a closed pattern but a pattern with gaps in it, gaps like missing teeth, like a fence broken open.

Like a door ajar.

Yara sat on the frozen floor of the nave where their teacher had burned, holding a child who was coming apart along seams someone else had sewn, and felt the truth arrive in them the way cold arrives — not all at once, but everywhere at once, undeniable.

He had cut the return. He sings it to himself.

Every child in Greyhollow learned the Sustaining Hymn before they learned their letters. Two hundred years the crown had sung it at every coronation, every festival, every hanging — the Hymn of Continuance, the song that held the realm together, that made the harvests true and the rivers keep their banks and the law bind strong and weak alike. Everyone knew it was old. Everyone knew it was holy. No one asked who wrote it, because the answer was always the same: the First King, in his wisdom, by divine favor.

But songs weren't written by kings. Songs were written by singers. And singers, real singers, the Choir's singers, had built their art on a single law older than any throne: *a song given away returns greater; a song kept is a song that eats.*

The First King hadn't composed the sustaining hymn. He had *taken* it — torn the founding song out of the Choir's living mouths, cut away the verses that gave it back to the world, and kept the remainder for himself and his heirs. A stolen engine, running on fuel it could not replenish. Running, for two hundred years, on the surety of murdered voices.

And now it was failing. Yara had heard it failing — had heard the hollowness under the festival hymns for years and told themselves it was distance, weather, their own damaged hearing. It was not distance. It was a debt coming due, two centuries late, with interest compounded in blood.

And the payment the crown would reach for, when the hymn finally stuttered and died, was the last vessel of the original song.

Was Merrin.

"Oh, little wren," Yara said aloud, and their voice shook. "What have they put inside you?"

Merrin stirred. Her eyes opened — clear, present, ordinary, and that was somehow the worst part, that she surfaced from the deep water with no memory of having drowned.

"You're crying," she observed, with a child's blunt astonishment.

"No, I'm not." Yara wiped their face with the heel of their hand. "It's the smoke. Old houses smoke."

"It's not smoking." She sat up in Yara's lap, wobbling, and studied them with disconcerting seriousness. Then she reached up and patted their cheek twice, the way one pats a horse that has spooked. "It's all right," she said. "Whatever it is. It's all right."

It was not all right. It was possibly the least all right thing in the history of the realm. But Yara laughed anyway — a short, cracked sound, startled out of them — and gathered the girl up, and stood, and looked down the long ranks of scorched pews toward the broken doors and the white morning beyond.

They had come here to hide. To wait out the storm and the fever and slip away east before the Hunt's net drew tight. That plan lay on the abbey floor now with everything else the Choir had left behind.

Because Brannoc had said it, drunk and bitter, weeks ago in Lowmarsh: *the seal won't hold forever, Yara. Seals are for letters, not for children. Sooner or later she opens, and the only question is whether anyone's standing near enough to catch what spills out.*

She was opening. Here, now, ahead of schedule, in the one place in the world where the leaking truth could be understood — and the one place the Hunt would search the moment anyone thought to look.

Yara looked at the girl in their arms, sleeping again already, exhausted by her own depths, her breath rattling less than yesterday. The fever was breaking. Or rather — Yara understood this with a new and colder clarity — the fever was *finishing*. What it had been sent to do, it had done. The first crack was through. There would be more, and each one wider, and the crown's riders were already on the roads asking questions in every market town between here and Greyfall.

They could run east. Lose themselves in the Thornfell deeps, maybe buy the child another winter, another year, while the seal cracked on its own schedule and the hymn failed on its own, and the realm came apart blind, screaming, believing its own lies to the end.

Or they could go where the truth was needed. Where the hymn would be sung loudest, and fail most publicly, and where one swordspeaker with a broken oath and a cracking child might stand in the right place at the wrong time and do something no one had done in two hundred years.

Tell the whole story. Out loud. In the one voice the crown feared more than armies.

Yara stood in the nave of the Hollow Abbey, in the ashes of everyone they had ever loved, and found — not courage, nothing so clean — but a kind of terrible arithmetic. The hiding had bought ten years and it had bought nothing. The silence had protected exactly one person, and that person was standing in front of them with a broken-open heart and a kingdom's worth of stolen memory bleeding through it.

"Get your boots on, little wren," they said softly. "We're going to Greyfall."

Behind them, in the broken clerestory, the wind moved through the old stones one last time — and this time Yara did not pretend it was only wind. It was a chord. Three notes, rising.

It sounded, unmistakably, like a door opening.
