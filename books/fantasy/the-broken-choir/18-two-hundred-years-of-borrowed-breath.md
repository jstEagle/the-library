## 18. Two Hundred Years of Borrowed Breath

The Chancellery kept its clocks wrong on purpose.

Vael had noticed it his first week in the capital, eleven years ago, a young Inquisitor still smelling of the road and the incense they burned over the cells. Every clock in Greyfall's palace ran a half-minute slow—deliberately, he'd learned, so that the Chancellor was never late to anything, because nothing could begin until Ives arrived. The whole machinery of government waited on one man's footsteps. It had seemed then like a small vanity, a quirk of power, the kind of thing old men collected the way other men collected snuffboxes or grudges.

Now, standing in the antechamber outside the Chancellor's private study with a leather folder of evidence that would unmake two centuries of history pressed against his ribs, Vael understood it differently. The clocks were not a vanity. They were a demonstration. *Time itself waits for him.* And Vael was about to walk into that study and tell the man who owned time that it was running out.

The antechamber smelled of beeswax and cold marble and, beneath both, something faintly sweet that Vael had never been able to name—the same sweetness that hung in the resonance cells beneath the palace, where memories were drawn out of prisoners like thread from a spindle. He had asked a steward about it once, early on. The steward had smiled and said it was the smell of stability, my lord, and Vael had laughed, and then stopped laughing when he realized the man had not been making a joke.

"His Excellency will see you now," said the undersecretary, a narrow young woman whose eyes went to the folder at Vael's chest and away again with the practiced blankness of palace staff. "You have until the fourth bell."

Until the fourth bell. Everything in this building was measured in bells, in breaths, in borrowed time. Vael inclined his head and pushed open the door.

* * *

Chancellor Aldous Ives was feeding birds.

That was the first thing Vael saw, and it always disoriented him, no matter how many times he witnessed it: the most powerful man in the realm standing at an open window in the dead of winter, scattering seed onto the sill for sparrows that came and went like small brown thoughts. Ives was seventy-one years old and looked sixty, which everyone attributed to good blood and better physicians. His hands were steady. His back was straight. His hair, white as fresh paper, was combed with geometric precision, and his eyes were pale grey—the grey of frost forming on glass, beautiful and patient and utterly without warmth except, perhaps, for the birds.

"Inquisitor-General." Ives did not turn. A sparrow landed on his outstretched finger, took its seed, and left. "You come up from Greyhollow. How was the country? Still full of mud and treason?"

"Both in generous supply, Excellency."

"Mm." Ives closed the window. The cold snapped off like a cut thread. "Sit. You've brought me something heavy—I can tell by how carefully you're carrying it. Men carry bad news gently, like a wounded bird. Good news they throw around like a ball."

Vael sat in the chair across the desk. The study was exactly as it always was: books arranged by height rather than subject, a fire burning low and clean, a single portrait above the mantel showing the First King Corvane at his coronation, crowned and serene, one hand raised in blessing. Vael had looked at that portrait a thousand times. Today, with what he knew, it looked less like a blessing and more like a theft caught mid-gesture.

He laid the folder on the desk between them and did not open it yet.

"I spent nine days in Greyhollow," Vael said. "Registering the gifted, per your directive. And interrogating those who resisted."

"And?"

"And I found something I was not looking for." He opened the folder. Inside were three things: a sheaf of transcriptions from the memory-extraction sessions, a tuning diagram copied from a seized swordspeaker's papers, and—a piece of evidence he had obtained through means he did not intend to discuss—a fragment of the sustaining hymn's score as it was sung in the great cathedral, annotated by his own hand. "Excellency, before I show you, I want you to know that I verified everything twice. Then a third time. I wanted to be wrong."

Ives's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it breaks. "Show me."

Vael showed him.

He began with the mathematics, because mathematics was the language Ives trusted above all others—he had been a surveyor's son, and he still thought in measurements. "The sustaining hymn is sung at the cathedral every day at dawn. Two hundred and six years, uninterrupted. Its purpose is well known: it stabilizes the realm's resonance field, keeps the wild harmonies of the old war dormant, maintains—" he paused, choosing the official phrasing with care— "civic calm."

"The official phrasing is fine, Vael. Continue."

"The hymn is not original." He turned the transcription around. "You'll see the harmonic structure here. Fourteen movements, built on a root cadence that repeats every ninth measure. Now compare it to this—" he slid the seized swordspeaker's diagram forward— "which is a fragment of the Choir's founding song, recovered from materials predating the Purge by forty years. The root cadence is identical. Not similar. Identical. Note for note. The crown's hymn is the Choir's founding song, restructured, slowed, and stripped of its words."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the cathedral bell rang the third hour.

Ives looked at the papers for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was mild, almost bored. "Of course it is. Where else would we have gotten it?"

Vael had prepared himself for denial, for rage, for the smooth deflections of a lifetime politician. He had not prepared himself for *of course*. The word landed in his chest like a dropped stone into a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

"You... acknowledge it."

"Vael." Ives finally looked up, and there was something almost pitying in the frost-grey eyes. "What did you think we sang? Did you imagine the First King composed it himself, some morning after breakfast? Corvane couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. The Choir made the founding song—it was theirs, woven from ten thousand voices, the greatest working of resonance ever achieved. And when the war ended, the new realm needed it more than the singers did. So the crown took it. That is not a scandal, Inquisitor-General. That is the foundation."

"Took it," Vael said carefully. "Or stole it."

"The distinction matters to historians and priests." Ives folded his hands on the desk. "To me it matters only that it works. Which brings us, I assume, to the reason you wanted to be wrong. The third verification."

Vael's mouth had gone dry. He made himself continue, because this was the part that mattered, the part he had ridden through sleet to deliver. "The hymn works. For now. But it is failing. The root cadence has drifted—two hundred years of being sung by voices with no gift in them, day after day, generation after generation, each choir teaching the next from imperfect copies. The drift is small. Cumulatively, it is fatal. I charted it against the resonance records going back eighty years." He turned the final page. "At the current rate of decay, the hymn will fail to hold within fourteen months. Perhaps sixteen if we have a mild spring. After that, the wild harmonies wake. After that, Excellency, we are back in the years of the Broken Choir. Unbound resonance. Singers' madness. The war."

He had expected many reactions. What he got was worse than any of them: Ives nodded. Slowly, unsurprised, the way a man nods at a physician confirming a diagnosis he made himself months ago.

"Thirteen months," the Chancellor said. "My own analysts put it at thirteen. Yours are more optimistic because they don't account for the acceleration." He tapped the transcription. "The drift isn't linear anymore. It's compounding. We have perhaps a year. Less, if the girl reaches the capital."

The room tilted slightly. Vael gripped the arm of his chair. "The girl."

"Merrin." Ives said the name the way a jeweler says the name of a stone. "Eight years old. Seal scar over the heart, work of the Last Cantor—you confirmed it yourself in your report from Greyhollow, though you buried it in paragraph nineteen, which I noticed, Vael; I notice everything you bury. She carries a sealed fragment of the founding song. Possibly the largest surviving piece of it. Possibly the living heart of it." He rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the sparrows hop on the outer sill, locked out by the glass. "Do you understand what she is?"

"A child."

"Do not be sentimental with me. It doesn't suit you; you've never once been sentimental in eleven years of service, and I would hate to see you start now." Ives's reflection watched Vael from the darkening glass. "She is a vessel. The Last Cantor, dying, poured the true song into her rather than let the crown keep it—and sealed it so deeply even she doesn't know it's there. That seal is cracking. My sources say she's already leaking. Harmonies that mend tools. Words in the old tongue. If the seal breaks fully, the founding song returns to the world in its complete form—and the moment it does, every ear that hears it will know our hymn for what it is. A copy. A theft played on a broken instrument. The legitimacy of two hundred years of rule, gone in the space of one verse."

"So we let her go," Vael said. "We let the seal break. The truth comes out, and the realm survives it. People have survived truths before."

"People survive winters too," Ives said mildly, "but we do not open the doors and invite one indoors." He turned from the window. "Consider what actually happens, Vael. Use your famous rigor. The hymn fails regardless—thirteen months, remember. The question is only what stands when it falls. If the song returns through the girl, uncontrolled, in some market square or mountain shrine, it arrives as chaos. Millions of minds receiving two centuries of suppressed memory at once. Riots. The provinces seceding. Old war-pacts waking. The Kestrel clans settling scores that are older than the crown itself. Or—" and here his voice softened into something almost gentle, which was how Vael knew he had arrived at the part he truly cared about— "we take her first. Gently. With care. We bring her here, beneath the palace, where the resonance cells are. We do not break the seal. We *manage* it. We build a cradle around her, a sustained binding, and we anchor the failing hymn to her—to the true song sleeping inside her. The crown does not fall. The crown is *renewed*. She becomes the instrument the realm plays. Perfectly tuned. Forever."

"She is eight years old."

"She is eight years old *now*," Ives said, as though noting the weather. "Bindings mature with their vessels. In twenty years she will be a woman who has never known any other life, who will believe, if we are careful, that she chose it. Children are adaptable. It is one of their virtues." He returned to the desk and sat, folding his hands, and for a moment the portrait of Corvane above the mantel seemed to lean over his shoulder like a colleague. "I am ordering the Hunt redirected. All resources. The girl is to be taken alive—alive and *unharmed*, Vael, and I mean unharmed in the deepest sense. No extraction devices near her. No rough handling. Her mind must arrive intact or she is worthless. Any officer who damages her answers to me personally. At any cost. Do you understand the order?"

"At any cost," Vael repeated. The words tasted like the sweet smell in the antechamber.

"There is one complication." Ives picked up a paper from a stack beside him—Vael recognized the broadside format, the Hunt's own printing presses. "Yara Eriksen. The last swordspeaker. Your people cornered them at a tollhouse and lost nine weapons and their dignity. Eriksen has the girl. Eriksen knows what she carries—or is learning it. And Eriksen, unlike the child, cannot be allowed to reach the capital. A swordspeaker with full voice, in Greyfall, during the Hymnal Festival?" Ives shook his head slowly. "Eriksen is to be killed. Quietly, if possible. Publicly, if necessary. But killed. The girl comes to us breathing. The speaker does not."

* * *

Vael walked back down through the palace in a daze, past the slow clocks, past the guards who saluted him without seeing him, down the long stair that led toward the resonance cells—not because he meant to go there, but because his feet knew the way and his mind had left the building entirely.

*She becomes the instrument the realm plays.*

He had served the crown for thirty years. He had joined the Inquisition at nineteen because his village had been burned in a resonance accident—a hedge-witch's working gone wrong, eleven dead including his mother, and the Inquisitors who came afterward had restored order, had explained, had *made it make sense*. Order out of the wild song. That was the whole creed, and he had believed it the way bedrock believes in weight. He had extracted memories from traitors and madmen and children too old for crying, and he had told himself each time: *this is the price of calm. This is the wall that holds back the flood.*

And all along, the flood had been held back by a stolen song, sung badly, wearing out like a rope fraying strand by strand—and the man who maintained the wall had known, and had planned, when the rope gave, to tie a living child to the breach.

Vael stopped on the stair, in the cold air that rose from the cells below, and pressed his hand flat against the stone.

Here was the thing he could not stop turning over, the splinter working deeper: Ives had not lied to him. Had not denied, deflected, or dressed the theft in ceremony. *Of course it is. Where else would we have gotten it?* As if the crime were so old it had become architecture—as if two hundred years were enough to launder murder into masonry. And perhaps it was. Perhaps that was what time did for the powerful that it did for no one else. The clocks waited for Ives. The history waited for Ives. Even the truth, given enough years, learned to wait for him.

But the hymn did not wait. Thirteen months. And somewhere in the Thornfell mountains, a child with a scar over her heart was leaking the world's oldest song into the snow, and a swordspeaker who should have died ten years ago was walking her toward—toward what?

Toward the capital, if Vael knew his adversary. Everything he had read about Yara Eriksen—the file was thin, mostly rumor and a decade of frustrating silence—suggested someone who hid, who endured, who did not seek stages. But hiding ended when you were hunted hard enough. And Ives had just ordered the hardest hunt in the Inquisition's history.

Which meant that in a matter of weeks, perhaps days, Yara Eriksen and Merrin would be driven down out of the mountains and into the arms of either the Hunt or the crowd. And Vael would be standing between them, holding the keys.

He thought of Brannoc, the drunk healer in his cell below—the man had fed him something under the knife, Vael was nearly certain now; the vision of the Choir's fall he had extracted was too beautiful, too perfectly shaped, like a story polished by telling. A false memory, offered up as sacrifice. Why? To protect Eriksen. A broken man in chains, with the extraction device drinking his mind, had spent his last usable treasure not on mercy for himself but on a lie for a friend.

Vael had interrogated hundreds of prisoners. None of them had ever given him a lie that felt like a gift.

He stood on the stair a long moment, listening to the palace breathe around him—the ticking of the lying clocks, the distant murmur of clerks maintaining the fiction, and beneath it all, so faint he might have imagined it, the daily hymn still hanging in the stones like perfume, like the sweet smell that was not stability at all but decay, patiently perfumed.

*Whom do you serve?*

The question arrived quietly, the way frost arrives on glass, and he could not answer it, and he understood that the inability to answer was itself the answer.

Above him, the fourth bell rang—his audience was over, his time with the Chancellor spent. Below him, the cells waited with their sleeping prisoners and their humming walls. Ahead of him lay orders he had accepted aloud: *the girl alive, the speaker dead, at any cost.*

Vael climbed the stairs. At the top, he turned not toward his own offices but toward the archive, where the old resonance records were kept—the ones predating the crown's careful cataloguing, the ones from the years of the fall. If he was going to serve anyone, he intended to know, first, exactly what it was he had been serving.

Behind him, far below, the resonance cells hummed their endless note, and somewhere in the frozen mountains, a seal cracked a little wider, and the borrowed breath of the realm grew shorter by one more day.
