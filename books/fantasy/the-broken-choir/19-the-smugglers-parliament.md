## 19. The Smugglers' Parliament

The Kettle was not a place so much as a permission—a hollow beneath the Thornfell massif where three rivers of meltwater had argued their way through limestone for ten thousand years and left behind a cathedral no god had sanctioned. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some drowned organ. Lanterns swung from ropes strung between them, and the light they threw was the color of old honey, trembling on wet stone, on faces Yara had known for a decade and had never once been introduced to by true name.

They came as the clans came: by rope-ladders down chimneys of rock, by coracle up the underground river, by paths that existed only in the walking of them. Yara counted forty-one fires before they stopped counting. The smugglers of Thornfell did not gather often. The last full parliament had been called eleven years ago, over the question of whether to burn the tollhouse at Nine Bends, and that gathering had ended with two men dead and a feud that still smoldered between the Vetch clan and everyone who had ever traded east of the Greywater.

Tonight the question was larger than a tollhouse.

Yara stood at the edge of the great chamber with Merrin's hand small and cold inside their own, and watched the clans assemble, and felt the old animal knowledge rise in them—the knowledge that had kept them alive for ten years, whispering *be small, be nothing, be stone*—and knew that tonight it would have to be strangled and buried, because being small would save neither of them now.

"You don't have to do this," Merrin said. She had learned to say it quietly, in the tone of someone offering an exit rather than an argument. Eight years old, and she had already mastered the smuggler's first virtue: never corner anyone, including yourself.

"Yes, I do." Yara crouched to her level. In the lantern-light her face looked older than eight—thin, watchful, the seal scar at her collarbone catching the glow like a coin at the bottom of a well. "Listen to me. Whatever happens in there, whatever they say about me, about you—you keep your hood up and your mouth shut, and if I put my hand on your shoulder twice, you run back up the goat path to the cache and you wait three days. Three days, no matter what you hear."

"Three days," she repeated. "Then what?"

"Then you go to the Ferrywoman's sister at Coldharbour and you say the word 'kestrel,' and you do what she tells you."

"And what if you're—"

"Merrin."

She shut her mouth. But her eyes stayed on Yara's face with that terrible, unchildlike attention she sometimes had, as though something behind her sealed heart were reading the room over her shoulder. "You're afraid," she said. Not accusing. Observing. As if reporting weather.

"Afraid is sensible," Yara said. "Come on."

* * *

The parliament convened around the Deepfire—a natural chimney in the cavern floor where the underground river dropped into darkness, and where, generations ago, some forgotten clan-mother had built a fire-iron cage that burned without smoke, fed by some draft from the deep places. Its light came up red from below, so that every face at the parliament ring was lit like a face at a wake.

Old customs governed the speaking. Each clan sat in its own arc of stones brought from its own territory—Vetch slate, Corbie granite, pale river-cobbles for the Ferrier families, black basalt for the Kestrels. A speaker stood, named their clan and their grievance, and threw a token into the Deepfire. If the fire flared, the grievance was heard; if it guttered, the speaker was done. Everyone knew the draft did the work, and everyone pretended otherwise, because a people who live by hidden roads need a few honest superstitions to hold the dark together.

Harrow Vetch spoke first, because Harrow Vetch always spoke first; he had been born first, he claimed, in every sense that mattered.

"The Hunt rode through my territory four days past," he said, throwing his token. The fire flared. "Burned the waystation at Stony Ford. Took Dree Halfmark's boys for questioning—questioning, they call it—and sent back what was left of one of them in a sack, with a bill for the sack." A rumble went around the arcs. "Now this swordswoman"—he did not look at Yara; he made a show of not looking, which was worse—"this swordswoman asks us to shelter her and her foundling while the Inquisitor-General turns the whole mountain over. I say: why should we? Every day she's under our stones is a day the Hunt has reason to dig. Let her run to the lowlands. Let her run to the sea. We are not her shield."

"We are not her anything," said Maren Corbie, a broad woman with a voice like a millstone. "But I'll say what Harrow won't. It isn't her we're deciding about. It's us. Because mark me—the Hunt doesn't stop at one fugitive. They've posted broadsides in every market town from here to Greyfall. They've got deaf men walking our roads with their hands flat against the rock, feeling for song. They took Brannoc Ashfell out of his own bed in Lowmarsh." She paused, let that land. Brannoc had been nobody's favorite—he'd drunk half his healing gift into oblivion—but he had set bones for half the clans' children over thirty years, and the name landed anyway. "If we give her up, we tell the crown that Thornfell can be frightened. And once the crown knows we frighten, it will come back for more. Registration. Tolls. Garrisons on the passes. You think the Hunt ends with one woman and a child?"

"It ends with her head on a pike in Greyhollow," said Harrow, "and my sons alive."

"That's one way of counting," Maren said.

The talk went on for hours, in the way of such things—circular, procedural, punctuated by tokens thrown and grievances aired that had nothing to do with Yara at all. A water-rights dispute between the Corbies and the Ferriers surfaced and was fought all over again. Someone accused the Vetches of selling route-maps to a Grayhollow fence. Yara stood at the rim of the firelight with Merrin beside her, hooded and silent, and listened to their life being weighed like contraband, and understood with a strange clarity that this was how most fates were decided: not in battles or throne rooms, but in smoky rooms full of tired practical people counting risks.

A Ferrier elder rose—an ancient man, Ferro the Younger, though he had been Ferro the Elder when Yara first ran these roads, and had simply refused promotion. "I knew your mother," he said suddenly, looking straight at Yara for the first time all night. The chamber went quiet. "Elin Eriksen. She carried salt for me over the Widow's Teeth in a whiteout, the year the crown doubled the tariff. Never asked extra. Never asked anything." He turned to the ring. "I say this only so you know I'm not speaking blind. The Eriksens paid their debts to these roads. But debts past aren't safety present. I abstain."

Abstention, among the clans, was a courtesy and a wound at once.

Then the Kestrel clan-mother stood.

Sera Kestrel was perhaps fifty, built like the mountain itself—low, dense, immovable—with iron-grey hair cut short as a soldier's and hands that looked like they had personally broken every law in the realm and enjoyed each one. She wore no ornament except a single kestrel feather worked into her collar-buckle, which was ornament enough; everyone in the chamber knew what it meant. The kestrel hovers. The kestrel watches. The kestrel strikes when it chooses, not when it is asked.

She did not throw a token. She walked to the edge of the Deepfire and stood looking down into the red rising light for a long moment, and the whole parliament waited, because Sera Kestrel had never once wasted a word in forty years of parliaments.

"I've heard a lot of arithmetic tonight," she said finally. "Harrow counts heads. Maren counts futures. Both count right, as far as they count." She turned. "Here's what nobody's counted. How many of you sang, as children? Lullabies. Work rounds. The harvest call at Greyhollow fair?" Hands shifted. Some faces closed. "Don't be shy. Half this chamber hums when they think no one's listening. My own grandmother used to sing the nets mended—literally mended, knots pulling tight on their own—and she'd have died before admitting it, and did die, quiet as a mouse, because in her day you hid it or you burned." Her voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was more frightening. "That was before the Hunt. Now the Hunt has deaf men who can feel a hummed note through six feet of limestone. So I ask you: how long before one of your people, one of yours, hums the wrong round at the wrong ford, and the sack comes back with a bill for it?

"You want to give this woman to the Hunt to buy quiet. Fine arithmetic. But quiet isn't for sale anymore. Quiet is over. The only question left is which side of it we stand on when the singing starts."

She looked at Yara then, fully, for the first time, and her eyes were the flat grey of the high passes in winter.

"Bring your child and stand in the light," she said. "Let them see what they're voting on."

* * *

Yara had rehearsed this speech for two days, walking the goat paths with Merrin asleep in the sling against their chest, composing and discarding. Speeches were not their craft. Words that could unmake steel were their craft, and those were bound behind an oath, and everything else that lived in their throat was clumsy.

They stepped into the full light of the Deepfire with Merrin's hand in theirs, and felt forty-one fires' worth of eyes take their measure, and began.

"My name is Yara Eriksen." The name went through the chamber like a draft through a mine—some faces tightening, some going carefully blank. "Some of you have carried my salt. Some of you have slept easier because I walked your roads quiet for ten years and told no one what I am. I am a swordspeaker. Last of the oath-bound. What that means, in plain terms: there is a word in the old tongue that will open any lock, any bond, any binding made of metal or of memory. I have spent ten years not saying it."

"Why tell us now?" That was Harrow Vetch, arms crossed. "Ten years quiet, and now you stand in our deepest hole and announce yourself with the Hunt a day behind you. Either you're desperate or you're stupid."

"Desperate," Yara said, and a few grim laughs moved around the ring. "Both, maybe. But hear the rest, because it's not only about me." They drew breath. This was the part they had debated whether to say—the part that was Merrin's secret, Brannoc's discovery, the thing that could topple thrones—and they had decided, walking those cold paths, that a parliament bought with half-truths was no alliance at all. "Three months ago I found this child frozen on the High Pass. She carries a seal-scar over her heart—the work of the Last Cantor, cut the night the Choir fell. Inside that seal is a piece of the Choir's founding memory. And what Brannoc read in that scar, before the Hunt took him, is this: the hymn the crown has sung to sustain the realm for two hundred years is stolen. Taken from the Choir's founding song, by the first king, along with the singers' lives. And it is failing. The realm has been living on borrowed breath for two centuries, and the loan is called due within the year."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere in the deeps below the Deepfire, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"That's a big story," Maren Corbie said slowly. "Big stories are usually lies, or bait."

"I know. I'd think so too, in your place. Which is why I'm not asking you to believe it. I'm asking you to believe this: the Hunt believes it. Why else would the Inquisitor-General spend a season and a fortune hunting one child across the whole of Thornfell? They've taken prisoners. They've burned waystations. They've posted my face on every wall from here to the capital. All this, for a smuggler and an eight-year-old." Yara let that sit. "You don't spend that much to catch a criminal. You spend that much to bury a truth."

"What do you want?" Ferro asked. Always practical, Ferro.

"Passage. Shelter. Your silence. In return—" Yara opened the satchel they'd carried down the goat path, and set it on the stone between themselves and the fire, and let the lantern-light do the persuading. Gold coin, crown-stamped, taken over ten years from the margins of a hundred runs. Beside it, a folded map—not drawn but sung into leather, the old way, the lines raised like braille under a fingertip: every hidden road in the western Thornfell, every cache, every crossing, every door in the mountain that the crown's maps did not know. "Roads and gold. The roads are worth more. With these, your runners can move cargo through the Hunt's cordons for a generation. And when the truth breaks—if it breaks—I will speak the names of every clan that sheltered us to whatever government comes after, and see your toll-rights written into law. Legitimacy. An end to running."

"Or," said Harrow, "we take the gold, sell your location to the Hunt, and collect the bounty besides. Five thousand crowns, last broadside I saw." He smiled without warmth. "I'm not saying I would. I'm saying you've given us the option, which is either trust or foolishness."

"It's the same thing," Yara said. "It always was."

The vote, when it came, was not a show of hands. Among the clans it was done with tokens into the Deepfire: cast iron for refusal, copper for shelter. Yara stood with Merrin's shoulder pressed against their hip and watched the tokens fall, one clan at a time, and tried to read the future in the sound of metal on stone.

The Vetches cast iron. Of course. Harrow didn't even watch it fall; he was already standing, gathering his cloak, done with the matter. Two of the Ferrier arcs cast iron as well—Ferro himself abstaining to the last, which was its own message. The Corbies cast copper, all of them, Maren meeting Yara's eyes with a nod that contained no comfort and considerable warning: *you owe me, and I keep books.* The smaller clans split, iron and copper falling in an uneven rain, until the sound of it blurred together and Yara stopped trying to count and simply waited, the way one waits at a sickbed.

Last of all, Sera Kestrel walked to the fire's edge.

She held a single token between finger and thumb, and she did not throw it.

"Copper buys passage," she said, to the chamber, to Yara. "Copper buys silence. Copper buys what anyone sells." She turned the token over. "My clan casts neither. My clan makes its own terms." She looked at Yara, and the flat grey eyes held something that might have been pity, or appetite, or both. "The Kestrels shelter you and yours through the winter. Roads, caches, runners, silence—the whole weight of my clan behind you, and we are the only clan the Hunt has never successfully followed into the high passes. When the time comes, my blades at Coldspring, if it comes to blades."

The chamber murmured. Even Harrow Vetch paused at the ladder.

"And the price?" Yara said. Their mouth had gone dry.

Sera smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Not yet. A price named too early is a price you can refuse. You'll have my protection first, and my price when I choose to name it—and you'll pay it, woman, because by then you'll have no other friends left in the world." She flipped the token at last, high, an easy toss, and caught it herself, and put it away in her collar. "That's how the kestrel hunts. We hover. We watch. And we strike when the prey has nowhere else to go."

Merrin's hand tightened in Yara's. Small, cold, and gripping hard.

* * *

They climbed out of the Kettle near dawn, up the rope-ladders and the goat path, into air so cold it tasted of iron. Behind and below them, the mountain swallowed its parliament; the clans dispersed along their separate roads like water finding old channels. Above, the sky was doing the thing it did in late autumn over Thornfell—piling up grey cloud in the west with a stillness that meant snow, real snow, the kind that closed passes and buried roads and made the whole world go quiet enough to hear a deaf man's footsteps.

Sera Kestrel walked beside them for the first mile. She had dismissed her escort with a jerk of her chin, and for a while she said nothing, just matched Yara's stride with the unhurried economy of a woman who had never once hurried anywhere in her life.

"Your child walks like a soldier," she observed at last.

"She walks like someone who's been hunted since summer."

"Mm." Sera glanced back at Merrin, who was dutifully pretending not to listen, which fooled no one. "The Cantor's work on her. I saw the scar when you stood in the firelight. I knew the Last Cantor, did you know that? Before the fall. She mended my brother's voice once—taken by a fever—and she did it for a song and a cup of soup, and laughed the whole time." Something moved behind the flat grey eyes and was gone. "So I believe your story, swordspeaker. That's worth knowing. I don't act on belief—I act on interest—but belief makes the interest cleaner."

"And your interest?"

"Is none of your business yet." They crested a shoulder of rock, and the whole western sky lay open below them, bruise-colored, waiting. Sera stopped and faced Yara squarely. "One thing I'll give you free, because free things bind tighter than sold ones. The Hunt pulled out of Greyhollow two days ago. All of it. Riders, hounds, the deaf men, the wagons with the cages. Everything, moving south and east, fast."

Yara felt the words land like cold water down the spine. "Moving where?"

"Where do you think? Toward the capital. Toward the festival." Sera pulled her collar up against the wind. "Your Inquisitor-General isn't searching the mountains anymore, woman. He's stopped looking for you." The kestrel feather at her collar trembled in the wind. "He's gone to prepare a trap he's sure you'll walk into—and the terrible thing, the thing you should chew on all the way to Greyfall, is that I think he's right."
