## 20. Cracks in the Seal

The mending happened first, and it was the kettle that told on her.

Merrin hadn't meant to do anything. The kettle belonged to the waystation at the top of the Kestrel road, a dented copper thing with a split seam that leaked a thin whistle of steam, and she had been waiting for the water to boil while Yara argued prices with the waystation keeper out in the yard. She was bored. She was cold. And without thinking—without deciding anything at all—she began to hum.

It wasn't a song she knew. That was the strange part, the part she would try to explain later to Yara and fail: the tune came up out of her like water from a well someone else had dug, rising through her chest and throat and out between her lips before her mind could catch it or name it. Three notes, then four, then a falling fifth that made her think of snow sliding off a roof, and as she hummed, the steam-whistle changed. It softened. It found her key. And the split seam in the copper—the one the keeper's grandmother had sworn could never be soldered right—closed with a sound like a knuckle cracking, smooth and bright, as if the metal remembered being whole and simply agreed to be so again.

Merrin stopped humming. The kettle went on boiling, whole and smug, and she stared at it with her heart going hard against the scar over her breastbone, that old pale star of tissue she'd had for as long as she could remember, which was not very long, which was only since the frozen pass, since Yara wrapping her in a coat that smelled of woodsmoke and horse, since *you're safe now* said by someone who looked like they didn't believe their own words.

She put her hand flat over the scar. Beneath her palm, faint as a second pulse, something hummed back.

"Merrin." Yara stood in the doorway, snow in their dark hair, eyes going from her face to the kettle to her hand on her chest. They had a way of standing very still when something frightened them, stiller than anyone Merrin had ever known, as if stillness were a door they could hide behind. "What did you do?"

"Nothing," Merrin said, which was true, which was the worst part. "I just hummed."

Yara crossed the room in three strides and lifted the kettle, turning it, running a thumb along the seam where the crack had been. Their face did something complicated—Merrin watched it happen, watched fear and wonder and an old tired grief all trying to use the same features at once—and then they set the kettle down very gently, the way you set down something that might be listening.

"We need to talk about your humming," Yara said.

"I'm sorry about the kettle."

"Don't be sorry about the kettle." Yara crouched so they were eye to eye, and up close Merrin could see the fine lines around their eyes, the gray coming in early at their temples, everything the road had written on them. "Be sorry if anyone else hears it. Can you keep it inside? When it wants to come out?"

Merrin thought about lying. She was getting better at knowing when people wanted lies—it was another thing she couldn't remember learning, this reading of faces, this knowing what lived behind words—but Yara had never once wanted a lie from her, and she didn't want to start paying in that coin.

"It doesn't feel like mine," she said. "It feels like it's *in* me. Like there's a bird in my chest and sometimes the door opens."

Something moved behind Yara's eyes at that, some memory with teeth, but all they said was, "Then we teach the bird to wait."

* * *

But the bird did not want to wait, and after the kettle there was the horse.

They had taken new mounts at the waystation, trading the spent ones for two shaggy mountain ponies with winter coats like moss, and Merrin's pony was a dun mare named Sorrel who bit everyone except, apparently, Merrin. On the third morning out, crossing a high shoulder of stone where the wind came off the icefields hard enough to lean on, Sorrel shied at a shadow—a slab of rock fallen across the trail, nothing more—and Merrin, thrown forward against the mare's neck, felt the panic rise in the animal like heat from a forge.

And she hummed.

She didn't choose to. That was becoming the shape of her life, things happening through her instead of because of her: the note came out low and round, a sound more felt than heard, and Sorrel's panic broke apart like foam on a rock. The mare's ears swiveled back toward Merrin's mouth. Her breathing slowed. Her hooves, which had been skittering on the icy stone, planted themselves, and she walked calmly past the fallen slab as though it were an old friend.

Behind them, Yara's pony screamed.

Not whinnied—screamed, rearing, eyes rolling white, fighting the reins as though something under its skin were on fire. Yara fought it down, cursing, and got it turned in a circle twice before it would stand, and when it finally stilled, trembling, Yara looked at Merrin with an expression she had never seen on them before.

Merrin had learned Yara's expressions the way you learn weather: the flat watchfulness, the dry almost-smile, the tired kindness. This one was none of those. This one was the look of someone seeing a ghost walk past wearing a familiar coat.

"It didn't like my humming?" Merrin offered.

"No," Yara said. "It liked it too much. There's a difference." They dismounted, checked the dun's legs with hands that weren't quite steady, and wouldn't meet her eyes again until well past noon.

That night, camped in the lee of a granite spine with the fire small and shielded, Merrin woke to find Yara sitting awake, watching her across the flames. Not watching her—watching *over* her, the way a shepherd watches a field where the fence is broken.

"The horses know what you are before we do," Yara said quietly, when they saw she was awake. "Animals hear true. People have learned not to. That's the whole trick of the crown, Merrin—two hundred years of teaching people to be deaf."

"What am I?"

Yara was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "I don't know yet," they said at last, and it was the first time Merrin had ever caught them in a lie, because she could see—in the set of their mouth, in the way their hands closed around each other—that they knew exactly what she might be, and were praying to whatever gods smugglers kept to be wrong.

* * *

The argument in Lowmarsh was worse than the horse, because it involved people, and people broke differently than animals.

They had come down to the river town for salt and news, moving separately the way Yara always insisted, Merrin trailing behind with her hood up playing a mute orphan girl, which required no acting whatsoever on days when the thing in her chest was quiet. But in the chandlers' yard, two men were shouting—one of them the chandler himself, red-faced, and the other a barge-hand with a boat-hook in his fists—and around them a ring of townsfolk doing nothing, the way rings of townsfolk do, and the boat-hook came up, and the chandler grabbed a billet of firewood, and Merrin saw, with terrible clarity, the exact geometry of how the next three seconds would go: hook into collarbone, wood into skull, blood on the salt sacks.

So she sang.

One note. One clear, high, silver note that cut through the yard like a blade of light through a shuttered room, and every man in it stopped—not startled, not frightened, *stopped*, mid-word, mid-breath, the boat-hook hanging, the firewood drooping, twelve faces turning toward her with the blank wondering look of sleepers hearing their names called. For three heartbeats the whole yard hung suspended, and in that silence Merrin felt the thing beneath her scar stretch like a cat waking, vast and patient and pleased, and she understood suddenly, in her skin rather than her mind, that the silence was hers, that she had reached into twelve grown men and taken hold of their voices like handles—

She let go. The note died. The yard crashed back into motion, the argument resuming half a beat later as though nothing had happened, though the chandler's swing went wide and the boat-hand lowered his hook and neither of them quite knew why they'd lost their anger, only that they had, and they shuffled apart muttering, embarrassed, relieved.

Merrin walked away as fast as she could without running. Behind her she heard someone say, "Did you hear that? What was that?" and someone else say, "Bird, probably," and someone else say, "In winter?" and she kept walking, hood down, heart slamming, until she reached the alley where Yara waited with the salt, and Yara took one look at her face and knew.

"How many?" they asked.

"Twelve. Maybe thirteen. I don't—" Her voice cracked, and to her horror she found tears coming, hot and furious, the kind she hated because they made her feel eight years old, which she was, which was the whole unbearable truth of it. "Yara, I *stopped* them. I reached into them and I *stopped* them, and it felt—" She choked on the word. She said it anyway. "It felt *good*."

Yara knelt in the dirty snow and pulled her in, and held her, and over their shoulder Merrin saw the chandler's wife staring down the alley at them, at the crying child and the stranger kneeling in the filth, and Yara saw her too, and rose smoothly, and said loudly, "There now, love, I told you the fever would pass," and steered Merrin away with a hand between her shoulders like any weary parent, and only when they were three streets gone did they speak again, low:

"It should feel good. That's what no one tells you. The gift is sweet—that's the trap of it. Every swordspeaker who ever burned a village started by stopping an argument." A pause. "My mentor used to say the voice is a river and the oath is the banks. Without banks, a river is just a flood looking for somewhere to go."

"Who made the banks?"

Yara's jaw tightened. "We'll get there," they said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it, and Merrin filed it away with the other unanswered questions she was learning to carry: *what am I, who sealed me, why does the scar ache when the crown's bells ring.*

* * *

That night, in the loft above the ferry-house where the Ferrywoman's people let them sleep for the price of not asking questions, Merrin dreamed again.

She dreamed of towers—white towers, seven of them, arranged in a ring like fingers of a cupped hand—and ten thousand voices singing in a language that was older than the stones, and she dreamed she stood at the center of the ring with her arms open and the song poured *through* her, not from her, through her, the way a river pours through a valley, and every voice in the ten thousand touched hers and knew her and loved her, and she was not Merrin there, she was something with a name that had nine sounds and meant *the vessel that remembers*, and the singing was so beautiful that she woke herself weeping.

She sat up in the straw. Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the ferry-house: low voices, the clink of cups, the Ferrywoman's laugh like a knife being wrapped in silk. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds.

And beneath her hand, unbidden, her lips moved, and what came out was not a hum but a word, soft and clear, in the language of the towers:

*"Vessha ilen korath."*

*I remember the beginning.*

She didn't know how she knew the translation. She knew it the way she knew the taste of snow, the way she knew her own heartbeat—absolutely, without origin.

Downstairs, the voices stopped. All of them. Even the Ferrywoman's laugh, cut off mid-silk. In the sudden silence Merrin heard boots on stairs, and Yara appeared in the loft doorway with a lamp held high and their face drained white as the snow on the pass where they'd found her, and behind Yara, at the bottom of the stairs, the Ferrywoman stood wrapped in her grey shawls, looking up with eyes like coins at the bottom of a deep well.

"Child," the Ferrywoman said, in a voice that carried without effort, that filled the stairwell the way her own single word had filled Merrin's chest, "do you know what you just said?"

"No," Merrin whispered.

"Well," said the Ferrywoman, and her smile was the smile of a woman recalculating every debt she had ever owed, "I do. And I think it's time you and I talked price."

Yara stepped backward, putting themselves bodily between the stairs and the child, one hand drifting toward the sword they no longer wore openly. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"Oh, I'm not thinking anything, oath-keeper." The Ferrywoman began to climb the stairs, slow, unhurried, her shawls whispering against the rail. "For the first time in a very long while, I'm *listening*. Because that word she spoke hasn't been said aloud in this world for two hundred years—and every soul in this house who heard it just felt it in their bones. Including, I'd wager, certain hounds of quiet sleeping in the town below."

Outside, far off across the frozen marshes, something began to howl.

It was not a wolf.
