## 21. The Kestrel's Price

The Kestrel camp sat in a fold of the mountains like a coin dropped into a beggar's palm—hidden, and hidden well. Yara had been there twice before in ten years, both times at night, both times hooded, both times leaving with more questions than goods. This time they came by daylight, with a child's hand in theirs and the smell of woodsmoke rising to meet them like an old acquaintance.

Merrin had stopped shivering two days ago, which Yara chose to read as trust rather than exhaustion. She walked close, her small fingers hooked through Yara's belt-loop the way children hook themselves to things they have decided not to lose. The seal scar over her heart itched sometimes; Yara could tell because she scratched at it absently, the way one scratches at a word caught on the tip of the tongue.

"Don't talk," Yara murmured, for the hundredth time.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were humming."

Merrin pressed her lips together, wounded. The tune died between them, but not before Yara felt it settle somewhere under their ribs—a round, simple, old. Older than it had any right to be, coming out of an eight-year-old mouth. Yara said nothing about that. There was a whole country of things they weren't saying anything about, and they were all of them getting crowded.

The sentries found them before the camp did. Two figures unfolded from the scree above the trail, crossbows held low and easy, faces wrapped against the wind. One of them whistled three notes—descending, then a fourth held long—and Yara answered with the smuggler's counter-sign, a flat palm turned outward, fingers spread. *No steel. No song. Parley.*

"Yara Eriksen." The taller sentry lowered her bow. Her eyes went to Merrin and stayed there a moment too long. "Clan-mother said you'd come. Said you'd come *begging*, actually. Her words."

"Her words are usually accurate," Yara said. "It's what makes them so unpleasant."

The sentry laughed despite herself, and led them down.

* * *

The heart of the Kestrel camp was a ring of wagons around a fire-pit big enough to roast an ox, sunk into the earth and lined with stone so its light wouldn't betray them from the ridgelines. Around it, perhaps forty people moved through the business of living: mending nets, curing hides, arguing over dice. Children chased each other between the wagon wheels until a woman cuffed one gently and pointed him toward the woodpile. Ordinary life, pitched on a knife-edge of secrecy, the way smugglers had always lived.

Sera Kestrel sat apart from the fire, on a wagon-tongue worn smooth as a pew, working a whetstone along a skinning knife with the patience of someone who had done ten thousand other patient things in her life. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a door, with iron-grey hair braided back from a face that had been handsome once and had decided, somewhere along the way, that handsome was a poor investment. She didn't look up when they approached. She finished her stroke, inspected the edge against her thumbnail, and began another.

"You brought the child," she said.

"You heard."

"I hear everything that happens on my mountain, Eriksen. That's not a boast. It's the job." She set down the knife at last and looked up, and her eyes were pale grey, the colour of the granite all around them, and just as warm. "Sit. Both of you. Girl—there's bread on the third wagon, and a woman named Ottel who'll feed you if you let her. Go."

Merrin looked at Yara. Yara nodded. The girl went, walking backward for the first four steps, watching them both, before the smell of fresh bread won the argument.

"She doesn't remember her name," Yara said, sitting.

"I know what she is." Sera picked up the knife again, though she didn't work it. Just held it, the way other people held opinions. "Word came down from Greyhollow before you did. A seal scar over the heart. Work of the Last Cantor. You've been to Brannoc."

"He told me what he could. Then the Hunt took him."

"Took him slow, I'd wager. Vael likes slow." She said the Inquisitor-General's name without spitting, which somehow made it worse. "And now you're here. So let's not do the dance where you pretend you came for soup. What do you want, Eriksen?"

Yara had rehearsed this. On the road, over three cold nights, they had rehearsed it—the words arranged and rearranged like stones across a stream. And now, sitting in the firelight with forty lives flickering around them, every rehearsed word evaporated, and what came out instead was the truth, plain and unlovely:

"Passage. Protection. Your roads, your people, your silence. I need to get her south to Greyfall, through the winter passes, past the Hunt's net. I can pay in gold—I have gold. I can pay in roads—I know half the hidden ways through Thornfell better than your own scouts. And I can pay in—" They stopped.

"In what?" Sera asked softly. "Say it. You were going to say something interesting."

"In the truth," Yara said. "When it comes out. Whatever's sealed in that girl—Brannoc thinks it's the founding memory of the Choir. The thing the crown's hymn was cut from. If it comes out, if we make it come out, your clan will want to be standing on the right side of it when the realm finds out what it's been singing for two hundred years."

Silence, except the fire. Somewhere behind them, the dice game had stopped; Yara didn't turn to look, but they felt forty pairs of ears listening the way the mountain listened.

Sera set the knife down. This time she left it down.

"You know what my grandmother told me?" she said. "She was alive for the last years of the Choir—not the fall itself, she was too young, but the years after, when the purges started. She said the worst of it wasn't the burning. Burning's quick, and the dead don't gossip. The worst of it was the *quiet*. Whole valleys where nobody sang. Mothers hushing babies mid-note, afraid some passing rider would hear a harmony in the crying and call it treason. Ten years ago they did it again, smaller, and we all learned to be quiet all over again." She leaned forward. "I have spent my whole life teaching my people that the price of survival is silence. Now you walk into my camp and ask me to bet everything on noise."

"I'm asking you to bet on truth."

"Truth's a horse that's never won a race," Sera said. "But fine. Say I believe you. Say the girl carries what you think she carries, say the crown's hymn is stolen, say it all comes apart the way you dream it coming apart. Here's my question, and answer it carefully, because my people are listening and they deserve honesty: when it comes out—when the whole memory is sung, when the throne is naked in front of everyone—who speaks it? Who stands up in front of the realm and says *I am the one, this is the truth, mark me*?"

Yara opened their mouth.

Nothing came out.

Because they knew what she was asking. Not *who carries the memory*—that was Merrin, or would be, when the seal broke. Not *who knows the truth*—half the mountain would know it soon enough. She was asking who would stand in the open, in the capital, in the teeth of whatever remained of the crown's power, and speak under their own name. Their true name. The name that was already on broadsides nailed to tollhouse posts from Greyhollow to the lowlands, the name worth more gold than most men saw in a lifetime.

*Wanted: the Last Speaker.*

"I do," Yara said finally. It came out smaller than they wanted. "If it must be spoken, I'll speak it."

"Mm." Sera studied them the way she'd studied the knife-edge. "You hesitated."

"I hesitated because I'm not a fool."

"No. You hesitated because part of you was already planning how to be gone before the echo faded. Don't glare at me, Eriksen, I've spent thirty years reading smugglers and you're the most honest liar I ever met—you lie best when you're lying to yourself." She rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and walked to the edge of the firelight where the mountain dropped away into darkness. "My price. You want my roads, my riders, my silence, my people's blood risked alongside yours. Here's what it costs. Not gold. Not roads. When the day comes—whatever day, however it comes—you will stand in Greyfall, in the open, under your own name, and you will speak the truth yourself. And when it's done, you will not vanish. You will not slip over the pass into some new life under some new name while the rest of us live in the world your truth made. You will stay. You will be seen. You will *answer*."

"That's not a price," Yara said. "That's a sentence."

"It's the only price worth naming." She turned back, firelight on the granite face. "Anyone can burn bright and die. The mountains are full of brave corpses. What my people need—what the *realm* needs, if any of this is real—is someone who stays afterward. Someone the frightened can look at and say, *they spoke, and they lived, and they're still here, and so can we be.* You want to end the age of hiding? Then stop hiding. All the way. To the end."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then take your gold and try the lowland roads, and may whatever gods listen keep you. My clan stays quiet. We've gotten very good at it." She said it without malice, which was the terrible thing. It was simply arithmetic. Forty lives weighed against one promise.

Yara looked at the fire. At the mending and the dice and the children. At Merrin, three wagons away, sitting on a crate beside the broad woman Ottel, tearing bread, laughing at something—laughing, the first time since the bridge, and the sound went through Yara like a blade finding the gap in armor.

They thought of the shrine. Of the oath, sworn at nineteen with their mentor's hand on their shoulder: *my voice is bound, my voice is bridled, my voice serves restraint.* They had kept that oath for ten years in silence and five months in blood and broken bridges, and every keeping had cost something, and now this woman was asking them to swear the opposite of everything they'd been taught—to be seen, to be loud, to be *present* in the world like a lamp on a table instead of a coal banked in ash.

"I can't promise," Yara said.

Sera's eyebrows rose a fraction. Of all the answers, Yara suspected, that was the one she hadn't priced in.

"I can't promise," Yara said again, steadier. "Not because I won't. Because I don't know what I'll be when that day comes. I've spent ten years being no one, and I was good at it, and part of me—" they made themselves say it, made it true in the saying— "part of me is still planning the road over the pass. Part of me hears you ask and starts counting exits. That's not a flaw I can swear away tonight. If I give you my word knowing that, I'm giving you a counterfeit, and you'd know it, and I'd know it, and counterfeit coin poisons everything it touches."

The fire cracked. Somewhere a wagon creaked under shifting weight.

"So what are you giving me?" Sera asked.

"A true thing instead. I will go to Greyfall. I will see this through to the end—her end, the hymn's end, whatever the end is. And I will *try* to be the person you're asking for. I will try to stand in the light and keep standing. But I won't swear it on my name, because my name is the one thing I've got left that isn't broken, and I won't spend it on a lie—even a kind one." Yara stood. Their knees ached; they'd stopped noticing years ago when the ache became permanent. "Ask your people. Tell them exactly what I said. If half a promise and a hard road is worth more to you than nothing, we leave at dawn. If it isn't, we leave anyway, and I'll owe you nothing but the memory of your hospitality."

For a long moment Sera Kestrel said nothing at all. Then she laughed—a short, rough bark, entirely without humor and entirely genuine.

"Ten years I've heard about the Last Speaker. The ghost of Thornfell. The one who unmade a garrison's swords at Hollow Ford and vanished before the dust settled." She shook her head slowly. "And here you stand telling me you might run away. Do you know what that makes you, Eriksen?"

"Tired."

"*Human.*" She spat into the fire, an old gesture of sealing. "The ghost I couldn't help. The tired woman with a scared child and a half-honest tongue—that one I can gamble on. Dawn, then. Take the wagon with the false bottom; Rurik will drive you as far as the Nine Bends. And Eriksen—" she paused, and for one moment the clan-mother fell away and something older looked out, something that remembered quiet valleys and mothers hushing their babies, "—when the day comes, and it comes faster than you think, remember that you told me the truth about yourself. Remember that somebody knows you wanted to run. It's harder to run when you've been *seen* wanting it. That's not a threat. It's a gift. The only kind I have left to give."

* * *

Merrin was asleep before the wagon cleared the first ridge.

Rurik drove in silence, a leathery man with hands like tree roots, humming tunelessly through his nose in the deliberate way of a man who had trained himself never to hum anything real. Yara sat in the back with the girl's head in their lap, her breath fogging and fading in the cold air, and watched the Kestrel fires sink behind them like a handful of embers poured down the dark.

They should sleep. There would be no safe sleep for a long time—maybe never again, if Sera got her wish, if the day came and Yara stood in Greyfall and *stayed*. But sleep wouldn't come. Instead they sat with the girl's warmth seeping through their cloak and turned Sera's words over like river stones, looking for the flaw in each one, and finding none, and hating that.

*I will try.*

Two words. The smallest possible honest thing. And yet they sat in Yara's chest heavier than the oath had, heavier than the sword-oath, because the oath had been given by a nineteen-year-old who believed in causes, and this was given by a thirty-year-old who only believed in the girl breathing against their knee.

Merrin stirred. Murmured something. Yara bent close, expecting a dream-word, a child-nonsense syllable—

—and froze.

Because it wasn't nonsense. It was Old Tongue, the high cadence, the formal register that scholars needed a decade to parse, flowing soft and slurred out of a sleeping eight-year-old mouth: *"...and the ninth voice shall not be taken, but given..."*

Then she sighed, scratched at the seal scar, and slept on, ordinary again, her cheek smudged with bread crumbs.

Yara sat very still in the swaying wagon, the dark mountains sliding past, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever choice they had made at the fire, the choice had already begun making itself. The seal was thinning. The memory was waking. And somewhere ahead, in a city built on a stolen song, a Chancellor was counting the days his hymn had left.

"Dawn," Yara whispered to no one, to the girl, to themselves. "We move at dawn."

The wagon rolled south through the dark, carrying a liar's half-promise, a sleeping vessel, and the last unspent courage of a woman who had promised to try.
