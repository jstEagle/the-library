## 22. Winter March

The snow began at dawn and did not stop.

Yara had walked the high road between Thornfell and the lowlands in every season the mountains offered, and they had a private taxonomy for all of them: the wet snow that clung and dragged, the dry snow that hissed like sand, the treacherous crust that held until it didn't. This was none of those. This was the old snow, the mountain's own snow, the kind that fell in silence so complete it felt less like weather than like intention—as if the world above the tree line had decided to erase the road, the passes, the two small figures moving along them, and start again.

Good, Yara thought, pulling the wool scarf higher over their face. Let it erase us. Let it erase everything behind us too—the bridge at Coldspring, the dead riders frozen under the ice, the arrow that had grazed Merrin's shoulder and cracked something inside her that no eye could see. Let the snow fall on all of it until spring, by which time either they would be in Greyfall or they would be nothing at all.

Behind them, Merrin trudged in Yara's bootprints, each step placed exactly where Yara's had been, because that was how you crossed a cornice slope without waking it. The girl had learned fast. Children always learned fast; that was the cruelty of it. They learned the things that kept them alive and never asked why an eight-year-old needed to know such things at all.

"Yara." Merrin's voice came muffled through the white. "How much farther?"

"To the waystation? Half a day, if the pass holds."

"No. To the end."

Yara did not answer at once. Ahead, the trail bent around a shoulder of black granite furred with rime, and beyond it the world simply stopped—no ridge, no valley, only the pale blur of falling snow meeting the paler blur of cloud, so that walking forward felt like walking off the edge of a page.

"Nine days," Yara said finally. "Ten, in this. Greyfall sits in the river valley where the mountains give up. We go down out of Thornfell, across the Sere Plain, and then we're in the crown's country, where every mile is watched and every innkeeper has a broadside nailed to his door with my face on it."

"My face too?"

"Not yet." Yara glanced back. The girl's cheeks were scoured red, her lashes crusted white, the seal scar at her collar hidden beneath three layers of Brannoc's old sweaters. "They don't know what you look like. That's worth more than gold. Guard it."

Merrin nodded with the grave seriousness she brought to everything now, and Yara turned back into the wind and hated themselves a little for teaching a child to think like a fugitive. But the alternative was teaching her nothing, and children who knew nothing ended up in the resonance cells beneath the palace, or worse—in Vael's quiet rooms with their brass instruments and their patient, courteous questions.

They walked. The snow fell. Somewhere behind them, days back now, the Hunt was regrouping, and somewhere ahead the capital glittered with its borrowed song, and between those two facts there was only this: one foot, then the other, then the other, into the white.

* * *

The waystation was not a station anymore. It had been a smuggler's cache once—a hollowed-out cairn below the treeline with a stone chimney venting smoke through cracks no soldier would ever bother to read—but the Kestrel clan had walled it up years ago after a betrayal Yara had never heard the full story of. What remained was a chamber barely tall enough to kneel in, dry as old bone, smelling of pine pitch and cold stone, and utterly invisible from the trail unless you knew to count eleven boulders past the split birch and look down.

Yara counted. Looked down. Dug.

Inside, they got a fire going no larger than a cupped hand—dry juniper, burned in a stone hollow, its smoke swallowed by the chimney's maze—and melted snow in a tin cup, and shared a heel of black bread and a strip of smoked goat that Sera Kestrel's people had pressed on them at the last safehouse. Merrin ate slowly, the way she did everything, chewing as though tasting were a duty owed to the food.

Then, without warning, in the firelight, the girl began to hum.

Yara went still.

It was not the seal leaking—that sound was different, a wrongness, a chord that made the teeth ache and the horses scream and grown men forget what they'd been saying mid-word. Yara had heard enough of it since Coldspring to know it in their bones. This was not that. This was small. Deliberate. A melody of perhaps six notes, rising and falling, round and simple, the kind of tune that exists in every village in every age and belongs to no one.

"What is that?" Yara asked, before they could stop themselves.

Merrin looked up, caught, a little guilty. "A round. The goose-girls sang it. Before." She said *before* the way she always did now—a door closed gently on a room she couldn't enter. Her life before the pass, before the snow, before Yara, sealed away with everything else. "You sing it in a circle. Each person starts when the person before reaches the middle. It makes—" She frowned, searching. "It makes more than the song. Everyone sings the same thing but it comes out different, and it holds itself up. Like a roof."

"A round," Yara said. Their voice came out strange. "Yes. That's what a round is."

"You know it?"

"I know the principle." Yara fed a twig to the fire. "The Choir didn't sing rounds. Rounds are for people. Choir-work was—" They stopped. Ten years of silence did not break all at once; it broke in small places, like ice. "Choir-work was architecture. One voice carrying one line, bound and bound again, so that nothing escaped and nothing changed. Ours wasn't a roof. It was a vault."

Merrin considered this with her whole body, the way children do, knees drawn up, chin on them, firelight swimming in her dark eyes. Then she said, with the terrible directness of the young:

"Sing it with me anyway."

"Merrin—"

"It's just a song about geese. Nothing happens. Nothing *unmakes*." She scooted closer, cross-legged, undaunted. "Please. It's been nine days of walking and counting rocks and being quiet. I want to sing something with someone. That's all."

And there it was, the thing Yara had been outrunning for ten years, delivered by an eight-year-old in a stolen sweater: not a demand, not a threat. An invitation. *With someone.* As though singing were not the most dangerous act in the realm. As though it were simply what voices were for.

Yara looked at the fire. At the small scarred girl waiting patiently, humming her six notes again, coaxing.

"The words," Yara said at last, hoarsely, "are about a goose who won't fly south."

"She stays all winter," Merrin said, delighted. "And everyone tells her she'll freeze, and she says the pond is hers and she'll stay till spring, and then in spring she's still there, and nobody remembers why anyone ever left."

Something moved in Yara's chest that had no name they were willing to give it. They cleared their throat. It had been ten years since they had sung anything—not Choir-work, not even a tavern tune, not a note. A swordspeaker's discipline was total; the gift lived in the voice, and the voice was a blade that never fully sheathed. Even humming was a risk. Even breathing too rhythmically, some nights, in the early years.

But the fire was small. The snow was deep. No hound could feel resonance through a mountain's heart of stone, and no rider would climb the White Shoulder in this. And the child was looking at them with an expression Yara recognized from the inside: the expression of someone asking to be less alone.

"All right," Yara said. "Teach me the line."

Merrin taught it. It took three tries, because Yara's ear kept reaching for harmonies that weren't there, reaching for the great load-bearing intervals of training that had been drilled into them before they were Merrin's age, and each time the girl laughed—not unkindly—and said, *No, simpler, it's just a goose,* and each time Yara stripped another ornament away until what remained was plain as bread and warm as a hearthstone.

Then Merrin said, "Now I start, and when I get to the top of the hill—you start. From the beginning. Don't wait for me. Just go."

They sang.

It should have been nothing. Six notes, a silly lyric, a child's round in a hole in a mountain while the world tried to bury them both. But when Merrin reached the top of the phrase and Yara entered underneath her, the two lines braided, diverged, met again, and the little tune held itself up in the air between them like a roof, exactly as the girl had promised, and Yara Eriksen—who had unmade steel with a word, who had watched their mentor burn, who had spent a decade learning to be silent as stone—felt tears freeze on their cheeks before they noticed them forming.

They sang it four times through. On the fourth, Merrin's voice cracked with tiredness and Yara carried both lines alone for a bar until the girl found her way back in, and neither of them remarked on it, and the fire burned down to embers, and outside the snow went on erasing the world with its vast, indifferent patience.

"That," Merrin said sleepily, curling against Yara's side as though it were the most natural thing in the world, "was better than the goose-girls."

Yara did not trust their voice. They put an arm around the child instead, and watched the embers, and did not sleep for a long time.

* * *

Two days later the storm broke and gave them one clear hour at the top of the White Shoulder, and in that hour Yara saw the Sere Plain spread below like a frozen sea, and far out on its southern edge, a smudge of grey-gold that might have been haze and was not: Greyfall, the capital, forty miles distant, wearing its winter light like a crown.

They also saw, on the plain's northern rim, a line of movement. Too regular for caravans. Too slow for wolves. Columns, in the snow. The Hunt, sweeping the lowlands in methodical ranks, burning the brush as they went to deny shelter, driving everything ahead of them like beaters driving game.

Toward the capital. The same direction they were going.

"They're not hunting us," Yara murmured, more to themselves than to Merrin. "Not anymore. They're herding. Vael doesn't want to catch us in the open—he wants us funneled. Every road to Greyfall will be watched. He's building a corridor and he knows we'll walk down it, because we have no choice."

"How do you know it's him?" Merrin asked. "The one thinking it?"

"Because it's clever, and patient, and it treats people like water—something to be channeled, never wasted." Yara shouldered the pack. "I met his kind once. Before."

They descended. The wind rose again behind them, as though the mountain were sorry to see them go.

* * *

On the fifth night they made camp in a hollow of the Sere's edge-forest, a stand of black pines that kept the worst of the wind off, and for the first time in days there was wood enough for a real fire, and time enough to sit beside it without counting minutes.

Merrin was asleep almost before her head touched the bedroll. Since Coldspring she slept hard and long, the way bodies do when they are quietly fighting something, and Yara had taken to sitting watch over her with a vigilance that had nothing to do with riders. Twice in five nights the girl had spoken in her sleep—not the old-tongue dreaming of the first weeks, which had been eerie and distant, but ordinary words, small ones. *Don't take the pond. Don't take the pond.* The goose, Yara thought. Even asleep, she was defending her small territories.

Yara fed the fire and found, to their surprise, that they were not alone with their thoughts. The habit of solitude ran deep, but it had been developing cracks all winter, and tonight the crack widened into speech before they could stop it.

"I'm afraid," Yara said aloud, to the fire, to the sleeping child, to no one—to the first listener their fear had ever been granted.

The words hung in the pine-smelling dark. Yara stared at them, so to speak, astonished they had come out.

"I have been afraid for ten years," they went on, quietly, because the habit of quiet was deeper than the new habit of speaking. "Afraid of the Hunt, afraid of the roads, afraid of my own voice. But it was a small fear. A manageable fear. Fear of losing what I had left, which was almost nothing, which meant there was almost nothing to lose." A log shifted; sparks climbed. "This is different. This is fear of failing something. Of taking a child to the gates of the thing that wants her and betting her life on a song. And I find—" Their jaw worked. "I find I don't know how to carry it. I know how to carry silence. Nobody ever taught me this."

The fire popped. Merrin stirred but did not wake.

"You asked me once," Yara said, softer now, "who made me promise. The oath. You asked it like it was a simple question, and I gave you the answer I was given: the Choir made us promise, all speakers promise, power bound to restraint, the sword that swears never to leave the sheath unless—" They shook their head. "But that wasn't your question, was it. Your question was *who*. Who stood in front of a child and made her swear away her own voice. And the honest answer is: Master Ilvane did. My mentor. The woman who burned. She knelt me down on stone when I was younger than you and she said, *Your voice is a door, and doors open both ways, and what comes through the other way will wear your face.* And I believed her. I believe her still. But I have begun to wonder—" Yara's breath fogged and unraveled. "I have begun to wonder whether she was protecting me, or protecting everyone *from* me, or protecting the oath itself, which is not the same thing as protecting people. Whether the restraint was wisdom, or just fear wearing armor. Whether ten years of silence saved the world or only saved me the trouble of finding out what I'm for."

The confession sat in the clearing like a third presence. Yara found that their hands were shaking, and let them.

"And now I'm going to walk you into Greyfall," they said, "and break a promise I've kept since childhood, in front of ten thousand people, and I don't know if it will free the realm or drown it, and I don't know if I'll survive my own voice, and the only thing I know—all the way down, the one true thing—is that I can't turn back. Because turning back means letting the hymn fail in the dark, letting Ives find some other child, letting the lie run another two hundred years until it kills everyone it touches." They looked at Merrin's sleeping face, slack and young and marked with a scar she didn't remember earning. "So yes. I'm afraid. I wanted you to hear it someday, in case I don't get the chance to say it when it matters. Being brave isn't not being afraid. Someone told me that once, and I didn't believe them until right now, saying it to a sleeping child by a fire I probably shouldn't have lit."

The wind moved through the pines. Far off, a branch cracked under snow-weight, and Yara's hand went to their knife out of ten years' habit, and relaxed.

Merrin opened her eyes.

Not all the way—just a sliver, firelight catching the dark of them, and Yara understood with a lurch that she had been awake for some unknown portion of it, perhaps all of it, listening the way children listen: completely, and without mercy, and without judgment either.

"I already knew," she said, drowsy. "About the fear. You hold your shoulders like the goose-girls did when the tax-man came." She burrowed deeper into the bedroll. "It's all right, Yara. You can be afraid. I'll be brave enough for both of us until you're ready. That's what a round is. Somebody carries the line until you come in."

She was asleep again within moments, breath evening out, one mittened hand curled near Yara's knee as though to make sure the promise stayed put.

Yara sat unmoving for a long time, throat tight, watching the fire.

*Somebody carries the line until you come in.*

All the theology of the Choir—all the vaults and bindings and oaths sworn on stone—and a child had just handed them the whole truth of it in a sentence, wrapped in a song about geese. The gift had never been meant for vaults. It had been meant for circles. For rounds. For people holding the line for one another until every voice found its way home.

* * *

They came down off the Sere's edge on the eighth day, and the world changed.

The pines gave out. The plain opened wide and white and featureless, and the sky above it was the color of pewter, and the wind here did not gust but simply blew, steadily, from the south—from the capital—carrying with it, faint and constant, a sound Yara had not heard in ten years and had hoped never to hear again at close range.

The hymn.

Every city of the crown sustained it: the great sustaining song, sung continuously since the founding, by rota, by law, by voices paid in bread and coin, rising from the temples and the palace towers and diffusing over the land like heat over a forge. From forty miles it was nothing—a pressure at the edge of hearing, a wrongness in the wind's tone, the way a struck bell keeps ringing long after the ear stops minding it. But Yara's ears were not ordinary ears, and what they heard beneath the hymn's vast surface made their stomach drop.

It was fraying. Audibly fraying. Threads of the harmony dropping out and not being picked up; phrases arriving a half-beat late; and underneath, growing daily, a thin sour note like a hairline crack in a bell, running through the whole two-hundred-year chord from top to bottom.

Brannoc had warned them. *The hymn is dying,* the old healer had said, drunk and fierce in his Lowmarsh kitchen. *It's been dying for years. They're propping it up with harvested voices, with those poor devils in the cells, and it buys them months, not years. When it goes, it goes all at once, and everything built on it goes with it.*

Merrin had stopped walking. She stood facing south into the wind, head tilted, and her lips moved slightly, following the hymn's contour the way a child follows a familiar prayer—and then, horribly, naturally, she began to hum the missing parts. The dropped threads. The late phrases, restored. Her small voice slid into the gaps in the great song forty miles away and filled them, briefly, perfectly, the way water fills a footprint.

And far to the south, Yara would have sworn on any god living or dead, the hymn steadied. For three heartbeats, the crack in the bell closed. Forty miles away, someone in Greyfall must have felt the sudden strength and wondered at it.

Then Merrin swayed, and Yara caught her arm, and the moment broke.

"Don't," Yara said—too sharply, and gentled it. "Don't sing with it. Not yet. Not until we choose the time. If they feel you—if *he* feels you—we lose everything. Do you understand?"

Merrin looked up at them, flushed with effort and something brighter than effort, something that frightened Yara more than any rider ever had: recognition. The girl was recognizing the hymn. Piece by piece, thread by thread, the seal was showing her what the crown's great song was made of, and where it had been cut from, and whose voices had been stolen to make it.

"It's ours," Merrin said simply. "Yara. The big song. It's *ours*. They took it."

"I know," Yara said. "I know. Nine more days, little one. Nine days, and then we tell them all."

They turned north-by-east, off the watched road, onto a smuggler's track that Sera Kestrel had scratched into birchbark for them—a route through the frozen fens that no soldier's map showed. Behind them the columns of the Hunt swept the plain. Ahead, Greyfall glittered on its river, singing its stolen song into the winter sky.

And on the second morning of the fen crossing, as they picked their way along a causeway the color of old bone, Merrin suddenly seized Yara's sleeve and pointed back the way they had come.

Far off, at the fen's edge, small with distance but unmistakable, a single rider had stopped on the causeway's end. He did not follow. He did not turn back. He simply stood his horse in the wind, watching them go, and even at that distance Yara felt the weight of the gaze like a hand between the shoulder blades—and saw, when the rider lifted one arm in something that was not quite a salute and not quite a warning, the glint of Inquisitor-General's silver at his throat.

Vael had found them. And he was letting them walk on.
