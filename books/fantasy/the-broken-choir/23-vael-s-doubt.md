## 23. Vael's Doubt

The extraction chamber beneath the Chancery had been built by men who feared music.

Vael had always found that telling. The walls were stone cut thick as a man's arm, and every surface had been scored with grooves—hundreds of them, running in patterns that were almost, but not quite, harmonious. The architects had believed that discordant geometry could swallow resonance whole, that a room made of argument could not be made to sing. They had been half right. In this room a voice went in and came out flattened, pressed like a flower between the pages of a ledger. But nothing built by human hands was truly silent, and Vael had spent enough nights here to hear what the stones still held: a faint hum, low and persistent, like a hive dreaming. Like something waiting to be remembered.

He stood at the brass rail and watched Brannoc Hale in the chair below.

The healer had not slept well. That much was plain even to eyes less trained than Vael's—the tremor in the hands strapped to the armrests, the gray bloom of exhaustion under the eyes, the way the man's jaw worked as though chewing on some last stubborn sentence he refused to swallow. Three days in a cell did that. Three days and the anticipation of the chair, which was often worse than the chair itself. Vael had seen men weep before the extraction ever began, beg, bargain, confess to crimes they had never committed simply to fill the terrible waiting with words.

Brannoc had done none of these things. He sat in the resonance cradle like a man settling into a bath he'd paid for, and when Vael descended the iron stair, the old drunk looked up with bloodshot eyes and said, "You'll want to open the window after. It stinks in here when I'm done."

"Done with what?"

"Dying, probably." He grinned, gap-toothed, unrepentant. "They say it feels like dying."

"They say many things." Vael took his place at the console, laying out the silvered diadem with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments. Its filaments caught the lamplight, fine as spider silk, fine as harp strings. "In my experience, what people say about pain is almost always an understatement dressed as a boast."

"You're not what I expected," Brannoc said. "The Inquisitor-General. I thought you'd be a butcher. You talk like a librarian."

"I have been called both." Vael fitted the diadem over the man's skull, fingers deft, impersonal. "Hold still. This is easier if you don't fight it—and harder for me if you do, which means it will be harder for you. Resonance punishes resistance twice."

"And if I sing instead?"

Vael paused. For the first time he looked directly into the prisoner's face, and Brannoc looked back, and there was something in the old man's expression that Vael would spend the rest of his life trying to name—not defiance, exactly. Not fear. Something older than either. The look of a man who has already decided how a story ends and is merely curious whether you will read it correctly.

"If you sing," Vael said quietly, "I will hear it."

"That," said Brannoc Hale, "is the idea."

* * *

The machine worked by sympathy. That was the elegant lie at its heart: that memory was a song stored in flesh, and the diadem a second voice that need only find the melody and follow it, note by note, until the whole song lay open on the wax cylinders and copper plates of the console. The Chancery called it *extraction*. The technicians who ran the devices in the capital called it *harvest*, when they thought no one important was listening. Vael had never cared for either word. Extraction implied removal without cost. Harvest implied ripeness, abundance, a field that would grow again.

What it actually resembled, in his long experience, was burglary. A man's past opened like a house at night, and you moved through its rooms with a lamp, touching everything, taking what you were ordered to take, leaving fingerprints on the lives of strangers.

He set the first filament against Brannoc's temple. "Begin whenever you're ready."

"Oh, I've been ready since Greyhollow." The old man closed his eyes. And sang.

It was a small song at first—a workman's tune, a walking rhythm, the kind of thing a man hums over a mortar and pestle. Lowmarsh dialect, rough vowels, a tune about eels and a faithless wife. Vael followed it down through the layers as he had followed a thousand memories down through their layers: the smell of river mud, a crooked doorframe, a woman laughing with her sleeves rolled up. Ordinary life, rendered in perfect fidelity. The machine drank it and Vael let it drink, waiting, patient, for the strata that mattered. Ten years ago. The purge. The Choir. Whatever this man had witnessed of Yara Eriksen, the last speaker, the one whose face was now nailed to every post from Thornfell to the sea.

He found the fire first. He always found the fire first; fire was the spine of that year, the way all memories of the purge bent toward burning the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone.

But this fire was wrong.

Vael realized it slowly, the way one realizes a dream is a dream—not all at once but in accumulating details, each too vivid, each slightly too arranged. The abbey burned around him in colors no fire makes: green-gold at the edges, violet at the heart, and the smoke rose not upward but outward, unfurling like petals, like the opening of some enormous flower, and within the smoke there were figures—singers, hundreds of singers, standing untouched in the flames with their mouths open, and they were not screaming.

They were singing.

The sound arrived then, and it arrived all at once, the way dawn arrives—all the darkness gone between one breath and the next. Vael had heard the sustaining hymn every day of his life, as every subject of the crown had, thrumming beneath the world's ordinary noise, so constant it had become inaudible, like the sound of one's own blood. He had assumed the Choir's music would resemble it. Grander, perhaps. Deeper. But of the same family, the same grammar, the same obedient architecture.

This was nothing of the kind.

It began in the lowest register he could perceive and kept descending past the floor of hearing, into a region where sound became something felt in the teeth and the sternum and the wet dark of the body, and above it voices climbed in intervals that should have been dissonance and were instead a beauty so acute it registered as grief. The harmony did not resolve. It *refused* to resolve—it turned every cadence into a doorway, every ending into a beginning, and Vael understood, standing in the heart of a forged conflagration inside a dead man's memory, that he had spent his entire life listening to a copy and mistaking the copy for the art.

Because it was forged. Some part of him, the part trained in thirty years of interrogation, knew it even as he drowned in it. Memories extracted under the diadem had a texture, a grain; they wobbled at the edges where the mind's own doubt intruded. This vision had no wobble. It was too complete, too composed, every detail placed with intention—the violet heart of the flame, the petaling smoke, the faces of the singers each distinct and serene. No memory was ever this well-made. Memory was a drunkard; this was an architect.

*He's building it,* Vael thought, from very far away, from the brass rail and the lamp and the scored stone walls. *He's composing it as I watch.*

And still he could not look away. Still he could not stop the machine from following, because the machine followed beauty the way water follows gravity, and so did he.

The vision turned. The abbey's great doors opened—or seemed to open; the fire parted like a curtain—and through it came riders in armor the color of old honey, the livery of the first king's household two centuries gone, and they carried not swords but instruments: long brazen horns, curved and cruel, and when they sounded them the singers' song cracked down the middle like ice on a spring river. Vael felt the crack in his own chest. He felt the harmony die by degrees, voice after voice falling silent, and each silence was worse than any scream, because each silence was a door closing on a room he had only just discovered existed.

And above it all, faintly, woven through the ruin like gold thread through black cloth, he heard the melody that the horns were stealing. Taking. Rolling up like a carpet, folding the Choir's founding song into their brazen throats, carrying it away to a palace where it would be sung by one voice instead of ten thousand, sustained by decree instead of devotion, owned instead of shared—

The vision ended.

Vael came back to himself with his hands flat on the console and his breath coming hard and his cheeks wet. He wiped them absently, without shame; there was no one to see but a prisoner, and prisoners saw everything anyway.

Below him, Brannoc Hale opened his eyes. They were clear. Clearer than they had any right to be after three days in a cell and an hour under the diadem, and in them Vael read a question, gentle and terrible: *Did you hear it? Do you understand now?*

"The cylinders," Vael said. His voice came out sanded smooth. "Play back the last section."

The technician at the secondary console—young Pell, who had served him faithfully for six years—turned the crank. The wax gave up its treasure in thin scratches and whispers. Eel songs. River mud. A woman laughing. Then static, a long stretch of it, and then—nothing. Nothing at all where the fire and the flower-smoke and the stolen song had been. Forty minutes of the richest sensory experience of Vael's professional life, and the machine had recorded a hole shaped exactly like a masterpiece.

"There's nothing on it, sir," Pell said, bewildered. "The needles barely moved. It's as if—" He hesitated. "As if he dreamed it straight into your head and left the instrument empty."

"Yes," Vael said. "That is precisely what he did."

He looked down at Brannoc. The old man smiled, closed his eyes, and appeared to fall asleep in the chair, peaceful as a child, having given away nothing and everything.

* * *

That night Vael did not sleep. He sat in his quarters in the Chancery annex with the lamp turned low and the city humming beneath his window—that constant, beloved, subliminal drone of the sustaining hymn, the sound he had been raised inside of, the sound that meant *order*, that meant *the realm endures*, that had been the floor of his existence for fifty-one years.

He listened to it as he had never listened before.

The hymn was supposed to be seamless. Two hundred years of uninterrupted song, renewed at every Hymnal Festival, the crown's great miracle: a working of resonance so vast it knitted the kingdom together, steadied harvests, calmed floods, kept the peace in men's hearts the way a dam keeps a river. Every child learned it in school. *One voice, one realm, one song.* Vael had recited it with the rest. He had hunted swordspeakers in the name of it, believing—truly believing—that every unregistered voice was a crack in the dam, a wild note that could bring the whole edifice down upon the innocent.

He had told himself it was duty. Sitting in the dark, he made himself say it plainly, without ornament: *I believed the hymn protected them. I broke singers to keep the song.*

But the vision had shown him the original. And now, attending to the copy with new ears, he heard what he had spent a lifetime not hearing.

The sustaining hymn was thin.

Not weak—thin. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. A thin thread can hold enormous weight; it simply cannot bear inspection. The hymn had breadth, endurance, reach—but it had no depths. Where the Choir's song had turned every cadence into a doorway, the hymn resolved every phrase neatly, obediently, the same resolution, over and over, century after century, like a sentence repeated until the words wear hollow. It was a melody that had forgotten it was once a harmony. It was one voice doing the work of ten thousand, and doing it competently, and growing tired.

*How long,* Vael wondered, *has it been tired?*

He lit a candle, took out paper, and began—for the first time in his career—to investigate his own employer.

He started with what any honest inquiry must start with: dates. The hymnal archives in the capital were sealed to everyone below the Chancellor, but Vael had spent three decades making himself useful to men who liked being flattered, and seals were flattery's natural prey. He recalled that the festival liturgies were reprinted every twenty-five years, and that the printing houses kept proof sheets. Proof sheets were not sealed. Proof sheets were considered beneath notice—corrected drafts, printer's waste, the scaffolding around the cathedral of the official text.

By the third bell he had a runner fetching him a decade of them from a print-shop owner who owed his son's freedom to Vael's quiet intercession years ago. By the fifth bell the pages were spread across his desk, and Vael was comparing, line by line, the sustaining hymn as printed across eighty years.

The changes were tiny. That was what made them damning. A grace note removed here. A passing tone smoothed into the main melody there. An interval that had been a fourth in the edition of his grandfather's youth was a third by the edition of his father's, and a unison by his own. Each correction had been justified, no doubt, as standardization, as purification, as the removal of regional error. But taken together, across eight decades, the pattern was unmistakable to a man who had just heard the original:

The hymn was being simplified. Deliberately. Generation by generation. As though someone were trimming a garment smaller and smaller to fit a body that was shrinking—as though the song were a muscle going gently, carefully, invisibly into atrophy, and someone in the palace knew, and had known for a very long time, and was managing the decline the way one manages a dying parent: with smaller portions each year, and a smile, and the lights kept bright so the children won't notice.

Vael sat back. The candle guttered. Below his window the hymn hummed on, faithful and thinning, and for the first time in his life the sound frightened him—not because it might fail, but because of what its failure would mean about everything he had done in its service. If the crown's great miracle was a theft—if the order he had enforced with fire and the diadem was merely the shape a stolen song made when it was held too tightly by too few hands—then the Hunt was not protection. It was concealment. Every singer broken had been a witness silenced. Every voice harvested—

He stopped. His own thought turned on him like a blade finding its mark.

*Every voice harvested.* That was what the Chancery called the work. That was the word the technicians used when they thought no one important listened. And Vael had never asked why the crown needed so many extracted memories, so many copper plates of other people's pasts stacked in the deep vaults, if the hymn was self-sustaining. He had assumed scholarship. He had assumed vigilance.

Or had he assumed nothing at all? Had he simply never looked, the way one does not look at the sun?

He thought of Merrin—the child, the seal scar, the orders from the Chancellor himself: *alive, at any cost.* He had obeyed that order without asking why a crown that possessed the eternal hymn should want a child so badly. Now the question assembled itself, piece by piece, with the cold click of a lock turning: a failing hymn needed a source. A stolen song needed a living vessel to steal from anew. The hunt for the gifted was not a purge.

It was a shopping expedition.

Vael rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over the sleeping capital, its thousand candles, its spires, its contentment. Somewhere out in the winter mountains, a woman with a ruined reputation and a child with a cracked seal were running from him—from *him*, specifically, from his warrants and his hounds—and he understood suddenly that he no longer knew which of them was fleeing the greater danger.

He returned to the desk. On clean paper, in cipher of his own devising, he wrote a single line and folded it into his boot, where he kept the only things in the world he trusted:

*If the hymn fails, who benefits from the manner of its failing?*

Then he snuffed the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the thinning song, and waited for morning, and for the answer he was beginning to dread: that it would fail soon, and that someone intended it to fail in exactly the right way, at exactly the right moment, with exactly the right child in hand—and that the rider dispatched at dawn with fresh orders for Coldspring bridge would carry his seal at the bottom of the warrant.
