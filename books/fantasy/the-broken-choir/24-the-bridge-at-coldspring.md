## 24. The Bridge at Coldspring

The river came down out of the high snowfields in a hurry, as if it too were fleeing something, and where it met the old king's road it spread wide and shallow over pale stones before narrowing again beneath the bridge at Coldspring. The bridge itself was older than the crown—three arches of mountain granite, moss-slicked, built by masons whose names had been sung out of the world. In summer it carried wool carts and pilgrims. In winter it carried nothing but wind.

Yara saw it from the ridge and knew, in the way the body knows a fall before the mind consents to it, that they would not cross it unopposed.

"They're already there," Sera Kestrel said beside them, glassing the far bank with a strip of polished horn. She had come this far with them herself—a thing clan-mothers did not do, a thing she had not done since the Fen War—and her breath came out in white flags that the wind tore apart. "Six riders on the near approach. More under the arches. I count banners."

"I count them too." Yara did not need the horn. The Hunt rode with resonance-tipped lances now, and even at half a mile the tips sang against each other, a thin insect whine that only the gifted heard—or the deaf, who felt it in their teeth. "Vael didn't send soldiers. He sent listeners."

Below them, tucked into the lee of a fallen pine, Merrin slept. She had slept more and more these last days, and her sleep had changed quality: no longer the boneless collapse of an exhausted child but something deeper and less voluntary, a tide going out of her and not coming all the way back. Her lips moved sometimes. Once, waking, she had hummed three notes of a round Yara had never taught her, and the frost on the pine needles above her head had melted in a perfect circle, and both of them had pretended not to notice.

"She shouldn't be here," Yara said.

"She shouldn't be anywhere," Sera said. "That's what being hunted means. There is no *shouldn't* left for her. Only *where*." She folded the horn away. "We can go around. The gorge narrows two miles north. Icy footing, a drop that kills, but no bridge, no riders."

"And if there are riders at the gorge?"

"Then we'll wish we'd died here instead." Sera smiled without humor. She was a broad woman gone grey at the temples, with hands like root-balls and a voice that could carry across a smugglers' parliament without rising. "I'm giving you the choice because you're the one carrying her. But hear me, Eriksen: my clan paid a price to walk you this far, and the debt isn't discharged yet. I'd rather not spend Kestrel lives on a bridge for nothing. So choose well."

Yara looked at the bridge a long time. Three arches. Six riders visible, likely twelve hidden. A river running fast enough to drown a grown man in thirty seconds of cold. And somewhere behind them, always behind them, the hounds of quiet—deaf men with their palms open to the air, reading the tremble of a sealed memory through six feet of stone.

Going around meant the gorge, and the gorge was a throat. If Vael had listeners on the bridge, he had thought about the gorge too.

"We take the bridge," Yara said. "At first light, when the ice on the approaches is still hard. Fast, loud, straight through. Speed is the only armor we have."

Sera studied them. "And if speed fails?"

"Then I stop being careful," Yara said, and heard how much the words cost, and hated that Sera heard it too.

* * *

They came down in the blue hour before dawn, when the world is all shadow and intention and nothing has yet been decided. Yara carried Merrin on their back, the child's arms looped loose around their neck, her cheek against their shoulder, her breath slow and sea-deep. Brannoc had wanted to carry her; Yara had refused without explanation, and Brannoc, who understood everything about guilt and nothing about letting it go, had not argued. Some weights a person needs to feel in order to keep walking.

The approach to the bridge was a causeway of packed snow over gravel, and their boots made almost no sound on it. The river roared underneath, filling the ears, drowning thought. Good. Let the river think for everyone.

The first rider came off the far end at a trot, lance couched, and Yara saw the resonance tip catch the pre-dawn light and throw it back wrong—not a gleam but a shiver, light that vibrated. Behind the rider, torches bloomed along the far bank. Not six. Not twelve. Two score at least, and between the arches the shapes of the deaf listeners, kneeling with bare palms flat against the granite, feeling for the song in the girl on Yara's back.

"Hold!" the lead rider shouted, and his voice cracked on the word, and Yara understood with a cold clarity that these were not veterans. Conscripts. Boys in borrowed mail, told they were hunting monsters.

Yara set Merrin down behind the bridge's near pier, in the black angle where stone met water-smoothed ice, and pressed one finger briefly to the seal scar through her coat—the child stirred but did not wake—and then stepped out onto the open span.

"I am unarmed," Yara called, and let the river carry it. "There's a child asleep by this pier. Whatever your orders say, look at her and decide what they're worth."

For one held breath, the span was silent except for the water.

Then the lead rider spurred forward, and the others came with him, and the morning broke open.

* * *

Later, Yara would try to remember the fight as a sequence of decisions and fail. Memory gave it back as weather: a storm of hooves and torchlight and the whine of resonance tips, and themselves moving through it with ten years of buried instinct rising like floodwater.

The first lance came low, aimed to sweep the legs, and Yara went over it—not dodging so much as refusing it, the body finding the gap the way water finds the crack. They caught the haft as they passed, turned the rider's own momentum against him, and put him into the river. He surfaced gasping, alive, his lance spinning away downstream. One. Not killed. Held.

The second and third came together, and here the arithmetic got worse, because resonance tips were not made to kill swordspeakers cleanly. They were made to *unmake*—to drink the vibration out of whatever they touched, flesh or steel or oath. Yara took the second man's thrust on their forearm, wrapped in a fold of cloak, and felt the cloth die against the tip, felt the skin beneath go numb and singing-wrong, and answered with an elbow to the jaw that dropped him across his saddle. The third man hesitated—he was nineteen at most, and his eyes went from Yara's face to the sleeping child by the pier and back—and in his hesitation Yara read his whole short future, and stepped inside his lance, and broke it across a knee with a sound like a snapped promise.

But there were forty of them, and the listeners between the arches had found what they were looking for. Yara felt it happen: a change in the air pressure of the world, a gathering, the way a room changes when someone opens a door onto winter. The deaf men began to sing—not with throats, they had none of that gift, but with hands, slapping the granite in complex arrhythmic patterns, and the bridge itself began to answer. The stone hummed. The ice on the roadway shivered into needles. And Merrin, asleep against the pier, cried out in a voice that was not entirely her own—a thin silver thread of sound that made every horse scream and every torch gutter—and the seal on her heart, strained past bearing, began visibly to fail. Light leaked through her coat seams like dawn through shutter boards.

"There," one listener screamed, pointing with a whole arm. "THERE, she's opening, TAKE HER ALIVE—"

The riders surged. Yara stood over the child with empty hands and a broken oath humming in the marrow, and did the only arithmetic left: how many bodies between Merrin and the far bank, and whether their own voice, used at full and forbidden strength, would kill the girl it was trying to save.

They were reaching for the answer—the terrible one, the one that ended either in madness or surrender—when the night caught fire.

Not fire. Horns. Three long notes from the northern ridge, a clan-call older than any crown, and then the ridge itself seemed to move. Kestrel riders came down the scree in a controlled avalanche, forty strong, torches streaming, and they did not charge the Hunt's line—they split it, the way water splits on a prow, two wedges driving for the flanks while a third swept the far bank and cut the riders off from their own reinforcements. Sera Kestrel rode at the wedge-point with a river-iron maul across her saddle bow, and her war-cry was not a cry at all but a name, her own, spoken once, which was somehow worse than any scream.

The battle did not so much turn as dissolve. Conscripts faced with clansmen who fought like family quarrels—with fury, yes, but also recognition, restraint, an unwillingness to spend anyone cheaply—broke in knots rather than lines. Yara saw a Kestrel rider deliberately take a lance-blow on his shield rather than answer it lethally. Saw another drag a drowning conscript out of the river by his collar and dump him, coughing, on the bank, out of the fight. This was what Sera had bought with her unnamed price: not an army, but a people who remembered why they were fighting.

In four minutes it was over. The listeners fled first, slipping away between the arches like eels, and the surviving riders threw down their weapons when Sera's voice rolled across the span: "Kestrel ground! Kestrel mercy! Down and live!"

And then, in the ringing quiet after, with the river still roaring its one indifferent note, Yara turned to the pier—

—and found Brannoc already there, on his knees in the trampled snow, and knew before they reached him.

Merrin lay on her side, small and terribly still, her coat scorched open over the heart. A single arrow stood in the snow beside her, its resonance tip dark and dead, spent. It had not struck her full. It had grazed her—a furrow along the ribs, shallow, bleeding sluggishly, a wound that would heal in a week.

It was not the wound.

The seal scar over her heart had opened. Not torn—opened, deliberate as a door, a hairline of white-gold light running along the old scar like the first crack in a dam. And through the crack, faintly, audible to everyone on that bridge—to conscripts and clansmen alike, to the deaf and the hearing, to the river itself—came singing. Ten thousand voices, very far away, singing the first three notes of a song that had not been heard in the world for two hundred years.

Every person on the bridge had stopped to listen. Even Sera. Even the wounded. A boy of the Hunt knelt in the snow with tears freezing on his face, and when Yara looked at him he said, lost, "What *is* that? What is that? I know it. I know it from somewhere—"

Brannoc's hands hovered over the girl, healer's hands, afraid to touch. "The arrow's resonance went into the seal," he said, and his voice shook. "It didn't hurt her. It *woke* her. Yara—the fracture's spreading. I can't close this. No one can close this. It was made to open."

The light pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm. Merrin's eyes fluttered, unfocused, fixed on something above the winter stars.

"Towers," she whispered. "So many towers. And everyone is singing."

Yara knelt and gathered her up, and the moment their skin touched hers the fragmentary song poured directly into their bones, and Yara—who had sworn silence, who had buried the Choir's memory deeper than grief, who had spent ten years teaching themselves that some songs must stay dead—recognized it instantly, completely, the way you recognize your own face.

It was the founding song. The true one. The one the crown had stolen.

And it was still going, note after note after note, unwinding out of an eight-year-old girl on a frozen bridge, and there was nowhere left in the world to hide it.

Behind them, Sera Kestrel dismounted slowly, staring at the child in Yara's arms, and said quietly, "Well, Eriksen. Now you know what my price buys. Get her to the capital before the whole realm hears her—because by every god the mountains remember, *they will.*"

Far to the south, past the dark ridgelines, Greyfall glittered against the night like a crown of embers. And in Yara's arms, Merrin sang on.
