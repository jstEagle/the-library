## 25. What Leaks Through

The fever came in like weather.

Merrin felt it first as a warmth behind her eyes, then as a ringing that was not a sound at all but a pressure, the way the air feels before a storm breaks over the high passes. Yara had wrapped her in every blanket they owned and propped her against the wall of the shrine's inner chamber, where the old carved voices in the stone seemed to lean close, listening. Someone had built a fire. Someone had pressed a cup to her lips—bitter, resinous, tasting of pine and something older—and told her to drink, little bird, drink.

She drank. And then the seal broke open like an egg struck against a bowl's rim, and everything inside came pouring out.

* * *

She was standing on a green hill under a sky so blue it hurt, and she knew—with the strange double knowledge of dreams, knowing both that she was eight years old and shivering with fever in a mountain shrine, and that she was also someone else entirely, someone tall and strong-voiced—that this place had no name yet because names had not been needed.

The grass came up past her knees. Wildflowers moved in waves when the wind crossed them, gold and white and a deep impossible violet, and the air itself hummed. Not with insects. With intention. It was the hum of a thing about to be made, the held breath before the first note.

Others were gathering on the hill. She could see them coming from all directions, walking up the long slopes in ones and twos and families, carrying nothing but themselves. A woman with silver braids and soil on her hands. A boy not much older than herself leading a blind grandfather by the elbow. Twin sisters who walked in perfect step without seeming to know it. An old man whose voice had gone rough as gravel but whose eyes were wet already, though nothing had happened yet.

They came because they had heard. That was all. Word had passed from village to village, from hearth to hearth, the way sparks pass in dry grass: *There are others like you. There are others who hear the world singing back.*

Merrin turned her dream-hands over and saw they were the hands of a grown woman, scarred at the knuckles, a lute-string callus on the left thumb. She knew this body. She knew its weight and its aches and the way it stood slightly forward on the balls of the feet, always ready to move toward a sound.

Her name had been Sefa.

The name arrived whole, like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples of it went through her: *Sefa, daughter of the river country, singer of the second voice.* She was twenty-six years old and she had walked eleven days to be on this hill, and when she crested the rise and saw the others—all of them, dozens of them, hundreds by evening—she began to weep without any sadness in it at all.

Because for her whole life she had been alone with it. The hearing. The way a broken fence-post sang a thin note of complaint when the wind found it, and how she could touch it and hum to it and feel the wood settle, eased, mended. The way her mother's grief had hung in their house like smoke until Sefa had sung it low and slow into the floorboards and the house had breathed again. The way people in her village had begun, gently, kindly, to look at her sideways. The way her father had said, not unkindly, *Perhaps you should sing more quietly, little one.*

And here, on this nameless green hill, a woman with silver braids took her hand and said, "You too? You hear it too?" and Sefa said yes, and the woman laughed and cried and said, "Then we are not monsters. We are only early."

* * *

They called themselves the Choir because there was no other word big enough.

Not a choir of throats—a choir of presences. They discovered it in the first weeks, tentatively, the way children discover that if they shout into a canyon the canyon shouts back. One voice alone could mend a fence or ease a fever or coax a stubborn field into bearing. But two voices, joined, could do what neither could alone. Three could calm a flood-swollen river. Seven, standing in a ring around a dying orchard, could bring it back from the root.

And when they were many—when they stood together on the hill in the blue hour before dawn and let their voices find each other the way streams find the sea—the world itself leaned in to listen. Crops rippled though there was no wind. Sick animals lifted their heads. The light changed, went golden and thick as honey, and everyone who stood in that circle felt the same thing at the same moment: that they were not making the song. They were remembering it. That the song had been there all along, underneath everything, holding the world together the way marrow holds a bone, and they had been given ears to hear it and voices to answer it.

*We are not the source,* the silver-braided woman said—her name was Ondra, and she became their First Cantor, though she hated titles and would only accept it because they needed a word to call her by. *We are the echo. Never forget that. The song belongs to everyone. We have only been lent the hearing of it.*

Lent. Merrin felt the word go through her like a cold blade, even in the dream, even two hundred years downstream. Because she knew—she knew with the sick certainty of memory rather than foresight—what would happen to things that were merely lent.

* * *

The years of the Choir were the years the old songs still remembered, before anyone thought to burn them.

They built no fortress. They built a hall of timber and thatch on the green hill, and then, because pilgrims kept coming, a guesthouse, and then a school, and then the abbey—*the abbey*, Merrin thought, *I have slept in its bones*—with its great round chamber where the acoustics were so perfect that a whisper spoken at the center could be heard in every seat. They taught anyone who came with the hearing, and they turned away no one who lacked it, because Ondra said the gift was not a rank but a rain: it fell where it fell, and the wise build cisterns, not crowns.

Kings came to them. That was how it started. That was how it always started.

The first king—his name was Aldric, and history would remember him as Aldric the Founder, Aldric the Wise, Aldric whose face was stamped on every coin in the realm—came to the hill as a young warlord with mud still on his boots and three rival lords' blood on his sword. His lands were broken. His people were starving after the Long Dry. He had heard rumors of singers who could heal fields, and he came not kneeling but bargaining, which Merrin's memory noted and filed away: he never once knelt.

Ondra met him in the round chamber with forty voices behind her, and she did not refuse him. That was her great mercy and her great error, and Merrin understood now, feeling it from inside Sefa's chest, that Ondra had known the risk and chosen hope anyway, the way you choose to walk on ice because the far shore holds everything you love.

"We will teach your land to bear again," Ondra said. "We will sing the rivers clear and the blight from your wheat. We ask nothing."

"Nothing?" The young king laughed. "No one asks nothing."

"We ask that you remember," Ondra said, "that the song is not ours and cannot be yours. It belongs to the ground and the rain and every throat that draws breath. We will lend you our voices. You may not purchase them. There is no price, because there is no ownership. That is the whole of the law."

Aldric looked at her for a long time. Merrin, standing inside the memory, watched his face and saw what Ondra perhaps chose not to see: not gratitude. Calculation. The look of a man measuring a well and thinking about who controls the bucket.

He bowed—shallowly, the bow of an equal—and said, "Then I will remember," and every word of it was true, and none of it meant what she hoped.

* * *

For twenty years, the bargain held, and it was beautiful, and Merrin grieved even as she lived it because she knew the date that was coming the way you know a wound under a bandage.

The Choir sang the Long Dry to its knees. They walked the king's roads in companies of twelve and twenty, and where they passed, wells filled and cattle fattened and children who would have died in the winter instead lived to grow old and tell their grandchildren about the year the singers came. Aldric's broken kingdom knit itself into a realm. The rival lords bent their knees or were brought low, and the crown that Aldric forged was forged, everyone agreed, with the help of heaven—for what else could you call a harvest that came in singing?

And the people loved the Choir. That was the thing. That was the poison in it. Merrin walked through the market towns in Sefa's body and felt the love come off the crowds like heat off a forge—children running alongside them, old women pressing bread and flowers into their hands, men removing their hats as the singers passed. And she felt, beneath the love, the other thing, the quieter thing, in the way some of the men looked: fear. Wonder curdling toward envy. The ancient arithmetic that says: *if they can do this, what can they not do?*

Sefa felt it too. She said so, once, in the round chamber, standing among the forty. "The king sends more messengers each year. He asks us to sing his coronation hymn. He asks us to bless his armies' banners. He asks, and asks, and each asking is smaller and politer than the last, and each one is a hook."

"He is grateful," said one of the twins.

"He is a king," Sefa said. "Gratitude is a debt, and kings do not carry debts. They collect them."

Ondra raised her hand for quiet, and the chamber went silent the way it always did, the good silence, the listening silence. "We will sing," she said, "and we will stay ourselves, and we will trust the world we are mending to mend us in turn." She smiled her tired smile. "What else is there?"

Merrin, inside the memory, wanted to scream at her. *There is running. There is scattering. There is never letting one throne know where all of you sleep.* But the memory would not bend. It played as it had played, and she could only watch, and ache, and learn.

* * *

Aldric was old when he did it. Old, and afraid—not of death, though death sat with him now at every meal, but of the thing every king fears more than dying: ending. His son was weak. His grandsons were worse. And the realm he had built was held together, he understood at last, by nothing but the Choir's goodwill. Take away the singers and the harvests stayed—but the *reign*, the dynasty, the idea of the crown as something sacred and necessary, that rested wholly on the fact that the crown alone could call the singers, command the miracle, stand between the people and the power.

He did not want the gift destroyed. Destruction had never entered his mind. He wanted it *owned*. Singular. Inheritable. A treasure in the vault beneath the throne room, passed from king to king, so that no heir need ever be strong or wise or good—the crown would simply *sing*, and the singing would make obedience feel like worship.

Merrin learned all of this later, in fragments, in testimony, in the confessions of the men who did it—because the memory she carried held not only the founding but the fall, and the fall was the part the seal had guarded most jealously, the part that burned going down like swallowed coals.

It began with a theft so simple it was almost funny. Aldric's scholars—three of them, men who had studied at the abbey itself, who had eaten the Choir's bread—worked out that the great sustaining hymn, the one sung each dawn in the round chamber, the one that settled into the land like rain and held the realm's health in its harmonies, could be written down. Notated. Fixed. The living thing pinned like a moth to a board. They copied it onto vellum in a cipher of their own devising and carried it out of the abbey in a false-bottomed grain cart, and Aldric paid them each a farm and a title, and two of them lived long enough to regret it.

But a written hymn was only paper. Sung by ordinary throats, it was only music. The hymn drew its power from the voices that had grown it, generation upon generation, each singer adding a thread, the way a river is made by every tributary. To own the song, Aldric needed the singers—or he needed them gone, so that no one could ever say the crown's version was a copy, a theft, a hollow thing.

So he invited them to court.

All of them. A festival, he said. A century-mark celebration, twenty years since the Long Dry broke. Come to the capital, be honored, sing for the realm you saved. And Ondra, who had spent two decades telling herself that kings could be trusted with borrowed things, who had grown old and tired and hopeful, said yes.

Merrin stood in Sefa's body on the palace steps and felt the wrongness before she could name it. The city was beautiful—white walls, gold roofs, the new cathedral half-built above the harbor—but the air tasted of tallow and iron, and there were too few guards visible and too many doors closed, and the heralds who greeted them smiled with their mouths only.

"They know," Sefa whispered to Ondra, on the second night, in the rooms the crown had given them. "Something is wrong. Let us leave tonight. Let us scatter, as we should have done years ago—"

"And confirm every fear he has ever harbored?" Ondra's old face was gentle. "If we run, we teach him we were dangerous. If we stay, we teach him we are only singers." She took Sefa's hand, the same gesture from the green hill, a lifetime ago. "Stay close to me tomorrow, river-daughter. Whatever comes."

Whatever came came at dawn.

* * *

Merrin had thought, before the seal cracked, that she knew what murder was. She had seen the hounds take people in Greyhollow. She had heard the stories of the purge ten years ago; she had hidden in Yara's shadow while the Hunt burned the last holdouts from the fells. She had imagined the fall of the Choir as a battle.

It was not a battle. It was a slaughter arranged like a liturgy.

The forty were led into the great hall at first light to sing the morning hymn for the assembled court. The acoustics were magnificent. The hall had been designed, Merrin understood with a horror that transcended the dream, by the same three scholars—it had been *built* for this, its pillars placed to trap resonance, its gallery shaped to hold sound in and keep witnesses' cries from reaching the street. The forty took their places. Ondra stood at the center. The king watched from his balcony, old and straight-backed, and his face wore the expression of a man attending the reading of a will he has already written.

The hymn began. Forty voices rose. And in the fourth bar, at the exact point where the harmony opened widest—where every singer's gift was fully spent outward, given to the song, defenseless as a sleeping child—the soldiers came off the galleries.

Merrin screamed, and it was Sefa screaming, and it was Merrin screaming in a shrine in the Thornfells, and the two screams were the same scream across two hundred years.

She saw it all. The memory gave her all of it, merciless, in full sensory fidelity, because that was what the seal had been made to preserve: the truth, whole, so that someday someone could carry it out. She saw the silver-braided Ondra take a spear through the chest mid-note and *keep singing*, because the gift does not stop at the edge of pain, and her voice held the harmony alone for six impossible seconds while the others died around her, six seconds during which the crops of the entire realm leaned toward the capital, and then her voice broke on a note that was not a note but a name—Merrin never learned whose—and stopped.

She saw the twins die back to back, in step, as they had done everything. She saw the boy who had led his blind grandfather up the green hill put his body over an old man's and take four arrows and hum, humming, humming, a lullaby, until he didn't. She saw the hall fill with the smell of blood and beeswax candles, and she heard the most terrible sound of all, which was the silence afterward: the sustaining hymn cut off mid-phrase, the realm's held breath released all at once, and somewhere far away—she felt it through the floor, through the stone, through the roots of mountains—a river jumped its banks, and a field of wheat grayed overnight, and a child in a village by the sea woke crying for no reason she could name.

Forty-one singers had come to the capital. Forty died in the hall.

One escaped. A girl of nineteen, the youngest, who had been late to the assembly because she had been sick in the courtyard from nerves, and who heard the silence from outside the doors and understood, and ran. She ran for nine days. She carried nothing but the memory, sealed into her own heart by a dying teacher's desperate art—the Last Cantor's work, the sealing that would be passed down, voice to voice, keeper to keeper, until it reached a smuggler's road in the Thornfells and an eight-year-old child with no name she remembered.

The girl survived. The girl hid. The girl grew old in the fells teaching one student, then another, whispering the truth down the generations like a coal passed hand to hand in the dark. And the crown, meanwhile, took the stolen vellum and built a ritual around it: once a year, at the Hymnal Festival, the sustaining hymn sung by ten thousand conscripted throats who did not know what they were singing or where it came from, a corpse of a song dressed in robes and paraded through the centuries, growing thinner every year, a fire fed on its own ashes.

Two hundred years of borrowed breath.

And Aldric—Aldric the Founder, Aldric the Wise—stood on his balcony over the bodies of the forty and declared a new holiday, and minted new coins, and wrote into law that the gift of hearing was hereby a crime against the realm, because a kingdom that murders its singers must forever hunt their echo.

* * *

"—Merrin. *Merrin.*"

She surfaced like a drowning swimmer breaking into air.

Firelight. Rough wool against her cheek. Yara's face above hers, drawn and gray in the flickering light, one hand cradling the back of her head, the other gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise. Behind Yara, Brannoc hovered with a cup steaming in his shaking hands, and his eyes were wide and wet.

"You were singing," Yara said. Their voice was hoarse, scraped raw. "In your sleep. You were singing in *two parts*, Merrin, and the stones were answering, and the fire—" They stopped. Swallowed. "The fire burned blue for a while. Brannoc says—"

"I heard it." Brannoc's voice cracked. He knelt, set the cup down, and took her small hand in both of his huge ones. "Girl. What did you see?"

Everything. The green hill. Ondra's tired smile. The spear taking the First Cantor mid-note. The stolen vellum. The hall built like a trap. The one who ran.

Merrin tried to say it and found her throat ruined, her lips cracked, her whole body wrung out as if she had been pulled through a keyhole. So instead she did the only thing left to her: she lifted her free hand, trembling, and pressed it flat against Yara's chest, over the heart, the way the dying teacher had once sealed the memory into the runner's breast on a road outside a burning city.

And she hummed.

Three notes. The opening of the founding song—the true one, the whole one, the one that had been murdered in a beautiful hall so that a crown could wear its skin. Just three notes, barely voiced, a thread of sound.

Yara went rigid. Brannoc made a sound like a man taking a blow, and sat down heavily on the shrine floor, and covered his mouth with both hands.

Because they knew it. Both of them knew it. Everyone in the realm knew it—sung every year by ten thousand unknowing throats at the Hymnal Festival, printed on banners, carved over palace gates. The Crown Hymn. The song of Aldric the Founder, the song of continuity, the song the whole realm stood upon.

But from Merrin's broken-open heart it came out *whole*, and the wholeness was the horror: the festival version was missing its center. Two bars, torn out—the bars where the singers had given themselves away, the bars that named the lending and forbade the owning. The crown had been singing a hymn with its heart cut out for two hundred years, and no one alive had ever heard the missing part.

Until now. Until a fevered child in a mountain shrine hummed three notes, and the old stones of the Choir's own shrine rang them back like a bell struck after long silence, and the fire flared blue, and outside, far below the pass, in the dark valleys, dogs began to howl.

Yara caught Merrin's wrist gently and lowered her hand, and their eyes—those careful, buried, exhausted eyes—were shining with something between terror and revelation.

"That's why they want her," Yara said quietly, to Brannoc, to the fire, to no one. "Not the gift. The *proof*." They looked down at Merrin, and she watched them understand, watch the last door of their hiding life swing shut behind their eyes. "She isn't carrying a weapon. She's carrying the receipt."

Far to the south, beyond the snow-line, torches were kindling in the valley towns. Word traveled fast when dogs howled at nothing. And somewhere in that spreading dark, riders were already turning toward the mountains—toward the shrine, toward the child, toward the choice that waited now for Yara Eriksen like a blade laid flat across the road.
