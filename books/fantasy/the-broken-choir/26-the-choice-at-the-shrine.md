## 26. The Choice at the Shrine

The shrine had not changed. That was the cruelest thing about it.

Yara had expected ruin—expected the mountain to have done what mountains do, to shrug stone from stone, to let snow and root pull the old place apart the way it had pulled apart everything else the Choir had built. But the cave stood as it had always stood, its mouth a low arch of grey granite furred with frost-lichen, its interior dry and still and smelling faintly of cold iron and old smoke, as if the fires of ten years ago had never quite finished going out.

Merrin stopped at the threshold, her breath clouding in the thin air of the pass. She was thinner than she'd been at Coldspring—the fever had burned something out of her that food alone could not restore—and she stood with her shoulders drawn in, her hands pressed flat over the seal scar beneath her coat, the way she did now whenever they came near high places. Near singing places.

"It's loud here," she said quietly.

"I know."

"It's not sound-loud. It's—" She searched for the word, frowning, and Yara's chest tightened, because the gesture was so precisely the one Sera Vane used to make, all those years ago, when the old cantor reached for language the tongue had no room for. "It's like standing inside a bell after someone has struck it. Like the ringing never stopped. It just got too quiet for ears."

Yara looked at the child—at this small, fever-scarred vessel carrying ten thousand dead voices in a knot of sealed memory over her heart—and said nothing, because there was nothing true to say that would not frighten her.

They went in.

* * *

The shrine's heart was a chamber no larger than a modest kitchen, but the Choir had known how to make small spaces hold large silences. The walls were cut with channels—grooves worn smooth by centuries of hands, running in patterns that meant nothing to the eye and everything to a voice. When the singers had gathered here, those grooves had carried their harmonies through the stone like veins carry blood, until the whole mountain hummed. Now they were empty. Now they were just scars in rock, and Yara ran a thumb along one out of habit, feeling the cold, feeling nothing answer.

There should have been an altar. There wasn't. The Choir had never kept gods; they had kept each other. Instead there was a shallow basin of black basalt, wide as a shield, and around its rim, cut deep enough to survive any weather, the names.

Yara knelt beside it and began, as always, with the oldest ones.

It was a rite without theology. You touched each name. You spoke it—not sang, never sang, the oath forbade singing here more than anywhere—and you gave it back its weight. *Sera Vane, who taught me that silence is also a note.* *Ollin Brask, who could unmake a sword at forty paces and mended children's toys on rest days.* *The twins, Asha and Ivet, who died holding the pass at Redwater so that others could run.* Name by name, year by year, the basin filled with the dead the way a cup fills with water: silently, completely, to the brim.

Merrin watched from the wall, knees drawn up, chin on them. She had seen the rite once before, on the road from Lowmarsh, and had asked no questions then. Tonight she asked one.

"Were you there? At the end?"

Yara's hand paused over the name of a girl called Fen, who had been nineteen.

"No," they said. "I was in the capital. That was the point of me. The Choir kept one speaker away from the abbeys, always—one voice hidden, one seed left in the ground—in case the harvest ever came." A breath. "I was the seed. I hid while they burned. That is the whole of my courage, Merrin. I ran well."

"That's not the whole of anything," Merrin said, with the flat certainty of eight-year-olds, and Yara laughed despite themselves—a short, cracked sound that echoed strangely in the tuned stone—and went on with the names.

But the question stayed in the chamber after she asked it. Questions did that here. The walls held them the way they once held song.

* * *

Later, when Merrin slept wrapped in both their cloaks before the small fire, Yara sat at the basin and did the arithmetic they had been refusing to do since Coldspring.

The arrow had changed things. Everyone knew it, though no one said it aloud. A resonance-tipped shaft, meant not to kill but to *crack*—the Hunt's craftsmen had learned, somewhere in these ten years, how to do deliberately what the seal had been made to prevent. The graze along Merrin's ribs had opened a seam in the Last Cantor's work, and through that seam the founding memory leaked. Not in dreams anymore. In waking hours. In the way the girl hummed broken cartwheels whole. In the way she had looked at the innkeeper's wife in the last village and said, gently, terribly, *you lost a son to the levy, and you've never told anyone you're angry about it,* and the woman had dropped a crock of butter and stared at her as if God had spoken.

The seal was failing. And a failing seal did not merely leak—it *pulled*. Yara had felt it on the road, that gravitational drag, the sense that every gifted ear within fifty miles was slowly turning toward the child like flowers toward a sun only they could see. The Hunt didn't need hounds anymore. They needed only to stand still and wait for the mountain to deliver her.

So: the choice. It arranged itself in Yara's mind with the clean, hateful clarity of a blade laid on linen.

Stay. Take Merrin deeper into the Thornfell, past the smugglers' roads into country where even the Kestrels didn't go, and hide her until the seal either healed or killed her. Let the crown's hymn fail on its own schedule. Because it *would* fail—Brannoc had been certain of that under the knife, and Vael's own people had whispered it, and Yara had heard the hollowness with their own ears at the tollhouse, that faint fraying beneath the great sustaining chord the realm had sung for two hundred years. Two centuries of borrowed breath, and the lender long dead. Within a year, perhaps two, the hymn would falter, and the realm would come apart on its own—no truth required, no child carried into the lion's mouth. Roads would close. Granaries would empty. Lords would raise banners over the ruins and call it order. Tens of thousands would die in the unraveling, but they would die of the crown's own theft, and Yara's hands would stay clean, and Merrin would live, or might live, hidden in the high white country while the world below tore itself along old seams.

Or go. Carry her down out of the mountains, across the winter passes, into Greyfall itself, into the city where Vael hunted and Ives schemed and the Hymnal Festival would put ten thousand throats within arm's reach of the truth. Speak the memory aloud. Break the oath. Risk the madness that had eaten the old Choir alive—the same unbinding that had turned Cantor Vane's last song into a scream that killed three of her own students before the soldiers' torches ever touched her. Risk Merrin most of all, because a public unsealing was not a gentle thing, and no one—not Brannoc, not Sera Kestrel, not Yara—knew what would happen to a vessel that opened fully. She might become the greatest voice in a thousand years. She might become a wound in the world.

And if it worked—if the crowd remembered, if the stolen hymn collapsed under the weight of what everyone suddenly knew—then what? A realm with no legitimacy. A throne of ash. Chaos, yes—but chaos chosen, chaos *honest*, chaos that people walked into with open eyes instead of being dragged into blind.

Clean hands and a slow disaster. Or bloody hands and a chance.

Yara sat with it until the fire burned down to coals, and the coals to ash, and the ash to grey memory of fire, and understood at last that the choice had already been made—that it had been made in a market square in Greyhollow the day an old woman was dragged away for humming, and confirmed in a snowdrift the day Yara dug a nameless child out of the white and could not, in the end, walk away. Every step since had been the road to the city. The only thing left to decide was whether to admit it.

They looked at Merrin, asleep. In the ember-light the seal scar showed faintly at her collar, a pale whorl like a fingerprint pressed over her heart, and even in sleep her lips moved, shaping syllables of a language dead two hundred years.

"You deserve to be asked," Yara said softly. "Not commanded. Asked. Even if you're eight. Especially because you're eight, and everyone else will think that means your answer doesn't count." A pause. "But I know what you'll say. You'll say the truth belongs to everyone it was stolen from. You've been saying it in your sleep for a week."

The coals ticked. Somewhere far off, wind moved along the pass with a sound almost—almost—like a held note.

"Who made you promise?" Merrin had asked, weeks ago, by another fire. The question had taken root in Yara like a splinter working toward the heart. Who indeed. The oath-bound swordspeakers had sworn restraint to the Choir, and the Choir had sworn stewardship to the realm, and the realm had murdered its stewards and stolen their song, and for ten years Yara had honored an oath whose other party was a corpse. Restraint in service of a lie was not virtue. It was collaboration, dressed in humility.

Yara rose, crossed the chamber, and knelt at the basin one last time. Did not touch a name. Only laid both palms flat on the cold basalt rim, as if taking the whole dead Choir by the hand.

"I'm going to break it," they told them. "The oath. Maybe the gift with it. I don't know what happens after. But I'm tired of guarding a grave and calling it faithfulness. If any of you are anywhere—if any part of you is still in the stone, in the seams of the world—then come with us to Greyfall. Sing through me if you can. Forgive me if you can't."

The shrine said nothing. Shrines are good at that.

But as Yara lifted their hands, the frost-lichen on the arch above caught the last coal-light, and for half a heartbeat the grooves in the walls seemed to deepen, seemed to *listen*, and the air pressure shifted the way it does when a great door opens very far away.

Then it was only a cave again, and cold, and quiet.

* * *

Merrin woke before dawn, the way she always did now, surfacing from dream like a swimmer who has learned not to gasp. She found Yara packing—efficiently, in the dark, the way of people who have decided.

"We're going to the city," Merrin said. Not a question.

"If you'll come." Yara set down the pack and crouched to her level, which took some doing, because their knees had opinions about crouching that they had never had at thirty. "Listen to me, Merrin. Truly listen, the way you listen to everything else. What's happening to you isn't going to stop. The crack in your seal will widen whether we hide in a hole in the mountain or walk into the biggest crowd in the realm. So we can wait here for it to happen to you—alone, in the dark, with no one but me to catch what comes out—or we can go down there, where the memory came from, where the people it was stolen from live, and we can choose the moment. Choose it together. Give it to everyone at once, so no one person has to carry it." They swallowed. "Including you. *Especially* you. You've carried enough of other people's history for one childhood."

Merrin considered this with a gravity that belonged to no eight-year-old and to every prophet ever ignored.

"And if it breaks me?" she asked. "If opening it all the way is like the cantor lady? The one who screamed?"

Yara flinched. Of course she knew. The leaks ran both ways; the founding memory had shown her the purge, had shown her Sera Vane's last hour. "Then I'll be holding the other end of the song," Yara said. "Whatever it does to you, it does to both of us. That's the promise. Not that you'll be safe—I can't promise that, and I won't insult you by pretending. The promise is that you won't be *alone* in it. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that's ever mattered."

Merrin looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, businesslike, and began rolling up her blanket with the fierce competence of a child who had learned that competence was survival.

"The Ferrywoman said you lied to her," she remarked, stuffing the blanket into the pack. "About the memory. Is that why she's angry?"

"She's angry because I treated her like a toll instead of a door." Yara shouldered the pack. "We may need doors, going south. All of them. Even angry ones."

"There's a road," Merrin said, and then stopped, and pressed her hand to her scar, and her eyes went distant and slightly sideways, the look she got when the leak ran strong. "Down the east face. Under the ice bridge. The pilgrims' stair—they walled it up after the fall, but the wall is only stone, and stone is just a word waiting to be unsaid." She blinked, returned, and looked faintly embarrassed. "Sorry. It does that."

"Don't apologize." Yara stared at her, and felt the old dread and the new hope arrive together, twinned, inseparable, like the two edges of one blade. Ten years Yara had guarded these roads, and here was a child reading them out of a dead woman's memory like a book left open on a table. "That stair hasn't had feet on it since before I was born."

"I know." Merrin pulled her hood up. "She says hurry."

"*She* says—"

But Merrin was already walking toward the cave mouth, toward the grey dawn and the east face and the long white drop of the pass, small and certain as a compass needle, and Yara stood a moment longer in the shrine of the dead with their heart hammering a rhythm that felt disgracefully like joy.

Then they followed her out, down the mountain, onto the road to Greyfall.

Behind them, in the tuned and empty dark, the grooves in the stone held the shape of two sets of footsteps the way a bell holds the shape of its last ring—and if the mountain hummed, very faintly, as the door of morning closed behind them, no one was there to say whether it was only wind.
