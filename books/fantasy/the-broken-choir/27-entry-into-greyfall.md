## 27. Entry into Greyfall

The road to Greyfall was paved with the bones of old songs.

Yara had told Merrin that once, on a night when the girl couldn't sleep and the fire was low, and Merrin had frowned and said that roads weren't made of songs, they were made of stones. Yara had almost explained. Had opened their mouth to say: *every stone on this road was sung into place, child, before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath; every mile of it laid by voices that could persuade granite to lie down like a tired ox.* But explaining meant admitting what they were, and admitting what they were meant the next question, and the next, so Yara had only said, "Old roads remember being made," and let it go at that.

Now they walked that road with three hundred pilgrims, and Yara felt every stone humming underfoot like a struck bell that no one had bothered to silence properly.

The disguise was good work — Sera Kestrel's people did not do anything badly. Yara wore the undyed wool of a penitent, hood back to show a face weathered by mountain wind, hands wrapped in the grey cord of the Pilgrimage of Breath. Merrin walked beside them with her hair cropped short and her cheeks smudged with ash, a child of mourning walking to the capital to pray for a dead parent, which was true enough in spirit if false in letter. Around them flowed the festival crowd: farmers who had sold a winter's worth of grain for the journey, merchants with their hats off, old men leaning on their sons' arms, all of them singing.

That was the thing about the Hymnal Festival. Everyone sang. It wasn't optional in any way that mattered. The sustaining hymn rose from ten thousand throats along the processional roads at dawn on the first day of Deepwinter, and every soul in the realm was expected to add their breath to it, because that was how it had always been done, because the song held up the crown, because the crown held up the world. No one asked what the song *held up* anymore. No one remembered there had ever been another song beneath it.

Yara kept their mouth closed and hummed nothing, and prayed to whatever listened that no one would notice one silent throat among thousands.

Merrin noticed immediately. She glanced sideways, ash-smudged and sharp-eyed, and mouthed: *You're not singing.*

*I never sing,* Yara mouthed back.

*You sing when you think I'm asleep.*

*Then you're dreaming.*

But Merrin's hand found theirs anyway, small and cold inside the sleeve, and did not let go, and Yara did not make her.

* * *

They came over the last ridge an hour past dawn, and Greyfall opened below them like a wound dressed in silk.

Yara had seen the capital twice before the purge — once as a child, carried on a Choir elder's shoulders through streets hung with banners, and once at fourteen, standing in the great amphitheater while the Cantor's voice filled the bowl of stone until Yara's ribs ached with it. Both times the city had seemed to *shine*, and Yara had understood even then that the shining was not metaphor. The hymn ran through Greyfall like light through glass. Every wall the singers had raised, every arch the singers had bent, every fountain the singers had taught to fall upward on feast days — all of it drank from the same deep well, and the well was full, and the world was right.

The city still shone. That was the horror of it. From the ridge, in the low winter sun, Greyfall was beautiful — the white walls, the seven towers of the palace rising out of the heart of it like fingers of a buried hand, the river Coldspring running silver through its legs. A city built by gods, people said. Built by singers, Yara knew, and the difference mattered more than blood.

But now, with ears trained by ten years of silence and one season of listening to Merrin's leaking seal, Yara could hear what the beauty was standing on.

It was the pauses.

A healthy song has no pauses. A sustaining hymn is a woven thing, each voice catching the next before the note can fall, and when the Choir had sung it — Yara had been too young to sing it, but old enough to stand in the loft and feel it move through the floorboards into their bare feet — there had been no gaps at all, no seams, just an unbroken fabric of sound that the whole city wore like skin.

What rose from Greyfall now had seams everywhere. Ten thousand voices, and between them, hairline cracks of silence, little stutters where a phrase should have resolved and didn't, where the harmony reached for a note and landed a finger's width off. To the crowd it would sound like nothing. To the crowd it probably sounded like grandeur, like the weight of two centuries of tradition pressing down on the music and making it solemn. But Yara stood on the ridge with Merrin's cold hand in theirs and heard a foundation settling. Heard stone shifting on stone. Heard, unmistakably, a building deciding whether it was going to stand.

"You hear it," Merrin said. Not a question. Her voice had gone strange — flat, careful, the voice she used when the seal leaked and she was trying not to drown in what came through. "Underneath. There's something underneath the song."

"What does it sound like to you?"

She was quiet a long moment. Below them the processional road bent toward the River Gate, and the singing swelled as more pilgrims joined it, and the cracks widened to swallow the new voices whole.

"Like someone singing a song they've only heard," she said finally, "from the next room. Through a wall. They know the shape of it. They don't know why it goes where it goes." She looked up at Yara, and her eyes were too old for her face, the way they got when the founding memory pressed against the scar over her heart. "It's our song. Isn't it. The one I dream about. They took it and they're singing it wrong."

Yara had known this since the abbey, since the scorched pews and the fragments that had come pouring out of Merrin like water through a split dam. Knowing it in a ruin, alone, was one thing. Hearing a child say it plainly, with the stolen hymn rolling up the hillside around them, was another. Yara's throat worked.

"Walk," they said. "Don't stop walking. And Merrin — whatever happens in there, whatever you hear, however wrong it sounds — you keep your mouth shut and your eyes down. You're a grieving child today. Nothing else."

"And you?"

"I'm nobody," Yara said. "I've had practice."

* * *

The River Gate swallowed them with the rest of the tide.

Guards lined the gate passage, but they were not looking for faces. They were looking for silence. Two of them stood with their palms pressed flat against the gatehouse wall, eyes half-closed, and Yara understood with a lurch what they were doing: feeling the resonance, tasting the crowd's song for the one voice that didn't join it. The Hunt's methods, grown fat and comfortable enough to stand in broad daylight at a festival gate. Sera's intelligence had warned of registration points; it had not mentioned this.

So Yara did the hardest thing they had done in ten years.

They sang.

Not the gift — never that, not here, not with the walls themselves drinking sound — but the hymn, the public verses, the ones every child learned with their letters. Yara's voice came out rusty and low, pitched into the middle of the crowd where one more voice was a drop in a river, and they shaped the stolen phrases as faithfully as they could bear to, and hated every syllable. Because Yara knew this melody. Knew it the way you know a face you've seen in nightmares. It was the founding song, the Choir's own root-melody, the one the elders had sung at investiture with tears running down their faces — and it had been cut apart and reassembled, verses reordered, harmonies flattened, the living spine of it pulled out and replaced with something rigid and repeatable. Hearing it sung by ten thousand innocent mouths was like watching a beloved teacher's corpse propped up and made to dance.

Beside them, Merrin's lips had gone white. She was singing too, small-voiced, obedient — and then, on the third verse, her voice slipped. Slid sideways off the official line and found the *true* resolution, the one the original song demanded, the one two centuries of editing had sanded away. For half a bar her clear child's voice rang out with the correct note, the lost note, and the guards at the wall both turned their heads at once like hounds scenting meat.

Yara's hand closed on Merrin's shoulder and squeezed, hard, and Merrin caught herself, dragged her voice back to the false line, and dropped her eyes. The guards stared a moment longer. Then a drunk pilgrim behind them bellowed the verse an octave too high and knocked his neighbor into a fruit stall, and the moment drowned in laughter and cursing and rolling apples, and the guards turned away, and Yara breathed again.

*Never again do that,* they breathed into Merrin's ear. *Not even the right note. Especially not the right note.*

"It hurt," Merrin whispered back. "Singing it wrong. It felt like — like biting tinfoil."

"I know." Yara looked ahead, up the processional avenue toward the palace towers, and felt the full weight of what they had come to do settle onto their shoulders like wet wool. "We'll fix it. All of it. Just — not yet. Not today. Today we're nobodies. Say it."

"We're nobodies," Merrin said, and for the first time since the bridge, since the arrow and the fever and the fragments, she smiled — a thin, fierce, knowing smile far too old for eight years old. "Nobody important. Just pilgrims."

"Just pilgrims," Yara agreed, and did not believe either of them for a moment.

* * *

Greyfall inside the walls was a city holding its breath without knowing it.

The festival had eaten everything. Banners of white and gold hung from every balcony. Vendors sold honey-bread and tin whistles and little clay crowns that children wore until they crumbled. Priests of the Hymnal Order moved through the crowds in grey-and-gold vestments, blessing doorways, and the crowds blessed them back, and everyone was warm and loud and generous, because that was what the festival was for — gratitude, the priests called it. Gratitude for two hundred years of the crown's protection. Gratitude for the song.

And under it all, the fraying.

Yara heard it everywhere once they started listening. The fountain in the Processional Square was supposed to run upward on feast days — Yara remembered it, four years old and shrieking with delight as silver arcs climbed past their face. Today it merely gurgled, ordinary, and a priest was loudly explaining to a disappointed cluster of children that the miracle was *resting*. A merchant's awning sagged where its frame should have held true for decades; the man kept glancing up at it with vague unease, unable to name what was wrong. At the edge of the square, masons were repointing a wall that had needed no mortar in living memory, and the foreman was arguing with his journeyman about whether the stone was *shrinking*, which was absurd, which was impossible, which was exactly what happened when the song that persuaded stone to hold its shape began to lose its grip.

Small things. Deniable things. A city full of people explaining away the creaks in a floor they'd forgotten was ever built.

"They can't hear it," Merrin murmured. They had stopped at a bread stall, buying nothing, watching. "All these people. They've never heard it *right*, so they don't know this is wrong. It's like—" she groped for it, "—like everyone in the world wearing shoes two sizes too big, and no one remembers feet come in other sizes."

"That's the cruelty of it," Yara said quietly. "The theft worked because it was complete. Nobody alive remembers the original except you and me and a handful of ghosts in a cell under that palace." They nodded, almost imperceptibly, toward the seven towers. "Which means nobody will believe us. Unless—"

"Unless they remember," Merrin finished. She said it simply, without fear, and that frightened Yara more than any terror could have. Somewhere in the leaking dark of her sealed memory, the girl had already made peace with what was coming. She knew what opening the seal meant. She had known since the shrine, maybe since the snow.

"Merrin—"

"I'm hungry," she announced, with the flawless timing of an eight-year-old, "and you owe me honey-bread, because you said in Lowmarsh that capitals have the best honey-bread, and this one smells like candles and horse."

Yara bought the honey-bread. It cost three coppers and tasted like ash, though Yara suspected that was their own mouth and not the baker's fault. They ate standing in the shadow of a bell tower, Merrin licking honey from her thumb, and above them the tower's great bell sat silent in its frame, waiting for noon, when the Festival Bell would ring and the whole city would kneel and the Chancellor would appear on the palace balcony and the sustaining hymn would be sung in its full ceremonial form for the first time this year.

Two days from now, Yara thought. Two days until the climax of the festival, until the final singing, until the moment when every eye in Greyfall would be turned upward toward that balcony. Whatever they were going to do — and the plan was still less a plan than a direction, smuggler tunnels and Vael's treacherous keys and Brannoc somewhere below the earth — it would break over the city at that moment or not at all.

A bell-ringer climbed the tower above them, and Yara watched him cross himself at the bell, muttering something. Asking its pardon, maybe. Old habit from older times, when bells were tuned by voice and answered to their makers.

Noon came. The Festival Bell rang.

The sound went out over the city, and Yara flinched, because the bell was *cracked*. Not visibly — the bronze gleamed, the striker fell true — but the tone came out fouled at the center, a worm of dissonance coiled inside the sweetness, and Yara saw that they were not the only one who heard it. Faces in the crowd tightened. A dog howled. An old woman near the stall pressed her hands over her ears and whispered, "It didn't use to sound like that," to no one, and her daughter shushed her, embarrassed, and pulled her away.

Merrin had gone very still, honey-bread forgotten, her hand pressed flat over the scar beneath her shirt.

"It's failing," she said softly. "The whole song. Everything they built on it. It's all coming apart at the same speed, all together, like a rope unraveling." She looked up at Yara, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "There isn't much time. A year, maybe less. When it goes, it won't go slow. It'll go all at once, like ice."

Below the palace, somewhere under those seven towers, in cells Yara had only heard described in whispers, Brannoc was sitting in the dark with the truth in his chest and Vael's knives waiting. Above the palace, a Chancellor was preparing to bind a child to a dying song rather than let the lie collapse. And between those two weights stood Yara Eriksen, last of the swordspeakers, disguised in penitent's grey, holding a sticky child's hand in a crowd of ten thousand people singing a stolen hymn — and for one vertiginous moment the urge rose up like floodwater, the urge to throw back the hood, to climb the bell tower, to open the unbound voice and pour the whole truth into the city right now, consequences be damned, oath be damned, let the ice crack where it stood.

Yara breathed. Let the flood recede. Ten years of practice, spent in a single long exhale.

"Not yet," they murmured — to Merrin, to themselves, to the cracked bell overhead. "Tomorrow night. The drains, the keys, the cells. We get Brannoc first. Then the whole city hears it at once, from every voice at once, and no one can unhear it." They took Merrin's hand again. "Come on, pilgrim. We need to find a doorway, and Sera's people left us a map drawn in candle smoke, and I want walls around us before dark."

They turned down a side street, two anonymous figures in a river of festival grey and gold, and behind them the bell's fouled note faded slowly over the rooftops like a bruise spreading through silk.

Neither of them saw the man in the plain clerk's coat who had been standing at the corner of the square for an hour, neither singing nor eating nor praying, whose eyes had followed the silent-throated penitent and the ash-cheeked child since the River Gate — and who now turned and walked, unhurried, toward the palace, carrying a description that was already arranging itself into a report:

*One adult, one child, arrived with the dawn pilgrims. Neither registered. The child sang one true note.*

*Requesting instruction.*
