## 28. Prison of Glass

The cells beneath the palace were not made of stone.

Brannoc had understood that within the first hour, though his body took three days to accept it. The walls were glass—no, not glass, something older and stranger, a substance that drank light and gave back nothing, smooth as river ice and warm as skin. When he pressed his palm against it, he felt a faint tremor, like the pulse of a sleeping animal, and he knew then what he was lying inside of.

A throat. They had built their prison inside a throat, or shaped it like one, and every wall was tuned to swallow song.

He had stopped trying to sing on the second day. It wasn't that the cells prevented it—they did something worse. Whatever he gave voice to came back to him hollowed out, stripped of everything but the shape of itself, a husk of melody with nothing living in it. He had tried the old healing cadence, the one he'd used on fevered children in the Choir's last summer, and heard it return to him flat as a coin dropped on slate. After that he kept his mouth shut and counted his breaths and tried very hard not to weep, because weeping, too, was a kind of music, and he could feel the walls drinking that as well.

They fed him twice a day through a slot in the door. Bread, broth, water in a wooden cup—wood, he noticed, never metal, never anything that might ring. Someone below had thought about this for a long time. Someone below had been collecting voices for years, maybe decades, and had learned all the small ways a prisoner might make one.

*You are alive,* he told himself, in the dark, in the rhythm of his own heartbeat because he dared not trust even his inner voice to stay unharvested. *Alive is a position. Alive can be moved from.*

It was what he told himself, and it was mostly a lie, and he held onto it anyway the way a drowning man holds a rope he knows is fraying.

* * *

On the fourth day—or what he believed was the fourth day; time down here was a rumor—the door opened, and Inquisitor-General Vael walked in carrying a lantern instead of a torch.

Brannoc had met the man only once before, across an interrogation table, and had spent that meeting doing the hardest work of his life: feeding a false memory into the extraction device while keeping his face slack and his fear genuine enough to be convincing. He remembered Vael's hands, long and careful, adjusting the dials of the machine the way a musician tunes an instrument. He remembered thinking: *this man loves the gift more than he loves the crown, and someday that will matter.*

"Master Brannoc." Vael set the lantern on the floor between them and sat, folding himself down with the unhurried ease of a man visiting a friend. Up close, without the grey uniform and the silver chain of office, he looked tired. "I owe you an apology."

Brannoc said nothing. His voice was the last thing he owned down here, and he wasn't spending it on pleasantries.

"You gave me a memory," Vael continued, as if Brannoc had spoken. "The fall of the abbey. I have turned it over perhaps two hundred times since, and I find—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—I find it does not decay the way true memories decay. True memories blur at the edges. Yours is sharp as an etching. Beautiful, even. Too beautiful."

*Careful.* Brannoc kept his eyes on the lantern flame. *He is fishing. Do not rise to the hook.*

"I am not here about your forgery," Vael said, reading him with irritating ease. "I am here because you are the only man in this building who might answer a question honestly, and I have begun to suspect there are very few honest men left above ground." He leaned forward. "How long has the sustaining hymn been failing?"

The question landed in Brannoc's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He had wondered, in the smugglers' low house in Lowmarsh, listening to Merrin's sealed heart hum in her sleep. He had wondered, watching the crown's processions pass through Greyhollow, the choirboys' voices thin as winter ice. But hearing it asked aloud—in this place, by this man—made it real in a way his own suspicions never had.

"I wouldn't know," Brannoc rasped. His voice came out ruined, days of disuse. "I'm a drunk and a liar. Ask your machines."

"I have. They tell me the hymn's harmonic foundation was laid two hundred and eleven years ago and has been patched four hundred times since." Vael's face was unreadable in the lantern light. "They also tell me the patches are failing faster each year. Within twelve months, perhaps eighteen, the song will not hold." He stood, brushing off his coat. "When that happens, Chancellor Ives intends to bind a living speaker to the throne as a replacement engine. A child, Master Brannoc. You know which child."

The door closed behind him. Brannoc sat in the dark for a long time afterward, and the walls drank his rage the way they drank everything else, and he hated them for it, and hated more that the rage was the only warm thing left in him.

But under the rage, something else had kindled. Vael had come alone. Vael had come *asking*. And a man who asks questions he already suspects the answers to is a man whose loyalty has begun to crack—and cracked things, Brannoc knew, could be broken open.

*Hold on, girl,* he thought, toward wherever Merrin was, whether she was still with Yara or already in these depths. *The walls are thinner than they look.*

* * *

It was the humming that changed everything.

On the sixth night—he had decided it was night by the schedule of the food slot, which opened at intervals his body insisted on calling morning and evening—he heard it through the wall. Faint. Almost nothing. A child's counting rhyme, the kind sung while skipping rope in any village yard, except that the intervals were wrong. Not wrong badly. Wrong *deliberately*, folded into the old quarter-tones that predated the crown's standardized scale, the microtonal slides that the Harmonic Edicts had outlawed a century before Brannoc was born.

Someone in the next cell was singing in Old Tongue intervals and disguising it as a nursery rhyme.

Brannoc lay still for a long moment, his heart hammering. Then, carefully, silently, he pressed his palm flat against the warm glass wall and listened with the part of him that had never stopped being a healer. Resonance traveled through the strange material better than sound through air—he could feel the song's structure in his bones, its architecture, its intent. And underneath the skipping-rhyme surface, he felt the message the singer was hiding:

*I hear you breathing. Are you one of us?*

His eyes stung. Six days of silence, six days of believing himself the last loose thread in a tapestry the crown had finished weaving years ago—and now this. He answered the only way he could without giving the listeners above anything to harvest. He tapped the old Choir signal against the glass: three slow, two fast, one slow. *Present. Hidden. Waiting.*

The humming stopped. Then, after a long pause, the answer came back through the wall in taps, hesitant, as if the hand making them hadn't used the code in years:

*Present. Hidden. How many fingers do you count?*

*One,* Brannoc tapped. *Just me.*

*No.* A pause. *There are nine of us down here. Nine who survived the tuning.*

Nine. Brannoc closed his eyes and let the number settle into him like a coal into ash. Nine speakers, alive, beneath the palace—not dead in the purges, not scattered to the winds, but *kept*, harvested cell by cell, voice by voice, their songs siphoned off to patch a dying hymn. He thought of the extraction device, of Vael's careful hands, of the bread and the wooden cup and the walls that drank music, and he understood at last what this place was.

Not a prison. A larder.

* * *

Over the following days, the wall became a country unto itself.

The woman in the next cell—she would give only her first name, Sefa, and only after three nights of exchanged signals—had been taken eleven years ago from a fishing village on the coast, arrested for singing her drowned brother's name in a mourning cadence. Before her, in the cell beyond, was a man called Odo who had been a choir-master in a provincial cathedral until he noticed the sustaining hymn's bass line didn't match any manuscript older than the founding of the Ives dynasty, and had made the mistake of saying so aloud. Beyond Odo, a pair of sisters taken together. Beyond them, others whose names arrived tap by tap like coins passed hand to hand in the dark: Maret. Ilse. A boy of perhaps sixteen called Tam, whose tapping was quick and frightened and who had been down here so long he'd forgotten the color of sky.

None of them knew how many cells the block held. None of them had ever seen another prisoner's face. The crown's method was isolation absolute—except that the crown had built its larder out of resonant glass, and resonant glass carried what it was made to carry, and somewhere in the palace's founding, some architect's arrogance had outlived his caution.

"They think the walls keep us apart," Sefa tapped one night, and Brannoc could feel, through the vibration, something that was almost laughter. "They built us a choir loft and called it a dungeon."

It was Tam, on the ninth night, who asked the question that undid him.

Through the long chain of relays—tap to tap to tap, cell to cell, the code traveling down the row like a runner passing through villages—came the boy's small, urgent signal: *Is it true the swordspeakers are all dead? The ones who could unmake things? My mother said one survived the abbey fire.*

Brannoc's hand hovered over the glass.

Ten years ago, in the smoke and screaming of the Hollow Abbey, he had watched a girl of nineteen drag a wounded novice through a postern gate while the Inquisition's fires ate the roof beams, and he had believed—with the whole of his heart, because believing it was easier than knowing—that both of them had died on the mountain. He had drunk to their ghosts for a decade. He had built his cowardice on top of their graves, brick by brick, telling himself there was no one left to fight for.

And then, five months ago, a swordspeaker with a scarred jaw and ten years of silence in her eyes had walked into Lowmarsh carrying a child with a seal scar, and Brannoc had looked at Yara Eriksen and felt his grief crack open like river ice in spring.

*One survived,* he tapped back. *She lives.*

The silence that followed lasted so long he thought the relay had broken. Then Tam's answer came, and behind its careful rhythm Brannoc felt a trembling he recognized from sickbeds and deathwatches, the shaking of someone holding something too large for their hands:

*Ask her if she remembers Wren. Wren of the west stairs. She pulled me out too. We got separated at the tree line. They told me she burned.*

Brannoc went cold from the scalp down.

Wren. He knew the name. He knew it the way you know a word from a language you spoke as a child—Yara had never spoken it aloud in his hearing, not once in all their strained, transactional years of acquaintance, but he had seen it in the way she flinched from certain songs, certain memories. On the third night of their uneasy alliance, half-drunk on Brannoc's bad whiskey, she had said only this: *I had an oath-sister. The fire took her. I checked the bodies. There weren't enough bodies to check.*

Everyone had believed Wren dead. Yara had mourned her for ten years, had built her whole silent exile on top of that grave alongside Brannoc's—and the grave was empty. The girl on the other side of four cells of resonant glass, tapping out a dead woman's name with a living hand—

No. Not a girl anymore. A young man now, or nearly. Ten years changes a body. But the name was the name, and the west stairs were the west stairs, and Brannoc sat in the dark with his palm against the glass and wept for the first time since his arrest, silently, because the walls were always drinking, and let them drink this too.

*She lives,* he tapped again, when he could trust his hands. *Her name is Yara now. She is coming. I don't know how or when, but she is coming, and when she comes, she will burn this place down to its foundations or die trying.*

*That,* came the reply, after a long moment, and Brannoc could have sworn he felt the ghost of a laugh in the vibration, *sounds like Wren's people.*

* * *

He began, that same night, to work.

Not singing—the walls saw to that—but mapping. Every speaker in the row had spent years learning their cell the way a blind man learns a room: by touch, by echo, by the small betrayals of air current and temperature. Through the relay chain, Brannoc collected what they knew. The food slots opened from a corridor on the north side; the corridor sloped downward toward the east, where the air tasted of river water and old stone, which meant old stone meant foundations meant possibly, *possibly*, a way through that predated the glass. The extraction chamber lay somewhere above and central; prisoners went up and came back diminished, and Tam had stopped going up eight months ago when the guards decided his voice was worth more intact. And once a day, deep beneath everything, the floor hummed—a vast, slow pulse, like a heart beating under the mountain.

"That's the hymn," Sefa explained, when Brannoc asked. "The real one. They pipe it up from somewhere below to feed the throne room's echo. Some days it stutters. Have you felt it stutter?"

He had. Twice, now. A hitch in the great pulse, a skipped beat, followed by a shudder through all nine cells like the flinch of a dying animal. Each time, the guards above had grown louder, more hurried. Each time, the food had come late.

*The song is failing,* Brannoc tapped down the row, on the twelfth night, laying the truth among them like a card turned face-up. *Vael told me himself. Within a year, perhaps less. And Ives means to bind a child to the throne to replace it. A living engine. You all know what binding means.*

He felt the horror travel the chain, cell to cell, a cold current through warm glass. Binding was the oldest obscenity of their art—to take a voice that could choose and make it a mechanism that could not, to nail a soul to a function. The old kings had done it to criminals. The Choir had died rather than permit it.

*So here is what we do while we wait,* Brannoc tapped, and his hands were steady now, steadier than they had been in twenty years, steadier than they had ever been over a sickbed or a bargaining table or a bottle. *We learn our walls. We learn our guards' feet. We count the stutters of the hymn and mark which days they worsen. And when the doors open—when the festival brings the whole court down here, or when the song fails in public and panic opens every lock in this palace—we will not be nine separate prisoners waiting to be harvested.*

*We will be a choir.*

The answer came back along the chain, tap by tap, voice by voice, nine hearts answering in the dark: *Present. Ready. Waiting.*

And far above them, muffled by fathoms of stone and glass and the weight of a lying kingdom, Brannoc felt the great pulse of the stolen hymn shudder again—longer this time, harder, a stumble that no patch would hide—and in the silence that followed, faint as a moth against a window, he heard the first thin sounds of panic beginning in the corridors overhead.

The festival had begun. And the song was starting to break.
