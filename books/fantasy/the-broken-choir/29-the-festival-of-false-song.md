## 29. The Festival of False Song

The bells of Greyfall began at dawn.

They started in the Cathedral of the Ascending Voice and spread outward through the city like ripples from a stone dropped in still water—first the great bronze throats of the temple district, then the smaller bells of the merchant quarters, then the cracked little handbells that children ran through the slums ringing for coppers. By the time the sun cleared the eastern wall, the whole capital was ringing, and beneath the ringing, faint as a heartbeat under silk, the sustaining hymn rose to meet the day.

Two hundred years it had been sung without pause. Two hundred years of borrowed breath. Every citizen of Greyfall had been born inside its sound the way a fish is born inside water, and not one in ten thousand had ever thought to wonder what held them up.

Today was the Hymnal Festival, when the song was sung loudest, proudest, publicly—the one day the crown let the people hear the thing that carried them, though the people did not know it was carrying anything at all. They thought it was only music. They thought it was only tradition.

Yara Eriksen stood in the pilgrim crush on the Processional Way and listened to the foundation of the realm audibly fray.

* * *

Vael had dressed carefully.

Not in the grey of the Hunt—he had set that aside three days ago, folded it into a chest in his chambers with a deliberateness that had frightened his own valet—but in court black trimmed with silver, the dress uniform of an Inquisitor-General attending the festival in an official capacity. The silver was old-fashioned, almost archaic: interlocking circles worked along the collar, a pattern no one at court recognized anymore.

He knew what it was. He had found it in the palace archives, on a portrait of the first Cantor, painted two hundred years ago by an artist whose name had been struck from every record except the canvas itself. Interlocking circles. Voices joined so that no single voice could be silenced without silencing all.

The crown wore a different pattern now. A single circle, closed upon itself. One voice, owned.

From the reviewing stand beside the Chancellor's dais, Vael watched Ives work the crowd the way a fisherman works a line he already knows is hooked. The Chancellor stood robed in white and gold, hands lifted, face turned up toward the cathedral spires with an expression of such practiced ecstasy that half the square below seemed to catch it like a fever. *Behold your king's devotion,* the face said. *Behold how the realm is loved.*

And the hymn swelled, ten thousand festival singers in their white robes arranged in tiers up the cathedral steps, and the sound rolled over Greyfall like weather.

Vael heard what the crowd could not.

He had spent four months now in the archive vaults with candle stubs and stolen keys, reading what the Chancellery had spent two centuries burying: the original notation of the founding song, recovered fragments, a Cantor's marginalia in a hand that shook with rage. He had learned the hymn the way a physician learns a body—every part, every function, every place where disease might hide. And this morning, standing in the cold bright air, he could hear the disease plainly. Notes arriving a hair late. Harmonies that leaned where they should have stood. A sustained chord in the upper registers that wavered like a candle flame in a draft.

It was failing. Not in a year, perhaps. Perhaps sooner. Perhaps—

The chord broke.

It was small. It lasted less than a breath. Somewhere in the third tier of singers, a woman's voice simply stopped—not faltered, not cracked, but *stopped*, the way a string stops when it snaps, and for one full second the hymn had a hole in it, a silence shaped exactly like a missing note, and every soul in the square felt it pass through them like a cold wind through a shut house.

Then the other voices poured into the gap and the hymn went on, seamless, magnificent, as if nothing had happened.

But everyone had heard it. Vael watched it move through the crowd like a shiver through a horse's hide—a ripple of heads turning, of glances exchanged, of mothers' hands tightening on children's shoulders. An old man near the stand crossed himself with the old sign, the double-crossing that the Church had forbidden sixty years ago, and did not seem to know he had done it.

On the dais, Chancellor Ives smiled wider.

"Did you hear," said the woman beside Vael, a countess furred against the cold, her voice bright with the particular delight of the fashionable alarmed, "the third tier? Right in the middle of the Sanctus movement. Someone fainted, I expect."

"Fainted," Vael agreed.

"The Crown will have the poor creature's name by nightfall, I don't doubt." She laughed. "What a scandal, on the festival day of all days."

"Yes," Vael said. "What a scandal."

He looked at Ives again—at the white and gold, at the lifted hands, at the smile that had not moved by a single muscle when the hymn tore—and understood, with the calm of a man stepping off a ledge he has been circling for months, that the Chancellor had known this would happen. Had perhaps *arranged* the tier assignments himself. Had wanted a stumble today, small enough to control, public enough to justify whatever came next.

Whatever came next. Vael had read the requisitions. Resonance cells expanded. New bindings commissioned from the artificers' guild, specifications sealed even from him. A child's measurements, taken twice.

*Ives means to replace the failing hymn with a singer,* he thought. *A living engine. He means to do to one child what the first king did to ten thousand.*

The hymn swelled toward its climax, and Vael, Inquisitor-General of the realm, hunter of speakers, burner of the Thornfell holds, quietly made his decision. He did not yet know what form it would take. He only knew which side of it he would stand on.

Below the dais, in the crowd, someone was humming a cadence the Church had forbidden for two centuries, soft under her breath, and no one was dragging her away.

* * *

Yara had never hated a crowd before. They had feared crowds, avoided them, moved through them like smoke through cracks. But standing pressed shoulder to shoulder among forty thousand festival-goers on the Processional Way, they discovered hatred—or something near it, something with hatred's heat and grief's weight.

Because Yara could hear what the hymn was supposed to be.

Ten years of silence had not dulled the ear; if anything, the silence had honed it, the way blindness sharpens touch. And what Yara heard rising from the cathedral steps was a corpse wearing the founding song's clothes. The melody was right. The architecture was right. But the heart of it—the interlocking voices, the parts written so that each singer carried a piece another could not reach alone—had been cut out and replaced with a single unbroken line, one voice multiplied ten thousand times, obedient, identical, dead.

*That's what he did,* Yara thought, and the thought had Merrin's dream-voice in it now, the child's memories leaking into their own mind through ten weeks of shared roads and shared fires. *The first king. He couldn't own a song that needed everyone, so he made one that needed no one.*

Beside them, Merrin's hand had gone rigid in theirs.

Yara looked down. The girl was staring at the cathedral steps with an expression Yara had last seen on the frozen pass where they'd found her: blank, absent, somewhere else entirely. Her lips were moving. No sound came out. Under Yara's fingers, her pulse hammered quick and wrong.

"Merrin."

Nothing.

"Merrin." They turned, putting their body between the girl and the press of the crowd, and spoke close against her ear. "Look at me. Look at my eyes. You're on the Processional Way. It's cold. You're wearing Sera Kestrel's grey cloak, and you hate the hood, and there's ash from last night's fire on your left boot. Come back."

Merrin blinked. Her eyes focused—on Yara, past Yara, on the cathedral, on something behind the cathedral that only she could see. When she spoke, her voice was very small and very steady, the way children's voices go steady when they are saying something enormous.

"That's my part." She raised one hand slightly toward the tiers of singers. "The high line, the one that keeps breaking. That was mine. Before. I used to sing it, and when I sang it, the towers—" Her hand closed. Her face crumpled and reassembled itself, eight years old again and terrified. "Yara, they're singing it *wrong*. They've been singing it wrong for so long it's forgotten how to be sung right, and it's *tired*, can't you feel it, it's so tired—"

"Hush." Not because Yara didn't believe her. Because three people nearby had turned at the word *towers*, and because the girl's voice carried a harmonic underneath it that made the hairs on Yara's arms rise, a resonance that had nothing to do with volume and everything to do with truth, and truth was the most dangerous sound in Greyfall today.

Around them, the crowd was changing. It was subtle—Yara had spent a decade reading crowds the way other people read weather—but it was unmistakable. The festival joy was still on every face, but underneath it something else had begun to move, slow and cold, like water finding a crack in a dam. People who had come to sing were listening instead. Really listening. An apprentice stonemason near them had stopped mid-conversation with his sweetheart, his head tilted, his brow furrowed, chasing some wrongness he could feel but not name. A fruit-seller had abandoned her stall to stare at the cathedral. Somewhere back along the Processional Way, a child began to cry—not tantrum-crying but the flat, continuous crying of a child who senses a storm coming and cannot say why.

"They heard it," Yara murmured. "When it broke. They all heard it, and now they can't stop hearing what's wrong with it."

Merrin's fingers tightened around theirs. "Is that bad?"

Yara thought about Sera Kestrel's price. *Speak it publicly, under your own name, and do not vanish after.* They had told her they could not promise. Standing here, in the shadow of the lie made audible, they found they were no longer sure whether the fear they felt was of speaking or of what would happen the moment they did.

"I don't know," they said honestly. "Ask me again tonight."

Above the cathedral, the bells changed their pattern—from celebration to the deep measured tolling that meant the King's Devotion, the moment when the whole city knelt. Forty thousand people sank down onto the stones around Yara and Merrin, and for one strange suspended instant the two of them were the only ones standing, exposed, visible, a swordspeaker and a sealed child upright in a kneeling city.

Then Merrin tugged Yara's sleeve and pointed, and Yara saw the soldiers moving along the edges of the square—not festival guards but Hunt troopers in their grey, moving with purpose, converging not on the crowd but on the cathedral's western door, where a side stair led down to places Yara had heard of in smuggler songs and never believed: the cells where voices go.

"They're going below," Merrin whispered. "During the Devotion. While everyone's kneeling."

Yara's blood went cold and then hot. *Brannoc.* The old healer had been in those cells for three weeks. Whatever was happening below the cathedral was happening to him now, while forty thousand people knelt and sang the thief's song over his head.

"Stay with the Kestrels," Yara breathed. "Whatever happens. Stay with—"

The hymn broke again.

This time it was not one voice. This time a whole section failed at once—the lower registers, the deep supporting tones that no one consciously hears but everyone stands on—and the sound that came out of the choir instead was a groan, brief and awful, like a building settling wrong, like the earth clearing its throat. The Devotion bells stumbled. Recovered. Stumbled again.

And the crowd did not kneel back up into festival joy. The crowd stayed kneeling, but differently now—heads lifting, faces turning to neighbors, mouths opening on questions no one dared ask aloud. Panic did not erupt; panic *seeded*. Yara could see it going into the ground of the crowd like winter wheat, patient, certain to come up.

An old woman near them looked straight at Yara—looked, and did not look away—and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly one row:

"My grandmother used to hum something different. Before the Register laws. Something with *parts* in it." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "I'd forgotten until just now. How does a person forget a song?"

Yara had no answer. The woman nodded slowly, as if the lack of answer were itself an answer, and turned forward again to kneel in the cold and listen to the realm's foundation crack.

* * *

In the resonance cells beneath the cathedral, Brannoc felt the hymn fail the way a man feels a change in pressure behind a storm front—in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his throat, in the iron band around his skull that drank his gift and gave back static.

He had been in the dark long enough to measure time by meals and by the small sounds of the prison: the boots above, the drip in the northeast corner, the breathing of the others. There were others. He had found them the way the blind find walls—by echo. A woman two cells down who wept in perfect fifths. A man across the corridor who tapped rhythms against his wall at night, old road-rhythms, smuggler-signals, and Brannoc had answered once, cautiously, and gotten back the Kestrel clan's safe-passage cadence, and had nearly wept himself.

And one more. Farther down, past the turn where the corridor bent out of sight. A presence he had not felt in ten years and had mourned in the shrine caves with the rest of the dead. A presence that had begun, three nights ago, to hum.

Now the hymn overhead stuttered, and in the stutter's wake every gifted ear in the prison heard the same thing: the sustaining song, naked, exhausted, running on nothing.

"Well," Brannoc rasped, to the dark, to the others, to the humming presence far down the corridor. "There it goes."

Boots on the stairs. Many boots. Keys turning—not his cell, not yet. The woman who wept in fifths went silent. The rhythm-tapper stopped tapping.

Brannoc pressed his back to the wall and did the arithmetic he had been doing since the day Vael's extraction device had first touched his temples. They had taken three sessions from him. Three false memories planted, layered, aged convincingly—a forged vision of the Choir's fall so beautiful it would make any interrogator doubt everything he thought he knew. Vael had swallowed it; Brannoc had felt him swallow. But Vael was not a fool, and fools did not keep men alive this long in these cells unless they suspected the meal was poisoned and wanted to taste again.

The boots came closer. His cell door's lock began to turn.

Brannoc closed his eyes and began, silently, to compose.

* * *

On the dais, Chancellor Ives lowered his hands as the Devotion ended, and the crowd rose around him like a field of uncertain wheat.

Vael watched him lean to the Master of Ceremonies, murmur something, watch the murmur travel down the hierarchy of officials in a chain of nods. Within a minute, criers were moving through the square announcing that a singer of the third tier had suffered a fall, that she was recovering, that the festival would proceed, that His Excellency the Chancellor invited all loyal subjects to remain and rejoice.

A lie, delivered smoothly, received smoothly. The countess beside Vael repeated it to her neighbor with relief. Heads nodded. Shoulders loosened. The seed of panic, so recently sown, was being trampled flat by the heel of authority, and it was working—it was working, and that was the most frightening thing Vael had witnessed all day.

*One stutter,* he thought, *and they are grateful to be lied to. What happens when it fails for a full minute? An hour? What happens when the hymn stops altogether and forty thousand people discover at once that the ground was never there?*

Ives turned, and for a moment his gaze crossed Vael's. The Chancellor's smile remained precisely where it was, but something behind it assessed, weighed, filed. *He knows I know,* Vael realized. *He has known for weeks. He keeps me close the way a man keeps a knife he hasn't decided whether to use.*

"Inquisitor-General." Ives's voice was warm, carrying, pitched for the officials nearby. "You seem pale. The cold, no doubt. You should go inside—see to your prisoners, perhaps. I understand the western cells require attention today. Consider it your festival duty."

It was a dismissal shaped like a courtesy, and a summons shaped like both. *Go below. Be seen going below. Whatever happens to the healer today, your name will be on it.*

Vael bowed. "Your Excellency is generous."

He descended from the dais into the crowd, and the crowd parted around his court blacks without knowing why, and he walked toward the cathedral's western door with the interlocking circles at his collar hidden beneath his cloak, and with every step the decision he had made grew more solid, more inevitable, less like a choice than like a memory of something he had always been going to do.

At the western door, a Hunt sergeant saluted him. "General. We're to bring the healer up for a fourth session. Chancellor's orders."

"Fourth session," Vael said. "Alone?"

"Chancellor's observers will attend, General. From the Guild of Artificers." The sergeant hesitated. "There's... equipment coming up from the palace. For the binding work. If the healer resists—"

"He won't resist," Vael said quietly. "He'll give me exactly what he wants me to have. He's been doing that for three sessions." He met the sergeant's eyes. "Which is why, this time, I will be asking different questions."

Inside the western door, the stair went down into the dark, toward the cells where voices go, toward Brannoc's waiting silence, toward whatever the old healer had spent three weeks composing in the dark.

And somewhere across the square, unnoticed by soldiers and Chancellor alike, a grey-cloaked pilgrim with a child's hand in theirs slipped backward out of the crowd, turned down an alley no map recorded, and began to run.
