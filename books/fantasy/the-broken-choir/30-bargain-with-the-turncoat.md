## 30. Bargain with the Turncoat

The safehouse above the dyer's shop smelled of mordant and old wool, and Yara had not slept in it so much as lain down inside their own exhaustion. Three nights since the festival. Three nights since the hymn had stuttered in front of forty thousand throats and the whole city had felt, for one held breath, the floor of the world go soft beneath it.

They lay on a pallet behind stacked bolts of undyed linen, boots on, sword within reach of a hand that no longer entirely trusted itself. The unbinding at Coldspring had left a rasp in their voice, a hairline crack they could feel when they swallowed, like a flaw in fired clay. Small things came out wrong. *Yes* sometimes arrived as a whisper. Sometimes as nothing.

So when the floorboard two rooms away sighed under a weight that was too deliberate to be a rat and too light to be Sera Kestrel's people—Yara heard it, and did not move.

A second board. A third. Whoever it was knew houses, knew which boards sang and which kept silent, and was choosing the silent ones with the care of a man who had done this before. Not a smuggler, then. Smugglers walked loud in other people's territory on purpose; it was courtesy.

Yara closed their eyes and listened past the footsteps, the way Brannoc had taught them twenty years ago in a green valley that no longer existed. Resonance clung to everything—a room remembered who had moved through it, the way a bell remembered being struck. Most people's traces were mud and bread and sweat. This one carried ink. Cold iron. And underneath, faint as a watermark: song. Old, disciplined, trained into the bones by someone who understood what voices were for.

Yara knew only one man in Greyfall who walked through the world leaving a signature like a signed confession.

*Inquisitor-General Vael.*

They should have run. Every instinct honed across ten years of Thornfell passes said *run now, out the dye-yard, over the wall, gone before he rounds the second door.* But their body had been hollowed out by three sleepless nights, and something colder than instinct had settled in them since the festival—since they had stood in a crowd of pilgrims' robes and heard the sustaining hymn falter like an old man climbing stairs, and understood that there was no longer anywhere to hide from what was coming. Running was a strategy for people who still had a world to hide in.

The door to the loft room opened without a sound. Oiled hinges. Of course.

Yara opened their eyes.

Vael stood in the doorway with a shuttered lantern in one hand and his hat in the other, as though he were a caller paying a social visit and had simply found the wrong address. He was not what the broadsides promised. The woodcuts gave him a hook nose and a hangman's stare; in person he was mild, almost soft, with the kind of face that juries trusted and children did not cry at. His grey cloak bore no insignia. He had come to a hunted fugitive's hiding place unarmed, or pretending to be, and he set the lantern down on a dye-stained table with the exaggerated care of a man demonstrating that his hands were empty.

"Yara Eriksen," he said quietly. "You're smaller than your legend."

"You're politer than yours." Their voice came out as the whisper again, and they hated it. They sat up slowly, hand finding the sword hilt—not drawing, just resting there, the way you rest a hand on a fence post. "How did you find this house?"

"The Kestrels have a cousin in the palace kitchens who owes me a debt older than your career." He said it plainly, without triumph, and Yara felt the ground shift under the truce before it had even been offered. *He can burn our allies whenever he chooses. He wants me to know it.* "Before you decide how to feel about that—you should know I've told no one. Not the Chancellor. Not my own clerks. As far as the Hunt is concerned, the last swordspeaker vanished into the river districts three days ago, and I have men wasting shoe leather looking."

"Why?"

"Because I need to speak with you before anyone else does, and because every hour I spend hunting you is an hour I'm not spending on what actually matters." He lowered himself onto a stool across the room, unhurried, and folded his hands. In the sliver of lantern light his eyes were tired in a way that no performance could fake. Yara had spent a decade reading faces across smuggling tables, learning the grammar of lies. Vael wasn't lying. That was the most frightening thing about him.

"You stood in Cathedral Square during the festival," Vael said. "Third row of pilgrims, hood up, on the left side near the alms trough. You flinched when the hymn broke."

"I flinch at bad singing. It's a curse."

"It broke," Vael said, ignoring her, "for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds, and four hundred people fainted, two hundred wept without knowing why, and six died—one of them a bishop. Do you know what the official record says caused it?" He smiled, thin and humorless. "A gas leak from the undercrofts. The Chancellor announced it within the hour. Within the *hour*, Eriksen. You cannot draft a lie about a gas leak in an hour unless the lie was written long before the gas leak was needed."

Yara said nothing. The linen bolts around them seemed to lean in.

"I have served the crown for nineteen years," Vael went on. "I built the resonance cells beneath the palace. I designed the extraction engines. I have put more gifted souls under the knife than I can number, and I told myself each time that it was necessary—that the gift was a fire, and fire must be licensed or it burns cities." His hands unfolded, refolded. "Then a month ago I extracted a memory from a drunk old healer in Lowmarsh, and what he gave me was beautiful, and it was false, and the fact that it was *false* is what ruined me. Because I went looking for the true version. And I found it."

"In the archives."

"In the archives, in the ledgers, in the acoustic surveys of the palace foundations that no one has read in a century because no one wanted to know why the throne room was shaped like the mouth of a choir loft." He leaned forward. "The sustaining hymn is stolen. It has been stolen for two hundred years. It was taken from the Choir of the Dawn—the founding song, the one your order died rather than surrender—and it has been sung by conscripted voices ever since to hold the realm's oaths together. And it is failing. Not in fifty years. Within the year. Perhaps within the season. When it goes, every oath sworn under it goes with it. Every marriage contract, every land deed, every soldier's allegiance, every judge's sentence. Two centuries of borrowed breath, and the debt comes due all at once."

"I know," Yara said. "I've known since a ruined abbey in the high passes. Where do you think I've been going? On holiday?"

Something flickered across Vael's face—surprise, quickly mastered, and beneath it, absurdly, relief. "Then you understand the arithmetic. The Chancellor understands it too. That is precisely why I am here, and why you should be afraid in an entirely new way." He rose, paced once to the window, checked the shutter gap out of professional habit. "Ives has stopped trying to preserve the realm. He is trying to preserve the *throne*. There is a rite—old, ugly, forbidden even by the standards of the old wars—that can bind a living vessel to the hymn permanently. One voice, chained to the foundation, singing forever so everyone else can keep pretending. The vessel must carry the founding memory intact." He turned. "The vessel must be your child."

"Merrin is not my child." The words came out sharp enough to hurt the crack in Yara's voice; they tasted blood at the back of their throat and ignored it. "And she is eight years old."

"Yes," Vael said simply. "Ives knows that. It doesn't slow him down. It may, in fact, be part of the appeal—younger vessels bind cleaner. I have read the rite. I wish to God I hadn't." For a moment the Inquisitor-General looked like a man standing in a burned house, turning over things that used to be furniture. Then the mildness reassembled itself, and Yara understood that this was the discipline of him: not coldness, but a dam holding back something enormous.

"So here is my proposition, and I will make it only once, and then I will leave and you will never see me again unless you choose it." He took a ring of keys from inside his cloak—heavy iron, palace-stamped—and laid them on the table beside the lantern. "These open the resonance cells. There are eleven speakers alive down there, including your healer. I will open those doors myself. I will give you the rite's location, the guard rotations, and the Chancellor's private schedule for the next nine days. And when the truth is spoken—in whatever square, whatever manner you intend—I will stand aside. My hunters will receive orders to stand aside. The Hunt ends."

Yara stared at the keys. Ten years of hiding made them look like a trap, because everything always was. "And the price."

"My name." Vael's voice did not change at all, and that was how Yara knew how much it cost him. "When you sing the whole memory—when every soul in Greyfall learns what the first king did, and what the Chancellors have done since—my name must be spoken among the deceived. Not the guilty. I have spent nineteen years as the crown's knife, Eriksen. I extracted memories from children. I signed the orders that emptied villages. If the realm remembers me only as the man who hunted the last of the Choir, then everything I do from this night forward is worth nothing—no one follows a monster toward the light, they only run faster. But if they remember that the knife turned in the hand—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I am not asking to be forgiven. I am asking to be *usable*. Let history file me among the deceived, and I will spend whatever years I have left being useful. That is the whole price. That is all of it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the dyer's cat crossed the yard, and its small weight rang faintly against Yara's senses like a dropped coin.

It would be so easy. That was the obscene part. Say yes, take the keys, save Merrin, free the prisoners, sing the truth—and let one sentence of mercy launder nineteen years of ash. The deceived. As if a man who built the extraction engines could be *deceived* about what they were for. As if the knife got to testify about the hand.

But then—Yara thought of Brannoc under the knife, feeding Vael a forged vision so lovely it had cracked him open. *Sacrifice disguised as defeat,* they had called it, and hated themselves for admiring it. And they thought of the crowd in Cathedral Square, four hundred people falling like cut wheat when the hymn stuttered, and asked themselves the question they had been avoiding since the abbey: *when the truth comes out, who will the city believe?* A hunted smuggler with a damaged voice and a bounty on their head—or the Inquisitor-General of the Crown Hunt, standing in plain view, saying *I was lied to, and here is the proof.*

Hate him. Use him anyway. Both things could be true. Both things were probably supposed to be true.

"If you betray us," Yara said, "if one key is false, if one rotation is off by a single watch—if Merrin so much as bruises on the way out of that prison—I will not kill you. Killing is quick and I owe you worse. I will speak your true name into the memory instead. Everyone will remember exactly what you are. Forever. That is the power you're bargaining with, Vael—it isn't steel. It's that I can make the whole world remember you accurately."

For the first time, the Inquisitor-General went pale. He covered it well—a half-breath, a blink—but Yara saw it, and saw also that he did not withdraw the offer. He had known this might be the answer. He had come anyway. Whatever else he was, he was not a coward, and that was somehow worse.

"Agreed," he said.

"And one more thing." Yara stood, joints cracking, and crossed to the table. They did not touch the keys yet. "You don't get to call her *the vessel*. Not in my hearing, not in anyone's. Her name is Merrin. She likes honey on bread and she's afraid of deep water and she taught me a round that children sing in the lowlands, badly, and she laughs at how badly. She is a person. The moment she becomes a thing in your mouth, this bargain dies and I go to Sera Kestrel and we do it the hard way, with fire. Are we clear?"

Vael looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—recalculation, or perhaps recognition, the look of a man discovering a rule he'd forgotten existed.

"We're clear," he said. "Merrin."

Yara picked up the keys. They were cold, heavier than iron had any right to be, and they rang against each other with a small clear note that hurt the crack in their voice like a splinter. Nine days. Guard rotations. A rite that bound a child into a wall forever. Beneath the palace, eleven voices waiting in glass cells, and somewhere down there, Brannoc, alive, because a turncoat's conscience had finally outweighed his career.

Vael retrieved his hat and moved to the door, then paused with his hand on the frame, back still turned.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the healer's false memory—the beautiful one, the one that ruined me—it showed the Choir's fall. And in it, there was a speaker who stayed behind to hold a door so the others could escape. Everyone assumed it was invention. Sentimental fabrication." He glanced back, just slightly, lantern light catching the edge of his jaw. "Was it?"

Yara's throat closed. Ten years ago. Smoke, and a mentor's voice singing the door shut behind them, and the smell of burning wool that they had never been able to smell since without tasting grief.

"Get out," they whispered.

Vael nodded, as if that too had been an answer he expected, and was gone the way he came—silent boards, oiled hinge, a signature of ink and cold iron fading from the room until only the lantern remained, and the keys, and the long dark shape of the road beneath the palace waiting at the end of them.

Yara sat back down on the pallet and held the keys until their chill stopped feeling like someone else's. Then they began, in the dark, to plan a descent.

* * *

Dawn found them at the dyer's yard wall, where Sera Kestrel materialized out of the fog with the punctual menace of weather. Her eyes went straight to the iron at Yara's belt.

"Those are palace keys," she said.

"They are."

"Whose debt bought them?"

Yara looked east, where the palace spires stood up out of the fog like the masts of a drowned ship. "Ours," they said. "Paid for with a promise I'm not sure we should keep. Get the clan ready. In three nights, we go under the palace—and Sera, I need fires. All of them. Everywhere."

Sera Kestrel smiled the way winter smiles, and went to wake her people.
