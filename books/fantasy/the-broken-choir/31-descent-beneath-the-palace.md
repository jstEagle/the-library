## 31. Descent Beneath the Palace

The drains of Greyfall were older than the crown that sat above them.

Yara had known this in the abstract way a smuggler knows anything—rumor traded over casks of unlicensed brandy, a Kestrel clan-mother's offhand remark about "the old water-roads," a map inked on hide and sold for more than a horse. But knowing and standing inside it were different things. The tunnel mouth yawned beneath the Pilgrim's Bridge like a throat, breathing out air that tasted of stone and stagnant centuries, and Yara stood at its edge with Vael's iron key cold in their palm and thought: *I am about to walk into the body of the lie.*

"Say it again," Merrin whispered beside them.

"The words?" Yara glanced down at her. In the grey pre-dawn light, under the bridge's arch, the girl looked small and stubborn and far too awake. "You'll remember them better if you don't hear them twice."

"I want to hear them twice."

Yara almost smiled. Almost. Their mouth had forgotten how, these last weeks; it did something stiff and unfamiliar instead. "*Vael's key opens what Vael's name closes,*" they recited quietly. "*The third grate past the singing stair. Count the turns of the water. If the water runs backward, run.*"

"Why would water run backward?"

"Because someone upstream wants it to." Yara tucked the key into the lining of their coat, against the breastbone, where it lay like a second scar. "Tide-works. Old engineering. Or new engineering pretending to be old. Either way, it means the palace knows someone's coming."

Merrin nodded with the grave solemnity she'd worn since Coldspring, since the arrow, since the seal had begun to come apart in her chest like wet paper. She hadn't slept well in three nights. Yara could hear it—not with ears, but with the low, constant hum that leaked from her now even when she was still, a resonance like a struck bell fading and never quite finishing its fade. It made Yara's teeth ache. It made something behind their sternum ache worse.

"You could stay with Sera's people," they said, though they both knew how the offer would land. They'd made it every night for four nights. It was becoming liturgy.

"I'm the one the song is *in*," Merrin said. "You're just the one who can carry me."

"That's not—"

"It is, though." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow harder to bear. "Brannoc says a vessel doesn't get to choose where it's poured. But I'm choosing to walk there myself. That has to count for something."

Above them, across the black ribbon of the river, Greyfall was waking. Bells for the Hymnal Festival's second day. Lanterns being lit along the processional way. Somewhere up there, ten thousand voices were warming themselves on the sustaining hymn—the stolen song, the borrowed breath—and none of them knew they were singing on a corpse.

Yara looked at the tunnel. Looked at the child. Took her hand.

They went down into the dark together.

* * *

The smuggler maps had not lied, but they had flattered.

Sera Kestrel's cartographer—a half-blind woman named Odile who drew by memory and touch—had rendered the drains as clean lines and confident arrows: *here the passage widens, here the old Choir-era brick begins, here you surface beneath the Records Annex.* What the map could not render was the smell, which was the smell of two hundred years of a city's secrets dissolving; or the sound, which was worse, because the drains *sang*.

Yara noticed it within the first hundred paces. A faint harmonic in the running water, a note held so long and so steadily that the mind stopped hearing it as sound and started hearing it as architecture. They stopped once, hand raised, and pressed their palm flat to the slick brick wall.

There. Underneath everything. The hymn.

It ran through the palace foundations the way blood runs through a body. Not loudly. Not obviously. Just *constant*—a thread of sustained tone woven into the very masonry, into the mortar between stones, into the water itself where it passed through channels cut long ago by hands that had known exactly what they were doing. Yara had heard the hymn sung in chapels and market squares all their life, thin and official and dead. This was different. This was the hymn as it had been meant to be: rooted, structural, load-bearing.

And fraying. Even here, even now, Yara could feel the places where the thread thinned to nothing—gaps in the song like gaps in a smile, patched and patched again until the patching was most of what remained.

"They built the palace on top of it," Yara murmured. Not to Merrin. To the dark. To no one. "Or they built it *out of* it."

"What?"

"Nothing. Keep counting grates."

They counted. One. Two. The third grate past the singing stair—though nothing here sang except the water, unless you counted the walls, and Yara was trying very hard not to count the walls. Vael's key fit the lock mechanism with a click so soft it might have been an apology. The grate swung open onto a junction where three passages met, and Odile's map, consulted by lantern-light shielded under Yara's coat, said *left for the Annex, right for the cells, straight for the old cistern and the way nobody comes back from.*

Left, then. Left, with Merrin's small hand gripping theirs, and the hymn humming in the soles of their boots like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

* * *

They nearly died at the first light.

Not died, precisely. Nearly *ended*. There was a difference, and Yara learned it in the space of six heartbeats in a widening of the tunnel where the old Choir brick gave way to newer work—palace work, smooth-cut granite, and set into the granite at intervals of ten paces, shallow bowls of polished metal that caught the lantern-glow and threw it back wrong.

Resonance dishes. Yara had read about them in Brannoc's fevered descriptions of the cells below the palace, and reading was nothing. Standing before them was like standing before a row of closed eyes that were not asleep.

"They feel vibration," Yara breathed. "Any vibration. Footsteps. Voices. A heartbeat too loud."

Merrin's hand tightened. "Then how do we—"

"Slowly. Softly. And we don't sing." Yara swallowed. "Whatever happens, whatever you hear down here, however much the song in you wants to answer the song in the walls—you don't sing. Do you understand me? Not a note. Not a *hum*."

Merrin's face did something complicated. She was eight years old and carrying the founding memory of the world's oldest order, and Yara was asking her to keep it silent while walking through the machine built to harvest it. She nodded anyway. Children were good at impossible instructions; they'd had a lifetime of practice.

They crossed the dish-lit stretch in ninety seconds that felt like ninety years. Yara counted their own heartbeat and hated it, because the thing they were trying hardest to keep quiet was the thing that never stopped. Halfway through, Merrin stumbled on a loose flagstone—a single clack of boot heel on stone—and every dish in the corridor *shivered*, a ripple passing across their polished faces like wind over water, and somewhere above and ahead a bell that was not a bell began to toll, one soft note, questioning.

Yara froze. Merrin froze. The note hung.

Then, distantly, a door slammed, a guard laughed at some joke, and the questioning note dissolved into the ordinary noise of a palace morning. The dishes stilled.

Yara discovered they had been holding their breath since the flagstone. They let it out by millimeters.

"*Backward,*" Merrin whispered suddenly, gripping their sleeve. "The water. Look."

In the channel along the corridor's edge, the trickle of water had stuttered—stilled—reversed, running gently uphill toward them for the space of five heartbeats before resuming its course.

*If the water runs backward, run.*

But run where? Back was the junction, the long dark, failure, Brannoc still in the glass cells, everything still lost. Forward was whatever waited. Yara stood in the middle of the choice with a child's hand in theirs and understood, with terrible clarity, that the tide-works weren't a trap for intruders at all. They were a *flush*. Something upstream had been disturbed, and the palace was clearing its throat.

They ran forward. Quietly. As fast as quiet allowed.

* * *

The passage opened without warning into space.

Yara killed the lantern and stood blinking in gloom that was not total—far above, filtered through grates and grime and two hundred years of soot, daylight came down in pale shafts, and by that light Yara saw where they were.

A cistern. No—not a cistern. A hall. Vaulted, vast, its pillars carved in spirals that Yara's hands knew before their eyes did, because they had traced those same spirals on the ruined pews of the Hollow Abbey, on the scorched lintels of the mountain shrine. Choir-work. Pre-crown work. The palace had not been built beside this place; it had been built *over* it, swallowing an older holy structure the way a lie swallows a truth—keeping the bones, discarding the voice.

And the pillars were singing.

Up close, with nowhere left to hide from it, Yara could finally hear what the drains had only hinted: the sustaining hymn did not merely pass through the palace. It was *anchored* here, wound around these pillars in slow spirals of sound, each pillar holding a phrase, the whole vaulted dark holding the chord. Two centuries of borrowed breath, exhaled into stone by generations of court singers who had never been told what they were really doing—or perhaps had been told, once, long ago, and had chosen the comfort of forgetting.

Yara walked the length of the hall with their head tipped back, reading the song the way other people read scripture, and what they found in it made their stomach turn over slowly and settle, heavy.

It wasn't just stolen. It was *edited*.

The founding memory that leaked from Merrin's seal—the towers, the ten thousand voices, the first king's knife—had a shape, and this hymn had the same shape with pieces missing. Every verse that named the singers. Every line that honored the source. Cut away and replaced with filler harmonies, praise-language, the king's name where the Choir's name should be. Someone had taken the holiest song in the world and performed surgery on it, and the surgery had been brilliant, and the surgery was failing, and the whole realm was standing on the scar tissue.

"This is where they keep it," Yara said aloud, and their voice echoed strangely, harmonizing with the pillars whether they willed it or not. "This is the architecture of the lie. Not the throne room. Not the law courts. Here. Under everything. A stolen song holding up the ceiling."

Merrin had gone very still near the central pillar. Her eyes were wide and unfocused, fixed on something several feet inside the stone, or several centuries away.

"It misses its ending," she said.

Yara went cold. "What?"

"The song." Her voice had that doubled quality it got when the seal leaked—hers and something older braided together. "It's been cut so long it forgot it was cut. But stone remembers. Stone is *embarrassed*." She put her small hand flat against the pillar, and the hymn in the granite shuddered, bent, and—for three unbearable seconds—tried to resolve toward a cadence it had been denied for two hundred years.

Every resonance dish in the palace, Yara would think later, must have screamed.

"Merrin." They pulled her hand back. Gently. Desperately. "Not yet. Not *here*. When we sing it, everyone hears it at once, or no one does. That was the whole point. Remember?"

Her eyes focused. She blinked, and she was eight years old again, frightened and sorry. "I didn't mean to."

"I know." Yara crouched to her level. Up close, in the pillar-light, they could see the seal scar over her heart glowing faintly through her shirt like a coal under ash. "Listen to me. What you have in you isn't a weapon. It's a *witness*. And witnesses don't testify in corridors. We get Brannoc. We get the others. Then we stand in the light, in front of everyone, and we finish the song. All of it. Together. Can you hold until then?"

Merrin considered this with the terrifying seriousness of children. Then she nodded once.

Above them, muffled by stone, the festival bells rang the hour—and underneath the bells, unmistakable now that Yara knew how to listen, the palace's answering machinery whirred and shifted, and the water in every channel reversed direction at once.

Someone knew. Maybe not who. Maybe not what. But the palace had felt a hand on its oldest wound, and it was looking.

* * *

The last stretch to the cells was a staircase, and the staircase was guarded.

One man. A single sentry on a landing halfway down, bored, picking his teeth with a sliver of wood, his lantern turned toward the descending steps rather than the ascending ones—which meant he was watching for prisoners coming *up*, and had never in his post's history considered that anything might come from below.

Yara weighed him the way they weighed all obstacles now: with a decade of practiced invisibility in one hand and a newly rediscovered gift in the other. Before Greyhollow, before the tollhouse, before the word *unmake* had come back into their life like a bad debt, the answer would have been simple—wait, circle, find another way. Now the other way was a whisper and a squad of disarmed men remembering the sound of their own weapons dying.

They chose the old way. It cost more than it used to.

They waited in the shadow of the stairwell's turn, Merrin pressed against the wall behind them, and watched the sentry's lantern sway, and counted his habits—he shifted weight every forty breaths, checked the upper stairs every hundred and twenty, and between checks, for perhaps thirty breaths, his eyes went to the little window slit and the festival light beyond it, and his shoulders relaxed, and he was somewhere else entirely.

On the fourth cycle, Yara moved.

Three steps, silent on the balls of their feet. The man's gaze was on the window. Yara's arm went around his throat, and they lowered him to the stones the way you lower a sleeping friend, and took his ring of keys, and did not kill him, and shook afterward anyway—because the ease of it, the *familiarity* of it, was its own kind of horror. Ten years of silence and the body remembered everything, eager as a dog.

"You didn't hurt him," Merrin whispered, peering at the snoring sentry.

"No."

"But you wanted to be ready to."

Yara didn't answer. Some truths you carried; you didn't hand them to children.

The keys were heavy, stamped with the sun-and-bar of the Inquisitorial office, and one of them—cold, ornate, heavier than the rest—was Vael's. *Vael's key opens what Vael's name closes.* At the bottom of the stairs stood a door of blackened steel with no visible lock at all, only a recess shaped like a sigil, and when Yara pressed Vael's key into the recess it sank, turned, and the door unsealed with a sigh like a held breath released after two hundred years.

Warm air came out. Air that smelled of glass and fear and, very faintly, of voices.

Beyond the threshold, rows of pale lights glimmered in the dark—the resonance cells, the prison of glass, and somewhere among them Brannoc, alive, if the truce held, if Vael kept his word, if anything in this city built on a stolen song could be trusted to stay true for one more hour.

Yara took Merrin's hand one final time.

"Stay behind me," they whispered. "And whatever you hear in there—whatever *sings* in there—"

"I know," Merrin said. "Don't answer."

The door swung wide, and the cells received them.
