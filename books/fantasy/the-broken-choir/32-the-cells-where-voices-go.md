## 32. The Cells Where Voices Go

The drains beneath Greyfall smelled of two hundred years of borrowed breath.

Yara had known that phrase would come to mean something different down here, but they had not expected it to be literal. The tunnels ran slick with condensation, and the air itself seemed thick, almost sweet, as though the palace above exhaled its sustaining hymn through the stone and the stone had grown fat on it. Every few paces the walls changed character—ancient foundation blocks older than the crown, then dressed granite from the first dynasty, then pale marble salvaged, if the smugglers' maps were honest, from the ruins of the Hollow Abbey itself. The realm's whole history pressed in around them, layer over layer, and every layer had been built by someone who wanted very badly not to be remembered.

Vael walked ahead with a shuttered lantern and the unhurried gait of a man touring his own house. He had changed out of his Inquisitor-General's whites into the grey of a minor palace clerk, and without the insignia he looked smaller—not diminished, exactly, but human-sized in a way that unsettled Yara more than his authority ever had.

"The resonance cells are below the waterline," he said quietly. "Deliberately. Sound carries better through wet stone. The architect understood that a voice is a thing you can drown or a thing you can drown *in*." He glanced back, lantern light catching the edge of his jaw. "You'll want to be careful which you choose."

"I've been choosing for ten years," Yara said. "I'm told I'm good at it."

"You're told a great many flattering things about yourself, I suspect. Smugglers flatter the people they need." Vael turned down a branching passage where the map Sera Kestrel's people had drawn showed a stair. "Here. And after this, no more talk. The stones listen. They always have."

Merrin's hand found Yara's in the dark. Her palm was cold and her grip was fierce, and she did not let go even when the stair narrowed and they had to walk single file. Yara felt the girl's pulse hammering against their fingers and thought, absurdly, of the frozen pass, of the small weight of her in the snow, of how much simpler everything had been when the only choice was whether to walk away.

They descended.

* * *

The cells were worse than Yara had imagined, and Yara had imagined them often since Brannoc was taken.

Not dungeons in any honest sense—the Inquisition did not use chains and straw. Each cell was a smooth-walled sphere of fitted stone barely large enough to lie down in, entered through a low round hatch, and every surface was carved with grooves in patterns Yara knew intimately: cadence-lines, the notation of the Choir, the same script their mentor had burned teaching them. The prisoners were not kept behind bars. They were kept inside an instrument. The walls drank whatever sound they made—their voices, their weeping, their prayers—and gave nothing back, so that within hours a speaker began to feel deaf, and within days began to feel *unreal*, screaming into a throat of stone that swallowed everything and returned silence like a debt collector.

There were nine spheres set into the rock in two rows, and seven of them were occupied.

Yara saw it in the way Vael's lantern moved across the hatches: a flicker of something in his face that was not pride. Regret, maybe. Or inventory.

"Seven," Vael said. "Two died in extraction. I won't pretend that doesn't sit poorly with me." He produced a ring of keys, each one shaped like a tuning fork, and stood before the first hatch without opening it. "Once these open, the resonance will change. The walls stop drinking. Someone above will feel it within minutes—I give us perhaps a quarter hour before the guard rotates down. Your smuggler friends' fires begin at the third bell. I suggest your work finishes before mine does."

"And if I find you've lied," Yara said, "if this is a trap—"

"Then you'll kill me, and my death will be one more true thing sung in this place, and perhaps the walls will finally have something worth keeping." He said it lightly, but his hand was steady on the keys, and his eyes, when they met Yara's, held none of the performance she'd seen in Greyhollow. Whatever Vael was now, he was not performing. "Open them all," he said. "Or open none. But decide quickly. Ives does not sleep well these days, and sleepless men send soldiers."

Yara took the keys.

The first fork-key slid into the hatch lock and hummed faintly against their palm—a note so low it was felt more than heard—and the hatch swung inward on darkness and the smell of a body kept too long in one small room.

A woman looked up at them from the floor. She was gaunt, grey-haired, her lips cracked and moving silently around shapes that might have been songs or curses. For a long moment she stared at the lantern light as though it were a memory she distrusted. Then her gaze found Yara's face, and her cracked lips parted.

"*Little wren,*" she rasped, in a voice ruined by disuse. "*They said the wrens were all dead.*"

Yara knew her. Ten years of hiding had not been enough to burn her out of memory: Maren Solvane, second voice of the western choir-house, who had taught Yara the breathing exercises that let a speaker hold a note until steel forgot it was steel. They had all believed her dead in the purge. She had believed herself forgotten. Neither of them had been right, and there was no time to say any of it properly.

"Maren." Yara's voice came out strange, thick. "Can you walk?"

"I can *sing*," Maren said, and laughed—a dry, terrible sound, like wind through a burnt forest. "Walking is negotiable."

Yara got an arm under her and lifted. Behind them, Vael was already at the next hatch, and the next, fork-keys turning, and one by one the spheres gave up their silence. A young man who wept without sound. An old ferryman whose hands shook too badly to grip the ladder. A boy of maybe sixteen who came out swinging, fists flying before anyone could speak, until Merrin caught his wrist and said, in a voice that was not entirely her own, "*Enough,*" and he stopped as though the word had been nailed through him.

Seven speakers stood swaying in the corridor between their prisons, blinking in the lantern light like things pulled up from deep water. None of them asked questions. That was the discipline of the old order, Yara thought—or perhaps just the exhaustion of people who had learned that questions were a luxury the walls could tax.

Brannoc was in the last cell.

* * *

He was sitting cross-legged in the center of his sphere when the hatch opened, and he did not move. He looked up at Yara with an expression of profound, offended calm, as though they had arrived late to an appointment.

"Well," he said. His voice was hoarse but intact, and underneath the hoarseness Yara could hear the careful control of a healer who had spent weeks rationing his own words. "Took you long enough. Do you know what they feed you down here? Gruel. Sung gruel. There's a blessing worked into it that makes it taste of nothing, and I have eaten nothing-flavor for nineteen days, and if you ever let me be captured again I will haunt you personally."

Yara laughed. It broke out of them half-sobbed, ugly and sudden, and they pulled him up by both arms and held him hard enough that he grunted.

"Careful," he muttered into their shoulder. "Nineteen days. Everything hurts."

"What did he take from you?" Yara asked, pulling back to search his face. "Vael. What did he extract?"

Brannoc's eyes went briefly, deliberately, to Vael standing at the end of the corridor with his lantern lowered. Something passed between the two men—something Yara could not read and did not like.

"Nothing useful," Brannoc said. "I fed him a fiction. A beautiful one. The fall of the Choir, the way it should have happened, martyrs and clean grief and a villain with a face you could hate." He smiled thinly. "It cost me three days of vomiting to build it strong enough to pass as memory. But he swallowed it whole, our meticulous Inquisitor-General, and I'd wager it's still rattling around in him doing damage." He clapped Yara's arm, and his voice dropped, and the healer's steadiness fell away all at once, leaving only the man underneath, tired and afraid and fiercely glad. "The girl. Is she—"

"Standing right here," Merrin said, "and fine, mostly, and the seal is cracking, and I keep dreaming in a language nobody speaks anymore, and I would very much like to stop being the most important person in the world, please and thank you."

Brannoc looked at her for a long moment. Then he crouched, wincing, and took her hands, and Yara saw him do the thing healers do—read her, quickly and completely, pulse and breath and the tremor in her fingers and the light in her eyes.

"It's accelerating," he said quietly, to Yara, over her head. "The seal. Another week, maybe less, and it opens whether anyone wants it to or not. If it opens down here—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. A sealed founding-memory rupturing beneath the palace would level the hill and everyone on it.

"Then we finish tonight," Yara said.

Above them, muffled through forty feet of wet stone, the third bell began to ring.

* * *

It started as a smell.

Smoke, thin and acrid, threading down through the ventilation shafts, and with it a sound Yara felt before hearing: the far-off, arrhythmic thunder of a city waking wrong. Bells answering bells, but not in pattern—in panic. Somewhere above, Sera Kestrel's people were keeping their promise.

"They're early," Vael said, and there was something in his voice Yara had never heard there before. He had gone to the shaft and was looking up into it as though he could see the fires. "That's not diversion. That's—" He turned, and for the first time since Greyhollow, the Inquisitor-General looked genuinely uncertain. "Kestrel clan marks. The granary quarter. The tanneries. The *records office*, do you understand? They're burning the census rolls. The Hunt's registries. Every name we ever collected."

"Loyalties pay their debts in flame," Yara said. "Sera told me that once. I thought it was a figure of speech."

"It was a threat," Vael said. "I mistook it for a figure of speech too." He straightened, and the clerk's greyness fell off him like a shed coat; whatever he became next, he became it quickly. "Listen to me. The fires buy minutes, not escape. When the resonance of these cells went quiet, the wardens above felt it—I guarantee it. Within moments there will be soldiers coming down the main stair, and they will not be four men with cudgels. Ives will have sent his resonance-guard. Plate-steel, oath-bound, deafened on purpose so the gift cannot touch them."

"Deaf men in armor," Maren Solvane said from the wall, where she had braced herself upright. Her ruined voice had found some iron in it. "We broke those once. At Coldspring Bridge, before you were born, little wren. You break them by making the *stone* sing, not the air. They can't hear, but their bones can."

"Can you?" Yara asked.

Maren smiled with cracked lips. "Ask me if I can breathe."

The speakers arranged themselves in the corridor without being told—seven of them, plus Yara, finding spacing by instinct, by the old geometry of the choir-house, and Brannoc took Merrin's shoulders and drew her back against the wall with a look that brooked no argument. The boy who had swung fists earlier planted himself beside the ferryman. The young man wiped his face and stood straight.

Yara felt it building among them before anyone made a sound: the hum, the gathering, the sense of separate voices leaning toward one another like grass toward sun. Ten years alone, whispering single syllables into wagon locks and river ice, and now—now there were *others*, and the power rose between them the way water rises in a shared well, higher than any one of them could draw alone.

The main stair boomed. Boots. Many boots, descending in step, and beneath the boots a low mechanical drone—the resonance-guard's armor singing its deadening note, swallowing sound as it came.

"Now," Maren said.

They sang.

Not the stolen hymn. Not anything the crown had ever sanctioned. Maren led with an old cadence, a working-song from the western houses, and Yara's voice slid under it and the others stacked above, and the corridor's wet stone took the sound and *became* it. The drone of the approaching armor stuttered. The boots faltered on the stair. And the stair itself—old granite, laid by masons who had learned their trade from Choir-trained singers, every block still holding the ghost of a blessing in its grain—the stair began to resonate, gently, deeply, the way a bridge resonates just before it decides what it is going to do.

The first soldiers came around the landing into a hallway that was humming at the frequency of their own skeletons. Two of them simply sat down, abruptly, as though their legs had received other instructions. Their captain shouted orders that dissolved into vibration before they left his mouth. Yara stepped forward, alone, to the foot of the stair, and sang one word—one unbound syllable, the first they had spoken at full voice in a decade—and the soldiers' weapons came apart in their scabbards, blades slumping into red-orange slag that dripped and hissed on the steps.

The soldiers looked at their empty hands. Looked at Yara. And then, with the particular wisdom of men who had been paid to fight rather than hired to die, they turned and climbed back up the stair as fast as their armor allowed.

Silence, except the fire-smell and the far bells and the ragged breathing of seven freed prisoners.

"That," Brannoc said faintly, from the wall, "was showing off."

"That," Yara said, and found they were shaking, exhilarated and horrified in equal measure, the old hunger in their chest purring like a fed animal, "was a mistake. They saw my face. All of them did. There's no invisibility after tonight, not ever again."

"There was never going to be," Brannoc said. "Not if you meant to see this through. Come on. Which way, Kestrel's maps?"

"Up through the cistern gallery, then the old pilgrim stair into the chapel undercroft." Yara turned to Vael, who stood apart, lantern dark now, watching the slag cool on the stairs with an expression Yara couldn't parse—awe or grief or the specific vertigo of a man watching his life's architecture come down. "Inquisitor. Your part isn't done. The binding rite—Ives will move it up now, tonight, while the city burns. Where?"

Vael was quiet a moment. Above them, through the smoke-hazed shafts, came a new sound: not bells, not boots, but a great crowd-noise, thousands of voices rising together out of the burning city—not screams, Yara realized. Singing. Broken, frightened, tuneless singing, ordinary people with no gift at all, singing in the streets because the fires had shaken something loose and no one knew what else to do with their mouths.

Vael heard it too. He closed his eyes briefly.

"He'll take her to the Hymnal Chamber," he said. "Below the throne room. Where the crown has kept its stolen song for two hundred years." He opened his eyes and looked at Merrin, and then at Yara, and whatever he said next he said like a man stepping off a ledge. "And I will go up ahead of you and stand in his way for as long as a deceived man can stand against the thing he served. Go. Quickly. And when you sing your truth up there—" his mouth twisted, "—remember my name belongs with the fooled. Not the guilty. That was the bargain."

"A bargain's a debt," Yara said. "Smuggler law. Debts get paid."

They went up through the cistern gallery with seven freed voices and one cracking seal and the whole city burning above them like a signal fire lit for the end of an age—and somewhere below the throne room, Yara knew, a Chancellor was preparing to chain a child to a stolen song, and the only thing standing between them was a turncoat with a guilty conscience and a swordspeaker with a broken oath and a voice that, God forgive them, still wanted to sing.
