## 33. Ives Unmasked

The resonance cells had a way of making even silence feel like an accusation.

Chancellor Aldous Ives stood at the center of the binding chamber, two hundred feet beneath the palace of Greyfall, and listened to the hymn fail.

It was not a dramatic failure. That was the horror of it. The sustaining song that had held the realm together for two centuries—the song every schoolchild learned was the breath of the Crown itself—did not shatter or scream. It thinned. Like a rope fraying strand by strand, like a hearth burning down to embers no one tended. He could feel it in the stone under his boots, in the faint tremor of the great resonating pillars that ringed the chamber, each one carved with the names of kings who had never once wondered where their power came from.

Ives had wondered. Ives had always wondered. That was the difference between him and every man who had held this office before him: they had believed the lie so thoroughly they never needed to learn its shape. He had gone looking, forty years ago, a young clerk with too many questions and access to the deep archives, and he had found the truth folded away in a locked folio beneath three seals and a dead language.

*The founding song was stolen. The singers were murdered. The Crown has been singing with a dead woman's voice ever since.*

And now the dead woman's voice was giving out.

* * *

He heard the footsteps before the doors opened—Vael's tread, always measured, always precise, like a man counting his steps toward something. The Inquisitor-General entered without ceremony. Two Hunt guards remained outside; Vael had long since earned the privilege of private audiences, which was precisely what made him dangerous.

"You sent for me," Vael said. His eyes went immediately to the central dais, where the extraction apparatus waited—a thing of glass and silver wire and old, patient cruelty—and then to the figure restrained within it.

Merrin hung in the cradle of the device, small and light as a bird in a snare. They had dressed her in white, because the rite required it, and someone—Ives suspected one of the softer-handed acolytes—had brushed her hair. She was conscious. She had been kept conscious for six days, because the seal responded to consciousness the way a knot responds to tension.

She was not crying. Ives had expected weeping, or screaming, or the dull collapse he had seen in grown men after a single night in the cells above. Instead the child watched him with dark, level eyes, and when their gazes met she said, in a voice that carried strangely in the resonant air:

"You're the one who's afraid."

Ives did not flinch. He had not built a career on flinching. But he felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs, and he noted, with the cold bookkeeping precision that had defined his life, that they had landed at all.

"Chancellor," Vael said quietly. "A word."

* * *

They spoke on the stair landing, out of the child's hearing—or what passed for a child's hearing, these days. Vael had closed the door behind him, and in the lamplight his face had the careful blankness of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"The extraction is not proceeding," Vael said. "The seal is Last Cantor work. Brannoc told us nothing useful—he fed the device a forgery, and a beautiful one—but the girl is another matter. Every attempt to draw the memory out meets resistance that isn't hers. It's *woven*. Whoever sealed her meant for it to open whole or not at all. We can pull fragments, but the founding memory—the complete truth you want—it will not come out in pieces. And the fragments we do pull are destabilizing her. Another week of this and there will be nothing left to bind but a husk."

"Then we stop pulling," Ives said.

Vael's stillness sharpened. "You ordered extraction."

"I ordered *results*. Extraction was your method." Ives turned back toward the door, toward the chamber beyond, and let himself look at the problem the way he had looked at grain shortfalls and border levies and every other catastrophe that had crossed his desk in thirty years of service: as arithmetic. "The hymn fails within the year. You have read my projections. Without it, the wards on the northern wall decay in a season. The granary bonds that hold the lowland lords in treaty are anchored to the same resonance—they unravel, and with them twenty years of peace. Greyfall's aqueducts sing on the old harmonics; they crack. This is not metaphor, Inquisitor-General. The Crown does not merely *use* the song. The Crown *is* the song. Remove it and you do not have a crisis of legitimacy. You have a thousand years of architecture, law, and covenant coming down at once, and four million people underneath."

"I know what the hymn holds up," Vael said. "I have spent months learning exactly what it holds up. What I do not yet know is what you intend to put in its place." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Or whom."

Ives studied him. Here was the moment—the one he had rehearsed in a dozen variations across sleepless nights, because Vael was not a fool and fools were easy. Vael had charm the way other men had fists, but beneath it ran a current of genuine conviction, and conviction was the one material Ives had never learned to counterfeit cheaply.

So he would not counterfeit it. He would tell the truth. It was, he reflected, almost a luxury.

"Come," he said. "I will show you what I intend. Then you may decide whether you still serve the Crown—or only your own conscience, which I suspect has been whispering to you lately. You've been asking questions in the archive. Under a clerk's name. Did you think I wouldn't know?"

Something moved behind Vael's eyes—annoyance, perhaps, or respect. He said nothing, which was answer enough.

* * *

The binding chamber had been built by the first king's engineers, though it had never once been used for its intended purpose. Ives walked its circumference slowly, and Vael followed, and Merrin watched them both from her glass cradle with the patience of the very young and the very old.

"Two hundred years ago," Ives said, "the Choir sang the founding song, and the song did everything you have seen the hymn do—held the wards, blessed the treaties, steadied the earthworks—and more. It was sung by ten thousand voices together, freely given, renewed daily. No single throat could carry it. That was its genius and its weakness. The first king wanted a realm that did not depend on the goodwill of singers. So he took the song, killed the singers, and bound what remained into the Crown's own resonance. A stolen engine, running on a murdered fuel. Every king since has inherited it and called it divine right."

"And when it runs out?"

"It was always going to run out. Stolen things decay. We have stretched two centuries from what should have lasted fifty—the Choir built better than the thief deserved. But the account is empty, Aldous—" he caught himself using Vael's given name, and did not correct it, "—the account is empty, and there is no more fuel of that kind left in the world. Except."

He stopped before the dais. Before the girl.

"She carries the founding memory entire. Sealed, whole, uncorrupted—not the degraded copy the Crown sings, but the original, living thing. If the seal opens, that memory enters the world again, and the world must decide what to do with it. Perhaps it frees everyone. Perhaps it burns everything. I have read the histories of what happened the last time the whole truth was spoken aloud in a public square." He let the silence sit. "I am not willing to gamble four million lives on a miracle of mass enlightenment. I am a steward, Inquisitor-General. Stewards do not gamble. Stewards *preserve*."

"So you'll extract it by force after all."

"No." Ives touched the rim of the glass cradle, gently, almost tenderly, and hated himself a little for how easily tenderness came to his hands when it was useful. "Extraction takes the memory out and leaves the vessel empty. That is the old way, and the old way is why we are here—two centuries of drawing down a principal that can never be repaid. I mean to do the opposite. Not take the song from her. *Bind her to it.* Permanently. The rite this chamber was built for. The founding memory stays whole inside her; her gift becomes the new resonance source; the hymn is sung *through* her, continuously, for as long as she lives."

Vael was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone very soft, which Ives had learned was worse than loud. "She is eight years old."

"She is eight years old," Ives agreed. "And she will be eight years old for the rest of her life, in every way that matters to the realm. Fed, clothed, honored—there will be a chapel built around her, do you understand? Pilgrims. Incense. A gilded loft, the finest musicians in the world to harmonize with her. She will be the most cherished prisoner in history. And she will hold up the wards, and the treaties, and the aqueducts, and the peace, until she dies. Which, if the rite is done properly, will be a very long time from now. The resonance sustains the vessel as much as the vessel sustains it. She may live a century. She may live longer."

"You're describing a child nailed to a throne."

"I am describing continuity." For the first time, heat crept into Ives's voice—not anger, but the exhaustion of a man who had carried an unbearable weight alone and been told he held it wrong. "You think I have not lain awake weighing this? You think I have not imagined some cleaner road? There isn't one. Yara Eriksen speaks the truth aloud, and either the realm tears itself apart remembering, or the truth becomes just another story people argue about while the wards fail anyway. Let the hymn fail, and the northern wall comes down on villages that have never heard the word 'Choir.' Do you know what the Kestrel clans do when the mountain wards fall? Do you know what the lowland lords do when their treaty-bonds snap? I do. I have read the dispatches from the last time. Forty thousand dead in a single summer, and that was with a functioning Crown to bury them." He turned to face Vael fully. "One child. One life, sustained, honored, *kept*—against millions. Show me the arithmetic that beats it. Show me, and I will open that cradle myself and carry her out of here on my own back."

Vael looked at the girl. The girl looked back.

"You keep saying *kept*," Vael said. "Not saved. Kept."

"Words are your trade, not mine."

"No," Vael said. "Words are the Crown's trade. That has rather been the problem, hasn't it?" He walked a slow half-circle around the dais, and Ives watched him the way a man watches weather. "Here is what troubles me, Chancellor. Everything you say is true. The wards, the treaties, the arithmetic—I've verified it myself, that's what my questions in the archive were for. And yet standing here, hearing you lay it all out, I find I believe every word and trust none of them. Because you have known about the failing hymn for how long? Ten years? Twenty? And in all that time you never sought another singer. Never sought to restore the Choir, or find the scattered speakers, or—gods forgive me—simply *tell* the realm and ask it to bear the cost together. You went looking for a vessel. You have been looking for a vessel since before the girl was born. Merrin didn't force this decision on you. You were waiting for her."

Ives said nothing. There was nothing true he could say that would help him, and he had promised himself, somewhere in the last year, that he was finished lying to the one honest man in his government.

"The rite requires a Cantor's authority," Vael went on. "Last Cantor work, to bind a seal of Last Cantor work. There is no Cantor. There is no one alive with the standing to perform it—which means you intend to force it through the extraction apparatus regardless, and the apparatus *breaks* what it touches. Your own engineers told you the binding will be imperfect. She will be in pain. Constant pain, for a century."

"Pain can be managed—"

"*She is eight years old.*"

The words cracked off the resonant pillars and came back doubled, trebled, the chamber itself repeating the accusation in voices that were not Vael's. Ives stood very still and let the echo die.

"You asked what I serve," Vael said quietly. "I'll tell you what I no longer serve. I don't serve arithmetic. I served it for fifteen years and it made me efficient at harvesting grief out of prisoners' heads and calling it justice. Somewhere between Greyhollow and Coldspring I lost the taste for it." He straightened. "I will not help you bind her. And Chancellor—you should know that the Hunt is not what it was three days ago. Half my riders were at the festival when the hymn stuttered. They heard the crowd hear it. You cannot order men to forget a sound."

"Then you leave me no choice," Ives said, and was surprised at how steady his voice remained, how much it sounded like his father's, like every chancellor's portrait in the long gallery above, all of them grave and dutiful and certain. "Guards will escort you to your quarters. You will consider your duty overnight."

"My duty," Vael said, moving toward the door, "has been considering me for some time. I don't think it likes what it sees." At the threshold he paused. "For what it's worth—she's right. You are the one who's afraid. Not of the truth. Of a world where the truth doesn't need you."

The door closed behind him.

* * *

Ives stood alone in the binding chamber for a long time. Above, muffled by two hundred feet of stone, the Hymnal Festival was dissolving into rumor and panic; he could feel it through the floor, a wrongness in the city's hum, a million small hearts falling out of rhythm.

Merrin spoke at last, her voice thin but clear.

"You really think one life is a fair price."

"I think it is the smallest price available," Ives said. "I have spent my whole life choosing among prices. No one gets to choose whether the bill comes due. Only who pays it."

"That's what the first king thought," she said. "About the singers."

The words went through him colder than any blade. She could not know that—could not possibly know it, except that the seal was cracking, and things were leaking through her that had been buried for two centuries, and the dead first king's logic had apparently survived long enough to be quoted back at his last heir by an eight-year-old in a white dress.

"They volunteered," Ives said, and heard even as he said it how hollow it rang, how it was the same sentence every thief had ever spoken.

Merrin tilted her head, studying him with terrible, gentle curiosity. "When you were a boy," she said, "you wanted to be a singer. Before the archives. Before the numbers. You used to sing to your mother in the kitchen, and she said you had a voice like clean water."

His breath stopped. "That memory is mine. It's in *my* head. How—"

"The room remembers," she said simply. "This chamber was built to hold songs. It's been listening to you for years, Chancellor. It knows what you gave up to become the man who would do this." Her small hand pressed flat against the glass. "It's not too late to give something else."

Far above, faintly, through stone and earth and the bones of the palace, Ives heard a sound he had not heard in two hundred years of his family's service: a single human voice, unbound and unafraid, beginning to rise through the drains and ducts and resonant veins of Greyfall.

Yara Eriksen was already descending.

And the chamber—the chamber that remembered—began, very softly, to hum along.
