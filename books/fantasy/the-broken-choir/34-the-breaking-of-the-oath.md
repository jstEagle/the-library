## 34. The Breaking of the Oath

The rite-chamber had been a chapel once. Yara could see its bones beneath the gilding—the arched windows bricked over and painted with scenes of the first king's coronation, the altar replaced by a dais of black stone, the choir loft above sealed with iron grilles as if the architecture itself had been ordered to forget what it was built for. Two hundred years of borrowed breath, Brannoc had called it, and now Yara stood in the throat of the lie itself, watching it prepare to swallow a child.

Merrin lay on the dais.

They had strapped her down with bands that were not rope and not metal but something woven—resonance-thread, dull grey, humming faintly against Yara's skin from ten paces away. The girl's eyes were open. They found Yara across the chamber, and she did not cry out, did not struggle. She only mouthed one word, over and over, shaping it with careful eight-year-old lips: *remember*. As if Yara were the one who needed telling. As if she already knew how this ended either way.

Chancellor Ives stood beside her with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting for bread to rise. He had not even bothered to look threatening. That was the worst of it—the mundanity. He spoke to his technicians in the mild tones of a steward discussing grain stores, adjusting the angle of a resonance lens, checking the flow of the sustaining hymn through conduits sunk into the palace foundations. Around the dais, twelve singers of the crown's own choir stood in their white robes, throats bare, ready to pour the binding song into the child until there was no Merrin left inside her—only an engine wearing her face, singing the realm alive for another century, and another after that.

*Bind her permanently to the throne.* Ives had said it plainly, not twenty minutes ago, when Vael's blade had been at his back and Sera Kestrel's fires were blooming orange through the high windows. He had said it the way other men said *we must balance the ledgers*. A child sacrificed to continuity. And he had believed, right up until the guards came for him at Vael's signal, that history would thank him.

Now he knelt where they'd left him, wrists bound, watched by two of Sera's smugglers who kept glancing at the ceiling as though the whole palace might come down on them. It might. Yara intended to see to it.

But the rite was already beginning.

The twelve singers drew breath together—a sound like the sea drawing back from a shore—and the hymn rose. Yara felt it in their teeth, in the old scar tissue over their heart where the oath-mark sat cold and silent. The stolen song. The founding memory flayed down to a skeleton and dressed in royal vestments, sung by voices that did not know they were singing a murder. It filled the chamber the way water fills a lung. The resonance-threads on Merrin's chest began to glow.

And the seal scar over her heart began to crack.

Yara heard it—not with the ears, but with the gift, the sense they had spent ten years strangling: a fine splintering, like ice on a pond underfoot, like a held breath about to break. Fragments leaked out of the child. Yara caught them unbidden, whole and blinding: *towers of white stone, ten thousand voices rising in dawn light, a song so large it had weather in it, rain and harvest and the smell of cut grass—*

And beneath it, older and darker: *a king kneeling among the singers, weeping, asking to be taught. A king rising with the song half-learned in his mouth. The towers burning. Ten thousand throats opened all at once.*

The chamber swam. Yara gripped the edge of a pew—one of the few survivors of the Hollow Abbey's twin, dragged here and gilded—and forced themselves to breathe. Across the room, Vael stood at the chamber door, keys in hand, holding off the last loyal guardsmen with nothing but his presence and his rank, buying seconds. His eyes met Yara's. He did not nod. He simply stepped aside from the aisle, which was its own kind of answer.

The threads on Merrin's chest flared white.

There was no more time for cleverness. No more roads, no more bargains, no more hidden passes through the Thornfell dark. Every path Yara had walked for ten years had led here, to this floor, this moment, this arithmetic: the binding would finish in minutes, and the only thing that could stop a song was a greater one.

The only thing that could stop it was the whole truth, spoken with full voice.

Yara looked down at their hands. The oath-marks ran across the backs of them like old frost-scars, silver-white, put there by Cantor Elenna's own mouth the day before the purge. *Power bound to restraint*, the words had been, sung into the skin so that every unbound note would burn like a brand. The oath was the reason Yara had survived when the Choir died. The oath was also the cage. Behind it lay the full voice—the voice that could unmake steel and memory alike, the voice that had burned out the minds of three Inquisitors' hounds in a single sustained note, the voice that had driven the last swordspeaker who used it, old Maera of the Western Stair, into a silence so total she had spent her final years unable to recognize her own reflection.

Madness lived behind the oath. Everyone in the old Choir had known it. That was why the oath existed—not to protect the world from them, but to protect them from themselves. The gift was not a sword. It was a flood, and the oath was the levee, and no levee had ever yet held against the sea forever.

*I can hold it*, Yara told themselves, the way Maera must have told herself, the way every speaker at every stair of the fallen order had told themselves in the last hour of their certainty. *I only need one song. One true song, and then I bind myself again, I go quiet, I walk away—*

The hymn surged. Merrin's small body arched against the threads, and her mouth opened, and what came out of her was not a scream. It was harmony. She was singing the founding song in her sleep, in her terror, in fragments, the seal shattering around her, and the twelve crown singers faltered—for one heartbeat the stolen hymn stuttered against something truer and older, something with rain in it—

Then Ives, kneeling, bloodied, began to laugh softly, and the singers steadied, and the threads drank deeper.

Yara walked forward.

Each step was a decision. That was the thing no one warned you about breaking an oath—that it does not break all at once, in a single grand gesture. It breaks in steps. One foot, then the other, up the aisle between gilded pews, past the smuggler with the nervous eyes, past the Chancellor whose laughter thinned as Yara passed him. The resonance in the air thickened until walking felt like wading upstream through a river of sound. The oath-marks woke on Yara's hands, warming, then hot, then searing, silver lines kindling as if the old vow knew what was coming and meant to make them pay for every inch.

At the foot of the dais, Yara stopped.

"Merrin."

The girl's eyes rolled toward them, unfocused, drowning in the song being poured into her. But at the sound of their voice—just their plain, quiet, human voice—something in her steadied. Her breathing slowed. Her gaze cleared by a fraction, enough.

"Listen to me." Yara climbed onto the dais, knelt beside her, laid a palm flat over the seal scar. It was hot as a forge-stone. "I'm going to do a selfish thing. I'm going to do the thing I swore I'd die before doing, and I need you to know it isn't bravery. It's just the only door left, and I'd rather walk through it with you than stand outside it being safe."

Her lips moved. *Remember.*

"Yes," Yara said. "That's exactly it. Remember everything. All of it. Even the parts that will hurt us." They leaned close, forehead nearly touching hers. "When I start, you'll feel me in the song. Don't fight it. Open your seal the rest of the way and give me everything—the towers, the voices, the king, all of it. We'll carry it together. Can you do that?"

"I'm scared," Merrin whispered. First full words in an hour. Her voice cracked down the middle.

"So am I." And it was true, and saying it aloud loosened something in Yara's chest that had been locked since Greyhollow, since the old woman dragged away for humming, since a frozen pass and a small shape in the snow. "Being scared and doing it anyway—that's the whole secret. Nobody sang the founding song because they were fearless. They sang it because the alternative was letting one man own the dawn."

Behind them, the hymn climbed toward its crescendo. The threads were glowing white-hot now. Somewhere below the floor, Yara could feel the palace's resonance-conduits straining, the whole rotten architecture of the lie gathering itself to pour into one small body.

Yara straightened. Drew breath.

The oath-marks ignited.

Pain like nothing in ten years of careful silence—white fire lancing up both arms, across the chest, into the throat, the vow exacting its price in advance, screaming *turn back, turn back, turn back* in a language older than the crown. Any ordinary speaker would have stopped. Any ordinary speaker would have crumpled. Yara stood in the fire and thought of Maera's empty eyes, of Elenna burning, of an old woman in Greyhollow humming a forbidden cadence while soldiers laughed, of a child in the snow with no name and a war sealed in her heart.

*Let it cost*, Yara thought. *Let it cost everything. Some things are worth their price.*

They broke the levee.

The first note came out of them like the mountain coming down.

It was not loud, at first—it was *large*. It began below hearing, in the bones of the building, in the stone, in the blood of everyone present, and it rose through the registers of the world, and where it passed, the stolen hymn simply came apart. Not shattered—*unraveled*, thread by thread, lie by lie, because Yara was not singing destruction. They were singing memory. The founding song entire: the dawn on the white towers, the ten thousand voices, the rain-and-harvest weather of it, the king kneeling in false tears, the towers burning, the throats opened, the theft, the murder, the two hundred years of borrowed breath—all of it, every true thing, poured into the chamber at a volume that was not sound but *truth*, undeniable, inescapable, filling every mind in Greyfall like floodwater filling a valley.

The twelve crown singers stopped mid-note. Their mouths stayed open. Tears ran freely down their faces—they were remembering too; the song gave no exemptions—and one by one they sank to their knees among the resonance-lenses, weeping, their instruments of theft clattering to the marble.

The threads on Merrin's chest turned to ash.

She gasped—came alive under Yara's hand—and then her voice joined theirs, high and clear and impossibly old, the seal opening the rest of the way, and the song doubled, trebled, became a chord that had not sounded in the world for two centuries. Yara felt her inside it, small and blazing, carrying the verses Yara's damaged memory could not reach, and for one suspended moment they were not speaker and vessel, not rescuer and rescued, but two voices of one Choir, the last and the first, singing the world awake.

The pain was beyond bearing. Yara bore it. The oath-marks were burning away entirely now, the silver scars charring and lifting from the skin like bark off birchwood, and beneath them Yara could feel something else—something vast and hungry and beautiful, the unbounded gift in its full flood, whispering that there was no need to stop, no need ever to stop, that they could sing down the palace, sing down the city, sing down the sky—

*Maera*, Yara thought, clinging to the name like a handhold on a cliff. *Elenna. Hold. HOLD.*

They held. Barely. By a thread finer than any resonance-wire, they held, and shaped the last verse, and let it go.

Silence fell on the rite-chamber like snow.

It lasted three heartbeats. Then, faint and far below, through floors and foundations and the buried arteries of the palace, Yara heard it begin: the sustaining hymn, the great engine-song that had droned beneath the realm for two hundred years, stuttering. Faltering. Coming apart note by note as the truth propagated outward through every conduit, every mind, every ear in Greyfall that had just received two centuries of stolen memory in a single breathless chord.

Above, through the high windows, the festival crowd's murmur changed key. Confusion first. Then a sound Yara had never heard from a crowd before and hoped never to hear again: ten thousand people inhaling at once.

Ives was staring at the dais, at the ash that had been his binding threads, at the child sitting up unbound, and his face—for the first time—held nothing at all. Not conviction, not cunning. Just a man watching his life's architecture come down.

"What have you done," he whispered.

"What was owed," Yara said. Their voice came out ruined, scraped raw, half its registers gone already—the cost arriving on schedule. They did not care. They slid an arm under Merrin and lifted her down from the dais, and the girl wrapped her arms around their neck, and her heart beat steady against their collarbone, unsealed, whole, hers.

Vael stood at the door, keys hanging forgotten from his hand, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on his face. He had heard everything. He had remembered everything. Whatever he had been hunting—the gift, the speakers, the harvest—he was not hunting it anymore. He looked at Yara across the ruined chamber and inclined his head, very slightly, like a man acknowledging a debt he would spend the rest of his life repaying.

Then the doors burst open, and Sera Kestrel strode in with smoke in her hair and half her clan at her back, took one look at the kneeling singers, the ash, the child, the broken Chancellor, and said:

"Well. You've gone and done it. City's waking up, swordspeaker, and it's going to want someone to shout at." She jerked her chin at the windows, where the crowd's single held breath was finally releasing—spilling out into the streets in a wave of sound that was not yet joy and not yet rage, but something rawer than either, something with no name yet, rolling toward the palace like weather. "Question is what it does next. And question is—" her eyes flicked to Yara's scorched, oath-bare hands, to the way Yara swayed on their feet, "—what *you've* got left to answer it with."

Merrin tightened her arms around Yara's neck. Below them, the old hymn stuttered again, weaker, dying.

And somewhere out in the waking city, the first voices began to sing back.
