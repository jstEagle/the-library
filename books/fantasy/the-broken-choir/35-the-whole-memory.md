## 35. The Whole Memory

The binding circle was drawn in gold.

Merrin knew it was gold because she could taste metal on the air, thick and sweet, like sucking on a coin. She knelt at its center in a chamber beneath the palace that smelled of candlewax and old stone, and Chancellor Ives stood at the circle's edge with his hands folded behind his back, watching her the way a man watches a hearth he has just lit—satisfied, proprietary, already planning what he will cook upon it.

"You are not afraid," Ives observed. It was not a question. He seemed faintly disappointed by it, as though fear were part of the ceremony and she had failed to provide her portion.

Merrin looked down at her hands. They were small hands. Eight years of living had not made them large. But they did not shake, and this surprised her more than anything else about this terrible night, because she had spent so long being afraid—afraid in the snow, afraid on the roads, afraid of hounds and riders and the sound of her own name, which she hadn't even known until Yara gave it back to her. She had been afraid for so long that fear had become like a coat she wore without noticing. And now, kneeling in the gold circle with the binding rite beginning to hum around her, she found she had taken the coat off.

"I'm not," she said. Her voice came out small but steady. "You should be."

Ives smiled the way adults smiled when children said things they thought were cute. "The hymn has sustained this realm for two hundred years, child. Do you know what happens when it fails? Famine. Fire. Brother against brother. I am not saving myself. I am saving everyone." He gestured, and the robed singers at the circle's edge began to intone the opening cadence—a stolen thing, Merrin realized now, a song cut from a larger body and forced to breathe on its own for two centuries, growing thinner every year, like a man surviving on borrowed blood. "You will be the vessel. You will be honored. Generations will speak your name with reverence."

*Generations will speak my name,* Merrin thought, *the way they speak the names of the murdered.*

Because that was what she knew now. That was what had leaked through the cracked seal over these long winter weeks, fragment by fragment, dream by dream: towers full of voices, ten thousand singers standing shoulder to shoulder, and the first king coming among them smiling, asking them to teach him the founding song, the one that held a realm together the way roots hold a hillside. And they had taught him. Because he was their king and they loved him. And then, in the night, he had come back with soldiers, and the towers had burned, and the singers had died with the song still in their throats, and he had carried the founding memory away alone and cut from it only what he needed—the sustaining hymn—and buried the rest, sealed it, hidden it, because the whole truth would have unmade him.

The whole truth was in her chest. It had always been in her chest. The Last Cantor had pressed it into an eight-year-old girl like pressing a seed into frozen ground, and now the ground was thawing, and the seed wanted very badly to grow.

She felt it stirring under her breastbone as the binding cadence rose around her. The seal scar over her heart—the pale star-shaped mark Brannoc had traced with trembling fingers in a Lowmarsh sickroom—began to warm. Not painfully. Warm the way a window warms when sun comes through it.

*Merrin.* The voice was not spoken aloud. It came through the stone, through the gold, through two floors of palace above her, thin and strained and unmistakably hers: *Yara's* voice, worn ragged from the descent, from the fighting, from ten years of silence finally breaking open. *Hold on. We're coming.*

*I know,* she thought back, though she didn't know if thoughts could travel. She wasn't a swordspeaker. She had never been trained. She was only a seed.

But seeds do not need training. Seeds only need to open.

The binding cadence climbed. The robed singers' voices wove together into the familiar shape—the hymn, the hollow hymn, the one sung at every festival of her short remembered life—and Merrin felt it trying to take hold of her, trying to thread itself through her ribs and make her into a bell that someone else rang. It felt like cold water poured into her veins. It felt like being made into a tool.

And underneath it, patient as bedrock, the true song waited.

She thought of Yara by firelight, explaining the oath: *power bound to restraint*, the words wrapped in wool and grief. She thought of the question she had asked, the one that had made Yara go quiet for a long time: *Who made you promise?* She thought of Brannoc singing lies into Vael's knife to keep her safe, of Sera Kestrel's clan burning half of Greyfall to buy her minutes, of the smugglers and the Ferrywoman and the old woman in Greyhollow dragged away for humming three forbidden notes—an old woman who had never done anything in her life except remember a little piece of something beautiful.

Everyone had paid for her. Everyone had bled and burned and lied and killed for the small sealed thing sleeping in her chest.

It was time, she decided, for the seed to stop costing other people.

Merrin closed her eyes. She put both small hands flat against her sternum, over the scar, the way Brannoc had shown her you press on a wound to feel whether the heart beneath still answers. And instead of resisting the binding cadence, instead of fighting the cold water in her veins, she opened.

Not all at once. A seal is not a door; it is a knot. And knots come undone thread by thread, if you know where the ends are. She didn't know how she knew—but she knew, the way she knew the dreams, the way she'd known the mending harmonies, the way she had always known things she couldn't name. The Last Cantor had left the ends loose on purpose. Had trusted an eight-year-old to find them someday.

*Forgive me,* Merrin whispered—not to Ives, not to the singers, but to the dead. To the ten thousand voices in the towers. *I'm going to say it out loud. All of it. Everything they took.*

The first thread slipped free.

Sound went through her like a spear of light.

* * *

Yara felt it before she heard it.

They were three levels up, in a corridor choked with smoke from Sera's fires, Vael at their side with his keys and his pale, calculating face gone strangely still. Below them, through the bones of the palace itself, something vast had begun to move—a resonance rising through the foundations, through the drains they had crawled through, through the mountain rock Greyfall was built on, and Yara's whole body recognized it the way a scar recognizes weather.

"No," Yara breathed. "No, no—she's opening it herself—"

"The binding rite," Vael said sharply. "If Ives completes the binding while the seal is—"

"It won't matter what he completes." Yara was already running, boots hammering stone, toward the stairwell, toward the gold-and-candle smell rising from below. Ten years of hiding had taught them exactly one lesson above all others: never let the gift out unbound, never sing with the whole voice, because the whole voice does not distinguish between what it unmakes and what it frees. "She doesn't know what it will do to her. A child can't hold the founding memory alone—it burned the Cantor half-mad just to *seal* it—"

"Then you had better get there," Vael said, running beside her, "before the realm learns what happens when no one holds it at all."

They burst through the chamber doors into light.

Gold light—the binding circle blazing, Ives shouting orders, the robed singers wavering as the floor trembled beneath them. And at the center, kneeling, small and straight-backed, Merrin with her hands pressed to her heart and her eyes closed and her lips moving, and pouring out of her—not sound, not yet, but *memory*, raw and radiant, leaking into the air like heat off a forge—

Yara did the only thing left to do. The thing they had sworn on their mentor's ashes never to do.

They stepped across the gold line, into the circle, and knelt beside her, and took her hand.

"Merrin." Their voice cracked on it. "Little bird. Look at me."

Her eyes opened. They were full of light—not reflecting it, holding it, the way a lamp holds flame—and inside that light Yara saw everything she had carried: the towers, the burning, the king's smiling face, the murder dressed as law. Two hundred years of buried truth looking out through an eight-year-old's face.

"He stole it," Merrin whispered. "Yara. He stole the song and he killed them for singing it. Everybody needs to know. Everybody *has* to know."

"I know."

"If I say it alone, it'll break me."

"I know that too."

Merrin's small fingers tightened around Yara's scarred ones. "Then sing with me."

Every oath Yara Eriksen had ever sworn rose up in that moment like a wall: the swordspeaker's vow, power bound to restraint, the promise made over a mentor's burning body that the voice would stay leashed until death unmade the leash. Ten years of careful silence. Ten years of a power that must never be used, hoarded like a miser's coin, spent only in whispers—one wagon here, one squad's weapons there, each use a small wound in the secrecy that kept them alive.

And none of it, not one word of it, had ever said: *even if the truth needs you.*

Yara had asked themselves the Ferrywoman's question a hundred times on the long road—*what is one true memory worth?*—and had always answered with a lie. No more.

"Together, then," Yara said. "On the breath. Don't push—let it carry you. And whatever happens—" their thumb brushed the seal scar, gentle as snowfall, "—whatever happens, I've got you. I won't let go."

"I won't either," Merrin said.

And Yara Eriksen broke their oath.

* * *

It began with one note.

Not the hymn's note—never that. This was older, deeper, the first note of the founding song, the one the first king had heard in the towers and coveted until coveting became murder. It came up out of Merrin's opened seal and out of Yara's broken vow at the same instant, two voices braided into one, and the moment it sounded, the binding circle *died*. The gold light guttered like a candle in a draft. The robed singers staggered, clutching their throats, as the stolen cadence they had been chanting simply unraveled in their mouths—thread pulled from cloth, cloth pulled from loom.

"What is happening?" Ives shouted. "Stop them! STOP THEM—"

But no one moved to stop them, because the memory was already loose.

It went out through the stone. Through the mountain. Through every wall and cellar and drainpipe of Greyfall, riding the same channels the crown's hymn had ridden for two centuries—the crown had built its own delivery system, Yara realized distantly, had threaded the whole realm with the stolen song's pathways, and now those pathways carried the truth home like blood returning to a drained body.

In the great hall above, where thousands had gathered for the festival, the sustaining hymn faltered mid-phrase.

The choir master lifted his hands. The singers tried again. And the hymn did not merely stutter—it *collapsed*, folding inward like a tent with its pole withdrawn, because the hymn had never been a whole song, only a severed limb, and tonight the body it had been cut from was speaking again, and no severed limb can sing in the presence of its own throat.

And then the crowd remembered.

All at once. Every mind in Greyfall, every mind touched by two hundred years of the sustaining hymn—which was every mind in the realm—received the founding memory entire: the towers and the ten thousand voices, the first king's smile and his soldiers' torches, the singers dying with the song in their throats, the theft dressed as divine right, the coronations, the wars, the taxes, the executions, two centuries of rule built on a grave and paved over with a borrowed breath.

A woman selling roasted chestnuts dropped her tray and sat down hard in the snow, weeping, saying *my grandmother, my grandmother sang that cadence, they hung her for it, I was told she was a traitor—*

A palace guard lowered his spear and stared at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, remembering his grandfather's grandfather's stories, the ones the family had whispered for generations and never believed: *we were singers once, we were singers once, before the king—*

In the cells beneath the palace, Brannoc pressed his forehead to the glass and laughed and sobbed at once, feeling the truth roll through the resonance walls like a tide, whispering, "There it is. There it is, you beautiful, terrible children. Sing it all."

Chancellor Ives stood in the failing gold circle and screamed orders that no one obeyed, because obedience itself had been built on the hymn, and the hymn was ash. His guards looked at him—at him specifically, at the man who had ordered a child bound to a throne as a living engine—and their faces changed, and they stepped away from him, one by one, leaving him alone in the center of his own dead ceremony.

And in the circle, Merrin and Yara sang.

It was not easy. The whole memory was a weight like a mountain range, and it poured through them both, scorching, and Yara felt their voice tearing along edges they hadn't known existed, felt the old madness circling like wolves at the border of the song—the fate of the old Choir, voices burned out from the inside. But every time the wolves came close, a small hand gripped theirs tighter, and a child's voice steadied theirs, and they steadied hers, two flawed instruments holding each other in tune.

The last verse was the hardest. It was the verse about what came after—the mourning, the scattering, the survivors who hid in mountains and marshes for two hundred years, passing down fragments in lullabies and work songs and forbidden humming, waiting. It named names. It named the old families, the dead speakers, the ones dragged from market squares for three forbidden notes. It gave them back their honor in front of the whole realm.

When the final note faded, the silence afterward was the largest sound Yara had ever heard.

Smoke drifted through the chamber. The binding circle's gold lay dull and dead as dross. Somewhere far above, through many floors of stone, came a sound like a city exhaling—ten thousand people breathing at once, after remembering everything at once.

Merrin swayed. Yara caught her before she fell, gathered her up, and held her, and the child's head lolled against their shoulder, exhausted past speech, her seal scar now silver-bright and cool as moonstone—open, emptied, at peace.

"You did it," Yara whispered into her hair, and found their own voice was a rasp, frayed and hoarse, a rope burned halfway through. "You brave, impossible little bird. You did it."

Merrin's eyes fluttered open, unfocused, a smile ghosting her lips. "Did we... win?"

Yara looked around the ruined chamber—at Ives alone amid his scattered guards, at Vael standing quietly by the door with an expression no one had ever seen him wear, at the dead gold, at the smoke, at the vast uncertain silence of a realm with no song holding it together and no lie left to hold it up instead.

"I don't know," Yara said honestly, because the child deserved honesty above all things now. "But everybody knows. Whatever happens next—we start from the truth."

From the doorway, Inquisitor-General Vael cleared his throat, and his voice, when it came, was quieter than they had ever heard it.

"Then you had better come quickly," he said. "Because half the city is on its knees and the other half is heading for the palace—and Chancellor Ives's remaining loyalists have just sealed the gates with the crowd outside. If what comes next is to be justice instead of slaughter... the realm will need to hear from its last speaker. Right now. While they're still listening."

Yara looked down at Merrin, asleep or nearly, safe in their arms, the whole memory given away at last.

"Help me stand," they said.
