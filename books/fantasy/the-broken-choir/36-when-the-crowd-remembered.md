## 36. When the Crowd Remembered

The song ended the way a dam ends.

Not all at once—there was a moment, a held breath of a moment, when every voice in the Festival Square had gone silent and every mind in Greyfall was full of something that did not belong to it. Ten thousand people stood in the falling ash-light of the collapsed hymn, and in each skull the same memory burned: towers of white stone, ten thousand voices braided into one, and a young king with hungry eyes standing at the edge of the light with a knife.

Then the screaming started. And then, strangely, it stopped.

* * *

Corporal Denna Marsh had served in the Crown Guard for eleven years. She had stood post through bread riots and border raids, through the funeral of one king and the coronation of no one worth remembering, and she had always believed—dimly, comfortably, the way one believes in the weather—that the hymn she'd been raised on was simply the way things were. The realm's song. The sound of order.

Now she stood in her armor at the square's eastern edge, halberd grounded, and the memory moved through her like floodwater through a cellar.

She saw the Choir's founding—not as history, not as the sanitized pageant they performed every Hymnal Festival with children in white robes—but from *inside*. She felt what it was to be one voice among ten thousand, to give her note into the great chord and feel it come back to her multiplied, to be small and immense at once. She felt the founding song rise out of grief, out of a plague winter when the singers had gathered simply to keep each other's hearts beating. It had never been a weapon. It had never been a throne. It had been neighbors holding hands in the dark, singing so no one would die alone.

And then she felt the knife.

The first king—Aldric, they called him in the pageants, Aldric the Gatherer—had not gathered anything. He had heard the song and wanted it the way a miser wants gold: entirely, exclusively, forever. He had invited the Cantors to his hall under promise of protection. He had sealed the doors. And when the singing would not stop even as the blades came down—when the dying voices only wove themselves deeper into the chord—he had cut the song out of their throats and carried it away still warm, still wet, and built a crown on top of it.

Two hundred years. Every festival. Every coronation. Every quiet evening when the palace hymn drifted over the rooftops and the city slept easier for hearing it—all of it sung on stolen breath, by murdered mouths.

Denna became aware that she was crying. Not quietly. The kind of crying that shakes the shoulders, that comes up from somewhere below discipline.

"Corporal." Sergeant Hale's hand on her pauldron. His face behind his visor was gray. "Formation. There's a crowd surging toward the palace steps, we're to hold the line, we're to—" He stopped. He was looking at his own hand as if it belonged to someone else. "We're to disperse them," he finished, without conviction.

The crowd was not surging. That was the terrible thing. Denna looked out across the square and saw forty thousand people standing utterly still, weeping or staring or holding strangers, and none of them moving toward the guards at all. They didn't need to. The line between the crowd and the palace had already dissolved—it had dissolved inside the armor, inside the skull, inside the place where obedience used to live.

A woman near the front—an old dyer with blue-stained fingers—looked directly at Denna and said, not unkindly: "You remember now too. I can see it."

"I remember," Denna said.

"So do I." The dyer spread her stained hands. "So does everyone. So who exactly are you pointing that thing at?"

Denna looked down at her halberd. Eleven years of drill lived in her arms, and her arms did not move. There was no enemy in this square. There was only everyone, all at once, carrying the same wound.

"Hale," she said. "Ground your weapons."

"You can't—there are orders—"

"There's a child somewhere in that crowd," Denna said, "or there was, an hour ago, and men like us killed her family so a dead king could keep humming. Ground them."

One by one, along the whole eastern edge of the square, the halberds came down—not thrown down, not broken, but lowered, gently, the way you lower something you have suddenly realized is precious and dangerous. The sound of forty spear-butts meeting cobblestone was very small. It was the loudest thing Denna had ever heard.

* * *

In the palace, Chancellor Ives heard the spears hit the stone through three walls and two closed doors, and understood that his reign had just ended in a sound smaller than a coin dropping.

He had been in the rite chamber when the hymn collapsed—the binding rite half-prepared, the girl's restraints gleaming on the obsidian table, the resonance engines primed—and he had felt the sustaining hymn die in his own chest like a heart stopping. Two hundred years of borrowed breath, and he had been the man holding the bellows when they tore.

He had run. That was the truth of it, though he would spend his last free hour dressing the flight in the language of strategy. He had run through the service corridors toward the vaults where the original score lay sealed, because if he could reach the *source*, if he could hold the stolen song itself, perhaps—

But the corridors were full of servants standing motionless in doorways, weeping, and they did not bow as he passed. They only watched him go by with eyes that remembered everything, and their remembering was worse than any mob.

He reached the council chamber and found it occupied.

Captain Renn of the Palace Guard sat in the chancellor's own chair, helm off, a big weathered man with twenty years of unquestioning service in his face—and now the questions were all there instead, crowded behind his eyes like refugees at a gate. Four guardsmen flanked the doors. Their weapons were grounded.

"Chancellor," Renn said. His voice was steady. That was somehow the worst part. "Sit down."

"Ives is the appointed steward of the realm—"

"We sang the festival hymn this morning," Renn said. "All of us. We've sung it our whole lives. My mother sang it to me." He turned his hand over, looked at the palm as though checking it for blood. "I remember my mother's kitchen, Chancellor. And I remember a tower full of people being murdered for that song, and I remember that you knew. You've known for years. The engines under the chapel—you signed the requisitions."

"The alternative was collapse," Ives said. He heard his own voice go smooth and reasonable, the voice of a hundred budget sessions and a hundred quiet cruelties, and hated it, and could not stop it. "The hymn was failing. The realm would have torn itself apart. I acted to preserve continuity—"

"A child," said one of the guardsmen at the door. A young woman, Ferro, who had once carried Ives's daughter on her shoulders through a harvest fair. "You were going to bind a living child to the throne. Permanently. We heard the rite orders. We read them aloud in the barracks last night because we thought they had to be forged."

"They were not forged," Ives said.

"No," Renn agreed. "They weren't."

Silence. Somewhere far below, the resonance engines ticked as they cooled, a sound like a house settling after its owner has died.

Ives looked around the chamber—at the portraits of stewards going back six generations, all of them wearing the same chain he wore, all of them, he understood now, custodians of the same theft—and felt something in him finally stop pretending. Not remorse. He searched for remorse and found only calculation still turning over, cold and automatic, looking for the angle, the lever, the remaining play. Perhaps that was the truest confession he could offer: that even now, ruined and cornered, he could not make himself mean it.

"What happens to me?" he asked.

Renn stood. He was not a cruel man; that had always been the trouble with him, that he was decent in a system that required him not to be, and decency deferred for twenty years had nowhere left to go but here.

"That's not ours to decide anymore," Renn said. "Everyone remembers, Chancellor. Everyone. The courts, the clans, the families of the Choir. There are more of us than there has ever been, and we all know the same things." He gestured to the doors. "You'll wait in your rooms. Under guard—our guard, such as it is. Until the city decides what it wants to do with you."

"And if the city decides nothing? If it tears itself apart, as I warned?"

Renn paused at the door. For a moment the big man's composure cracked, and Ives saw underneath it not hatred but grief—the grief of a man losing the shape of his whole life.

"Then we'll grieve while we build," Renn said. "That's more than the Choir got."

The doors closed. Ives sat alone beneath his ancestors' portraits, and for the first time in thirty years, Chancellor Edran Ives began, very quietly, to pray—to no god he had ever served, in words he had never learned, because the only prayer he knew now was the one the murdered singers had taught the whole city without meaning to: *remember us, remember us, remember us.*

No one came to take the prayer up. He said it alone until dark.

* * *

Vael stood on the balcony above the Festival Square and did not move for a long time.

Below him the crowd was beginning to move at last—not rioting, not marching, but doing something he had no category for. They were sitting down. Tens of thousands of people folding themselves onto the cold cobbles in family clusters and stranger-clusters, holding one another, some singing—not the hymn, never the hymn again—a low wandering melody that kept changing key because no one was leading it, because everyone was leading it. The gift, scattered. He could see it flickering among them like heat-shimmer: a mason whose cracked hands smoothed as he wept; two women whose argument dissolved mid-word into shared weeping; a boy whose touch made a dead lantern flare alight. Weak. Everywhere. Belonging to no one.

To everyone.

Inquisitor-General Corvus Vael had built his life on a simple architecture: the gift was a fire, fires must be controlled, and control required someone willing to do what comfort could not. He had harvested memories from screaming prisoners and called it necessity. He had told himself, in the honest hours, that he was the dam against the flood.

And now the flood had come, and it was not a flood. It was rain. It fell on everyone equally and no one drowned.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them—these hands that had operated the extraction device, that had held Brannoc's head steady while the old healer fed him a forged vision so beautiful it had unmade him from the inside—and he understood that Brannoc had known. Had seen what Vael was before Vael had seen it himself, and had chosen, in defeat, to save him. The false memory rose in him again: the Choir's fall, but sung rather than suffered, grief transfigured into something almost holy. He had thought it a lie. It had been a lie wrapped around the truth like a wick around flame.

"General." A lieutenant, breathless, at the balcony door. "The Guard has stood down across the city. The Chancellor is confined to his quarters. There's—there's no one to take orders from, sir. The Hunt is dissolved. The men are asking. They're asking what we do now."

Vael looked out at the seated city, at the leaderless song rising off it like mist off a morning river.

Yara Eriksen was somewhere down there. He had come to Greyfall to capture the last swordspeaker, and instead the swordspeaker had captured the entire capital in a single act of honesty, and the cell doors of the prison beneath the palace were open, and somewhere in the ash of everything Vael found that he was not angry. He was relieved. That was the discovery that undid him: ten years of methodical cruelty, and at the end of it, relief.

"Lieutenant," he said. "Take the extraction device to the square. All of it. The resonance cells' keys as well."

"Sir?"

"Give them to the crowd. To anyone. Let the people who were hurt decide what should be done with the instruments that hurt them." He removed the signet of his office from his finger and weighed it a moment—two centuries of authority, cold in his palm. Then he set it on the balcony rail, where anyone could take it, and walked past the lieutenant toward the stairs.

"Where are you going, General?"

Vael stopped. Below, the shapeless song swelled and shifted, and he thought he could pick out—impossibly, absurdly—one thread of it that sounded like an old drunkard's voice, rough and forgiving, singing somewhere in the dark of the prison levels where the freed speakers were climbing toward the light.

"To find the ones I hurt," Vael said. "And ask them what remembering costs. I suspect I'll be paying it a long time."

He went down into the city unarmed, and no one stopped him, and no one forgave him either—which he understood, standing among the weeping crowds, was the only justice he had any right to expect.

* * *

High in the shattered gallery above the square, Merrin Eriksen-for-no-one, eight years old and older than the mountains, leaned against Yara's side and watched the city learn to breathe.

"It doesn't hurt anymore," she said. "The song. It's not mine alone now. It's like... it went home to everybody."

Yara's arm was around her, and Yara's voice—ruined, rasped, half its register gone forever—was silent. But Merrin felt the answer anyway, in the pressure of the arm, in the tremor that ran through the swordspeaker's frame: not grief, exactly, and not joy, but the strange flat country that lies between them where survivors live.

Down in the square, the crowd began to sing the harvest round. The one from Greyhollow. The one a child had taught a hunted woman on a frozen road, which a woman had taught a clan, which a clan had carried into a burning city. Small voices took it up, then hundreds, then thousands, imperfect and unschooled and everywhere slightly different, and the differences did not break it—they *were* it, the round turning on itself like a wheel, no beginning and no crown and no end.

Merrin hummed along. Around her, faint as fireflies, ordinary gifts flickered awake in ordinary people, weak alone, strong together, belonging to no chosen ones at all.

And far to the north, on a pass no map named, a figure with a damaged voice stood listening to a wind that carried, impossibly, a fragment of that round—and turned toward home, or toward whatever came next, walking still.

But that road was not yet walked. In the ashes of the palace, the freed prisoners were emerging blinking into the torchlight, and among them, leaning on Brannoc's arm, someone Yara Eriksen had mourned for ten years was climbing the stairs—and Yara, seeing that face, made a sound that was not quite a word.
