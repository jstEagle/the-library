## 37. Ashes of the Throne

The palace smelled of smoke and cut stone.

Yara walked the great hall slowly, boots crunching on glass that had once been windows, on the shattered remains of the resonance chimes that had hung above the throne for two hundred years. They had burst during the singing—all of them, every bell and pipe and singing-stone in Greyfall, at the moment the whole memory went out from Merrin's small body and Yara's ruined throat into ten thousand minds at once. The chimes had not been able to hold both songs. Neither, it seemed, could anything else.

Sunlight came through the empty window frames in long pale bars, laying itself across the floor like shrouds. Dust turned in the light. Somewhere far off—three streets away, maybe four—a crowd was still gathered at the Chancery steps, arguing. Not rioting. Arguing. Yara had learned, in the six days since the singing, to tell the difference by sound alone. Riots had a rhythm to them, a drumbeat of fury. This was something rawer and stranger: the sound of a city trying to decide what it was now that it remembered everything.

Yara stopped at the base of the dais.

The throne itself stood untouched. That was the joke of it, if there was a joke anywhere in this. The gilded chair with its carved choir of angels along the back—the first king's little monument to the people he'd murdered—had not so much as lost a flake of gold leaf. Everything around it had broken. The throne remained, gleaming, absurd, waiting for an arse that would never warm it again.

*You should have burned,* Yara told it silently. *Of everything in this room, you most of all.*

But fire was Brannoc's argument now, and Brannoc was not here. Nobody was here. That was rather the point of coming alone.

Yara climbed the three steps to the dais anyway—not to sit, never to sit—and put one hand on the armrest. Cold gilt. Cold as the pass at Coldspring, cold as the snow where they'd found a child with no name and a scar over her heart. It had started there, or it had started ten years before that in a burning abbey, or it had started two centuries ago when a king heard a song he could not make and decided to take it instead. Yara no longer knew where stories began. They only ever seemed to end badly or end long.

"Talking to furniture," they said aloud. Their voice cracked in the middle of *furniture*, split into two rough halves that didn't quite rejoin. They winced. Six days, and it hadn't healed. Six days, and Brannoc's face each morning said what his mouth wouldn't: *it may not heal at all.*

That was the price. They had known the price going in. Every swordspeaker learned it with their first lesson: the oath did not bind the gift out of cruelty but out of mercy, because the unbound voice burned what carried it. Sing the whole truth unbound, and the truth would take its toll in flesh. Yara had sung anyway. Would sing again. But knowing a price and paying it were different countries, and Yara woke every morning now in the second one, throat raw as flayed skin, voice arriving in pieces like a smuggler's cargo after a rockslide.

They tried a hum—one low note, the first note of the road-songs they'd used for a decade to seal wagons and quiet hounds.

Nothing came.

Not silence exactly. Silence would have been restful. What came instead was a pressure behind the breastbone, a held breath that wasn't theirs, the sensation of a door swollen shut in its frame. The gift was still there. That was the terror of it. It sat in them like floodwater behind a dam, and the dam was their throat, and their throat was cracked.

Yara took their hand off the throne's arm as if it had bitten them.

* * *

They found Sera Kestrel in the eastern courtyard, directing the loading of grain wagons with the brisk tyranny of a woman who had never once doubted herself in public. Clan banners—kestrels in flight, grey on grey—hung from the colonnade where royal standards had hung six days ago. Some enterprising clan-child had already torn the crown off the royal arms. The eagle beneath looked embarrassed to be there alone.

"You look like death's unpaid debts," Sera said, without turning.

"I feel like them."

"Hungry?"

"Yes."

"Then eat." She thrust a heel of bread at them and finally turned, looking Yara up and down with those flat river-grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Your voice is worse."

"It's honest work," Yara rasped. "It sounds how I feel."

Sera snorted. Around her, Kestrels moved grain and casks and bundles of salvaged records with the efficiency of people looting a sinking ship—but carefully, Yara noticed. Not stealing. *Salvaging.* Ledgers, granary tallies, the water-rights rolls from the Office of Rivers. The boring bones of a country. Someone had to keep them from rotting while everyone else argued about justice.

"The council met again last night," Sera said, quieter now. "Guildmasters, the temple remnant, two of the border lords who came down when they heard. And your Ferrywoman, wearing a dress worth more than my clan makes in a year, sitting there like she owned the table."

"She usually does."

"They can't agree on anything." Sera said it without drama, which made it worse. "Half of them want to crown someone new by month's end—some cousin, some general, anyone, because a realm with no head terrifies them more than a bad head does. A quarter want to break the realm into its old kingdoms and be done with crowns forever. And the rest—" she paused, and for the first time since Yara had known her, Sera Kestrel looked tired, "—the rest keep asking what *you* want."

Yara stared at her. "Me."

"You sang the truth into their heads. They remember the first king cutting throats in the abbey. They remember two hundred years of borrowed breath. They also remember that you're the only person alive who seems to know how any of this works." She shrugged, a hard little motion. "People are stupid and frightened and they want someone to tell them the shape of tomorrow. You're the nearest shape."

"I'm a smuggler with a broken voice."

"You're a legend with a broken voice. Worse. Legends don't get to retire." Sera picked up a ledger, hugged it under one arm like a shield. "I kept my side of the price, Eriksen. You spoke under your own name, in the capital, in front of everyone. I'm not asking more of you. But I'll tell you what I told the council: ask the swordspeaker what she wants, and listen, because half this city trusts her more than it trusts any of us."

She walked off toward the granaries, barking orders, leaving Yara standing in the courtyard with bread they couldn't swallow past the lump in their throat.

*What do you want.* As if wanting were a thing Yara still owned. For ten years they had wanted only one thing—to stay invisible, to stay silent, to stay alive—and they had wanted it so hard and so long that all other wants had starved. Now invisibility was dead, silence was impossible, and alive was... complicated. What did they want? Merrin safe. Brannoc sober and forgiven, in whatever order those came. Sleep without dreams of burning choirs. A voice that worked. Rain on a high pass and no one hunting anyone.

Was that a country? Could you build one out of that?

* * *

Merrin found them at dusk on the palace walls.

Yara had gone up there to be alone, which lasted precisely until a small figure came pelting up the stair with the boundless criminal energy of an eight-year-old who had spent a week cooped up in a healer's wing. She skidded to a stop beside Yara, breathless, cheeks pink, and leaned on the parapet next to them as though they were two old soldiers watching a siege.

Below, Greyfall spread out under the first stars. Ten thousand hearth-fires, and not one of them ordinary anymore. Since the Dissolution—or the beginning of it; Brannoc insisted it wasn't finished, might take weeks, might take years—small gifts flickered through the city like sparks off a grindstone. A baker whose loaves rose twice as fast when she sang. Two dockworkers who'd stopped a fistfight Tuesday by humming, both of them astonished, neither of them gifted before. Children skipping rope in patterns that made the ropes glow faintly, to the mingled horror and delight of their mothers.

"Weak alone," Yara murmured. "Strong in chorus."

"What?"

"Nothing. Old lesson." Yara looked down at her. The fever was gone; Brannoc pronounced the fracture in her seal closed—not sealed, *closed*, the way a wound closes, leaving a scar that was hers now and no one else's. Her memories were her own. All of them, even the terrible ones, even the founding, even the king with his knife. She carried them the way children carry everything: lightly, terrifyingly, as if the weight of a murdered order and a stolen song were just another thing you kept in your pocket next to marbles.

"Brannoc says your voice is broken," Merrin said, with the tactlessness of the very young and the very fond.

"Brannoc says a lot of things."

"Did it hurt? When you sang?"

Yara considered lying. Decided against it. Lying to this child had stopped working somewhere around the marshes. "Yes."

"Was it worth it?"

And there it was—the question underneath all the others, the one the crowds at the Chancery steps were really asking, the one the council couldn't frame and Sera wouldn't press and Yara lay awake at night turning over like a stone with something living under it. Was it worth it? The realm had no government. The hymn was dead and nothing yet grew in its place. Vael was somewhere in the city doing—who knew what; he'd vanished after the fall, taking his doubts and his meticulous conscience with him, and Yara caught themselves scanning crowds for his grey coat and hating that they did. Half the border lords were mustering men. The gift was loose in ten thousand ordinary chests, destabilizing, unpredictable, occasionally setting horses panicking in market squares. And a child was asking if it was worth it, because children always asked the real questions, because no one had taught them yet that some questions you're supposed to leave buried.

"I don't know yet," Yara said.

Merrin nodded seriously, as if this were a satisfactory answer, which perhaps it was. Then she reached into her pocket and produced, of all things, a whistle. Carved bone, smuggler-make, the kind sold at every mountain fair for a copper.

"Sera's daughter gave it to me. Watch." She raised it to her lips and blew three notes—thin, wavering, utterly unremarkable except that on the third note the nearest torch-flame stood up straight and burned blue-white for the space of a heartbeat, then settled back to orange, sulking.

Yara's chest tightened. "Merrin—"

"I know. Small. Brannoc says small is fine. He says the whole point is small." She lowered the whistle, and her face did something complicated—young and old at once, eight years old and eight thousand, the last vessel of the Choir learning to be the first voice of whatever came next. "He says yours isn't small, though. Yours is stuck. He says the dam-word again." She looked up. "He thinks if you sing with other people—not alone, *with*—it might come back. He wants to try. With me. Tomorrow."

Yara said nothing. Below them the city murmured, argued, glimmered with ten thousand small uncertain lights. On the horizon, past the river, the beacon-fires of the border lords' camps were visible—two, three, four of them now, patient orange eyes in the dark, men who had heard the truth and decided it meant war after all, or opportunity, or revenge. Nothing ended. Truth didn't end things; it only cleared the ground. And cleared ground, Yara knew from the mountains, was where the fighting was always worst, because everyone could see everyone else and nobody owned the shade.

"Merrin," Yara said, and their ruined voice made the name come out in fragments, *Mer-rin*, like something breaking politely. "If the council asks me to help rule—if they ask me to stand up in front of everyone and be the shape of tomorrow—I don't know what I'll say."

"I know," Merrin said. "That's why Brannoc wants to fix your voice first." She grinned suddenly, gap-toothed, eight years old again, and grabbed Yara's hand. "Come on. Supper. Sera made the cooks make stew and everyone's afraid of her so it's actually good."

She tugged them toward the stair, and Yara went, letting a child lead them down out of the dark and the wind, past the untouched throne and the broken chimes, toward firelight and noise and the impossible daily business of building something out of ashes. Behind them the palace groaned softly in the evening wind, a vast wounded thing learning to be a ruin.

Tomorrow, Brannoc would try to mend a voice. Next week, the council would ask its question. And beyond the river, the beacon-fires multiplied nightly, one for each lord who believed the ending of a lie was the beginning of a war—

—and Yara, walking hand in hand with the last vessel of the Choir toward a bowl of stew, understood with perfect clarity that whatever came next would need a voice far more than it needed a sword.
