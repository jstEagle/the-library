## 38. Scattering the Song

The palace still smelled of smoke when they gathered in the great hall, but the fires had been Sera's people's work, and they had burned what needed burning and little else. Ash lay on the marble like grey snow. Through the shattered eastern windows—shattered by no one's hand, simply by the weight of the memory arriving all at once in ten thousand minds—the morning came in pale and cold and clean.

Yara stood at the center of the hall where the throne had been.

The throne was gone. Not toppled, not broken: *gone*, unmade in the last convulsion of the old hymn's collapse, as though the seat of two centuries of stolen song had finally been refused so thoroughly that even its substance gave up. Where it had stood there was only a rectangle of paler stone, an absence shaped like ambition, and Yara found they could not look away from it.

"You'll wear a groove in the floor," Brannoc said, coming up beside them. He moved slowly now; the resonance cells had taken something from his joints that the healers among the freed speakers could ease but not restore. His voice was rougher than Yara remembered from Lowmarsh, but steadier too. Sobriety had come back to him the way spring comes to a high pass—not all at once, but unmistakably.

"I keep expecting it to be there," Yara said. "I keep thinking I'll turn around and it will have grown back."

"Things grow back when the ground remembers them." Brannoc looked at the pale rectangle without flinching. "This ground has forgotten. That's the first mercy this city has given anyone in a long time."

* * *

They came through the doors in ones and twos at first, then in streams. The freed speakers from the cells beneath the palace—nine of them alive, three carried up on litters, one so old that her voice had gone to a whisper that nonetheless made the air shiver when she spoke. Then the Kestrels, Sera at their head with soot still in her hair from the diversionary fires, her clan-mothers flanking her like a wall. Then others: townsfolk from Greyhollow who had walked a hundred miles on rumor alone, ferry-keepers from the Road of Nine Bends, shepherds off the Thornfell passes, a whole contingent of Greyfall's own fishwives who had been singing work-songs over their nets since dawn and had noticed, they said, that the nets came in heavier when they sang together now.

And everywhere, ordinary people. People with no gift at all, or none yet, standing at the edges of the hall with the wary hopefulness of those who have survived something terrible and do not yet know what they are permitted to want.

Yara counted them, because counting was easier than feeling. Four thousand. Perhaps five. More coming.

"It won't hold them all," said Maren Solis, one of the freed speakers—a cellist's hands, a voice like a struck bell, arrested eleven years ago for teaching children a round that the crown had forbidden. She stood near the dais steps, testing the acoustics with small hummed notes that bloomed in the vaulted dark above. "The Dissolution needs every voice within reach. If half the city is outside the walls—"

"Then we go to the half outside the walls." Yara turned. "Or we open the doors wide enough that reach doesn't matter."

Maren smiled thinly. "You've thought about this."

"Every night since the shrine." Since the Choice at the Shrine, though Yara did not say the name of it aloud. Since the night they had sat with Merrin sleeping against their shoulder and understood that keeping the gift locked in chosen vessels—kept in *themselves*, kept in the child, kept in whatever new speakers the scattered bloodlines might throw up in another generation—was only the old war waiting for better weather. "I've thought about nothing else."

* * *

They rehearsed for three days.

Not the way the Choir had once rehearsed—Yara remembered the old discipline from their mentor's stories, the tower cells and the vow of silence between practices, the way a swordspeaker's gift was honed like a blade until it could cut memory from steel. This was nothing like that. This was Brannoc walking the hall with a stick, tapping out rhythms on pillars while farmers laughed at their own flat notes. This was Maren teaching the fishwives a descending line while their children invented words for it. This was the old woman with the whisper-voice, whose name was Ilsette and who had been imprisoned for forty years, conducting with two fingers and correcting no one, because—as she said, and said often—"the point is not that you sing well. The point is that you sing *together*. Perfection is what got us the throne in the first place."

Yara sang with them. That was the strangest part, the part that kept catching in their chest like a fishbone. For ten years they had not sung. For ten years the gift had sat behind the oath like a wolf behind a door, and now here they were in a ruined hall full of smugglers and netmenders, singing a simple rising fourth over and over, and the wolf was not straining at the door at all. The wolf was lying down. The wolf was listening.

On the second evening, Merrin found Yara at the edge of the rehearsal, sitting on the dais steps where the throne used to be.

"You're scared," the girl said. Eight years old, nine soon, carrying a fractured seal and a fever's worth of founding memory in her small body, and she said it the way children say true things: plainly, without cruelty, as an observation about weather.

"Yes," Yara said. Because they had promised, on the winter road, never to lie to her again about fear.

"Of what?"

"That it won't work. That I'll break the oath and the gift will just—stay in us. Stay in you." Yara looked at their own hands. The tremor had started after the Unbinding in the festival square, after they had sung with full unbound voice and felt something tear loose that did not entirely grow back. It was worse some days. "Or that it will work, and I'll stop being what I am, and I don't know who that is."

Merrin considered this with the terrible patience of childhood. Then she sat down on the step beside Yara and leaned her head against their arm.

"When we sang the whole memory," she said, "in the square. Did you feel everyone?"

"Everyone."

"They weren't strangers anymore. After." She frowned, searching for it. "It wasn't that I knew them. It was that knowing them didn't cost anything. Like breathing near people instead of holding your breath." She looked up. "Maybe that's who you are. Someone who doesn't have to hold their breath."

Yara did not answer. There was nothing to say that the girl had not already said better.

* * *

On the third morning, they opened every door in the palace.

It had been Vael's suggestion, delivered without ceremony at the edge of a planning session, in the flat careful tone he had adopted since the festival—the tone of a man rebuilding himself from the foundation stones outward. "If the Dissolution is meant for everyone," he had said, "then lock no one out. And if it is meant for no one to command—" he had met Yara's eyes, and there had been something in them that was not quite apology and not quite absolution, "—then let it be seen that no one commanded it. Let there be witnesses beyond counting."

So the doors stood open, all of them, the great bronze leaves pulled wide by teams of Kestrel clan-folk, and the people of Greyfall came up the palace hill like water finding its level. By midmorning the hall was full and the terraces beyond were full and the streets below were full, a city's worth of voices gathering under a sky scoured clean by three days of rain.

Sera Kestrel found Yara at the threshold of the great doors, looking down at the crowd.

"My clan-mothers asked me to ask you," she said, "one more time. Whether you're certain. Once the gift is scattered, it doesn't gather again. No more swordspeakers. No more Choir. Whatever comes next, it comes next for all of us, and there's no taking it back into chosen hands."

"No," Yara agreed. "There isn't."

"And you're certain anyway."

Yara thought of the cave shrine in the Thornfells, and the rite of remembrance performed alone year after year for dead friends, the loneliness of a power that must never be used. They thought of the old woman dragged through Greyhollow market for humming a forbidden cadence. Of Brannoc under the knife, feeding Vael a beautiful lie to buy a stranger time. Of Merrin in the snow, with no name and a scar over her heart, found because Yara's feet had turned toward a cry instead of away from it.

"I spent ten years being certain of the wrong thing," Yara said. "Certainty is cheap. This is just the bill."

Sera laughed—a short, smoke-rough sound—and clapped Yara on the shoulder hard enough to stagger them. "Kestrels settle debts," she said. "Whatever comes, you sang it under your own name. That was the price. Consider it paid."

* * *

They began at noon.

There was no signal, or rather the signal was Ilsette, the whisper-voiced elder, lifting two fingers from her litter in the center of the hall—and four thousand people, rehearsed for three days in a single rising line, breathed in together.

Yara felt it before they heard it: the crowd's breath moving as one body, the way a mountain feels snowload shift. Then the note rose, thousands strong, plain and imperfect and enormous, and Yara stepped forward onto the pale rectangle where the throne had been, and broke their oath for the second and final time.

The first breaking had been desperation—full voice torn open to stop a binding rite, power spent like a life's savings in a single hour. This was different. This was deliberate. Yara reached down into the place where the gift lived, past the scar tissue of the oath, past the tremor in their hands, and took hold of the whole of it—the unmaking word, the memory-craft, the ten generations of inherited thunder—and offered it up into the rising line of the crowd's song.

Not gave. *Offered.* The distinction mattered, Brannoc had insisted. A gift can be seized. An offering must be received.

The crowd received it.

Yara felt the gift leave the way a river feels itself reach the sea—not ending, but spreading, becoming suddenly too vast to have edges. The song swelled and changed key, and Maren's bell-voice caught the new line and passed it down through the fishwives, and the fishwives passed it to the shepherds, and the shepherds to the ferry-keepers, and everywhere the gift went it grew smaller and stranger and more numerous. A woman on the west terrace gasped as a decade of suppressed grief left her throat as light. A Kestrel boy of twelve laughed out loud because he could suddenly hear the color of his sister's voice. Old Ilsette's whisper unfurled into something that shook dust from the rafters, forty years of silence spending itself in a single sustained tone.

And in Yara's chest, the wolf stood up, and walked out the open door, and did not look back.

What remained was not emptiness. That surprised them most. What remained was quiet—the ordinary human quiet that Yara had almost forgotten existed, the quiet of a body that is only a body, a voice that is only a voice. They sank to their knees on the pale stone. Their hands did not tremble. For the first time in ten years, they did not tremble, because there was nothing in them left to tremble against.

Around them the song went on, and on, and began—this was the miracle, this was the thing Yara would spend the rest of their life failing to describe—to *need* no conductors at all. Voices found each other across the terraces. Lines braided themselves without instruction. Somewhere below in the streets, people who had never entered the palace, who had heard only the wash of sound rolling down the hill, picked up the melody by ear and added their own descant, and their small new gifts flickered—weak alone, a candle each, nothing any tyrant would ever bother to steal—but together, oh, together, the whole hillside rang like a bell struck by sunlight.

Brannoc knelt beside Yara. Tears ran freely down the old healer's face and he made no effort at dignity about it.

"Do you feel it?" he asked. "Tell me you feel it."

"I feel—" Yara stopped. Started again. "Quiet."

Brannoc laughed, wet and wondering. "That's what it sounds like from inside. From out here it sounds like the end of the world and the beginning of it having the same note." He gripped Yara's shoulder. "It's done. Do you understand? It's done. No more chosen ones. Only neighbors."

Only neighbors. Yara looked out over the terraces—at the fishwife teaching the harmony to a Kestrel spearman, at two children discovering they could make a dropped bucket mend itself if they hummed at it together and giggling at the wrongness and rightness of it, at Vael standing alone at the far edge of the crowd with his hat in his hands and his face turned up like a man hearing rain after drought. At Merrin, somewhere in all of it, singing with a voice that was only a voice now too, and laughing.

No more swordspeakers. No more Choir. No more seals, no more oaths, no more hunts. The age of chosen wielders ended not with a battle but with a song handed out like bread.

Yara stayed on their knees a long time, and then, because the quiet in their chest was not emptiness after all but room—room like a cleared field, like an open road—they stood up.

* * *

The song lasted until dusk. When it finally settled, it did not stop so much as come home to roost in ten thousand separate breasts, and the city exhaled, and people began, hesitantly, to speak to one another in ordinary words again, and found that ordinary words had gotten heavier and sweeter somehow, the way bread tastes after famine.

In the emptied hall, lit by lanterns the Kestrels hung from the rafters where chandeliers had once proclaimed a crown's glory, the survivors of everything gathered around fires built in iron braziers, because no one wanted to be in a small room tonight, and no one wanted to be alone.

Brannoc sat with Merrin asleep against his good shoulder, her breath slow and even, the seal scar over her heart faded now to a thread of white, just a scar, just skin. Maren Solis argued happily with Ilsette about what should be taught to children first—the mending-hum or the truth-rounds. Sera Kestrel drank someone's very bad wine and pronounced it excellent. Vael sat at the fire's edge, accepted a bowl of stew from a fishwife who clearly knew exactly who he was and served him anyway, and ate it like penance and like supper both.

Yara stood apart, at the great doors, looking down at the city.

Greyfall glittered. Not with festival-light—with window-light, ten thousand hearths, and here and there a flicker that was not quite lamplight, small gifts waking in ordinary houses, a spoon stirring itself, a wilted flower perking up, a crying infant soothed by a mother's new harmonic. Weak alone. Strong in chorus. Ungovernable, now, in the deepest sense: there was nothing left to seize.

"You'll go, then." Brannoc had come up beside them without sound, which for a man his size remained a professional mystery. Merrin was still asleep in his arms. "Tomorrow. Or tonight, if the quiet gets too loud."

"In the morning." Yara had promised the girl's aunt in the river country a message, months ago and a lifetime ago. There was a family downriver that Merrin barely remembered and that remembered her, and a girl ought to know where home was before she decided whether to keep it. After that—after that, the long roads. The passes. Walking. "She comes with me as far as the river fork. Then—"

"Then you keep walking." Brannoc said it without judgment, which was its own kind of kindness. "Ten years hiding, a season of war, and your legs won't believe it's over unless they prove it. I know the ailment. I prescribe distance."

"And you?"

"Greyhollow. Someone has to reopen the healing house, and apparently I'm sober enough to be volunteered." He shifted Merrin gently. "She'll write. Children do, when you give them a reason and a postal road."

Yara nodded. Below, the city murmured. Somewhere a group of neighbors was singing the harvest round—the one Merrin had taught Yara on the winter march, the one that had existed for centuries in kitchens and fields, too small and too common ever to be worth stealing—and the round carried up the hill on the night air, and as it passed the palace doors Yara heard, woven into it, faint and flickering and utterly unremarkable, a dozen small new gifts keeping perfect time.

The age of chosen ones was over. The morning would bring the long road down, and a child delivered home, and a walker with no gift and no oath and no name that anyone hunted anymore.

Yara Eriksen stood in the doorway of the ruined palace, listened to the neighbors sing, and—for the first time in ten years—was not afraid of the silence when the song ended.

Because it didn't end. It just went home.
