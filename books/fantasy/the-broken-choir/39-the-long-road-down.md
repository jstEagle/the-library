## 39. The Long Road Down

The road to Merrin's village was not a road at all, or had not been for a long time. It began as one—somewhere south of Greyhollow, a rutted cart-track that the maps still insisted on—but by the third day it had dissolved into shepherd paths, then into deer trails, then into nothing more than a suggestion of passage through the bracken, as if the land itself had decided long ago that some places were better left unvisited.

Yara walked it anyway. They walked it slowly, because Merrin's legs were short and her silences were long, and because neither of them was in any hurry anymore. That was the strangest part of the aftermath, Yara thought—the absence of hurry. For a year they had moved like hunted things, always measuring distance against pursuit, always sleeping with one ear open for hounds. Now there was no pursuit. There was no hunt. There was only the enormous, unfamiliar weight of an open road, and the question of what a person did with freedom once it stopped being theoretical.

Merrin walked ahead of them, kicking a stone along the trail with the fierce concentration of an eight-year-old who has decided that this stone, this particular stone, must go all the way home. Her braid had come half undone. There was burdock in it. She had refused all offers to fix it.

"You're doing the face again," she said, without turning around.

"What face?"

"The sad face you do when you think I can't see. The one where your mouth goes flat like a shelf."

"I don't have a shelf mouth."

"You absolutely have a shelf mouth." She sent the stone skittering down the slope and went after it. "Brannoc says you've been making it since Greyfall."

"Brannoc talks too much."

"Brannoc says that too." She crouched over the stone, considered it, abandoned it. The ritual complete. She fell back into step beside Yara, close enough that their sleeves brushed. "Are you going to leave me there?"

The question landed without warning, the way children's questions always did—no approach, no windup, just the blade already resting against the bone. Yara kept walking. If they stopped, they would have to answer while looking at her, and they weren't sure they could manage both.

"That's where your family is," they said. "Your mother's sister. The farm outside Vell's Crossing. Brannoc found them through the parish rolls."

"I know what a family is." Merrin scuffed her boot through the bracken. "I'm asking if you're going to leave me *there*."

"Merrin—"

"Because I remember some things now. Not everything. But some." Her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was handling something fragile inside herself. "I remember a woman singing while she kneaded bread. I think that's my aunt. And I remember being carried on someone's shoulders, and there was smoke, and everyone was running, and I remember—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I remember that I asked someone to stay once, and they didn't."

The wind came up the valley and moved through the bracken in a long silver rush, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the mountain did their talking for them, which was a mercy.

"The people who left you," Yara said finally, "were trying to save you. They gave you to the Cantor because the seal was the only thing that could keep you alive. Keeping you hidden from me—from everyone—was part of it."

"I know. Brannoc explained."

"And I'm not those people."

"No," Merrin agreed. "You're worse. You promised."

Yara looked down at her. She was watching them with those grey eyes that had seen ten thousand voices die before she was old enough to name what she'd seen, and there was no accusation in her face at all, which was somehow much harder to bear.

"I haven't promised anything," Yara said.

"You're about to. You get the same face when you're about to promise something you don't want to promise. Your shelf mouth goes *extra* flat."

And Yara laughed—a rough, startled sound, rusty from disuse—and put a hand on top of the ruined braid, burdock and all, and said, "We'll see what the road does. Roads change things."

"That's not a promise."

"No," Yara admitted. "It isn't."

But it was the closest thing to honesty they had left, so it would have to serve.

* * *

They came down out of the high country on the ninth day, and the world changed gradually, the way the world always did—pine giving way to birch, birch to hedged fields, wild land to land that bore the fingerprints of use. Stone walls appeared, mossy and ancient and repaired in patches across two hundred years of small attentions. A shrine to some local saint whose name had worn off the niche. A well with a tin cup chained to its rim, and Merrin drank from it without thinking, and Yara watched her do it and felt something in their chest turn over slowly, painfully, like a great door being opened after a long winter.

Because this was it. This was the thing the whole year had been for. Not the fall of a chancellor, not the shattering of a stolen hymn, not even the Dissolution itself, with its ten thousand small flames passing into ordinary throats. It was this: a child drinking from a public well in a country where no one was hunting anyone anymore.

A woman was mending a drystone wall at the field's edge. She straightened as they approached, shading her eyes, and Yara saw the recognition move across her face—not of Merrin, whom she couldn't possibly know, but of the shape of her, the blood-shape, the way a face echoes through a family like a note through a chord.

"Oh," the woman said. Just that. The trowel slipped from her hand into the grass. "Oh, *look* at you."

Merrin stood very still. "Do I know you?"

"No," the woman said, and her voice broke clean in half on the word, and she knelt down right there in the mud of the gateway with her arms open. "No, love. But I knew your mother. And you have her exact chin, and I have wondered every single day for eight years whether you were—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Merrin crossed the distance between them in three steps and buried her face in the woman's shoulder, and the sound she made was not crying exactly, or not only crying—it was the sound of a sealed thing opening, the same sound Yara had heard from the girl's scarred heart in the hollow abbey, translated now into something human and small and unbearably loud.

Yara stood at the gate and did not go in.

This was right. This was how it should be. They had known it since the Coldspring bridge, since the fever, since the night Merrin had sung the founding memory into every mind in Greyfall and then, afterward, exhausted and burning, had reached for Yara's hand in the dark and held it until she slept. They had known it, and they had prepared for it, and none of the preparation helped at all.

The aunt—her name was Sefja, they learned later; she talked the way lonely women talk when the loneliness breaks, in floods—led them both up to the farmhouse through the gathering dusk. There was a husband, broad and quiet, who shook Yara's hand and held it a beat too long, and Yara understood that he knew. Everyone knew now, in the way everyone knew everything after Greyfall; the memory had gone into the water table of the world, and you could not live under this sky and not carry some of it. He squeezed Yara's hand and said only, "There's stew," which was its own kind of absolution.

Dinner was long and warm and full of Sefja's talk—her sister, Merrin's mother, dead in the purges' first wave; the farm; the cows with absurd names; a cousin who had married a ferryman. Merrin sat between them and glowed like a banked fire coming back to life, laughing at jokes whose context she lacked entirely, and Yara sat at the edge of the lamplight and understood that they were watching a person be reassembled. Piece by piece. Bread and milk and aunts and absurd cows. No gift required. No gift wanted.

Afterward, Merrin walked them out to the barn where they'd been given a bed in the loft. The stars were out, hard and cold and numerous. She stood in the doorway with her arms wrapped around herself, and for a while she said nothing, and Yara let the silence stand, because they had learned that from her—that silence could be a room two people shared rather than a wall between them.

"You're going tomorrow," she said. Not a question.

"Early. Before the mist burns off."

"Where?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's a lie. You always know. You knew every road we ever took before we took it. You used to draw them in the dirt with sticks when you thought I was asleep."

Yara leaned against the barn post and looked up at the stars, at the slow wheel of them, and told the truth because she had earned it and because lying to her now would be like spitting on everything they'd built together.

"South, maybe. Or west, along the coast. There are places I've never seen because I spent ten years looking over my shoulder instead of ahead. There's a shrine on the far side of the Thornfells that I owe a visit. There's—" They stopped. Started again. "There's a version of me that existed before all of this, and I'd like to find out if any of her is still in here somewhere. That's the honest answer. I'm going to go look for her."

"Is she worth finding?"

Yara laughed again, that same startled bark. "Ask me in a year."

Merrin came forward then and hugged them, hard, her face pressed into their coat, and Yara held on and felt the small fierce engine of her heartbeat and thought: *this is the cost. This is the actual price. Not the voice, not the scars, not the oath broken in a palace square before ten thousand witnesses. This. Walking away from the one person in the world who knows your shelf mouth.*

"You'll come back," Merrin said into their coat. "Someday. When you find her."

"If I find her," Yara said, "I'll bring her here, and you can judge for yourself."

"That's a promise."

"It's a promise."

She pulled back, wiped her nose on her sleeve in a manner Sefja would certainly have corrected, and fixed Yara with a look of terrible eight-year-old gravity. "Good. Because I'm learning things. Brannoc is teaching me letters, and Aunt Sefja is teaching me bread, and sometimes when I sing the harvest round the wheat stands up straighter, and nobody thinks it's strange anymore. Nobody's afraid. So when you come back, there will be a place. That's my promise."

She went inside. The lamp in the farmhouse window burned for another hour, and Yara lay in the hayloft listening to the unfamiliar sounds of a house at peace—a dog turning in its sleep, a beam settling, wind in the barley—and slept better than they had in fifteen years, and hated how much that proved.

* * *

Yara left before dawn, as promised, and Sefja, bless her, pretended not to see from the kitchen window, pressing a wrapped parcel of bread and cheese into their hands at the gate with red eyes and no questions. The mist was on the fields. The road ran south and west, and Yara took it at a walker's pace, because there was nowhere to be and no one chasing them, and the strangeness of that had not yet worn off.

For the first week, the silence was the worst of it.

Not the quiet of the mountains—they had lived ten years in that quiet and called it home. This was different. This was the silence where Merrin's voice should have been: the endless commentary, the questions that arrived like thrown stones, the off-key humming of the children's round she'd taught them on the Winter March, the little cough she made before saying something difficult. Yara caught themselves turning to speak three times in the first day alone. By the fourth day they had stopped turning, but the habit of listening remained, an amputation phantom, an ear cocked toward a presence that wasn't there.

Their voice was damaged. They had known it would be—had felt it tear in the palace square when they'd sung the whole memory unbound, the oath breaking like a dam and taking part of the riverbank with it. Some days it was nearly normal. Other days the words came out sanded-down, frayed at the edges, a whisper wearing the costume of a voice. The healers in Greyfall—new healers, chorus-trained, working in circles of six and seven where one gifted voice used to stand—had been honest with them. *It may mend. It may not. You spent yourself like coin, and some coins don't come back.* Yara had thanked them and walked out into the ruined city and felt, beneath the grief, an unexpected lightness. A swordspeaker who could barely speak. The joke was almost too neat. Somewhere, Yara was certain, the old Choir's ghosts were laughing.

The country changed as they walked. In the lowlands, the king's roads were still broken from the uprising's last days, though crews worked on them now—ordinary crews, paid by councils of towns instead of a crown, arguing cheerfully about drainage. In the villages, the new shrines had no saints in them, just open rooms with good acoustics, where anyone could come and sing and, if the stories were true, occasionally make the candles flicker. Nothing dramatic. Nothing chosen. A thousand tiny miracles, each one weak as a candleflame alone, and that was precisely the point. Yara passed one such shrine at dusk in a town called Marleford and stood in the doorway listening to a dozen farmers butcher a roundelay, and when the candles guttered and flared in time with the chorus, an old man beside Yara chuckled and said, "Happens most evenings, if we hit the harmony right," as if reporting on the weather, and Yara had to walk out into the yard and stand among the geese for a while before they could trust their face.

That was the other cost, the one no one had warned them about: the world kept proving them unnecessary. Every harmonious candle, every mended wall, every argument settled by a well-sung word was a small monument to the fact that the age of chosen wielders was over, and good riddance, and thank you, and don't let the gate hit you on the way out. People were kind to them. That was the worst part. They were kind the way you're kind to a veteran of a war everyone's relieved is finished—with warmth, and gratitude, and a subtle, unbearable suggestion that the past tense applied.

In the second month, on a coastal road where the gorse bloomed yellow against grey sea, Yara met a woman walking the other way with a fiddle on her back, and they fell into step, and she turned out to be a former Hunt rider—one of the deaf men trained to feel resonance, disbanded now, walking wherever his feet took him, which happened to be the same direction as hers. His name was Corvan. He couldn't hear the sea at all, he told them cheerfully over a shared fire, but he could feel it through the ground, a vast slow pulse, and he thought that might be better than hearing it. "Everything's still singing," he said. "Always was. We just finally got permission to listen." Yara lay awake long after he slept, looking at the stars, turning the sentence over like a stone with something bright underneath.

*Everything's still singing.* Then why did the road feel so empty?

Because, answered some honest voice deep in them, the thing you miss isn't the singing. It's being needed by it. Ten years hiding, one year hunted, one year carrying the fate of the realm in a child's scarred heart—and now the fate of the realm was distributed evenly among several million people, none of whom needed Yara Eriksen for anything, and the justice of that was absolute and the loneliness of it was bottomless, and both things were true at once, and no one had ever said that justice would feel like this. Like a hand with nothing left to hold.

They walked on.

* * *

Autumn came down the valleys like a slow tide, gilding the birches, smoking the morning air. Yara crossed back into the Thornfells in the last week of the season, drawn north by something they didn't examine too closely—the cave shrine, perhaps, the rite of remembrance, the debt owed to the dead. The hidden roads remembered their feet. Their body knew every turning before their mind did, the smuggler paths opening like sentences half-remembered, and there was a comfort in that, in being known by stone if not by people.

At the shrine, they lit the candles. All of them, this time—not the careful few of the old private rites, but every stub in the niche, forty small flames for the forty names they still carried, and then, after a long moment, a forty-first for Brannoc, who was alive, who deserved a candle for living as much as the others for dying. The mountain air moved the flames gently. Yara sat down on the cold floor with their back against the stone and spoke aloud, in their frayed voice, the names, one by one, and found that it hurt less than it used to, and that this too was a kind of loss—who were they, if the grief was fading? Grief had been load-bearing for a decade.

When the last name was spoken, they sat in the dark a while longer, listening to the mountain breathe.

Then they rose, and shouldered their pack, and walked out into the early snow.

The pass south of the shrine was the one where they'd found her. Yara knew it before they saw the frozen stream, before the leaning pine—knew it in their knees, in the specific angle of the wind. They stood at the top of it for a long time, looking down at the white curve where a small figure had lain in the snow a lifetime ago, a child with a seal scar and no name, and Yara had stood in this exact spot weighing her life against their safety and finding, to their permanent surprise, that the scales had already tipped.

The wind came up the pass, as it always did, moving through the pines in a long silver rush.

And underneath the wind—faint, so faint it might have been memory, might have been the mountain itself remembering—Yara heard singing.

A harvest round. Off-key. Carried on the wind from impossibly far away, from a farmhouse south of Greyhollow where a girl was learning bread and letters and the shape of her own unsealed voice, singing to the wheat, or to the cows, or to no one, the way children sing, the way everyone sang now, weak and ordinary and utterly free.

Yara stood in the snow and listened until the wind shifted and the song was gone—or folded into the wind, indistinguishable, everywhere.

Then they turned south, tightened the straps of their pack, and started walking, and did not stop.
