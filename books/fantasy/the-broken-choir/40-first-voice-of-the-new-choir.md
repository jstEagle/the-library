## 40. First Voice of the New Choir

The harvest came in golden and heavy, bending the stalks along the terraces above Greyhollow until the whole valley looked like a sea that had decided, after long wandering, to lie down and rest.

Merrin stood at the edge of the threshing floor with a basket of early apples on her hip and watched the villagers gather for the round. A year ago—she measured time now in years, though she had only eleven of them, and the first eight were a locked room she had learned to stop knocking on—a year ago this square had been ash and splinters. The King's men had burned the eastern quarter during the Hunt, and then the Hunt itself had burned when the truth went through Greyfall like fire through dry grass, and for a season Greyhollow had been a town of tents and quarrels and people staring at each other across cookfires, each wondering what the person beside them remembered.

Now there were roofs again. New timber, pale as bone against the older beams. A new well-head carved with wheat sheaves instead of the crown-and-choir sigil that no one wanted anymore. And on the threshing floor, forty-some voices lifting the harvest round into the blue autumn air.

It was not a beautiful song. That was the thing Merrin had come to love about it. The old songs—the ones that lived behind her ribs, the ones Brannoc said she would carry whether she wanted them or not—those were beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful, all purpose and edge. The harvest round was rough. Old Tam's voice cracked on the high part and always had. The miller's twin daughters kept sliding into harmony a half-beat late, grinning at each other when they caught it. Someone's dog howled along from the edge of the crowd and nobody shooed it off.

And yet.

When they reached the third verse—the verse where the singers name the fields, one by one, so the land knows it is seen—something flickered through the crowd like sun through moving water. Merrin felt it in her teeth. A warmth in the air, faint as breath on a cold window. The wheat in the unthreshed stacks seemed to lean, very slightly, toward the singers. Old Tam's bad knee, which he complained about from first frost to planting, straightened with an audible pop, and he laughed mid-line and lost his place and the round wobbled and righted itself the way water rights itself around a stone.

No one commanded it. No one bound it. It simply rose out of forty ordinary throats, did its small work, and settled back down like a bird coming to roost.

That was the Dissolution. That was what Yara had bought with their voice, standing in the broken heart of the palace, singing the whole memory into ten thousand minds at once. Merrin had been there. She had opened her own seal willingly, had let the founding memory pour through her—not stolen, not hoarded, but *given*, given to every soul in Greyfall simultaneously, the first king's murder and the Choir's grief and two hundred years of borrowed breath all arriving in every head at once like weather. She remembered the silence afterward. Not the silence of emptiness but the silence of a held note finally allowed to end.

She had been eight years old and empty, then. Sealed, nameless, a vessel walking through snow. Now she was nine—Brannoc had determined her birthday by the scar's healing, to within a fortnight, and declared it a festival—and she was full, but full the way a cup is full, not the way a dam is full. What lived in her could be shared. That was the whole difference. That was everything.

"Merrin!" Brannoc's voice carried over the crowd, gravelled and warm. He stood by the cider press with his sleeves rolled up, and even from here she could see the tremor in his hands that the cells beneath the palace had left him, the tremor that never quite went away. He didn't hide it anymore. He said hiding things was how the last age got built. "You're supposed to be *helping*, apprentice, not communing with the wheat."

"I'm observing," she called back, using his own word against him, and the nearby villagers laughed, because everyone knew Brannoc's apprenticeship rules: observe, record, rest, and never—*never*—sing alone what can be sung together.

She threaded through the crowd to him. People touched her shoulder as she passed—not deference; she'd hated that at first and Brannoc had put a stop to it with a speech about vessels and hubris—but the easy touch of people who knew her. The cooper's wife pressed a honey cake into her hand. One of the Kestrel riders, wintering in the valley, winked at her. Sera Kestrel herself sat on a hay bale near the press, boots crossed at the ankle, watching the round with the expression of a woman auditing a debt she considered paid in full.

"You'll sing the closing," Sera said. It wasn't a question.

"She will," Brannoc said. "If she wants."

"I want," Merrin said, and found it was true. A year ago the words *I want* had felt like contraband, goods smuggled out of someone else's house. Now they were just hers.

* * *

The closing of the harvest round was traditionally sung by whoever had lost the most to the last winter, so that the year's grief got a place in the song instead of under the floorboards. This year there had been no famine, no purge, no burning. There had been a hard March and a flooded millrace and old Widow Corren passing in her sleep, mourned properly and at length. So the choosing had caused an argument, and the argument had ended with the village deciding that since no one had lost enough, the closing should go to the two people who had lost the most in the *old* winters: the apprentice and the healer.

They sang it together, Brannoc's ruined baritone and Merrin's clear child's voice, the old cadence that had once been forbidden on pain of death and was now just a tune people knew, the way they knew the road to the mill. Around them the small gifts flickered again—someone's bandaged finger knitting faster than it should, the cider barrel settling its lees without being told, a moment of perfect stillness in a squabbling pair of brothers who would swear forever after that they couldn't remember why they'd been fighting. Harmless. Small. Strong only in chorus, weak alone, exactly as designed.

Exactly as Yara had designed it, on the worst day of their life, with their voice tearing like wet cloth.

Merrin held the last note longer than the tune strictly required, and let it go gently, and the crowd breathed out all together, and the festival spilled into eating.

* * *

Evening came down the valley in bands of copper and violet. Lanterns bloomed along the new-built street. Merrin escaped the feast—Brannoc pretended not to see, which was his way of granting permission—and climbed the path behind the shrine.

The shrine had been rebuilt too. Smaller than before, humbler: a cave mouth walled with fieldstone, a lintel of mountain oak, and inside, no idol at all. Just names. Hundreds of them, cut into the stone walls by anyone who came with grief or gratitude—the dead of the Choir, the dead of the Hunt, the dead of the war that hadn't happened, thanks to a choice made on a frozen pass by a smuggler who had every reason to keep walking.

Merrin lit the lamp. She read a few names, as she always did. Then she did what she always did last: she stepped out onto the ledge below the shrine, where the wind came up off the high passes, and she listened.

This was her private rite now. Brannoc knew about it and approved, in his grudging way. *The gift is gone from the chosen,* he liked to say, *but listening is not a gift. Listening is a discipline.* So she listened.

The wind off the Thornfells carried everything up here if you gave it time: the feast below, a cart on the far road, the river arguing with its stones. And sometimes—rarely, three times in a year, four—something else. Something thin and far and unmistakable.

She heard it tonight.

A voice, riding the wind down from the high passes where the smugglers' roads wound, a voice singing fragments of a children's round—the very round Yara had learned from her, that blizzard week, badly, patiently, note by note. The voice was rougher than it used to be. Damaged, the healers said, perhaps forever; the price of the unbinding, the cost counted honestly, as Yara had insisted on counting everything. But it was carrying. It was carrying beautifully.

Merrin closed her eyes and let the sound wash over her and did not cry, because she had promised herself she wouldn't, and because it wasn't sad. It was the opposite of sad. It was a voice on a far pass, still walking, still free, still singing a song a child taught it, and somewhere out there in the wide world the last of the oath-bound swordspeakers was doing whatever Yara Eriksen did now—which was, as far as anyone knew, walking, fixing small wrongs quietly, refusing thrones, refusing titles, refusing the letters that arrived monthly from three different provisional councils begging them to *please come lead something.*

Yara sent no replies. But twice a year, sometimes, a package arrived at Brannoc's door with no return mark: mountain honey, a knife sharpener, once a small carved bird that Merrin kept on her windowsill. Proof of life. Proof of the road.

The voice faded as the wind shifted. Merrin stayed on the ledge a while longer, arms wrapped around her knees, watching the first stars come out over the peaks. Somewhere past those peaks, past the Nine Bends and the Ferrywoman's rebuilt crossing and the low country where the new councils argued in loud healthy voices about boring important things, Yara was walking. Would keep walking. Might come down out of the hills someday, might not. Hope without certainty—that was the shape of it, and Merrin had learned that this shape could hold a whole life if you let it.

She stood, brushed off her dress, and paused.

Because the wind had shifted again, and brought one more thing with it.

Not a song this time. Words. Faint, torn by distance, but clear enough—clear enough that her breath caught, and her hand flew to the seal scar over her heart, which had been quiet for a year and now, for one heartbeat, warmed like a banked coal.

From the high pass, thin as thread, a voice calling down the wind:

*"Merrin! If you can hear me—come see what I found."*

Then nothing. Just wind, and stars, and the feast-lanterns glowing far below like earthbound constellations.

Merrin stood very still on the ledge, nine years old, apprentice to a healer, first voice of a choir that had no chosen ones—only neighbors. Her heart was hammering. Her scar was warm. And already, without deciding it, she was smiling.

She turned and ran down the path toward the light, shouting for Brannoc, shouting for her pack, shouting the best news of her short loud life into the gathering dark:

"She's found something! Yara's found something—and she wants us to come!"
