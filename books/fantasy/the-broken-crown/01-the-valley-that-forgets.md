## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats knew before she did.

Zephyrine felt it in the way they lifted their heads at once, all seven of them, ears swiveling toward the eastern ridge like a congregation hearing the first note of an organ. She followed their gaze and saw the clouds coming over the shoulder of the mountain — fat, gray, ordinary clouds, dragging their bellies across the sky the way they had every autumn of the ten years she'd lived in the Vale.

Rain, then. Just rain.

She said it out loud sometimes, to hear how it sounded. *Just rain.* The words still fit strangely in her mouth, like shoes borrowed from someone with different feet. In Veyra, weather had never been just anything. Weather had been hers. When the storm-fronts rolled off the Sea of Glass, the king's heralds would ride through the streets crying her name — not her family name, which was common as bread, but the name they'd made for her: *the Storm-Caller, Zephyrine Nystrom, Shield of the Realm.* And she'd stand on the seawall with her arms raised and pull the lightning down before it could strike the fishing fleet, and ten thousand people would cheer, and she would feel — this was the part she never told anyone — she would feel like a door that something vast kept opening through.

Here in the Vale, nothing opened through her. Here, when thunder came, it came because warm air met cold air over the mountains, and it did not ask her permission, and it did not need her. The first summer, she had climbed to the high pasture during a storm and stood with her arms raised until her shoulders burned, waiting for the old communion, the rush of the sky pouring into her like water into a cup.

Nothing. The lightning struck a pine two hundred yards away and killed it, indifferent as a falling stone. She had sat down in the wet grass and laughed until the laughing turned into something else, and after that she had stopped climbing to the high pasture in storms.

That was the bargain of the Vale. Everyone knew it. Magic could not follow you across the boundary stones. Whatever you had been beyond them — sorcerer, witch, warlock, weapon — inside the valley you were only flesh. The elders said it plainly, the first day they took her in: *You will be safe here. You will also be less. Decide if you can live with both.*

She had decided. Ten years ago, bleeding and half-mad with grief, hunted by three kingdoms and her own conscience, she had decided. And for ten years the decision had held, mostly, the way a dam holds — visibly, effortfully, with small leaks nobody mentioned.

She gathered the goats and started down toward the village, and the rain caught her halfway there, warm and unhurried, drumming on the brim of her hat. Behind her, on the ridge, the dead pine from that first summer had finally fallen sometime in the spring, and now lay where it had landed, going soft, feeding beetles. Even the tree had gotten more use out of her failure than she had.

* * *

The festival banners were already up when she reached the village square, red and gold cloth strung between the eaves of the chandlery and the millhouse, sagging under the rain. Zephyrine stopped at the edge of the square and looked at them for a long moment, the way you look at a letter you know is bad news but haven't opened yet.

Harvest Home. Of course. It came around every year whether she was ready or not, which was to say never.

She should have remembered. Sefa had been talking about it for a week — the archivist's daughter, nineteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, who came up to the pasture twice a month to copy the old boundary inscriptions and talk Zephyrine's ear off about valley history. *You have to come this year,* Sefa had said, crouched over her rubbing in the mud by the shrine wall, ink-stained fingers tracing letters older than anyone could read. *Elder Maren says the whole valley turns out. There'll be dancing. There'll be the lantern boats on the millpond. You can't spend another Harvest Home alone with your goats, Zephyrine, it's morbid.*

*I like my goats,* Zephyrine had said. *They don't ask questions.*

But Sefa had a way of getting under things. *Nobody here asks questions either,* she'd said, not looking up. *That's what you like about us, isn't it? We're the valley that forgets.*

Zephyrine hadn't answered. The girl wasn't wrong, exactly. The Vale forgot — that was its nature, or near enough. People drifted in over the mountains carrying whatever they carried, and the valley's quiet settled over them like snow, and within a few years even they couldn't quite say what they'd been running from. There were men in the village who had been soldiers; you could tell by the way they stood, but if you asked them about their wars they'd frown like you'd interrupted a dream. There was a woman at the weavery who sometimes sang in a language no one recognized, and when asked, she'd smile vaguely and say it was just a tune that got stuck in her head.

The Vale didn't erase anything, the elders insisted. It only let things rest.

Zephyrine tied the goats in their pen behind the cottage and stood in the doorway watching the rain come down harder, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the weather changing.

* * *

She went to the festival. She never meant to, and she always went.

It was the lantern boats that did it, the way they always did. She arrived after dark, when the rain had thinned to mist and the square was thick with people — farmers and weavers and the two brothers who ran the smithy, everyone flushed with cider, children running wild between legs with paper crowns on their heads. Someone had built a bonfire big enough to be seen from the ridges, and the heat of it pushed back against the autumn cold, and the fiddlers were playing something in six-eight time that made even the old men tap their boots.

Zephyrine bought a cup of mulled cider she didn't want, just to have something to do with her hands, and stood at the edge of the firelight being nobody, which was a skill she had refined to an art.

Then the lantern boats came out.

They launched them on the millpond at full dark — little wooden hulls no bigger than a loaf of bread, each one carrying a candle and a slip of paper. You wrote a wish on the paper, or a grief, or a name, and you set it afloat, and the current took it out toward the sluice gate, and in the morning the warden of the pond collected them and burned them unread. Sending your troubles downstream, the tradition was called. Nobody in the Vale could say who had started it. It was simply old.

The whole village turned out along the banks, hundreds of candle-flames reflected in the black water, and the singing started — a low, wordless melody that rose and fell like breathing. Zephyrine stood among them with her untouched cider and watched the little lights drift away, and thought: *I could write Veyra on a slip of paper. I could write the names. I could write what I did, and set it on the water, and tomorrow it would burn, and no one would ever—*

"Zephyrine!"

Sefa appeared out of the crowd, breathless, cheeks pink, a garland of late marigolds crooked around her neck. She grabbed Zephyrine's sleeve with both hands like a child claiming a prize. "You came! I told Elder Maren you'd come. She didn't believe me."

"Elder Maren is a wise woman," Zephyrine said.

"She's a stubborn woman." Sefa towed her toward the fire, chattering — the lantern count was up this year, forty more than last, Elder Tomas said it meant good luck, though Sefa privately thought it just meant more people had troubles, which was statistically inevitable — and Zephyrine let herself be towed, and for a while, an hour maybe, it almost worked. The warmth of the fire. The fiddles. A child asked her to lift him so he could see the boats, and she lifted him, and his weight against her hip was so startlingly human that something in her chest cracked open a finger's width.

This could be enough, she thought. This could be the whole of it. Goats and festivals and a girl who talked too much and rain that was only rain. Ten years of safety, purchased at the price of everything she'd been, and surely at some point the account balanced. Surely at some point you stopped reaching for a door that wasn't there.

Then the child's mother called him away, and Sefa got pulled into a dance by a miller's boy with flour in his hair, and Zephyrine was alone at the edge of the firelight again, holding a cold cup, watching the last lantern boats go out toward the sluice.

And the ache came back. It always came back at exactly this moment, the moment the festival proved it could nearly hold her. Because here was the truth she circled every year and never landed on: safety was not the same as peace. Safety was a room with the door bricked shut. It kept the wolf out, yes. It also kept out the wind, and the sea-smell, and the sound of your own name spoken by someone who knew what it meant.

Somewhere beyond those mountains, the world was happening. Kingdoms rising, kingdoms burning, storms rolling off the Sea of Glass that no one was calling down, that no one needed her for. Somewhere, people she had failed were remembering her — or worse, forgetting her, letting her fade into rumor, into a story told badly. And she was here, tending goats in a valley that let things rest, growing older by one harvest festival at a time, safe as a coin buried in the ground.

A coin isn't safe, she thought. A coin is just spent slowly.

The last boat reached the sluice gate and its candle guttered and went out, and the crowd sighed — that soft collective exhale, troubles sent downstream — and began to break apart toward home. Sefa waved to her across the dying fire, mouthed *next year*, and Zephyrine smiled and nodded and hated herself a little for nodding.

She walked back up the hill to the cottage in the dark, past the boundary stones with their worn inscriptions, and the mist moved around her without stirring, and the night stayed perfectly, obediently calm, because in the Vale the sky knew better than to answer her.

She was halfway to her door when she saw the light.

Down at the treeline, at the very edge of the valley where the pines went dark against the stars — a flicker. Not lantern-light. Not bonfire-light. Something whiter, jerkier, like a signal mirror catching sun where there was no sun. It pulsed twice, three times, and went out.

Zephyrine stood in the wet grass with her heart suddenly loud in her ears, and every instinct she had spent ten years burying sat up at once like a dog hearing its name.

No one came to the treeline at night. No one signaled. The Vale had no enemies worth signaling, and no friends close enough to try.

The light pulsed again — weaker now, desperate now — and she was already moving, already running down through the pasture with the cold air cutting her lungs, before she had decided anything at all.
