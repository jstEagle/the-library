## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The goats found him before I did.

I heard them first — that particular bleating they do when something is wrong in their small world, a sound I'd learned to read the way other shepherds read weather. Not the complaint of a fly or a stolen mouthful of grass. The alarm note. I dropped my crook against a fence post and ran up the eastern slope with my skirts gathered in one fist, and the Vale spread out behind me like a held breath: green fields, whitewashed houses, the festival banners already going up in Harrow's Field for the Longlight.

The goats had ringed themselves around a shape at the treeline.

He was face-down in the bracken, half across the boundary stones, as if he'd been crawling toward the valley and his body had simply quit a few feet short of mercy. He wore no livery I recognized — just road-stained wool and a courier's oilskin satchel, the kind riders carry when what they're carrying matters more than they do. His boots were ruined. His hands were ruined worse, torn from crawling over stone.

I should have called for help. That was the sensible thing, the Vale thing to do. Ten years in this valley teaches you the liturgy of sensibility: fetch the healer, send for the elders, don't touch anything strange.

Instead I knelt beside him, because I saw his wrist.

There was a mark there, inked into the skin beneath the grime and dried blood — a spiral broken by three horizontal strokes, like a storm seen through a shuttered window. I knew it. I had worn it once myself, painted on my collarbone in silver before the Rite of Calling, back when I still had a home to be branded by. It was a Veyran sigil. The mark of the storm-wardens' messenger corps.

Nobody outside Veyra used it. Nobody *could* use it; the ink was bound, keyed to bloodlines, useless in any other hand. Which meant this man was either Veyran —

— or he had killed someone who was and taken the mark off their corpse.

"Hey." I turned him over. He was young, younger than me, maybe twenty-five, with a beard grown wild around a face gone gray as ash. His lips were cracked. When he breathed it sounded like wind through a broken fence. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. They were the pale gray of rain clouds, and for a moment they fixed on me with an intensity that made my spine go cold, because he wasn't looking at a stranger. He was looking at *me*. At something specific.

"You're her," he rasped. "The storm-caller."

Every hair on my arms stood up. "You're mistaken. I'm a goatherd."

"Nystrom." My name came out of him like a stone dropping into a well. "Zephyrine Nystrom. They said — the last maps said you went east. Into the dead valley." A wet laugh rattled out of him. "Of course. Of course you'd hide where we can't follow."

I looked over my shoulder at the Vale. The banners in Harrow's Field were tiny bright stitches. Someone was laughing down by the well. The whole valley sat in its bowl of hills like a sleeping child, safe and smug and utterly unaware.

"Who sent you?" I asked quietly.

"Doesn't matter." He tried to push himself up and failed, coughing, and when he pulled his hand away from his mouth there was blood on it, dark and arterial. "Doesn't matter anymore. Listen. Listen to me, because I don't have long and you don't have time."

"I'm getting the healer—"

"*No.*" His hand shot out and caught my wrist with a strength that startled me. His grip was ice and iron. "No healers. No elders. If the Wardens learn the map woke, everything — everyone — is lost. Do you understand? There are men following me. Gray men. They'll be here by tomorrow's dusk, maybe sooner. You have to be gone before they come."

"Wardens," I repeated. The word tasted like copper. I hadn't heard it in ten years, and hearing it now was like hearing your own name spoken at a funeral. "There are no Wardens anymore. There's nothing left to ward. Veyra fell."

Something moved across his face — grief, yes, but underneath it something worse. Pity.

"That's what they wanted you to think," he said. "That's what they wanted *everyone* to think."

* * *

I got him under the rowan tree at the slope's shoulder, out of the sun, and gave him water from my flask in small sips while he talked in fragments, the way dying men talk — a sentence here, a sentence there, gaps between like missing teeth. I kept glancing at the treeline. The forest beyond the boundary stones was old growth, dark even at midday, and it had always seemed to me like a wall. Now it felt like a door left ajar.

"Ten years ago," he said, "when they exiled you — do you remember what the decree said?"

"I remember every word." I said it flatly, but my hands weren't steady. "Treason. Unlicensed calling. The destruction of the northern fleet. Blah, blah, blah. I've had a decade to memorize my own indictment."

"The fleet." He nodded slowly. "The fleet burned. The king died. And then—" He stopped. Coughed. Started again. "And then Veyra didn't fall. Veyra *stopped*. One night, and every road leading in led nowhere. Every map went blank at the border. Traders forgot the way home. Ambassadors arrived at court asking after a country no one had ever heard of. The conquerors — the Karsite coalition, the ones your war was losing to — they marched on Veyra and found only forest. Empty forest, older than any record."

"That's not possible."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But it happened. The mages did it. The great working, the one they'd sworn was impossible — they unmade the country. Not the land. The land's still there. The *idea* of it. The memory. They cut Veyra out of the world's mind and sewed the wound shut, and everyone inside has been living in the fold of that seam ever since." His cloud-gray eyes searched my face. "And everyone outside forgot there was ever anything to mourn. Except the ones who couldn't forget. The exiles. The ones already cast out, like you. Like me."

"My family," I said. My voice came out very small. "My mother. My brother. Are they—"

"Alive. As far as I know. Everyone inside is alive." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was full of apology. "That's the horror of it, storm-caller. They're all alive. They just don't remember being anyone. The erasure doesn't stop at the border. It works inward, year by year, name by name. First the country forgot itself. Now the people are forgetting themselves. Surnames first. Then given names. Then faces. In another generation there'll be nothing left in there but smiling shapes, tending fields, waiting for a king none of them can describe."

The water flask slipped in my hand. I caught it against my knee.

"Why tell me?" I managed. "Why me? I'm nobody. I'm less than nobody — I'm the traitor who burned the fleet. If Veyra's mages could unmake a country, surely they can fix whatever's wrong without the help of their favorite criminal."

"They're all dead," he said simply. "Or sealed inside with the rest. The working took everything they had. There's no one left who can undo it from within — and no one outside remembers enough to try." He fumbled at the clasp of his oilskin satchel with fingers that wouldn't obey him. "Except you. Because you were outside when it happened. Because you're Veyran to the bone and the magic knows it. And because—" he laughed again, that terrible wet laugh, "—because you're the strongest storm-caller the bloodline ever produced, and the Door of Returning answers to storms. It always has. That's why they exiled you, Zephyrine. Not for treason. For *this*. They saw it coming. They knew someday someone would need to call the storm home, and they couldn't risk the Wardens finding you first."

"What Wardens?" I grabbed his shoulder. "What Wardens, damn you? Tell me plainly."

"Hollow Wardens. The erasure's keepers. Mages who survived the working by giving themselves to it — they patrol the edges of the forgetting, hunting anything that remembers. Maps. Songs. People." His eyes flicked to mine. "People like me. People like you. They've spent ten years making sure the exiles stay scattered and silent. And now the map has woken, and they will come for it, and for whoever holds it."

"The map," I said. "What map?"

He pressed the satchel into my hands.

* * *

It was lighter than I expected. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age and handling, was a folded square of heavy vellum, and when I opened it my breath stopped.

It was a map of the lands east of the Vale — the lands I had crossed a decade ago, half-dead, half-mad, fleeing the soldiers who'd escorted me to the edge of nowhere and told me never to look back. I knew those roads. I knew the river crossing where I'd nearly drowned, the shrine where I'd slept in a hayrick, the pass through the Teeth. All of it was inked in fine, sure lines.

But past the eastern edge of the chart, where every map I'd ever seen dissolved into blank parchment and the cartographer's honest confession of *here be nothing*, this map continued. Faintly. Shiftingly. Lines that seemed to move when I wasn't looking directly at them, roads that rearranged themselves between one blink and the next, and at the center of it all a symbol drawn in faded gold: a door, standing open, with a storm rising behind it.

"It redraws itself," the courier whispered. "It follows the truth, and the truth keeps moving. The old cartographer — Aldric Vane — he made seven of them before the Wardens caught him. This is the last. It cost eleven people their lives to get it this far east." He swallowed, and I watched the swallow hurt him. "It cost me my brother."

"Why give it to me?" I asked, though some cold, clear part of me already knew. Some part of me had known since I saw the sigil on his wrist. "Why not burn it? Why not let it end?"

"Because Veyra needs its storm back." He said it the way men say prayers — worn smooth, carried a long way, meant for someone else to answer. "The Door of Returning is the seam in the working. The one place the erasure is thin. Open it, and the memory comes back — all of it, everything they buried. The names. The songs. The country itself." His hand rose, trembling, and closed around my wrist above my pulse. "But the Door only opens for a storm. A true calling. The bloodline's own weather. There's no one left, Zephyrine. No one but you."

"I can't." The words came out of me before I could weigh them, raw and reflexive. "You don't understand what I am. What I did. They didn't exile me for being strong, they exiled me because I *burned the fleet* — four hundred men, I killed them in nine minutes, I was twenty-two years old and I killed four hundred men because I was angry and afraid and the storm came when I called it and it didn't *stop*—"

"I know," he said.

"—and you want me to walk back into the thing I destroyed and open a door with the same power that—"

"I know what you did." His grip tightened. Blood flecked his lips. "I've read the trial records. I walked six hundred miles carrying them. You want to know why the records matter?" His cloud-gray eyes held mine, and there was no pity in them now, only a fierce, exhausted certainty. "Because the fleet you burned was carrying the Karsite invasion force. The one that would have reached Veyra's coast two days later. You didn't destroy your country, storm-caller. You delayed its murder by ten years. Long enough for the mages to finish the working. Long enough for your mother to forget your name instead of watching her city burn."

The world went very quiet. Somewhere below us, a goat bleated. The festival banners snapped in a wind that smelled of cut grass and baking bread, and I sat under a rowan tree holding a self-redrawing map, and I could not feel my hands.

"You're lying," I said. "You're lying to make me go."

"I'm dying," he said. "Dying men don't lie. We just finally say things plainly." He sagged back against the rowan's roots, and the fierceness drained out of him, leaving only weariness and a strange, light relief, like a man setting down a load he'd carried too far. "The Wardens will reach the valley by tomorrow. Maybe tonight. They can't enter — nothing of the working can cross the boundary stones — but they'll wait. They'll wait years. They're patient the way graves are patient. So you have a choice, and it's the same choice you've always had: stay in the valley where nothing can touch you, or walk out and be what you were made to be."

"I was made to be a weapon."

"You were made to be a *storm*," he said. "Weapons are what other people turn storms into."

I looked down at the map. The golden door seemed to shimmer at the edge of my vision, and the roads around it had shifted again — I was almost certain they had — drawing themselves toward something, or away from something, or perhaps simply breathing.

When I looked up, he was watching me with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors during my first year of exile: the look of someone letting go of a rope they've been gripping so long their hands have grown around it.

"There's a woman," he said. "In the capital. Her name is Maerin. She runs a candle shop near the old palace steps, except she doesn't remember it's the old palace steps, she thinks it's always been a candle shop. Before the working she was my—" His voice caught. "She was going to marry me. I got out on the last night, carrying dispatches. I thought I'd be back in a month." He smiled at the sky through the rowan branches. "I've spent ten years walking toward a country that isn't there. Don't do what I did, storm-caller. Don't spend ten years. Go now."

"Tell me your name," I said. "If I'm to carry this — tell me your name, so I remember you."

He considered that. Something like amusement touched his ruined face.

"Tamsin Hale," he said. "Courier, third rank, storm-wardens' service. Sworn to the crown of Veyra, which no longer exists, in service to a king whose name I'm starting to lose." He closed his eyes. "Funny. I used to be able to see his face."

"Tamsin." I said it firmly, like driving a stake. "Tamsin Hale. I'll remember it."

"Will you?" His eyes opened halfway. "Then you'd better hurry. Memory's a country too, and it's losing ground everywhere."

* * *

He died an hour before sunset, with his head against the rowan's roots and his hand still loosely touching the map in my lap, as if even at the end he was making sure of it. I closed his eyes and straightened his collar, because whatever else he'd been, he'd been Veyran, and so was I, and we are a formal people even in grief.

Then I sat in the long amber light and did the arithmetic of my life.

Behind me: the Vale. Ten years of safety measured out in goat milk and festival bread and the elders' gentle, suffocating kindness. A valley where my storms were only weather, where lightning was a summer inconvenience and thunder was something you slept through. A place where I was nobody, which I had told myself was the same as being free.

Ahead of me: a treeline, darkening now, and beyond it a world I had fled with the taste of ash in my mouth. Hollow Wardens. A door. A storm that had been waiting inside me for ten years like a banked fire, patient and hungry, and God help me, when I thought of calling it — when I let myself imagine, just for one forbidden second, the sky answering me again — I didn't feel dread.

I felt homesick.

That was the worst of it. Not fear. Homesickness. For a country that no longer existed, for a version of myself I had every reason to despise, for the crackle and weight of the storm sitting in my chest like a second heart.

Down in Harrow's Field, the first lanterns of the Longlight festival were being lit. I could hear faint music. Someone would notice soon that I hadn't come down with the goats. Someone would climb the slope, find the fresh-turned earth I'd have to explain, the blood I'd have to wash from my hands, and the elders would ask their kind, careful questions, and I would stand there in the lantern light and realize that safety, it turns out, is just a smaller cage with softer bars.

I looked at the map one more time. The roads had settled, and now they ran unmistakably east, out of the blank margins of the world, toward the little golden door.

Veyra needs its storm back.

"All right," I said aloud, to Tamsin Hale, to the map, to the treeline, to whatever was listening on the other side of it. "All right. But I'm telling you now — I'm not the hero of this story. I'm the reason it needs one."

The wind came down off the eastern hills and stirred the rowan's leaves, and for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat I would think about for the rest of my life — the wind tasted of ozone and salt and the sea off Veyra's coast, ten years and six hundred miles away.

Then it passed, and the valley smelled of grass again, and I stood up, and began to plan how to steal out of the only safe place I had ever known.
