## 10. An Alliance of Liars

Kestrel had been following the storm-caller for three days before she understood that she was no longer hunting her.

That was the trouble with the erasure. It worked on everything — roads, names, the color of a river in autumn — but it could not work on a Warden's memory, because a Warden's memory was the lock, not the door. Kestrel remembered Veyra the way a scar remembers the knife: precisely, uselessly, and always.

She crouched in the bracken above the old drover's road and watched Zephyrine Nystrom make camp. The exile moved like someone who had spent ten years learning to be small. She built her fire low, fed it dry wood so it wouldn't smoke, and sat with her back against a stone and the map spread across her knees, and she read it the way you'd read a letter from someone you loved who had died badly.

Kestrel had orders. She had carried them for ten years, folded into her chest beside the memory of Veyra's green hills and the smell of bread on a market morning in Serevan, and the orders were simple: nothing leaves the erasure. No map. No witness. No storm.

The map had woken three days ago. She had felt it the way you feel a tooth begin to ache — a faint wrongness at the edge of the world, ink remembering itself. The Wardens' network had lit up like a struck bell, and Kestrel had ridden east through country that was forgetting its own shape, past villages where the wells had no names and the churchyards were full of stones that said only *here lies*.

And now here she was, forty paces from the woman whose storms had ended the war, watching her read a dead country by firelight, and Kestrel's hand would not go to her sword.

*One more time,* she thought. That was all. One more look at something real.

She despised herself for it with a thoroughness that felt almost like comfort. Self-contempt was an old coat; she wore it well.

* * *

The problem with confronting a storm-caller, Kestrel reflected as she stepped out of the treeline into the firelight, was that there was no elegant way to do it.

Zephyrine came off the stone fast — faster than Kestrel expected, faster than a woman should move after three days of hard road — and lightning gathered around her fingers before Kestrel had finished her first step. It crackled blue-white along her knuckles and lit her face from below, and Kestrel saw the terror in it, and the hunger, and knew both of them exactly.

"Warden," Zephyrine said. Her voice was rough. "How long have you been watching me?"

"Three days." Honesty first. It disarmed people; they expected lies from Wardens, and the truth made them doubt their own training. "Since the treeline. Longer than that, really. Since the map woke."

"Then you know I won't give it up."

"I know you nearly killed a boy two nights ago." Kestrel kept her hands loose at her sides, open, empty. The boy's face still came to her at night — sixteen maybe, Warden-sworn for a year, sobbing into the mud with his heart stuttering wrong under Kestrel's palm while his sergeant screamed for water. "He'll live. He'll never march again. His name is Aldric Vane. He wanted to be a cartographer before the Wardens took him."

Something flickered across the storm-caller's face. Guilt, or its cousin. Good. Guilt meant the stories about her were wrong, or at least incomplete.

"You've come to take the map," Zephyrine said.

"I've come because if I don't, another Warden will. One who follows orders better." Kestrel let that sit. "There are eleven of us left, Nystrom. We were twelve. Warden Sorrel went into the fog last spring and didn't come out, and the fog doesn't explain itself."

"And you're telling me this why?"

Because I have been standing in the dark for ten years holding a door shut, and tonight my arms are tired. Because I want to ask you what the map says. Because I dreamed about my sister's kitchen last night and when I woke up I couldn't remember whether the table was oak or pine, and I am going mad by inches, and you are walking toward the only place in the world where any of it might be put back.

What she said was: "Sit down, storm-caller. I'm not going to fight you in the dark. And you're not going to fight me, because you can't — not without killing me, and you've already proven this week how much you enjoy that."

The lightning guttered. Zephyrine's jaw tightened, but she sat, slowly, keeping the map in her lap and her body angled so the fire was never between them. A soldier's habit. Kestrel approved of it and hated that she approved of it.

She sat too, across the flames, close enough to see the map's edges curling in the heat.

* * *

Up close, the map was worse than she'd imagined.

Kestrel had seen the charts the Wardens kept — the official ones, the ones that showed Veyra as a blank space with a border drawn around it and a single word in the margin: *settled*. This was not that. This was a living thing. The ink moved when you weren't looking directly at it; roads shifted like veins under skin; and there, in the northwest corner, where the capital should have been, the parchment shimmered with a light that wasn't light at all.

"Serevan," Kestrel said quietly. She couldn't help it.

Zephyrine looked up sharply. "You remember it."

"I remember everything." The words came out harder than she intended. "That's what a Warden is. The mages needed anchors — minds that would hold the shape of the country while the erasure took everyone else. Someone has to remember what's being forgotten, or there's nothing to forget *from*. Eleven of us, carrying a whole kingdom in our heads, so that seven hundred thousand people could sleep safely inside a lie."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes." Kestrel turned her hands over, studied the burn scar across her left palm — earned at nineteen, sealing the first boundary stone, her sister waving to her from the far side of it, already beginning to forget. "It is. Would you like to know why we did it anyway?"

The storm-caller said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a nightjar called, once, twice, and fell silent as if embarrassed.

"The Conquest," Kestrel said. "You were there. You ended it — everyone says that, the great storm that broke the siege lines at Redford. What nobody says is what came after. The Emperor's curse-mages. The Unbinding. You've heard of it?"

"I've heard the word."

"It's a plague that eats countries. Not the people — the people it leaves alive, deliberately, broken and useful. It eats the *country*. The language first, then the laws, then the names, then the graves. By the end, Veyra's children would have been born speaking Imperial, kneeling to a god with an Imperial face, walking over our ancestors' bones without knowing they were bones. The curse-mages were already at the borders. We had perhaps a season." Kestrel heard her own voice go flat, recitation-flat, the way it always did when she approached this part. "So the Queen's mages did the only thing left. If the curse eats what is known, make the country unknown. Hide it inside its own forgetting. A wound disguised as nothing. The curse-mages arrived at a border and found no border, no kingdom, no names to curse. They stood in empty grass for a week and went home."

"And the people?"

"Live inside it. Dreaming. Smiling." Kestrel looked at the fire. "Safe."

"You say that like it's a victory."

"I say it like the survivor I am." She met Zephyrine's eyes. "My sister is in there, storm-caller. In the fog. She lives in a village outside Serevan — lived, I mean, ten years ago when I last saw her through the boundary. She has a husband I've never met and a child I've never met, and she doesn't remember me, because I signed the order that took her memory away, and I did it because the alternative was watching the Empire grind her into paste. So yes. I know it's monstrous. I built the monster. Now tell me what your dying courier told you, and why you think tearing the wound open helps anyone."

* * *

To her surprise, Zephyrine told her.

Not all of it — Kestrel could hear the gaps, the places where the exile chose her words like stepping-stones — but most of it. The courier. The sigils. *Veyra needs its storm back.* The map that rearranged itself. The grave of the cartographer, which Zephyrine described in a voice that had gone carefully neutral, and which confirmed everything Kestrel had just said and worse: that the erasure had been a bargain, a choice, a door locked from the inside.

"So you understand," Zephyrine finished. "Your monster isn't a monster. It's a mercy. That's what the grave says. That's what the map says." She laughed, short and bitter. "And I still can't stop reading it. Do you know what that's like? Knowing a thing is wrong and wanting it anyway?"

"Yes," Kestrel said. "Quite well."

The fire burned down. Neither of them fed it. The night pressed in close, and the map lay between them on Zephyrine's knees like a sleeping animal, and Kestrel found herself staring at the shimmer where Serevan should be, and thinking about oak tables, or pine, and her sister's laugh, which she could still hear perfectly, which was the cruelest thing the erasure had left her.

"Give me the map," she said.

"No."

"I'm not going to destroy it." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she couldn't call them back, and she discovered she didn't want to. Ten years of orders, and here they were, dissolving in a campfire's light. "I should. My orders are to destroy it and everyone who's touched it. But I—" Her voice caught. She hated that. She pushed through. "I want to see it. The Door of Returning. Whatever's at the end of your roads. I want to stand in Veyra one more time before I decide what it's worth. Is that treason? Fine. Call it treason. I've been committing it in my head for a decade; doing it on my feet hardly changes the arithmetic."

Zephyrine stared at her. "You're lying. This is a trick. Wardens trick."

"Of course it might be," Kestrel agreed. "You have excellent reasons not to trust me. Here are mine for trusting you: one, you could have killed me the moment I stepped into the light, and didn't. Two, you wept over a boy you tried to kill — don't argue, I was in the trees, I saw your face. Three—" and here she stopped, because three was the true one and the true ones were hardest. "Three, you're the only other person in the world who knows what I know, and I am so tired, Nystrom. I am so tired of being the only one who remembers the rain in Serevan."

Silence. The nightjar called again, farther off.

"The truce holds until the Door," Zephyrine said at last. "After that, we're strangers again. Or enemies. Whichever the truth makes us."

"After the Door," Kestrel agreed.

They shook hands across the fire like merchants closing a deal neither believed in, and Kestrel felt the storm-caller's pulse hammering in her palm, quick and frightened and stubbornly alive, and thought: *we are both liars, and the country we're lying for may not deserve either of us.*

* * *

She took first watch, because she didn't trust herself to sleep near the map, and because watching was easier than dreaming.

Zephyrine slept curled against the stone with the map tucked inside her coat, and even asleep she looked like a woman braced for a blow. Kestrel studied her and tried to fit her to the stories. *The Storm of Redford.* *The Weapon of Veyra.* *The traitor who broke the siege and then broke everything else.* The girl in the stories had eyes like weather and no heart at all. The woman by the fire had cried over a boy soldier and read a dead country's map like scripture and shaken hands on a truce she probably knew was folly.

Which meant the stories were wrong, or the erasure had gotten into them too, sanding the edges off the truth until it fit whatever shape the survivors needed.

Kestrel looked east, toward the gray shimmer on the horizon that wasn't dawn and wasn't weather, toward the place where a kingdom slept inside its own forgetting, and let herself do the thing she never let herself do.

She remembered.

Rain on the tiles of the palace roof. Her sister, seventeen, laughing at her own joke before she'd finished telling it. The bell of the morning market. The smell of cut hay above the river. The Queen's face at the last true council, gray as ash, saying *we will be no one rather than theirs*, and every mage in the room bowing, and Kestrel bowing lowest of all because she was no mage, only a soldier, and soldiers understand surrender better than anyone.

The map stirred in Zephyrine's coat. Kestrel saw the glow leak through the fabric, faint as a heartbeat, and felt it answer something in her own chest — the anchor pulling at the anchored, memory calling to memory across the dark.

*Soon,* she thought, and didn't know if it was a promise or a threat, or to whom.

Then the glow changed. Brightened. The map was waking fully, ink running like spring thaw, and down the valley — south, toward the city with no name — Kestrel saw lights moving through the fog. Lanterns. Many lanterns, in a pattern she recognized from ten years ago and had prayed never to see again.

The Wardens' muster signal. All eleven, converging.

They had found her cold camp from two nights back. They had found the boy, and heard his story, and drawn the obvious conclusion.

Kestrel stood, drew her sword, and threw the scabbard into the fire.

"Wake up, storm-caller," she said. "We're out of time."
