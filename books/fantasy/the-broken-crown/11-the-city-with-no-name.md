## 11. The City With No Name

The road stopped pretending to be a road three miles from the city.

It simply gave up — cobbles dissolving into packed gray earth, then into something like ash, then into nothing at all, as if the ground itself had grown tired of holding a shape. Zephyrine walked where the map said a road had been, and the map was the only thing that remembered. Its ink had grown restless over the last two days, lines crawling like insects disturbed under a stone, and now it pulled in her hands with a gentle, insistent tug, the way a hound pulls a leash toward home.

"There," Sefa said.

The city rose out of the fog the way a ship rises out of a grey sea — first the suggestion of towers, then their bones, then the whole pale mass of it, walled and vast and utterly silent. No smoke from the chimneys. No bells. No dogs. A city of that size should have made a sound, Zephyrine thought, some low collective hum of ten thousand lives. This one made nothing. It sat in the fog like a held breath.

"Does it have a name?" Sefa asked.

Zephyrine looked down at the map. Where the capital should have been labeled — and she knew, from the courier's dying words and from the shape of her own buried childhood, that it had a name, a good one, a name with a river in it — there was only a smooth blank space, the parchment there worn thin as old cloth, as if a thousand thumbs had rubbed at the letters trying to bring them back.

"No," she said. "Not anymore."

* * *

They passed through the gate without challenge, because there was no one to challenge them. The gatehouse stood open, its portcullis raised and rusted into that position, its guard-posts empty. On the wall above the arch, where a city's name would be carved into the stone, the stone itself was smooth. Not defaced. Smooth — as though the masons had never finished their work, though Zephyrine could see, when she ran her fingers over it, the faint ghost of chisel-marks beneath the surface, a hundred letters' worth of intention, erased down to the grain.

"It's warm," Sefa said, her hand flat on the stone beside Zephyrine's. "Why is it warm?"

Zephyrine didn't answer. She was listening to something else, something she hadn't felt in ten years, and the feeling frightened her more than any Warden's horn. Magic. Not the wild crackle of the borderlands, not the map's restless tugging — but magic in the air itself, ambient and thick, like humidity before a storm. It pressed against her skin. It pressed against the place inside her where her storms lived, coiled and sleeping, and it *stirred* them, the way a hand stirred the embers of a banked fire.

*Careful,* she told herself. *You came here to be careful.*

The streets inside the walls were clean. That was the first wrong thing. No refuse, no mud, no litter — but no people sweeping either, no sign that anyone had swept. The cleanliness had the quality of a stage set between performances: everything in its place, nothing lived-in. Shutters hung straight. Laundry lines stood empty. A child's hoop leaned against a doorpost as if its owner had stepped inside a moment ago and would return any minute.

Except the doors didn't open. And the owners didn't come.

"Zephyrine." Sefa had stopped at a fountain in a small square. It was running — water silvering down over a carved figure of a woman holding a broken crown — and gathered around it, sitting on its rim as though it were the most natural thing in the world, were people. A dozen of them. A fishmonger in a stained apron. A woman in a fine gown gone gray at the hem. Two soldiers, armor dulled. An old man holding a lute he never played.

They were all smiling.

"Hello," Sefa said.

The fishmonger looked up. His eyes were the color of the fog. "Good morning," he said warmly, and his voice was kind, and it was also completely empty, a bell with no clapper. "Lovely day in—"

He stopped. His smile stayed exactly where it was, but behind it something reached for a word and found a hole.

"—in the city," he finished.

"Which city?" Sefa asked gently.

The fishmonger's brow creased, a small furrow of effort, like a man trying to recall a dream. "The city," he said, with the mild surprise of someone stating the obvious. "The only one."

Zephyrine stepped closer. Up close she could see that the man's apron was stained with fish-blood that had long since gone gray, that his hands were clean, that his stall — she glanced back down the street — was empty, had perhaps always been empty. He was a fishmonger the way an actor is a king. He was performing a life he no longer had.

"What's your name?" she asked him.

He smiled at her, and for one flickering instant — less than a heartbeat — the smile cracked, and underneath it she saw something that made her chest seize: *terror*. Pure, animal, ancient terror, the look of a man drowning in his own skull, reaching up through the fog toward her with everything he had left.

Then it smoothed over, and he was warm and empty again.

"I'm the fishmonger," he said.

"That's not a name," Sefa whispered.

"It's what I'm for," he said pleasantly.

* * *

They moved deeper into the city, and the deeper they went, the more the dream thickened.

Near the walls, people had been vague — the fishmonger, the silent houses, the empty streets. But toward the city's heart, the fog took on a golden tint, and people appeared in numbers, and they were *doing* things. A market square where vendors sold bread that steamed and fruit that gleamed, though Zephyrine watched a woman buy a pear, bite it, and chew with an expression of mild, unfocused pleasure — and the pear never diminished. A tailor's shop where a man was fitted for a coat that had no sleeves, and the tailor pinned the empty air with great seriousness. A schoolyard where children recited something in unison, and when Zephyrine slowed to listen, the words dissolved before they reached her, like names on the tip of a tongue.

"It's a court," Sefa said quietly. "Look. It's the whole court. They're reenacting it."

She was right. As they climbed the long avenue toward the citadel hill, the scenes around them resolved into something older, more formal. Ladies with fans they didn't open. Guards at doors that led nowhere. A herald standing on a corner who, every few minutes, straightened and announced in ringing tones — "His Majesty, the—" — and then faltered, and smiled, and began again, over and over, a man caught in the moment before a name.

"They're not living," Zephyrine said. "They're rehearsing. A country rehearsing itself." She thought of the map's crawling ink, of the courier's sigils, of the grave they'd left behind in the hills — *I did what love required and I do not ask forgiveness* — and felt the ground of the whole mission tilt beneath her. "The erasure didn't kill them. It put them on stage."

"Is that better or worse?" Sefa asked.

Zephyrine didn't have an answer. She watched a young woman dance with a partner who wasn't there, following steps she must have learned in another life, her face serene, her eyes fog-colored, and decided it might be both, and that both might be unbearable.

* * *

The citadel had no banners. Of course it didn't — banners bear names, and names were the first thing the fog had eaten. But its great doors stood open, and warm gold light spilled out, and music drifted down the steps: a waltz, or the memory of a waltz, played by musicians whose instruments had no strings.

Inside, the throne room had been transformed into a ball.

That was her first thought — *a ball* — and her second thought was that it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and her third thought, arriving cold and quick on the heels of the other two, was that it was a cage painted to look like one.

Chandeliers burned with light that cast no shadows. Gowns of silver and deep blue swept across a floor that had been polished, impossibly, by no one. Old men with dukes' postures bowed to ladies whose titles had dissolved but whose courtesies remained, worn smooth and bright from endless use, like river stones. At the far end of the hall, on a dais beneath a canopy of gray silk, sat an empty throne — and beside it, a smaller chair, and beside that, a smaller one still, a whole descending row of chairs for a royal family whose names no one could say, all of them empty, all of them facing a room full of people who bowed to them anyway.

"They've been doing this a long time," Sefa murmured. "Look at the floor. Look at the *wear*."

She was right. The flagstones before the throne were worn into shallow dips, the way temple steps wear under centuries of pilgrims' knees. The gowns, for all their splendor, were mended — beautifully, invisibly mended, but mended. The music had the slightly frayed quality of a tune played ten thousand times.

"How long?" Zephyrine asked aloud, and an old woman nearby turned her fog-colored eyes and smiled.

"How long has the ball been?" the woman said. Her voice was warm. Her voice was a bell with no clapper. "Since the beginning, dear. The ball is always. Isn't it lovely?"

"Where did you get the gowns?" Sefa asked.

"The court provides."

"Who provides?"

The woman's smile flickered — that same hairline crack Zephyrine had seen in the fishmonger, terror showing through like light through a split in a wall — and then sealed itself. "The court provides," she said, a little more firmly, a little less warmly, and turned away, and resumed her dancing with her invisible partner, and Zephyrine understood that the woman had just gone as close to the edge of her own mind as she could bear, and had stepped back from it, and would step back from it every time, forever, because the alternative was to fall.

"Zephyrine." Sefa's hand closed hard around her wrist. "Look at the doors. The side doors."

Zephyrine looked. Along both walls, tall doors opened onto corridors — and the corridors were dark, and the dark in them was not ordinary darkness. It moved. Slowly, gently, the way fog moves, but it *moved*, and it was watching the ballroom the way a shepherd watches a flock.

"They can't leave," Zephyrine said. The truth of it settled into her stomach like a stone dropped into a well. "The fog isn't just in the air. It's the walls. It's the *rule*."

A serving girl glided past with a tray of wine that never diminished, and Zephyrine caught her arm — gently, barely a touch — and the girl turned, and she was young, sixteen perhaps, with a face that might once have been quick and clever.

"What's your name?" Zephyrine asked her.

The girl smiled the smile. "I'm the cupbearer."

"Before that. Before the ball. What was your name?"

The fog-eyes held hers. And Zephyrine watched — she would remember this for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be — she watched the girl *try*. She saw the effort move through her face like weather: the smile straining, the brow knotting, the lips parting around the first shape of a sound. A name has a first letter. The girl found the letter. Zephyrine saw her find it, saw it almost arrive—

The girl's eyes flicked toward the dark corridors. Just once. Just for an instant.

—and the letter drowned, and the smile smoothed over, and the girl said, pleasantly, in a bell with no clapper: "I'm the cupbearer. It's what I'm for." And she glided on, and the wine never diminished, and the waltz played on without strings.

Sefa was crying without noise, the way she'd learned to cry on the road, the way you learn to do anything when the fog might be listening. "They're in there," she whispered. "All of them. Everyone the courier — everyone from home. They're in *there*, under it. They keep almost remembering."

"Almost is what they're given," Zephyrine said. Her hands had curled into fists without her permission, and she felt, deep beneath her fear, the old heat stirring — the storm answering the storm in her chest, magic pressing in from all sides of this golden, suffocating room, whispering that it could crack the chandeliers, shatter the doors, blow the fog out like a snuffed candle.

*And kill them all doing it,* she reminded herself. *Erasure is a wound. You don't tear a wound open. You find out what closed it, and why, and who holds the needle.*

She forced her fists open. She made herself look at the beauty a moment longer — the light, the gowns, the worn and shining floor, the empty thrones receiving their endless bows — and let it curdle in her, let it become what it was: the loveliest prison ever built, built by people who loved the prisoners enough to lock the doors and call it mercy.

"We find whoever built this," she said quietly. "The matriarch. The one the grave told us about. She's here — the fog has a center, and it's her."

"And then?"

"Then I ask her a question." Zephyrine looked at the cupbearer, already lost again in her rounds, already beautiful, already nothing. "I ask her how a bargain that saves a people by unmaking their names was ever worth the price. And then—"

The music stopped.

All at once, mid-phrase, every instrument silent, every dancer frozen mid-step, every fog-colored eye in the ballroom turning — slowly, smoothly, all together — toward the two strangers standing by the wall.

Zephyrine's skin went cold.

The fog had noticed them.

From the far end of the hall, past the empty thrones, a door she hadn't seen before swung open onto darkness, and a voice came out of it — warm, ancient, and terribly kind — a voice that said:

"Welcome home, storm-caller. We've been expecting you."
