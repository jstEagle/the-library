## 13. Children of the Fog

The knocking did not stop.

It came from everywhere at once — the doors, the windows, the walls themselves — a slow, patient percussion like rain against glass, except there was no rain in the city with no name. Zephyrine stood at the window of the guest chamber and watched the fog press its gray face against the pane, and the sound continued, soft as knuckles, insistent as debt.

"They know you're awake," Kestrel said from the doorway. She had not slept either. Her Warden's coat was buttoned to the throat, though she wore no sigils anymore; she'd torn them off somewhere on the road south, and Zephyrine had pretended not to notice. "The erasure has been aware of you since you crossed the border. It's only now that it's decided to be *curious*."

"Curious," Zephyrine repeated. "That's what we're calling it."

"What would you call it?"

Zephyrine didn't answer, because the honest answer was *hungry*, and saying it aloud would make it true.

Sefa was already dressed, the map wrapped around her shoulders like a shawl — she'd taken to carrying it that way, close to her skin, as if the ink could warm her. "Mother Vessaine will want to see us," she said. "After last night. After what she told us." Her voice caught on the word *told*, because what the matriarch had actually done was confess, and confessions demand responses, and they had given none.

"We should go down," Sefa said. "Through the nursery wing."

"The what?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The nursery wing occupied the eastern face of the palace, where morning light would have fallen if morning light could reach this place. Instead the corridors glowed with a sourceless gray luminescence, the color of overcast sky at the hour when no one can say whether it's dawn or dusk. The walls were painted with murals: processions, harvests, coronations. Every figure's face had been left blank. Not defaced — never defaced; the paint was pristine — simply unfinished, as if the artist had understood that faces were exactly the kind of detail an erasure would take first.

And the children were everywhere.

They ran races down the long corridor, barefoot and silent, their footfalls making no sound on the stone. They played a game with a ball of woven rags, tossing it in perfect arcs, catching it without looking. They sat in circles and braided each other's hair with the mechanical tenderness of dolls. There were dozens of them. Maybe more than dozens. Zephyrine tried to count and found her eyes sliding off the number the way a hand slides off wet glass.

None of them were crying. None of them were fighting. None of them were doing any of the loud, chaotic, glorious things children do, and it took Zephyrine a full minute to understand why the silence bothered her more than screaming would have.

"Sefa," she said quietly. "What are their names?"

"That's just it." Sefa's hands were fists at her sides. "They don't have any."

A boy of perhaps seven detached himself from the rag-ball game and approached them. He walked the way the courtiers poured wine — smoothly, correctly, without a single wasted motion or a single spontaneous one. He stopped in front of Zephyrine and looked up at her with polite gray eyes.

"You're the Visitor," he said.

"I'm Zephyrine."

The boy considered this. "That isn't a role."

"It's a name."

"Names are for things that stay." He said it without malice, without sadness, the way you'd recite a rule of grammar. "Nothing stays here. So we have roles instead. I'm the Runner. That's the Laugher—" he gestured at a small girl by the wall, who smiled on cue, wide and vacant, "—and the Braider, and the Quiet One, and the One Who Asks Questions."

"The One Who Asks Questions," Zephyrine said. "Which one is that?"

"She's coming. She always comes." The boy — the Runner — tilted his head, listening to something Zephyrine couldn't hear. "You shouldn't answer her, though. The others say answering makes the fog sad."

"Why would answering make the fog sad?"

But the Runner had already turned back to his game, and the ball of rags rose and fell, rose and fell, in its endless perfect arc.

* * *

She came at the end of the corridor, where the gray light pooled thickest.

She was small — six, maybe seven years old, though age sat strangely on all of them, like clothing borrowed from someone else. She wore a smock the color of undyed wool, and her hair was white. Not old-woman white, not winter white: blank white, the white of paper before anything is written on it. She walked past the racing children, past the braiding circle, straight to Zephyrine, and stopped an arm's length away, and looked up.

Her eyes were gray too. Everything here was gray. But there was something in them — a flicker, a friction, the smallest possible spark of *want* — that made Zephyrine's chest tighten.

"You're the One Who Asks Questions," Zephyrine said.

"Yes." The girl folded her hands. "It's my role. I ask questions. Nobody answers them, because answers make the fog sad. But I keep asking, because that's my role, and if I stop being my role I don't know what I am."

The words went through Zephyrine like a blade slipped between ribs — clean entry, no resistance, all the damage hidden inside.

"What's your question?" Zephyrine asked. "I'll answer it. Whatever it is."

The girl glanced sideways, quick and guilty, toward the far end of the corridor where a Warden-shaped shadow drifted through the fog — not Kestrel; one of the others, one of the loyal ones, patrolling. Then she stepped closer, so close Zephyrine could see that her gray irises were not entirely gray after all. There was brown in them. Buried brown, deep brown, the brown of turned earth, of acorns, of a world before the fog.

"When I look in the mirror," the girl whispered, "there's nothing looking back. Just gray, and then me standing in it. Mother Vessaine says that's normal. But the mirror in the west hall is older than the fog, and sometimes it shows a different girl. A girl with dark eyes. Is that me? Are those my eyes? What do my eyes look like?"

The question hung in the air, small and enormous.

Behind Zephyrine, Sefa made a sound she quickly swallowed. Kestrel had gone very still.

Zephyrine crouched. Slowly, so as not to startle, she brought herself level with the child, and took the small blank face in her hands, and looked into those buried-brown eyes for a long moment while the fog pressed against the windows and the silent children raced their silent races.

"Brown," she said. "Your eyes are brown. Dark brown, like river stones after rain. Like the earth in spring when you turn it for planting. And whoever gave them to you — your mother, or her mother, or some grandmother back down the line — they must have been beautiful, because these are beautiful, and eyes like this don't come from nowhere. They come from people. Real people, with names, who loved somebody enough to pass them down."

The girl didn't smile. Something better happened: her whole face *changed*. The smooth doll-mask cracked, and underneath it was a child — frightened, hopeful, seven years old and starving for one true sentence.

"River stones," she repeated. "After rain."

"After rain," Zephyrine promised. "I've seen a lot of rain."

"What was my name?" The question came out fast now, tumbling, a dam breaking. "Before? Did I have one? Did my mother hold me and call me something? Was it pretty? Do you think it was pretty?"

"I don't know your name." Zephyrine's voice nearly failed her. She steadied it. "But I know someone who might be able to find it. And when I do, I'm going to walk through every hall in this palace and say it out loud until the walls remember it. Until *you* remember it. That's a promise, little one. And I've broken a lot of promises in my life, but not that kind. Never that kind."

The girl reached up and touched Zephyrine's cheek with one finger, feather-light, the way you touch something you're afraid will vanish.

"You feel like weather," she said.

"I am weather."

"Is that why the fog is scared of you?"

Zephyrine froze. "The fog is scared of me?"

The girl nodded solemnly. "It talks at night. When everyone's asleep. It talks to itself, the way lonely people do. Last night it said—" she screwed up her face, reaching for the exact words, "—it said, *the storm is in the house, the storm is in the house, and the Door remembers her.*"

Down the corridor, the rag-ball dropped.

Every child in the nursery wing stopped at once — mid-stride, mid-braid, mid-smile — and turned their heads toward Zephyrine in perfect unison, thirty blank faces catching the gray light like thirty mirrors. For three heartbeats the silence was absolute.

Then, as one, they went back to their games.

The girl squeezed Zephyrine's finger once, hard, a grown-up gesture in a child's hand. "You shouldn't have answered me," she whispered. "Now the fog knows you can hear me. And it knows I can hear *you*." She stepped back, folding her hands, becoming the One Who Asks Questions again, smooth and correct. But her eyes — her brown, buried, river-stone eyes — held Zephyrine's for one last second, and in them was a message no erasure could scrub out:

*Hurry.*

* * *

They didn't speak until they reached the guest chamber and barred the door.

"The fog talks," Sefa said. She was pacing, arms wrapped around herself. "The fog *talks*, and a seven-year-old is the only one who hears it, because she's the only one the erasure hasn't finished hollowing out. Zeph, she asked about her *eyes*. She looks in a mirror and sees nothing. These children were born inside this. They've never had a name. They've never—"

"I know."

"—and Mother Vessaine wants us to let it *finish*. She stood in front of us last night and asked us to let it finish, and I almost agreed, I *almost*—"

"I know." Zephyrine sat on the edge of the bed and put her head in her hands. Ten years ago she had called down a storm on a battlefield and burned a kingdom's future to ash, and she had carried that weight across half the world, and she had thought she knew the shape of guilt. She had known nothing. Guilt was not what you did to the dead. Guilt was a small white-haired girl asking what her eyes looked like, and having no one alive who could tell her.

Kestrel spoke from the window, where she'd been watching the fog churn. "The Door remembers her," she quoted softly. "That's not fog-talk. That's a warning. Or an invitation." She turned. "You need to understand what you've just done in that corridor. You made a promise to a child born inside the erasure. The erasure heard it. Everything inside these walls hears everything — I helped build the listening part, twenty years ago, before I understood what we were building it *for*." Her jaw tightened. "Vessaine will learn what you promised within the hour. And she will not let you keep it."

"Then I'll keep it anyway."

"You don't grasp it. You're thinking of her as a prisoner to free. Vessaine thinks of her as a *sacrifice already made* — a lamb on an altar who must not be allowed to climb down, because if one climbs down, the bargain breaks, and the conquerors' curse comes back for all of them. To Vessaine, you're not a rescuer. You're the wolf at the door wearing a rescuer's face." Kestrel crossed the room, and for the first time since the treeline, she took Zephyrine's shoulder in a grip that was almost desperate. "The Door of Returning opens at moonrise tomorrow, in the ruined shrine on the western border. If you mean to do this — if you truly mean to break the erasure rather than let it sleep — you cannot wait for permission. There will be no permission. There will only be the hunt."

"The hunt," Zephyrine said.

"The moment Vessaine declares you a traitor to the bargain, the fog itself becomes her weapon. It will find you anywhere in this city. It will take Sefa first, because Sefa carries the map." Kestrel's voice dropped. "We leave tonight. Before the moon rises. Before she finishes deciding."

Zephyrine looked at the barred door, and beyond it at the gray halls full of smiling courtiers and nameless children, and she felt the grief in her chest stop churning and begin, at last, to crystallize — grief hardening into something with edges, something that could be held, something that could be aimed.

A girl with river-stone eyes was waiting for her name.

She would have it by moonrise, or Zephyrine Nystrom would burn the fog down trying.

She stood. "Get the map," she said. "Wake Sefa. We go tonight."

Outside the window, the fog drew back from the glass — slowly, deliberately, like a predator crouching —

and every bell in the nameless city began to ring.
