## 15. The Hunt Begins

The bell began to toll before dawn.

Not a festival bell — I'd heard those in the Vale, bright and round and stupid with joy. This was a cracked sound, a wound of a sound, one long note dragged out and left to die over the rooftops. It came from the palace spire, where the fog was thickest, and every person in the nameless city stopped what they were doing to listen.

They smiled while they listened.

That was the worst part. Bakers with flour on their hands, women drawing water, children chasing a hoop down an alley that had no name — all of them turned toward the sound and smiled, gentle and vacant, like the bell was telling them something lovely.

"It's her," Sefa whispered beside me. We stood in the shadow of a dyer's awning, three streets from the matriarch's hall, our plan still warm in our mouths and already dead. "She's waking them up."

"She's not waking them," Kestrel said. Her voice was flat as slate. "She's pointing them."

I understood then what the bell was for. Not alarm. Direction. The fog that filled this city wasn't weather and it wasn't enchantment, not exactly — it was the erasure itself, given breath, and it needed no eyes because it *was* the eyes. The matriarch had only to tell it who we were.

And she had told it everything.

*Traitors,* her voice had said, or rather the fog had said it with her mouth, when we fled the hall under the arches of the dream-court, past courtiers frozen mid-courtesy with wine glasses they never drank from. *Three traitors carry the map. The tall one remembers too much. The young one carries names. And the storm-caller—*

*The storm-caller is the reason you must forget.*

*Find them. Unmake them gently.*

Gently. As if there were a kind way to be erased.

"Split up?" Sefa asked, but her voice shook on the second word, and Kestrel and I answered together: "No."

We ran instead.

* * *

The city fought us in ways a city shouldn't.

Streets we had crossed an hour ago were simply gone — not blocked, not guarded, but *absent*, replaced by blank gray stretches where the fog lay so thick it had texture, like wet wool. I put my hand into one once, before Kestrel could stop me, and my fingers came back numb to the wrist, and for three full seconds I couldn't remember why I'd raised my arm.

"Don't touch it," Kestrel snapped, hauling me back. "It doesn't take your hand. It takes the part of you that wanted to."

"So we go around."

"There is no around. There's only through, and it knows we know that."

The map, pressed against my ribs inside my coat, had gone hot. Not warm — hot, feverish, like a living thing with a racing heart. When I pulled it out, braced against a wall in an alley that smelled of tallow and rain that never fell, the ink was moving. Roads crawled like veins. The Door of Returning, which had sat at the map's western edge since the cartographer drew it, now pulsed at the center, dragging every line toward itself like water finding a drain.

"It's trying to lead us," Sefa breathed.

"It's trying to save itself," Kestrel said. "The map is Veyra's last memory. Of course it runs."

Then the fog found its voice again, and we stopped talking about maps.

It came from everywhere and nowhere — a whisper layered under the bell's dying note, and it knew our names. Not the names we carried. The names the fog had made for us. *Storm-caller.* *Name-carrier.* *Rememberer.* It said them softly, almost lovingly, from doorways and chimneys and the gaps between cobblestones, and wherever it spoke, people came out.

They didn't run. That was the horror of it. They *walked*, unhurried, smiling their soft smiles, shopkeepers and grandmothers and a boy of maybe ten still holding his breakfast bread, and their eyes were full of fog, and they moved to close the streets behind us like fingers closing around a moth.

"They're not attacking," Sefa said, backing against me. "Zephyrine, they don't even know they're—"

"I know." My throat was tight. "That's the point. She won't let them be guilty. She'll make the fog do everything, and they'll wake tomorrow remembering nothing but kindness."

Kestrel drew her warden's blade — gray steel, edge-less, made not to cut flesh but to cut *meaning* — and set her back to ours. For one strange moment the three of us stood in a ring in a nameless street while a whole city closed like a hand.

"The aqueduct," Kestrel said. "The old one, north quarter. The fog thins where the stone still remembers being built. Move."

We moved.

* * *

We ran through markets where vendors watched us pass without seeing, through a square where a fountain flowed upward, through a colonnade whose pillars bore carvings that smoothed themselves blank as we went by — history being eaten in real time, right in front of me, and I couldn't stop to mourn it because the fog was learning.

That's what I felt most, running. Not fear of the Wardens, not fear of the matriarch. Fear of the fog's *attention*. At first it reached for us clumsily, a blind thing groping along walls. But each time it touched us and failed, it came back smarter. It learned Sefa's stride. It learned the rhythm of Kestrel's breathing. It learned that I flinched left when lightning-memory prickled in my palms, and after that it always came from the left.

By the time we reached the aqueduct, I understood we weren't fleeing a place. We were fleeing a predator that got faster the longer it knew us.

The aqueduct rose above the northern quarter on legs of pale stone, and Kestrel was right — the fog broke apart beneath it like smoke under a window, thinning to wisps, and in the clear air I could see the mason's marks still cut deep: *V.R., year of the twin harvests, Aldous Wren, master of works.*

A name. A real one, carved by hands that had been proud.

Sefa stopped dead beneath it. "Aldous Wren," she read aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Grandmother told me that name. He built the fountain in her village. She used to say—" She pressed her hand flat against the stone. "He was real. All of it was real."

"Sefa." I touched her shoulder. "We have to keep—"

The bell tolled again, closer, and the fog poured back into the street like a tide returning, and this time it brought shapes with it.

They came out of the whiteness walking upright but wrong — figures made of fog wearing the suggestion of armor, the suggestion of faces, hollow men the erasure had dreamed up out of the city's own forgetting. Hollow Wardens, but not Kestrel's kind. Kestrel's kind had chosen their hollowness. These had been *assigned* it.

"Run the span," Kestrel shouted, and put herself between us and them.

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do.

Kestrel's blade met the first hollow man and unmade him with a sound like a page tearing — but there were six more, then ten, flowing up over the aqueduct's lip like milk over a pot's rim, and two of them slipped past her entirely, silent as spilled water, and came for Sefa.

I called the storm.

I didn't decide to. Ten years of nothing in my blood, and then the sky over the nameless city answered me like a dog answering a whistle — a fork of white fire straight down through the fog, close enough that the thunder slapped us all flat against the stone. The hollow men burst apart. The fog screamed. Somewhere below, real people cried out in real voices, frightened, awake for one terrible instant.

And Sefa was gone.

No — not gone. Taken. One of the fog-shapes had wrapped itself around her in the chaos, and where it touched her she didn't struggle; she *dissolved*, her outline going soft, her shout turning to a murmur, and then both shape and girl sank backward off the aqueduct into the white like a stone dropped into milk, and the surface of the fog closed over them smooth as a pond.

"*Sefa!*"

I was at the edge before Kestrel caught my collar. Below, the fog lay flat and innocent. No ripple. No trace. As if the street had never held a screaming girl with ink-stained fingers and her grandmother's name folded safe in her pocket.

"They have her," I said. The words came out very small. "The fog has her."

"No." Kestrel pulled me back from the edge, and for the first time since the treeline I saw fear move across her face — real fear, the kind she'd been holding off duty like a shield. "Worse. Look at the stone."

Where Sefa's hand had pressed against the mason's mark, the carving was fading. *Aldous Wren* was going gray at the edges, letters dissolving like sugar in water.

"It's not keeping her," Kestrel said quietly. "It's reading her. Everything she remembers, everyone she remembers — her grandmother, the village, the names she's collected on the road. Sefa is a walking archive, Zephyrine. She's the most dangerous thing in this city besides you and that map." Her jaw tightened. "And the matriarch is going to pour her out until there's nothing left to pour."

The map burned against my ribs. I pulled it out, and the ink had rearranged itself one final time: every road, every river, every line converging not on the Door of Returning anymore, but on the palace spire, where the bell hung silent now, waiting.

It wasn't showing me the way out.

It was showing me where they'd taken her.

"Kestrel." I rolled the map and shoved it inside my coat, and the storm stirred in my chest, eager, awful, glad to be asked. "How fast can you get me to the spire?"

She looked at me — at whatever was rising in my face — and I watched her choose, again, the way she'd been choosing since the inn, duty on one side and something truer on the other.

"Faster than the fog," she said. "Hold on to me. And storm-caller — whatever they've done to her, whatever you see when we get there—"

"Don't tell me to be careful."

"I was going to say don't hold back." She gripped my wrist, gray-eyed, half-traitor, all in. "Not this time. Not for her."

Below us, the fog began to rise.
