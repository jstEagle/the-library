## 21. A Country Made of People

I woke to the smell of bread.

Not storm-smell, not ozone and scorched stone — bread, yeast and woodsmoke, ordinary as morning. For a long moment I lay in the narrow bed they'd given me in the house that had no name yet and listened to the city breathe. Somewhere below, a hammer rang against metal. Somewhere farther off, a child was laughing at something, and the laugh had a name attached to it now. I could hear it through the window: *Maret! Maret, come back here!* A mother's voice, exasperated and alive.

Names everywhere. Ten days since the rain, and the city still couldn't stop saying them.

I sat up slowly. My body had opinions about everything — my hands trembled when I lifted the water cup, my legs ached like I'd run a country's length, which, in fairness, I had. The healers said the storm had taken something out of me along with the erasure. Maybe it had. When I reached for the old familiar pressure behind my ribs, the place where lightning used to live, I found only quiet. Not emptiness. Quiet. Like a field after harvest.

I didn't know yet if it would grow back. I was trying not to mind either way.

* * *

The square had been called the King's Square for four hundred years, according to Sefa, who had found the name in a merchant's ledger that survived because nobody had thought it important enough to burn. Now people just called it the Square, and the namelessness of it felt less like a wound every day and more like an invitation.

That was where they'd built the forge.

They'd carried the crown out of the palace ruins on a bier, the way you'd carry a body, and laid it on a table of rough planks so the whole city could come and look at it. And they had looked. For three days they came in a slow procession — farmers and cobblers and the gray-faced courtiers who'd slept through a decade of fog — and they stood over the broken thing and said nothing, or wept, or spat, or laughed until someone led them home. The crown had been split down the middle by the last blow of the conquest, before the mages made their bargain, before the forgetting. Two halves. A circlet of gold and iron and old black Veyran steel, cracked like an egg.

On the fourth day, a smith named Odile — she'd gotten her name back from her own mother, who remembered it the way you remember a song from childhood, half-sure, then suddenly all sure — stood up in the Square and said, "We could mend it."

The crowd went quiet.

"We have goldsmiths," she said. "We have the steel. We could mend it and put it on someone's head by winter."

And an old man near the front — I never learned his name, but I remember his hands, huge and scarred, a stonemason's hands — said, "Whose head?"

Nobody answered him. That silence lasted a long time, and it was the most honest silence I had ever heard in a public place. Because everyone knew there was no answer. The royal line was ash. The conquerors were a generation dead in their own lands, their curse spent, their armies dissolved into history. There was no one left to be king *of*, and no one left to be king *against*. The crown was a key to a door that no longer existed.

Odile looked around the Square, at all those faces waiting for someone else to decide, and I watched something move across her expression — the same thing I'd seen in the mirror ten years ago, the day I stopped waiting for Veyra to send for me.

"Then let's not mend it," she said. "Let's make it useful."

* * *

Sefa found me at the edge of the crowd. She moved differently now — quicker, louder, taking up space like she'd only just been told she was allowed to. She had ink on her fingers permanently these days; she'd appointed herself the city's archivist before anyone asked her to, collecting names the way other people collected shells. There were already four notebooks. She showed me one sometimes, pages of handwriting in three different inks: *This person remembers this. This person lost this. This person is looking for this.*

"You should be closer," she said. "You did this."

"I broke a sky open," I said. "They're doing this."

"You know what I mean." She bumped her shoulder against mine, careful of how unsteady I still was. "Come on. They're about to start."

The forge had been raised overnight in the corner of the Square, a brick hearth with a leather bellows, and Odile stood beside it with the two halves of the crown in her arms like an offering. A priestess of some small river cult — one of the dozens of faiths crawling back out of the fog — blessed the fire, or maybe just the people; the gods of Veyra were as half-remembered as everything else, and everyone seemed fine with that.

Then Odile put the crown in the coals.

I expected grief. I braced for it, the way you brace for a wave. But what rose off the crowd wasn't grief. It was relief — a sound halfway between a sigh and a cheer, hundreds of people exhaling four hundred years at once. The gold went soft and bright. The black steel held out longer, stubborn, and Odile worked it with tongs and sweat while the bellows roared, and little by little the shape of kingship came apart into shapeless glowing metal.

A boy beside me — he couldn't have been more than six, born inside the fog, named Maret by a mother who'd chosen it fresh off her tongue — tugged my sleeve.

"What was it?" he asked.

"A crown," I said.

"What's it for?"

And there it was, the whole question of the age, asked by a child with jam on his chin. I crouched down, joints complaining, and tried to find an answer that wasn't a lie.

"It was for telling people who was in charge," I said. "It's not for anything anymore. Watch."

Because even as I spoke, the first ingot came out of the fire, and Odile hammered it flat on her anvil, and an apprentice carried it to a second bench where an older woman bent it around a curved form — and it came up a plowshare. The crowd made a sound I'll carry to my grave. Not a roar. Something softer and more astonished than that, the sound of people watching a wound turn into a tool.

They worked through the morning. Plowshares first, because spring was coming and the fields outside the walls had gone fallow under the fog and everyone was hungry for the work of it. Then door-hinges, because doors were being hung on new houses all over the city, houses being built on streets whose names people were voting on in taverns each night. Then nails, buckets of nails, plain and honest. Someone shouted a suggestion — a bell, make a bell — and Odile laughed and said the crown wasn't big enough for a bell, and someone else said then we'll add our own metal, bring your broken things, and by afternoon there was a line of citizens holding out dented candlesticks and snapped scythes and a shattered sword or two, adding their private ruins to the public one, melting it all down together.

Kestrel stood apart from all of it, the way she always did, arms crossed, back to a wall. Watching me watch. She'd barely spoken to me since the Door, since she'd told me where to go and then turned her sword against the Wardens who'd been her family, since her sister had walked past her in the street without a flicker of recognition and Kestrel had smiled anyway and said, *she's free, that's what matters,* in a voice that convinced no one, least of all herself.

But she was there. She kept being there. It was starting to feel less like surveillance and more like company.

* * *

In the evening I walked the walls alone, because my legs needed the practice and my head needed the quiet. The city spread out below me, lamplit, half-built, loud. On the far side of the valley I could see the new fields already staked out, and beyond them the road that ran toward the Vale — toward the boundary stones that Sefa swore the elders were already arguing about lowering. An embassy had arrived two days ago. Actual ambassadors, blinking in the daylight, asking cautious questions about trade and weather and whether the storms were finished. I'd been invited to speak with them tomorrow. Me. The exile. The weapon. The woman who'd poured a decade of stolen sky through a dying door.

I still didn't know what I'd say to them. Probably I'd say yes to whatever they asked. Probably that was its own kind of danger — a woman with nothing left to lose saying yes too easily. But sitting on the cold stone with the whole living city humming beneath me, I found I wasn't afraid of it. For the first time in longer than I could measure, I wasn't afraid.

Footsteps on the stair. Kestrel, of course. She settled onto the wall a few feet away without asking, which was as close to friendship as either of us knew how to get.

"They'll want you for a symbol," she said. "The Storm-Caller. The Woman Who Broke the Fog. They'll put you in songs whether you like it or not."

"I hate songs."

"I know." A pause. "Refuse loudly. It makes better songs."

I huffed something that was almost a laugh. Below us, the forge still glowed, and the line of people with their broken offerings hadn't shortened. Somewhere a fiddle had started up, badly.

"My sister made bread today," Kestrel said abruptly, to the dark rather than to me. "She doesn't know why she cried while she kneaded it. She thinks it's grief. It isn't. It's just — room. All that room where the fog used to be." Her jaw worked. "She'll never remember me. I've made peace with — no. That's a lie. I haven't. I might never. But she's *here*, and she's hers, and she chose her own name yesterday. Brin. She picked it out of nothing." Kestrel shook her head slowly. "Out of nothing, Zephyrine. Do you understand? Nobody handed it to her. Nobody crowned her with it. She just — chose."

"That's the whole country now," I said. "Everyone choosing."

"And you?" She turned and looked at me straight on, the way she'd looked at me across a dozen standoffs, except there was no standoff left. "What do you choose?"

I looked out at the lamplit streets, at the forge and the fiddle and the mother calling Maret home, at the road running east toward a valley full of goats and elders and the only safety I'd ever known. Ten years ago I'd have crawled back to the Vale and thanked them for the walls. Five years ago I'd have said I belonged nowhere and meant it like a boast. Even a month ago, standing at the treeline with magic waking in my blood, I'd have told you I was going home to Veyra — the old Veyra, the true one, the one in the songs.

There was no old Veyra anymore. There was this. Unfinished, unbowed, arguing about street names, melting its crown into hinges. A country made of people instead of the other way around.

"I'm staying," I said, and heard how easily it came, and marveled at it. "Not as a symbol. As a neighbor. Somebody has to teach them what weather's coming, and apparently I'm qualified."

Kestrel nodded once, satisfied, and said nothing more, which between the two of us was practically an embrace.

Below us, the forge flared, and another piece of the crown went into the fire, and the city — nameless, newborn, entirely its own — hammered the night awake.
