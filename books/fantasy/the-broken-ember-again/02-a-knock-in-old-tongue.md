## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The closing bell at the Ledger Guild of Vharen did not ring so much as *settle* — a single bronze note that dropped through the guildhall like a stone through still water, and everything it touched stopped moving. Quills lifted from paper. Ink dried mid-stroke. Even the dust seemed to pause in the slanted amber light of the high windows, as though the building itself were holding its breath until morning.

Casper Hartwell had learned, over six years of indenture, to love and hate that bell in equal measure. It meant the day's tally was done. It also meant she was one day further into a debt whose principal never shrank.

She capped her inkwell with the small ritual care she gave all her tools — thumb on the stopper, quarter-turn, listen for the seal — and began squaring the day's entries. The impossible inventory waited in its column like patient animals: three bottles of thunder, harvested off the moor road during the storm of last spring, each one humming faintly against its shelf; a widow's second shadow, folded into a folio no larger than a prayer book, which occasionally shifted as if trying to remember where it was supposed to be standing; eleven feet of yesterday, sold by the yard to a playwright who wanted to write scenes he'd already lived. Casper had entered them all without comment, because comment was not in her contract. Her contract was numbers. Her contract was *accuracy*.

And accuracy, today, had been a knife.

The morning's audit had found her short by four marks — four marks she could prove she had never held, but proof was not a currency the guild accepted. The discrepancy went into her personal ledger, the slim black volume Master Quillon kept locked in his desk, the one with her name embossed on the spine in letters that always looked slightly fresher than they should. Four marks. At her current wage, that was nine days. Nine days added to a sentence already long enough that she had stopped counting it in years and started counting it in *versions of herself* — the girl who signed the indenture was someone she barely recognized now, and every month the distance between them grew, and the guild called that growth *interest*.

She pressed her palm flat against the ledger page, feeling the faint warmth of fresh ink, and thought: *one day I will add a column of my own.*

Then the knock came.

It was not a loud knock. That was what made it wrong. The guildhall doors were iron-banded oak two inches thick, built to admit merchants and repel beggars, and everyone who came to them knocked accordingly — a fist, a demand, an announcement. This was three soft taps, spaced like footsteps in an empty house. Like someone knocking not to be let in, but to ask whether the door was real.

Casper's head came up. Across the counting floor, old Merrick the assessor glanced toward the doors and frowned, his jowls gathering like a curtain being drawn. The junior clerks had already gone; only the senior staff remained, those bound by salary rather than shift, and the light through the windows had gone the color of honey left too long in the sun.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Three taps, a pause, three taps.

"That'll be the fishmonger's boy," Merrick said, but he didn't believe it, and neither did Casper, because the fishmonger's boy knocked like he was breaking down a door, having learned early that the guild only respected force.

Merrick heaved himself up with a grunt and crossed the floor, keys singing at his belt. Casper should have gathered her things. She told herself she was gathering her things. But her hands stayed on her ledger, and her eyes followed the old man to the door, and when he drew back the bolt and pulled it open on the blue-grey dusk of Vharen's streets, she saw what came in before she understood it.

A girl. Small — ten years old, perhaps eleven, though there was something in the way she stood that made age feel like the wrong instrument for measuring her. Mud to the knees, mud to the elbows, a smear of it across one cheek like a war paint applied by accident. Her hair had been dark once and was now mostly street. She was barefoot. In Vharen. In the month of Frostmere, when the gutters grew teeth of ice.

And she was talking.

Not crying, not begging — the guild got plenty of both, and Casper had long ago learned to file human misery under *not my column*. The girl was talking in a language Casper had never heard, and yet the sound of it reached into her chest and turned a key she hadn't known was there. The syllables were round and old, vowels that opened like doors, consonants that fell softly as ash. It sounded less like speech than like weather from another country. It sounded like something the world had agreed to forget, still speaking anyway.

"None of that," Merrick said gruffly, already reaching to shut the door. "Guild's closed. Come back—"

The girl said a word then — one word, urgent, aimed past Merrick directly at Casper as though she had picked her out from across the room the way a drowning swimmer picks out a lantern — and Casper's hand moved.

That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would wake her at night for weeks: her hand moved *before her mind caught up*. Her pen was uncapped. Her ledger was open. And her hand wrote, in her own neat guild-trained script, a line of translation beneath the day's final entry, as though the language had been waiting behind her eyes all along for permission:

*The fire is going out. I have come to the people who keep the books.*

Casper stared at the page. The words sat there in her handwriting, in her ink, in her grammar — and she had no memory of deciding to write them. It was as if someone had borrowed her arm for a moment and returned it slightly warmer.

"What did you say?" she heard herself ask, and was distantly surprised to find she had risen from her stool.

The girl looked at her. Her eyes were the color of banked coals — not red, not orange, but that deep patient glow a fire keeps when it has decided not to die. Mud-streaked, barefoot, half-frozen, and she looked at Casper Hartwell with an expression no child should own: recognition. Relief. And underneath both, a fear so old it had gone smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for generations.

She spoke again — a full sentence this time, quick and pleading — and again Casper's hand translated before her mind could object:

*Nobody remembers my name. I checked everywhere. You will remember. Your ink remembers things.*

"Girl," Merrick said, grabbing her shoulder, "I don't know what game—" He stopped. His frown deepened into something older and more frightened. "What is that tongue? Sounds like — I don't know what it sounds like."

"It sounds like Ossuary," said a voice from the top of the stairs.

Master Quillon descended the way he did everything: precisely, without hurry, as though the world were a document he was reviewing and had found mostly satisfactory. He was a narrow man, grey as ledger-paper, with spectacles that caught the lamplight and turned his eyes into two small eclipses. Casper had seen him angry exactly once in six years, and had spent the following week re-copying every entry she'd made during his anger, just in case. Now his face showed nothing at all, which was worse.

He crossed the counting floor slowly. The girl watched him come with her coal-glow eyes, and something in her posture shifted — she stepped back, half behind Casper, a movement so instinctive and so trusting that it landed in Casper's chest like a dropped coin.

"You know this child?" Quillon asked. His voice was mild. Mildness, in Quillon, was a blade being wrapped in cloth.

"No, sir."

"Then why," he said, and nodded at her open ledger, "is she in your books?"

Casper looked down. In the margin beside her translations, her hand had written a third line she had no memory of writing:

*Asset recovered. Custody pending. Do not let them take the page.*

Her mouth went dry. "I don't know, sir. She spoke, and I — the words came out onto the page. I didn't choose it."

"People always say that," Quillon murmured, "the first time." He extended one hand toward the girl, palm up, courteous as a banker. "Come here, little one. You're cold. We'll find you soup and a blanket and someone who can properly log you."

The girl flinched from his hand as if it were a brand. She said something rapid and defiant, chin up, and Casper's traitor pen — still uncapped, still hovering — scratched out the sense of it across the margin:

*You are one of the ones who wrote us down. I can smell the erasure on you.*

Quillon went very still.

For three heartbeats the counting hall was silent except for the tick of the great regulator clock and the wind worrying at the door Merrick had not quite closed. Then Quillon took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with terrible calm, and put them back on.

"Merrick," he said. "Seal the doors. All of them. Ground floor, cellar, the postern. Wake the under-masters."

"Sir?" Merrick's keys had stopped singing. "It's closing bell. The protocol says—"

"The protocol," Quillon said softly, "does not account for a child walking out of a country that does not exist. Seal the doors."

* * *

They sealed the doors.

Casper stood at her desk and listened to the bolts go home one after another around the guildhall's perimeter — a sound she had heard a hundred times without thinking, iron finding iron, and tonight it sounded like a lid closing on a box with something alive inside it. The lamps were lit. The under-masters came down in nightshirts and half-buttoned coats, grumbling, then stopped grumbling when they saw the girl. One of them, a wide soft man named Aldery, took a single look at her bare frozen feet and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with pity.

"She shouldn't be able to be here," Aldery whispered. "The wards on the threshold—"

"I'm aware of the wards," Quillon said.

The girl had not moved from her spot near the door. She stood in the pool of lamplight with her arms wrapped around herself, mud drying on her skin, watching the masters gather the way a wild animal watches a circle close. And Casper — Casper found she had drifted, without any decision on her part, until she was standing between the girl and the ring of guildmasters. She noticed this about herself the way one notices one has been walking in one's sleep: with alarm, and no idea how far back the habit went.

"Hartwell," Quillon said. "Your ledger, please."

She gave it to him. What else could she do? Refusal was a word the guild kept in a very expensive dictionary. Quillon read the marginal lines — her lines, her handwriting, words she had never chosen — and his face performed no expression whatsoever, which from Quillon was a performance in itself.

"Interesting," he said. "You translate it."

"I don't speak it, sir. I've never heard it before tonight."

"No one has. Not for two hundred years." He handed the ledger back, and his fingers brushed hers, dry and cool as vellum. "And yet your hand knows it. Curious. There are scholars in this city who would pay a year's wages to hear what you just heard. There are others who would pay considerably more to make sure no one ever hears it again." He turned to the assembled masters. "The child stays tonight. In the secure archive. Logged as found property, pending assessment. Hartwell."

"Sir?"

"You will prepare the intake entry. Standard form. Description, condition, provenance unknown." A pause, thin as a razor. "And you will bring me the page you've already written. All of it. Margins included."

"Yes, sir."

The girl was led away by two under-masters, gently enough, the way one carries something breakable that also might bite. At the foot of the stairs she twisted around and looked back at Casper, and said one more thing — quiet, almost conversational, a sentence tossed like a rope across water.

Casper's hand, still holding her own ledger against her chest, twitched. She did not write it down. She didn't dare, with Quillon watching. But the translation arrived in her mind unbidden, complete and warm and terrible, settling behind her ribs like a swallowed coal:

*Don't let them put me in the books. People who go in the books don't come out.*

* * *

Later — much later, when the guildhall had gone dark except for the watch-lamp in the stairwell and the building creaked through its nightly settlement like an old ship — Casper sat at her desk in the clerks' dormitory with a candle stub and the day's ledger open in front of her.

She knew she should sleep. She knew that tomorrow would bring intake forms and assessments and whatever quiet machinery the guild turned on when something impossible walked through its door. She knew that a smart woman — a survivor, a debtor, a person whose entire life was organized around the principle of *not my column* — would copy out the required pages, deliver them to Quillon, and think no further about mud-streaked girls or languages two centuries dead.

Instead she looked at the margins.

*The fire is going out. I have come to the people who keep the books.*

She read it again. And again. And the longer she read, the more a cold realization climbed her spine one vertebra at a time, because it wasn't the girl's words that frightened her anymore.

It was her own ink.

Six years she had written in this guild. Six years of tallies and transfers, of assets entered and debts compounded, and she had believed — she had *needed* to believe — that the ledgers recorded the world. That the bottles of thunder existed first, and the entries came second. That the books were mirrors.

But her hand had known a dead language. Her hand had written sentences she never chose. And sitting alone in the dark with the candle guttering, Casper finally allowed herself to finish the thought she had been refusing since the door opened:

If the ledgers did not record the world — if they *made* it, bound it, held it — then every entry she had ever written was a link in some chain. Every asset logged was something held. Every debt compounded was someone kept. The widow's second shadow, folded in its folio, shifting restlessly because it wanted to be standing somewhere. The eleven feet of yesterday, shelved and catalogued. And somewhere below, or behind, or between the pages — a country, maybe. A fire. A nameless girl who had walked across a frozen city barefoot because the books were the only place left that remembered her, and the books were also the cage.

Casper looked down at her hands — ink-stained, competent, obedient hands — and saw them for the first time as what they were.

Not a scribe's hands.

A jailer's.

The candle stub drowned in its own wax. In the dark, she could hear the guildhall breathing around her, the slow tick of the regulator clock, the murmur of the sealed doors standing guard on every wall. Somewhere above her, in the secure archive, a girl with coal-glow eyes was falling asleep in a country that had forgotten how to say her name.

And somewhere below — Casper was suddenly, unshakably certain of this, certain the way one is certain of a sound in the night — something was burning. Something old. Something that was, very quietly, going out.

She did not sleep at all.
