## 3. What the Guild Keeps

They took the girl away before Casper could ask her name.

Two guild wardens, gray-robed and gray-faced, had lifted her from the counting floor as though she were a mislaid parcel — one under each arm, her muddy boots scuffing twin dark lines across the polished oak. She had not struggled. That was the worst of it, Casper thought afterward. The girl had looked back once, not at the masters, not at the wardens, but at the ledger open on Casper's desk, at the page where her own hand had written words she did not know in a language that should have been dead, and then the doors of the inner stair had swallowed her.

The closing bell never rang that night. Casper sat at her desk with her pen still inked and drying, watching the words on the page. *Vharen-ke-tollun. Vharen-ke-mira.* The letters sat there in her own careful script, patient as flies, refusing to be unmade.

She had been a scribe for eleven years. She knew what happened to entries made in error: they were struck, the page fined against the scribe's account, the error entered into the Book of Corrections in red ink so that the mistake itself became part of the record. Mistakes did not vanish in the Ledger Guild. Mistakes compounded.

And yet no one had come to fine her. No one had come to strike the line. The wardens had come for the girl instead, which meant — and Casper's stomach turned slowly over as she understood it — that the entry was not an error. It was an *acknowledgment*. Something had arrived at the guild's doors speaking Old Tongue, and the guild's books had simply... recorded it, the way a scale records weight.

Her hand had known the language. Her hand, which had never been taught it.

"Casper Hartwell."

Master Quillon stood at the end of the row of desks, and the whole counting floor went quiet around him the way water goes still around a dropped stone. He was not a large man, but he occupied space the way a mortgage occupies a house — thoroughly, and with the expectation of permanence. His chain of office was plain iron, unadorned, because Master Quillon held that ornament was interest paid to vanity.

"Master Quillon." She rose. Her knees felt borrowed.

"The upper room. Now. Bring nothing."

* * *

The upper room sat above the vaults and below the masters' gallery, a windowless chamber where the guild conducted its least pleasant arithmetic. A single lamp burned in its bracket, and the flame bent toward Quillon as he entered, as if even fire kept accounts with him.

He gestured her into the chair across the bare table. It was the chair without arms. There was another chair, behind his side of the table, that had arms, and a high back, and she understood the furniture the way she understood everything in this building: as terms of a contract.

"Do you know," Quillon said, settling himself, "what the guild's founding charter says is our first duty?"

"To keep accurate accounts, Master."

"Wrong." He let the word sit between them like a coin on a counter. "Anyone can keep accurate accounts. Fishwives keep accurate accounts. Our first duty — the first line, in the founder's own hand — is *that nothing owed shall pass out of memory*. Memory is the collateral, Hartwell. Every debt in this city is secured against remembering. That is why we are the Ledger Guild and not merely clerks. Do you understand?"

"I—"

"You wrote two lines tonight in a tongue that has not been spoken in Vharen for two hundred years." He opened a folder — actual paper, actual string-tied folder, which meant this was not guild business but something older and stranger wearing guild business as a coat. "Your hand translated it. Not your education; we both know your education stopped at third-form grammar. Your *hand*. Tell me why."

"I don't know, Master."

"Don't know, or won't say?"

"They are different questions with the same answer, Master. I don't know."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. It might have been approval. With Quillon, approval and displeasure wore the same narrow expression, like twins in matching coats.

He turned the folder around and opened it. Inside lay a single sheet, ancient, browned at the edges, sealed twice with wax stamped by sigils Casper did not recognize — and yet some low cellar-room of her mind stirred at the sight of them, the way a dog stirs at a whistle pitched too high to hear.

"This," Quillon said, "is an asset registry. Entry one hundred and fourteen. Read it."

She read. The old script was dense, formal, and her eyes moved through it with terrible ease, translating as they went, so that she heard the words in her head in a voice that was not hers:

*Item: one (1) citizen, minor, female, of the country of Ossuary. Held in perpetuity under the Accord of Forgetting. Asset class: irreplaceable. Condition: living. Interest accrues annually against the principal of the national debt. Custody: the Guild of Ledgers, Vharen branch, until such time as the debt is discharged or the asset expires.*

Below it, in fresher ink, a long column of figures. Two centuries of annual interest, calculated and carried forward, calculated and carried forward, a sum so vast it had ceased to resemble money and become a kind of weather.

Casper set the page down very carefully, as though it might go off.

"She's not a refugee," Casper said. "Or an orphan. She's — inventory."

"The guild prefers 'asset.'"

"She's a *child*."

"The guild," Quillon said, with the mildness of a man correcting a junior clerk's addition, "does not deal in children. It deals in obligations. Two hundred years ago, a country defaulted on a debt it could not pay — a debt incurred in the oldest currency there is — and the settlement was total. Ossuary was not conquered, Hartwell. It was *foreclosed*. Everything in it, everyone in it, every street and song and name, was taken into custody and written out of the world's books. What remains of it is held here. In trust." He tapped the registry with one dry finger. "In this building. Under contract. Accruing."

"That's not possible."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. And yet the accounts balance, which in my experience is the only proof that matters. Impossible things come through this guild every day, Hartwell. Bottled thunder in cellar nine. A widow's second shadow, impounded for unpaid duties. You've tallied them. Did you think they were curiosities? They are *receivables*. Fragments of holdings too small or too strange to have been fully unwritten. And once every few generations, the largest holding of all sends us a reminder that it is still on our books." He folded his hands. "Tonight it walked in covered in mud and asked, in effect, to speak to someone in accounts."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere far below them — three floors down, four, deeper than the guild's own plans admitted — Casper felt rather than heard a slow, vast pulse, like a heart turning over in its sleep. She had noticed it before, on late nights, and told herself it was the river under the foundations. She told herself that again now, and for the first time in eleven years, did not quite believe herself.

"What happens to her?" Casper asked. "The asset."

"Standard procedure for undocumented arrivals pending classification. Assessment of remaining value. Then disposition."

"Disposition," Casper repeated. The word tasted of copper and cold cellars.

"You find the vocabulary distasteful."

"I find it *precise*, Master. That's worse."

For a moment — a moment only, gone as fast as it came — Quillon looked at her with something that was not mastery at all. It was tiredness, old and load-bearing, the tiredness of a man who has carried a beam on his shoulders so long he has forgotten it is not part of his skeleton.

Then it was gone, and he was drawing a fresh sheet toward himself, and dipping his pen, and becoming again the iron chain and the dry voice.

"The girl requires a custodian. Someone who can read what she says before the scholars finish arguing about whether the language can be read at all. Someone meticulous, incurious in the correct ways, and sufficiently indebted to the guild that her discretion is not a matter of character but of arithmetic." He wrote as he spoke. "You have been selected."

Casper's mouth went dry. "Selected. Or assigned."

"In this room those words are synonyms." He finished writing, blotted the page, and slid it across. "Read your terms."

She read. *Custodial assignment, indefinite duration. Custodian assumes full responsibility for the condition, containment, and accounting of the asset. Any loss, escape, deterioration, or unauthorized communication pertaining to the asset shall be charged against the custodian's outstanding balance.* Below that, the figure of her own debt, copied out in full for the first time in years — forty-one pounds, twelve shillings, sixpence, and the interest that woke up every morning hungry — followed by the words: *said balance now secured additionally against the custodian's person.*

Against her *person*. Not her wages. Her.

"If she runs," Casper said slowly, "I pay for it."

"If she runs, you will spend the rest of your life paying for it, and the guild will spend that same life collecting, and neither of us will enjoy the arrangement." Quillon capped the ink. "If she deteriorates — if the asset loses value — you will answer for the depreciation. If she speaks to anyone unauthorized, you will answer for the disclosure. Do you begin to see the shape of your position, Hartwell?"

"It's a punishment."

"It is a *trust*," Quillon said, "disguised as a punishment, which is how all trusts work. Sign."

"And if I refuse?"

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw in his face the thing she would spend many sleepless nights trying to name: not menace, but recognition. The look of a man seeing, in a younger face, a hunger he remembered feeding on himself.

"Refusal is an entry too," he said quietly. "Everything is an entry. Refuse, and I write it down, and the guild keeps what it keeps, and you will never learn what your grandmother's name is doing in the deep archive."

The room went very still. The lamp flame stood straight up, listening.

"My grandmother," Casper said, "has been dead nineteen years."

"Yes," said Master Quillon. "And her account is still open. Sign the custody order, Hartwell, and perhaps in time you'll be cleared to read why."

He held out the pen.

Outside, the city of Vharen settled into its nighttime sounds — cart wheels, distant bells, the river worrying at its stones — all of it ordinary, all of it bookable, all of it resting atop ledgers no one read anymore. And beneath the floor, beneath the vaults, beneath even the deepest cellar where the bottled thunder muttered in its sleep, that slow vast pulse came again, fainter than before. Fainter than last year. Fainter than the year before that.

Casper took the pen. The nib scratched against the signature line, and the sound it made was small and final, like a match being struck in a very large dark room.

"I'll need access," she said, when it was done. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "To her. To whatever records pertain to the asset. And I'll want the attic above the scriptorium — the old one. If she's to be contained somewhere, it should be somewhere with a lock I hold the key to. My responsibility, my arrangements. It's in the terms."

Quillon almost smiled. Almost. "Meticulous," he said. "I did say you would do."

He rose, gathered his folder, and paused at the door with his hand on the iron handle.

"One more thing, custodian. The asset arrived asking for something. In her language. The wardens couldn't make it out; the scholars are still fighting over the grammar. But your hand wrote it down before they sealed the doors, and I have read your translation." He did not turn around. "Do you know what she asked?"

Casper's throat tightened. "No, Master."

"She asked," Quillon said, "*who remembers me?*" He opened the door. "You do now, Hartwell. Officially. Try to consider what that makes you."

The door closed behind him. Casper sat alone in the windowless room with the lamp and the signed order and the slow, failing heartbeat of something enormous sleeping under the floor, and understood that she had just been handed a key to a door she was now forbidden to open — and that she had never in her life wanted anything the way she wanted to open it.

Above the scriptorium, in the locked attic, a mud-streaked girl was waiting to learn whether anyone in the world still remembered her name.

Casper picked up the lamp, and went to find out.
