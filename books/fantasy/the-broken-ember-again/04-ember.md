## 4. Ember

The attic of the scriptorium smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old paper left too long in the sun — a smell Casper had always found comforting, the way other people found bread comforting. Tonight it felt like a lid on a jar.

She climbed the last rung of the ladder with the girl's supper balanced on one hand — bread, hard cheese, a cup of watered wine she'd watered further when no one was looking — and stepped into a room that the Ledger Guild had forgotten so thoroughly it had almost stopped existing. The rafters sagged under the weight of ledgers nobody had opened in living memory. Boxes of misfiled entries leaned against boxes of misfiled entries. A window the size of a hymnal let in the last grey light of evening, and moths ticked softly against the glass like small insistent clerks demanding an audit.

The girl sat where Casper had left her that morning, cross-legged on a pallet between two crates of condemned records, exactly as still as furniture. She had been still all day. Casper knew because she had come up twice to check, telling herself it was inventory management, custody of assets, nothing more.

"Here." She set the plate down within reach. "Eat. You didn't touch breakfast."

The girl looked at the food without touching it. Her eyes were the color of banked coals — not the flat brown of ordinary eyes but something with heat buried far down in them, the way a hearth holds fire after the fire has gone to sleep. Mud had been scrubbed from her face at Quillon's order, but her hair still held the grit of whatever road she'd walked to reach Vharen, and her hands — small hands, ink-stained now from the pens Casper had given her to keep them busy — folded and unfolded in her lap like two birds deciding whether to fly.

"You can talk," Casper said. "I know you can. You talked at the door yesterday. The whole guild heard you."

Silence. A moth struck the glass again. Somewhere below, the closing bell's echo had long since died, and the building settled into its nighttime creaks — the guildhall breathing around them, stone ribs expanding in the cooling air.

Casper sat down across from her, folding her own legs because standing over the girl felt like something a master would do, and she was tired of being what masters did. "They've assigned me your care," she said. "Custodian. Which is a word they use for people who guard things, and also for people who guard *against* things. I haven't decided which I am yet."

The girl's eyes flicked up. Something moved behind them — not understanding, not quite, but the shape of listening.

"I'm going to tell you something I probably shouldn't," Casper went on, because the attic was dark and high and far from every ear in Vharen, and because she had spent eleven years learning that secrets kept in ledgers were heavier than secrets kept anywhere else. "Yesterday, when you spoke at the door — that language, the one no scholar in this city knows — my hand wrote it down. Before I understood a single word of it. My pen translated you while my head was still catching up." She turned her palm up, studied the ink stains in the creases of her fingers, the permanent shadow of her trade. "Ink isn't supposed to do that. Ink does what it's told. So either I'm going mad, or you're carrying something in you that remembers how to speak to paper."

The girl tilted her head. Then, slowly, deliberately, she reached out and touched the edge of Casper's sleeve — just the wool, just a brush of fingertips — and said a word.

It was not a word in any tongue Vharen owned. It was low and round and warm, a word shaped like a held breath, and as it left her mouth Casper felt her own hand twitch toward the pocket where she kept her pencil, felt the fingers curl around it without permission, felt the sudden terrible urge to *write*.

"No," she said aloud, to herself, to the pencil, to whatever old machinery in her blood had answered the girl's voice. "No writing. Not tonight."

* * *

They ate together. Or rather, the girl ate — cautiously at first, watching Casper take a bite of the same bread before trusting it, a habit that told Casper more than any interrogation could have about what kind of hunger the girl had known — and Casper talked, filling the silence the way she filled margins, with small careful annotations.

"My name is Casper Hartwell," she said. "It's a debt-name, technically. Hartwell entered the guild's books three generations back and never left. I've been indentured since I was nine, which means I have been property for longer than I have been a person, if you follow the arithmetic most people use." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile wasn't bitter. Up here, under the moth-buzzing lamp, bitterness seemed to lose its footing. "You don't have a name in any ledger I can find. Quillon has you logged as *Asset 77-Vellum*. Which is obscene. A number for a person who talks in a language older than the guild."

The girl swallowed her cheese and watched her.

"So you need a name," Casper said. "Not for the books. For you. Names are — " she searched for the words, and found, oddly, that the truest ones came easily here, in the dust and the lamplight — "names are doors. My mother used to say that. Say someone's name right, and you open a door into them. Say it wrong, and you knock on a wall forever."

At the word *doors*, the girl went very still.

Casper noticed. Of course she noticed; noticing discrepancies was the whole of her trade, the one skill the guild had sharpened in her until it cut. "Doors," she repeated, softer. "That meant something. Where did you walk from, child? What country has roads that leave mud like that, and a language that makes paper listen?"

The girl set down her cup. She pressed both palms flat against the floorboards, as though checking that the world beneath her was solid, and then she began — haltingly, in gestures first and then in that round warm language, and finally, word by word, in broken trade-tongue that she must have been assembling all day from the scraps she'd heard spoken around her — to answer.

What she built, fragment by fragment, in the space between the crates and the moths, was this:

A country. She patted the floorboards — *here*, the gesture said, though the gesture could not possibly mean here — and drew shapes in the dust with one finger: a coastline, a curve of hills, streets laid out like the veins of a leaf. A city with lamps that burned without oil. She cupped her hands around an invisible flame and her face changed as she did it, lit from inside by a memory, and for one moment Casper saw what the girl must have looked like in that other place: not a refugee, not an asset, but a child running home through lamplight.

"Fire," the girl said, in trade-tongue, holding the invisible flame. "Never. Out." She shook her cupped hands — *never, ever* — and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "Under. Everything. Under floor. Under city. Sleep. Like — " she frowned, hunted for the word, gave up on trade-tongue, and made instead a slow breathing motion with her whole body, ribs expanding, shoulders rising and falling, the unmistakable rhythm of a great lung at rest.

"A fire that breathes," Casper said quietly. "Under the city."

The girl nodded, satisfied, and moved on — and this was the part that Casper would carry to her grave, the part she would later write down and burn and write down again: the girl mimed trading. One hand giving, one hand taking. She pointed at her own temple, then offered the invisible thing outward — *giving a memory* — then took the invisible thing in — *receiving one*. She did it again, quicker, delighted, and the delight cracked Casper's chest open like ice on a spring river. In the girl's country, her gestures said, people traded memories the way Vhareni traded coin. A first snowfall for a wedding day. A grandmother's laugh for a stranger's lullaby. Nobody hoarded. Everybody carried pieces of everybody else.

"That's why the language works on paper," Casper breathed. "If memories are currency, then words are — " she stopped, because the thought arrived whole and terrible: *then words are minted coin, and whoever controls the words controls the treasury.* The guild. The ledgers. Binding contracts. Her whole life spent keeping books that bound.

But the girl wasn't finished. And what came next she did not mime. She spoke it, in her own tongue, low and steady, and Casper's pencil-hand ached in her pocket the entire time, translating without her consent, so that even now she could read the passage in her mind's eye as though it lay open on a desk:

*One morning the names began to go. Not the people — the names. My mother called me and halfway through calling me her mouth forgot the shape. She stood in the doorway moving her lips around an empty space. By winter she did not know there was a space. The adults forgot us one by one, oldest memories first, like frost eating a window from the edges in. My teacher forgot the alphabet. The baker forgot bread. My mother forgot me last, because mothers are stubborn, and then one day she smiled at me the way you smile at weather.*

The girl said all of this without weeping, in a voice worn smooth as river stone, and somehow that was worse than weeping. Casper sat with her fists in her lap and did not move, because moving would have broken something, and the moths ticked against the glass, and above Vharen the stars came out over a city that had never heard of the country whose last citizen sat starving gently in its attic.

"Why you?" Casper asked at last, barely above a whisper. "Why did they forget everyone and not you?"

The girl considered the question with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she touched her own sternum, over her heart, and said a single word in her own language — the warm, round, ember-colored word she had said when she first touched Casper's sleeve.

And Casper understood, the way her hand understood before her head did, that the word was a name. That it was *her* name. That the girl was the only person left alive who could say it, because saying it required a fire that no longer existed anywhere except behind her ribs.

"They couldn't forget you," Casper said slowly, working it through, "because you're made of the thing they were forgetting. You're not a citizen who survived. You're — " she reached for it — "you're what's left of the country itself. Walking around. Breathing."

The girl shrugged, the shrug of someone who has carried a heavy thing so long it has grown into her spine. Then she pointed at Casper. At Casper's pencil-hand. At the floorboards, and beyond them, down, down through four floors of stone and shelving, down to wherever the guild's foundations met whatever the guild was actually built on top of.

She said another word. This one Casper's hand refused to translate — flinched from translating — and that refusal frightened her more than anything the girl had said all night, because her hand had never once disobeyed her, and things only refuse when they are afraid.

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow and the girl had finally, blessedly, curled onto her pallet and gone to sleep with one hand still cupped around some private warmth — Casper sat alone among the condemned ledgers and tried to think like a bookkeeper instead of like a person, because thinking like a person had become unbearable.

Facts, she told herself. Enter the facts.

Fact: the girl was logged as an asset acquired two hundred years ago, per Quillon's own mouth, and contracts in this guild accrued interest, and interest compounded, and no contract in the history of the Ledger Guild had ever simply expired.

Fact: the girl spoke a dead language that lived in Casper's hand.

Fact: beneath the floorboards of the guildhall, if a child's gestures could be believed, something slept and breathed and was very old, and the girl had pointed *down* with an urgency that no translation was needed to read.

Fact: Casper Hartwell had spent eleven years believing her debt was a number. And numbers, she had always believed, were clean. But the girl's story had gotten under the numbers the way damp gets under plaster, and everything Casper had trusted was beginning to blister and lift.

She looked at the sleeping child — at the coal-dark eyes shut, at the small flame-colored name no one else alive could pronounce — and felt something shift in her chest that had no column in any ledger she'd ever kept. It was not pity. Pity she knew; pity was cheap and the guild dealt in it daily. This was heavier. This was the weight of being *entrusted* with something, and entrusted things, unlike assets, could not be entered and closed out. They stayed open. They accrued.

"Ember," she said softly, to the sleeping girl, trying the word on. Not the true name — she could never say the true name — but a Vhareni shadow of it, a door knocked on gently. "I'll call you Ember. Until you can be called what you are."

The girl did not wake. But the lamp flame, guttering low in its dish, stood up straight for a moment — tall and clear and gold — as though it had heard, as though somewhere far below the floorboards something vast and sleeping had turned over in its dream.

And in the silence that followed, Casper heard — or imagined she heard, though her hands had never once lied to her — a sound rising faintly through the joists of the scriptorium attic. Slow. Rhythmic. Patient as interest.

Something under the guildhall was breathing.

And tonight, for the first time in two hundred years, it sounded labored.
