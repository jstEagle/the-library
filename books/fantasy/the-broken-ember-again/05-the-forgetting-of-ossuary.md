## 5. The Forgetting of Ossuary

Before there was a ledger, there was a lamp.

Ember remembered this the way she remembered breathing — not as a fact but as a rhythm, something her body had done before her mind had any say in it. The lamps of Ossuary were not lit; they were *woken*. Each one held a sliver of dragon-fire, coaxed down from the mountain hearths generations ago and divided and divided again, like bread broken among a family that never stopped growing. You leaned close to a lamp in the evening and spoke to it, softly, the way you'd speak to a sleeping child, and it opened one amber eye and filled the room with light that smelled faintly of warm stone and cinnamon and the far-off memory of smoke.

Her mother's lamp was old. Older than their street, older than the bridge of woven copper that crossed the canal behind their house. It had been woken by Ember's great-great-grandmother, who had carried its flame home from the mountain in a lantern made of pressed glass, walking for nine days so that the fire would learn her heartbeat before she set it down. That was how it worked in Ossuary: nothing was simply owned. Everything was *known into keeping*. A flame you hadn't spoken to would gutter out of spite. A door you hadn't named would open onto someone else's yesterday.

And oh, the doors.

Ember stood on the threshold of her own front door now — stood there in memory, which is to say she stood there truly, because in Ossuary memory was a place you could go. The door was blue as a beetle's back, and carved into its lintel were three words in the old tongue, the words her father had spoken over her cradle when she was born:

*Ash remembers what burns.*

She said them aloud, and the door did what doors did in Ossuary. It did not open onto the street. It opened onto *yesterday* — onto this same threshold at this same hour, but twenty-four hours younger, where her mother knelt in the doorway mending the hem of a coat, humming, unaware that her daughter was watching her from tomorrow. Ember could have stepped through. She could have sat beside her mother's younger self and asked about the song, and her mother would have answered without surprise, because in Ossuary time was less a river than a room, and everyone in the family moved through it the way you moved from kitchen to garden — freely, familiarly, leaving the door open behind you.

That was the first thing the erasure took. Not the past. The *doors* to it.

But she was getting ahead of herself, the way grief always does, running ahead to the wound before it has finished learning your name. Let her stay, a while longer, in the country that was whole.

* * *

Ossuary traded in memories the way other countries traded in salt or wool.

There was a market for it — of course there was; every country has its market — down along the Canal of Small Hours, where the stalls opened only after dusk and closed before the lamps woke. The memory-brokers sat behind counters of black glass, and on the counters they laid out their goods like sweets: a vial of the smell of rain on hot slate; a jar of somebody's grandmother's laugh, still warm; a ribbon of a first snowfall, tied at both ends so it couldn't melt. You could sell a memory you didn't want — the day your tooth was pulled, the humiliation of a wedding gone wrong — and buy one you did. A sailor sold his fear of drowning and bought, instead, the memory of floating, so that even in storms his body remembered peace. A widow sold the worst hour of her husband's dying and kept everything else, and walked home lighter, and lit her lamp, and wept at his photograph without knowing why, and was glad of it.

It was not theft. That was the thing outsiders never understood, the thing the ledgers later twisted. In Ossuary a memory given freely lost none of its truth for the giver. You did not forget what you sold. You simply agreed that someone else could carry it too — the way a song is not diminished by being sung in two houses. Memory in Ossuary was not possession. It was *company*.

Ember's mother had sold memories at that market exactly once. Ember had gone with her; she had been small enough to ride on her mother's hip, her cheek against the wool of her collar. Her mother had sold the memory of a fever — Ember's fever, the winter she was two, the long terrible nights of it — to a young couple whose own child had died of something nameless. They needed to know what surviving felt like, the broker explained, so they could stop being afraid of the shape of their grief. Her mother had held the vial up to the lamplight, and inside it Ember's fever swam like a minnow, all its terror intact, and her mother had said, "Take care of her. She's brave," meaning the memory, meaning Ember, meaning both, and the young woman had wept and thanked her, and they had walked home under the waking lamps with coins in their pockets and nothing missing at all.

Nothing missing. That was the phrase. It was what you said at the market, what you said when you sealed a trade, what you wrote above the doors of the counting-houses: *nothing missing, nothing taken.* A promise that in Ossuary, exchange never meant loss.

Ember would think about that phrase, later, in the years of ash. She would think about it with a bitterness that tasted like copper coins held too long in the mouth.

* * *

The morning the erasure began was a Tuesday, though no one knew yet that it mattered. Tuesdays in Ossuary were for washing linens and arguing politics and feeding the canal-fish, which rose to the surface in silver shoals to be hand-fed bits of honeyed bread. Ember was eleven years old and immortal in the way all children are immortal, which is to say she had plans. She was going to the market with her friend Sefa to trade Sefa's memory of a broken arm — he'd decided it was more trouble than it was worth — and then they were going to climb the bell tower of the Counting-House of Hours and watch the lamps wake across the whole city at once, ten thousand amber eyes opening in the dusk, the whole country blinking itself awake.

She was pulling on her boots when the first word went.

She didn't see it go. That was the cruelty of it — the erasure did not announce itself. It began in the middle of an ordinary sentence, in the middle of an ordinary room, with her mother calling up the stairs:

"Ember, love, if you're going out, take the — take the—"

Her mother's voice stopped. Not hesitated. *Stopped*, like a clock with a finger laid against its hands. And in the silence Ember heard something she had no word for then and has spent every year since trying to find one for: the sound of a word dissolving. It was not silence. Silence is empty. This was full — full of a thing that had been there a moment ago and was now becoming the idea of a thing, and then the rumor of a thing, and then nothing at all, the way a name written on a window in breath fades as the glass dries.

"Take the what?" Ember called down.

And her mother, standing in the kitchen with the basket in her hands, looked at the object she had been reaching for — a flat woven thing, a thing with handles, a thing you carried bread in — and her face did something Ember had never seen a grown face do. It went smooth. Not confused. Smooth, like water closing over a dropped stone. She looked at the basket the way you look at a stranger's hat left on your table: present, inexplicable, faintly embarrassing.

"What was I saying?" her mother asked, and laughed, and the laugh was wrong, it was a laugh with a hole in the middle of it, and she picked up the basket anyway because her hands remembered even though her mind had been tidied, and she set it on the table and did not look at it again.

Ember went downstairs slowly. "Mama. What's that called?"

"That?" Her mother glanced at it. "I don't know. Something of your grandmother's, maybe."

The word for it had been *seleth*. Ember knew this the way you know a dream after waking — not as knowledge but as the scar of knowledge, the shape of where it used to be. She turned the absence over in her mouth. Seleth. Seleth. But even as she reached for it, the reach came back empty, and worse than empty: it came back with a gentle insistence that there had never been anything to reach for, that baskets — no, that *carrying-things* — no, that — and here her own mind smoothed over, water over stone, and she heard herself say, "Never mind," in her mother's own puzzled tone, and the two of them stood in the kitchen together, loving each other completely, one word poorer.

By noon, the whole city was limping on its language.

You could see it in the streets. A fishmonger gesturing at his stall and saying "the — the swimming ones," his customers nodding too quickly, everyone agreeing not to notice that the word *fish* had gone out of the world between one tide and the next. Children inventing words on the spot, because children do, filling gaps with *thingy* and *that-stuff* and sounds they made up whole, and the adults repeating these inventions gratefully, hungrily, because a gap in the language was like a missing tooth — the tongue kept going to it, worrying it, unable to leave it alone. By evening the city had agreed, without ever discussing it, to call the canal-fish *shimmers*, and the agreement felt like charity, and it was actually surrender.

The doors went next.

Ember watched it happen from the bell tower, where she had gone with Sefa after all, because children keep their appointments with wonder even as the world ends. From up there you could see the whole pattern of Ossuary: the copper bridges, the black-glass market stalls, the ten thousand thresholds each carved with its three words, each opening onto some neighbor hour of the family's shared past. And she saw — she *saw* it, and would spend her life failing to make anyone believe she'd seen it — a stillness move across the city like weather. Street by street, door by door, the carvings on the lintels faded. Not chiseled away. *Faded*, the way ink fades, the way breath on glass fades, and behind the fading came the smoothing: doors that had opened onto yesterday now opened onto plain stone walls, or onto today, only today, today forever, the same gray room of the present with no windows in it.

Sefa grabbed her arm. "Ember. Say the words on your door. Say them fast, before I—"

"*Ash remembers what burns,*" she said.

He frowned. "Say what?"

"Ash remembers—" and the sentence came apart in her mouth like wet paper. She could feel the words still, could feel their weight and shape the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth, but when she pushed air through them they made no sense, they were sounds wearing the costumes of words, and beneath them rose that gentle, terrible insistence: *there were never any such words, little one, there was never any such grammar, you are confusing yourself.*

"No," she said aloud, to the tower, to the city, to whatever was doing this. "No, those are mine. My father said them. Over my cradle. He said—"

What had he said?

She had his voice still. That, for now. The low rumble of it, the way it caught when he laughed, the particular warmth of him saying her name — *Ember* — as though the word were a coal he was passing carefully from his hand to hers. She held the voice with both hands, inwardly, the way you hold a bird. But his face was going. She reached for it and found a warmth with no features, a beloved blur, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was how it would go: not all at once, but in pieces, in installments, a word a day, a face a week, until she was standing in the ruins of her own heart unable to read the inscription.

"They're taking the names," Sefa whispered beside her. He was crying without seeming to know it. "Look — look at the Counting-House. Look at the founders' wall."

She looked. The founders' wall bore the names of everyone the country had ever been — every citizen, every generation, cut in flowing script that glowed faintly at dusk, because in Ossuary your name was a small lamp too, and it burned as long as anyone could read it. And the names were going out. Line by line, letter by letter, like windows going dark down a long street at curfew. She watched a name gutters — watched the letters dim, soften, slide — and she heard, very faintly, from somewhere below in the darkening streets, a sound she would carry longer than any word: a woman screaming a scream that ended in the middle, because the woman had forgotten, mid-scream, what she was screaming about.

* * *

They gathered in the temple that night, the ones who still could. The temple of the First Flame, where the mother-dragon's original gift was kept in a hearth the size of a harvest moon, and the elders stood around it holding hands like children crossing a road.

"The ledgers," the eldest was saying, and her voice shook. "Someone has written us into a ledger. Someone has bound us. Words are the anchors of this country — our doors, our names, our trades — and someone has taken the pen and is writing us *out*." She gripped the hands beside her. "We can fight ink with ink. We can remember aloud. We can teach the children the old tongue tonight, every word we can still find, and hide the words inside the children where the pens can't—"

She stopped.

She stood there, mouth open, hands still clasped, looking at the great flame with an expression of mild, pleasant vacancy, and then she smiled at the elder beside her and said, "Lovely evening, isn't it?" and the flame in the hearth flickered, once, like an eye closing against dust.

Ember's mother pulled her close then, pulled her in tight, her chin on the top of Ember's head, and Ember felt her mother's heart going hard and fast against her ear, a frightened bird, and heard her whisper into her hair, over and over, urgent as a prayer, urgent as a debt:

"Your name is Ember. Your name is Ember. Your name is Ember. Hold it. Whatever happens, hold it. Don't let go of it. Your name is—"

"Mama," Ember said. "Mama, I know. Mama, I'm not letting—"

But even as she said it, she felt it begin: a softness at the edges of the word, a loosening, as though the name were a knot someone else's fingers were patiently working at from the other side of her. *Ember.* She said it harder. *EMBER.* She dug her nails into her palm and said it into her mother's collar, into the wool, into the dark. And somewhere very far away — beyond the temple, beyond the mountains, in some gray room she could not imagine, at some desk she could not picture, above some page she would spend two hundred years trying to reach — she felt, distinctly, the scratch of a pen.

And the pen was not finished.

* * *

In the attic of the Ledger Guild of Vharen, two centuries and a world away, a mud-streaked girl woke with the word still in her mouth — the last word, the one she had held onto through everything, the one she had hidden so deep that even the ledgers had failed to dig it out.

She lay in the straw a moment, listening to the strange dry building breathe around her, listening to the scratch of a scribe's pen somewhere below.

Then she sat up, wrapped her arms around her knees, and said it aloud to the dark, quietly, the way you feed a small fire:

"Ember. My name is Ember."

Below her, faintly, the pen scratched on.
