## 6. Ledger of Debts

The oldest volume in the Ledger Guild of Vharen was kept on the lowest shelf of the deepest archive, and it was not locked.

Casper had noticed this years ago, when she was still new enough to find the guild's habits strange rather than merely exhausting. Everything else in Vharen was locked — the ink stores, the counting rooms, the doors at closing bell, even the privies required a token from the duty clerk. But the founding ledger sat in its alcove behind a curtain of grey felt, gathering dust that no one was permitted to sweep, because dust, Master Quillon had once said, was evidence of time, and time was an asset the guild did not trade in.

She thought about that now as she stood before the curtain with a lamp in one hand and her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

It was past midnight. She should have been asleep in the custodian's cot beside Ember's pallet in the scriptorium attic, listening to the girl breathe in a language Casper still could not name but whose rhythms had begun to feel like home. Instead she lay awake turning over the phrase Quillon had used that afternoon — *asset logged two hundred years ago* — and the way he had said *two hundred* with the flat certainty of a man reading from a page he'd memorized. Two hundred years. The girl was perhaps ten. Which meant the contract predated her, which meant someone else had signed it, which meant—

Which meant names. And if there were names, there were records. And if there were records, they would be here, in the place where the guild kept everything it refused to let anyone forget.

The felt curtain whispered aside under her fingers. Cold air rolled out of the alcove, smelling of stone and old tallow and something faintly sweet, like fruit rotting in a sealed room. The founding ledger lay open on its lectern — always open, she realized now; she had simply never looked — its pages the colour of tea left too long, its margins crowded with annotations in hands spanning generations.

Casper set down the lamp and wiped her palms on her apron. Her fingers, so steady over columns all day, shook now like a novice's.

She began at the beginning.

The first pages were contracts of incorporation: the guild's charter, witnessed by three magistrates whose names had been struck through in red — struck through, she noted, in the same precise horizontal line the guild used to close a settled account. Further in, the entries grew stranger. Land grants for parcels that read like descriptions of weather. A deed of purchase for *one (1) season, autumn, unspoiled*. A bill of sale for *the sound of bells, parish of [illegible], forty-one in number*.

Her skin prickled. These were the impossible things. Not curiosities acquired by eccentric collectors, as she'd always assumed — but bought and sold here, at the root, where the guild's whole tree had grown from.

She turned pages faster. The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick without looking away from the paper.

And then, near the bottom third of the volume, in a hand smaller and more careful than any around it, she found a name she knew.

*Sandoval.*

Not her own. Not her mother's. *Maren Sandoval* — her grandmother, dead before Casper turned six, a woman who existed in memory only as flour-dusted knuckles and a humming voice and the smell of yeast rising in a kitchen that had since been sold twice over. Maren Sandoval, party to a contract dated one hundred and ninety-eight years after the guild's founding. Forty-odd years before Casper was born.

Casper read the entry once. Then again, slower, moving her lips over the words as though sounding them aloud might change their meaning.

*Maren Sandoval, of the parish of Lowbridge, does hereby sell unto the Ledger Guild of Vharen the following: one (1) location, being the last standing door of the country called Ossuary, together with all rights of passage thereto, in perpetuity.*

In consideration whereof: the preservation of one (1) name from erasure, being *Ilse Sandoval*, daughter of the seller.

Casper's knees went soft. She caught the lectern's edge and held on.

*Ilse.* Her mother. Her mother, who had died plain and ordinary and beloved, who had never once in Casper's hearing said anything stranger than *mind the wet paint*. Her mother's name had been purchased. Bought back from something — from some *process* — the way one might buy a child out of indenture. And the price had been a door. The last door out of a country Casper had never heard of until four days ago, when a mud-streaked girl had spoken a language no living scholar knew and Casper's own hand had translated it before her mind could catch up.

*Ossuary.* The word sat in her mouth like a coin she'd swallowed years ago and only now coughed up.

Below the entry, in the margin, in her grandmother's small careful hand — the same hand, Casper realized with a lurch, that had written the recipe card still pinned above her own stove — ran a single line:

*I am sorry for what I could not carry. Forgive me what I chose.*

There was more. There was always more; the guild saw to that. Beneath the principal transaction, the ledger recorded the interest schedule, and Casper — who could calculate compound interest in her sleep, who had been doing it since she was nine years old — followed the arithmetic down the column with mounting horror. The debt had not been discharged. It had been *transferred*, generation to generation, each heir inheriting the balance plus accrued interest, until the obligation came to rest on the current holder of the name.

On her.

Every copper she owed. Every year of her indenture. Every deduction from her wage, every fine levied against her for smudged entries or late tallies — none of it had ever been payment toward anything. It was interest. Interest on a debt incurred before her birth, for a purchase she had never agreed to, secured against a country she had never known existed.

She was not poor. She was *mortgaged*.

Casper stood in the cold alcove for a long time, the lamp burning low, and felt something in her chest rearrange itself — the way ice shifts on a river just before it breaks.

* * *

She did not go back to the attic. She climbed instead, past the counting rooms and the scriptorium, up the narrow stair to the masters' gallery, where the lamps burned all night behind frosted glass, and knocked on the door marked with Quillon's seal.

He opened it himself, which surprised her. She had expected a clerk, a rebuke, an escort back to bed. Instead Master Quillon stood in his doorway in shirtsleeves, grey-haired, unarmoured by his usual formality, holding a cup of something steaming, and for one absurd moment he looked less like a master of the Ledger Guild than like an old man who could not sleep either.

"You've been in the archive," he said. Not a question.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you wouldn't." He stepped back from the door. "Come in, then. Shouting wakes the building."

The room beyond was spare almost to cruelty: a desk, a chair, a shelf of ledgers, a window overlooking the dark quadrangle. On the desk lay a single sheet of paper covered in figures, half-finished. Casper looked at it and understood, with a strange calm, that Quillon had been doing the same arithmetic she had. Perhaps for decades.

"Say it," he said. "I want to hear you say it."

"My grandmother sold the door." Her voice came out level, which astonished her. "The last door out of Ossuary. To save my mother's name. And I have spent my life paying interest on it." She set the words down between them like coins on a counter. "You knew. You assigned me to the girl knowing exactly whose granddaughter you were putting in that attic."

Quillon lowered himself into his chair. He looked suddenly very tired, the way furniture looks when someone finally sits in it after years.

"Yes," he said.

"Why?" The calm cracked. Fury came up through it like water through floorboards. "Was it a joke to you? Some elegant symmetry? Put the debtor's blood next to the asset and see what happens?"

"It was mercy," Quillon said quietly, "the only kind I have left to give. That girl is going to die in this building, Casper. Assets are liquidated when their value exceeds their carrying cost, and her carrying cost grows daily while her value—" He stopped. Started again. "I put you there because you are the best scribe this guild has produced in thirty years, and because your grandmother's handwriting is in that ledger, and because I thought — foolishly, sentimentally — that blood might remember what ink has tried to make it forget."

"Forget." Casper seized the word. "You keep using that word. Forget. Ledgers don't make people forget, Master Quillon. Ledgers record. That's the entire point of them. That's the *religion* of them. You taught me that myself, my first week — *what is written cannot be unwritten*—"

"No," Quillon said.

The word fell into the room like a stone into a well. Casper heard it land far below.

"No," he repeated. "That is what we tell the apprentices, because the truth would empty the guildhall in a night. Listen to me, girl, and listen carefully, because I will say this once and then we will both pretend I didn't." He leaned forward, and the lamplight carved deep shadows under his eyes. "A ledger is not a mirror. It is a *hand*. What the guild writes, becomes. When we record that a widow's second shadow exists, it exists. When we record that a season was sold, it can be bought. And when we record—" his voice roughened, "—when we record that a country does not exist, then it does not. Not in memory. Not in maps. Not in the mouths of the people who loved it. The ledgers don't record forgetting, Casper. They *enforce* it. Two hundred years ago this guild wrote Ossuary out of the world, line by line, contract by contract, and the world obeyed us, because the world has always obeyed its books."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if afraid to move.

Casper thought of the bottled thunder in the inventory vault. Of the widow's shadow, filed under *miscellaneous effects*. Of Ember, saying words that dissolved off her tongue mid-sentence, and the way the girl had wept without knowing what she wept for. Of the fire beneath the floor, breathing like a lung, keeping alive a country that existed nowhere but in a furnace room and a child's failing memory.

"You erased her," Casper said. "All of them. An entire country of people, and you wrote them out like a bad entry."

"The guild did. Before my time. Before my father's time." Quillon's jaw worked. "I was born there, Casper."

The fury in her chest stuttered.

"I was born in Ossuary," he said, "and I was six years old when the writing began, and I survived because my parents paid the guild the only currency it accepted — memories, traded away like coin until they forgot me first and I forgot them, and what was left was a boy with no country and a talent for numbers, which the guild took in, the way it takes in everything it has unmade." He laughed, short and terrible. "You want fury? Keep yours. Mine burned out fifty years ago and left nothing but ash and bookkeeping. Yours is young. Use it while it's warm."

Casper's hands had curled into fists without her noticing. She forced them open. Her palms ached; she'd driven her nails in.

"The door," she said. "My grandmother sold the last door. Where is it?"

Quillon looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes — fear, or pity, or the ghost of a boy watching his country be written away.

"That information," he said, "is the single most dangerous sentence in this building. If you speak it aloud anywhere the walls can hear — and the walls are the ledger, Casper, the guildhall itself is a ledger made stone — the guild will know within the hour, and the liquidation of your little Ember will proceed tomorrow morning instead of next month."

"Then write it," Casper said. "You're a master. Write it where only I can find it. You've spent fifty years being ash — be something else for one night."

Quillon rose. He crossed to his shelf of ledgers, ran his finger along the spines, and drew out a slim volume Casper had never seen, bound in leather gone soft as cloth. From it he took a loose sheet, yellowed, folded into eighths. He held it a moment, weighing it, this old man with his cold tea and his terrible knowledge.

"Your grandmother left a note in the margin of the founding ledger," he said. "*Forgive me what I chose.* Did you read it?"

"Yes."

"She wasn't asking forgiveness for selling the door." Quillon pressed the folded paper into Casper's hand and closed her fingers over it. "She was asking forgiveness for what she couldn't do — she couldn't carry the location out of the archive herself. They watched her too closely. So she hid it, and she trusted that someday, someone with her blood and her stubbornness would come looking." His voice dropped. "I've kept that paper for forty years waiting for you to be angry enough to ask. Go back to the attic. Read it by the girl's light. And Casper—" he caught her arm at the door, grip fierce, "—whatever you find, whatever you decide: the fire beneath the floor is dying faster than the ledgers admit. You have days. Perhaps fewer."

Casper stood in the corridor with the folded paper burning against her palm like a coal, and below her feet, deep beneath the guildhall's stones, she felt — or imagined she felt — the great slow breath of the sleeping flame, drawing shorter.

Somewhere above, in the scriptorium attic, a girl slept in a language no one else alive could speak.

Casper climbed toward her, taking the stairs two at a time.
