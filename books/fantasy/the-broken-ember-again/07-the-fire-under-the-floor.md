## 7. The Fire Under the Floor

Ember felt it first in her teeth.

She was sitting cross-legged on the attic floor, watching Casper's pen move across the page — that quick, careful scratching, like a beetle walking through dry grass — when the ache began. It started low, behind her back molars, a dull pressure as if something were trying to be born there. Then it spread down into her jaw, her throat, the hollow of her chest, and she understood what it was before she had any word for it.

The fire was hungry.

Not hungry the way fire is usually hungry — greedy, leaping, wanting. This was a different hunger. The hunger of something very old trying to breathe through a straw.

"Ember?" Casper had looked up. She always noticed when Ember went still; she said Ember went still the way other people screamed. "What's wrong?"

Ember pressed her palm flat against the attic floorboards. The wood was cold. That was wrong too. Everything in Ossuary had been warm at its bones — the streets in winter, the well-water, the doorframes you leaned against while talking to a neighbor. Warm because underneath everything, under every road and root cellar and grave, ran the fire. Not a fire you could see. A fire you could *feel*, the way you feel your own heartbeat without putting a hand to your wrist.

"It's dying faster," she said. Her voice came out strange, rusty. Some words of the old tongue still lived in her mouth, but this came out in guild-speech, flat and gray. "Underneath. It's — " She searched for the word and found only the shape of it, a hollow where meaning used to sit. "It's getting smaller."

Casper set down her pen. Ember had learned to read Casper's silences the way she'd once read weather: the small frown meant calculation, the pressed lips meant fear being filed away for later, the slow blink meant Casper was deciding how much truth she could afford. Now all three came at once, stacked like coins.

"You can feel it? From up here?"

"I could feel it from across an ocean." Ember kept her hand on the boards. "If there were an ocean anymore. If there were anything anymore." She looked up. "You keep it down there, don't you. Your guild. You built your house on top of it."

"We didn't build anything on top of anything," Casper said, too quickly, and then stopped, because they both heard what she'd said. What she'd admitted.

Ember stood. Her knees cracked; she was growing, Casper had told her, feeding her bread and cheese and apples with the grim determination of a woman balancing accounts. Growing hurt. Growing meant time was passing, and time passing meant the fire had less of it left.

"Take me down," Ember said.

"There's nothing down — "

"Casper." She said the name carefully, the way you hold something with a chipped edge. "I have been patient in this attic for — I don't know how long. Days. Weeks. My country is under this floor, or under this city, or under whatever your guild nailed down over its grave, and it is *dying*, and I can taste it going out like a wick." Her voice shook. She hated that it shook. In Ossuary, no one's voice ever shook, because grief was something you could trade away when it got too heavy. Here, grief just accumulated, like debt. Like everything here accumulated. "Take me down. Or I will find my own way, and I will break things doing it, and then your masters will ask you why."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. Then she did the thing Ember had come to recognize as the truest thing about her: she picked up her pen, considered it, and set it down again — deliberately, facing away from herself, as if disarming it.

"All right," she said. "But we go quietly, and we go now, during the change of watch. And if anyone asks, you're inventory being moved to lower storage. That's even true." Her mouth twisted around the word *inventory*. "That's the one lie I won't have to tell."

* * *

The guildhall had more floors below the ground than above it. Ember counted them as they descended, and lost count somewhere past the fifth staircase, because the stairs stopped behaving like stairs.

They were narrow things, spiraling down through stone that grew older as they went — not older the way ruins are old, but older the way *language* is old. The walls changed under her fingertips. Upstairs, the guildhall was all pale dressed granite and brass fittings, everything squared and numbered and labeled. Down here the stone went dark and grainy, shot through with veins of something that glimmered faintly when she dragged her hand across it, like fish scales, like ash that hadn't finished deciding whether it was dead.

And the doors. Every landing had a door, and every door was sealed — not locked, *sealed*, with lead strips hammered across the frames and stamped with the guild's seal: a closed book over crossed quills. Wax had been poured into the keyholes and the wax itself had writing pressed into it, tiny characters Ember couldn't read but could *feel*, the way you feel a headache coming.

"What are these rooms?" she whispered.

"Storage," Casper said. But her voice had gone thin. "Lower archive. I've never — I didn't know any of this existed. I've worked here six years and I thought the sub-levels were three floors of wine cellars and paper stores." She ran her lantern along a wall where someone had scratched tally marks, hundreds of them, thousands, descending into darkness. "This isn't on any plan I've ever filed."

"They sealed them," Ember said. "Your guild sealed them and then sealed the knowing of them. That's what they do. That's all they do." She touched one of the lead seals and flinched. Cold. Colder than the stone, colder than the dark, a cold with intention in it. "These aren't storage rooms. These are rooms that used to be *somewhere else*."

Casper stared at her. "What do you mean, somewhere else?"

Ember pressed her ear against the door instead of answering. Beneath the lead, beneath the wax, beneath two hundred years of silence, she heard it: rain. Rain falling on tile roofs, on clay, on leaves. A specific rain. The soft double-patter of Ossuary's autumn rains, the ones that came off the mountains in the ninth month and smelled of iron and crushed mint.

Her eyes stung. "There's a street behind this door," she said. "Or what's left of one. They folded the streets up and put them away, like linens." She stepped back. "Come on. It's louder below."

"Louder?"

"The fire. Listen." She took Casper's lantern-hand and pressed it flat against the stairwell wall, the way her mother had once pressed Ember's hand to her own throat so she could feel the hum of the hearth-dragon sleeping under their quarter. "Feel."

Casper's face changed. Ember watched it happen — watched the scribe's composure crack and slide like ice on a spring river. Whatever she felt through the stone, it frightened her.

"That's not possible," Casper breathed. "That's a heartbeat. That's — that's enormous."

"She's breathing shallow," Ember said. "Come."

* * *

The last staircase was not a staircase. It was a throat.

The walls narrowed and then opened all at once, and Ember walked out of the dark onto a ledge of black rock, and stopped, and forgot how to breathe.

The furnace room — cavern wasn't a big enough word; neither was cathedral — stretched away beneath the guildhall's roots like the inside of a buried heart. The ceiling arched up into darkness where the foundations of Vharen hung upside-down, pillars of masonry descending like stalactites, and between them, filling the whole floor of the cavern, lay the fire.

Not burning. *Sleeping.*

It was coiled in a great basin of fused glass — sand melted to mirror by centuries of its presence — and it was small, that was the terrible thing, it was no bigger than a hay wagon, a heap of embers the color of old blood and cooling iron. But every few moments the whole heap *rose*, swelling like the chest of a sleeping animal, and light rolled off it in a slow wave across the cavern walls, and Ember saw that the rock itself was veined with gold — with dragon-gold, the metal that isn't metal, the stuff that runs in the veins of fire-drakes when they give their hearts to a place instead of keeping them for themselves.

And with each breath, the light came back weaker.

Ember stood on the ledge with her hands pressed to her mouth and wept without noise, because she knew her. She knew this fire. Not this fire specifically — fires don't live that long, even these fires — but her line, her lineage, the daughter-of-daughter-of-daughter of the flame that had warmed Ossuary since before Ossuary had a name. Somewhere in the erasure, in the folding-up of streets and the unwriting of names, the last drake-heart had been brought here, banked, caged, and put to work. Two hundred years of heating the guild's contracts. Two hundred years of holding a country in stasis, one ember at a time.

"She's almost out," Ember said. Her voice echoed and came back wrong, flattened, as if the cavern itself were tired. "Do you understand? When she goes out — "

"What happens?"

"Everything that's held stops being held." Ember turned to look at Casper, and saw her own fear reflected and doubled in the scribe's face. "The streets behind those doors. The memories in your ledgers. Me. We're all — " she groped for the guild-speech word and found it, ugly and perfect, "— *accrued*. All of it resting on her. And she has been burning backwards, Casper. Burning down instead of up. Feeding on — " she faltered. On what? On the dregs of a country. On stored summers. On the memory of warmth itself. "On whatever's left to feed on. And there isn't much left."

Below them, the ember-heap drew breath. Rose. Fell. The light swept the cavern, gilded the hanging pillars, caught the sweat on Casper's brow — and guttered, dimming at its edges, and for half a heartbeat the whole vast room went gray as ash.

Then it rose again. Barely.

"How long?" Casper asked. Her voice was the ledger-clerk's voice, the one that wanted numbers, because numbers could be carried and feared could not. "Ember. How long does she have?"

Ember listened. She closed her eyes and listened with the oldest part of herself, the part the guild couldn't file, and what she heard was a rhythm like a slowing pulse, like footsteps walking away down a long corridor, like the last page of a book turning toward its cover.

"Days," she said. "A handful of days. Maybe less, if they draw on her hard." She opened her eyes. "Your masters must know. They must be able to feel it too. So either they're doing something about it — or they're deciding what to salvage before she goes."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. Down in the basin, the fire breathed, and breathed, and each breath bought a little less time than the one before.

"Ember," Casper said slowly, "if the ledgers are binding contracts — if everything your country is, is written down and enforced by ink — then what happens to all of it when the fire that enforces the ink goes out?"

Ember looked at the dying heart of her country, coiled small as a campfire in a room built to hold a god.

"I think," she said, "we're about to find out. Unless we write something first."

She felt rather than saw Casper turn to look at her — felt the weight of the scribe's attention settle on her like a hand.

"Write what?"

"The true ledger," Ember said. "The one they burned. The one with my country *in* it, instead of *under* it." She pointed down at the guttering flame, and her finger did not shake, though everything else in her wanted to. "Because if she dies with the false books still standing, Ossuary doesn't get free, Casper. Ossuary just ends. And I am not — "

The word broke in her throat. *I am not going to let that happen.* But underneath it, quieter, older, in the tongue that only she remembered: *I am the last citizen, and a citizen's job is to keep the fire.*

Above them, far above, through five sealed floors and a city's worth of stone, a bell rang the hour. And in the silence after it faded, both of them heard it — faint, unmistakable, from the direction of the stairs:

Footsteps. More than one pair. Descending.
