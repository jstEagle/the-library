## 8. Interest Accrued

The notice came down from the upper floors on a Tuesday, pinned to the assignment board in the tally room with a brass pin the size of a thumbnail, and Casper read it three times before the words stopped sliding away from her.

*By order of the Masters of the Ledger Guild of Vharen: Asset 4471-O (provenance: Ossuary, residual) to be assessed for liquidation. Conversion of intangible holdings to commence upon completion of inventory. Custodian to prepare asset for valuation.*

Asset 4471-O. Ember.

Casper stood before the board with her ledger satchel still slung across her body, ink-stained fingers resting on the strap, and felt the words rearrange themselves into something she had spent a month refusing to see. She had thought — she had *told herself* — that custodianship was a punishment, yes, but a survivable one. That she would keep the girl fed and quiet and out of the masters' way until the guild's attention drifted elsewhere, the way it always did, the way it drifted from everything that didn't accrue.

But nothing in the guild failed to accrue. That was the first law of the books. Interest was not a punishment here; interest was the natural state of all things. Debts grew like mold in a damp cellar, like ivy over a headstone, patient and green and inevitable. And Ember was not a ward or a charge or a child under protection. Ember was an *asset*, logged two centuries ago under a contract whose principal had long since been paid, whose interest had compounded through generations of clerks who never once asked what it was they were collecting on.

Liquidation. Casper knew what the word meant in guild terms. She had entered it into ledgers often enough, in her neat, small, blameless hand. Liquidation meant that whatever could be sold, was. A bankrupt weaver's loom went to auction; a drowned merchant's ships were broken for timber; and an asset's intangible holdings — its memories, its language, its griefs, its lullabies — were drawn off like sap from a tapped tree, decanted into vials and phials and stoppered jars, and priced by weight of wonder.

She had shelved those vials. Row upon row in the vault beneath the tally room, each one labeled in clerk's shorthand: *rain, coastal, uncommon*. *Mother's voice, fragmentary*. *The smell of bread in a city that no longer exists*. She had assumed, with the comfortable cruelty of someone doing a job, that they came from somewhere else. From dead people. From no one.

From a country that had been tapped dry two hundred years ago and left standing, hollowed, while the guild collected the drip.

And now they had found one last living vein.

* * *

She climbed to the scriptorium attic faster than caution allowed, taking the narrow stair two steps at a time, and found Ember sitting cross-legged by the dormer window with the slate Casper had given her balanced on her knees. The afternoon light came through the glass thick and gold, full of dust motes turning like slow snow, and the girl was drawing — not letters this time, but shapes. Circles within circles. A spiral that might have been a shell or a staircase or a snail's dream.

She looked up when Casper came in, and her face did the thing it always did, the thing that had undone Casper's transactional heart without her permission: it lit. Not the way children in stories lit up, all at once and sweetly, but the way a lamp lights when you trim the wick — a small adjustment, a steadying, as if Casper's arrival simply made the room burn cleaner.

"You're walking fast," Ember said. Her Vharenne had improved enormously in a month, though the old tongue still lived underneath it like a river under ice; sometimes mid-sentence she'd slip, and a word would come out round and ringing and utterly untranslatable, and she'd laugh at Casper's face. "Fast-walking means bad news. In Ossuary we said the messenger's feet know before his mouth does."

"Ember." Casper sat down across from her, on the floor, because the attic had one chair and she couldn't bear to put it between them. "There's something I have to tell you, and I need you to let me tell all of it before you say anything."

Ember set down the slate. She folded her hands in her lap, an oddly formal gesture, and waited. Two hundred years old, perhaps. Eleven years old, certainly. Both at once, the way a tree is older than its rings.

"The guild is going to take your memories," Casper said. "The ones you have left. They're going to draw them out and bottle them and sell them, the way they've been selling your country for two hundred years. It's called liquidation. It starts — soon. Days." Her voice held. She was proud of that, later, in a grim and useless way. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from me and not from a stranger with a tapping-needle. And I'm telling you because I am going to do something about it, and if it goes wrong, I wanted you to know that I tried."

Ember was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a gull cried over the rooftops of Vharen, and somewhere below them the guildhall ticked and settled around its secrets like an old man around his pains.

"My mother," Ember said finally, "used to hide things in her cheeks. Seeds. When the forgetting started, when people began to lose whole streets, whole *seasons* — she'd hold seeds in her mouth so there was always something true in her body that wasn't written anywhere. She said the guild can only take what's recorded." Her eyes came up, dark and steady. "Is that true? Can they only take what's recorded?"

"I don't know," Casper admitted. "I think — I think memories that live only in you are harder to draw. But they'll have ways. They've had two hundred years of practice."

"Then we do what my mother did." Ember picked the slate back up, and her chin set in a line that Casper would come to know very well in the weeks ahead: the line of someone deciding, not hoping. "We hide things where they aren't written. You're a writer of books. Where would you hide something from other writers of books?"

Casper opened her mouth to answer — and stopped, because the answer arrived fully formed, cold and complete, the way answers do when they've been waiting in you longer than you knew.

"In plain sight," she said. "In the binding itself."

* * *

That night, after the closing bell and the masters' departure and the long ritual of locking the tally room — three keys, two wards, one spoken seal in a language that predated the guild's own founding — Casper sat alone at her desk with a single candle and the day-book for the current quarter open before her, and committed her first crime.

It should be said that Casper Hartwell was, by every measure the guild possessed, an excellent scribe. Her figures balanced to the copper. Her hand was legible without being memorable. She had never once, in nine years of indenture, required correction. This was not virtue. This was survival: in a house where bookkeeping was binding, errors were wounds, and wounds in the books festered into debts, and debts into ruin. She had watched a fellow apprentice ruined over a transposed digit — not fined, not dismissed, but *entered*, his name moved from the column of assets to the column of liabilities, after which the guild's interest worked on him like water works on a cliff. He was still in the building somewhere. She tried not to think about it.

So she knew the books the way a deer knows the forest: every path, every snare, every place where the ground went soft. And she knew, better than anyone alive except perhaps Master Quillon himself, that the guild's ledgers did not merely record reality. They *enforced* it. An entry made correctly became true. An entry made incorrectly became a debt. And an entry made skillfully falsely —

An entry made skillfully falsely became a different truth entirely.

The procedure for liquidation was codified, ancient, and rigid. Before any asset could be assessed, a preparatory survey must be filed: a full accounting of the asset's remaining holdings, itemized, witnessed, and entered into the day-book under the custodian's seal. The survey triggered the assessment. The assessment authorized the drawing. The chain was iron, and iron chains are only as strong as their weakest link, and the weakest link in every chain the guild had ever forged was a tired clerk alone at night with a candle and a pen.

Casper dipped her nib. Her hand, she noted distantly, was perfectly steady. Some part of her had expected it to shake; instead there was a strange clarity, a hush, like the moment after lightning when the air goes clean and thin. The thrill of it frightened her more than trembling would have. *This is what Quillon feels,* she thought, and then, more honestly: *this is what my grandmother felt.* Because someone in her bloodline had already done this — had already stood in front of these same books and traded truth for love — and the debt Casper carried, the unpayable inherited interest that had bound her to this desk since she was sixteen, had flowed from exactly this kind of night, this kind of candle, this kind of steady, terrible hand.

She began to write.

The survey took shape beneath her nib, line by careful line, and every line was a lie dressed in perfect truth. *Asset 4471-O, intangible holdings:* she wrote, and then she gave the guild what it expected — fragments, dregs, the leavings of two centuries of tapping. *Residual memory, auditory: one (1) lullaby, partial, degraded.* There had been four lullabies once. Ember had sung them to her in the attic, all four, in the old tongue, and Casper had wept and pretended she hadn't. Three of them she now entered as *degraded beyond recovery* — technically arguable, if degradation were measured in the guild's own units, which measured fidelity to original recording rather than survival in a living throat. The books defined the terms. Let the books hang themselves.

*Residual memory, olfactory: none recoverable.* Ember remembered the smell of Ossuary's rain — had described it to Casper in exhaustive, heartbreaking detail, rain that smelled of hot stone and something flowering that had no Vharenne name. Casper entered *none recoverable*, and the entry, made correctly, made it so — or would, once sealed. Somewhere in the girl's mind, a scent would dim, fold itself away behind glass, untouchable. Casper felt the cost of that like a tooth pulled. Every lie she wrote would take something real from Ember, wall it off against the guild's needles, and the wall would not distinguish between thief and protector. Protection and theft, done in this ink, wore the same gloves.

*Holdings total, assessed value:* and here Casper paused, nib hovering, and performed the arithmetic that mattered. The guild's assessors worked from the survey's stated value. Too low, and the discrepancy against prior records would raise flags; too high, and the liquidation would proceed with vigor. What she needed was not to stop the process — she could not stop it, not openly, not yet — but to *slow* it, to stretch days into weeks, because the cartographer's note in her grandmother's margin-hand had promised a meeting, and meetings took time, and time was the one commodity the fire beneath the floor was spending faster than anyone above it knew.

So she wrote a value that was precise, defensible, and poisoned: an assessment built on a clause so obscure that only three clerks in the guild's history had ever invoked it — the *dormancy provision*, which held that assets whose provenance-country no longer legally existed could not be liquidated at standard rates, but must instead undergo *reclassification*, a process requiring review by the full conclave of masters, in session, with witnesses. Reclassification took months. Reclassification had been invoked twice in two hundred years and both times had died in committee.

She finished the final figure, blotted it, and sat back.

The candle flame leaned and righted. The guildhall breathed around her, that vast slow respiration she had felt ever since the night Ember showed her the furnace far below — the sleeping dragon-fire inhaling, exhaling, thinner now, a lung going gray. And the books, she realized, were answering it. As she looked down at the day-book, the fresh ink seemed to sink slightly into the page, deeper than blotting could account for, the way water sinks into a mouth. The entry was being *swallowed*. Accepted. Filed into the great body of the guild's record like a coin into a miser's purse.

Something in the building shifted — not sound, exactly, but attention. Casper had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that the guildhall had noticed her. Not Quillon, not the night watch, but the building itself, the ledger made stone, the architecture of enforcement turning one blind marble eye toward the small warm point of fraud burning at a desk in the tally room. Somewhere far off, a door closed that no one had touched. Somewhere below, a page turned by itself.

Her hands were shaking now. Now, when it was done. The thrill drained out of her and left terror in its place, terror with a strange sweetness underneath, like honey poured over a blade. She had crossed. There was no uncrossing. Nine years of blameless service, and she had burned it in forty minutes with a borrowed clause and a steady nib, and the worst part — the part she would carry like a coal in her chest — was that she would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, as many tomorrows as Ember needed, because some debts were worth accruing.

She sealed the survey with her own stamp, pressed her thumbprint beside it in the wax as regulation demanded, and blew out the candle.

In the dark, the day-book lay open on her desk, and she could have sworn the blank page opposite her entry was warmer than the air of the room — waiting, the way a creditor waits, patient and green and inevitable, for the interest to come due.

Three floors above, in the masters' wing, a bell that had not rung in sixty years sounded once, softly, and stopped.

Casper stood in the darkness of the tally room, listening to it fade, and understood that the reclassification clause would buy her time — but that the guild had already begun to ask who had spent it.
