## 10. Borders That Unwrite

Ember had never taught anyone anything before.

In Ossuary, children learned the way rain learned to fall — by being surrounded by it, soaked in it, until it was simply what they were. There had been no lessons, no slate and chalk, no patient repetition. Her mother had sung the door-songs while kneading bread, and Ember had absorbed them the way dough absorbed water, without effort, without knowing she was learning at all.

Now she sat cross-legged on the attic floor of a guild that had eaten her country, trying to teach its grammar to a scribe with ink-stained fingers, and discovered that knowing a thing and giving it away were two entirely different acts — like swallowing and feeding, like breathing and singing.

"Say it again," Casper said.

Ember said it again. The word for *threshold* — *hesseth* — three sounds folded into one another like hands clasped in prayer. The first sound opened. The second sound named what stood on either side. The third sound asked permission of both.

Casper's mouth stumbled through it. Her accent was terrible. It was also, Ember thought with a strange tightness in her chest, the most beautiful thing she had heard in two hundred years, because it was the first time anyone had tried.

"In Ossuary," Ember said, drawing shapes in the dust with one finger, "you didn't build a door and then walk through it. That's backwards. That's—" she searched for the guild's words, "—that's like writing a ledger entry before the trade happens."

"We would call that fraud," Casper said dryly.

"Yes. Fraud." Ember smiled despite herself. "The door came second. First you spoke the place. A threshold is just a name said twice — once for where you are, once for where you're going. If the names are true, the space between them gets... thin. Soft. You step through the way you'd step through a curtain."

"And if the names aren't true?"

"Then you're just a person standing in a doorway saying words." Ember looked down at her finger, tracing the same shape over and over in the dust: a line crossed by another line, the sign they drew above every door in Ossuary, the mark that meant *this opening has been introduced to itself*. "That's why the erasure worked. They didn't burn the country. They burned the names. A door with no true name on either side isn't locked. It's nothing. It's a hole in a wall leading nowhere, forever."

The attic was quiet. Below them, the guildhall breathed its evening sounds — the settling of shelves, the tick of the great clock in the tally-hall, the distant murmur of masters closing their books. Somewhere under all of it, so deep that only Ember felt it, the dragon-fire exhaled its slow, failing breath.

She felt it gutter as she sat there. A long inhale. A shorter exhale than yesterday. She had stopped mentioning it to Casper. Some arithmetic was too cruel to say aloud.

"Show me again," Casper said. "Slowly. I want to write it down."

"No writing." Ember covered the dust-shapes with her palm. "Not yet. The old tongue doesn't live on paper first. It lives in the mouth. Paper is where it goes to sleep." She hesitated, then added, because honesty had become easier between them lately, "And because everything written here belongs to them. I don't want our words sleeping in their beds."

Something moved across Casper's face — not quite fear, not quite agreement. "All right. Mouth only."

"Mouth only." Ember straightened. "Lesson one. In your language, a place is a thing. You say *the river*, *the city*, *the room*. The place sits there and you move around it. In my language, a place is a verb pretending to be still. Every place is doing something — holding, spilling, remembering. So when you name a place truly, you have to say what it's doing."

"What's this room doing?"

Ember listened. Not with her ears — with the older sense, the one that had gone hungry for two centuries and was now, slowly, greedily waking. The attic creaked and settled around them, full of forgotten ledgers and dead moths and the ghost-smell of sealing wax.

"Holding its breath," she said. "It's been holding its breath since they sealed the doors downstairs. It's waiting for someone to tell it it can let go."

Casper went very still. Then, quietly, in the old tongue, in an accent like a cart wheel on cobblestones, she said: *"Hesseth vael orren."* The threshold holds its breath.

The attic sighed.

It was a small thing — a shift in the air, a loosening, the way a room changes when a window opens somewhere below. Dust lifted and resettled. One of the dead moths tumbled from a beam. But Ember felt it go through her like warm water, and when she looked up, Casper was staring at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger.

"It answered," Casper whispered. "I said a sentence in a dead language and the *room* —"

"The room isn't dead. Only the names were." Ember reached out and took Casper's ink-stained fingers, unfolded them, pressed her own palm flat against the scribe's palm. "Again. This time, don't translate first. Don't think it in guild-tongue and then push it into mine. Think it *in* mine. The words have to be born there or they come out hollow."

"How do I think in a language I've known for two days?"

"You already knew it longer than that." Ember held her gaze. "Your hand translated me the night I knocked. Before your mind caught up. Remember? Your ink knew my tongue, Casper. Ink remembers what it's made to forget. Somebody in your blood wrote in this language, and the writing never fully washed out."

Casper's jaw tightened. She didn't answer. She had not spoken much about her grandmother since the archive — since the contract, since the sold door, since the discovery that her whole life of debt was interest paid on an act of love. But Ember watched the knowledge move behind her eyes like weather.

"Again," Ember said gently. "This room. What is it doing?"

Casper closed her eyes. Her lips moved silently, rehearsing. When she spoke, the words came slower but truer, the vowels rounder, the third sound of *hesseth* asking its proper permission:

*"Hesseth vael orren. Hesseth suven kai."*

The threshold holds its breath. The threshold remembers who passes.

The candle between them flared — not dangerously, just a lean toward Casper, the way a plant leans toward light. And then something else happened, something Ember had not intended, had not warned her about, because she had not known it would happen, because no one had ever practiced the old tongue inside the belly of the guild before.

Casper flickered.

There was no better word for it. For half a heartbeat she was simply *less* — her outline uncertain, her face like a reflection disturbed by a dropped stone. Ember saw the candlelight pass through the edge of her shoulder. Saw, where Casper's left hand gripped her right, her own fingers curling through empty air.

Then Casper was back, solid and gasping, snatching her hand away and pressing it flat against the floorboards as though checking that she herself existed.

"What was that?" Her voice cracked on the last word. "What did you — what did I —"

Ember's stomach had turned to ice. Because she did know. She knew exactly what it was, and she hated herself for not seeing it sooner.

"The guild noticed you," she said quietly.

"Noticed me *how*?"

"You spoke a true name in their house. Their bindings are made of false ones — every ledger, every contract, every entry is a false name nailed over a true one. When you said the true name out loud, it..." Ember struggled for the guild's vocabulary and failed, and so said it the old way: "The book disagreed with you. And the book is winning, for now. It started un-writing the part of you it owns."

"I'm not owned by any book."

"Everyone in this building is owned by a book." Ember kept her voice steady, though her heart was hammering. "You told me yourself. Debt is binding. Your indenture is an entry, Casper. An entry can be amended. An entry can be..." She couldn't finish it. *Struck.* The word wouldn't come. In Ossuary they had a gentler phrase: *given back to the ash.*

Casper stared at her hand, turning it over, counting her fingers as though confirming inventory. "My mother's face," she said suddenly. "Just now, for a second, I couldn't — I know I have a mother, I know her name, but for a second her face was..." She looked up, and there was real terror in her eyes now, the terror of a woman whose ledgers had always balanced discovering an entry she could not reconcile. "Is that what forgetting feels like? Is that what happened to your country?"

"Yes." Ember said it plainly, because Casper had earned plainness. "That's exactly what it feels like. A face with the features smoothed off. A name you can spell but not hear. It starts at the edges. It works inward. And it does not stop on its own."

* * *

They did not stop practicing. That was the mad part, Ember thought later — the part she would remember, if memory survived, with something like awe. Casper flexed her newly-verified fingers, looked at the candle, looked at Ember, and said: "Again."

"You'll lose more than faces."

"I'll lose them anyway, if we do nothing. At least this way I lose them *toward* something." She pulled a scrap of blank paper toward her, then — remembering — pushed it away again, and instead smoothed a patch of attic dust with her sleeve. "Mouth and dust. Show me how doors were anchored."

So Ember taught her, and Casper flickered, and they went on.

She taught her the grammar of approach: how a traveler in Ossuary began speaking the name of their destination a mile out, quiet as prayer, each repetition wearing the distance thinner, until the last syllable landed on the doorstep itself. She taught her the rule of twos — that no door answered a single voice; a threshold needed to be named from both sides, which was why Ossuary's partings were so elaborate, why even a trip to market ended with two people calling the house's name back and forth until the door closed satisfied between them.

"That's why I knocked," Ember said softly. "Two hundred years, and the first thing I did was knock. I didn't know where I was. I didn't know what was left. But I knew doors need two voices, and I was so tired of being only one."

Casper's eyes were wet. She blinked it away and repeated the rule of twos in the old tongue, and flickered again — longer this time, a full breath's worth of absence, during which Ember sat alone in the lamplight with a girl-shaped gap beside her and understood, with terrible clarity, what the guild would take from her if it won.

When Casper returned she was shaking. "I lost something that time. I don't know what. I just know there's a hole where it was, and the hole is shaped like —" she pressed her fist to her sternum — "like something important. Something warm."

"Tell me your name," Ember said quickly. "Right now. Out loud. Names anchor people the way they anchor doors."

"Casper Hartwell. Indentured scribe, Ledger Guild of Vharen, debt number—" She stopped. Frowned. "No. Not that part. Just — Casper Hartwell. My grandmother was Maren Sandoval. She grew roses that bloomed out of season. She hummed when she cooked. She—" Casper's breath caught. "There. Whatever I lost, I found some of it. She hummed. Off-key. I'd forgotten the off-key part until just now, and it came back when I said her name."

"Names call things home," Ember said. "Even across two hundred years. Even across an erasure. Say yours again whenever you feel yourself thinning. And say mine."

"Ember."

"Not my Ossuary name. My Ossuary name is—" and here Ember faltered, because it lived behind the same wall as everything else, a syllable-shaped shadow on the far side of the forgetting. "It's gone. You gave me this one. So it's yours to keep saying. Promise me you'll say it even if I can't hear it. A name spoken is never fully lost. That's lesson eleven, and it's the only one that matters."

"I promise." Casper said it the old way, formally, the vow-shape Ember taught her: *"Suven kai, Ember. Suven kai."* I remember who passes. I remember who passes.

Outside, the guild bell rang the ninth hour, and beneath their feet the dragon-fire inhaled, and did not exhale for a very long time.

* * *

They worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. By the end Casper could speak a dozen true phrases — small ones, household ones, the grammar of thresholds and hearths and rain. Each cost her something. Ember kept count the way Casper kept accounts: a childhood pet's name, gone. The color of a dress she'd loved, gone gray in her telling. The taste of a specific meal, dissolved. Casper catalogued the losses aloud at first, grimly precise, and then stopped cataloguing, and Ember understood that the silence was worse than the list.

Near midnight, Casper paused mid-phrase and frowned at the window.

"What is it?"

"The tower lamp." Casper nodded toward the glass, where the guildhall's central beacon should have thrown its slow revolving glow across the rooftops. "It sweeps past this window every forty seconds. It hasn't swept in —" she counted — "it's been a while. It's stuck facing east."

"So?"

"So the tower lamp never sticks. It's wound like the clock, checked like the ledgers. Unless someone ordered it stopped." Casper rose, moving to the window with a scribe's careful economy, and peered down into the dark street. "And unless someone wanted this side of the building unobserved tonight."

Ember joined her. Far below, at the edge of the lamplight's reach, two figures stood in the mouth of the alley across the street. They weren't talking. They weren't leaving. They were simply positioned, the way entries are positioned in a ledger — one at the debit, one at the credit, waiting for the sum to be called.

"They've been there since the eighth bell," Casper murmured. "Maybe earlier. Watching the window." She turned to Ember, and in the drowned candlelight her face was pale but level, and there was something new in it — something that hadn't been there two days ago, before the knocking and the naming and the flickering. Resolve, maybe. Or its twin, recklessness. "Every hour I practice, I get thinner and they get closer. And every hour I practice, we get one phrase closer to a door that actually opens." She exhaled slowly. "Ember. Lesson twelve. What's the grammar for *we don't have much time*?"

Ember took her hand — carefully, the way you hold something that has begun to flicker — and gave her the phrase.

Casper repeated it perfectly, without a single flicker, as if the words themselves refused to be unwritten. And down in the alley, one of the watching figures lifted a lantern, deliberately, letting its light spill across the window glass — a summons, or a warning, or the guild's cold arithmetic announcing that the sum had finally been called.

Casper's hand tightened around Ember's. "Tomorrow," she said, "we find the cartographer."
