## 11. Quillon's Confession

The summons came at the hour when the guildhall forgot to be a building and remembered only that it was a ledger.

Casper felt it in her teeth first — a low vibration through the floorboards, like a clerk clearing his throat before delivering bad news. Then the corridor outside her cell of a room rearranged itself. She had learned in the past three days of hiding, of forging entries by candlelight with Ember asleep against her shoulder, that the guildhall did not have hallways so much as it had *entries*, and when it wished to summon you, it simply wrote you where it wanted you. The door she had locked was now a door that opened onto a stairwell she had never climbed, descending into a part of the guildhall that smelled of cold stone and older ink.

She went down because there was nowhere else to go, and because the alternative — being written somewhere against her will — was worse.

The stairwell let out into a chamber beneath the archive levels, a room she had not known existed in eleven years of service. It was small and round, windowless, lit by a single lamp whose flame burned a color she had no name for — not gold, not orange, but something between amber and grief. Shelves lined the curved walls, but they held no ledgers. They held objects. A child's shoe, cracked with age. A brass door-knocker shaped like a sleeping bird. A jar of rainwater, labeled in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood.

And Master Quillon, standing among them with his back to her, looking smaller than she had ever seen him.

"You've been busy," he said, without turning.

Casper said nothing. Her hand drifted toward her coat pocket, where the forged page lay folded — the entry she had falsified to buy Ember time, moving the liquidation hearing from Thursday to a date that did not exist. *The ninth of Frostmonth.* A month the calendar had never contained. She had thought herself clever. She had thought the old bindings wouldn't parse it.

"The ninth of Frostmonth," Quillon said, turning now. His face in the strange lamplight looked carved from tallow, soft in the wrong places. "You wrote a month out of the air and slotted it into a binding contract, and do you know what happened, Hartwell? For one hour, the guildhall believed it. For one hour, there *was* a ninth of Frostmonth. Somewhere in this building, a drawer opened that hasn't opened in two hundred years." He lifted his hand, and she saw it trembled — not with rage. With something worse. "Do you understand what you did? You didn't forge an entry. You proved the books can be made to remember things that were taken from them."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Casper said, which was a lie so flat it embarrassed them both.

Quillon exhaled through his nose. He crossed to a shelf, took down the jar of rainwater, and set it on the table between them with a care that bordered on reverence.

"Sit down, Casper."

Not *Hartwell*. Not *Scribe*. Her given name, spoken by a man who had never once used it in eleven years of service. She sat, because her legs had decided without consulting her.

* * *

The lamp hissed. Outside — far above, or perhaps very near; distance meant nothing down here — the guildhall groaned through its nightly reconciliation, columns settling, corridors re-shelving themselves.

"Do you know what I was doing," Quillon said, "the night they erased my country?"

Casper's breath stopped in her throat.

"I was six years old." He spoke in the tone of a man reading from a ledger he had memorized against his will, each figure exact, each figure hated. "We lived on Lanternmaker's Row, in the city of Vael Ossuary, third district, where the doors opened onto yesterday if you knocked politely. My mother made lamps that burned dragon-fire — real fire, the deep kind, the kind that breathes. On the last night, the sky over the city went the color of wet paper. And the words started going."

"Going?"

"Off the tongue." He touched his own mouth, briefly, as though checking that everything was still attached. "My father was saying grace at the table and halfway through, the word for *home* came out of him as nothing. Just a shape his mouth made around an absence. He tried again. Nothing. By morning half the street names were gone, and the people who'd carried them were gone with them — not dead. *Unwritten.* You can't miss someone you cannot hold in your mind, girl. That's the cruelty of it. Grief needs a handle. They took all the handles."

Casper thought of Ember in the attic, of the fragments the girl had coaxed out of her own dissolving memory — *a fire that never went out, adults who forgot her name one by one* — and her hands closed into fists beneath the table.

"You were born there," she said. "In Ossuary."

"I was born there," Quillon agreed. "And I am, by any honest count, a traitor to it. Because I survived. Do you know how a six-year-old survives the unwriting of a nation?" He smiled, and it was the worst thing Casper had ever seen on a human face — not cruel, but *tired*, worn smooth as a stone in a river of guilt. "He makes himself useful to the ones holding the pen."

* * *

He told it plainly, which somehow made it unbearable.

The Ledger Guild had not always been what it was. Two centuries ago it had been a counting-house like any other — until it was approached, Quillon said, by men who called themselves Reconcilers, who had discovered something terrible about the nature of debt: that a debt could be levied against anything, if the contract was written well enough. Against gold. Against land. Against *memory*. Against existence itself.

Ossuary owed them. No one living knew what for — that was rather the point — but the original contract existed, signed in ash, sealed in the founding vault, and its terms were absolute. The country could not pay. So the country would be collected instead: every memory of it drawn off like coin from a dead man's purse, every citizen unmade or scattered, the whole nation filed away in stasis behind binding ledgers, accruing interest, until such time as the debt was discharged.

"They needed hands," Quillon said. "People who could still speak the old tongue, to help translate the country into entries. Children mostly. Children learn languages fast, and children can be bought cheap." His eyes went to the lamp, to its grieving amber light. "I sat at a desk for nine years and translated my mother's lullaby into an asset code. I translated the name of my street into a column of figures. I was very good at it. They gave me a pension and a title and told me I'd saved lives — that the ones we filed in stasis weren't destroyed, only *held*, and that someday, if the accounts balanced—"

He stopped. His jaw worked.

"Someday never comes, Hartwell. That's the architecture of it. Interest accrues faster than any payment. The debt is designed to be unpayable. I understood that when I was thirty and I kept signing anyway, because the alternative was joining the inventory." He gestured at the shelves. At the shoe. At the bird-shaped knocker. "These are the ones who refused. Or the ones who were too small to be worth filing whole. The guild keeps them here because even a fragment of Ossuary is valuable, and even a traitor gets sentimental."

"Why are you telling me this?" Casper asked. Her voice came out rough. "Why now? You've spent eleven years making my life arithmetic. You fined me a day's wage for a smudged folio. You threatened to have me *audited*—" The word still raised the hair on her arms; everyone in the guild knew what audit meant, even if no one said it. "If this is mercy, it's late."

"It's not mercy." Quillon leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the sweat on his brow, and Casper saw with a lurch that the man was afraid — genuinely, bone-deep afraid, in a way that had nothing to do with her. "It's arithmetic. You've proven the books can be rewritten. The girl is the last citizen, which means she's the last collateral, which means when the guild liquidates her assets, the final tranche of the debt gets paid — and the account closes. Do you know what happens to a stasis-bound country when its account closes?"

"No."

"Nothing happens. That's the horror of it. It doesn't die — dying is an event, and events require witnesses. It simply *stops being pending*. The fire goes out. The doors stay shut forever. Everything held in stasis becomes, retroactively, nothing at all." He pressed both palms flat on the table. "I helped build that machine. I will not watch it finish. Not while I still remember the word for home, even if I can't say it."

Casper stared at him. "Then help us. You know the bindings. You know the vaults. You could—"

"I could do nothing," he snapped, and for a moment he was the old Quillon again, the master with the ledger-stare, the man whose disapproval had weight enough to bend spines. "Don't mistake confession for alliance. If I'm seen helping you, I lose whatever small say I have left, and you lose the only person inside the machine who knows where the gears are. Which brings me to why you're actually here."

He drew a folded paper from his coat and laid it on the table. Casper recognized the seal — the guild's audit mark, the little iron stamp that preceded disappearances.

"This arrived an hour ago. Your forgery bought you time from the liquidation committee. It did not buy you time from the *fire*." He tapped the paper once. "The readings from the sub-levels. The furnace under the east range. Whatever's down there — and yes, I know you've been down there, the floors report footfalls, this entire building is a book that reads itself — it's failing. Faster than the models predicted. Dragon-fire isn't fuel, Hartwell, it's *patience*, and two hundred years of being asked to wait has nearly spent it."

"How long?"

Quillon looked at her, and in the amber light his face seemed suddenly, terribly old — older than his years, older than the guild, old as a country that no longer existed.

"Days," he said. "Three. Four if the wind stays calm. When it gutters, the stasis fails everywhere at once, and everything Ossuary still holds — including the girl, including whatever's left of the door — goes out with it." He stood, straightening his coat with the mechanical precision of a man who had nothing left to straighten. "So run your clever forgeries, Scribe. Write your impossible months. But understand what you're racing. It isn't the guild."

He paused at the stairwell door, his back to her.

"And Hartwell. Before you decide what I am — check the deep archive. Volume one of the founding contracts. Look at who countersigned the original debt." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "You may find the traitors keep better company than you think."

Then the corridor took him, and the stairs wrote themselves shut behind him, and Casper sat alone in the amber dark with a jar of two-hundred-year-old rain and the sudden, pounding certainty that her grandmother's name was waiting for her in the oldest book in Vharen.
