## 13. The Auction

They dressed the guildhall in mourning colors to sell my country.

I did not know that was what they were doing, not at first. I stood at Casper's side in the gallery above the trading floor, wearing a borrowed coat too big for me, my hair braided tight so no one would look twice, and I watched men in gray hang black banners from the balconies and thought: *they are grieving something.* That was before I understood that grief, in Vharen, was just another color of money. The banners were not mourning. They were packaging. Black made the gold of the price-plates shine brighter. Black said *this is rare, this is solemn, this is worth more because it is the last.*

The last. Yes. Everything on the block today was the last of something, and every last thing had once been mine.

* * *

I had never seen so many people in one room who were not Ossuvaran. Two hundred years I had slept in the ledgers, folded small between entries like a flower pressed in a book, and when I woke the world was full of strangers — but I had imagined strangers as a kind of weather, distant, passing. This was different. Below me, filling the trading floor in rows of velvet chairs, sat three hundred people who had come to buy pieces of the place where I was born.

A woman in oyster-gray silk fanned herself with a catalog. A fat man with rings on every finger ate candied ginger from a paper cone and let the sugar fall on the head of the person in front of him. Clerks moved along the aisles with trays of little glasses of wine, because grief and greed both go down easier sweetened. At the front of the room stood an auctioneer's podium draped in the same black cloth as the banners, and beside it, on a table of green velvet, the lots.

I had seen them being carried in that morning, under guard, each in its own case. Bottles mostly. The Ledger Guild kept Ossuary's residue in glass — vials of rain, jars of lamplight, stoppered flasks of sound. I knew what they were the way you know your own teeth. When the first case was opened and the auctioneer lifted a vial to the light, the whole hall leaned forward like wheat in wind, and something in my chest turned over and lay still.

"That's the rain," I whispered.

Casper's hand found mine on the armrest. Her fingers were cold. "Which rain?"

"Ours." My voice came out strange, flattened, the way it did when I was about to cry and refused to permit it. "It fell slower there. It had further to fall. The clouds over Ossuary were old clouds; they'd been raining since before the city was built, and they'd learned patience." I could hear it even now, through the glass, through two hundred years and a sealed stopper — that slow silver drumming, patient as a grandmother. "Lot forty-one," I read off the plate beneath it. "Residual atmospheric phenomenon, provenance unverified."

"Unverified," Casper repeated softly. Her jaw did the thing it did when she was angry and keeping it in a ledger somewhere behind her eyes. "Two hundred years of careful bookkeeping, and our rain is *unverified.*"

"Provenance unverified" meant no one living could say what it was. That was the whole trick of the erasure, I understood now. They had not destroyed my country. You cannot destroy a country outright; it fights you, it leaves splinters. Instead they had unwritten it — pulled the name out of every mouth and page until nothing pointed at anything anymore — and what remained, orphaned, nameless, became *inventory.* A vial of rain with no country attached was just weather in a bottle. Weather in a bottle was valuable. Weather with a name attached was contraband.

So they sold my home in pieces, and called it salvage.

* * *

The auctioneer was a thin man with a voice like a knife drawn slowly across silk. He began with the small things, warming the room the way you warm a pump before drawing water. A jar of light from lamps that burned without oil — the dragon-fire's children, I thought, my throat tightening, *those are my city's lamps, those are the lights Mama walked home beneath* — went for eleven crowns to the ringed man with the ginger. A stoppered flask of birdsong, provenance unverified, lot twelve, sold before the auctioneer finished describing it. A bolt of cloth woven from wool that remembered being warm — the weavers of the Sallow Quarter used to sing into the fleece so the winter coats would hum lullabies in the dark — sold to the oyster-gray woman, who laughed with her friends and said she would make cushions of it.

Cushions. She would make *cushions.*

"Do you want to leave?" Casper murmured. "I can get us out. Quillon gave me passes for the east door—"

"No." I said it harder than I meant to. A man two rows down glanced up. I lowered my voice. "No. If I leave, then nobody watches. Nobody remembers what they paid for her song. Somebody has to be the witness. That's—" I swallowed. "That's a thing we believed. In Ossuary. That everything witnessed stays real a little longer. Someone has to watch them buy my mother's lullaby, or else it dies twice."

Casper looked at me for a long moment. In the gallery's dimness her eyes were the color of wet slate, and I saw her decide something, though I didn't yet know what. "Then we watch together," she said. "And we write it down after. Every lot. Every price. Every face."

"You're going to keep a ledger of the auction?"

"I'm going to keep a ledger of the *theft,*" she said, and took out her notebook, and that small stubborn motion — her pen uncapped, her hand ready — steadied me more than any vow could have.

* * *

Lot nineteen was my mother's lullaby.

I knew it before the clerk even set the case on the velvet table. It was a little brass cylinder, no longer than my thumb, wound like a music box's heart, and around its middle someone — my mother, her own hands, her ink-stained fingers — had tied a thread of red wool. For luck. For love. So the song would know its way home.

The auctioneer held it up and the room hushed, because even unwritten, even unnamed, some things carry their weight in the glass. "Lot nineteen," he intoned. "Residual auditory memory, contained within a mechanical song-cylinder. Condition: exceptional. Effect upon handling: profound." He smiled his knife-silk smile. "Gentlemen, ladies — I am advised by the appraisers that this piece produces in the listener an acute sensation of *homesickness.* Bidding will open at twenty crowns."

Homesickness. That was what they were selling. Not the song — the ache of it. My mother had sung that lullaby into the cylinder the winter I was six, when the first forgetting had begun to gnaw at the edges of Ossuary and she wanted to make sure that even if she lost me — even if the words went out of her mouth mid-verse, even if she looked at me one morning with a stranger's polite blankness — some part of her would still know me. *Sleep, my ember, the fire keeps watch, the doors are all dreaming, the rain knows your name—*

Twenty-five. Thirty. The ringed man again. Forty, from the oyster-gray woman, who wanted homesickness for her salon. Fifty. Sixty. They bid on my mother's voice the way they'd bid on a horse, and the numbers climbed, and I sat very still with Casper's cold hand in mine and performed the oldest discipline of my people: I witnessed. *This is the lullaby of Maren of the Ember Row, daughter of the lampwrights, who tied red wool so the song could find its way home. It is being sold for sixty crowns. I am watching. It happened.*

Seventy-five. Eighty. Ninety.

It went for ninety-four crowns to a collector whose face I memorized deliberately, feature by feature, so that someday — someday — I could stand outside whatever house hung that cylinder on its wall and listen to my mother sing through a stranger's window.

I did not cry. I want that recorded. I sat in the gallery of the men who erased my country and I did not give them my tears, because tears, in that room, would only have been another asset.

* * *

The lots blurred after that. A key that opened a door in a house that no longer existed — the auctioneer joked about that, and the room chuckled, not knowing they were laughing at the shape of the whole world's wound. A pressed flower from a garden watered with memory. A child's drawing that changed slightly whenever no one looked at it. Each one passed across the podium, each one sold, and I wrote none of it down because Casper was writing all of it, her pen moving in fast sure strokes, her face like carved wood.

Then the clerks brought out the long tray, and the auctioneer's voice changed — dropped lower, went reverent, went *hungry* — and I felt Casper go rigid beside me.

"Lots fifty-two through sixty," he announced. "Concentrated mnemonic essence. The appraisers have classified these as the finest pieces of the day. Each vial contains a complete, intact recollection — a lived moment, preserved whole. Ladies and gentlemen, these are not fragments. These are *entire yesterdays.*"

Six vials on green velvet, glowing faintly, the way embers glow under ash. And I knew them. Oh, I knew them. I had been carried past the deep vaults often enough in my long sleep, logged and relogged, and I had learned to read the guild's shelf-marks the way other children learn street signs. Those six vials were days of Ossuary. Whole days. A market morning. A festival of doors. A wedding. A funeral. A night of the great rain. A day, ordinary and golden, when nothing happened at all except that everyone was alive and knew each other's names.

"Fifty-two," the auctioneer began. "Market morning, unknown city, high summer—"

*Unknown city.* My city. The fish stalls and the bell-cart and old Yeva arguing about the price of eels, all of it, sold as *unknown city, high summer.*

The bidding was faster here, fiercer. These were the connoisseurs' lots. Eighty. One hundred. One hundred thirty. The ringed man bought the wedding for one hundred sixty and toasted himself with wine. The oyster-gray woman took the festival. A pale scholar with hungry eyes bought the funeral without blinking, and I hated him with a purity I had not known I possessed.

And then there was one left, and it was small.

"Lot fifty-eight," said the auctioneer. "Mnemonic essence, minor classification. Contents: brief domestic scene, low informational value. A kitchen. A woman singing while bread proves by the stove. A child—" he consulted his card, and something flickered across his professional composure, some instinct of unease he smoothed away in half a heartbeat, "—a child asleep in a chair. Provenance unverified. Bidding opens at ten crowns."

My mother's kitchen. The yellow afternoon. The smell of bread and lamp-oil. Me, asleep in the chair with my cheek on the table, listening to her sing without knowing I listened. Low informational value. Ten crowns.

Nobody bid.

Ten crowns, the auctioneer tried again, and the room rustled and murmured and looked elsewhere, because the big lots had spent their appetites and a small grey vial of somebody's unverified kitchen was not worth the effort of lifting a paddle. Ten crowns. Any advance on ten? The silence stretched. Somewhere a cork popped at the back of the hall. The auctioneer's smile thinned.

And Casper Hartwell stood up.

* * *

I knew what she was going to do maybe one breath before she did it, because I had watched her for weeks, and I knew the particular stillness she got when the ledger in her head refused to balance and she decided to balance it anyway, cost be damned.

"Ten crowns," she said clearly, from the gallery, and three hundred heads turned.

"Casper," I breathed. "You don't have ten crowns. You told me you don't have *one* crown."

"I know what I told you," she said through her teeth, smiling at the room like this was all perfectly normal, a scribe making a modest purchase, nothing to see. "Bid with me. Say a number. Loudly."

"What?"

"*Trust me.*" And then, louder, to the floor: "Fifteen! Fifteen crowns!"

The ringed man, bored, feeling generous, or perhaps just enjoying the spectacle of a guild scribe spending money she visibly did not have, raised one finger. "Eighteen."

"Twenty-five," said Casper instantly.

He laughed. "Thirty. Come along, girl, don't bankrupt yourself for a trinket."

"Forty," said Casper, and her voice cracked on it, and I understood then what she was doing, what she had whispered to Quillon in the corridor that I hadn't been allowed to hear, why she had insisted we come at all, why she had counted the lots all morning with her pen moving. She wasn't buying a vial. She was buying *standing.* She was putting herself on the record, in front of three hundred witnesses, as the person who wanted this worthless thing — and the guild's own rules, the rules she had served since she was nine years old, the rules that said every bid is a binding contract — would have to honor it.

"Fifty," said the ringed man lazily, because it amused him.

"One hundred," said Casper Hartwell, who had four coppers and a debt she would die owing.

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone has either gone mad or gone magnificent, and no one can tell which. The auctioneer peered up at the gallery. "Madam — sir — the Guild requires assurance of funds for bids above fifty crowns. Do you wish to declare your account?"

And Casper, my Casper, the scribe with the ink-stained fingers and the inherited chains, drew herself up and said, in a voice that carried to the black banners and back:

"I declare my account. I am Casper Hartwell, indent scribe of the Ledger Guild of Vharen, third generation, and I pledge against this purchase the whole of my remaining term of service — fourteen years, two months, and eleven days — to be extended at the Guild's discretion, interest accruing, per statute nine of the founding contracts."

She had calculated it. Of course she had. She had sat up nights calculating it, the way she calculated everything, and she had found the one currency she owned enough of: herself. Fourteen years. She had just bid fourteen years of her life for a vial of my mother's kitchen, and she had done it in perfect statutory language, in front of the entire trading floor, so that no clerk could quietly lose the entry, no master could void it without three witnesses signing.

The silence lasted five full seconds. Then the auctioneer, who was after all only a servant of the books, bowed his knife-silk bow. "Declared and entered. Lot fifty-eight — do I hear any advance on one hundred crowns?" He waited. The ringed man examined his ginger and declined. "Sold. To the scribe Hartwell, at one hundred crowns, against term of service."

The gavel fell like a small, clean execution.

* * *

They let her take possession at the side counter, because the law was the law even when it was humiliating, and it was humiliating — I watched the clerk's smirk, heard the whisper running the rows: *the Hartwell girl, the debtor, spent her life on a bottle of nothing.* Casper signed the transfer with a steady hand and only I, standing pressed against her side, could feel how hard she was shaking.

We walked out through the east door with the passes Quillon had given us, past the black banners, past the trays and the wine and the buyers already haggling over delivery of my city's rain. Casper carried the vial cupped in both hands like a bird with a broken wing. We did not speak until we were three streets away, in the shadow of the fishmonger's stair, and then she stopped and held it out to me.

"It's yours," she said. "It was always yours. I just — held the paddle."

I took it. The glass was warm. Through the grey-gold swirl inside, faint as breath on a window, I could see it: the kitchen, the proving bread, the chair, the sleeping child. My mother's mouth moving in a song the vial remembered even if the world did not.

"Casper," I said. "Fourteen years. You gave them fourteen years."

"I gave them what they think I'm worth," she said. "There's a difference. There's always been a difference — I just finally know which side of it I'm standing on." She looked back toward the guildhall, its black banners snapping in the wind off the harbor, and her jaw set in that way I had learned to read like weather. "Ember. When they carried the deep-vault lots out this morning, I checked the manifest against the registry. There were seven vials of concentrated essence in the vault inventory."

"There were six on the tray," I said slowly.

"There were six on the tray." Her voice dropped. "One vial is missing from the auction. And according to the registry, it was withdrawn yesterday — by Master Quillon's personal seal."

Above the fishmonger's, a window banged open, and a hoarse voice we both knew growled down at us: "Well? Are you two going to stand in the street all day, or are you coming up? Because I've had a visitor, and you're not going to like what he left behind."
