## 14. Run

The bell began to toll before Casper finished the falsified entry.

Not the closing bell. Not the market bell, nor the bell that summoned scribes to audit, nor any of the dozen bells whose voices she knew the way a shepherd knows his flock. This was a sound she had never heard and yet recognized instantly, the way one recognizes one's own name shouted in anger — a deep, grinding peal from somewhere beneath the floor, as if the guildhall itself had cleared its throat to say *her*.

She had been a forger for less than an hour.

In that hour she had done three things. She had entered into the ledger of liquidation a false valuation of Asset 7741 — Ember — reducing her assessed memory-content from *substantial* to *negligible*. She had backdated the entry with ink mixed from ash and tallow so it would smell old to the auditors' noses. And she had signed it with Master Quillon's hand, which she had studied for six years across a hundred thousand entries until she could reproduce its small, weary hook on the letter Q better than Quillon himself.

It had felt, while she did it, like nothing at all. Her hand had moved with the calm of long practice, because tallying was what she did, was all she was, and forgery was only tallying pointed in the wrong direction. It had felt like relief.

Now the bell tolled again, and every candle in the Scriptorium leaned eastward at once, though there was no wind.

"Casper." Ember stood in the doorway of the copying alcove, and the girl's face had gone the color of the paper on the desks. "The building knows."

"Buildings don't know things."

"This one does." Ember's hands were flat against the doorframe, fingers spread, reading it the way Casper read a ledger. "It's stone, but it's been written on. Every wall. Every stair. It's a book, Casper. It's a great fat book pretending to be a building, and it has just found a line in itself that doesn't add up."

The candles leaned further. Somewhere above them, a shelf of ledgers groaned under no weight at all.

Casper closed the liquidation ledger with a softness that felt obscene, as if loudness might provoke it. The columns inside were still wrong. She had made them wrong on purpose, and now the purpose sat in her chest like swallowed coal. Six years of indenture. Six years of never once being late, never once blotting a page, never once costing the guild a single copper of error — all of it spent in one hour of beautiful, deliberate ruin.

"They'll check the valuations at dawn," she said. "The auction is tomorrow. If they find my entry—"

"They've found it." Ember turned her head, listening to something in the walls. "It's arguing with the other numbers. I can hear it. The building doesn't like sums that don't come out even."

*Run*, said some cold clear part of Casper's mind, the part that had survived her childhood by calculating exits the way other children counted stars. *You have perhaps four minutes. The stairs to the ground floor are watched after dark. The clerks' corridor connects to the counting room, the counting room to the west archive, the west archive to—*

To nowhere. She had mapped the guildhall in her head for six years the way prisoners map walls, and every route ended in a locked door or a guarded one, because the Ledger Guild had not survived two centuries of holding impossible inventory by leaving ways out.

But Ember was not looking at the doors. Ember was looking at the wall.

"It moves," the girl whispered. "When nobody's watching, it moves. Grandmother told me. Doors in Ossuary walked at night, and the guild built this place out of our walking doors and nailed them still with contracts." Her eyes came up, wide and burning. "Casper. The corridors here aren't where they are. They're where the *ledger says they are*. If we change what the ledger says—"

"There's no time to write anything."

"We don't write." Ember grabbed her wrist, and her grip was astonishingly strong for so small a girl. "We *read*. Come on. Read fast and walk faster."

They went out through the alcove's far arch, and the guildhall received them the way a mouth receives a word.

* * *

The Scriptorium's main hall was empty at this hour, rows of desks pale as teeth in the candlelight, but the hall was wrong. Casper knew it was wrong the way you know a face in a dream: the proportions had shifted. The far wall, which should have been forty paces distant, was sixty. Or twenty. The rows of desks ran north-south when she knew — she *knew*, she had aligned them herself a hundred times — that they ran east-west.

"The building is re-indexing," Ember breathed. "Looking for us. It files everything, Casper. It's trying to file us under *found*."

A desk slid. There was no other word for it. One moment it stood in its row; the next it had stepped sideways with a sound like a page turning, inserting itself into their path with the smooth inevitability of an entry posted to an account. Then another. Then a third, the whole row shuffling itself across the floor in a slow, silent, terrible migration, corralling them toward the masters' stair, where footsteps were already descending.

"Left," Casper said. "The marginalia corridor."

There was such a corridor. Every proper book had one, and the guildhall, being a book, kept its notes in a narrow passage behind the main shelving where apprentice errors went to be corrected — a corridor so little used that dust lay on its floor in unbroken grey velvet. It appeared between two shelves only when Casper looked for it, and she looked for it now with the ferocity of prayer, hauling Ember by the hand, and the gap opened for them just as the first clerk rounded the masters' stair with a lantern and a shout.

"*Hartwell!*"

She did not answer. Answering was posting to the account. She pulled Ember into the marginalia corridor and ran.

The corridor was barely shoulder-width, lit by nothing, and it behaved like the margin of a page: cramped, irregular, full of things crossed out. Doors along its length stood half-dissolved, their wood gone grainy and half-legible, and through one Casper glimpsed a room that was mostly erasure — furniture like smudged charcoal, a window showing white. A correction, waiting to happen. She did not look twice. Looking twice was how the building learned your face.

Behind them the shout multiplied. Lantern-light bloomed at the corridor's mouth, and with it came a sound Casper felt in her teeth: the scratch of many pens. The clerks were writing as they ran, calling out descriptions of her — *female, dark hair, green coat, asset in custody* — and each spoken line became real somewhere behind them, becoming barriers, becoming doors that hadn't existed a moment ago swinging shut.

"They're describing us into corners," Ember gasped. "We have to stop being describable."

"How does one stop being describable?"

"You get *smaller* in the sentence." Ember hauled her sideways through a gap Casper would have sworn was solid wall, into a stairwell that smelled of cold iron and old arithmetic. "Don't run in straight lines. Straight lines are easy to enter in a column. Zigzag. Be a rounding error."

So Casper Hartwell, who had spent six years being the most legible creature in Vharen — punctual, precise, correct to the last copper — became an error.

She took stairs two at a time going down when down was expected, then doubled back up through a hatch she'd only ever seen in the fire-warden's maps. She cut across the counting room, where the great brass scales for weighing coin hung motionless as gallows, and as she passed, every scale tipped toward her, all at once, all pointing like compass needles. The building was weighing her. Finding her heavier than she should be. Finding the debt of her treason accruing in real time.

Somewhere below, the grinding bell tolled a third time, and the sound was closer, and it was not a bell. It was the building clearing away whatever stood between itself and balance.

* * *

They came out on the Long Gallery, the vaulted spine of the guildhall where the founding ledgers slept behind glass, and Casper stopped so abruptly Ember struck her hip.

The gallery was gone.

Not empty — *gone*. Where the Long Gallery ran, two hundred feet of marble and glass and the oldest books in Vharen, there was instead a blank expanse of wall, seamless, unbroken, utterly without seam or history, as if the gallery had never been built. As if it had been *redacted*.

"No," Casper whispered. She pressed her palm to the wall. It was warm. Stone should not be warm. "This corridor has stood since the Founding. It can't just—"

"It's not gone." Ember pressed her ear flat against the stone, eyes shut. "It's been *moved*. Filed somewhere else. The building put it away so we can't use it." Her voice wavered, and for one moment she sounded exactly her age. "Casper, it's doing to the hallway what the guild did to Ossuary."

That landed in Casper's chest like a dropped weight. Of course. Of course this was how it was done. Not with armies, not with fire — with filing. You didn't destroy a country; you mislaid it, thoroughly, permanently, with excellent record-keeping. The guildhall was simply doing to two fugitives what the guild had done to a nation, and it did it without malice, without effort, the way a ledger balances: automatically, inevitably, because the numbers demanded it.

And the numbers demanded them caught.

The wall began to move.

It did not lurch. It *revised*. The blank stone flowed with the smooth patience of drying ink, and as it flowed, text surfaced in it — enormous characters rising through the stone like corpses through pond-water, letters tall as doors:

**DISCREPANCY NOTED. ASSET 7741 AND ASSOCIATE HARTWELL, C. OUT OF POSITION. CORRECTING.**

"Associate," Casper said wildly. "Six years and I'm an *associate*."

"Casper."

"Right. Yes. Running."

Ember seized her hand and did something Casper had never seen her do: she spoke to the building. In the old tongue. Three syllables, low and urgent, words that tasted of rain on hot stone and lamps that burned without oil — and the wall *hesitated*. The great letters paused mid-surface, flickering, as though a clerk had looked up from his desk uncertain he had read the figure correctly.

"What did you say?"

"I told it my name." Ember's chin lifted. "My real one. The one they couldn't erase, because I'm the last copy of it. The building is made of Ossuary's bones, Casper. Deep down, under all their contracts, it remembers who built it. It doesn't want to correct me. It wants to *obey* me. But it can't do both, and while it's confused—"

"—we're a rounding error." Casper was already moving. "Where?"

"Down. Always down. The confusion won't hold."

They ran, and the guildhall stopped pretending to be architecture.

Stairs stretched beneath them, each flight longer than the last, the steps multiplying like figures in a compounding column. A landing rotated under their feet with the groan of a turning page. Doorways opened onto rooms that were clearly other rooms' interiors — Casper sprinted through a linen closet into the middle of the Assay Office, past clerks frozen mid-reach, through the far door and out into a corridor that had been, until this moment, on the third floor. Behind them the building sealed its own passages with walls that grew like scar tissue, and ahead of them it opened others — opening them deliberately, sweetly, into dead ends furnished with waiting enforcers, offering shortcuts the way a spider offers silk.

Casper learned to distrust generosity. Twice she nearly took a helpful door; both times Ember dragged her back, and both times the welcoming passage snapped shut a heartbeat later like a trap on a bone.

"It's balancing," Ember panted as they pounded down yet another impossible stair. "Every door it opens for us, it closes somewhere else. Every step we take, it enters somewhere. We're spending the building, Casper. It's going to make us pay for every inch."

"Then let it bill me," Casper snarled, and something in her own voice startled her — a heat she had never once permitted herself in six years of perfect service. "I'm already ruined. I forged a master's hand. I stole guild property." She glanced at Ember, running beside her, mud-streaked and magnificent. "You're not property."

Ember smiled at her, breathless, and for half a stride neither of them was running from anything at all.

Then the floor tilted.

Not much. Two degrees, perhaps three — the gentle slope of a page held slightly wrong. But the guildhall was a ledger, and a ledger abhorred a tilt, and Casper felt the whole vast weight of the building begin, with glacial patience, to *correct*: stones shifting in the walls, staircases leaning themselves plumb, corridors rotating by fractions toward true. And they were standing, she realized with a lurch worse than any fall, on the one surface in the entire structure that could not be corrected — because they were standing on the discrepancy itself. On the lie she had written. The building was tilting the world to slide them into the column where they belonged.

Below them, through a grate in the floor, Casper saw firelight. Real firelight, orange and alive, breathing.

The furnace levels. The fire under the floor — the dying dragon-flame that Ember had led her to once already, the heart that kept two centuries of stasis beating. The building was sliding them down toward it too, because that was where liquidated assets went to be rendered down.

Or — and the thought arrived whole, blazing — because that was where the building's oldest pages were. Its foundations. Its *first draft*.

"Ember. The furnace levels. The old stone down there — is it written on too?"

"Everything here is written on."

"Then it can be *edited*." Casper wrenched open the grate. Heat rolled up, thick with the smell of sulfur and old, old patience. "The building balances because someone wrote the balance. Well. I'm a scribe. I've been balancing other people's lies for six years."

She swung herself down into the dark, Ember dropping beside her, and above them the guildhall screamed — there was no other word for it — a shriek of grinding stone and tearing vellum as the discrepancy it had been chasing slipped off its page entirely.

They landed in the furnace gallery, in the breathing red light of the dying flame, and Casper turned to look back up the shaft they'd descended.

The shaft was sealing itself, stone flowing smooth, and through the closing gap she heard boots — dozens of pairs — and one voice she knew, dry as ash, calling down with terrible calm:

"Hartwell. The building has you filed. There is nowhere in this structure that isn't a page." A pause, and then, almost gently: "*Come up, child. Ink always comes home.*"

The gap closed. Darkness, and firelight, and the sound of the flame breathing like a lung with hours left in it.

Ember's hand found hers in the red dark.

"Casper," she whispered. "Look at the walls down here."

Casper turned. The furnace gallery's ancient stones were covered — *covered* — in writing. Not guild script. The old tongue, thousands of lines of it, glowing faintly ember-orange wherever the firelight touched, and among the words, over and over, in a hand she knew better than her own:

Her grandmother's.

* * *

The oldest pages, Quillon had told her once, drunk on his own guilt, are kept closest to the fire.

Casper stood in the breathing light with a fugitive girl's hand in hers and her grandmother's handwriting climbing the walls around her, and understood at last why the building had driven them down. Not to trap them.

To show them where the first draft was buried.

The flame guttered. Somewhere above, the guildhall rearranged itself around their absence, patient as interest. And Casper Hartwell, forger, debtor, associate, reached out and laid her palm flat against her grandmother's words, and began — very carefully, very fast — to read.
