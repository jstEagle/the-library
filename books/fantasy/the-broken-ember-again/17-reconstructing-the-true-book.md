## 17. Reconstructing the True Book

The drowned cartographer's rooms smelled of lamp oil, brine, and old paper gone soft at the edges, the way paper does when it has wept for years. Vess had spread every map he owned across the floor of his attic like a man laying out a body for burial, and now he knelt among them on his knees, a stub of pencil behind each ear, three more tucked into the sweatband of his hat, muttering coordinates to himself in a voice roughened by forty years of drink and two hundred years of grief.

"Here." He tapped a chart of the northern coast so faded the sea had gone the color of weak tea. "This inlet doesn't exist anymore. The guild unwrote it in my grandfather's time. But look—" his finger traced a curl of coastline that ended in nothing, a peninsula amputated mid-stroke, "—the ink remembers being told to stop. Ink always remembers."

Casper knelt across from him with a fresh ledger open on her knees—the good one, sewn by her own hand from rag paper and linen thread, because no guild-made book could be trusted to hold what they were about to write. The candle between them guttered whenever the building shuddered, which was often now. Somewhere far below their feet, under six sealed floors and a century of masonry, the dragon-fire was dying, and the guildhall felt it the way a body feels its heart faltering: a tremor, a coldness in the extremities, the lamps burning brown instead of gold.

"Again," she said. "From the top of the street."

Vess closed his eyes. He did this before every recitation, she'd noticed—a small ritual, a diver filling his lungs. "Cinderlane," he said. "It ran east from the Bellfounders' Gate. On the left side going down: a chandler's, a house with blue shutters where the Rell family kept bees on the roof, a shrine to the tide-mother with a crack in her left knee that children were forbidden to touch and always touched."

Ember, curled in the window seat with her knees drawn up, translated as he spoke. Not word for word—Ossuary's tongue didn't work that way, Casper was learning. It worked the way water works, finding the shape of what contained it. Each name Vess dredged up, Ember caught and turned over and gave back in her own language, and when she spoke it the words did something to the air. They *anchored*. Casper felt it against her skin like the moment before a storm breaks, a pressure, a rightness.

And then Casper wrote it down.

That was the part that frightened her most, if she was honest, and she was trying very hard these days to be honest about everything. Her hand moved before her mind agreed to it. The old tongue poured through her nib in characters she had never studied—loops and ligatures and strokes that crossed themselves like latches, like doors—and when she looked at what she'd written she understood it the way you understand your own heartbeat, without having decided to.

"The grammar of place," Ember had called it, back in the first hours, when they'd still been speaking in whispers. "A thing isn't real in Ossuary until it has a name spoken in place. That's why the erasure worked. They didn't burn the country. They unspoke it."

"And this book can respeak it."

"This book *is* the country. Or it will be, if we finish."

*If.* Casper bent over the page and wrote *chandler's*, and the wax-smell of tallow candles filled the attic so suddenly that Vess looked up, startled, sniffing at air that should have held only fish and lamplight. The fragments were leaking. Every entry made the room a little more Ossuary, a little less the world. She could hear, faintly, beneath the scratch of nibs, a sound like surf against a harbor wall that had not existed for two centuries.

They worked. The night deepened around them.

* * *

Quillon arrived past midnight, and he arrived the way weather arrives—not announced, simply suddenly there, filling the doorway of the attic stair with his broad gray presence. Casper's hand froze mid-word. Beside her, Vess went rigid as a man hearing the bailiff's knock.

But Quillon only stood there a long moment, looking at the maps, at the girl in the window seat, at the ledger open on Casper's knees full of writing that would have gotten them all rendered down to component debts within the hour if any guild auditor saw it. His face did something complicated. Guilt wearing armor, she had thought once, and the armor tonight was badly dented.

"I bought us until dawn," he said. "The night auditors believe there's a discrepancy in the sub-cellar inventories. I gave them a very boring error to hunt. Boring errors are the best kind; no one wants to be the one who found them." He came into the room slowly, as if the floor might object. "Then I'm expected at the morning reconciliation, where I will report that the error was nothing."

"You're lying to the guild," Casper said. "You've spent your whole life not doing that."

"I've spent my whole life doing exactly that." He lowered himself onto Vess's only stool, which groaned but held. Up close she could see how old he'd become in four days—how the guilt-armor had been load-bearing, and the war underneath was showing through. "I helped write the erasure, Hartwell. Do you understand what that means? Not signed it. *Wrote* it. My hand made the sentences that unmade a country. There is no penance for that. There is only whatever comes next." He nodded at the ledger. "So. What do you need from me?"

Casper had rehearsed cruelty for this moment. She found she had none left. "The founding contracts," she said. "The first bindings. If we're going to write a book the guild can't parse, I need to know exactly how theirs works—what shape the trap is, so ours can be the opposite of it."

Quillon was quiet for a while. Below them, faintly, the building shivered again, and somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked like a fist.

"They'll feel it soon," he said finally. "The fire failing. When they do, they won't send clerks. They'll send the Enforcement ledgers, and those books are..." He rubbed his face with both hands. "There's a vault under the founding hall. First Contract archive. Everything the guild ever bound is anchored there. If you mean to open a door out of Ossuary, Hartwell, the door is in that vault. And the way to it runs through every binding they ever wrote."

"Then tell me everything," Casper said, and dipped her pen. "Start at the beginning. Start with ash."

He started with ash.

* * *

Dawn was a rumor they ignored. The candle burned down and Vess fed it another, and another, and the attic filled with a haze of tallow smoke and memory. Fragments arrived in waves now, summoned by the growing book itself—it had begun, Casper realized with a chill that was half terror and half joy, to *call*. People who carried pieces of the erased country were dreaming of it tonight, all across Vharen, and some of them were waking and walking toward the guildhall without knowing why.

They came up the stairs one and two at a time.

An old woman who sold eels at the Saltmarket brought a recipe for honey-bread that her grandmother had hummed while kneading, though she'd never known where the song came from. A locksmith's apprentice carried a single street name his father had said deliriously on his deathbed—*Vellum Row, Vellum Row*—and wept when Ember spoke it back to him and the room briefly smelled of rain on warm stone. A ferryman. Two sisters who owned a dye shop and between them remembered a children's counting rhyme. A man who had nothing but the sound of a particular bell, a bell whose note he could reproduce perfectly with a wet finger on a wineglass rim, and when he did it, everyone in the attic heard, for three ringing seconds, a city at evening.

Each fragment went into the book. Casper wrote until her wrist screamed and then wrote with the other hand, and Ember sat beside her now, shoulder to shoulder, translating, correcting, sometimes simply resting her forehead against Casper's arm for a moment before continuing. The ledger fattened. The attic grew crowded with ghosts that were not ghosts—smells, sounds, the pressure of streets, the warmth of ovens, a market's murmur layered under the scratch of pens like sediment under a river.

And the fire below guttered.

Casper felt it happen twice during the night, unmistakably, the way you feel a sleeping person stop breathing beside you. The first time, the candle flames all leaned sideways together, toward the floor, as if bowing. The second time, the warmth went out of the room entirely—not cold, but *unwarmed*, heat withdrawing like a tide—and every person in the attic stopped working at once and looked at the floorboards.

"It's down to a thread," Ember said quietly. She had gone very pale. "When I was small, the fire breathed slow, like my mother sleeping. Now it's—" her hands shaped something small in the air, "—like a candle in a doorway with the door opening."

"How long?" Vess asked.

Ember didn't answer. She didn't have to. Everyone in the room did the same arithmetic, and everyone arrived at the same terrible sum: *not enough nights.*

"Then we don't sleep," Casper said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was the only kind of steadiness she'd ever possessed. "We write. All of it. Every scrap anyone carries. If the book is finished—if the country is *spoken* whole—then maybe the fire doesn't need to hold it anymore. Maybe the holding passes to us." She looked around the attic, at the eel-seller and the ferryman and the sisters and the man with the bell, strangers an hour ago, citizens of nowhere, and heard herself say the thing she had been circling all night: "We are the ledger now. All of us. That's what a country is. It was never the guild's contract. It was always just—people agreeing to remember each other."

Nobody applauded. That wasn't the kind of room it was. But the eel-seller wiped her eyes with her apron and said, "Well. Someone fetch me more ink, then," and the work resumed, and that was better than applause.

* * *

Near what should have been morning, Casper's grandmother surfaced.

Not literally—though in that attic, that night, Casper would not have bet against it. It was the handwriting. Vess had unrolled one last map, a survey of the guild district itself, drawn in a young and furious hand, and Casper knew it instantly, the way you know your own face in a dark window. The strong downstrokes. The little hooks on the ascenders. Marginal notes in the same fierce script she had followed through the archives like breadcrumbs: *ask about the third seal*, *they lie about the fire*, *the door is not lost, it is KEPT*.

"She drew this," Casper said. "Before I was born. Before—" Before she sold the door's location, before she traded one daughter's memory for another daughter's life, before the debt became Casper's spine. "She was mapping the guild from inside. She knew. All those years, she knew, and she couldn't say it, so she hid it in margins."

Ember leaned over the map. Her finger stopped on a notation near the founding hall—a small symbol Casper hadn't learned yet, a circle with a vertical stroke through it, like a door seen edge-on.

"That's the sign for *threshold*," Ember said softly. "In the grammar of place. Your grandmother marked where the door is." She looked up at Casper, and in the candlelight her eyes were the exact color of banked coals. "Under the founding hall. In the vault. The man downstairs was right."

Downstairs. Where Quillon had gone an hour ago, alone, to keep his appointment with the morning reconciliation—to lie to the guild one more time, or perhaps, Casper thought with a cold drop through her stomach, to buy them their last hour with something larger than a lie.

The building shuddered. This time it did not stop. The tremor ran up through the floorboards and into Casper's teeth, and every candle in the attic flattened to a blue bead of flame, and from far below—six floors down, beneath the sealed levels, beneath the furnace room where a flame older than the guild breathed its thread of breath—came a sound none of them had heard before.

A door, very deep underground, being unlocked.

Or being broken.

Casper closed the ledger, hugged it to her chest, and stood. Around her, the fragments of a dead country rose from their chairs.

"That's our cue," she said. "Everyone who can walk—we go down. Bring the book. Bring every word you carry." She was already moving toward the stairs, Ember's hand finding hers in the smoky dark, certain and small. "The vault opens tonight, or nothing opens ever again."
