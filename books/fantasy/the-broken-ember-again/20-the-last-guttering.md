## 20. The Last Guttering

Ember felt the fire die the way you feel a held breath finally released — not as an ending, but as a letting-go.

She had been listening to it all her life without knowing she was listening. The dragon-fire under the guildhall had been a sound beneath every other sound: a slow tidal breathing in the stone, a warmth at the soles of her feet even in winter, a faint copper taste on her tongue when she woke. In Ossuary, before, there had been a thousand such fires, one in every hearth of every house, and they had all been the same fire, the way all rivers are the same rain. When the country was unwritten, the thousand fires collapsed into this one, folded down and down like a letter creased into a pocket, and it had burned here in the dark for two hundred years, keeping what was left of home warm enough not to crumble.

Now it was going out.

She stood in the founding vault with Casper's hand around hers, and the great door — older than the guild, older than the forgetting, its wood grown from trees that had once shaded a street called Lullaby Row — stood open by the width of two fingers. Through the gap came air that did not belong to any ledger. It smelled of salt and cut grass and bread and rain that had somewhere honest to fall. The world's air. The unfiled air.

And behind her, below her, through the floor, the last ember of Ossuary guttered like a candle cupped against wind.

* * *

The vault was round, which no guild room was; the guild built in rectangles because rectangles could be counted. This room predated counting. Its walls were ash-dark stone veined with something that glimmered when Ember moved, as if the rock remembered being lava, or being loved. The door filled one wall entire, banded in bronze gone green, carved over every inch with names — thousands of them, tens of thousands, spiraling inward toward a lock shaped like an open hand.

It had been sealed behind the guild's first contract, signed in ash instead of ink, and it had opened only when Casper struck her own name from every book that held it. Ember had watched her do it. She would carry the watching of it for the rest of her life, however long or short that life proved to be: Casper Hartwell standing before the master ledger with the quill trembling not from fear but from the effort of meaning it, and then the stroke, clean and final, and the small sound the guildhall made when a name left it — a sound like a sigh, like a page turning, like a bird leaving a branch.

Afterward Casper had looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, and said, in a voice gone strangely light, "I don't know who I am." And then she had smiled, and it was the first smile Ember had ever seen on her that wasn't rationed.

"I know who you are," Ember had told her. "You're the one who opened the door."

Now the door was open, and the fire was dying, and those two facts were the same fact, and Ember stood between them and could not move.

* * *

The others had gone ahead — or rather, they had been taken ahead, gently, by the wind that was now pouring out of the dying country. The cartographer had wept at the threshold and let the wind carry him through, clutching his drowned maps to his chest like a child's toys. The descendants of the unwritten had gone one by one, each pausing to press a palm flat against the door's carved names, each whispering the fragment they carried — a word, a street name, a recipe for honey-bread that no oven outside Ossuary could ever quite manage — giving them back to the country even as they left it. And Quillon. Quillon had stayed longest, standing in the vault's center with his head bowed while the warm wind moved through his grey hair, and he had said, so quietly Ember almost missed it, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry," in the old tongue, the grammar of place, the language that anchored doors — and then he too had stepped through, and the door had not closed behind him, because it was done closing forever.

That left Ember, and Casper, and the fire.

"Tell me what it feels like," Casper said. Her voice had no name in it anymore. That was the strangest thing about her now — not the blankness where her name had been, but how everything else had rushed in to fill the space, how much more *her* she seemed without the word that owned her. "The fire. I can feel the floor getting cold but I don't know what I'm feeling."

Ember closed her eyes and listened.

"It's not pain," she said. "Everyone thinks it would be pain. But it's — do you remember the day we found the attic window stuck? And you forced it open and the whole city came in at once, the noise and the smell, and you laughed?" She felt Casper nod. "It's like that. Two hundred years of holding everything in one small room, and now the window is open, and everything wants out."

The wind stirred again, warmer than before, impossibly warm for air that had crossed a threshold from nowhere. It smelled, suddenly and specifically, of Ossuary. Ember's knees nearly buckled. It was the smell of the market at dusk — roasting chestnuts and wet cobbles and the green bite of the canal — and underneath it, fainter, the smell of her mother's shawl, of lamp-oil and cedar, of the particular dust of the schoolroom where she had learned to write her name in a script that no longer existed anywhere but inside her.

Her mother's lullaby had been sold at auction. A stranger in a good coat had bought it for eleven marks and carried it away in a vial, and Ember had watched, and Casper had bid everything she didn't have to win back one small vial of memory — not the lullaby, but another one, a lesser one, the memory of a dog whose name Ember couldn't recall until the vial broke open against her palm in the alley and the dog came flooding back, ears and mud and all, and she had cried in Casper's arms over a dog while her mother's voice went home with someone else.

The wind knew the lullaby anyway. The wind had been there. It carried the melody now, broken into pieces, distributed across the exhalation of a whole country — three notes in the smell of chestnuts, four in the warmth, the rest in the soft settling of ash somewhere far below their feet — and Ember understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was the last time. When the wind finished exhaling, the song would be gone. Not hidden. Not stored. Gone, the way a country can be gone.

* * *

"You have to choose," Casper said. She didn't say it cruelly. She said it the way she read aloud figures that couldn't be rounded — plainly, because plainness was a kindness when the number was terrible. "I won't choose for you. No one gets to choose for you ever again. But Ember — the door is open now, and it won't stay open past the last of the fire. And the fire—"

"I know about the fire."

"The world will forget you." Casper's hand tightened around hers. Without a name, her grip was just a grip, human and warm and unregistered. "That's what it does. It forgot a whole country; it will forget one girl by morning tea. You'll step through and be a stranger everywhere, speaking a language no one knows, carrying memories no one can verify. You'll be — you'll be inventory again, in a way. Unclaimed inventory. It will be hard, Ember. It might be harder than anything."

"And if I stay?"

Casper didn't answer. She didn't have to. They both felt the answer rising through the floorboards, cold now where warmth had lived for two centuries.

If Ember stayed, she would go with it. She was the last citizen; the fire was the last hearth; and a hearth without its people was just a hole in a wall. When the ember finished guttering, whatever remained of Ossuary — the stasis, the staved-off crumbling, the two hundred years of borrowed time — would finish too, and it would take its last inhabitant with it, gently, the way sleep takes a child. She would become part of the exhale. One more note in the wind. Her mother's lullaby would get its ending after all, sung in the only voice left that knew it.

It would not hurt. That was the horror of it, the seduction of it. It would not hurt at all. It would feel like being put away properly, like a book returned to its shelf, like a name written in the right hand at last.

* * *

Ember walked to the center of the vault and knelt and pressed both palms flat against the stone floor.

Cold. Truly cold now, for the first time in the history of the world, the floor of Ossuary was cold. Beneath her hands she could feel the shape of the fire's absence the way your tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. And yet — not absence entirely. Embers. The word was her name and she had never, until this moment, understood why her parents had chosen it. An ember is not a flame. An ember is what a fire leaves when it stops being a fire — a promise made of endings, heat without light, the part of burning that waits. Fires need feeding. Embers need only carrying.

Below her, in the dark, the last ember breathed. Once. Twice. Each breath longer than the last, the pauses between them filling up with silence, and in the silences Ember could hear everything the fire had been holding: her city's rain on its roofs, the creak of the ferry across the canal, the school bell, the argument the baker had had with his brother in the spring of the last year, unresolved, still waiting to be resolved. Two hundred years of ordinary life, kept warm. All of it wanting out. All of it trusting her to let it out well.

"Ember." Casper knelt beside her. "Whatever you choose, I—"

"Don't say goodbye." Ember lifted her head. "Please. If you say goodbye I'll think this is a story with an ending, and I've had enough of endings. Every story I was ever in ended badly. Let this one just — pause. Like a door held open."

Casper's face did something complicated and brave. "All right," she said. "No goodbyes."

The wind gusted again, and this time it spoke.

Not in words. The old tongue had never needed words for the large things; words were for the small ones, for prices and directions and the names of dogs. The wind spoke in the grammar of place, the deep structure underneath the language, and what it said was: *here*. What it said was: *this was real*. What it said was every street name at once, Lullaby Row and Cinder Lane and the long avenue called simply Enough, and the name of the ferryman, and the name of the baker's brother, and the name of the dog — *Bramble*, his name was Bramble, oh, Bramble — and the name of her mother, which Ember had not heard spoken aloud in two hundred years and which rose through her now like water rising in a well, filling her, overflowing her—

"Mama," she said, in the old tongue, and the vault rang with it, and every carved name on the great door glimmered in answer, ten thousand dead citizens saying *yes, yes, we remember her too.*

And then, softer, the wind said the thing it had come all this way to say. It said her name. Not *Ember* — that was the new one, the attic one, the one Casper had given her with such careful tenderness among the dusty ledgers. It said her true name, her Ossuary name, the one her mother had whispered into her hair every night, the one that meant *the light that stays when the fire goes elsewhere.* It said it once, completely, and then it began, syllable by syllable, to take it back.

That was how the erasure worked. She had known this since she was small. The country hadn't been destroyed; it had been *unwritten*, and unwriting happened at the edges first, and she was the last edge. As the fire died, the country would take back its name from her mouth, and then its streets from her memory, and then its faces, and if she stayed she would feel all of it leave, gently, gently, until there was nothing left of her to feel anything.

Unless she went through.

Through, she would keep it all — every street, every face, every note of the stolen lullaby — but she would keep it alone, in a world that would look at her and see nothing worth remembering, a girl with mud on her dress and an ancient grief in her mouth. Through, she would be the whole country in one small body, and no one would ever know, and some nights, maybe most nights, the weight of being the only citizen of somewhere would crush her flat.

Stay, and dissolve sweetly into the dark. Go, and carry the dark into the daylight, alone, forever.

Those were the choices. They had always been the choices. She had simply never let herself read them clearly until now, kneeling on the cooling stone with the wind of her homeland combing through her hair like fingers.

* * *

The fire took its last breath.

Ember felt it pass — a long, slow exhalation from the depths, two hundred years leaving in a single warm wind that rose through the vault and swirled the ash and lifted every loose page and feather and thread of dust into a slow golden spiral, and in the spiral, for one impossible moment, she saw them all. The whole country, rendered in motes. Streets and rooftops, the canal glittering, laundry lines, the schoolroom window, her mother at a doorway with her hand raised — waving, always waving, that was the memory the vial had held, the one Casper had bought back for her, her mother waving from a doorway — and the spiral turned, and the images thinned, and the wind carried them up and out through the gap in the great door, out into the world, spending itself at last, generous to the very end.

Ossuary exhaled. And was quiet.

The cold that followed was absolute and clean, like the inside of a star's memory. The carvings on the door dimmed. Somewhere above them, Ember knew, the guildhall was changing — the ledgers blanking themselves page by page, the impossible inventory evaporating, the bottled thunder going silent, the widow's second shadow slipping free to rejoin its owner. A building built to hold a country finding itself suddenly empty, and not minding.

The gap in the door remained. Two fingers wide. Then three, as if the door itself were breathing — or as if it were waiting.

"Ember." Casper's voice, nameless and steady. "The wind is stopping. Whatever's going to happen is going to happen now."

Ember looked at the gap. Through it: the world. Grey morning light, the smell of rain coming, the ordinary enormous noise of a city that had never heard of Ossuary and never would. A world that would forget her by morning tea.

She looked back at the cold floor, at the country that had made her and unmade itself and trusted her with everything it had left.

Then she looked at Casper — this strange, fierce, ruined woman who had struck her own name from the books to open a door for a girl she'd been ordered to guard, who had bid everything she didn't have for a stranger's memory, who was kneeling in the ash of a dead nation without a single regret Ember could find in her face.

Alone, the wind had said. Alone, alone. But the wind had said it before Casper reached out and took her hand.

Ember stood up.

"Casper," she said — the name that wasn't a name anymore, the empty word, and watched it land like a gift anyway — "I know a story. It doesn't end. Do you want to hear how it starts?"

She turned to the door, and put her hand on the bronze, and pushed.
