## 2. The Word Before Kingdoms

The child slept in the scriptorium for three days, curled on a pile of vestment offcuts like a fox that had decided the henhouse was now its territory, and I spent those three days learning that I had been wrong about everything.

I want to be clear about what kind of wrong. Not the ordinary wrong of a scribe who miscounts a column of figures — though heaven knows I've done that too, and blamed the ink. This was the deeper wrong, the kind that starts in the spine and works upward. For twenty-six years I had been told, and had believed, that magic could not enter this valley. That was the whole point of the valley. The temple stood here because here, and only here, the world was honest. Ink meant what it said. Debts stayed paid or unpaid. Nothing hummed.

And yet on the first night, while Sister Maren snored in her cell two doors down, the wards above the scriptorium door began to sing.

Not loudly. A sound like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, so faint I thought at first it was my own ears ringing from too many hours of copying. But I got up, candle in hand, and stood beneath the lintel where the old ward-stones were set into the wall — stones so ancient no one living knew their purpose, which the elders called "foundational" when they meant "we have no idea" — and I watched them glow. A dull ember-color. The color, I would later realize, of the thing the child carried pressed against its sternum like a stolen coal.

The wards sang all night. By morning they had stopped, as if embarrassed, and the elders convened an emergency session from which I, being merely indentured property with excellent penmanship, was excluded. This did not stop me from listening at the door, which I regard not as eavesdropping but as archival research conducted in real time.

"The wards are responding to something," Elder Corvane said. He has a voice like a door hinge that has been oiled just often enough to be annoying rather than silent. "Something that should not be in the valley."

"The child," said Elder Maren — my Maren, my snoring, kind Maren, which stung more than I expected.

"The child is mud and hunger," Corvane replied. "Children are always mud and hunger. It is whatever the child *carries* that concerns me."

I slipped away before they could say anything I'd have to pretend I hadn't heard, and went back to the scriptorium, where the child was awake and watching me with eyes the color of pond ice, and where I made the decision that would ruin my life in the most interesting way possible.

I decided to treat the child's speech as a grammar problem.

You have to understand: this is how I love things. My mother died when I was nine; I loved her through the letters she'd left in a recipe book, annotating measurements in a shorthand only she and my father understood. My father vanished when I was fourteen — vanished is the temple's word; *sold* is mine, though I didn't know yet how right I was — and I loved him through the single letter he sent after, full of strange pauses, as if he were writing around something standing behind him. And Tam. Tam I loved through everything, through the games we invented with the old alphabets in the temple library, through the cipher we built together the winter before he disappeared, a cipher based on the pre-kingdom runes carved into the valley's boundary stones. Tam was eleven and I was fifteen and we were going to be great scholars together, and then he walked out one morning to check the snares and never came back, and the searchers found his coat folded neatly on a rock by the hollow line, folded the way you fold a coat when you expect to come back for it, or when someone has told you that you won't need it where you're going.

So yes. When a feral child spoke a tongue that existed before kingdoms, I did not pray about it. I opened a grammar.

* * *

The old grammars live in the restricted stacks, which means they live wherever I say they live, because I am the one who re-inks the catalog and the catalog is the temple's true scripture, whatever the elders think they're preaching. I pulled three volumes over two nights: Hallowell's *Primer of the Deep Tongue*, a water-damaged lexicon attributed to some anonymous sister of the fourth century, and — my prize — a fragmentary grammar bound in grey leather with no title, which I had found years ago misfiled under agricultural ledgers and had kept quiet about ever since, because some discoveries you hoard the way dragons hoard, out of instinct rather than greed.

The child sat across the table from me, eating the bread I'd smuggled in, watching my pen move.

"All right," I said. "Let's try this again. You said — and I'm writing this phonetically, so forgive me — *'kess iru vael Thackeray.'*"

At the name, the child went still. Not frightened still. The stillness of someone who has been waiting for you to catch up.

"*Thackeray,*" I said again. "That's — that's a name. That's my name. Where did you get my name?"

The child touched its own chest, then pointed at me, then made a sound I can only render as a low falling tone, and I felt that tone somewhere behind my ribs, the way you feel a bell.

I worked until dawn. This is what I found, and I set it down here exactly as I worked it, because if anyone ever reads this, I want them to see the moment scholarship stopped being a wall I hid behind and became a door I couldn't close.

*Kess* — Hallowell gives "to carry" or, in the older stratum, "to carry what was given." *Iru* — a marker for the second person, but reflexive; not *you* but *you-who-are-the-same-as.* And *vael* — the anonymous grammar was explicit about this one, almost urgent, the way a text gets when its author has been burned once: *vael, n., the name-thread; that part of a soul which answers when the family name is spoken.* The old tongue did not separate people from their names the way we do. Your name wasn't a label. It was a limb.

So the sentence the child had spoken on the first day, the sentence everyone assumed was gibberish, resolved to something like: *I carry what was given, you-who-share-the-name.*

And then I turned the grey-leather grammar to the appendix — the part written in a different hand, cramped and hurried, added by someone who had learned the deep tongue late and imperfectly — and I read the example sentence the author had chosen to illustrate the word *vael*, and the floor of the world tilted.

The example name was *Thackeray*.

Not a common name. Our name existed in exactly one family line in this valley, in a house by the mill that had been empty eleven years, in temple records I personally maintained. The author of that appendix had written our name into a centuries-old grammar, and beneath it, in the same cramped hand, a gloss: *the thread runs to the court; the payment was recorded; the fragment answers still.*

I sat there until my candle drowned in its own wax, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. The handwriting was cramped and hurried and slanted left, and I had seen it every week of my life since I was fifteen, because the temple keeps its records forever, and among those records is a boy's copybook practice page, and a letter full of strange pauses, and a signature on a debt-bond — Tam's bond, taken up by me at fifteen when the temple offered to feed the orphaned Hartwell girl in exchange for her brother's debts becoming hers.

Wait. No. Let me be precise, because precision is the last thing I had left. The name in the grammar was *Thackeray*. My name is Freya Thackeray. But Tam and I — we took our mother's name after our father's debts surfaced, shed the old one like a skin. Our name before that, the name on our births, the name our father spoke —

It was the same name. It had always been the same name. *Thackeray* wasn't even ours originally; it was a scribal corruption, entered by some lazy clerk generations back, and we'd worn the error so long it had grown into us. The name in the grammar was the true form. The name the child carried. The name that answered.

Tam hadn't run away. Tam had been *collected*. Sold — or sold himself, or been sold by our father's creditors, the records blurring exactly the way records blur when powerful parties prefer ambiguity — to the court beneath the mountain. And the child, carrying its stolen ember of soul-fire, speaking the deep tongue, calling itself *you-who-share-the-name* —

The child looked at me across the dead candle and said, in its rusted-bell voice, the falling tone again, and this time I understood it without any grammar at all.

*Brother. Fragment. Home.*

* * *

They held the tribunal in the chapter house on the fifth day, and they made the mistake of seating me in the front row, presumably because a literate witness lends proceedings an air of legitimacy.

Elder Corvane presided. The child stood in the center of the room, washed now against its will, looking small and ferocious in a borrowed novice's robe. The ward-stones above the door glowed faintly, like coals deciding whether to catch.

"We have deliberated," Corvane announced, in the tone of a man who had deliberated alone and required witnesses. "The child is an anomaly. The valley's sanctity depends on anomalies being removed. There exists — " and here he paused, delicately, the way men pause when they're about to spend someone else's money — "a proper channel for such removal. The child will be surrendered to the wardens who serve the mountain court, who have, I'm told, expertise in these matters."

"The mountain court," I said, before my survival instincts could tackle my tongue. "You mean the court that buys souls."

A silence with a great deal of texture in it.

"Sister Thackeray," Corvane said patiently, "the temple does not concern itself with theological rumor."

"No," I agreed. "It concerns itself with debt. Which is why I know the mountain court pays promptly." I stood up. Somewhere in my chest the scholar and the sister and the orphan were all reaching for the same weapon, and what my hand closed around, naturally, was a quotation. "Chapter nine of the Foundational Accord, the passage your own lectern is carved with: *What enters the valley in innocence shall not be rendered out in bondage.* Innocence, Elder. Not citizenship. Not payment history. The child came in innocence. Your own stone says so."

Corvane's face did something complicated. "The Accord predates the current interpretation."

"Most truths do."

Maren, beside him, closed her eyes briefly — a prayer, or an apology, or both. And Corvane, who was many things but not stupid, understood that he had lost the room's heart even if he retained its authority, and pivoted the way pivoting men do.

"The matter is not yours, Sister. You are indentured. Your bond specifies obedience in matters temporal and spiritual. Surrender of the anomaly is both." He let that settle, then added, almost gently, the way you press a bruise to prove you know where it is: "Unless, of course, you'd prefer to discuss terms. The wardens arrive within the week. They are, I'm told, generous with those who cooperate. Debts have been forgiven for less."

There it was. My debt — eleven years of ink and silence, the price of my brother's disappearance twice compounded — dangled in front of me like bait on a hook, and every elder in the room watching to see which way the fish would turn.

I looked at the child. The child looked back, and touched its chest, where the ember slept, and said nothing, because it had already said everything: *you-who-share-the-name.*

Eleven years ago someone had folded a coat neatly on a rock by the hollow line, and I had spent every day since paying for the space where my brother used to be. I knew exactly what the temple was asking me to do. It was asking me to fold another coat.

"No," I said.

I said it quietly, which somehow made it worse for everyone. "No. Find another way to balance your books, Elder. This one stays."

Corvane sighed, and it was the sigh of a man closing a ledger on a loss. "Then I regret to inform you, Sister Thackeray, that your refusal constitutes breach of bond. The wardens will be notified of two irregularities upon their arrival." His eyes moved to the child, then back to me, and there was something in them that might, in a better man, have been pity. "Two."

* * *

That night I packed the grey-leather grammar, Hallowell, the lexicon, my good pens, and every scrap of coin I had not surrendered to the debt. It fit in one satchel. Eleven years of my life, and it fit in one satchel; I've thought about what that means more than once.

The child waited for me at the scriptorium door, already wearing its mud like armor newly reapplied. Above us, the ward-stones were singing again — louder now, insistent, the glass-rim note climbing toward something like alarm.

"They're coming early," I told the child, though I don't know how I knew. Perhaps the wards knew. Perhaps the ember did. "Tonight. Now."

The child took my hand. Its grip was astonishing, a grip like a root, and it tugged me not toward the chapel or the gates but toward the eastern wall, toward the hollow line, toward the boundary stones Tam and I had ciphered against a lifetime ago — and I understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only when it's too late for clarity to be useful, that the child had known the way out all along.

Of course it had. It had come in that way.

Behind us, at the valley's edge, the air had begun to bend — I could see it bending, light curving like heat-haze in midwinter — and something was walking through the bend, wearing a face I almost recognized.

"Warden," the child whispered, in the tongue before kingdoms, and pulled me into the dark.
