## 3. Debt Collectors

The emissaries arrived on a Tuesday, which struck me as deeply unfair. Catastrophe, in my experience, should have the decency to arrive at night, or during a storm — some hour when the world already expects to be upended. Instead they came at midmorning, while I was copying the tithe ledgers and the light through the scriptorium windows was doing its best impression of mercy.

There were three of them. That was my first clue something was wrong; nobody travels in threes unless they want to be seen as important, and nobody who is genuinely important travels in threes because they can't afford the company.

They wore borrowed faces. I don't mean that poetically. I mean that the faces did not fit — not badly, not in any way you could point to and say *there*, but the way a coat fits a man it was made for someone else. The woman in front had kind eyes set slightly too far apart, and a smile that arrived half a heartbeat before the rest of her expression agreed to it. Behind her stood two men with the same jawline, which would have been unremarkable except that they were clearly not brothers.

"Sister Anwen," the woman said, using the name the temple had given me along with my debt. "We've come about the child."

I kept my hands flat on the ledger page, because hands that tremble over ink ruin ten years of work. "The temple has many children."

"The temple has one child who speaks the tongue before kingdoms." She said it gently, the way you'd tell a widow her house had burned. "And one scribe who has been reading grammars she is not authorized to open. We know about the third shelf, Freya Thackeray. We know about your brother."

My brother's name sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Ten years since he walked out of this valley and never came back. Six years since the letters stopped. And now a stranger in an ill-fitting face said his name like she owned a piece of it.

Which, I suppose, she did.

"We're prepared to be generous," she continued. "Your debt-bond — eleven years remaining, isn't it? Eleven years of ink and silence. We will dissolve it. Today. A signature, and you walk out of this valley a free woman." Her smile widened by exactly one tooth. "All we ask is the child."

Behind me, from the corner where I'd tucked her among the blank folios, the child made a sound. Not a word. A small, low note, like a struck bell heard through water.

*Don't answer her,* I thought, absurdly, as if the child could understand me, as if warning her against the obvious trap were some great act of protection. *Don't say anything in that language. Don't let them hear how much you matter.*

"Give me a moment," I said aloud, "to consider."

"Of course." The woman folded her too-kind hands. "Though I should mention — the offer expires when we leave this room. The court beneath the mountain is patient about centuries, Freya Thackeray, but not about afternoons."

Eleven years. I did the arithmetic the way I do everything, compulsively and in the margins: eleven more years of copying tithes and death-records and the elders' increasingly paranoid correspondence. Eleven years until I could walk past the valley's edge stones without my feet flinching. I was twenty-six years old and I had spent nearly a third of my life paying for my father's debts, and here was a door out of it, held open by something wearing a face like a rented room.

All I had to do was hand over a mud-caked child who had looked at me three days ago with eyes the color of banked coals and spoken a word that made the temple wards hum for the first time in four hundred years.

A word, I was almost certain, that contained my family name inside it.

"I'll need to fetch her," I said.

The smile stayed fixed. The eyes moved. "Sister Anwen—"

"Freya," I said. "You used my true name. It seems only fair I get to use it back."

And then I did the single most reckless thing of my carefully ruled, margin-noted life. I picked up the child, who weighed approximately nothing and smelled of riverbank and woodsmoke, and I ran.

* * *

You are running.

This is a thing your legs remember from before, from the other place, though there you ran on ground that helped you and here the ground is only ground, dumb and indifferent, and it catches at your feet like it wants to keep you. The woman carries you. Her heart hammers against your ear, quick and stubborn, a bird refusing to land.

The borrowed-face people are behind you. You can feel them the way you feel weather coming — a pressure, a wrongness, a taste like coins at the back of your throat. They are not running yet. That frightens you more than running would. Things that wear faces do not need to run. They simply arrive where they have decided to be.

But the woman runs anyway, and her fear is loud and human and strangely warm, like a blanket with holes in it.

She smells of ink. Everything about her is ink — her fingers stained silver-black at the tips, her sleeves marked with old corrections, her thoughts (you can hear them, faintly, the way you hear mice in walls) moving in straight ruled lines even now, even fleeing: *the east path, the ford is flooded, the edge stones past the mill, no one goes past the mill.*

No one goes past the mill because the valley ends there. Because magic cannot follow. That is what everyone says, all the time, the way people say *the sun rises* — a fact so old it has forgotten it was ever a promise.

You are the proof the promise is breaking.

Because as she runs, you feel it — thin threads of the other place leaking into this one, following you like water finding cracks. The grass ahead of you greens too fast. A crow watches you pass and its eye holds a whole sentence. And under everything, growing louder with every step, a sound you know from before, from the mountain, from the cold glittering dark:

*Come home, little ember. Come home, little ember. Come home.*

You press your face into the ink-woman's shoulder and hold on.

* * *

The mill was abandoned, which meant it was the one building in the valley the elders didn't watch, which meant it was exactly where I'd have staked out an ambush if I were cleverer than I am. Fortunately for us, the emissaries were not interested in ambush. They were interested in conversation.

The woman stood at the far end of the mill floor, dust drifting down through the broken roof around her like the valley itself was trying to bury her politely. Her face still didn't fit. Up close, in the slanted light, I could see the seams of it — not literal seams, but a kind of shimmer, the way heat shimmers over a road in summer.

"You've run a long way," she said, "to arrive exactly where we knew you would."

"Noted," I said. "Anything else? The weather? My posture?"

"Freya." My name again, in her mouth like a coin being flipped. "Think about what you're doing. You are a scribe. Your entire value — to your temple, to yourself — is precision. Rules. The valley works because it is a place where things stay written. Magic cannot enter here. That is the covenant. That is why your brother sold himself *outside* these stones, because here he was safe from—" she gestured, a small elegant motion that took in the mill, the mountains, the whole crooked architecture of the world, "—from *us*. And now you propose to carry a fragment of stolen soul-fire directly across the hollow line. Do you know what happens to unbonded souls outside the valley?"

"No," I admitted. "But I notice you didn't offer to tell me."

"They dissolve," she said softly. "Or worse. They're *collected*. The court bought what it bought, scribe. Every fragment, every ember, every scrap of soul-fire ever signed away belongs beneath the mountain. That child is not a person to them. She is an *invoice*."

The child stirred against my hip. And then, in her impossible language, in that tongue that predated kingdoms and possibly predates the concept of borders, she said something short and fierce.

The emissary went very still.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"It doesn't translate," the woman said, and for the first time her borrowed face showed something real underneath — something that might have been fear. "That's rather the problem."

"She's saying no," I said. "I don't need a grammar for that. I've spent eleven years saying it myself, mostly to superiors." I shifted the child higher on my hip. "Here's my counteroffer. You go back beneath your mountain. You tell your court that the debt of Freya Thackeray remains unpaid, and that she intends to keep it that way, because a debt is the only chain I own that I chose — and I find I'd rather owe my temple than owe *you*."

"That's not a counteroffer. That's a eulogy."

"I'm a scribe. I'm very good with words. Eulogies especially — I write them for a living." I stepped backward, toward the mill's rear door, toward the slope beyond it, toward the gray line of standing stones on the ridge where the valley ended and the world got strange. "One more thing. Tell your masters the child said something else, too. Something I *did* understand, because it wasn't in any language at all. She said: *you can't collect what refuses to be counted.*"

I turned and ran again, and this time the emissaries did not follow, and their not-following was the most frightening sound I had ever heard.

* * *

You climb.

The hill above the mill is steep and the ink-woman's breath tears in her throat but she does not put you down, not once, not even when her legs shake. Humans are fragile. You had forgotten how fragile. In the other place, bodies were optional. Here they break and bleed and keep going anyway, which is either stupidity or courage, and you are beginning to suspect the difference is smaller than anyone wants to admit.

The standing stones rise ahead of you, gray and leaning, older than the valley's name for themselves. Between them the air looks wrong — not dangerous-wrong, but *unfinished*, like a page where the scribe stopped mid-word.

The ink-woman stops at the first stone. Her chest heaves. "Once we cross," she pants, "everything changes. The rules end. Magic comes back. I don't know what it does to me. I don't know what it does to *you*."

You look up at her. You reach out and touch her ink-stained fingers with yours, and you let a little of what you are leak through the touch — warmth, ember-light, the memory of a fire that was once a whole person.

Her breath catches. But she doesn't pull away.

"Right," she says, voice steadier than her hands. "Right. Well. I've read enough contracts to know the terms are always worse than advertised. Might as well sign."

She steps between the stones.

The world bends.

It is not dramatic, at first. The light changes color the way it does before a storm. Then the grass begins to grow as you watch it, green climbing the hillside in visible waves, and the crow from the mill speaks a full sentence in a language you both somehow understand (*they are coming, they are always coming*), and the ink-woman makes a small wounded sound and staggers, because the air out here is thick with everything her valley-starved body was never built to hold.

You slide from her arms and stand in front of her, small and mud-caked and burning quietly, and you raise your face toward the mountain on the horizon — the beautiful, terrible mountain, the cold glittering dark that has been calling you home since the day you were stolen out of it.

*No,* you tell it, in the tongue before kingdoms, in the voice of every soul it ever bought and lost. *I am not coming home. I am making a new one.*

Far below, at the tree line, three figures step out of the shadows wearing borrowed faces.

And beside you, the ink-woman draws a breath of magical air that hurts her like drowning, squares her shoulders like a woman preparing to argue with a mountain, and says:

"Well. I hope you appreciate, child, that I am doing this *out of contract*."

Ahead, the road unwinds through a country where anything might be true — and somewhere on it, you both feel it, the pull of the caravan of the marked, the ones who sold pieces of themselves and lived to wander.
