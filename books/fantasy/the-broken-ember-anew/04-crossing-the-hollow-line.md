## 4. Crossing the Hollow Line

You have been empty your whole life, and you did not know it until now.

That is the thing nobody tells you about being a fragment: you do not feel like a piece of something. You feel whole. You feel like the only real person in a world of echoes. The valley felt that way — soft and gray and muffled, like living inside a closed mouth. You thought that was what the world was.

Then the ink-woman takes your hand and pulls you across the hollow line, and the world *opens*.

It happens between one step and the next. One moment you are walking through wet grass at the valley's edge, mud sucking at your feet, Freya Thackeray's grip on your wrist tight as a manacle and twice as warm. The next moment —

Color. Not the thin washed-out colors of the valley but colors with *weight* to them, colors that lean against your skin. The grass ahead of you is so green it hums. The sky goes from pewter to a blue so deep it looks like looking down instead of up. And underneath everything, under the ground and the wind and your own small ribs, there is a sound like a great bell struck once, very far away, still ringing.

Magic. That is the word the temple people use when they say it cannot come here. Out here, past the line, it does not knock. It pours in through every seam of you, and you understand, all at once, standing in the wet grass with your mouth open, that you were built to hold this. That the emptiness you carried through the valley was not emptiness at all. It was *room*.

You laugh. It comes out of you sideways, a sound you have never made before, high and cracked and delighted, and Freya flinches as if you have thrown something at her.

"Child," she says, and her voice is strange — thinned out, stretched, like a word spoken underwater. "What — "

The magic is going into her too. You can see it. It slides off her like rain off oiled cloth, most of it, but some of it sinks in, and where it sinks in she shudders. Her knuckles are white around your wrist. Not holding you anymore. Holding herself up.

"It hurts," you say. You know this without knowing how you know it. There is a lot you know like that, suddenly. Things arriving in you along with the light. "The air here doesn't want you."

"The air and I have an understanding," Freya says through her teeth. "It tolerates me. I tolerate it. We are professionals." She takes one step forward and sways, catches herself on a fence post that isn't there — or wasn't, a moment ago; out here the posts seem to be deciding whether they exist based on how badly someone needs them. She leans on it anyway. "Keep talking. Your voice sounds — " She stops. Frowns. Tries again. "Louder. Out here."

She means: more real. You can hear it yourself. In the valley your words came out flat, like coins rubbed smooth by too many hands. Here each one rings.

*Ember,* says something inside you, in the old tongue, the tongue before kingdoms. *Little ember.*

And then you remember.

* * *

You do not want to remember. Remembering arrives anyway, riding the magic like a rider on a black horse, and it sets itself down inside you and will not be refused.

You are sitting in a hall lit by candles that burn without heat, and the walls are made of agreements. You know that sounds mad. The walls are made of agreements — shelves and shelves of them, contracts bound in vellum and iron, and each one whispers its own terms in a language like dry leaves. You are small. You are sitting on a floor of polished stone that reflects a ceiling full of stars that are not stars but *clauses*, arranged in constellations of obligation.

A voice above you, kind the way a trap is kind:

"You were bought honestly, little one. A soul, sold in seven parts. Six remain below, working. This seventh part was owed to us as interest." A pause. "But interest, we find, walks. Interest climbed out of the ledger and grew hands. So we must ask ourselves what to do with a debt that has learned to run."

You look up. You see a face wearing another face loosely, like a coat two sizes wrong.

"You will go back into the book," the face says gently. "All debts return home."

And you ran. You do not remember running, but your legs remember, and your lungs, and somewhere in the running the world went gray and quiet and safe, and there was a valley where magic could not follow because the valley had been *signed out of the contract*, centuries ago, by people who wanted nothing to do with any of it — and you fell through its door like a coin dropped through a slot, and an ink-woman found you sleeping in her scriptorium among the ledgers.

You come back to yourself kneeling in the wet grass. Freya is crouched in front of you, both her hands on your shoulders now, and her face is very close, and even half-drowned in air that hates her she has managed to arrange her expression into something dry and skeptical.

"You went somewhere," she says. "Your eyes went somewhere. Where?"

"A hall," you say. "With walls made of contracts."

Something moves behind her eyes — recognition, and grief, and a third thing she shuts away quickly, like a drawer slammed shut. "Of course they are," she mutters. "Why would the walls be stone like a normal court. Why would anything be *normal* again." She shakes you once, gently, by the shoulders. "Listen to me. Fragment-of-a-soul-whose-name-I-refuse-to-say-yet. Whatever you saw, whatever they told you — you are not a debt. Debts don't get muddy. Debts don't steal bread off temple windowsills and lie about it badly."

"I didn't lie about it badly," you say, offended.

"You said a dog took it. There are no dogs in the temple precincts. I checked."

You stare at her. She stares back, and the corner of her mouth does the thing it does — the small traitorous twitch she probably thinks she hides — and despite everything, despite the hall and the face and the bell still ringing under the world, you feel something in your chest that is not memory and not magic.

Warmth. Borrowed. But yours while you have it.

* * *

You walk. Or rather, Freya walks, leaning on nonexistent fence posts and breathing like each breath costs her a coin she cannot spare, and you walk beside her, and the country unrolls around you like a story being told too fast.

Out here the world is *drafted*, not finished. You begin to see it after an hour or so: hills that repeat themselves exactly, as if someone copied them and got lazy about variation. A river that flows uphill for twenty paces, loses its nerve, and flows back down. Birds whose song is slightly ahead of their flight, so that you hear them arrive before they do. Magic everywhere, thick as pollen, and it keeps trying to give itself to Freya, and her body keeps refusing it, politely at first and then less politely.

By late afternoon she has gone gray as valley-stone.

"Stop," you say.

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of old porridge."

"That," says Freya, "is my natural complexion, and I'll thank you not to editorialize." But her knees buckle on the word *editorialize*, and you catch her — you, half her size, catching a grown woman, which should be impossible except that out here the magic is pouring into *you* like water into a cup shaped exactly for it, and the cup turns out to be stronger than it looks. You lower her onto a flat rock that has thoughtfully become flat since she started falling toward it.

Her lips are tinged blue. Her breath makes a sound like paper tearing.

"What's happening to me," she says. It is not quite a question. She is a scribe; she wants the citation, the source, the footnote.

"The air is full of magic," you say. "Your body was raised in a place with none. It's like — " you search for it — "like a fish pulled out of water. Only backwards. You're a fish being drowned in air."

"How poetic. Did you learn that in the hall with the contract walls?"

"I learned it just now. Words come easier out here." You take her hand. Her fingers are cold. "Freya. If we stay out here, you die."

"If we go back," she says, "they catch you, and they put you back in a book, and I spend the rest of my life copying ledgers knowing what I traded for my comfort." She closes her eyes. Opens them. "I've done the arithmetic, child. I'm a scribe. Arithmetic is the one prayer I know."

"There's a third option."

"There is always a third option. It's usually worse than the first two."

"The caravan," you say. And you do not know how you know this either, except that the magic knows it and the magic is in you now, humming in your hollow places, filling you the way broth fills a hungry belly. "The ones who sold pieces of themselves. They walk the roads out here. They've learned how to live between — how to carry a little magic without drowning in it. They'd know what to do for you."

Freya is quiet for a long time. Wind moves through grass that is too green, and somewhere uphill a bird sings its song twice, once early and once on time.

"People who sold pieces of themselves," she repeats slowly. "To survive."

"Yes."

"And you're one of those. Aren't you. A piece. Sold." Her thumb moves over the back of your hand, back and forth, an absent motion, the way she strokes her pen when she is thinking. "God help me. I'm traveling with a clause."

"I prefer *fragment*."

"I prefer *child*," she says, sharp and sudden, and her voice cracks down the middle of the word. "Whatever the court calls you. Whatever the ledger says you are. You are a muddy, thieving, lying-about-badly *child*, and I am going to keep you one, if it kills me." She stops. Blinks. "Which, at current rates, it may."

You do not say anything. You climb onto the rock beside her, because the rock is big enough and because she is cold and because you have decided — in the way that fragments decide things, absolutely and without evidence — that this woman is yours now, the way the stolen ember was yours, the way the old tongue is yours. Found things. Kept things.

After a while her head tips sideways and rests, very lightly, on top of yours.

"I had a brother," she says, to the grass, to the wind, to no one. "He'd have liked you. He talked too much and stole things and lied about it badly." A long breath, torn-paper slow. "We don't speak of him in the valley. We especially don't speak of *where he went*."

The bell under the world rings again, fainter, and in its fading you feel the pull — the same pull you felt at the valley's edge, but closer now, warmer, threaded with woodsmoke and horse and voices speaking a dozen borrowed tongues. Down in the fold of the next hill, lanterns are lighting one by one, though the sun has not yet set. Wagons. Wheels. People.

"They're there," you whisper. "The marked ones."

"Mm." Freya does not lift her head. "If they ask," she says, "we're a scribe and her apprentice, traveling to — somewhere respectable. If anyone asks what happened to your family, you're mine now. Fully. Signed and witnessed." Her hand finds yours in the darkening grass and holds on. "Can you remember that?"

*Apprentice.* The word settles into your hollow places alongside the magic, and it fits almost as well.

"Yes," you say.

Down the hill, the first lantern of the caravan swings toward you through the dusk — and the figure carrying it stops dead at the sight of you both, and raises the light higher, and says in the tongue before kingdoms:

"Well. The mountain's missing piece, walking free — and an *unmarked* soul beside it. Tonight's already interesting."
