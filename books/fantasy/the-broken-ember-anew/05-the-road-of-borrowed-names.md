## 5. The Road of Borrowed Names

The caravan found us before we found it, which I suppose is the way of caravans — they move like weather, and you notice them when they're already overhead.

We had been walking three days out of the valley, following the old drover's road north toward the mountain, and I had learned two things in that time. First: the world beyond the wards was not the howling chaos the temple elders had always described. It was simply *larger* — louder, greener, more insistent about its own existence. Second: my body hated it. Every breath felt like drinking water that someone had whispered into. The child, by contrast, thrived. They ate wild sorrel with the enthusiasm of a goat and slept curled against my side like a coal that refused to go out.

I had stopped trying to name the child. Names, I'd decided, were things you gave to things you understood, and I understood nothing except that when the child spoke in that pre-kingdom tongue, the air around us grew briefly honest.

So it was dusk on the third day when we crested a rise and saw the fires below — a dozen wagons strung along the road like beads on a wire, hung with lanterns of colored glass, and people moving between them who all, without exception, turned to look at us at exactly the same moment.

"They know we're coming," I murmured, and the child made a sound I had come to translate as *obviously.*

An old woman detached herself from the nearest fire and came up the hill to meet us. She walked with a stick she clearly did not need, using it instead as a kind of punctuation for her opinions. She was small and brown and wrinkled like a well-read page, and when she looked at me, I felt catalogued.

"You're the scribe," she said.

"I'm—"

"From the valley. The one who ran." She said it without judgment, the way one notes that a river runs downhill. "Word travels faster than feet out here. I'm Maren. You'll eat with me tonight, because you look like a woman who hasn't eaten properly since she decided her principles were worth more than her supper."

I could not argue with this. It was, irritatingly, accurate.

* * *

The caravan called itself the Long Road Company, though Maren admitted over barley stew that "company" was generous. "We're what's left," she said. "People who sold something to survive, and then discovered survival has a taste."

I looked around the fire. A man with no shadow — not dim, *absent*, his outline crisp against the firelight where darkness should have pooled. A young woman whose reflection lagged half a second behind her movements, so that mirrors seemed perpetually surprised by her. A boy of perhaps fifteen who never blinked; he'd sold his tears, Maren said, and now his eyes dried like paper in the sun.

"The soul-marked," I said quietly.

"Sold souls, sold pieces of souls. The court beneath the mountain doesn't take whole people, usually. It takes *parts*. Your memory of your mother's voice. Your ability to feel cold. Twenty years of your dreams." Maren tapped her stick against the ground. "Me? I sold my grief. Best bargain anyone ever made me, worst thing I ever did. You can't mourn properly without it. I remember my daughter died, but it sits in me like a fact in a ledger. No weight to it at all."

The child, sitting beside me, went very still. I put a hand on their shoulder without thinking, and they leaned into it.

"And the court keeps those pieces?" I asked.

"In the vaults beneath. In the contract." Maren's eyes were sharp as awls. "You've seen soul-fire, haven't you? I can tell. You flinch when you look at the little one too long, like there's light behind their face."

I didn't answer. The child carried an ember of stolen soul-fire — a fragment escaped from the court's vaults, wearing flesh it had grown for itself. That was why the emissaries hunted us. That was why we walked toward the very place that wanted us dead or worse.

"Why does the caravan travel toward the mountain?" I asked. "If the court took pieces from all of you?"

Maren smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Because once a year, the court opens its lower galleries for the Renewal. And once a year, some of us walk down and ask for our pieces back." She snorted. "And the court laughs, and names a price, and most of us walk away again. But we walk down anyway. Hope is a stubborn animal. Doesn't know when it's been beaten."

* * *

I did not sleep that night. Instead I sat by the dying fire and did what I have done since I was six years old and first pressed a reed pen into wet clay: I thought about documents.

Maren found me there near dawn, or perhaps I found her — she moved quietly for a woman with an opinionated stick.

"Can't sleep either?" She lowered herself onto a wagon step. "Or is it the thinking kind of sleeplessness? I can see the gears."

"I keep coming back to what you said. About the contract. The master contract that holds all the sold souls."

"What of it?"

"I'm a scribe," I said. "Indentured, trained, bonded — I've spent eleven years copying temple ledgers, land grants, debt instruments, mortuary rolls. And every document I've ever read has a structure. A grammar. Even the cruelest ones. Especially the cruelest ones — cruelty loves precision." I wrapped my hands around a cup of tea gone cold. "A contract that holds thousands of souls across centuries? That's not just power. That's *text*. And text can be read."

Maren studied me for a long moment. "You talk about contracts the way our priest used to talk about gods. With fear and hunger both."

"My brother sold his soul to that court," I heard myself say. It was the first time I'd spoken it aloud since deciphering his name woven through the child's pre-kingdom words. "He vanished when I was twelve. Everyone said he died. But his name is in the language before kingdoms, braided through a spell of ownership, and I *read* it. I read my own family bound into someone else's ledger."

The child stirred in the wagon behind us, and Maren glanced toward the sound, then back at me.

"And what do you intend to do about it, scribe?"

"I don't know yet. Read more, I suppose. It's the only thing I know how to do."

"Then let me teach you the part they don't teach in temples." Maren leaned forward, and the firelight caught the hollow places in her face. "Out here, among the marked, we've learned something the valley never taught you. Reading isn't passive. A reader completes a text. Every contract ever written waits for a reader to make it real — and that means every contract ever written can be *unmade* by reading it differently."

"That's not how law works."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "That's how *magic* works. Law is just magic that got bored and started wearing clothes."

I laughed — actually laughed, for the first time since the child had appeared at the scriptorium door — and the sound startled birds out of a nearby hedgerow.

"Come here tomorrow," Maren said, standing, retrieving her stick. "Bring whatever you copied from the child's speech. Bring your grammars. We'll read together, you and I. And Freya Thackeray—" she paused at the edge of the firelight, "—when we reach the mountain, and you stand in front of whatever passes for a door down there, remember: the court bought souls because souls are *legible*. They can be written down, owned, filed. Which means somewhere in that glittering dark, there's a page with your brother's name on it."

She tapped her stick once against the earth, punctuating the sentence.

"And pages," she said, "can be torn out."

* * *

The next morning I sat with Maren in the back of her wagon while the caravan rolled north, surrounded by books she'd collected over sixty years of walking — some printed, some hand-copied, some that hummed faintly when touched, which I tried not to think about too hard.

The child sat across from us, watching with the fierce attention they gave everything, and when I unrolled my transcriptions of their speech, they reached out and touched the glyphs almost tenderly, the way you'd touch a photograph of home.

"This word here," Maren said, tapping one of the braid-like characters. "It recurs in every phrase the child speaks that references belonging. Home, kin, hearth. Do you see the root inside it?"

I looked. And looked again. My breath caught.

"It's the same radical that appears in the binding-spell around my brother's name."

"Not the same," Maren corrected gently. "Inverted. Mirrored. The word for belonging, written backwards, becomes the word for ownership. The court didn't invent its contracts, scribe. It *translated* them. Took the oldest language of connection and reversed it into a language of possession." Her eyes gleamed. "Which means every contract in that mountain is built on a grammar that predates it. A grammar it borrowed and bent."

"And a borrowed thing," I said slowly, feeling the idea take shape like a figure emerging from fog, "can be returned."

"Now you're thinking like a danger to the established order." Maren grinned, gap-toothed and delighted. "Tell me, when you copy a document, do you merely transcribe? Or do you understand what you're writing?"

"Both. Always both. A scribe who copies blindly propagates errors."

"Mm. And if you were given a contract to copy — the master contract itself, say — could you reproduce it faithfully? Every clause, every seal, every hidden proviso?"

I considered lying. Decided against it. "Yes. If I could study it long enough. Copying is... it's how I pray, honestly. My hand knows things before my head does."

"Then here's what I know about the court beneath the mountain." Maren's voice dropped, and even the humming books seemed to lean closer. "They are proud. Pride makes them ceremonial. Ceremony means *public documents*, displayed, renewed, recited. And once a century—" she held up one finger, "—they hold the Renewal, where the master contract is brought out and reaffirmed before witnesses. Before *readers*."

"When is the next Renewal?"

Maren smiled, and outside the wagon the road stretched north toward a mountain that was beginning to resolve itself on the horizon, gray and patient as a closed fist.

"Soon," she said. "Sooner than you think. Sooner, I suspect, than the court would like — because they've felt the ember moving toward them, and prideful things get careless when they're frightened." She closed her hand around mine, her grip dry and strong. "Rest, scribe. Eat. Learn the child's words until they live under your skin. Because in a few days' time, you're going to walk into the largest library of stolen souls in existence — and you, my dear, are going to be the only person in it who reads for love rather than profit."

That night, the child fell asleep against my ribs, and I lay awake watching the mountain grow in the dark like a slow tide.

Somewhere inside it was a page with Tam's name on it.

I had spent eleven years learning to write what other people told me to write.

I was done taking dictation.
