## 6. What the Mountain Remembers

The mountain has a smell.

You would not know this, because you have never been eaten by a mountain before. I have. Or almost. Or in dreams that are not quite dreams, which is worse, because you cannot wake up from something that is already awake inside you.

It smells like coins left too long in a dead man's pocket. Like candle smoke and cold iron and the particular sweetness of fruit rotting somewhere it cannot be found. The caravan calls it *the mountain's breath*, and they say it respectfully, the way you talk about a creditor who is standing behind you.

I am getting ahead of myself. Freya says I do that. She says it in her scribal voice, the one that means *this sentence needs restructuring*, and then she usually feeds me something, so I have decided to forgive her for it.

We are walking toward the mountain. Everyone in the caravan is walking toward the mountain, because everyone in the caravan has sold a piece of themselves to the court beneath it, and pieces want to go home the way tongues want to find the gap where a tooth was. Asha, who sold her laughter. Tam, who sold his memory of his mother's face — he remembers that he had one, remembers loving her, remembers everything *around* the hole, like a picture with a person cut out of it. The old woman, Maren, who will not say what she sold, which frightens me more than anything else on this road, because I know what it costs to keep a secret about yourself.

And me. I am not going home. I am going back to the place that made me, which is different, and I would explain how if I could, but the words for it live in the language before kingdoms, and Freya only knows fragments, and I only know fragments, and between us we are mostly gaps.

* * *

The nightmares started three nights ago, when the mountain first showed its teeth above the tree line.

Here is what happens: I sleep. That part is normal. Then I am small again — smaller than I am now, which is already small — and I am standing in a hall made of glass and gold that has been melted together by someone who did not care whether they went with it. There are thrones. There are many thrones, because the court does not have one king; it has a *quorum*, and the quorum is always hungry, and hunger is the only law down there.

In the dream, I am on a table. Not a cruel table. A kind one, padded, warm, the way a trap is warm when it wants you comfortable. And there are voices all around me, beautiful voices, voices like honey poured over knives, and they are saying:

*Name it.*

That is the whole ceremony. That is the entire terrible engine of the place. Before you can enter the court beneath the mountain, you must name something you love and leave it at the door. They take it out of you gently. That is the horror no one warns you about — not the pain, the *gentleness*. They lift it out like a splinter, like a stone from a boot, and they set it in a little alcove with your name on it, and it glows there, and you feel *lighter*, and you thank them.

Then you walk into the court, and the thing you loved stays glowing in its niche forever, feeding the lamps.

The lamps of the court are all lit with love. I remembered that before I remembered my own name.

* * *

"Ember."

Freya's voice. In the waking world, she calls me Ember, because the first thing I ever said to her was a word from the old tongue that means both *ember* and *the last light you carry out of a burning house*. She thought it was a name. It is not a name. But names are just words we agree to answer to, and I have agreed, because she feeds me and mends my coat and reads to me from grammars in a voice that makes even declensions sound like stories.

"Ember, you were crying in your sleep."

"I don't cry," I said, which was true in the way that most lies are true — technically, structurally, while being entirely false.

"You were crying in your sleep," she repeated, in the scribal voice, and sat down beside me on the bedroll. Around us the caravan's fires burned low. The mountain filled half the sky now, black against the stars, wearing snow like a collar it resented.

"The court takes things," I said. "When you come in. You have to give them something you love. They keep it. They burn it slowly, for light."

Freya was quiet for a long time. She is good at quiet. Scribes are trained in it the way soldiers are trained in marching.

"And you remember this because—"

"Because I was made there." I pulled my knees up. "Out of a piece of somebody. A soul somebody sold. They grew me around it like a pearl around grit. I don't know whose soul. I don't know if the person is still alive or still *anyone*. But I know the doors. I know the price of the doors."

"Then we don't use the doors," Freya said, and there was something fierce and foolish in it, the valley-girl stubbornness that got us both out of the temple and halfway across a country that bends. "There must be another way in."

"There isn't. There's the door, and there's the long dark under the roots where the things without names live, and I have met them in the dream too, and I promise you the door is better."

She looked at the mountain. I watched her look at it, and I saw her doing sums behind her eyes, because that is what she does instead of panicking — she calculates. I have never met anyone who calculates grief the way Freya Thackeray does, quietly, in columns, with margins.

"What did you leave?" she asked finally. "At the door. When they made you."

"Nothing," I said. "They took the piece of soul. That was the toll. I was the toll." I smiled at her, because smiling is a tool and I am very good with tools. "Don't make that face. It's efficient, at least. No alcove with my name on it. Nothing of mine burning in their lamps."

"That's not comforting, Ember."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be information. You're welcome."

* * *

But here is the thing I did not tell her, because telling her would have been like handing a knife handle-first to someone standing behind you:

The door works both ways.

Whatever you name at the threshold, whatever you leave glowing in its little niche — the court keeps it, yes, burns it slowly, yes. But it also *uses* it. Love is fuel, and fuel remembers its shape. On the worst nights, in the deepest halls, the court shows you your own love, burning, and asks you to do one more small thing, sign one more small line, and you do it, because the alternative is watching the fire gutter.

Everyone who enters comes out owing more than they carried in. That is not a rule written anywhere. That is simply what the mountain remembers, and what the mountain remembers, the mountain repeats.

* * *

Two days from the gates, Maren caught up with me on the road. She walks with two sticks and the patience of a woman who has outlived every argument she ever started.

"You've been dreaming of the doors," she said. Not a question.

"How do you know?"

"Because you've stopped humming." She lowered herself onto a boulder with the care of someone negotiating with her own knees. "Children hum when they're safe. You haven't hummed since the mountain came over the ridge."

I hadn't known I hummed. I filed that away — *I hum when I'm safe* — as evidence, filed next to *I cry in my sleep* and *Freya knows it*, in the growing drawer of things about myself that other people see first.

"Maren. What did you sell? To the court."

She looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes are the color of tea brewed too long, and they miss nothing, which is its own kind of debt.

"A clever question deserves a clever answer," she said, "so: I sold the version of myself that could have been happy elsewhere. They were very pleased with it. Rare stock." She tapped her stick against the stone. "Now let me tell you something worth more than my secrets, little ember. When you reach the gate — and you will reach the gate, because you're the kind of creature doors open for — they will ask you to name what you love. And you will be tempted to lie. To name something small. A button. A song. Something you can afford to lose."

"Isn't that wise?"

"It's suicide," Maren said cheerfully. "The court can smell a cheap offering the way a dog smells fear. Name something small and they'll dig until they find the real thing, and they'll take *that* plus your dignity. No. If you must pass those doors, you name the truest thing, and you hold its weight in your chest the whole way through, and you do not let them see it bend you." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Grief anticipated is grief halved. Grief hoarded is grief that keeps."

"Grief that keeps," I repeated.

"Write that down. Your scribe will want it for her book."

* * *

That night I watched Freya by the fire. She was mending the seam of my coat again — it does not need mending that often; I think the sewing is for her, the way counting is for me — and I watched her mouth move sometimes, silent, practicing nothing.

"Freya. When we get to the gate. What will you name?"

Her needle stopped. Just for a heartbeat. Then it kept moving, steady as a pulse.

"Nothing," she said.

"They won't accept nothing."

"Then I'll think of something." She didn't look up. "Eat your stew."

And that is when I understood, with the cold sliding certainty of a dream coming true in daylight, that Freya Thackeray had already chosen what she would leave at the door. She had chosen it weeks ago, maybe the night she first read my word in an old grammar and found her family's name woven through it like a thread through cloth. She was carrying it the way smugglers carry contraband — close to the body, wrapped in ordinary clothes, never once spoken aloud.

She was going to name *me*.

Or she was going to name him — the brother whose soul the court bought, the brother she had crossed the world to bargain for — and I could not decide which possibility frightened me more, because one meant I would burn in their lamps, and the other meant she would trade away her reason for coming, and either way the fire would be fed with the same fuel:

Love. Hoarded. Kept.

Above us, the mountain breathed its coin-and-candle breath, and far below, in a hall of glass and gold, I knew the quorum had begun to stir. Doors do not open for us, I thought. We open for doors.

Three days to the gate. And Freya was still practicing saying nothing.
