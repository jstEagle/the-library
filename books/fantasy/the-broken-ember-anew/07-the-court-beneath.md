## 7. The Court Beneath

The mountain swallowed us politely.

That was the worst part, I decided somewhere between the third and fourth gate — the courtesy of it. I had expected teeth. I had prepared, in the small hours when the child slept against my side, for a descent full of screaming stone and grasping shadows, something out of the cautionary tales the temple fed its indentured children along with their gruel. Instead we were met at the first gate by an usher who bowed as though we were expected guests rather than trespassers, and offered us wine.

I did not drink it. The child did not either, which told me more about the child's memories than any nightmare had.

"Freya Thackeray," the usher said, reading from a card that had not existed a moment before he reached into his coat for it. "And guest." He pronounced *guest* the way one pronounces *vermin* in polite company — clearly, and with a small pause afterward to let everyone appreciate the restraint involved.

The child's hand found mine. Small fingers, ink-stained now from our weeks on the road — I had taught them letters by firelight, and they had taught me that the word before kingdoms had no word for *debt*, which I found both beautiful and damning.

We descended. Stairs first, then a road that sloped gently down through rock that glittered like a merchant's idea of heaven. Veins of light ran through the walls — gold, mostly, but here and there a blue so deep it looked like the sky remembering itself. Chandeliers hung from ceilings that must have been a hundred feet high, each crystal catching the glow of the veins and scattering it into a rain of small suns. It was beautiful the way a ledger is beautiful: everything accounted for, nothing alive.

*You must name what you love,* the old woman had told us, back on the Road of Borrowed Names, her soul-marked hands folded like sleeping birds. *The mountain takes it at the door. Not forever. Just long enough to make you agreeable.*

I had named nothing aloud. I had spent the entire descent hoarding my loves like contraband — the child's breath warm against my shoulder, the smell of fresh ink, my brother's laugh, which I had not heard in eleven years and carried anyway, the way you carry a coin too worn to spend but too dear to discard. If the mountain wanted them, it would have to pickpocket me, and I had been poor long enough to guard my pockets with my life.

At the final gate, two guards in livery the color of dusk examined us. One held up a lantern made of what appeared to be solidified moonlight and passed it over the child, who flinched.

"It has no name," the guard reported to someone behind us.

"It has several," I said. "None of them yours."

The guard looked at me with the weary expression of a man who has heard every variety of cleverness and found none of it admissible. "Names are required for entry."

"Then enter me under the name I was born with," I said, "and let the child come in under mine. Freya Thackeray, and Freya Thackeray the lesser. We are, after all, a matched set."

Somewhere behind the guards, someone laughed. It was a familiar laugh, and it went through me like a blade going through wet paper — clean, almost painless, and then all at once not.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was holding audience.

I had imagined thrones and torches, wailing petitioners, some grand architecture of cruelty. What I found instead was a ballroom. An actual ballroom, vast and vaulted, where couples turned in slow measure across a floor of polished obsidian while a string quartet played music that sounded almost, but not quite, like anything human. Along the edges stood the courtiers — beautiful in the way frost is beautiful, in the way a contract is beautiful, all their loveliness arranged to conceal the terms.

And at the far end, at a desk instead of a throne, sat a figure in silver-grey, writing.

He did not look up. But I knew the set of those shoulders. I had watched them hunched over copybooks in a kitchen in the valley twenty years ago, a boy of ten practicing his letters because our mother said a scribe's hands must be steady before a scribe's heart can be trusted. I knew the way his left elbow tucked in, the way his pen moved in strokes too even to be human anymore.

"Aldric," I said.

The music did not stop. The dancers did not falter. But the pen stopped, and that was worse — that small, precise cessation, as if the whole room were an instrument and he had lifted the needle from its groove.

He rose. He turned. And there was my brother's face, older by eleven years and younger by none, wearing an expression I had never seen on it: serenity. Aldric had never been serene in his life. Aldric had once set fire to our mother's herb garden to prove a point about the flammability of doctrine. This man looking down at me was smooth as a finished page.

"Freya," he said. "You've gotten thin. The valley does that. It starves you slowly and calls it virtue."

"You're alive." My voice came out smaller than I intended. Eleven years I had built his death into a monument and mourned at its base, and here he stood, breathing, elegant, his cuffs embroidered with sigils I recognized from the oldest grammars — the language before kingdoms, rendered in thread.

"I'm employed," he corrected gently. "There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the law here. Come. Walk with me. The court will want to receive you properly, and I would rather you heard certain things from me than from them."

He led me along the edge of the ballroom, past courtiers who inclined their heads to him with deference I might have found impressive if I hadn't noticed that none of them quite looked at him. They looked near him. Around him. As though he occupied a space slightly adjacent to being seen.

"The chief recorder," I said. "They told me on the road. The soul-marked speak of you the way sailors speak of a lighthouse that occasionally eats ships."

"They're welcome to their poetry." He smiled, and it was his smile, gods help me, the crooked one, the one that used to mean trouble in the best way. "It's honest work, Freya. That's the joke nobody appreciates. I keep their records. Every contract, every signature, every soul weighed and entered in the great book. Someone has to do it accurately, or the whole edifice collapses into mere robbery. Accuracy is the last dignity left to the sold."

"And your soul?"

"Is in the great book," he said, "under my own hand, in my own ink. Which means it is safe. Safer than yours, sister, standing here uncontracted in the one place in creation where an unbroken soul is worth more than the mountain it sits under."

* * *

He took me to his chambers — rooms of terrible tastefulness, all slate and silver, with a window that looked out onto a wall of living rock veined with that deep blue light. He poured tea. Actual tea. He remembered how I took it, which undid me more than anything else that night.

"The child," he began.

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. I've read enough contracts to know how they open. *Whereas the party of the first part.* No, Aldric. I'm not abandoning the child."

He set down his cup with a click of perfect control. "Then listen anyway, because you always were better at reading than listening, and this needs both." He leaned forward. "Do you know why they want the child? Not sentiment. Not cruelty for its own sake — cruelty here is always efficient, never decorative. The child is a fragment of a sold soul that escaped the book. A loose entry. And a loose entry is a wound in the ledger, and a wound in the ledger is a threat to every contract ever signed. Including mine. Including," he added softly, "the one that holds Mother's memory intact."

I went still. "What did you say?"

"When I sold my soul, I purchased terms. You'd have done the same, if you'd had anything to sell. I bought continuance — her memory preserved, her grave tended, her name spoken in the valley until the world ends. That clause is written in the great book, Freya. In my hand. And if the ledger falls, the clause falls with it. Everything I traded myself for comes undone." His voice cracked then, just barely, just enough to show me the boy underneath the lacquer. "So yes. I am asking you to give up the child. And I am asking you to take my place."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped into a well I couldn't see the bottom of.

"Your place."

"Chief recorder. It's not torment, it's *work*, and I am good at it, and I have made it bearable — I have softened clauses, delayed collections, misfiled cruelties in ways they've never caught. Another century of me doing that is worth more than one escaped fragment, Freya. Take the desk. Take the pen. Go home to the valley with your debt forgiven — they'll agree to it, they'll be *delighted* — and let me keep writing. Everyone lives. Isn't that what you crossed the world to arrange? Everyone lives?"

I looked at him. At the embroidery on his cuffs, the sigils of a dead language stitched by hands that no longer trembled. At the serenity that was not peace but anesthesia, the calm of a man who has solved his suffering by ceasing to feel it. He thought he was offering me mercy. He thought he was offering himself as a martyr, and the tragedy of it, the thing that made my chest hurt in a way I hadn't felt since I was nine years old watching soldiers march him away toward a debt he didn't owe, was that he believed it.

"Aldric," I said carefully, "who renews your contract?"

His teacup paused halfway to his mouth. "What?"

"Contracts. All of them. Who signs them back into being? There's always a renewal. Nothing down here lasts without maintenance — even the rock outside your window is veined with light someone feeds. So who renews yours?"

Something flickered behind his eyes. Something quick and frightened and very much alive, a fish under winter ice.

"That's not—" He set the cup down. "That's not a question you should be asking here."

"I'm a scribe," I said. "Questions are the only inheritance I have. Who renews it, Aldric?"

And he looked at me — really looked, for the first time since the ballroom — and I saw the horror arrive in him slowly, the way dawn arrives in a valley: not all at once, but inevitably, touching the highest things first.

"Freya," he whispered, "the centennial renewal is in six days."

I know. I had known since the Road of Borrowed Names, since an old woman with bird-folded hands taught me that reading is a kind of power and every power has a flaw written into it somewhere, because nothing that men or mountains make can bear close reading and survive.

Six days. A great book full of souls. A brother who had forgotten he was a prisoner because the cell was comfortable. And somewhere in these chambers, in this room where he kept his records, the master contract itself — the one document in creation I would give anything to read.

"Aldric," I said, rising, smoothing my travel-worn skirts like a woman preparing to do arithmetic, "show me the great book."

He stared at me. "Why?"

"Because," I said, "you asked me to take your place at the desk. Consider this my first day of training."

Outside the window, deep in the blue-veined rock, something ancient shifted its weight, and the light in the veins dimmed — briefly, like a held breath — as though the mountain had heard me, and was deciding whether to be afraid.
