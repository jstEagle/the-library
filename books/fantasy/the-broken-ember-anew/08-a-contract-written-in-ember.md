## 8. A Contract Written in Ember

My brother had a saying about contracts: *the ink is always hungrier than the hand.* He said it the day he signed his apprenticeship papers with a merchant caravan, grinning, fourteen years old and certain the world would make room for him. I thought of that saying now as I stood in the archive beneath the court — because Tam's ink had turned out to be very hungry indeed, and it had eaten him down to an elegant hollow of a man who poured wine for things that wore faces.

The master contract lived in a vault at the heart of the court, behind a door of black glass that showed you your own reflection wearing someone else's expression. The court had given me access to it. That was the trap, of course. They believed a scribe from a magicless valley would stand before the founding document of their entire dominion and see only words — a wall of them, dense and ancient and deliberately unreadable.

They had forgotten what I was. Or rather, they had never bothered to learn. To the court beneath the mountain, a valley scribe was a domestic animal, useful for copying, harmless as a spoon. They did not know that in the temple scriptorium we spent our lives reading documents written by people who *did not want them read* — tax ledgers padded to obscure theft, indulgences drafted in triple negation, deeds of indenture designed to be signed quickly and understood never. I had spent eleven years learning how lies arranged themselves on a page. And this — this was the largest lie I had ever seen, and it was beautiful, and it was terrified.

I found the flaw on my third night of study, somewhere past the hour when the court's false candles guttered blue and even the vault seemed to hold its breath.

Here is what the contract said, if you read it the way it begged not to be read:

Every soul sold to the court was bound in perpetuity — except that perpetuity was not a word the contract ever used. It used *term*. A term of one hundred years, renewable. And renewal, buried in a subordinate clause so deep it took me two nights and a headache like a splitting log to excavate it, required *willing assent renewed upon each centennial observance*. Not the original signature. Not the soul's passive presence. Assent. Fresh, voluntary, spoken or inscribed, every hundred years, or the bond lapsed and the soul returned whence it came.

The court had been renewing these bonds for centuries through ceremony — the great Renewal, held in the Court of Ember, where the bound were gathered and bathed in the mountain's fire until they nodded, or wept, or simply failed to say no loudly enough. The court called that assent. Perhaps it even was, in the way that a drowning man consents to the sea.

But the clause did not say *ceremony*. It said *willing*. And willingness, I knew from a lifetime of watching debt do its work, could be withdrawn. Could be *refused*. Publicly. In front of witnesses. In front of the contract itself.

And the centennial — I checked the court's own calendar three times, hands shaking, because surely I was wrong, surely the universe was not this generous — fell in six days.

Six days. The whole architecture of the court, every hollowed servant and harvested fragment and elegant monster in its glittering dark, balanced on a single word they had hidden so well they had stopped believing anyone would find it. They had grown lazy with power. Power does that. It is the one debt no creditor ever fears calling due.

I sat back on the cold floor of the archive, surrounded by my transcriptions, and laughed — actually laughed, alone, in the belly of the thing that owned my brother — until the laugh turned into something else and I pressed my sleeve against my mouth and breathed until it passed.

Then I got back to work. Because finding the flaw was half the battle, and the other half was surviving long enough to use it.

* * *

You dream again, and this time you do not run inside the dream.

You stand in the Court of Ember, which is not a court but a throat — a vast descending chamber where the walls are warm and wet-looking and the light comes from below, ember-light, the color of the thing you carry under your ribs. You know this place. You were made here. You were *poured* here, a measure of someone taken from the fire and set aside, and the taking left a shape in the air that still remembers being full.

In the dream, the throne is occupied. It is always occupied. The one who sits there has your face the way a mask has a face — close enough to wound. It speaks without moving its mouth, and its voice is the sound a contract makes when it is being read aloud by someone who loves it.

*The little ember comes home,* it says. *We felt you reading our book, scribe's shadow. We felt her fingers on our clauses.*

You wake with Freya's name in your mouth — she taught you her name, syllable by patient syllable, the way she teaches everything, as though names are doors worth unlocking gently — and you find her asleep over her papers, ink on her cheekbone, her breathing too fast for true sleep. She wakes when you touch her arm. She always wakes all at once, like a snare springing.

"You dreamed about the court," she says. Not a question. She has learned to read you almost as well as you read fires.

You show her. You have learned to show her — you press the dream against the inside of your eyelids and she leans close and *looks*, because she is strange for a grown person, strange and good, and she believes you about things.

When it is done she is quiet for a long time. Then she says, "It knows I've been reading."

You nod. You felt it too. The mountain's attention, turning toward the small room where the two of you hide among borrowed names, the way a flame turns toward a draft.

"Good," Freya says, and her smile is terrible and wonderful, the smile of a woman holding a lit match in a paper house. "Let it watch. Let it wonder what I found." She pulls you against her side, and her heart is hammering, and hers is not the only one. "Six days, little ember. Six days, and then we stop hiding."

* * *

The court's response came faster than I expected, and subtler than I feared — at first.

On the fourth morning, the servants began bringing extra portions to our table. Sweetmeats. Wine older than kingdoms. On the fifth, an invitation arrived, written in gold leaf on vellum soft as skin: the Lord Recorder — my brother, wearing his court face — requested the pleasure of the valley scribe's company at a private viewing of the Renewal preparations. *A courtesy,* it said, *to one who appreciates the dignity of old forms.*

I declined. The messenger's borrowed face smiled a fraction too long before bowing out.

By evening, the corridors had changed. Not locked — the court never locks anything; locking implies something worth stealing. But the routes between our rooms and the archive grew longer, more winding, past guards who stood a little too attentively, past doors that hummed faintly when I touched them, tasting me. And the whispers started. I caught fragments in the pre-kingdom tongue — the child's tongue, which I now knew well enough to be wounded by — words like *scribe* and *clause* and *unmaking*. They were not whispering about whether I had found something. They were whispering about what to do with me once they decided what I'd found.

That night I copied the flaw out one final time in cipher of my own devising — temple shorthand layered over ledger notation, gibberish to any courtly eye — and sewed the pages into the lining of my coat, against my heart, where a debt collector once told me all valuable things are carried.

Then I went to find the child, and found instead the old woman from the caravan waiting outside our door, leaning on her stick, her soul-mark glowing faintly through her shawl like a coal wrapped in wool.

"They've moved the Renewal," she said without preamble. "Was to be held at century's turn, proper and public, all the bound assembled. Now it's tomorrow night. Closed session. No witnesses but the court itself." Her eyes, pale as frost on a window, held mine. "Whatever you found in that book, girl, they mean to renew it before you can speak it. A hundred years of assent, collected in one quiet evening, behind closed doors."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Can they do that? Change the date?"

"The court can do anything," she said, "except survive without consent. That's the joke of it, dear. All that power, and they still need us to say yes." She pushed off the wall and shuffled toward the darkening corridor, then paused. "Tomorrow night, then. You'll want to be early. Doors open for those who look like they belong."

"And if I don't belong?"

She smiled with most of her teeth. "Then you'll want to look like something worse."

I stood in the corridor a long moment after she'd gone, listening to the mountain breathe around me — that vast, slow, satisfied respiration of a thing that believed it had already won. Terror sat in my chest like swallowed ice. But underneath it, coiling and bright, something else was waking. Eleven years I had been told my skill was small. Ink and silence. Copying other people's words in a valley where nothing magical could follow.

But words were the only magic the court had ever truly possessed. And I had just read theirs to the bone.

Tomorrow night, in the Court of Ember, before the throne and the throat and the thing that wore my brother's face, I would stand up with their own founding document in my hands and read the clause they had hidden for a hundred years — aloud, slowly, in front of every soul they had bought.

Unless they found my coat first.

Somewhere far above, through leagues of stone, the mountain shifted its weight, and the candle flames all leaned toward me at once, like an audience settling in.
