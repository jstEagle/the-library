## 9. The Reading

They dressed me for the ceremony in silver, because the court believed everything could be improved by silver, and I let them, because a scribe knows that the costume of an occasion is just another kind of punctuation. You dress the sentence before you deliver it. You make them lean in.

The chamber they called the Amphitheater of Renewal was carved from the mountain's living bone, its walls veined with light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. Tier upon tier of seats rose into darkness, and every seat was filling. The soul-marked who had traveled with us on the road were there — the old woman, Maren, whose name was not Maren; the twins who had sold their laughter in separate transactions and could no longer laugh at the same joke; the ferryman with his purchased courage. They had come down the mountain behind me, drawn by whispers, by hope, by the particular gravity of a rumor that said: *someone is going to read it aloud.*

Below us, arranged on a dais of black glass, sat the court. I will not describe their faces. Borrowed faces are like borrowed pens — you can always tell the hand isn't the owner's.

And beside the dais, at a lectern of pale stone, stood my brother.

Aldous. He wore robes of deep green now, and his hair was combed, and his posture was perfect, and when our eyes met across that vast humming dark he looked at me the way a man looks at weather he has already decided to endure.

In my sleeve, folded small as a moth, I carried the copy I had made. Three nights of work in the guest cell they'd given me — generously, arrogantly, certain that a scribe with no magic in her blood could do nothing but admire the architecture of her own cage. The master contract itself lay on the dais, open, enormous, its pages the size of doors, its ink still wet after a hundred years. Wet ink. That detail had kept me awake more than any fear. A contract renewed is a contract rewritten, and what is rewritten must be *read* to be binding. Their own law. Their own grammar. I had merely done what any scribe would do.

I had read it.

All of it. Every clause, every subclause, every elegant little cruelty buried in the footnotes like thorns in wool.

"Freya Thackeray." The voice came from the center of the dais, from the one they called the Understeward, whose face had once belonged to someone kind. "You come uninvited to the centennial renewal. Speak your purpose, or be named and claimed."

Named and claimed. In this place, a name was a handle, and a handle was for pulling.

I walked down the steps into the bowl of the Amphitheater, silver dress whispering, and I felt every eye — the court's cold attention, the soul-marked's desperate hope, Aldous's careful blankness — settle on me like snow.

"My purpose," I said, and my voice carried better than I expected, "is reading."

A ripple went through the tiers. Somewhere above, Maren laughed her bought-back half-laugh.

"The contract," I continued, "requires willing renewal every hundred years. That renewal is three days away. But a renewal —" I turned to face the tiers, addressing the soul-marked now, the sold, the fragmented, the people who had traded pieces of themselves to survive winters and famines and griefs — "a renewal must be *consented to*. And consent, under the court's own founding law, requires full disclosure of terms." I drew the folded pages from my sleeve. "Has anyone here ever been fully disclosed to?"

"Objection," said the Understeward, silk over iron. "The contract is sealed law. It cannot be read by the unbound."

"It's being read by a scribe," I said. "Reading is my trade. It is the only magic my valley ever taught me, and you should have checked which valley I came from before you sent collectors wearing borrowed faces into it." I unfolded the first page. My hands were steady. I had copied ledgers for eleven years; I could hold a line straight through anything. "Clause One. *The seller surrenders the named portion of soul in perpetuity, renewable each century by silent assent.* Silent assent. Do you understand what that means?" I looked up at the tiers. "It means that if you do nothing — if you are too tired, too broken, too far away to object — your silence counts as yes. Every hundred years, they take it again. Forever. For nothing new. You were paid once, and they intend to collect until the sun burns out."

The Amphitheater had gone very quiet. Even the walls' slow pulse seemed to lean closer.

"Clause Nine," I read on. "*Portions may be combined, divided, or invested at the holder's discretion.* Invested. Your grief, your laughter, your courage — they lend your souls back out at interest. Some of you have been paying interest on your own hearts." I heard someone weep in the upper tiers. I did not stop. "Clause Twenty-Two. *Fragments escaping custody shall be reclaimed by any means, cost immaterial.* Cost immaterial. That clause is why a child was hunted across two kingdoms. That clause is why my brother —"

My voice caught. Just once. A scribe's flaw: the personal marginalia.

"That clause is why my brother has stood at that lectern for thirty years writing down other people's surrender."

Aldous closed his eyes.

"Enough." The Understeward rose, and the light in the walls flared red. "You trespass on law older than your alphabet, scribe. Take her. Name her. Claim her."

Guards moved — things in shapes almost human — and I did the only thing left in my inventory. I held up the pages and I raised my voice to its ledger-room pitch, the one that cuts through a room full of arguing clerks:

"Article Final! The article none of you have ever heard, because it's on the last page and no one reads the last page!" The guards hesitated — hesitation, in a court of law, is a crack. "*Should the terms be publicly read and found cruel by free consensus, the contract may be voided against consideration equivalent.* Equivalent consideration. Do you know what the court considers equivalent to every soul it holds?" I turned to the dais. To the thing wearing a kind face. "One soul, freely given, never sold, never split, never touched by your ink. Whole. Unbroken. Mine."

Silence. The terrible, ringing silence of a bargain landing.

"You would give yourself," the Understeward said slowly, "for all of them."

"I would *offer* myself," I corrected. "This is a negotiation, not a sacrifice. There's a difference, and the difference is written down. I want the contract voided. All of it. Every fragment returned or released. The child walks free. And my brother's signature comes off your rolls forever."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then I read the rest aloud," I said. "There are forty-one clauses. I've only done three. And every soul-marked person in these tiers is listening, and free consensus, remember, is *their* verdict, not yours. You wrote the escape hatch yourselves a hundred years ago, because you never imagined anyone would climb through it. Arrogance is also a kind of ink. It stains."

The Understeward looked at the tiers. At the hundreds of faces that had come down the mountain on a rumor. At the twins holding hands. At Maren, standing now, her chin lifted. The court had ruled by silence for a century. Now the silence belonged to everyone else.

And then Aldous moved.

He stepped away from the lectern, and the green robes suddenly looked heavy on him, like something he'd been carrying rather than wearing. He crossed the black glass floor to where I stood, and up close I could see what thirty years of serving them had done: the hollows, the shine, the way his eyes moved like a man reading a room for exits that weren't there.

"Freya," he said quietly. "You don't know what they'll do to a whole soul down here. What it's worth. What they'll *make* of it."

"I know what they made of yours," I whispered. "I'm canceling the order."

He laughed — a cracked, rusty sound, the first true thing I'd heard from him in thirty years — and then he did something no one in that Amphitheater, mortal or otherwise, expected. He took the pen from behind his own ear. His recorder's pen. The instrument of every surrender he'd ever documented.

And he signed.

His name, beneath mine, on my terms — not the court's. The moment the ink dried, the walls screamed. Light poured out of the veins in the stone like blood from a struck vein, and the great contract on the dais shuddered, its wet century-old ink beginning to boil.

"Voided against consideration," Aldous said, loud enough for the whole tiered dark to hear, "witnessed by two signatures freely given. Let the record show." He looked at me, and his hollow eyes were, for one impossible moment, full. "Let the record finally show something true."

The Understeward's borrowed face began to slip.

And beneath our feet, the mountain began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

They say — well. No one says anything yet. It is happening now, as I write this by ember-light, the child asleep against my shoulder, the pages of the master contract dissolving one clause at a time into drifting gold. The court is not ended; courts like that are never simply ended. The Understeward has withdrawn to negotiate, which is how predators say *please*. And the consideration they claim — my soul, freely given — has not yet been collected.

There is time. There is always time between the signing and the taking. That gap is where scribes live.

But the last page of the contract is burning differently than the others. Slower. And in the ash, I can see letters forming that were never in the original text — old letters, pre-kingdom letters, the tongue the child speaks.

The contract, it seems, has a postscript.

And it is addressed to me.
