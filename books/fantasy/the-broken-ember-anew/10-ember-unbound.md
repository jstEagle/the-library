## 10. Ember, Unbound

You do not remember being born the first time. You remember this.

The reading ends — Freya's voice still hanging in the dark like a struck bell that refuses to finish its note — and then the contract shatters.

Not breaks. Shatters, the way ice shatters on a warm pond: all at once, everywhere, with a sound you feel in your teeth rather than hear. The great scroll beneath the mountain, the one older than kingdoms, older than the valley, older than the word *older* — it comes apart into a thousand thousand slivers of light, and every sliver is a name.

You know because you can read them now. You could never read them before; names were the thing the court took from you first, before your memories, before your shape, before they pressed you small and burning into a child's body and threw you out a door to be hunted. But now the light is falling upward through the cavern like snow in reverse, and each flake has a name written in it, and you are standing in the middle of a blizzard of everyone who was ever sold.

Some of them go home. You feel them leave — a tug behind your ribs, one after another, like doors opening down a long corridor. Somewhere a farmer wakes with his courage returned to him. Somewhere a queen remembers why she wept at her daughter's wedding. Somewhere a boy gets back the laugh he pawned for bread in a famine winter, and laughs, and doesn't know why, and doesn't need to.

And some of them dissolve. That's the other thing nobody tells you about souls: some of them have been gone so long there is no home left to return to. Those ones don't fall or fly. They just brighten, and brighten, and become the kind of light that isn't going anywhere anymore, and then they aren't slivers at all but warmth, spreading through the stone, through the air, through *you*.

You are not afraid of those. You were almost one of those. You understand them the way you understand your own hands.

Freya is beside you. She is holding your hand so hard it hurts, and she is crying without any of the noise humans make when they want to be comforted, which you have learned is the honest kind of crying. Her brother is in front of her.

He came apart more slowly than the others. When the contract broke, something in him unclenched — you saw it, the way a fist opens finger by finger — and for a handful of heartbeats he was *there*, all of him, the boy who taught a little sister her letters, the man who sold himself so she would eat, the hollow courtier who had forgotten he was ever either of them. He looked at Freya and his mouth shaped a word in the language before kingdoms.

You translated it for her, because it was yours to translate. It was her childhood name. The one no ledger records.

Then he smiled — really smiled, the whole face, the whole soul, what was left of it — and went up with the rest of the snow, and did not come down.

Freya's hand in yours does not let go for a very long time.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain does not end so much as empty. Without the contract, the courtiers are only people — pale, strange, glittering people who spent centuries being magnificent because magnificence was the collateral on their own bargains, and who now stand in their jewel-lit halls looking, for the first time, mortal. Some of them weep. Some of them rage. One of them, an old lord with a crown of fused glass, kneels in the middle of the floor and begins picking up shards of dead light as if he could reassemble them, and perhaps he will pick them up forever, and perhaps that is its own kind of payment.

Nobody stops you when you walk out. There is nothing left to stop anyone *for*.

The road home is longer than the road there, the way roads always are. Freya's body fights the magical air less than it did — or maybe she has simply stopped fighting it, stopped flinching from every whisper of power the way she was raised to flinch. In the caravan towns, the soul-marked look at her differently now. They heard. Word travels faster than horses when ten thousand souls change address in a single night. An old woman with a moth-eaten shawl presses Freya's hand and says, "Reader," the way other people say *your grace*, and Freya goes red to her ears and mutters that she only ever read aloud, anyone could have done it, and the old woman laughs and says that is exactly what makes it a miracle instead of a trick.

You walk beside her. Sometimes you ride on her shoulders when your legs get tired, because you are still small, whatever else you are now. At night you teach her words. She teaches you letters. Between you, you are slowly building a bridge out of grammar.

The valley takes you back the way valleys take everything: without comment. The wards hum once as you cross the hollow line — recognition, not warning — and go quiet again, and the mud is mud, and the rain is rain, and magic pools against the border like a tide against a seawall and cannot follow you in.

You are glad. You have decided you are a valley creature. You like a world where things stay where you put them.

The temple elders meet Freya at the scriptorium door. They look at her — at the debt-bond scar on her wrist, which faded somewhere on the road home, the ink of it simply lifting out of her skin like dye from cloth in too much water — and they look at you, and their faces do a complicated arithmetic.

"The bond is void," the eldest says finally. "The debt was held by... parties who no longer hold anything."

"Then I'm free," Freya says.

"You are free."

"Good." She looks at the door she spent seven years copying ledgers behind. "I quit."

The eldest sputters something about duty, and Freya says — and you will keep this sentence your whole life, you will say it to your own children someday if children are a thing valley creatures have — "Duty isn't a cage they put you in. It's a fire you carry. Mine just changed shape." Then she walks past all of them, with you on her shoulders, and takes the small cottage at the valley's edge with the crooked chimney, because apparently saving the world pays out in real estate.

* * *

Now it is me telling you this part, and I will tell it plainly, because the child would only make it strange and lovely, and some things should be plain.

Winter came. The child sleeps in the loft above my desk, in a nest of blankets they arranged themselves with an exactness that suggests the court taught them *something* useful. By day they learn their letters and terrorize the neighbor's geese and speak the language before kingdoms to the sheep, who appear to understand it better than I do.

By day, I write.

Not ledgers. Never again ledgers. I am writing a book — the book, the one I have been circling my whole life without knowing it. A grammar of the tongue that existed before kingdoms, yes, but also a record: of the caravan of the soul-marked, of the road of borrowed names, of the court and its glass-crowned lords, of a brother who signed himself away twice, the second time freely, the second time *for* me. The elders call it dangerous. The elders called the child dangerous too, and now the child eats dinner at their table twice a week, so I have learned to weigh their opinions accordingly.

My hand has changed since the mountain. The ink behaves oddly around me now — leans toward the page before I dip the pen, as if eager. Magic still cannot enter the valley, but it seems I brought a little out with me, tucked in the lining of my coat like contraband, and it lives in my fingertips and makes my letters truer than they used to be. Every word I write in the old tongue glows faintly if you hold it near a flame. I try not to think about what that means. I fail most days.

Yesterday the child climbed down from the loft at dawn and stood over my desk, watching the pages dry, and said — in the valley tongue, carefully, having learned it the hard way, word by stubborn word:

"When it is finished. What happens?"

I didn't pretend to misunderstand. We don't do that to each other anymore.

"Then someone reads it," I said. "Somewhere far from here. And maybe they learn the words. And maybe, a hundred years from now, when some new court rises in some new mountain and starts buying souls again — because they will, the hungry never stay fed — someone will already know how to read the contract. Will already know that reading is a kind of power."

The child considered this with the gravity they bring to everything, geese included.

"And you will be dead," they said.

"Almost certainly."

"Good," they said, in the tone of a scholar confirming a citation, and went down to torment the geese.

So: the work continues. My brother's name is on the first page, in the language before kingdoms, glowing softly whenever I light the lamp. The child's true name — the one the court stole, the one the shattered contract gave back — is on the last page, though the book has no last page yet, and I begin to suspect it never will.

This morning there was frost on the window, and in the frost, drawn by a small determined finger, a sentence in the old tongue. I sat down and translated it, and my eyes did the dishonest kind of crying, and then I dipped my pen.

It said: *We stayed. We are the ember that did not go out.*

I wrote it down. Of course I wrote it down.

That is what I do now.
