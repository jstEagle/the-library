## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The bone-vault smelled of tallow and old winters, and Ansel Okafor loved it the way other people loved their mothers.

They sat at the workbench nearest the far wall, where the lamplight pooled amber and the rest of the vault fell away into shadow—shelves upon shelves of catalogued dead, each wrapped in linen, each tagged in the guild's careful hand. *Acquisition date. Provenance. Asking price.* The Guild of Impossible Things sold everything: bottled storms, maps of places that did not exist, love spelled into rings. And bones. Always bones. The guild had been trading in bones since before anyone now living could remember why.

Ansel's commission lay across two folded cloths: a femur, honey-pale, belonging to a woman named Marta Voss who had died eleven years ago of a fever that started in her lungs. Her daughter wanted a keepsake carved—a sparrow, she'd said, because Mother always said sparrows were souls too impatient to stay put. Ansel had been shaping the wing for three nights now, and for three nights Marta Voss had been talking to them.

She wasn't a talkative ghost. Most weren't, once the flesh was gone; what remained was less a voice than a weather, a pressure against the inside of Ansel's skull. But Marta murmured. She murmured about her garden, about the particular way her daughter laughed through her nose, about a debt of four silver owed to a man named Corvin that nobody else knew of. Ansel had learned long ago to let it wash over them like rain on a roof—to hear without listening, the way you might live beside a river.

It was a gift they had never told the guildmasters about. It was the reason they'd taken the ledger-keeper's post at all: quiet work, invisible work, sorting acquisitions no one else wanted to think about. In thirty-one years of life, invisibility had kept Ansel Okafor safe, fed, and unremarkable, and they intended to die exactly as they had lived—unnoticed, unmolested, and moderately well paid.

The knife whispered along the wing-bone. Marta murmured something about frost coming early this year.

Then she stopped.

Not faded. Not drifted off, the way the dead did when their attention thinned. She *stopped*—mid-murmur, mid-thought, like a door slammed on a word—and the silence she left behind was so total that Ansel's own heartbeat sounded obscene.

"Marta?" they said softly, out of habit, though no one was there to see them speak to bone.

Nothing. And then Ansel felt it—the whole vault felt it—a vibration rising up through the floor, through the bench, through the teeth. Every shelf of the dead, hundreds of them, thousands, all at once began to *hum*.

Ansel stood so fast the stool went over behind them. The hum climbed, became a keening, became a sound with edges. Down the rows of shelved remains, the linen wrappings shivered though there was no draft. The lamp flame bent sideways. And above it all, faint but unmistakable, came a scream—not from any single bone but from *all* of them, a chorus of the dead crying out in one voice—

—and then, silence again. Deeper than before. A silence like held breath.

Ansel ran for the vault's high slit window, the one that looked north over the rooftops toward the border hills.

The stars were going out.

Not clouds. Not fog. They were going out *in order*, west to east, like lamps being snuffed by a hand moving down a corridor—one, then another, then a whole constellation drowning at once, until the sky over Vellmark was a black so complete it looked like a hole cut in the world. Ansel pressed their forehead to the cold stone and watched the last star die above the northern passes, where the border wards stood in their ancient ring of standing stones.

The wards. The wards that had held the wild things out for six hundred years. The wards every child learned were anchored to the land itself, to bedrock and deep water and the old kings' blessing.

Ansel knew better.

Ansel had known better for four years, ever since the night they'd stayed late cataloguing the back-collections and found an entry that made their hands go cold: *Lot 114. Royal femur, provenance disputed. Sold to private buyer, spring of the Warding Renewal, 40 years prior.* Disputed. As if a king's thigh bone could be *disputed*, the way a silver spoon might be. As if the guild hadn't known precisely whose leg it was carving a profit from.

They had told no one. They had closed the ledger, and their own mouth, and gone on being invisible. Because the alternative—the truth that the realm's great protection had been anchored not to bedrock but to a murdered king's skeleton, and that the guild had *sold pieces of it*—was a truth that would burn the Guild of Impossible Things down to its foundations. And the guild was the only home Ansel had ever had.

Now the sky was empty, and somewhere beyond the hills, whatever the wards had held back for six centuries was no longer being held back at all.

Footsteps thundered in the corridor above. Voices—many voices, sharp with fear poorly disguised as authority. Then the great bronze bell in the guild's atrium began to toll, over and over, the signal no one had rung in living memory: *seal the doors.*

Ansel didn't move. They stood in the dark vault among the humming dead, and the dread that settled over them was not the clean fear of danger but the sick, slow horror of a life cracking open like ice underfoot. Everything they had built—the quiet ledgers, the harmless days, the careful nothing they made of themselves—all of it balanced on the guild's crime staying buried. And the crime had just torn the sky open.

Boots on the stairs. Iron keys. The vault door boomed shut and the lock turned, and a voice called through the bronze grille, Master Halloway's voice, pitched flat with effort:

"Vault is sealed. No one enters, no one leaves. Whatever you heard tonight—you heard nothing. The guild's position is silence. Am I understood?"

Silence. Of course. Silence was the guild's oldest product.

Ansel looked at the half-carved sparrow on the bench. Marta Voss's bone had gone utterly still, utterly mute, and somehow that frightened them more than the screaming had. The dead knew something. The dead had all stopped talking at once, the way people stop talking when someone terrible walks into a room.

"Yes, Master," Ansel called back, voice steady, invisible. "I heard nothing."

The footsteps receded. The lock did not turn again.

Ansel stood alone in the sealed vault for a long time, breathing the tallow-and-winter smell of the only home they had, listening to the dead say nothing. Then, slowly, their eyes went to the back wall—to the oldest shelves, the back-catalogue, the lots nobody asked about anymore.

Somewhere in those shelves was Lot 114's twin. Somewhere was more of him. And if the wards had screamed and died, then whatever remained of the king those bones belonged to might be the only witness left who could say what had actually happened forty years ago—and what was coming now, starless, across the hills.

Ansel picked up their knife, put it away, and picked up a lamp instead.

"I'm sorry, Marta," they murmured to the unfinished sparrow. "Your wing will have to wait."

They crossed the vault toward the back-catalogue, and behind them, in the dark, every bone in the guild's keeping began—very softly—to whisper.

* * *

The whisper had a direction.

That was the first impossible thing. The dead murmured, they droned, they wept in place—but they did not *point*. Yet as Ansel moved down the back-catalogue rows with the lamp held high, the whispering swelled and faded like a tide, swelling when they turned north, fading when they turned south, until they stood before a shelf near the ceiling marked in faded ink: *ROYAL ACQUISITIONS — DO NOT DISPLAY.*

Ansel fetched the rolling ladder and climbed, heart hammering against their ribs like a fist on a door. The lot tags up here were older, the handwriting different—sharper, hungrier. Lots 110 through 118. A rib. Two vertebrae. A hand, wrapped separately, its tag stained with something brown. And there, third from the end:

*Lot 114. Royal femur. Provenance disputed. SOLD — private buyer, spring renewal, forty years past.*

Sold. Empty slot. Ansel stared at the gap where it had lain, and the whispering around them rose to a hiss, dozens of voices tangling, and beneath the tangle one voice began to separate itself from the rest—deep, weary, unmistakably *royal*, unmistakably furious, unmistakably afraid.

*"They took my leg,"* the voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, from the ribs and vertebrae and the stained hand. *"And then they wonder why the sky went dark."*

Ansel gripped the ladder and did not look down, because looking down meant admitting they were speaking with the dead in a sealed vault while the guildmasters' boots paced overhead.

"Who are you?" they breathed.

The whisper gathered itself, and Ansel felt it settle against their mind like a crown settling on a brow—heavy, cold, inevitable.

*"Find the rest of me, little carver,"* said the ghost of King Aldric. *"Before the thing wearing my crown finishes wearing my kingdom."*

Below, at the vault door, the lock began to turn.
