## 2. What the Dead King Remembers

The whisper started three days after the stars went out, and it came from the back-catalogue.

Ansel heard it first as a thread of sound beneath the guild's morning noise—the scrape of chisels, the groan of the dumbwaiter, Master Corvane's voice drilling the apprentices on proper lacquer technique. But this was not living sound. It came from the vault's deepest shelf, where bones waited in their numbered boxes for commissions that might never come, and it said, over and over, in a voice worn smooth as river stone:

*They sold me. They sold me and they did not write it down.*

Ansel's hands went still over the commission piece—a child's memorial plaque, its little carved wren half-finished. Every bone in the vault spoke if you listened long enough. Most murmured. The recently dead grieved; the old dead dreamed. But this voice had *direction*, and it had *purpose*, and it had been waiting for someone like Ansel to hear it since before Ansel was born.

They should have ignored it. That was the first rule of the bone-vault, unwritten but absolute: *carve what is asked, hear what must be heard, speak nothing.* Ansel had kept that rule for eleven years. It was the rule that kept them invisible, kept them safe, kept the small warm life they'd built among the ledgers intact.

Ansel set down the chisel, told the duty clerk they were checking inventory discrepancies, and went into the dark of the back-catalogue with a lamp and a lie.

* * *

The box was marked 114-K, accessioned forty-one years ago, contents listed as *bovine femur, ceremonial, provenance unrecorded*. Ansel knew before lifting the lid that the entry was false. The ledger-keepers' oldest trick: when something could not be recorded, record it as nothing at all. A blank was a confession to anyone who knew how to read one.

Inside lay a human femur, longer than any Ansel had ever handled, dense and yellow-white with age, and carved—so finely the work was nearly invisible—from crown to condyle with a king's life. Coronation. Harvests. A war won without a battle. And then, near the head of the bone, the carving simply *stopped*, mid-line, the way a sentence stops when the speaker's throat is cut.

Ansel held it in both hands, and the ghost rose out of it like heat off summer stone.

He was tall, the way dead kings are tall—all the pettiness burned away, leaving only the shape of what he'd been. Grey-bearded, grey-eyed, wearing a crown that flickered at the edges as though it couldn't decide whether it still existed. He looked at Ansel with an expression no ghost should manage: not rage, not sorrow, but *recognition*, sharp and terrible.

"You hear," he said. "After forty years, finally, someone who hears."

"I'm not supposed to be listening." Ansel's voice came out smaller than intended. "I shouldn't be here."

"No," Aldric agreed. "You shouldn't. And yet." He gestured with a hand that was mostly suggestion, and the vault around them seemed to thin, the shelves going grey and distant. "Look at me properly, carver. Look at what your guild did."

"You're King Aldric." Ansel didn't need the confirmation. The regalia was unmistakable, and so was the grief rolling off him in waves. "You died forty years ago. The histories say fever."

"The histories were written by the men who smothered me with a pillow while I slept, and then carved my death out of my own bones so I could never finish dying." His flickering crown guttered. "Do you understand what an unfinished death is, bone-carver? A story with no ending eats whatever comes near it. They took my ending and buried it in a box, and the thing that paid them for the silence—it wore my brother's face for a while, and then his son's, and now it wears the boy on the throne, and every year it grows fatter on a kingdom that cannot close its eyes."

"The wards." Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "The border wards went silent the night the stars did. Everyone says it was sabotage from outside."

"The wards were anchored to *me*." Aldric's voice cracked like ice on a winter pond. "My bones were the kingdom's quiet promise: a king who died whole keeps the dark outside. But I never died whole. I've been leaking for forty years, and the thing that murdered me has been drinking, and now—" he spread his hands, "—now the cup is empty and the dark has noticed."

Ansel thought of the night the stars went out. Of the guildmasters sealing the doors, ordering silence, Master Corvane's face gone the color of tallow. *They knew,* Ansel realized. Not the details—maybe not even the shape of it—but they knew the guild had sold something it should never have owned, and they had spent three days hoping no one would ask what.

"They don't know you can hear," Aldric said quietly. "But they will. Someone will notice which shelf you stood at. Someone always notices."

* * *

Someone noticed within the hour.

Ansel made it as far as the ledger room, the femur wrapped in a recording-cloth beneath their coat, before the enforcers came through the door—Vess and Halloway, the guild's quiet instruments, the two who handled debts the guildmasters didn't want spoken aloud. Vess carried a suppression rod; Halloway carried rope and a smile that had never once reached his eyes.

"Inventory discrepancy," Vess said. It wasn't a question. "Box 114-K. You signed out the key, Okafor."

"I was re-shelving."

"Re-shelving." Halloway's smile widened by a degree. "With the bone in your coat? We can feel it humming from the doorway. Whatever's in there, it's singing loud enough to wake the whole catalogue."

Ansel backed toward the window. Eleven years of invisibility, of careful, unremarkable competence, of being the quiet keeper nobody bothered to remember—and it was ending in a ledger room at midday because a dead king wouldn't stop telling the truth. "The wards fell because of this bone. Because of what the guild did with it. Don't you want to know—"

"We know enough." Vess's knuckles whitened on the rod. "We know that if the founding families' names get attached to a regicide, there is no Guild of Impossible Things anymore. There are gallows, and there are ash heaps, and everything we built burns with us. The guild survives because this stays buried. You survive because you help keep it buried." She raised the rod. "Last kindness I'll offer."

And that was the moment Ansel understood the true shape of the trap—not the enforcers, not the threat, but the terrible arithmetic underneath. Everything Ansel loved lived inside these walls. Sefa's laugh across the breakfast table. Old Marta teaching cipher in the evenings. The apprentices' chalk-dusted arguments. All of it purchased with a dead king's silenced tongue, and all of it forfeit the moment the truth walked out the door.

Loyalty had never felt like a knife before. It did now.

The window exploded outward—glass, frame, and a body in a ledger-keeper's coat—and Sefa landed in the courtyard below, rolled, and came up shouting, "ANSEL! *Now!*"

Later, Ansel would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. There was a thrown inkwell, Halloway's curse, the suppression rod's white crackle grazing Ansel's shoulder and taking the feeling from their left arm. There was Sefa's hand hauling them through the broken window, the two of them running through the spice-market with enforcers behind and the bone against Ansel's chest beating like a second heart.

They didn't stop until the drainage tunnels under Tanner's Row, where Sefa pressed her back to the wet brick, breathing hard, a red line already soaking through her sleeve where Vess's blade had found her.

"You knew," she said. Not accusatory. Worse—*understanding*. "When the stars went out, you knew something. You've known for days. So you're going to tell me everything, Ansel, starting with why the guild just tried to bury us both."

Ansel unwrapped the femur. In the tunnel's darkness, the carved lines caught what little light there was and seemed to move, the unfinished story still reaching for its ending.

"Because forty years ago," Ansel said, "the people who built our home helped murder a king. And the thing wearing his crown is coming to collect."

Above them, through grates and stone and forty feet of earth, the city bells began to ring—not the hour, not an alarm, but the coronation renewal peal, early, unscheduled, wrong.

The king was summoning his realm. And somewhere in the palace, something ancient was smiling with his mouth.
