## 3. The Crown That Wears the Man

The palace had never been built for a starless night.

Ansel felt it the moment they passed through the servants' gate in Sefa's wake—a wrongness in the stones, like a house holding its breath. Torches burned in every bracket, hundreds of them, and still the dark pressed down between the flames as if the light were something borrowed that would soon be called back. Above the central tower, where the Star of Vess should have burned on coronation nights, there was only a hole in the sky.

"Stop fidgeting," Sefa murmured, not turning around. She wore a scullery livery two sizes too big, her ledger-keeper's ink-stained fingers hidden in a washerwoman's apron. "You look like a thief."

"I am a thief."

"You're a *carver* with a stolen bone in your coat, which is worse." She glanced back, and even in the torchlight Ansel could see the fear she was trying to carve out of her face. "We get to the crypt gallery, you listen to whatever the dead king has to say, and we're out before the renewal rites begin. Agreed?"

Ansel's hand pressed against their chest, where the royal femur lay wrapped in oilcloth against their ribs. It had been whispering all day—not words anymore, but *urgency*, a tide pulling toward the palace. Toward the thing that wore Aldric's crown.

"Agreed," they said, and lied only a little.

* * *

They moved through the palace like water finding cracks. Sefa knew the service corridors from years of auditing the crown's accounts—she could recite which steward skimmed from which pantry—and she threaded them past guards who stared at nothing, past servants who walked too quickly and spoke too little. The whole palace had the quality of a held breath.

It was near the Great Hall that Ansel first heard him.

Not Aldric. The living king.

They had stopped at a junction where a corridor opened onto the hall's eastern gallery, and below, through a screen of carved stone, Ansel saw the throne dais being prepared for the renewal: priests in ash-grey robes, censers swinging, and at the center of it all King Edvard—what had been King Edvard—seated while attendants adjusted the black iron circlet on his brow.

Ansel heard dead people through carved bone. They always had. But this—

This was a living man screaming inside his own body.

It came to them not through any carving but through the femur against their chest, which had gone hot as a coal: Edvard's voice, thin and ragged, echoing up from somewhere deep beneath his own skin. *Help me. Please. I can hear myself saying things. I can hear my own mouth and it isn't mine.* A pause, and then the scream again, wordless now, the sound of a man beating his fists against the inside of a locked door.

"Ansel." Sefa's hand closed hard on their wrist. "You've gone white. What is it?"

"He's awake in there." Their voice came out cracked. "The king. He's been awake the whole time. He's been *screaming* the whole time, and no one—" Ansel's throat closed. Forty years of ledgers. Forty years of quiet columns and discreet silences, and beneath all of it a man had been screaming in a room no one thought to check.

Below, the attendants stepped back. The crowned figure rose.

And spoke.

It was Edvard's mouth, Edvard's face, but the voice that came out was layered, resonant, a chord of voices braided into one—the way a bone sounds when ten dead people are trying to speak through it at once. *"Let the renewal begin,"* it said, and the priests bowed, and none of them flinched, and Ansel understood with sick clarity that everyone in that hall had already decided not to hear what they were hearing.

"The wards didn't just fall," Ansel whispered. "They were *eaten*. And now it's renewing itself. Coronation by coronation. It's been doing this for forty years, Sefa. Every renewal rite, it takes a little more of him."

Sefa's jaw tightened. "Then we have until the rite ends. Crypt gallery. Now. Move."

* * *

The crypt gallery ran beneath the Great Hall like a spine—a corridor of royal tombs, each sealed behind iron grilles, each marked with a plaque bearing dates of reign. Ansel had heard the dead murmuring here even through three floors of stone; now, walking among them, the murmur became a chorus, dozens of monarchs muttering in their marble beds about taxes and betrayals and the cold.

Aldric's tomb was at the far end, older than its neighbors, its grille rusted, its plaque defaced decades ago. Someone had chiseled out the death-date and left only the birth: *ALDRIC, SON OF MAREN.*

"He has no ending," Ansel said softly. "That's why the ghost can't rest. That's why the thing can feed. A story without an end just... keeps going. Keeps *being eaten*."

Behind the grille, in the dark, bones lay arranged on a stone bier. Full skeleton. Whole. Waiting.

Ansel reached for the lock—and the temperature dropped.

"You know," said a voice behind them, mild as a guildmaster discussing quarterly accounts, "I told them you'd come here. Veyra wanted to simply burn the vault and be done with it. I said no. I said the Okafor child would lead us straight to the last loose thread."

Three enforcers stood in the gallery's entrance. Ansel knew the speaker—Corvane, senior enforcer, the man who had signed off on half the guild's discreet disappearances. His hand rested on a short iron rod that hummed with ward-light.

"Corvane." Sefa stepped sideways, putting herself between him and Ansel. "You don't want to do this in a royal crypt. The priests will notice."

"The priests are busy." He smiled without warmth. "The guild sold those bones thirty-eight years ago, ledger-keeper. Do you know what happens to us if the realm learns that? Every family that ever lost someone to the border wars will come for us with fire. The guild survives because the crime stays buried. You understand buried things, Okafor. You keep our ledgers."

"I keep *true* ledgers," Ansel said. "That's the whole point of a ledger."

Corvane sighed, genuinely regretful, and raised the rod.

What happened next took less time than a heartbeat to remember and a lifetime to relive. Sefa moved—not away from Ansel but *into* the space between, her shoulder catching the first lash of ward-light meant for Ansel's chest. She made a sound Ansel had never heard her make, sharp and small, and went down against the tomb grilles, and Corvane stepped over her the way one steps over a dropped ledger.

Ansel did not think. Ansel *ran*—down the gallery, through the rusted grille they'd already jimmied, into the dark of Aldric's tomb, hauling the iron door shut behind them as the enforcers' boots thundered closer. The lock was old. Old locks answered old bones. Ansel pressed the femur against the mechanism and whispered, "*Please*," and the dead king's bone remembered being a key to his own house, and the lock turned.

Boots struck the grille. Iron sang. Through the bars, Corvane's face appeared, furious and lit by ward-glow. "It won't matter," he said. "Whatever you do in there, we'll bury it. We always bury it."

Ansel knelt beside the bier, unwrapped the femur, and laid it among its brothers. Against their ribs, their heart hammered. Behind them, beyond the iron, Sefa lay bleeding on the stones and did not move, and the grief that rose in Ansel was so vast it had no bottom—*she took it for me, she took the blow meant for me, because I was the one who could finish this*—and beneath the grief, colder and clearer, the truth they had been avoiding since the stars went out:

There was no version of this where Ansel went back to being invisible. No version where the ledgers stayed quiet and the guild stayed safe and the screaming king stayed screaming. The safe life had died the night the stars did. All that remained was to choose what to build on its grave.

They drew their carving knife.

Above, muffled through stone, the renewal rite began to chant. Below, in the dark, King Aldric's ghost gathered at the edge of the lamplight, watching Ansel kneel at his own remains with a blade in hand.

"Tell me everything," Ansel said to him. "Tell me how you died. All of it. I'm going to cut it into your bones, and then, Your Majesty—we are going to end your story."

The ghost smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ansel had ever seen.

"Then begin," Aldric said, "with my brother."
