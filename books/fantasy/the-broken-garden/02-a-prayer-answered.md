## 2. A Prayer Answered

The widow's name was Merrin Hale, and she lived at the bottom of Rope Street where the fog came in off the harbor and never quite left, pooling in doorways like something that had drowned and forgotten to move on. Yara climbed down to her house three nights after the market, as he had promised, with a candle wrapped in oilcloth and a lie ready in his mouth.

The lie was this: that the candle was blessed by the shrine of the Tidemother, that a priest had prayed over it, that if she lit it at her son's bedside and let it burn through the night, the boy would have an easier time of the fever. Shrines were safe. Priests were safe. The temple's own sanctioned blessings could be sold without shame, and if the widow repeated the story to a neighbor, the neighbor would nod and think nothing of it.

The truth was folded into the wax, and Yara had spent six hours folding it there.

He knocked the way the poor knocked — two soft raps, apologetic, already halfway to leaving — and Merrin Hale opened the door with a shawl clutched around her shoulders and eyes that had stopped sleeping some days ago and never started again.

"You came." She said it the way people said *thank you* when they'd forgotten how. "The charm-seller. You came."

"Yara." He stepped in out of the fog. "I told you I would."

The house smelled of vinegar and sickness and the sweet-rot smell of a fever that had been burning too long. There was a boy in the back room, nine or ten years old, laid out on a cot that had been dragged close to the window as if fresh air could be coaxed in through the glass. His name, Yara remembered from the night market, was Tam. His small chest rose and fell too fast, and his lips had gone the color of the harbor in winter, and when Merrin knelt beside him and smoothed the damp hair from his forehead, he didn't stir at all.

"The physiker came Tuesday," she said, following Yara into the room. Her voice had the careful flatness of someone who had rehearsed not crying. "He said to keep him warm and comfortable. He said it the way you'd say it about a horse."

"Physikers charge by the visit," Yara said. "They can't afford to be wrong, so they're never anything but certain." He unwrapped the candle. It looked ordinary — tallow, a wick, nothing more. He'd made sure of that. The magic in it wasn't in the candle at all; it was in the room, in the way he'd keyed the charm to Merrin's voice, so that when she spoke over the flame it would wake and do its quiet work. "Light it at his head. Let it burn all the way down. Don't trim the wick, and don't put it out, even if it does something strange."

"Strange how?"

Yara had prepared an answer for that too, something bland about how blessed candles guttered, how it was a sign the prayer was being heard. But standing in that small room with the boy's shallow breathing and the mother's hollow eyes, the prepared words felt like coins rubbed smooth of their faces.

"Just strange," he said. "It's nothing to fear."

She lit it with a spill from the hearth, cupping the flame with both hands as though it were a bird that might startle, and set it on the stool by the cot. The light fell gold across the boy's face, ordinary as bread, and Yara felt the charm beneath the wax stir and begin to unfold — slow, patient, a seed deciding the soil was warm enough.

He should have left then. He always left then. Three years in Grayharbor had taught him the arithmetic of survival: sell, withdraw, vanish. Never watch your own work. Watching meant caring, and caring meant staying, and staying was how charm-sellers ended up on the temple's lists, and the temple's lists ended at the pyre.

He stayed.

Merrin walked him to the door and pressed a few coppers into his hand, and he tried to refuse them and failed, because refusing a poor woman's payment was its own cruelty. "If it works," she said, gripping his wrist, her fingers cold and strong, "I'll pray for you. I don't have anything else to give, but I'll pray."

"Prayers are free," Yara said. "That makes them the only honest currency in Grayharbor."

She laughed — a broken, surprised sound, as if she'd forgotten the shape of it — and he went out into the fog, and made himself walk away, and made himself not look back.

He lasted four streets.

* * *

He told himself he was going back for professional reasons. A charm-seller who didn't know how his wares behaved was a charm-seller who got caught. That was all. That was the whole of it. He turned at the corner of Salt Lane and walked back up Rope Street with his collar up and the fog swallowing the sound of his boots, and he told himself the same lie all the way to the widow's window, where he stopped in the dark beneath the eaves of the fishmonger's shuttered stall and looked in through the glass.

The candle was burning blue.

Not guttering blue, not the momentary blue of a draft or a salt-damp wick. Burning blue the way the deep sea burns, the way a star burns that is older than any god with a name — a steady, patient, impossible blue that threw no heat and cast shadows that fell the wrong way, leaning toward the flame instead of away from it. Yara's breath stopped. His hands, pressed flat against the cold shutter, had gone numb without his noticing.

He had not made that.

He knew his own work the way a locksmith knows his locks, and there was nothing in the charm he'd folded into that wax — nothing in any charm he had ever made — that could do *that*. His magic was small magic, hidden magic, the magic of a man who had spent three years teaching himself to be a whisper: luck folded into copper, health coaxed into tallow, a little warmth, a little ease, nothing that would ever make the temple's inquisitors look twice. What burned in the widow's window was not a whisper. It was a voice. It was the voice of something vast that had been silent for a hundred years and had just, without asking anyone's permission, decided to speak.

Inside, Merrin Hale was on her knees beside the cot.

Yara watched through the glass as the boy Tam opened his eyes.

It was not the waking of a fever breaking. There was no confusion in it, no weak blinking, no slow return. The boy's eyes opened and were *clear*, bright and present and looking directly at the blue flame as though it had called him by name, and he drew a breath that went all the way down, and he sat up — sat *up*, three days past the point where sitting up should have been possible — and said something Yara couldn't hear through the glass. Merrin's hands flew to her mouth. Then she was holding him, both of them wrapped around each other, rocking, and the candle burned on between them, blue as the space between stars, and the shadows in the room knelt toward it.

Yara's legs stopped working.

He sat down hard against the fishmonger's stall, in the dark, in the fog, and pressed the heel of his hand against his sternum because something behind his ribs had *caught* — a hook, a fishhook of light set deep in the meat of him, and it had just been pulled taut. It didn't hurt, exactly. It was worse than hurt. It was the feeling of hearing your own name called from a house you'd lived in before you were born, a house you had no memory of, in a voice that knew you.

*Homesick.* That was the word, and it made no sense, because you could not be homesick for a place you had never been. But that was what it was: a terrible, specific longing, aimed at nothing, aimed through the walls of the world, and the blue candle through the widow's window was burning on the far side of the longing like a lamp in a window of a home he'd been stolen from as an infant.

The old magic. Everyone knew the stories — the gods who had gone silent a century ago, the wonder that had been outlawed, the temple that had burned the hedge-witches and the god-touched and sealed the old roads. Everyone knew that the age of answered prayers was over, that the silence was permanent, that the only magic left was the small smuggled kind that men like Yara sold in night markets to people who couldn't afford physikers.

The candle in the window was not small magic.

The candle in the window was a prayer being *answered* — actually answered, from the other side, by something that had heard and chosen to respond — and Yara sat in the fog with a hook behind his ribs and understood, with the cold certainty of a man watching the first crack cross a frozen river, that the silence had ended, and that it had ended *here*, in a room where his own charm had been the door.

He had lit it. Whatever was moving beneath the world, his little folded whisper of luck had been the keyhole it came through.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He went back to his rooms above the map shop — his legitimate rooms, the ones that existed in the daylight world, above Cartwright & Sons where he was the junior partner nobody's son — and he sat at his desk with a single ordinary candle lit in front of him, gold-flamed, obedient, and he watched it burn until the sky went gray.

The blue was still behind his ribs. The hook had not let go. When he closed his eyes he could feel it pulling, gently, the way a current pulls, toward something down and deep and south — beneath the mountain, some part of him supplied, and he didn't know how he knew that, and the not-knowing frightened him more than the knowing would have.

At dawn he did the only thing he knew how to do with a fear too large to hold: he worked.

He pulled out the charts. Not the harbor charts, not the coastal surveys that paid the rent — the others. The ones he kept in the false-bottom drawer beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, drawn in two hands: his mother's, fine and strange, full of coastlines no ship had ever sighted; and his own, which he told himself were exercises of imagination and had never quite believed were. He spread the oldest of them across the desk — a map with no title, showing a country beneath a country, rivers that flowed uphill, a road of roots descending toward a mountain that was drawn not as a shape but as a *presence*, the ink around it darker, as if the paper itself were afraid.

He had drawn that mountain eleven times over the years, in different inks, at different ages, always the same. He had told himself it was a recurring dream, nothing more, the way some men dream of falling and some of teeth.

This morning, with the hook behind his ribs pulling south, he looked at the mountain and felt it look back.

"Old magic's dead," he said aloud, to the empty room, in the flat voice he used for lying to himself. "A hundred years dead. The temple made sure of it. There's nothing down there but stone and roots and the dark."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

He sat back. His hands were not quite steady, and he watched them the way one watches a stranger's hands, because they wanted something — they wanted a pen, they wanted ink, they wanted to *draw*, and that wanting did not feel like his own. It felt inherited. It felt like something older than him moving through his fingers the way water moves through old pipes, taking the shape of the channel but not caring about it.

Outside, Grayharbor was waking. Carts on the cobbles, gulls over the fish market, the first smoke from a thousand chimneys — the ordinary machinery of a city that had outlawed wonder and called the outlawry peace. Somewhere down on Rope Street, a boy who should have died in the night was eating breakfast, and his mother was keeping a promise to pray for a charm-seller, and neither of them knew what had actually come into their room when the flame turned blue.

Yara rolled up the map of the country beneath the country and put it away, and locked the drawer, and sat with his hands flat on the desk.

The prayer, Merrin had said. *I'll pray for you.*

He thought of her kneeling in the blue light, and the boy waking clear-eyed, and the shadows leaning toward the flame like courtiers toward a throne, and the hook behind his ribs pulled again — south, and down, and *home*, said a voice that was not his, in a language he had never learned and somehow had never forgotten.

For the first time in three years of careful, small, hidden living, Yara Ravensworth was afraid not of being caught.

He was afraid of being *found*.

He was still sitting there, dawn going to full morning, when the knocking came at the shop door below — three raps, hard, deliberate, nothing like the way the poor knocked — and a voice he did not know called up the stairwell, asking for the cartographer.

It was a woman's voice, and it carried the particular authority of someone accustomed to doors being opened, and Yara stood very still in his room full of outlawed maps and listened to his own heartbeat, and behind his ribs, the hook pulled tight.
