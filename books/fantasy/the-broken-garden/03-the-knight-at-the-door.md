## 3. The Knight at the Door

Yara did not answer the first knock. He had survived three years in Grayharbor by understanding that doors were confessions, and the speed with which you opened one told the person on the other side exactly how much you had to hide.

He counted instead. One. Two. Three. Long enough to seem like a man coming up from a cellar or down from a nap, a man with nothing in his hands.

The knocking came again — three raps, hard, deliberate, spaced like a drill. Not the widow's timid scratch. Not the furtive double-tap of the night market's regulars. This was the knock of someone who knocked for institutions.

He took the stairs slowly, and as he went he did what he always did: he became small. Shoulders rounded, chin tucked, the slight stoop of a tradesman who spent his life bent over a table. His face settled into its public mask, pleasant and forgettable, the face of Yara Senn, cartographer, licensed by the Guild of Ways, payer of taxes, keeper of no secrets worth a constable's time.

The woman standing in his shop doorway made smallness very difficult.

She filled the frame without being large — tall, broad through the shoulders, wearing travel leathers gone gray with road dust over what had once, unmistakably, been temple black. The cut of the collar was still there, the high closed throat of the Order of the Vigilant Flame, but the sigil had been cut away. He could see the stitches. Someone had unpicked it thread by thread rather than burn it, which meant she had done it herself, which meant it had mattered.

Her hair was dark, cropped short and practical, damp at the temples from the morning fog. Her eyes went past him before they ever landed on him — swept the shop left to right in a single practiced motion, cataloguing exits, sightlines, and every object in the room as though each might be evidence.

"Yara Senn," she said. Not a question.

"Cartographer," he said, and gave her the smile he gave guild inspectors. "If you need a chart of the harbor approaches, I'm your man. If you've come about the roof, I've already complained twice."

"I haven't come about the roof." She reached into her coat and produced a folded paper, and even folded, even creased soft from handling, he knew what it was. The wax seal had been broken and resealed — someone had read this document before she carried it, and she had let them think she didn't notice. "Sef Andric. Formerly Knight-Errant of the Vigilant Flame. I have a warrant of inquiry."

"A warrant." He took it because refusing would be worse. The paper named him — *the charm-seller operating in Grayharbor's night market* — in a clerk's careful hand, and beneath it, in a different ink, a list. Candle-burnings. A fever broken. A child's cough stilled. Dates going back eleven days.

Eleven days. Since the blue flame.

"You're investigating answered prayers," he said, keeping his voice light, bored. "That's a lot of parchment for folk getting better."

"The outlawry of wonder is not a lot of parchment. It is four hundred pages, and I have read all of them." She watched him read. He felt the watching like weather. "The temple takes miracles seriously, Master Senn. Miracles are either thefts or heralds, and both require an investigation."

"And which do you think these are?"

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, quickly suppressed, like a fish turning under ice. "I think," she said, "that you sell luck-charms."

"I sell trinkets. Copper wire, river stones, knotwork. People bring me their hopes and I braid them into something they can hold. It's comfort, not craft. The Guild has no objection."

"The Guild," she said, "has no objection to anything that pays its dues." She stepped inside without being asked, and he let her, because blocking the door would be worse, and because some traitorous part of him wanted to see what she would do next. She moved through his shop the way water moves through a hull — finding every low place. Her fingers hovered over a hanging row of charms without touching. "These are cold."

"It's a warm day."

"These are *cold*, Master Senn. Every one of them. As if something was taken out of them recently." She turned, and now those gray eyes fixed on him fully, and Yara understood with a lurch that he had been wrong about the danger in the room. The danger wasn't the warrant. The danger was that she was not looking for a criminal. She was looking for an explanation, and she would not stop until she had one, and explanations were the one thing he could not afford to give. "Where does a trinket-seller learn to empty a charm?"

"Practice," he said. "And a steady hand."

* * *

She stayed two hours.

He learned things about her the way she was learning things about him — sideways, by accident, by what she chose not to say. She had walked from the Temple of the Flame in Corvane, nine days east, and had been reassigned — *reassigned* was her word, delivered flat as slate — to a circuit of fishing towns where nothing happened, until things began happening. She drank none of the tea he offered. She sat with her back to the wall and her eyes on the stairwell. And when he asked, casually, why a knight of the Flame would trouble herself over village candles, she looked at him for a long moment and said:

"Because I was stripped of rank for saying the gods should not have been outlawed. So they sent me to prove I was wrong by showing me a world where nothing answers." Her mouth twisted. "And then the world started answering, and my superiors decided that was my fault somehow. Heresy attracts heresy. Like calls to like."

"That must sting."

"What stings," she said, "is that they're right." She said it lightly, but he heard the truth under it, the way you hear water under floorboards. "Whatever is waking down there — wherever these prayers are falling — it isn't the gentle kind. Answered prayers are never free, Master Senn. Something is collecting."

Behind his ribs, the hook pulled.

*Collecting.* He kept his face still. He thought of the blue flame, of the widow's son sitting up in bed with color in his cheeks, of the terrible homesickness that had ridden home with him through the fog like a stowaway. *Something is collecting.*

"You believe in the old gods," he said. It wasn't a question either.

"I believe in the record." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time she looked tired — genuinely tired, the fatigue of a woman who had been carrying a question too long. "Four hundred pages of law, and underneath all of it, one sentence no one will write down: we outlawed wonder because we were afraid of who it obeyed. The temple says the old gods were tyrants. The histories say the old gods went silent. Nobody says why. I've been asking why since I was nineteen years old, and it cost me my rank, and I still don't know." She stood abruptly, as if the honesty embarrassed her. "Show me your workshop."

"There's nothing—"

"Every craftsman has a workshop. Show me the room where you make the things that are cold."

* * *

Later — much later, when he understood everything, when he had walked beside her through the dark places of the world — Yara would try to remember why he opened the door at the top of the stairs. He never found a good reason. Fear made men stupid; attraction made them stupider. Perhaps it was simply that she stood in his narrow stairwell with road dust on her boots and a cut-away sigil stitched out of her collar, and she looked at him with the expression of someone who had been alone with a question for years, and some starved part of him recognized her the way one drowning sailor recognizes another.

Or perhaps it was the hook behind his ribs, pulling.

The workshop was behind a false wall in the back bedroom, and he showed it to her because she already knew it was there — he realized this only afterward, only when it was far too late, remembering how her eyes had flicked to the draft moving along the floorboards, the faint cool breath of hidden space. She had known before she climbed the stairs. The whole visit had been theater, and he had played his part beautifully.

The lamp came up on shelves of charts. Realms with no names. Coastlines no ship had ever traced, drawn in two hands — one faded, brown-inked, decades old; one fresh, black, precise. Rivers that flowed uphill. Mountain ranges annotated in a language that predated the outlawry. And in the center of the long table, weighted down with river stones, the map he had been working when the knocking came: the Undercourt, sketched from dreams, its cathedral of roots half-finished, its lanterns unlit.

Sef Andric went very quiet.

She walked the length of the shelves slowly, and Yara watched her take it in — the scale of it, the impossibility, the two hands across a hundred years — and he watched the moment arrive when she stopped being an investigator and became something more dangerous: a believer standing in front of an answer.

"This is Ravensworth work," she said softly. "This is the old line's cartography. I've seen fragments in the sealed archives. Fragments, under glass, guarded by three locks." She turned to him. "Who taught you this?"

"My mother," he said. It was almost true. "She died when I was young."

"And these realms. Where are they?"

"Nowhere," he said. "That's rather the point of maps, isn't it? They make nowhere somewhere."

"Don't." The word came out sharp, and she caught it, reined it. But her hand had risen toward the Undercourt chart and stopped short of touching it, hovering, the way she'd hovered over the dead charms — as if touching might confirm something she wasn't ready to confirm. "Don't do that. Don't be clever and small. I have spent nine days walking toward a town where prayers are being answered by something old, and every charm in your shop has been hollowed out like eggshells, and you have a map on that table of a place that officially does not exist, drawn in a hand the temple keeps under lock." Her voice dropped. "You are the most interesting thing I have found in six years, Yara Senn. Do not insult both of us by pretending otherwise."

His name — his false name — in her mouth sounded strange and close, like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there.

"And what will you do with interesting?" he asked quietly. "Your warrant says find the charm-seller. You've found him. Report me, and the temple comes, and whatever fragile thing is waking below goes back to sleep or comes up angry, and either way Grayharbor burns for it. Is that what the Flame wants?"

"The Flame wants obedience."

"And you? What does Sef Andric want?"

For a moment neither of them breathed. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the harbor bell rang the hour, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a pulse.

"I want to know why," she said. "I have wanted it since I was nineteen. And I think—" She stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like a footfall on ice. "I think you know. I think you've known your whole life, and you're so busy hiding it that you've never once looked at it straight on. And I think whatever is answering those prayers knows your name, because the night I arrived, an old woman's candle burned blue, and the child it healed woke up speaking of a garden."

The floor tilted. Or he did.

*A garden.*

"He spoke of a garden," Yara repeated. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later. Level, and mild, and perfectly, catastrophically calm. "Children talk nonsense."

"They do," she agreed. "They talk of gardens, and rivers that run uphill, and lanterns in the dark." She tapped the Undercourt chart, once, gently, with one finger. "They talk of *this*. Whatever you are, cartographer — trinket-seller, liar, heir of whatever this hand belongs to—" her finger moved to the faded brown ink, the mother-hand, the dead woman's lines, "—you are going to help me find out. Because I burned my last chance at restoration to walk here, and I will not leave with nothing."

"Or?"

"Or I report you," she said, "and we both find out what the temple does with the last mapmaker of the old line. I'd rather not. I've read the four hundred pages. There's a chapter on mapmakers." She held his gaze, and there it was again, the fish under the ice, the thing she kept suppressing — not cruelty, not duty. Recognition. She was looking at him the way he had looked at the blue flame: with dread, and with a homesickness for something he couldn't name. "You felt it too. When the candle burned. Don't lie to me about that. I saw it in your face the moment I mentioned the child."

He should deny it. Three years of survival said: deny it, shrink, become small, become Yara Senn, become nothing. Every instinct he had honed since the night his mother died whispered the old arithmetic — *be small, be forgotten, live.*

But the hook behind his ribs pulled, and for the first time in three years, Yara let himself be pulled.

"If I help you," he said, "you follow my rules. You don't touch the charts without asking. You don't speak of what's in this room. And when I tell you to stop asking questions, you stop."

"And when I don't stop?"

"Then you'll see why the temple keeps mapmakers in a special chapter."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face — brief, crooked, gone. She put out her hand. Her grip was calloused, warm, and it held on a half-second longer than a bargain required.

"Witnessed, then," she said. "Mapmaker."

"Witnessed," he said. "Knight."

"Not knight," she said, and the word had a scar in it. "Not anymore."

* * *

She left at dusk. He watched from the window as she crossed the market square, dust-gray and straight-backed, cutting through the evening crowd like a keel through water, and he stood there long after she was gone, one hand pressed flat against his sternum, feeling the hook pull with every beat of his heart.

He should feel trapped. He did feel trapped. He had invited the temple's hound into his den and sworn a witnessed bargain with her, and every rule of his survival screamed that he had just signed his own discovery.

But beneath the fear, unwelcome and unmistakable, was the other thing. The recognition. *Like calls to like,* she had said, meaning heresy — but she had been talking about them without knowing it. Two people carrying forbidden things, each certain they were alone with the weight.

He turned back to the workshop. The lamp was still burning over the Undercourt chart, and in its light the unfinished cathedral of roots seemed to lean toward him, patient as a debt.

On the table, where Sef Andric had stood, lay a scrap of parchment she must have dropped — a page torn from her field journal, dense with her narrow, disciplined script. He should not have read it. He read it.

*Day 9. The charm-seller is the real thing. Worse — better — than the reports. Hollowed charms mean he can take magic OUT, which means he can put it IN, which means the temple's whole taxonomy of him is wrong. Note for the report I will probably never send: he flinched at the word "garden." He has the old line's hands. God forgive me, I hope he runs. I hope he makes me chase him.*

Yara set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be a trap or might be a gift.

Then he picked up his pen, and unrolled a fresh sheet, and began — for the first time in three years, deliberately, wide awake, with a witness waiting in the city above — to draw the road beneath the mountain.

Behind his ribs, the hook pulled tight, and somewhere far below, something that collected debts turned its attention toward Grayharbor, and smiled.
