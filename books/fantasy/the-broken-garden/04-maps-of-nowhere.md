## 4. Maps of Nowhere

The knock came at the hour when Grayharbor pretended to sleep.

Yara knew every knock this city had. The widow's soft double-tap. The fishwife's impatient fist. The temple's formal triple beat that meant *open and be counted*. This one was none of them — two knuckles, measured, patient, the knock of someone who expected doors to open because she knocked on them, not because they wanted to.

He set down his pen mid-stroke. The coastline he'd been inking — a shore no ship had ever found, curving through a sea that existed only in his mother's hand and his own memory of her hand — sat half-finished on the table, and for a moment he simply looked at it, the way a man looks at a child he might not see grow up.

Then he blew out the lamp, folded the map into the false bottom of his drafting case, and went to answer the door as if he were only ever a cartographer, and a tired one at that.

Sef Andric stood in the stairwell with rain still beaded on her shoulders, though the storm had ended an hour past. She wore no armor tonight — just a gray traveling coat over the plain shirt of someone who had learned not to advertise what she was. But the temple knight showed through anyway, in the way she stood with her weight balanced on both feet, in the way her eyes went past him into the room before they came back to his face.

"Master Ravensworth." She used the name like a key tried in a lock.

"That's my mother's name," Yara said. "I go by Vane."

"Do you." Her gaze flicked to the drafting case by the wall. "May I come in, Master Vane? Or would you rather we conduct this in the street, where your neighbors can enjoy it?"

"There's nothing to conduct."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "There never is. That's what makes my work so difficult."

He should have shut the door. Three years of being small said *shut the door, smile, apologize, let her leave believing you are nothing*. But the blue candle was three days behind him now, and the hook behind his ribs had not stopped pulling, and some traitorous part of him — the part that drew coastlines no one had ever sailed — wanted to know what she knew.

He stepped aside.

* * *

Her room above the chandler's shop had been searched before he arrived; he could tell by the way things sat too neatly, books aligned by their spines, the bed made with military corners. Sef noticed him noticing and did not apologize.

"They gave me quarters in the temple annex," she said. "I don't sleep there."

"Why not?"

"Because I want to know what happens in Grayharbor at night, and the annex has very good walls." She poured water from a clay jug into two cups, an oddly domestic gesture from those balanced-knight hands, and pushed one toward him. "Three answered prayers in nine days. A fevered child singing in a language dead before the city had its name. A drowned man walking out of the harbor with saltwater in his lungs and a smile on his face. And now a widow's son, dying of lung-rot for a year, woke up whole because his mother burned a candle."

"You've been busy."

"The temple is busy. I'm trying to be something else." She sat across from him, elbows on knees, and for the first time since the night market he saw her look tired. Not weak — tired, the way a blade looks after it has been used all day. "Do you know what a *disgraced* knight investigates, Master Vane? Nothing. A disgraced knight is given the cases the temple wants quietly looked at and loudly forgotten. Answered prayers are supposed to be impossible. The gods have been silent for a hundred years. If they're waking up, the temple needs to know why — and it needs to know *before anyone else does*, and it needs me to find out while remaining officially a heretic who talks to tavern keepers."

"And do you? Talk to tavern keepers?"

"I talk to everyone. It's astonishing what people tell a woman they believe has already lost everything." She drank. "They tell me about a cartographer who sells charms he pretends are trinkets."

The water in Yara's cup suddenly required a great deal of attention.

"I'm not accusing you," Sef went on. "If I were, we'd be having this conversation with six brothers-in-iron at my back. I'm asking you. Because whatever is happening in this city started small and is getting less small, and the temple's answer to *less small* is fire. They burned the last god-touched district to its foundations eighty years ago. There's a reason the old quarter is called the Ashways."

"I've heard."

"Have you heard what's under the Ashways?" Her voice dropped, not for drama but for accuracy; he recognized the cartographer's instinct in it, the impulse to get the elevation right. "There are tunnels down there that aren't on any survey. My order's records mention them once, in a marginal note from before the outlawry. The priest who wrote it crossed the passage out and wrote beside it: *the road goes down farther than the world does.*"

Yara kept his face still. Behind his ribs, the hook pulled.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"A map," said Sef Andric. "Or the person who can make one."

* * *

He told himself he was leading her away from the truth. He believed it for almost exactly the length of the walk.

The workshop hid behind a bricked-over cold cellar in the tannery district, entered through a door that looked like a wall and smelled like lye enough to discourage curiosity. He had chosen it for the smell. He opened the false panel, lit the shielded lamp, and delivered the speech he'd prepared: this was where he kept his surveys, harbor charts, the tedious honest work of a tedious honest trade.

Sef walked past all of it without a glance.

She stopped instead at the far wall, where a hanging canvas covered something floor to ceiling, and she looked at him — one long look, giving him the chance he hadn't earned — and then she pulled the canvas down.

Silence filled the room the way water fills a hull.

The map was of nowhere. That was the only word for it. Coastlines that matched no chart in any harbor office, mountain ranges with names in a script older than the city, rivers that forked and rejoined in patterns no water follows — and yet drawn with such authority, such *certainty*, that the eye insisted the places must exist somewhere, if only one knew how to stand in them. In the lower corner, in faded brown ink, a signature: *M. Ravensworth.* Over it, fresher, in black: his own.

"My mother's work," Yara said quietly. It was pointless to lie now, and some exhausted part of him was relieved. "And mine."

"She's dead."

"Six years."

Sef moved along the wall. There were others behind sliding panels — a dozen, two dozen — realms stacked on realms, each one impossible, each one rendered with the loving precision of a place remembered rather than imagined. Her fingers hovered over the parchment without touching, the way people hover over relics.

"These aren't inventions," she said. "I've seen fantasy maps. Dreamers' nonsense, pretty lies. These are *surveys*. Someone stood in these places and measured them."

"Yes."

"Who?"

"I don't know." He heard his own voice go strange. "That's the truth, knight. My mother left me her hand — the way she drew, the way she held a line. When I draw, I draw places I've never seen. Places no living person has seen. I used to think I was mad. Then I thought I was gifted. Now—" He gestured at the wall, at the blue candle's aftermath hanging between them unspoken. "Now I think I'm remembering."

Sef turned to face him fully, and in the lamplight he watched something happen in her expression: the investigator receding, the believer surfacing — the heretic, the woman the temple had broken for thinking the wrong holy thought.

"When I was noviced," she said, "they taught us the gods didn't die. They were *bound*. Sealed behind the Raven Throne's last edicts, and the throne fell, and the seals stayed, and worship went nowhere, and the world went quiet." Her jaw tightened. "They taught it as history. I made the mistake of treating it as an injury. You don't bind something living and call the silence peace."

"No," Yara said. "You don't."

"The prayers being answered aren't going up." She tapped the lowest map on the wall — the one whose rivers flowed uphill, whose borders were drawn in red that had never dried. "They're going *down*. Something beneath us is hearing us again, Master Vane. And the temple will send soldiers, and soldiers with fire cannot march down roads that exist on no map. But you—"

"Don't." He stepped back from her, from the wall, from the shape his life was taking in her mouth. "Whatever you're about to ask—"

"—you could draw them a road."

"*Don't.*"

The word cracked out of him harder than he intended, and the lamp flame bent sideways though there was no draft, and both of them froze, watching it right itself. Sef's eyes went from the flame to him, and he saw her cataloguing it, filing it away with everything else.

"You're afraid," she said. Not mocking. Almost gentle.

"I'm *careful*. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm alive." He pulled the canvas back up over the maps, hiding the nowhere country, hating how much he wanted to pull it down again. "You don't know what asks to be mapped, knight. You draw a door on paper and somewhere something feels the ink dry. My mother drew these and it killed her, or worse than killed her, and she made sure I grew up knowing nothing — no name, no history, no *why* — because knowing is a scent and things below hunt by scent. So no. No roads. No descent. Whatever wakes, let it wake in the dark where it's been for a hundred years."

"It isn't sleeping peacefully," Sef said. "It's answering widows. It healed a dying boy, Yara. Does that sound like a thing that wants to stay buried?"

He had no answer. The hook behind his ribs pulled, and for one vertiginous moment he let himself feel what it actually felt like: not dread. Homesickness. A terrible, nameless homesickness, like standing in a strange city and smelling bread baked the way your dead mother baked it.

"I need time," he said finally.

"You have until the temple stops finding my reports ignorable." She gathered her coat. At the false door she paused, her hand on the frame, and did not look back at him. "For what it's worth — I don't think you're what I was sent to find. I think you're the only person in this city who knows what was found."

Then she was gone, and the smell of rain came in with her and left with her, and Yara stood alone among his impossible countries with his heart going like a hammer in a mine.

* * *

He did not sleep. He told himself it was the danger, the exposure, the knight who now knew too much — but by the third hour past midnight he had the lowest map unrolled on his table, the one with the uphill river, and his pen in his hand, and his mother's handwriting swimming up through the old ink like fish through dark water.

*The road goes down farther than the world does.*

He had always read that as warning. Tonight, tracing the red border with one finger, he read it differently. Not *do not enter*. Just — *farther*. A measurement. His mother had been a cartographer. Cartographers did not write warnings. They wrote *distances*.

His finger stopped at a mark he had never truly seen before, though his eyes must have crossed it a thousand times. A small annotation in the margin, in his mother's hand, in cipher — the simple substitution cipher she'd taught him as a game when he was five, *so you'll always have a secret, little raven*.

He decoded it without needing the key anymore, the way you decode your own name.

*Contract held in trust. The debt sleeps until the debtor wakes. When the gods stir, the collectors come. — M.R., for love, for his life, against his soul.*

The lamp flame bent sideways again.

Below the tannery, below the cellar, below the ash and the roots and the uphill river, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep — and Yara Ravensworth, holding his mother's cipher in his shaking hand, understood that the prayer being answered all over Grayharbor had been answered *about him*, and that the season of being small was over.

Somewhere in the dark, unhurried footsteps began to climb.
