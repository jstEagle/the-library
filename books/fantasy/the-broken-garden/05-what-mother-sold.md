## 5. What Mother Sold

The workshop smelled of iron-gall ink and old paper, and Yara had always thought that if a person could be homesick for a place they'd never been, then this room was the shape of that ache. He kept it behind the false wall of his lodgings on Tanner's Row, three turns of a hidden latch past a closet full of cheap coats, and he had never once brought anyone through it. Not in three years. Not since Grayharbor had decided, by decree and by fire, that wonder was a crime.

Tonight the lamp burned low because low lamps were safer, and Yara sat cross-legged on the floor with his mother's maps spread around him like fallen leaves.

They were hers. That was the thing no one alive knew but him. The charts pinned to the walls — the Sunken Reach with its upside-down waterfalls, the Glass Steppe where the horizon bent like heat-shimmer, the hundred leagues of the Vellum Coast drawn in a hand so fine it looked embroidered — every one of them was her work. Mirabel Ravensworth, cartographer of realms that did not appear on any atlas sanctioned by any crown or temple. And beneath her maps, layered over them like sediment, were his: the same coastlines redrawn at age nine, age twelve, age fifteen, each version more confident, more obsessive, more *hers*.

He had inherited her hands without ever being told whose hands they were.

He unrolled the map he'd taken down from the high shelf tonight — the one he'd been avoiding for a week, ever since the widow's candle had burned blue and something behind his ribs had pulled tight as a fishhook. It was a chart of the deep roads, the ways beneath the world, and unlike every other map in the room it was unfinished. The lower third simply stopped, the ink lines thinning out into nothing, as though the cartographer's courage had failed her partway down.

His mother's last work. Or nearly her last.

Yara traced the place where the lines died. His finger hovered there, and the hook behind his ribs tugged, gentle and horrible, like something on the far end of a line testing whether the fish was still attached.

"You're not going to tell me what's down there," he said to the empty room. "Are you."

The lamp guttered. In Grayharbor, lamps guttered all the time; the harbor wind got into everything. But this guttering had a rhythm to it, two short flickers and a long one, and Yara sat very still, because two short and a long was how his mother used to tap on his bedroom door when he was small enough to still believe she'd always come.

He told himself it was coincidence. He told himself that most nights, about most things, and most nights the telling held.

Then he saw the corner of the parchment that shouldn't have been there.

It was tucked inside the rolled map — a second sheet, folded small, sealed with a smear of wax that had once been green and had faded to the color of pond ice. He didn't remember it. He had gone through these maps a hundred times in three years, cataloguing, copying, memorizing, and he would have sworn on the temple's own iron altar that this fold had never existed. But the paper was old. Old the way the maps were old, yellowed and soft-edged, and the wax seal bore an impression he recognized from his mother's other documents: a raven with a key in its beak.

The house sigil. The sigil of a family that, as far as the city knew, had never existed.

Yara's mouth had gone dry. He broke the seal anyway. Some part of him understood, even then, that the letter had waited twenty-six years to be opened and that waiting any longer was no longer among its options.

The handwriting inside was his mother's, but wrong — hurried, the letters leaning like people running.

*If you are reading this, then the debt has woken, and I am beyond helping you, my love. Forgive me. Forgive me first, before you read further, because I cannot bear for your last memory of me to be what follows.*

*On the night you were born, I sold your soul.*

The words seemed to darken as he read them, the ink drinking the lamplight.

*Not wounded it. Not pledged it. Sold it, whole and clean, to the Court Beneath the Mountain, and the price they paid me was your life. You came into this world blue-lipped and silent, and the midwife shook her head, and I could not — I could not, Yara, do you understand, I could not let the world take you before it had even given you a name. So I went down to the old door in the cellar of Ravensworth, the door your father forbade me to touch, and I knocked, and something answered, and we made our bargain.*

*Your soul for your breath. They said the debt would sleep as long as the old gods slept. They said a century, they promised me a century, and I believed them because grief makes fools of us all.*

*But the gods are waking. I felt it before the end, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. When they wake, the contract wakes with them, and the collectors will come for what is owed. You will know them. They do not look like anything. That is how you will know them.*

*There is a copy of the contract in the atlas case, beneath the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth. Do not burn it. Whatever you feel when you read it, do not burn it. A contract destroyed in anger binds twice. Read it. Know its terms better than they do. Your father taught me that much before the end: in the Undercourt, knowledge is the only coin that cannot be devalued.*

*I loved you. Every map I ever drew was a road leading back to you. If I could trade places with the woman who made this bargain, I would make it again, and again, and again, and hate myself in exactly the same way each time.*

*Run if you can. Fight if you must. But live, Yara. Live loudly enough that they regret the price they paid.*

*— Your mother, who was a coward in precisely one thing, and it was you.*

Yara read it twice. Then a third time, moving his lips over the words like a man learning a language spoken only by the dead.

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight now, unnaturally still, as though the room itself were holding its breath.

*Sold your soul.* He kept circling the phrase, looking for the trick in it, the misreading, the way a tired eye might turn *sold* into *spared*. There was no trick. There was only the flat, terrible clarity of it, and underneath the clarity something worse: recognition. All those years of feeling watched in crowds. The dreams of descending staircases that never ended. The way holy places made his skin crawl and unholy ones made him feel, shamefully, *welcome*. He had thought it was the outlawry, the constant fear, the life lived small. He had thought everyone felt like a coat worn inside out.

He had been wearing someone else's claim on him his entire life, and he'd mistaken it for his own skin.

He laughed. It came out of him sideways, cracked, nothing like laughter, and it frightened him more than the letter had. Because here was the arithmetic of his existence, laid bare at last: he was alive because he was owed. Every breath he'd taken for twenty-six years was a loan against a debt he'd never signed. Somewhere beneath the mountain hung a lantern with his name on it, burning with the life he hadn't lived, and his mother had looked at that ledger and chosen breath over soul, and who could blame her, who on this earth could blame her —

He found he was on his knees. He didn't remember getting there. The letter lay on the floorboards in front of him and he couldn't look away from it, the way you can't look away from a wound to check whether it's yours.

"Mother." Her name came out wrecked. "Oh, Mother. What did you do?"

The room did not answer, but the hook behind his ribs pulled once, slow and deliberate, like a hand testing a knot.

* * *

The atlas case stood against the east wall, walnut and brass, the one piece of furniture Yara had spent real money on because a cartographer's charts deserved dignity even when the charts themselves were crimes. Its false bottom was his own addition, installed the week he'd moved in, and he had always assumed he was hiding his work from the world.

Now he knelt in front of it with the letter folded against his heart and understood that his mother had hidden something in the same place decades before he was born, in a house that had burned to its foundations before he could walk. The case was new. The hiding place was old. Some inheritances pass through blood and some through habit, and Yara was beginning to suspect he'd received both.

The oilcloth bundle was there, exactly where the letter promised, wedged between the case's frame and the floor, in a gap he had measured a dozen times and never found. It was cold. Colder than the room, colder than the night, cold the way stone is cold a long way underground.

He unwrapped it on the worktable under the full light of the lamp, because whatever this was, he refused to meet it half-blind.

The contract was written on vellum that wasn't vellum. He knew skins — calfskin, goatskin, the treated hide of exotic beasts that smugglers swore by — and this was none of them. It had a grain to it like fingerprint whorls, and it was warm now that it was out of its wrapping, warming toward the temperature of his hands, and he chose not to think about what kind of surface warmed to a human touch. The script across it was beautiful and wrong, each character drawn as though by a hand with too many fingers, and it was in two languages. The upper text was Old Imperial, which Yara could read. The lower text was the same contract again, and reading it made his teeth ache, and he understood that the lower text was not for him.

The upper text was for him. It began:

*Be it known beneath root and stone, in the Court Beneath the Mountain, that a bargain was struck on the first night of the winter of his birth between Mirabel of the line of Ravensworth, called also the Mapmaker, and the undersigned authority of the Undercourt, concerning one soul entire, unblemished, newly housed in flesh: the child Yara.*

His own name. Written in a court he'd never heard of, on a night he couldn't remember, by parties unknown, and yet the letters seemed to know him — the way the ink sat on the page reminded him unbearably of his mother's hand, as though the contract had learned its penmanship from watching her write.

He read the terms. He read them four times, because his mother had told him to know them better than they did, and because knowledge was the only coin that couldn't be devalued, and because his hands were shaking and careful reading was the only thing keeping him upright.

The soul of the child Yara, entire and unblemished, was conveyed to the Undercourt in perpetuity, in exchange for the continuation of his breath, his warmth, and his waking. The debt should lie dormant while the old gods slept. Upon the waking of the gods, the debt should come due, and collectors should be sent within one turning of the season. The bearer of the soul might redeem it by payment of equal weight: a crown, a god, or a soul freely given. Failing payment, the soul should be collected, and the flesh left standing, breathing, walking — empty.

*Empty.* The word went through him like a splinter seeking bone. Not dead. His mother's bargain would hold even after collection; he would keep breathing, keep walking, keep selling charms in the night market and drawing coastlines no one would ever sail. He would simply not be in there anymore. The contract was very precise about it, in its too-many-fingered calligraphy: *the vessel shall persist, that the bargain's kindness be not undone.*

Kindness. The Undercourt called it kindness.

Yara set the contract down. His hands were steady now, which surprised him; he had expected shaking, and instead there was a stillness in him like the center of a storm, like the moment on a cliff path when you stop deciding where to put your feet and simply walk.

One turning of the season. Winter was three months gone; the blue candle had been nine days ago. Call it ten weeks. Perhaps less. Collectors that did not look like anything, coming through a city that had outlawed wonder, hunting a name that appeared on a document older than the city's oldest charter.

And the redemption price. *A crown, a god, or a soul freely given.* He turned the three terms over like stones on a beach. A crown — he owned a bedroll, a false wall, and forty-one maps of nowhere. A god — the gods were just waking, and every story agreed they woke hungry. Which left the third term sitting there, patient as a trap: a soul freely given. His own, presumably, offered up in place of the one already claimed — a trade of nothing for nothing, a circle closing on itself. Unless.

Unless the contract meant any soul. Unless *freely given* was doing the work that *his* was not.

He looked at the lantern-image his mind had built — his own name on a dim glass cage, hanging in a cathedral of roots beside ten thousand others — and he thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I could pay with someone else. Anyone would do it. Half of Grayharbor would sell their grandmother's ghost for a favor from the court below.* The thought arrived fully formed, seductive and vile, and he made himself look at it, really look, the way he made himself look at his worst survey errors — because a mapmaker who flinched from bad terrain killed the people who trusted the map.

No. Whatever else happened, he would not become a man who paid his debts with other people's souls. His mother had bought his life with his self; he would not balance her books by spending someone else's.

Which meant the road ran down. Through the unfinished third of her last map, past the place where her ink had given out, into the court that held his name in its too-many-fingered hand.

Yara stood at his worktable until the candle burned down to a thumb's width, and then he did the only thing that had ever made the world make sense. He took down a fresh sheet of the good paper, ground his ink, and began to draw.

He started with the cellar door at Ravensworth — a house he had never seen, in a village he couldn't find on any chart, and yet his pen knew the door's proportions the way his feet, in certain dreams, knew certain staircases. He drew the door, and the passage behind it, and the river that flowed uphill, working from memory that wasn't memory, and as the lines accumulated he felt the hook behind his ribs shift from pulling to *guiding*, gentle as a hand on the small of his back.

He was halfway down the first staircase when he heard the knock.

Two short taps and a long one. On the street door of a shop that had no reason to receive visitors at the hour past midnight, in a city where temple knights had recently begun asking questions about answered prayers.

Yara set down his pen with great care. His heart was slamming, but his mind had gone clear and cold and fast, the way it did in the market when the watch came through — three years of practiced smallness rising up like a tide. He banked the lamp. He slid the contract into the oilcloth, the oilcloth into the false bottom, the letter into his shirt, against his skin, where it lay cold as a debt.

Then he stood in the dark of his workshop, surrounded by the maps of his inheritance, and listened to the silence where the knock had been, and understood with absolute certainty that whoever — whatever — had knocked was not going away.

Ten weeks, his mother had promised. Ten weeks before the collectors came.

The knocking came again. Two short taps and a long one.

Someone had read the same letter he had. Someone — or something — had learned how his mother used to knock.
