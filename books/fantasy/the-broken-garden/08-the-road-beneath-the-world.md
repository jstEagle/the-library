## 8. The Road Beneath the World

The mine mouth opened in the hillside like a wound that had never learned to close.

Yara stood before it with his satchel of maps pressed to his chest and felt the dark breathe out at him — cold, mineral, older than Grayharbor's oldest stones. Behind him, Sef struck flint to her lantern and the wick caught with a sound like a held breath released.

"You said you mapped this," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"From dreams." He heard how it sounded. He'd had three days to get used to how it sounded, and it still sounded like madness. "Every night for a year when I was nineteen. I'd wake with graphite on my fingers and these passages in my head, and I'd draw them before they faded. I thought they were invented. I thought all my maps were invented."

"And now?"

"Now I think I was dreaming with my eyes open into places that were already there." He touched the mine's timber frame, rotted soft as bread. "This brace. I drew it with a crack running left to right, here—" his finger found the crack, exactly there, "—and I remember being embarrassed that I'd drawn something so ugly."

Sef looked at him a long moment. The lantern light made her face half statue, half stranger. Then she shouldered her pack and stepped past him into the dark.

"Then lead," she said. "And if your dreams were wrong about anything, Yara Ravensworth, I want to know immediately."

* * *

The first mile was ordinary darkness. He could have navigated it blind — indeed, he nearly did, because the dream-maps in his satchel showed passages that matched the real ones so precisely he stopped checking after the third junction. Left where the water stain spread like a hand. Right where the ceiling dipped low enough to graze a tall man's crown. Straight on through the gallery where the support beams had been carved with miners' marks: little suns, little ships, a child's name scratched over and over until the wood went fuzzy.

*Elsbeth. Elsbeth. Elsbeth.*

Sef ran her thumb across the last one as she passed. "Someone waited here a long time," she said quietly.

"Or someone was waited for."

The river found them an hour down. It came out of a fissure in the wall no wider than a coffin, black and quick, and it did not pool or spread but ran — and Yara stopped dead when he saw which way it ran.

Uphill.

It climbed the passage's gentle slope without turbulence, without argument, a ribbon of night moving against every law he'd ever charted. His hands knew what to do before his mind caught up: he had the map out, the dream-map, and there it was in his own nineteen-year-old graphite — *the river runs wrong; do not drink; do not look too long.*

"Do not look too long," Sef read over his shoulder. "Charming. What happens if we do?"

"I don't know. I wrote it before I knew why." He folded the map away. "I have a theory now. Everything down here is memory. Stone remembers. Water remembers harder. If the river is flowing uphill, maybe it isn't moving at all. Maybe it's *returning*."

"To where?"

"To whoever remembers it first."

They walked beside it. The going was easy at first — the miners had cut a towpath, and their lamp-niches still held stubs of tallow gone to amber stone — and Yara found himself talking, because the dark demanded a voice or it filled itself with other sounds. He told her about learning letters from harbor charts. About his mother teaching him to hold a pen like a bird, loose enough that it could fly. He had never spoken of her aloud to anyone; the words came out strange, unused, like coins from another country.

Sef listened the way she did everything — completely, without mercy or comfort. Once, when he faltered, she said only, "Go on," and he did.

In return she gave him pieces of herself, grudgingly, like a miser paying a debt. Her sister's name was Wren. She had been sworn at fourteen. She had believed, truly believed, for eleven years, and belief lost was worse than love lost because at least with love you got to be angry.

"The temple teaches that wonder is a debt the world owes and we must collect it carefully," she said. "That magic is a loan taken out against human souls. I believed it. I *enforced* it." A pause. The river whispered uphill beside them. "Then I saw a god answer a prayer in a plague village when I was twenty-two, and the temple's own liturgy says gods don't answer anymore, and I stood there between two truths and one of them had just saved a child's life."

"What happened to the village?"

"Burned. For the miracle." Her voice didn't change at all, which was how he knew it cost her. "I reported it. That's the worst part. I wrote the report myself. They stripped my rank anyway — not for the burning, for *doubting the report*. For writing that the child had been healed by something with a face."

The passage narrowed. Their shoulders brushed in the dark, once, twice, and neither of them moved away.

* * *

By the second day — if it was days; the dark kept its own calendar — the world began to thin.

Yara noticed it first in the stone. The walls had stopped being entirely wall. Light passed through them sometimes, a green underwater shimmer, and within the shimmer were shapes: a kitchen, a woman kneading bread, a dog asleep by a stove. Not ghosts. *Memories.* The rock had soaked up centuries of the living like a sponge and now, pressed by their passage, it wrung them slowly out.

"They leak," Yara said, hushed. "Memories leak out of the stone."

A miner's last morning played across the wall as they watched — the taste of onions, the weight of a lunch pail, a wife's voice saying *mind the low gallery, love*. Then it guttered and was stone again.

Sef reached out and laid her palm flat against the rock. Her face went slack. When she pulled away her hand was shaking.

"What did you see?" Yara asked.

"A funeral." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Mine. Except I haven't died. Except it was true — the incense, the sisters singing, Wren crying — it was a memory of a funeral that hasn't happened, leaking backward, or—" she stopped. Started again. "This place doesn't care about *before* and *after*, does it."

"No. I don't think time is one of the things that survives down here."

They walked on more carefully after that, touching nothing. But the memories came whether invited or not. Warmth against Yara's ankle like a cat: a summer he'd never lived, someone else's childhood, haysmell and churchbells. A smell of ink that made his throat close, because it was *her* ink, his mother's, iron-gall and honey, and it rose out of the stone in a place where no one had ever written anything at all.

Once, Sef stumbled and he caught her arm, and for a moment the contact between them blazed with borrowed memory — not theirs, some long-dead lovers in this same passage, a hand catching an elbow in the dark exactly so — and they broke apart as if scalded, and walked ten paces apart for an hour, and then drifted back together because the dark was vast and they were small.

"We should sleep in shifts," she said.

"We've been sleeping in shifts."

"I mean properly. One watches. One sleeps deep enough to dream." She hesitated. "Your dreams are maps. Mine are just weather. So you sleep first."

He slept, and dreamed of the garden — always the garden now, petrified roses, paths his feet remembered — and woke to find Sef sitting very straight, lantern trimmed low, watching the wall where a memory of two children playing knucklebones repeated itself over and over, silent, like moths against glass.

"Your turn," he said.

She lay down stiffly, armor unbuckled piece by piece, and he watched over her and discovered that watching someone sleep in a place where the stone remembers things is a dangerous education. Because the stone began to remember *him*. Small things leaked up around her sleeping form: the way she checked a room's exits before she sat down; the exact pitch of her laugh, which he had heard exactly once, three days ago, and had thought about perhaps four hundred times since.

The stone noticed him noticing. And somewhere far below, something turned over in its sleep.

* * *

On the third day the river widened, and the memories thickened until walking through them was like walking through rain.

They came in gusts now, whole weathers of the past. Grief hit them in a cold front — some family's grief, generations old, a dead father's chair still pushed in at the table — and Yara leaned against the wall and shook while it passed through him. Sef crouched beside him, awkward, then not awkward, her hand between his shoulder blades, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the stone wasn't already saying better.

"How do people live down here?" she asked when it passed. "If any do."

"Maybe they don't remember how." He wiped his face. "Maybe that's the price of the road. You come down carrying yourself, and the mountain takes payment in installments."

"Then we pay attention to what it takes." She looked at him, and the lantern light caught something fierce in her. "When you feel something leaving — a name, a face, a summer — you tell me. We keep each other's inventory. Whatever the mountain steals from me, you hold. Whatever it takes from you, I hold. Agreed?"

It was such a simple thing. Such an impossible thing. To hand someone the contents of your self and say *keep this safe.*

"Agreed," Yara said.

They sealed it the way travelers seal things: he spoke his name to her, full and deliberate — *Yara Ravensworth, son of Maren, cartographer* — and she spoke hers back — *Sef Andric, daughter of Wren-and-the-temple, knight of nothing yet* — and the words sank into the dark like coins into a well, accepted, witnessed.

The river sang to them that night. Not words. Just the sense of words, the shape of meaning without the flesh, and both of them dreamed at the same time, side by side, and woke at the same moment with the same image fading: a door, deep in the mountain, standing open, and light coming out of it the color of a soul-lantern.

"That's ahead," Yara said.

"Yes." Sef was already packing. "And something knows we're coming. It's been getting warmer since midnight. Not temperature-warm. *Attention*-warm."

He felt it too. The memories in the stone had begun to organize themselves around their passage, the way birds go quiet before a storm, or loud before an earthquake. And threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable, rising up the wrong-way river like scent on a current:

His own name.

Not spoken. Remembered. From below, patiently, hungrily, something down in the roots of the world was remembering *Yara Ravensworth* — and each hour it remembered harder, and each hour he felt lighter, as though the remembering were drawing him up out of himself like thread off a spool.

"Sef," he said. His voice sounded far away even to him. "Check my inventory. What am I? All of it. Quickly."

She took his face in her hands, and looked, and her expression went from knight to terror in the space of a breath.

"Your eyes," she said. "Yara — your eyes are turning the color of the river."
