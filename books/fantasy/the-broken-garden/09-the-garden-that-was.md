## 9. The Garden That Was

The river that flowed uphill carried them for two days, and on the third day it stopped carrying them at all.

It simply ended — not in a waterfall or a pool but in a wall of stone so smooth and pale it looked poured rather than carved, the water sliding up its face and vanishing into a seam near the ceiling like smoke drawn into a chimney. Yara stood at its base with his boots in an inch of cold water and felt the map in his head go quiet.

Not empty. Quiet. The way a room goes quiet when someone is listening behind the door.

"This is where my dream-charts end," he said. "Everything past this point, I drew from nothing. Guesswork. Invention."

Sef crouched at the waterline, trailing two fingers through the current. She had not slept well — neither of them had, in the dark where memories leaked out of the stone like damp through plaster — and her braid had come half-loose, silver-streaked hair escaping down her neck. "You said you mapped from dreams."

"I said I mapped from *somewhere*." He touched the pale wall. It was warm. Everything else down here was cold as a dead man's cheek, but the wall was warm as skin, warm as a window in afternoon sun, and beneath his palm he felt — faintly, absurdly — the vibration of bees.

"Yara." Sef's voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was deciding whether to draw her sword or sheathe it. "What is that sound?"

He listened. Bees. Faint and rhythmic, a summer sound, a sound that had no business three miles under Grayharbor's gutters. And underneath it, thinner, almost lost: birdsong. A wren, maybe. Or something shaped like a wren by someone who had only ever heard one described.

"I don't know," he lied, because the truth was that some deep animal part of him knew perfectly well, and was already walking.

His feet decided before his mind did. He turned left along the wall — there was no path, no marking, nothing a sane cartographer would chart — and his boots found purchase on stone as if the stone had been expecting him. Ten paces. Twenty. A seam opened in the rock ahead, narrow as a coffin lid, breathing out air that smelled of rain on hot dust.

"Yara." Sef again, sharper now. "How do you know that's there?"

"I don't." He put his shoulder to the seam and pushed. "But it knows me."

* * *

They came out into light.

Not torchlight, not godlight, not any of the sick phosphorescences of the deep ways, but honest grey daylight, filtered through a canopy of branches that could not possibly exist this far beneath the world. Yara stopped so abruptly that Sef walked into his back, and then neither of them moved for a long moment, because the ruin of Ravensworth spread out before them like a sentence left unfinished.

It had been a great house once. He could see that even in ruin — the bones of it, walls of white stone furred with mineral bloom, archways that had framed views instead of doors. But the house was not what stopped his breath.

The garden was.

Every plant in it had been caught mid-bloom and turned to stone. Not withered — *petrified*, held at the exact instant of fullest flowering, so that roses the size of cabbages strained open forever toward a sun that had never risen here, their petals frozen in the last quarter-inch of opening. Hedges stood in sculpted waves. A climbing thing — jasmine, some part of him supplied, though he had never learned plants — mantled a broken pergola in blossoms hard as iron. And everywhere, threading between the flowerbeds, gravel paths of crushed white stone, immaculate, unswept for a century, yet holding no leaves, no dust, no decay.

"It's beautiful," Sef said, and her voice was strange. He glanced back and saw her standing very still, one hand pressed flat over the temple sigil at her breast — the sigil of an order that had cast her out — and her eyes were wet.

"You recognize it?"

"No." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at herself. "No. It's just—" She shook her head. "My grandmother kept a garden. Before the temple took me. Roses. I was six." She looked away, up at the impossible canopy. "I haven't thought about that in twenty years. This place makes you remember things you didn't keep."

Yara turned back to the garden, and the truth rose in him like floodwater: he remembered nothing either. Nothing. No mother singing here, no father's voice, no childhood scuffed knee on these paths. His earliest memory was Grayharbor — fish-rot and tallow-smoke, the night market's paper lanterns, the widow Maren's kitchen. Whatever life had happened in this place had happened to someone else, someone whose name he wore like a borrowed coat.

And yet.

His feet took the second path on the left. He did not choose it; his body simply went, the way water goes downhill, and when he tried to stop and examine the choice, his legs declined to be examined. The path curved past a petrified fountain — dry, its central figure a woman pouring a jug that would never pour — and forked at a sundial whose gnomon had been snapped off, and at the fork his feet went right without hesitation, without doubt, the way a man goes to his own door in the dark.

"Stop doing that," Sef said behind him.

"Doing what?"

"Knowing where you're going." She caught up, sword-hand flexing at her side, and he realized she was frightened — genuinely frightened, more than she'd been at the collector, more than at the uphill river. "You said you'd never seen this place."

"I've never seen this place."

"Then why do you walk it like you planted every stone?" She grabbed his arm and hauled him around to face her, and her grip was iron and her eyes were bright with something between fury and grief. "Yara. Look at your feet."

He looked. The gravel showed his bootprints — and beside them, overlapping, older prints in the same stride, same length, same slight turn of the left toe, printed in the stone itself as if a century of walking had worn his shape into the path and the path had been waiting to match it. Every step he took, the ground rose faintly to meet his sole, worn smooth exactly where his weight fell.

"The paths were made for you," Sef said quietly. "Or you were made for the paths."

* * *

He should have turned back then. Some part of him — the part that had survived three years in Grayharbor by being small, by being forgettable, by selling luck-charms with the true magic folded small inside them like contraband — that part screamed at him to turn around, to find another way to the Undercourt, to let this place stay dead and unnamed.

But the mapmaker in him wanted to chart it. And the orphan in him wanted, desperately, shamefully, to be known.

So he walked on, and Sef walked with him, her hand never far from her sword, and the garden gave up its secrets grudgingly, one turning at a time.

There was a gate of black iron, rusted open, its bars wrought into the shapes of reeds and rushes, and his fingers found the latch without looking. There was a stable block roofed in petrified ivy, and inside it stalls with names burned into the lintels — *Cloud, Bracken, the Mare Called Mercy* — and his throat tightened at *Mercy* with a grief that had no memory attached to it, pure loss with nothing lost. There was a well, capped with a slab, and when he passed it something beneath the slab knocked twice, patiently, like a visitor who would wait.

"Don't answer it," Sef said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were." She said it without accusation, just fact. "You answered the river. You answered the door you painted. You keep answering things, Yara, and one day something will answer back and it won't be polite."

He laughed — a short, unsteady sound. "That's the job. Cartography is just answering doors politely until one eats you."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

They came out of the stable yard onto a long terrace, and there the house showed its face at last: a facade of white stone three stories tall, its windows dark, its great door of black wood hanging by one hinge. Above the door, carved deep into the lintel, a raven spread its wings — and someone had chiseled the raven's eyes out, long ago, deliberately, gouging two raw wounds into the stone.

Sef went pale. "Temple work," she said. "That's defacement. Formal defacement. They only do that to—" She stopped.

"To houses of the old gods," Yara finished. "I know. I've read the edicts." He climbed the terrace steps slowly. "My family weren't just nobles, were they. Whatever Ravensworth was, the temple hated it enough to come down here and blind its symbol."

"Or the temple wasn't the one who blinded it." Sef's voice dropped. "Maybe whatever lived here did it first. Maybe the ravens closed their own eyes."

At the threshold, Yara's feet stopped.

Not hesitated — *stopped*, all motion gone, as if he had walked into glass. The doorway stood open before him, the dark hall beyond smelling of dust and cold hearthsmoke, and his body simply refused. His heart hammered. Sweat broke cold along his spine. Every instinct he owned lay down flat and played dead.

"I can't," he said, and was surprised at how thin his voice came out.

Sef stepped up beside him. She didn't touch him, didn't push, just stood at his shoulder close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, the only warmth in this whole buried country. "You don't have to."

"My feet know every path in this garden, Sef. Every path except into my own house." He stared at the dark doorway. "What does that mean? What kind of person is made for the roads out and barred from the road home?"

"The kind who was sent away," she said softly. "Or the kind who ran."

"I was a baby."

"Then someone ran *for* you." She turned to face him fully, and in the grey light her scarred face — the temple brand still faintly visible on her jaw, the mark of her fall — looked almost tender. "Yara. Listen to me. Whatever this place is trying to tell you, you don't have to take it at its word. Houses lie. Gardens lie worst of all — they grow whatever they're fed." She nodded at the petrified roses. "Someone fed this place fear. Or love. Enough of either will freeze a thing mid-bloom."

He looked at her — really looked, the way he looked at a coastline he intended to chart, taking in every feature, every hazard, every hidden depth — and thought: *she is the only true north I have found down here.* Then he thought: *careful.* Then he thought it anyway.

"The contract," he said. "The one with my name on it. My mother signed it the night I was born. If there are answers anywhere—"

"They're below us." Sef nodded at the flagstones. "The Undercourt pulls everything down. Even this." She toed a fallen petal, stone-hard, perfect as the day it froze. "Come on. We'll skirt the house. There'll be a way down from the far terraces — there always is, in places like this. Cellars. Tombs. Old temples built on top of older temples."

She was right, of course. She usually was, about practical things, and it was only the enormous impractical things — gods, souls, the price of love against duty — where he watched her stand at the edge of her own certainty and tremble.

They went along the eastern face of the house, and it was there, in the shadow of a collapsed colonnade, that Yara found the sundial garden.

It was small, walled, secret — a garden within the garden, planted (frozen) with night-blooming flowers that had opened for a moon that never came. At its center stood a low stone bench, and on the bench, laid with deliberate care, was a child's wooden bird.

A raven, carved from pale wood, worn smooth by small hands. Its eyes were two chips of jet. Its wings were folded, patient, waiting.

Yara picked it up and the world tilted.

Not memory — he had none to tilt into — but something worse: recognition. His hands knew the weight of it. His thumb fit into the wear-groove on its breast as if the groove had been carved by his own caresses, ten thousand of them, a childhood of handling that had happened to someone else and left its evidence in his flesh. He turned it over. Beneath the tail, in tiny scratched letters, in a hand he knew intimately because it was the hand from all the old maps, his mother's hand:

*For Yara, who will fly home.*

The bench was warm. The whole little garden was warm. Somewhere above the impossible canopy, impossibly far above, a bird sang — a real one, alive, three miles under the earth — and it sang the phrase over and over, four notes descending, and Yara's mouth formed the melody before his mind caught up, humming it complete, knowing the next verse, knowing the words, words in no language spoken in Grayharbor, a lullaby rising out of him like water from a tapped well.

He sank down onto the bench with the wooden raven clutched to his chest and shook.

Sef knelt in front of him. She didn't say anything for a long time. Then she reached out and covered both his hands — his and the bird's — with one of hers, rough-palmed, sword-callused, steady as a keel.

"You're not losing yourself," she said quietly. "You're finding too much of yourself all at once. There's a difference. Breathe."

"He knew this garden." His voice came out wrecked. "Somebody knew it. Somebody small, somebody who sat on this bench, and I have his hands, Sef, I have his *hands*, and I don't have anything else. Where did he go? What happened to him that left nothing but the shape of his walking?"

"Nothing happened to him." Her grip tightened. "He's here. He's the man who painted a door on a wall and walked through it. He's the man who paid double at the river so I'd keep my sister's face." She paused, and something crossed her expression — quick, quickly mastered — at the mention of the toll. "Whoever he was, he became you. That's not erasure, Yara. That's just growing."

"And if growing was the theft? If someone took a Raven child and made him small on purpose, made him *forgettable* on purpose, so the collectors wouldn't find him — and now the debt's come due and the hiding is over and everything he buried is blooming at once?" He lifted his head. The lullaby-bird had gone silent. "Sef. My mother didn't sell my soul to save me from poverty. She sold it to make me *no one*. To bury me so deep that even I couldn't dig myself up."

"Then she loved you the only way she could afford," Sef said. "People do that. Gods do worse." She rose, pulling him gently up with her, and nodded eastward, where the terrace ended in a tumble of shattered steps leading down into a darkness that breathed. "There. The way down. Are you ready?"

Yara looked back once at the garden — at the frozen roses straining open forever, at the blind raven over the door, at the little walled room where a wooden bird had waited a hundred years for hands that forgot it.

"No," he said honestly. "But my feet are."

They descended together, and behind them, soft as snowfall, the first petal in a century loosened from its stone stem and fell.

Neither of them saw it. But the garden did.
