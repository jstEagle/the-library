## 11. Toll

The god of small doors led them out through a gap in the world no wider than a handspan.

Yara went first, folding himself sideways, breath held, and came out on the far side of the mountain's root into air that tasted of iron and old water. Sef followed with the mouse-god cradled against her breastplate like something breakable — which, he had admitted in his small cracked voice, he very much was, now that doors were all he had left.

The passage opened without warning onto the river.

Yara had mapped rivers all his life. He knew them the way other men knew their mothers' faces: the gray arterial veins of Grayharbor, the silver threads on survey charts, the brown flood-marks on cellar walls. He thought he understood what a river was.

This one taught him otherwise in a single glance.

It flowed uphill.

Not violently, not as some carnival trick of reversed current — it climbed the passage's gentle slope with the unhurried confidence of water that had never once been asked to justify itself. It was black where the lantern light touched it and somewhere else entirely where it didn't, and its surface did not ripple so much as *remember* rippling, each wave a ghost of motion arriving slightly after the thing that caused it. The sound it made was not the sound of water. It was the sound of a voice speaking just below the threshold of understanding, continuous, patient, in a language Yara's bones recognized and his mind refused.

"Isolde's Vein," the mouse-god said, from the crook of Sef's arm. His whiskers trembled. "Or it was called that, when there were mouths to call it anything. It runs from the deep courts up to the world, carrying what the deep courts take. Memory, mostly. Sometimes names. Once — " the whiskers stilled — "once it carried a whole childhood, all at once, still warm."

Sef set him down on a shelf of stone well back from the bank. She had not spoken since the garden. Yara had watched her carry her silence like a soldier carries a wounded comrade — carefully, at cost, refusing to put it down. Now she stood at the edge of the black water and looked down into it, and whatever she saw looking back made her jaw tighten.

"How do we cross?" she asked.

"You don't." The mouse-god groomed one paw, not looking at either of them. "You *pay*. The Vein is a toll-road, little knight. Everything that passes pays. That is the whole of its nature and the whole of its law, and even the regent beneath the mountain does not cheat it, because even the regent remembers what it took from *him*."

"Pay with what?"

"What do you think?" The small god's eyes caught the lantern light, black and bright as the river itself. "You are walking through the body of the world, and the world is hungry, and you are full of everything you have ever been. The Vein takes memories. It will take what it judges equal to your crossing. You may choose which memories you offer — that courtesy is ancient and inviolable — but you must offer, and you must mean it, and you must not look back at what it keeps."

Yara felt the hook behind his ribs twist. He had lived three years by being small, by keeping his true self folded away like contraband charts beneath loose floorboards. And now the road itself demanded he open those floorboards and let the world pick through him.

"And if we refuse?" Sef asked.

"Then you stand on this bank until you forget why you came," said the mouse-god, "which is, I am told, a very peaceful way to end. The Vein is patient. It has never once needed to be cruel."

* * *

They walked the bank for an hour before choosing the ford.

That was how Yara thought of it, anyway — though the Vein had no fords, only places where the toll was lighter, where the water ran shallower over stones that had themselves forgotten they were stones. He found it by instinct, by the same dream-knowledge that had carried him through the collapsed mine: a place where the black water thinned to the depth of a boot-sole and the voice beneath it dropped to a murmur, almost shy.

He knelt at the edge and unrolled a scrap of oilcloth and began, because his hands needed work or they would shake, to sketch the ford. The pen moved and the lines came out wrong — too knowing, too certain, the strokes of someone drawing a place he had already stood rather than one he was seeing for the first time. He stared at the page. Somewhere under his sternum, the homesickness stirred again, that terrible unnamed ache, and for a moment he was certain he had crossed this river before, in a body smaller than memory could hold.

"Yara." Sef's voice, low. "What do we give it?"

He capped the pen. "Something we can spare."

"There's no such thing." She crouched beside him, forearms on her knees, and for once the temple-knight's armor of composure had a crack in it, and what showed through was young and frightened and furious about being frightened. "I've been cataloguing my whole life since the garden. Every memory I can't lose. My oath-day. My first watch. The face of every person I failed when I fell." Her laugh was short and bitter. "Do you know what I have left, Yara? After the fall? A handful of things. And now the road wants its cut."

"The road wants *a* cut," Yara said. "We choose which."

"We choose which wound to reopen. That's not mercy. That's just surgery without the sleep."

He wanted to touch her. He noticed the wanting the way he noticed weather — constantly, helplessly, from a prudent distance. Instead he said, "Give it something old. Something already scarred over. It takes what you offer truly; the mouse-god said so. So offer something that won't bleed."

"And what would you give, cartographer? Something already scarred over?" Her eyes searched his face. "You don't have any of those. I've watched you. Everything you remember, you remember *whole*."

He had no answer for that, so he gave none, and the silence between them filled with the river's subterranean murmur, and neither of them moved away.

* * *

Sef paid first.

She chose the spot herself, waded in to mid-shin with her boots on because — she said, with the ghost of her old dry humor — if she was going to be robbed she'd be robbed with dignity. The black water rose around her calves and the murmuring voice rose with it, and Yara saw her spine straighten the way it did before she entered any room where she expected to be judged.

"I offer," she said formally, and Yara realized she was using the cadence of testimony, the temple liturgy of witness, because it was the only honesty she had left that fit the shape required. "I offer freely, and I mean it."

The river listened.

Then it reached into her.

There was no other word for what he saw. Nothing touched her; nothing visible happened at all. But Sef's face changed — emptied — refilled — the way a room changes when someone walks through it taking one thing on their way out. She swayed. Yara lunged forward and caught her arm, and her weight against his hands was real and cold and shaking.

"It's done," she whispered.

"Sef. What did it—"

"Don't ask me that." Her eyes were wet and she clearly did not know why. "Don't. If you tell me what's missing, I'll spend the rest of my life feeling the shape of the hole. Just—" she steadied herself against his grip, then, deliberately, let go of it, "—just tell me later. When it matters. Tell me I chose it."

They crossed together after that, his hand at her elbow, the water pulling faintly at their ankles like a customs officer's fingers, curious, restrained. On the far bank Sef sat down hard on a stone and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

"I can't remember my sister's face."

The words came out flat, factual, a report filed against herself.

"She was real. I know she was real. Mira. Younger than me by two years. I taught her to swim in the temple cisterns when we were children, before I was taken for training. I know all of that the way I know the Articles of Severance — perfectly, from the outside. But her face—" Sef's hands lowered. She looked at Yara with an expression he suspected she would have killed a man to keep off her face in daylight. "It's gone. Not blurred. *Gone*, like a page cut cleanly out of a book, with the binding stitched closed after so you'd never know. Except I know. I know there was a face there, and I loved it, and now when I reach for it there's just — smoothness."

Yara sat down beside her. The stone was cold through his coat. Below them the Vein murmured on, climbing its impossible slope, carrying somebody's childhood somewhere warm.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it so completely that it embarrassed him.

"So am I." She laughed, hollow. "It's such a small thing to lose. People lose more every day in Grayharbor. Children lose parents. Whole streets lose their names when the tax rolls burn. One face, out of all the faces I've known—"

"It wasn't small."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It wasn't."

* * *

They made camp in a niche above the bank, a fold in the rock that the mouse-god pronounced safe — "the Vein doesn't climb, and nothing else here has the patience" — and took turns sleeping, because Sef insisted, because some disciplines survive even the loss of the reasons for them.

Yara took the second watch.

He sat with his back to the stone and watched the black water climb and thought about the shape of a hole in a woman's memory, stitched closed so neatly she'd have never known except that love leaves scars on the inside. And he thought: *it takes what you offer truly.* And he thought: *but who audits the offering?*

Because here was the thing he had not told her, the thing he had understood kneeling at the ford while his pen drew lines too knowing to be innocent: the Vein was a river, and rivers run both ways if you know the country. He had mapped this water once — long ago, in ink that wasn't fully his own, in a child's hand guided by a mother's desperate craft. Isolde's Vein, drawn in a margin of one of her charts, annotated in her tight slanted script: *toll negotiable at the shallows; payment binds the payer, not the payee; a debt shared is a debt halved.*

A debt shared is a debt halved.

And its corollary, which his mother had not written but which any cartographer worth his salt could derive: a debt *taken on behalf of another* is a debt doubled — and doubled debts buy doubled passage. Or, if you were clever, and Yara had survived three years in Grayharbor by being very clever indeed:

Doubled debts could purchase *exemptions*.

He waited until Sef's breathing slowed into true sleep. Then he took out his pen, and the little knife he used for cutting nibs, and opened his own palm, and let three drops of blood fall into the black water — because the Vein, like every honest toll-taker, accepted signature as well as coin.

"I offer," he whispered, in the dark, in the old words his mother's margins had taught him without his ever remembering learning them. "Freely. And I mean it twice."

The river stopped.

For the length of ten heartbeats the entire underground held still — the water frozen mid-climb, the murmur silent, the darkness itself seeming to lean in. Yara knelt at the bank with his bleeding hand outstretched and felt the attention of the Vein settle on him the way a magistrate's gaze settles on a petitioner: vast, unhurried, amused.

*Little raven,* said the voice beneath the water, and now he heard it plainly, and it sounded like his own voice played backward, and it sounded like his mother's, and it sounded like neither. *You wish to amend another's toll.*

"I wish to restore what was taken."

*Restore.* The amusement deepened. *Restoration is not among our services. We are a river, not a temple. We take. We keep. We do not give back.*

"Then I'm not asking for restoration." Yara kept his voice level, the way he did across a market stall when a buyer tried to haggle him below cost. "I'm offering a second payment. Equal to hers. Freely given, on her account. Your own law: a debt taken on behalf of another binds the payer double. Take my memory of her sister instead of hers. Let her keep the face. Give me the hole."

The silence stretched.

*You understand,* the river said at last, softly, almost tenderly, *that you are volunteering to be robbed. That you will wake tomorrow and feel the smoothness where a face was, and know there was love there, and not know whose.*

"Yes."

*And that she will never know what you paid. That you are doing this in the dark, while she sleeps, so that she cannot refuse it, cannot thank you, cannot weigh it against her honor and find it unbearable. That you are stealing her debt the way a thief steals gold — except what you steal is her grief.*

Yara's throat tightened. "Yes," he said again.

*Why?*

And because the river was old and honest in its way, and because lying to running water seemed both futile and blasphemous, he told the truth.

"Because she burned her warrant for me. Because she gave up her name and her order and her god to walk into the dark at my side. Because she lost her sister's face paying *my* toll, on *my* road, toward *my* debt — and I cannot fix any of the large things, but this one, this one small theft, this I can undo." His voice cracked and he let it. "Because I love her. There. Entered into the record. Now take the payment before I regain my senses."

The water laughed.

It was not an unkind sound. It was the sound rain makes on a roof when the drought finally breaks.

*Oh, little raven,* said the Vein. *Ninety-one years I have taken tolls from kings and corpses and gods, and never once has anyone volunteered. They bargain. They weep. They offer the memories they least value and are astonished when I decline. But you — you come to the water in the dark and try to cheat me by overpaying.* The murmur gathered itself, delighted. *Very well. The law is the law. A debt taken on behalf of another binds the payer double. I accept your signature. I accept your blood. And I will take — *

The river reached into him.

* * *

He woke at dawn-that-was-not-dawn, in the lightless deep, with Sef shaking his shoulder and saying his name in a voice pitched between irritation and fear.

"—third time. Yara. *Yara.*"

"I'm here." His mouth tasted of copper and river-water. His palm was healed, which it should not have been, sealed smooth as if the cuts had never been. "Bad dream. I'm fine."

"You went grey in your sleep. You were talking to someone." Her eyes narrowed. "In the old tongue."

"I talk in my sleep. Ask anyone I've ever camped near. It's a curse of the profession — maps leak into dreams."

She didn't believe him. He could see that she didn't believe him, see the temple-trained suspicion banked behind her exhaustion like coals. But she was also, he realized, too tired and too raw to press, and something else besides — something gentler. She looked at him the way she had looked at the petrified garden: as if he were a ruin she couldn't decide whether to excavate or protect.

"Eat," she said finally, and thrust half a ration bar at him, and turned away to strike the camp, and that was the end of it.

Except.

Except that all that day, as they followed the uphill river deeper, Yara kept reaching for something and finding smoothness. He noticed it the way you notice a missing stair in a familiar staircase — a lurch, a stumble of the mind, over and over. There had been someone. Someone Sef loved, someone whose face had been cut out of the world's ledger and pasted into his, and he knew this with absolute certainty and could not have said how he knew it, because the knowledge of the theft had been part of the payment, and the payment was spent.

But he remembered deciding. He remembered the dark, and his blood on the water, and the word *yes* said twice.

And when Sef paused at noon on a ledge above the climbing black water and went suddenly, quietly still — and then smiled, small and wondering, at nothing, at the middle distance, at a face only she could see — Yara looked at her and felt something in his chest crack open like ice in spring.

"What?" she asked, catching him watching.

"Nothing," he said. "You looked happy. For a second."

"Did I." She frowned at herself, puzzled, pleased. "I was thinking about — hm. I don't know what I was thinking about. Someone. I think someone taught me to swim once." She shook her head, laughing at herself. "The river's still in my head. Come on, cartographer. The water's getting faster."

It was. All afternoon the Vein quickened, its murmur rising toward speech, and the passage widened around it into vaulted dark, and the air grew warm and thick with the smell of roots and candle-smoke and something older underneath, something like a held breath. Twice Yara saw lights far ahead — not lantern-light, not fire. A soft gold glow, dozens of points of it, hanging in the blackness like fruit on a tree, swaying gently in a wind that did not reach them.

The mouse-god, riding in Sef's pack with his head poking out, had gone silent hours ago. Now he spoke, and his small voice was stripped of all its earlier hospitality.

"That's the court," he whispered. "Those are the lanterns."

"Lanterns of what?" Sef asked.

The little god did not answer her. He looked up at Yara instead, and his black eyes were wet, and full of a pity so old it had worn smooth.

"Of souls, raven-child," he said. "They hang the sold ones like lanterns. And one of them, little mapmaker — one of them has been waiting ninety-one years to be lit."

Ahead, the river plunged over a lip of black stone into roaring golden dark, and the warmth rose to meet them like an embrace, and far below — impossibly far, at the bottom of all the world — something vast and patient turned toward the sound of their coming, and began, with the slow delight of a creditor seeing his debtor crest the hill,

to smile.
