## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The river stopped flowing uphill at the mouth of the world.

Yara felt it before he saw it — a stillness in the water, as if it had arrived where it had always been going. The current that had carried them for three days through stone that remembered other people's lives simply laid itself down, wide and glass-black and silent, spreading into a lake that had no far shore.

"Is this it?" Sef asked. Her voice came out hushed, though there was nothing to wake. She stood hip-deep in the shallows where their path ended, one hand on her sword hilt out of habit rather than hope. Three days underground had worn her down to essentials: the blade, the oath she'd burned, and him.

"This is the door," Yara said. "There isn't one."

He knelt at the water's edge and unrolled the last of his mother's maps — the one he'd been afraid to look at too long, the one whose ink moved when he wasn't watching. It showed the lake, and beneath the lake, drawn in lines so fine they seemed less like penwork than like veins, the shape of what waited below. A cathedral. Roots. Light that was not light.

"Your mother drew this," Sef said. Not a question. She'd learned to read his silences faster than his words.

"She drew everything else from memory or dream. This one—" He turned the map over. On the back, in handwriting smaller than any he'd ever managed, was a single line: *It will know you are coming. Let it.* "This one she drew from the other side."

Sef said nothing for a long moment. Above them — if *above* still meant anything here — the collapsed mine shaft they'd descended was a memory of gray light, days gone. Below them was everything else.

"How do we get down?" she asked finally. "I don't swim in water that doesn't move."

"We don't." Yara rolled up the map and pressed his palm flat against the surface of the lake. It was warm. That was the worst part, somehow. Water this deep under a mountain should have been cold as a grave. Instead it felt like skin left too long in the sun. "We knock."

He didn't know how he knew. His hand simply made the gesture — two knocks, then one, then two again — a rhythm older than his hands, older than the language he thought in. The lake shivered. Rings spread outward from his palm, but wrong, concentric circles collapsing *inward*, toward some center beneath them, and the black water began to sink.

Not drain. Sink — the whole lake lowering like an elevator of darkness, carrying them down with it, until rock closed overhead and there was no more world at all, only descent.

* * *

They fell for an hour, or floated, or were lowered; Yara could never afterward have said which. The dark around them was not empty. It was *occupied*. He felt presences sliding past in the blackness — not collectors, not yet, but things like the space a collector would leave behind when it moved. Once, something brushed his ankle with what might have been curiosity. Sef's hand found his in the dark and did not let go, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it would have required deciding what it meant, and neither of them was ready to decide anything except *down*.

Then light.

It came all at once, from everywhere, and Yara understood in a single lurching heartbeat why his mother had written *let it* instead of *be brave*. No courage would have helped. What opened beneath them was beauty, and beauty was the trap.

The Undercourt was a cathedral built by no architect. Its pillars were roots — vast, pale, spiraling roots of some tree that must have been dying or dreaming since before the mountain above it had a name — and they rose hundreds of feet into a vaulted dark, branching, interweaving, holding up nothing and everything. Between the roots hung the lanterns.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They filled the cathedral the way stars fill a desert sky, each one a soft globe of light suspended from a root-branch by a stem of shadow, and each one burning with a color that meant something. Amber. Green. A blue so deep it hurt. They swayed very slightly, all together, as if breathing, and the light they cast pooled on the root-floor below in slow-moving tides.

"Oh," Sef breathed. Her sword-hand had gone slack. "Oh, it's—"

"It's people," Yara said.

The word dropped between them like a stone into the lake they'd left behind.

"Look closer." His voice came out flat, because if he let it do anything else he was going to be sick. "Look at what they're made of."

She looked. He watched her look — watched the awe on her face curdle exactly the way his own had, watched the knight's training come back into her spine vertebra by vertebra. Because now that you saw it, you couldn't stop seeing it. Each lantern held a shape. A figure, small, curled, luminous — a person folded up inside their own light like a moth in amber. And the colors weren't decoration. They were *lives*. The amber ones glowed with hearth-warmth, with harvests and weddings and children grown old. The green ones pulsed slow, like fields, like patience. One near them burned a fierce violent red, and when Yara looked at it he tasted blood and heard a crowd roaring, and understood that somewhere a soldier had sold the life where he died a hero, and that life was hanging here, burning, waiting for a buyer.

"They're not imprisoned," Sef whispered. "They're *merchandise*."

"The court doesn't keep souls in jars." Yara's mother's map had told him this much, in its moving ink. "It keeps them lit. A soul in the dark is just grief. A soul on display is inventory."

They touched down on the root-floor without any sensation of landing — one moment falling, the next standing, as if the ground had always been beneath them and only politeness had kept it away. The floor was smooth and faintly warm and gave slightly underfoot, like flesh pretending very hard to be stone. Overhead the lanterns went up and up into the dark, a sky of stolen lives.

Sef drew her sword. Yara didn't stop her. There was nothing to fight and they both knew it, but the weight of the blade in her hand seemed to steady her the way the map steadied him.

"Which way?" she asked.

He almost laughed. Almost. "You're asking *me*? I'm a mapmaker standing inside the one place no map has ever survived."

"You're a mapmaker," she said, "standing inside the place your mother mapped from the other side. So. Which way?"

He took out the last map and held it open, and the ink began to move.

Lines crawled across the parchment like roots growing in fast-forward, sketching the cathedral around them — pillar after pillar, gallery after gallery — and then, branching off from the great central nave, a single corridor drawn in gold that hadn't been gold a moment ago. At its end, the ink formed a small symbol Yara recognized from a hundred charts: the cartographer's mark for *here you are known.*

"It wants us to go left," he said.

"Maps don't want things."

"This one does. Everything down here wants something. That's rather the point of the place." He folded the map away. "Left, then. Quietly. We're not guests. We're inventory that walked in on its own legs, and I'd rather they didn't notice that too quickly."

But the lanterns noticed.

He felt it as they walked — a pressure of attention, soft as candlelight on skin. As they passed beneath the hanging lights, the nearest ones brightened. Just slightly. Just enough. A lantern of quiet green flared as Sef passed under it, and Yara saw her stumble, saw her press a hand to her chest, saw her lips move around a name she didn't say aloud. When he caught her elbow she was crying without knowing it.

"What was that?" she rasped.

"I don't know. Something that was yours once? Something that wanted to be?" He guided her onward, faster now. "Don't look up. Whatever you feel, don't look up."

"That's easy for you to say."

"It's not," he said, and did not explain, because ahead of them, past the last rank of pillars, the nave opened into the heart of the court, and there — alone among ten thousand burning lives — hung a lantern that gave no light at all.

* * *

It stood apart from the others, in a niche of roots shaped almost like an alcove, almost like a shrine, almost like a cradle. Every other lantern in the cathedral burned. This one did not. It hung from its stem of shadow like a lamp with no flame, a globe of gray glass, and inside it curled a figure that was barely a figure at all — a person drawn in outline, in negative, a silhouette of absence. Dim. Unlit. Waiting.

Yara's feet stopped moving. His body decided before his mind did, the way it had known every path through the ruined garden of Ravensworth, the way it had knocked on the lake in a rhythm he'd never learned. Some part of him had always known this thing was here. Had been walking toward it for twenty-six years.

"No," Sef said. She'd seen his face. "Yara. Whatever you think that is—"

"It has my chin." His voice sounded far away to himself. "Look at it. The way it's folded. I sleep like that. I've always slept like that, with my knees up, like I'm trying to take up less room, and I never knew why, and *it knows why*—"

"Yara."

"That's my soul, Sef." He was walking toward it. He couldn't not walk toward it. "That's what she sold. That's the shape of me, the whole of me, hanging in a cellar under the world while I walked around Grayharbor for three years being small enough to survive."

The niche received him. Up close, the gray glass was warm, like the lake, like everything down here — the Undercourt had the temperature of a living body and Yara was beginning to understand that this was not a metaphor. He raised his hand toward the lantern and stopped a finger's width from the surface, because some animal caution in him said that touching it would be like touching an open wound, his own, from the inside.

Through the glass, the dim figure stirred.

It turned its head. It had no face — just the suggestion of one, features sketched in the way an artist blocks out a portrait before committing to eyes — but Yara felt it look at him anyway, felt the attention of it like a hook settling gently behind his ribs, in the exact spot where the answered prayers had caught him weeks ago in Grayharbor. The widow's candle. The blue flame. All of it had been this — this lantern, feeling him approach, calling him home the only way a soul can call: by making the world strange until he followed.

"Hello," Yara whispered. Absurd. Insane. Speaking to a bottle in a basement. But the figure raised one dim hand and pressed it against the inside of the glass, opposite his, and the warmth of it came through like a fever, and Yara's vision blurred. "I'm coming back for you. Do you understand me? I don't care what it costs. I'm coming back."

Behind him, Sef had gone very still. He heard her breath change — the sharp intake of someone who has just realized the tactical situation is worse than reported.

"Yara," she said quietly. "How many lanterns did you count?"

"What?"

"When we walked in. How many?"

"I don't — thousands. Tens of thousands. Why?"

"Because when we entered, every light in this place was facing forward. Downward. Toward the nave." Her voice was level, the voice she used for reports, for hard truths delivered cleanly. "They're not facing forward anymore."

He turned.

Every lantern in the cathedral had rotated on its stem of shadow. Ten thousand souls, ten thousand stolen lives, hung in the dark with their faces — the dim, folded, waiting faces of everyone who had ever sold a piece of themselves to survive — turned toward him. Toward the niche. Toward the unlit lantern with his chin and his sleeping posture and his hand pressed against the glass.

And they were brightening. All of them. Slowly, together, like a city waking at dawn, like a congregation rising, like a held breath about to become a shout.

"They know you," Sef breathed. Her sword was up, pointless and brave. "Yara — they *know* you."

At the far end of the nave, deeper than the deepest root, something that had been sitting in the dark for a very long time began — softly, sweetly, in a voice Yara had spent twenty years trying to forget — to hum a lullaby.

His mother's lullaby.

The regent was awake.
