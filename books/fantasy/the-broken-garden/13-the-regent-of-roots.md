## 13. The Regent of Roots

The lanterns led them down.

That was the only way Yara could describe it — the hanging souls of the Undercourt swung gently on their root-cords as he and Sef passed beneath them, each dim flame tilting toward him like flowers toward a sun it had almost forgotten. He tried not to look at them. Looking felt like trespass. Each lantern burned with a life its owner had never lived: he could feel that now, the way you can feel heat from a stove without touching it. A farmer's forty unwritten harvests. A child's unopened letters. Somewhere above them, in Grayharbor or beyond it, people walked around with holes in their chests where these lights had been sold away, and most of them would never know why they woke some mornings grieving nothing.

"Stop looking at them," Sef said quietly.

"I'm not."

"You're counting them."

He was. He couldn't help it. It was what he did — he counted, measured, mapped. And the map forming behind his eyes was obscene: a cathedral of roots vast enough to swallow Grayharbor whole, ribbed with pale wood, floored in soil so black it seemed to drink the light of the thousand lanterns rather than reflect it. There were no walls here, exactly. The roots *were* the walls, braided into arches and pillars and pulpits, grown over centuries by something patient. The air tasted like rain on old stone and, faintly, like candle smoke.

Sef walked with her hand on her sword hilt. She hadn't let go of it since the river. Since the toll. Since she'd stood on the far bank trying to remember her sister's face and found, instead of a face, a shape — a warmth, a name, *Maren*, and nothing else. Yara had watched her stand there for eleven heartbeats before she turned around and said, in a voice scraped flat, "Don't. Don't say anything." So he hadn't. But he carried the weight of what he'd done — paid double, secretly, so that she kept *something* — like a stone sewn into his coat, tender and dishonest, and he suspected she knew anyway. Knights who lose their memories do not lose their instincts.

The path ended at a door.

It was absurdly small after the vastness of the root-cathedral — a door of dark wood set into the trunk of what might have been the mother-tree itself, carved with ravens in flight, and it was exactly, precisely, the door from the house where Yara had been born. He knew this the way he knew his own hands. He had never seen it. His mother's house in Grayharbor had a plain pine door with an iron latch. But this — this was the door of somewhere else, somewhere he had no memory of and yet recognized the way you recognize your own name spoken by a stranger.

"It knows us," Sef murmured.

"It knows *me*," Yara said. "Stay behind me."

"That's not how—"

The door opened before he could touch it.

* * *

She was standing in a room made of amber light and living wood, and she was wearing his mother's face.

Not an approximation. Not a resemblance. It was *her* — the laugh-lines at the corners of her eyes, the small scar above her brow that Yara had kissed goodbye at fourteen when she left for the fishing fleets and never came back, the way she held her chin slightly lifted as if perpetually listening for something just out of earshot. She wore a gown of woven roots and gray silk, and her hair was pinned up the exact way his mother had pinned hers on market days, with two wooden combs, one slightly crooked.

Yara stopped breathing.

"Hello, little raven," the woman said, and even the voice was right — warm, dry-edged, tired in the way of someone who has worked hard all day and is glad to stop. "You've grown. You have her stubbornness, I see. Come in. Both of you. You must be exhausted; the river takes more than it says it does."

"Don't," Sef breathed beside him. Her hand closed on his sleeve. "Yara. That isn't her. Look at the lanterns — look at what this place does with faces. It's bait."

"I know what it is." His own voice sounded far away. His feet were moving anyway.

The chamber inside was round and low-ceilinged, intimate as a kitchen, which somehow made it worse. A table of polished rootwood stood at its center, and on the table lay a single object: a contract, yellowed, sealed with black wax stamped by a raven in flight. The same contract he had found folded among his mother's maps three weeks ago, the one bearing his name in her handwriting — *the soul of my son, freely given, in exchange for his life* — except that here it lay open, and the ink was fresh, and the wax was soft, as if the bargain had been signed yesterday and was still cooling.

"Sit," said the thing wearing his mother's face. "Would you like tea? I keep meaning to learn what tea is. I'm told it's bitter and comforting, which sounds like most human institutions."

"You're not her," Yara said. It came out steadier than he felt. "My mother died four years ago. Drowned off Cape Merren. I lit the candles myself."

"And I am sorry for it," the regent said, and the sorrow in the borrowed voice was so perfectly calibrated that Yara's chest actually ached. "I borrowed her face because it was in the ledger. Everything given to this court stays in the court, little raven — every coin, every secret, every prayer. Your mother prayed to us once, you know. The night you were born. Her prayer is archived two floors below. Would you like to hear it sometime? It's very beautiful. Mothers pray beautifully; it's the one art they're all taught."

"Stop." Sef stepped forward, putting herself between Yara and the table, between Yara and the face. "You'll address the matter of the contract or we leave. We didn't come for reminiscence."

The regent looked at her — really looked, and for a moment the mother-warmth drained out of the eyes and left something older underneath, something with the patience of root systems and the appetite of winter. Then the warmth flooded back, seamless, and the regent smiled at Sef as if at a dog that had barked correctly.

"A temple knight. Fallen, aren't you? I can see the shape of the wound. They cut your rank out of you and left the faith, which is the crueler surgery." The regent tilted her head — his mother's head — with curiosity. "You came all this way with him. Through the uphill river. Past the toll. Interesting. Very interesting. But yes — business first. Sit. Please. Negotiations go better seated; standing is for soldiers and funerals."

Yara sat. Sef did not, but she moved to stand at his shoulder, and he felt her there like a wall between him and the deep water.

"The terms are simple," the regent said, folding her hands on the table beside the contract. "They have always been simple. This court runs on equivalence. Nothing is taken; everything is traded. Your soul hangs in my cathedral — you saw it, I think, on your way in. Dim. Unlit. Waiting. It has waited twenty-six years, accruing interest, gathering the weight of the life you didn't live here. To reclaim it, you must offer something of equal weight. No more, no less. The scales beneath this mountain have never once been wrong, and they have never once been kind."

"Equal weight," Yara repeated. "In what currency?"

"Oh, the usual ones." The regent began, absently, straightening the contract's edges — a gesture so precisely his mother's that Yara had to grip the edge of his seat. "A crown weighs much. A god weighs more. A willing soul, freely given, weighs — well. A great deal. More than any of those, in truth, though mortals always assume the opposite. Willingness is the rarest metal in any realm. Most souls come here dragged, or trickled, or sold by others. A soul that walks in on its own feet and says *take me* — that is worth cathedrals."

Silence. In it, Yara heard the lanterns outside humming, a sound like a congregation holding its breath.

"And if I don't pay?" he asked. "If I walk out?"

The regent's smile gentled, which was the most frightening thing it had done yet. "Then the collectors resume their work, and they are less courteous than I. The debt activates, little raven. It activated the night the old magic stirred — the blue candle, yes, we felt that all the way down here, it rang through the roots like a bell. You are a debt coming due, and debts of this size do not simply expire. They collect themselves, eventually, one way or another. I would rather it be the gentle way. I would rather it be *you*, choosing."

"Why?" The question tore out of him before he could dress it in courtesy. "Why the gentle way? Why my mother's face? Why—" he gestured at the room, the amber light, the kitchen-intimacy of it, the trap of it, "—why does a court of roots need to make a man comfortable before it eats him?"

For a long moment the regent said nothing. And then she rose, and crossed the room, and did the last thing he expected: she knelt beside his chair, close enough that he could see that the eyes were wrong after all — not wrong in color, but in depth, like windows painted on a wall, like a map of eyes rather than eyes — and she spoke softly, so softly that Sef leaned in to hear.

"Because I have worn ten thousand faces down here, and yours is the first that ever fit the crown I keep in the dark." Her voice dropped further. "Because your blood opened doors tonight that have been shut since the outlawry, and the roots grew toward you as you descended, and every lantern in my cathedral turned to look at you, and I have ruled this court for nine hundred years and I do not turn things away that the mountain itself reaches for." A pause. The painted eyes held him. "You think you came here to buy back a soul. Perhaps. But the mountain thinks otherwise, and the mountain is older than both our bargains, little raven. Equal weight. Bring me equal weight. And while you search for it — look closely at what your family was. Look in the archive. Ask why a throne of ravens needed to sell its own heir's soul to hide him."

She stood, smooth as water finding its level, and the mother-face resettled into pleasant hostess calm.

"You may stay in the guest galleries," she said brightly. "The roots will feed you. Do not wander past the third archway; the deeper courts are not currently... receptive to visitors. And do come back soon. I find I enjoy negotiating with you far more than I expected to."

At the door, Yara paused. He couldn't help himself. He turned back, and the words came out raw:

"What was her name? My mother. Her real name — the one she had before she was anyone's mother. Did she ever tell you, when she prayed?"

The regent smiled with his mother's mouth.

"She told me everything," she said. "That's what prayers are. Now go. The archive opens at the next bell, and you'll want to read it before you decide anything. Trust me —" and here the borrowed voice dipped, almost tender, almost cruel, "— you will want to know *why* she did it. Everyone always wants to know why."

* * *

The gallery the roots gave them was a hollow in the living wood, warm, dim, hung with dried herbs that smelled of his childhood kitchen so exactly that Yara sat down on the root-bed and put his head in his hands.

Sef barred the doorway with her sword across the frame — useless, probably, against a place like this, but she did it anyway, and he loved her a little for the uselessness of it.

"She wanted you to read the archive," Sef said. "Did you notice that? Every trap I've ever studied tries to keep you from the truth. This one handed you directions."

"I noticed."

"So either it's a second trap laid over the first—"

"—or the truth serves it better than the lie." Yara lifted his head. Outside the gallery's small window, the lanterns burned in their thousands, and among them — he was sure of it, sure the way he was sure of north — one flame had begun, very faintly, to glow. His. Waiting. "Sef. When she knelt down. What she said about the crown, about the mountain reaching for me — she wasn't seducing me. Or not only. She was *afraid*. Did you see it? Something down here wants me for reasons that have nothing to do with a debt, and she doesn't fully control it."

Sef was quiet for a moment. In the herb-scented dark, her hand found his — the first time she had reached for him rather than the reverse — and held on.

"Then tomorrow we read the archive," she said. "And whatever your mother did, whatever your family was — we read it together."

Above them, through miles of root and stone, a bell began to ring, slow and cold, counting down to morning.

And in the archive below, on a shelf of black glass, a prayer waited in his mother's voice — and beside it, filed with terrible neatness, a second document Yara's name had been written on long before he was born.

The seal on that one was not a raven.

It was a crown.
