## 14. Why She Did It

The archive of the Undercourt did not smell of dust.

That was the first wrongness Sef noticed, and she clung to it the way a drowning sailor clings to a spar — because noticing things was what kept her from screaming. The archive smelled instead of turned earth and candle smoke and something faintly sweet beneath it all, like fruit going over in a dark room. It smelled of graves that had been tended lovingly for a very long time.

The shelves rose out of sight into the root-cathedral's darkness, carved not from wood but from living rootwood, pale as bone where the regent's servants had pared them smooth. Scrolls lay in niches like relics. Books with covers of pressed bark. And everywhere, threaded through the shelves like veins through a wrist, thin filaments of silver light pulsed — slow, patient, rhythmic as breathing.

"The court remembers everything," the regent had said, before withdrawing up the aisle on legs that moved like willow branches in wind. "Every bargain struck beneath this mountain since the first stone was laid. Read what you wish, knight. Truth is free here. It is everything else that has a price."

Sef had been reading for an hour when Yara found her.

She heard him before she saw him — his footsteps had a particular hesitation she was learning to catalog, a half-beat pause at every threshold as though he were still asking permission to exist in any given space. Three years of being small, he'd told her once, in the dark of the uphill river. You learn to knock even on doors you own.

He came down the aisle with a lantern-soul's light caught in his face, making him look younger than he was and more frightened than he'd ever admit, and stopped when he saw what she held.

"You found it," he said.

"I found *a* contract." Her voice came out flat. She didn't correct it. "There are thousands here. Tens of thousands. Do you understand what this place is? Every one of these is a person."

"I know what this place is." He said it quietly, without heat, and that quiet was worse than anger. He had known longer than she had. He had grown up knowing, in whatever half-remembered way children know the shape of their own wounds. "Is it mine? The one you're holding?"

"It bears your name."

"Then let me see it."

She should have refused. Later — much later, in another life, in a garden neither of them could yet imagine — she would tell him that she should have refused, should have read it alone first and burned it if burning had been possible, and he would tell her that he'd have hated her forever, gently, the way he hated everything that protected him at someone else's expense. But standing there in the breathing dark with the silver light pulsing through the shelves like a heartbeat too large for any single chest, she turned the scroll toward him and said:

"Sit down first."

* * *

He sat. On the floor, cross-legged, like a boy at lessons, and she realized with a lurch that he had never had lessons — not these kinds, not the ones where you learned who you were from documents. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was the rest of him that shook.

The contract was written in ink that shifted color depending on the angle — brown, then black, then a deep arterial red — and the script was cramped and hurried in places, formal in others, as though its author had begun calmly and finished in terror.

Sef read it aloud, because Yara's eyes had gone strange, unfocused, tracking nothing, and she understood that he needed the words to come from outside himself. Someone else's voice. A witness.

"*In the year of the Grey Interregnum, on the night of the solstice, one Elara Ravensworth, of the House of Ravensworth, called also the Raven Throne, being of sound mind and unsound circumstance, does hereby convey—*"

"Stop." Yara's voice cracked on the word. "*Ravensworth.*"

"You didn't know your mother's name?"

"I knew her as Maera. Maera Vess. She sold charms in Kellin's Cross. She—" He stopped. Started again. "She died when I was nine. Fever. There was no fever, was there. There's never a fever."

Sef looked back down at the page and read on, because stopping now would be mercy, and mercy was a currency this place traded in, and she would not spend it dishonestly.

"*—does hereby convey the whole of the soul of her child, Yara, born this night, being the lastborn of the true line, unto the Court Beneath the Mountain, in perpetuity, in exchange for the following consideration: that the said child be hidden.*"

"Hidden," Yara repeated.

"Hidden," Sef confirmed, and her throat had gone tight around the word. "There's more. Listen. *Let the assassins of the Raven Throne search every cradle in the seven duchies and find it empty. Let their diviners scry for the lastborn's blood and find it veiled. For a soul given below cannot be seen from above; it falls outside every thread they spin. Take his soul, keep it unlit, and the boy will live to grow old in some gray harbor of the world, nameless and unmourned by power, which is the only mourning worth having.*"

The silence afterward was enormous.

Yara did not move. In the silver pulse-light his face had gone the color of the rootwood shelves, and his hands — those steady, steady hands — had closed slowly into fists against his knees, knuckles rising like stones through soil.

"She sold me," he said at last. Not loudly. Almost conversationally. That was the frightening register, Sef thought. The reasonable one. "All this time I thought — I built my whole understanding of her around a fever. Around bad luck. Around the world being cruel at random. And it wasn't random. It was a transaction. She priced me and she signed."

"Yara—"

"No." He was on his feet now, and the lanterns overhead seemed to lean toward him, hundreds of dim souls swaying on their root-cords like fruit stirred by wind. "No, don't do that. Don't soften it yet. Let me be angry for one full minute before you make me grieve. I've earned one minute of anger. I've spent three years being small and I have earned *one minute*—"

His voice broke apart entirely.

And Sef, who had sworn oaths once upon a time about the proper treatment of civilians, about the dignity of strangers, about a dozen beautiful abstractions that had gotten her stripped of rank and sent hunting miracles through a city that outlawed wonder — Sef crossed the aisle and took his face in both hands and made him look at her.

"One minute," she said. "Take two. But then you're going to listen to me, because I've read the whole thing twice, and you've heard four lines of it, and there is a difference between what happened to you and the story you're telling yourself about what happened to you. Do you hear me?"

His eyes were wet and furious and, beneath the fury, already breaking. He nodded once, jerkily, and stepped back, and wrapped his arms around himself like a man holding his own ribs together, and Sef picked up the scroll again.

* * *

"There are annexes," she said. "Addenda. Whoever drafted the main contract was a court clerk — the language is theirs, all that *hereby* and *in perpetuity*. But these sections are different. Look at the hand." She angled the parchment toward him. "This is your mother's writing. I've seen it in your workshop, on the margins of those maps. This is the same woman."

Yara made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "She wrote in the margins of everything. Maps, receipts, the backs of icons. She said blank space was just paper that hadn't decided what it wanted to be yet." His voice wavered. "I used to think that was charming."

"Then listen to what she decided this paper wanted to be."

She read.

"*To the clerk who takes this dictation: write the terms plainly, for plainness protects us both. But add this, in my own hand, so that whoever reads it in later years — and someone will, the court forgets nothing, that is its nature and its curse — knows why.*

"*I bore a son at solstice, and the stars marked him, and my family's enemies marked him faster. You below do not concern yourselves with the politics above, so let me explain what a Raven heir is. He is a vessel. From the moment of his naming, the old gods pour themselves into him, and he becomes a door, and through that door they feed — and what they feed on is worship, and worship is another word for obedience, and obedience is another word for chains. My brother wore the crown for eleven years. I watched what it made of him. I watched him smile at crowds the way a hook smiles at a fish.*

"*My son will not be a door.*

"*So I bring his soul below, where the throne's diviners cannot see it, and I ask you to keep it dark. Unlit. A soul in storage casts no shadow for hunters to follow. Yes, it is a sale — I know the forms, I grew up on the treaties my family signed with this court, I know you cannot simply hold a thing in trust like some charitable institution. Fine. Sell it, shelve it, hang it among your lanterns. Only keep it dark, and keep it safe, and when the boy comes for it — and he will come, he is a Ravensworth, we always come home eventually, God help us — treat with him fairly. That is my price beyond the price. Fair dealing with my son, whoever he turns out to be.*

"*He will hate me. I accept this. Hatred is a living thing; it grows and changes and can be outlived. What I could not survive is his death, or worse — his coronation. Let him hate me from inside a long and ordinary life. Let him curse my name in some gray harbor over cheap wine, and let him be alive while he does it, and let him be free.*

"*That is all. That is everything. Write the terms plainly and I will sign.*

"*— E.R.*"

Sef lowered the scroll.

Yara had not moved. The pulse-light washed over him in its slow tide, and tears were running down his face in complete silence, the way rain runs down glass, without drama, without permission, as though his body had simply stopped consulting him.

"She hid me," he whispered.

"She hid you," Sef said.

"By selling me."

"Yes."

"And it worked." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Sef had ever heard from him, worse than his scream at the collector's door, because it was aimed inward. "It worked, Sef. Three years in Grayharbor selling luck-charms under a false name and no one ever — the temple never found me, the throne's people never came, I thought I was just *good at hiding*, I thought it was my talent, my cleverness, my smallness. And it was her. It was her the whole time. I was invisible because she paid for the invisibility with—" He gestured upward, at the hanging lights, at the whole cathedral of stolen lives. "With *that*. With me."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes like that."

"How should I say it?"

"I don't know." He dragged both hands down his face. "Not like you agree with her. Not like it's simple. Because it isn't simple, is it? Tell me it isn't simple."

"It isn't simple." Sef set the scroll down on the shelf with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to let them. "Here's what I see. I was raised by the temple, Yara. I memorized doctrine before I could write my own name. And doctrine says a soul is inviolable — that no one may sell it, pledge it, or give it away, not for love, not for fear, not for anything. It's the first law. It's the law I broke my vows over, in a way, because I believed the gods deserved the same inviolability." She paused. "But I also had a mother. Briefly. Before the temple took me. And I remember—" Her voice caught, and she pushed through it. "I remember being small and sick with lungfever, and I remember her sitting up with me three nights running, and I remember thinking: she would burn the world down if it would let me breathe. That's what mothers are. They're the ones who burn things."

"So I should forgive her."

"I didn't say that."

"You're building toward saying that."

"I'm building toward telling you what's *true*," Sef said, stepping closer, close enough that the silver light put shadows in the hollows of his cheeks. "You want this to be a betrayal so you can stop loving her cleanly. Betrayal is clean. You cut it out and you're done. But this—" she tapped the scroll, "—this is a woman who chose between two terrible things with no good options, knowing you'd hate her, choosing your hatred over your grave. That's not clean. That's a wound that never closes, and she carried it for nine years, and then she died — or ran, or vanished, or whatever actually happened — carrying it. And now you get to carry it too. Congratulations. It's the inheritance nobody puts in a vault."

Yara stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes — the fury looking for somewhere to go and finding, everywhere it turned, the same exhausted truth waiting.

"She could have told me," he said finally, and his voice was smaller now, younger, nine years old in a gray harbor. "Before the end. She could have told me *anything*. Instead I got a fever and a box of her maps and a life I couldn't explain."

"Maybe she was protecting you right up until she couldn't anymore."

"Maybe she was a coward."

"Maybe," Sef said quietly, "she was exactly as brave as she could afford to be, and it cost her the only thing she had left to lose, which was you."

The anger went out of him then — not all at once but the way a fire goes down, collapsing inward, embers settling, heat still there but no longer roaring. He sank back down to the floor, and after a moment Sef sat beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they had ridden out the river-dark together, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

Above them the lantern-souls swayed in their slow rhythm. Somewhere deeper in the archive, pages turned of their own accord, the court murmuring to itself in its sleep.

"My whole life," Yara said at last, "I thought I was the thing that got thrown away. And I was the thing she was saving." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, like the gesture itself annoyed him. "Both of those are true at once. How does anyone live with both of those being true at once?"

"Badly, mostly," Sef said. "Then better. Then badly again. It's not a problem you solve. It's a weight you carry, and you find out who'll carry it with you." She felt him go still beside her, and immediately wished she could take the words back, and immediately, underneath that, didn't.

He turned his head. Their faces were very close. The light pulsed once, twice, three times, and neither of them moved, and the whole cathedral of roots seemed to hold its breath around them.

Then Yara exhaled and looked away, and laughed shakily, and said, "The regent said fair dealing. Her one condition beyond the price. Which means the regent is bound to treat with me honestly." His jaw tightened. "Which means the regent is going to tell me what this costs. And I already know, don't I. I already know there's no version of this where I just... take it back and walk out."

Sef reached over and took the scroll from the shelf, rolling it closed with deliberate slowness, buying herself a moment before she had to answer — because she had read further than she'd admitted, past the addenda, into the clerk's dry final notation, and there was a line there that had been sitting in her chest like swallowed ice for the past hour.

"Yara," she said. "There's a codicil. About what the court accepts as payment for a returned soul."

He looked at her.

"A crown," she said. "A god. Or a willing soul, freely given." She met his eyes and held them, refusing to look away, refusing to let him look away. "Those are the only weights that balance. And I need you to look at me right now and promise me you're not already doing the arithmetic."

The silver light pulsed.

Somewhere far above them, faint as a memory, Grayharbor's bells rang the second hour of morning, and the sound fell down through a hundred fathoms of root and stone and arrived in the archive thin and broken — like a message from a world that was already forgetting them.

Yara smiled at her. It was, Sef thought, the gentlest and most terrifying expression she had ever seen on a human face.

"I promise," he said, "nothing at all."
