## 15. Equal Weight

The regent's words hung in the root-cathedral air like smoke that refused to rise.

"A crown," she said, in his mother's voice, in his mother's face, wearing a smile his mother had never once worn. "A god. Or a willing soul, freely given. Those are the only currencies the Undercourt honors, little cartographer. Everything else is paper."

Yara's hand found Sef's without his permission. Her fingers were cold — colder than they should have been this deep beneath the world — but they closed around his with a grip like a vow.

"You'll forgive me," he said, and was proud of how steady his voice came out, "if I don't take financial advice from someone wearing my mother's face."

The regent laughed. It was a terrible sound, warm and wrong at once, like hearing a lullaby sung by something with too many teeth. "You have her tongue, too. She bargained the same way. Sweet words over a knife's edge." The borrowed green eyes glittered. "Take your time. The court has waited a hundred years for a Raven to walk these halls again. It can wait a season more."

She turned — and the turn itself was wrong, roots shifting to accommodate her as though the whole cathedral were a garment she wore — and was gone into the dark between the hanging lanterns. A thousand souls swayed gently in her wake, each one burning with the color of an unlived life. Yara did not look up at his own. He had looked once, on their arrival, and had not slept since.

* * *

They retreated to what passed for guest quarters in the Undercourt: a hollow in the living root, floored with soft grey moss, lit by a single lantern-soul that had been assigned to them like a servant. It glowed a gentle amber and hummed faintly, and if you listened too long you could almost hear it dreaming of a harvest it had never gathered, a marriage it had never stood in, a child it had never held.

Sef sat with her back against the root wall, sword across her knees even here, even now. Old habits. Dead oaths. She watched Yara pace the small space the way a hawk watches a rabbit that hasn't yet noticed the shadow.

"Stop walking," she said finally. "You're wearing a groove in the world."

"I'm thinking."

"You're spiraling. There's a difference. Thinking has a floor."

He stopped. He pressed both palms flat against the root wall, feeling the slow pulse beneath the bark, the court's vast heartbeat. "Three options," he said. "She gave us three."

"She gave *you* three. I'm still working out how to kill her."

"Sef."

"Roots burn, Yara. Everything burns. You just need enough fire and no fear of the smoke." But there was no conviction in it, and they both knew it. They had seen what the regent was. Fire was a language spoken by things that lived; whatever wore his mother's face predated fire's invention.

Yara slid down the wall until he was sitting beside her, shoulder to shoulder. The lantern hummed. Somewhere far above, impossibly far, Grayharbor was waking to its gray morning, gulls screaming over fish guts and tar, the widow from the night market lighting a candle that stayed obediently gold.

"A crown," he said. "That means going back up there. Taking the Raven Throne. Unbinding the gods, ruling whatever's left." He turned the idea over like a stone found in a shoe. "I'd be good at it, maybe. Mother's maps say the throne runs on blood. Mine would run on... I don't know. Ink. Doors."

"And you'd spend eternity being exactly what your mother sold your soul to escape." Sef said it flatly, but her hand moved — just slightly — until her smallest finger hooked around his. "Next."

"A god." He almost smiled. "Find one, bind it or bargain it, feed it to the court. Except the gods are either dead, mad, or chained, and the last one we met tried to eat our names."

"The mouse-god liked you."

"The mouse-god wanted me to promise it every door in creation. That wasn't affection, that was investment." He exhaled slowly. "Which leaves—"

"Don't." Her voice changed. All the dryness went out of it, all the deflection, and what remained was bare and sharp as a drawn blade. "Don't say it."

"It's the only one that works."

"It's the only one that *ends* you."

"That's rather the definition of equal weight, isn't it?" He kept his tone light, cartographer's tone, the voice he used for describing terrain to people who would have to cross it. "My soul for my soul. Clean exchange. No thrones, no deicide. One dim lantern goes home to its owner, and the court gets fed for—" he waved a hand vaguely upward, toward the thousand burning lives, "—however long a freely given Raven soul lasts. Centuries, she said. Everyone down here walks free eventually. Every soul in these roots gets its weight back."

"And you get nothing."

"I get my soul back first. That's not nothing. I get to be whole for a while before—" He stopped himself, but the sentence had already escaped, and he watched it land in her like an arrow.

"*Before,*" she repeated.

"For however long 'a while' turns out to be."

Sef moved then, fast, turning to face him fully, taking his jaw in one calloused hand and forcing him to look at her. Up close her eyes were the color of wet slate, and there was something in them he had only ever seen in maps of coastlines about to be eaten by the sea.

"Listen to me," she said. "I burned my warrant for you. I killed men for you. I gave my sister's face to a river for you — do you understand what that means? I cannot picture her anymore. I wake up knowing I loved her and reaching for her face and finding *water.* And I did it because I believed — I still believe — that some things matter more than duty. So hear me when I tell you what matters more than your arithmetic." Her thumb traced his cheekbone, rough and careful at once. "You are not currency. You are not a toll. And if you sign yourself away in some dark corner while I'm sleeping, so help me, Yara Ravensworth, I will find whatever afterlife they give temple knights and haunt you so thoroughly you'll beg for the regent instead."

Despite everything — despite the lantern humming its unlived dreams three feet away, despite the regent's smile still printed on the inside of his eyelids — Yara laughed. It came out cracked in the middle.

"Noted," he said. "No dark corners."

"I mean it."

"I know you mean it. That's the problem." He covered her hand with his own. "Because you're right, and I'm right, and those two things can't both survive this place. One of us is going to be wrong, Sef. I'm just trying to choose which one of us gets to be."

She didn't answer. She leaned forward and rested her forehead against his, and they stayed like that while the lantern dreamed and the roots pulsed and somewhere above them the regent waited, patient as geology, certain as winter.

* * *

He waited until he was sure she slept.

It wasn't hard to tell. Sef slept like a soldier even now — shallow, angled toward the door, sword-hand curled loose around the hilt — but her breathing slowed after a time, and the line of her mouth softened into something younger than the rest of her. In sleep she looked like what she might have been, in a kinder world: a woman who had never been given a god to serve, only mornings to keep.

Yara rose without sound. Three years of being small had taught him how to move through a room like a rumor.

He took out his kit — the same battered leather roll he'd carried since Grayharbor, since before, since always — and unrolled it on the moss. Ink. Chalk. A stub of blood-wax candle. And the thin bone stylus his mother had left hidden in the lining of his first satchel, which he had carried for years believing it was a tool, and which he now understood was a key.

By the light of the dreaming lantern, he began to draw.

Not a map of the Undercourt. He had tried that already, in the first hours after arriving, and the lines had crawled off the page like worms, refusing to hold still. This was smaller. This was a map of himself — or near enough. The shape of a soul, rendered the only way he knew how: as territory. Rivers of memory. Mountains of grief. The little walled garden at the center where a boy had once been happy, before he knew what anything cost.

If the regent wanted weight, he would show her weight. He would make her weigh it in front of witnesses. He would make the bargain formal, contractual, bound in ink the way his mother's contract had been bound — because contracts were the one law even the Undercourt honored, and a poorly drafted contract was the only weapon he had ever been trained to forge.

*A willing soul, freely given.*

Freely. His mother's had not been free — hers had been made under terror, under assassins' shadows, under the weight of a son who would die without her lie. Terror voided terms. He had read enough of her archive, with Sef, to know the court's own rules.

But his would be different. His would be clean. No coercion, no desperation written into the margins. He would wait. He would draft it properly. He would give it *freely*, in full knowledge, with steady hands — and because it was freely given, it could not be contested, and because it could not be contested, Sef could not undo it, and because Sef could not undo it—

Because she could not undo it, she would survive him.

That was the thought he kept returning to, the thought he handled carefully, like a blade by the flat. Not *I want to die.* He didn't. Gods, he didn't — he wanted mornings in Grayharbor, wanted to teach the widow's son to draw coastlines, wanted a hundred small ordinary years with a disgraced knight who kissed him among hanging souls. He wanted everything.

And that was precisely why it would work. A soul given reluctantly was worth less than its weight. A soul given *freely* — a soul that chose love over itself, cleanly, without flinching — that was the rarest coin in any court, above ground or below. It would buy more than his own lantern. It would buy the whole cathedral's freedom, every hanging life, the entire debt his family's dynasty had run up over centuries of feeding.

One soul against ten thousand. The best exchange rate in history.

He told himself it was strategy. He wrote it that way, in the margin of the map, in his neat cartographer's hand: *Optimal conversion. Minimal collateral. Preserves allied assets intact.* Allied assets. As if Sef were a bridge or a supply line. As if the word *love* weren't sitting in his chest like a swallowed star, too bright to look at directly.

The stylus scratched. The lantern hummed. And Yara Ravensworth, last heir of the Raven Throne, drew the borders of his own surrender and called it a plan.

He worked until the candle guttered, then rolled up the map and hid it inside his coat, over his heart, where a soul is supposed to live. Then he lay back down beside Sef in the grey moss, close enough that her warmth reached him, far enough that she wouldn't wake.

Her eyes opened anyway. Of course they did.

"Can't sleep?" she murmured.

"Mapping," he said. It was even true.

"Mm." She shifted, draped one arm over him with the easy authority of someone claiming territory, and pressed her lips to the back of his neck. "Map something soft."

He closed his eyes. He let himself have the warmth, the weight of her arm, the smell of steel and temple-ash in her hair. He let himself have it the way a man spends his last coins on a feast — deliberately, gratefully, knowing the bill.

Above them, in the dark of the root-cathedral, a thousand lanterns burned with unlived lives, and one of them — his own, dim, patient — seemed to flicker slightly brighter, as if it had heard him deciding.

And far below, deeper than the roots, deeper than the river that ran uphill, something old turned over in its sleep and began, very quietly, to count.
