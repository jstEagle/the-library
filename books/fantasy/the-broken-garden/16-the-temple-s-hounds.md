## 16. The Temple's Hounds

The river that flowed uphill sang them to sleep.

Yara woke first, as he always did — three years of selling charms in Grayharbor had taught him that sleep was a loan, not a gift, and the collector always came. But this time the sound that pulled him from the dark wasn't memory or fear. It was light.

It came down the tunnel behind them in long, pale shafts, like sun through water, though there was no sun this far beneath the world and no water but the impossible river running its uphill course past their small camp. The light moved with purpose. It swept the stone walls the way a hand sweeps a shelf, searching for something specific among the clutter.

He knew that light. Every charm-seller in Grayharbor knew it, knew it the way prey knows a particular silhouette against a particular sky. *Judgment-glass*, the temple called it. Light that found what was hidden. Light that asked questions of your blood and waited for the answer to damn you.

"Sef." He said her name quietly, the way you'd say *fire* in a crowded room — not loud enough to cause panic, loud enough to save lives.

She was awake before the second syllable left his mouth. He'd learned that about her too: she slept like a knife in a drawer, all edge and readiness, and came out of dreams without the fog most people carried. She sat up, and he watched her read the light in a single glance, watch her face do something complicated — recognition, dread, and beneath both, horribly, a flicker of longing, like an exile hearing her mother tongue spoken in a foreign street.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Can't tell. Three at least. Maybe more." He was already rolling the maps, already stuffing them into the oilcloth satchel he'd carried since Grayharbor. His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself with distant surprise. Somewhere between the widow's candle burning blue and the door he'd painted onto a wall with his own blood, his hands had stopped shaking when terror arrived. Terror had become a colleague. "They're moving slow. Sweeping."

"They're being thorough." Sef was on her feet, pulling her boots on. Without her armor — left behind, along with her rank and her name in the temple rolls — she dressed like any traveler, and somehow looked less like one than ever. There was a posture to her that no borrowed clothes could soften. "Inquisitors don't sweep unless they have a scent. They don't follow a scent unless they've been given a writ." She paused, and Yara heard the sentence finish itself in the silence between them. *A writ with my name on it.*

"They're not tracking me," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd understood it for days, maybe, and only now let himself say it aloud. "They're tracking you."

"The hounds of the Third Chair don't track outlaws." Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone walking across ice while describing the lake beneath it. "Outlaws get constables. I broke consecration oath. That's not a crime, Yara. That's a wound. And wounds—" she looked up at him, and the searching light caught the edge of her jaw, turned her eyes briefly silver, "—wounds bleed in ways they can follow."

The light swung toward them down the corridor.

* * *

They ran.

There was no dignity in it. Yara would remember it later — in the garden, in the years after, in the quiet hours when memory came to sit beside him like an old dog — not as flight but as flailing: two people stumbling through the dark guts of the world with a satchel of impossible maps and no plan beyond *deeper, deeper, deeper*. The river ran uphill beside them and they ran downhill against it, which felt right in the way that everything down here felt right and wrong at once, the geometry of the Underways agreeing with you only when it suited some older purpose.

The tunnels changed as they fled. Yara felt it through his boots before his eyes confirmed it. The stone had been worked once — not by miners, not by masons, but by hands that had wanted the rock to *mean* something. Ribs of darker stone arched overhead, too regular to be accidental, too organic to be built. Roots, he thought. Roots that had learned architecture. Or architecture that had dreamed of being roots.

"Left," he gasped, and took it, because the map in his head — the one drawn in his mother's hand, the one he'd memorized until it lived behind his eyes like a second set of eyelids — showed a junction here, and past the junction a gallery where the walls sweated cold, and past that—

Behind them, a voice. Not shouting. Worse than shouting. A voice pitched to carry without echo, trained into the stone itself, the way a bell is shaped to hold a note.

*"Sister Andric."*

Sef stumbled. Yara caught her elbow and felt her whole arm rigid as a fence post.

"You are called to account," the voice continued, patient as erosion. "By order of the Third Chair, under seal of the Ninefold Concord. Your warrant stands. Your fall is noted. Your return is—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel finding the seam between ribs, "—desired."

*Desired.* Yara heard the word land on her like a blow. He understood then, with the terrible clarity that danger sometimes granted him, exactly what the temple had done. They hadn't sent soldiers. Soldiers could be outrun, outfought, outlasted. They'd sent a voice that used her name. They'd sent the shape of forgiveness, dangled just ahead of her like a lantern on a pole, so that every step she took away from it would be a step she'd have to explain someday, to herself, in the dark, forever.

"That's Prelate Voss," Sef breathed. Not to Yara. To the air. To herself. "He taught me canon law. He—"

"Is he kind?" Yara asked. "Genuinely?"

She looked at him like he'd asked whether drowning was wet.

"Because if he's kind," Yara said, still running, still pulling her along by the wrist, "then that's the cruelest thing they could have sent. Kindness with a warrant behind it." He thought of his mother — of the faceless, grieving shape of her that lived in the contract bearing his name — and pushed the thought down where it couldn't trip him. "Don't answer him. Anything you say, he'll keep. Words are evidence down here. Everything is evidence down here."

The gallery opened around them like a held breath.

It was vast — a cathedral of stone that no human congregation had ever filled, its floor dusted white with something that might have been salt or ash or the powdered bones of the place's original purpose. Pillars rose into darkness. And along the walls, carved deep and worn shallow by time in alternating measure, ran lines of script in no alphabet Yara knew — except that his feet knew it, and his blood knew it, and when he passed the nearest pillar the carvings warmed against his palm like skin.

"Ravensworth work," he said, before he could stop himself.

Sef stared at him. "You said you never—"

"I didn't. I don't." He pressed his hand flat against the pillar, feeling the warmth pulse faintly, like a heartbeat slowed to geological time. "But my family did. Whatever else is true, whatever else they were — this is theirs. This whole road is theirs. Which means somewhere ahead there's a door only my blood can open, and somewhere behind us there are men who want to put my blood in a vial and study why."

The light found the gallery entrance.

Three figures, and Yara's guess had been nearly right. Three inquisitors in traveling grays, unarmored, unarmed in any way that mattered — because the weapon they carried was the judgment-glass itself, a lens of consecrated crystal mounted on a staff, throwing its pale interrogating beams ahead of them. Two were strangers. The third—

The third was young. Younger than Sef. A woman with close-cropped hair and the raw-knuckled grip of someone holding her staff the way you hold a railing over a long drop. And she was looking at Sef with an expression Yara recognized from mirrors, from market stalls, from every desperate soul who'd ever bought a luck-charm from him: the expression of someone who has run out of options and is praying the last one works.

"Voss isn't here," Sef said quietly. "He sent voices ahead. He's letting the hounds do the hunting."

"Can you talk to them?"

"No." She said it fast. Too fast. Then, slower, watching the three figures advance across the ash-white floor: "Maybe. One of them. The young one — Tamar. She was in my cohort, before—" Her jaw tightened. "Before I chose gods over doctrine and lost everything worth losing."

"And the other two?"

"The other two are what the Third Chair sends when it wants a problem *ended* rather than answered." She drew a breath, and Yara saw her square herself the way he'd seen her do it before every dark threshold: shoulders back, chin level, the knight reassembling herself out of spare parts and stubbornness. "Stay behind me."

"Sef—"

"*Stay behind me.*" And then, softer, without turning, without breaking her stride toward the advancing light: "Please. You're the map. Maps don't bleed."

*Maps don't bleed.* He almost laughed. Almost told her. Instead he did what she asked, and hated it, and followed.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, the way catastrophes do — compressed, bright, over before the mind agrees it has begun.

Tamar reached them first. Ten paces out, she halted, and the other two halted with her, flanking wide with the smooth drilled coordination of people who had done this many times to many people. The judgment-glass beams crossed over Sef's chest like a blessing performed backwards.

"Knight-Errant Andric." Tamar's voice cracked on the rank she no longer had the authority to confer. "Prelate Voss extends terms. Return with us. Submit to review. The Chair is prepared to be—"

"Merciful?" Sef's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I know how the Chair's mercy works, Tamar. I helped administer it."

"You don't understand. The writ isn't just for you anymore." Tamar's eyes flicked — one heartbeat, half a heartbeat — to Yara, standing in his shadow behind her. "The man you're harboring. The cartographer. There's a second writ. Sealed. I wasn't meant to tell you, but you should know what you're—"

"Tamar." The taller of the two strangers spoke for the first time. A man with a voice like dry paper and a face that had been arranged around patience the way a trap is arranged around bait. "Enough."

"He deserves to know what's coming for him, Brother Cassian. We all swore—"

"We swore obedience." Cassian hadn't raised his voice. He didn't need to. The judgment-glass in his grip tilted, very slightly, and its beam slid off Sef and settled on Yara instead, and Yara felt it like a hand pressing against his sternum, asking its silent question of his blood. He felt the answer gathering. Felt the old thing in him — the throne-shaped thing, the raven-winged thing — stir toward the light like a moth that didn't know it was flying into a lamp.

*Don't,* he begged it silently. *Not now. Be small. Be nothing. Be a charm-seller.*

The beam brightened.

And Sef moved.

Later, Yara would try to reconstruct it and fail. There was no decision in it that he could see — no moment where she weighed options, no breath drawn before action. There was simply a gap in the world where Sef had been standing, and then Sef was elsewhere, between the beam and Yara, and her hand had closed around the nearest thing — a fallen length of root-stone, a rib of the cathedral floor broken loose by centuries — and the tall stranger, Brother Cassian, had raised his glass-staff to strike her down, and she had struck first.

The sound was small. That was the horror of it. All the weight of consequence, all the finality, and the sound itself was barely anything — a dull knock, wood on bone, a sound like a door closing in another room.

Cassian sat down. That was how it looked. He sat down the way a puppet sits down when its strings are cut, and the judgment-glass rolled from his fingers and its beam spun wildly across the pillars, lighting the ancient Ravensworth script in strobing flashes, and then the light went out, and the gallery went dark except for the pale glow of the other staff, and the silence was enormous.

The second stranger — a heavyset man whose name Yara never learned — shouted something. Charged. Sef met him, and there was a tangle of limbs and gray cloth and a flash of the knife she wore at her hip for cutting rope, and then the man was on the ground holding his thigh and screaming words that weren't words, and Tamar was standing frozen with her staff half-raised and her face coming apart, and Sef stood in the middle of it all, breathing hard, the root-stone still in her hand, dark at one end.

Dark at one end.

"Oh," Sef said. Very quietly. Looking at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. "Oh, no."

"Sef." Yara stepped forward.

"Don't." She backed away from him. From all of it. From Cassian sitting motionless against the pillar with his head tipped at an angle necks don't forgive, from the wounded man moaning, from Tamar weeping without sound, from the whole ruined gallery. "Don't touch me. Don't— I killed him. Yara. I killed him. He was going to strike and I—" Her breath hitched, caught, failed. "I was sworn. Even fallen, even disgraced, I was sworn that my hands were for protection, that I would never— and I told myself burning the warrant was the cost, that going rogue was the cost, but this, this is different, this doesn't wash off, this—"

She was shaking. Yara had seen her face collectors and chasms and the regent's archive full of his mother's bargains, and she had never shaken. Now she shook like a struck bell, and the sound of her was worse than the body on the floor.

Tamar made a choice, then. Yara watched her make it — watched the young knight look at her dead brother-in-arms, at the woman she'd once followed, at the outlaw standing behind them both — and lower her staff. "I'll take Brother Olan back up," she said hoarsely, hauling the wounded man's arm across her shoulders. "I'll report... I'll report that we lost you in the deep ways. That the writ can't be served below the third gallery." Her eyes found Sef's. "You were good to me when I was new. I'm not paying a debt. I'm telling you what the debt was." And she dragged Olan away into the dark, leaving Cassian alone with them, alone with the silence, alone with the fact of him.

* * *

They buried him badly, because there was nothing to dig with and the floor was stone, and because Sef's hands wouldn't stop trembling enough to move rubble. In the end they covered him with fallen slabs from the collapsed archway at the gallery's edge, and Yara laid one of his mother's maps over the cairn — an old one, a duplicate, a chart of star-channels above a country that existed nowhere — because it seemed wrong to send a man into the dark without directions.

Then Sef walked to the far wall and stood facing it, and Yara understood that she was waiting for him to leave, or to condemn her, or to do whatever it was that people did after witnessing a thing like this. She was waiting to be handled.

He didn't handle her.

He came and stood beside her, not touching, shoulder a hand's width from hers, and looked at the wall too. The wall had nothing to recommend it. It was damp and dark and carved with dead letters. They looked at it together for a long time.

"In the temple," Sef finally said, "when a knight takes a life in service, there's a rite. An hour of silence, a vigil, a senior brother who sits with you and says the words that make it *ordered*. That makes it fit inside the shape of your vows." Her voice was scraped hollow. "I always thought the rite was for the dead. It isn't. It's for the living. It's so the killer doesn't have to be alone inside the thing they did." A breath. "I don't get the rite. I burned my right to it with the warrant."

"Yes, you did," Yara said.

Her head turned. He felt her looking at him, waiting for the cruelty or the comfort, whichever came first.

"So we'll make a new one," he said. "A worse one. A heretic's rite. No senior brother, no sanctioned words, no clean shape to fit it in. Just this: you stood between a light that was hunting me and me, and you did what standing there required, and I am alive because your hands were quicker than your grief. That's the whole liturgy. It's short. It's ugly. It's true." He turned to face her fully. "I won't tell you it wasn't murder, Sef. I won't tell you he deserved it or that the temple made you do it or any of the hundred lies that would let you sleep tonight. What I'll tell you is that I've sold luck to widows and lied to their faces about what was in the wrapping, and I know exactly what it costs to be the instrument of something you can't take back. And you don't have to be alone inside it. That's all I have. It's not a rite. It's just company."

For a moment she didn't move. Then her forehead came down against his shoulder — not an embrace, not yet, just weight, just surrender of a few pounds of exhaustion onto a surface that would hold — and she stayed there while the underground river sang its uphill song, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that needed saying and everything that needed silence.

When she straightened at last, her eyes were red but level, and something in her face had settled — not healed, nothing down here would heal, but *settled*, the way sediment settles after a flood, making the water briefly clear.

"There's a door ahead," she said. "Your family's door. The one only your blood opens."

Yara nodded slowly. He could feel it, three galleries down and one turn east, warm in his awareness like a coal wrapped in cloth. And beyond it — this was the part he hadn't told her, the part the map in his mother's hand made plain in its patient, terrible annotations — beyond it lay the Court Beneath the Mountain, and the lanterns, and the contract with his name on it, and whatever waited there wearing the regent's borrowed face.

Beyond it lay the reason the temple had sent hounds. The reason the writ bore a seal he recognized from his mother's maps. The reason the old gods were waking.

"Then we'd better open it," Yara said, "before they send something worse than kindness."

Far above them, faint as a memory of a memory, a bell began to ring — and it was not the temple's bell. It was deeper. Older. And it was ringing *for him*.
