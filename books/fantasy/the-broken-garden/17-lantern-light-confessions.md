## 17. Lantern-Light Confessions

The lanterns did not flicker. That was the first thing Yara understood about them, standing in the cathedral of roots with his neck craned back and his breath gone thin in his chest: they burned steady, every one of them, the way a heart burns when it has nowhere left to run.

There were thousands. Tens of thousands. They hung from the root-vault overhead on cords of pale fiber, each one a soft-glowing shape like a pear of glass, and inside each one moved something that was not quite light and not quite smoke — a slow turning, a slow reaching, the way a sleeper turns toward a window. The whole ceiling of the Undercourt was a sky of stolen lives, and the light they gave was warm, and gentle, and it made him sick to look at.

"His," Sef said quietly beside him. Not a question.

Yara had not spoken since they entered. He found his voice had gone somewhere without him. "Each one is a life someone never got to live." His own words came out flat, cartographer's words, surveying terrain. It was the only way he could keep standing. "The widow's son who dies at nine. The sailor who would have come home. The child who—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They hang them up and they burn with everything their owners were promised."

Sef's hand rested on her sword hilt, but she wasn't looking for enemies. She was looking up, and her face — the hard, wind-scoured face he had first seen across an interrogation table in Grayharbor, all suspicion and iron — had gone young somehow. Stricken. "I was taught these were stories," she said. "Warnings. *Sell your soul and this is where it goes.* A fable to keep children from bargaining with things under hills." Her jaw worked. "Nobody told me they'd be beautiful."

"That's how they get you to stop asking where they came from."

He hadn't meant to say it aloud. But it was true, and it sat between them now, and neither of them took it back.

They walked on through the grove of roots. The floor was soft earth, springy as moss, and it gave silently beneath their boots; even their footsteps seemed afraid to echo here. Lanterns passed overhead close enough to touch, and Yara kept his hands folded tight against his sides, because some part of him — the mapmaker, the reader of lines — wanted to reach up and trace the grain of each one, learn its shape, and he knew with a certainty beyond argument that if he touched one, something would look back.

Then he saw it.

It hung apart from the others, off a side-root in a shallow alcove, and it was different. The others burned gold-white, honeyed, full. This one was dim. Not dark — dim, the way an ember is dim, holding its fire close, saving it. And though he could not have said how he knew, he knew the way he knew the turn of a river before he'd ever walked its banks.

"Sef."

She turned. Followed his gaze. Went still.

The lantern turned slowly on its cord, and inside it the not-light pressed itself against the glass from within, once, twice — like a palm laid flat against a window. Like recognition.

"That's mine," Yara said. His voice sounded very far away. "That's my soul."

* * *

They stood beneath it a long time. Neither spoke. Above them the thousand borrowed lights burned on, indifferent or patient, he couldn't tell which, and below them his own soul hung dim and unlit, a lamp waiting for oil, a room waiting for someone to come home.

"It's small," Yara said finally, and heard the absurdity crack out of him as almost-laughter. "Three decades old and it's barely bigger than my fist. Is that what I am? Something you could hold in one hand?"

"You're holding yourself wrong." Sef's voice was rough. She had not taken her eyes off it. "A flame looks small until you put your hand in it."

"When I take it back—"

"*If.*"

"When," he said, because he had decided somewhere on the road beneath the world that *if* was a word for people with alternatives, "when I take it back, what happens to me? Does it go back in? Do I feel it?" He looked up at the dim ember of himself. "Will I know what I've been missing?"

Sef was quiet long enough that he turned to look at her. In the lantern-light her face was half gold, half shadow, and there was something in the shadowed half he'd never seen there before — not suspicion, not duty, not the careful blankness she wore like armor. Something unguarded and grieving.

"I think," she said slowly, "that you already know what you're missing. I think that's why your maps are the way they are. Why you draw places no one's ever seen like you're trying to remember them." She looked away, up into the burning crowd of souls. "You've been homesick your whole life for something you couldn't name. I watched you figure that out on the road. You thought it was the Undercourt calling its debt."

"And it isn't?"

"No." She shook her head. "It's this. It's the piece of you that's here. You've felt the shape of the hole your entire life, Yara. You just didn't know the hole had a name."

The words went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor. He opened his mouth to answer — to deflect, probably, to make a joke about inventory and missing stock, because that was what he did, that was how he'd survived three years of being small — and nothing came. Because she was right, and because they were standing directly beneath the evidence.

"My mother sold me," he said instead. Quietly. To the lantern more than to her. "Before I could walk. Before I could do anything worth selling. She looked at her newborn son and signed him over to whatever lives down here, and everyone keeps telling me it was love, it was protection, it was the only way—" His hands had curled into fists without his permission. "But I keep coming back to the arithmetic of it. She did the sums and I was the coin."

"She did the sums," Sef said, "and chose which coin to spend. There were two of you in that room, Yara. Your body and your soul. She could only save one."

"You don't know that."

"No," she admitted. "I don't. I'm telling you what I'd need to believe, if it were me. If I'd woken up thirty years later and learned my mother had sold a piece of me to save the rest." Her voice dropped. "I'd want to hate her. Hating her would be easier than believing she lay awake every night after, doing the sum again, looking for a different answer."

Yara stared at her. The lantern above them turned, and its faint ember-light moved across her face, and he realized his fists had come undone at some point without his noticing.

"You talk about heresy," he said. "Your trial. You've never told me what you actually said. What they burned you for."

Something shifted in her stance — the old reflex, the flinch toward the armor. He watched her fight it. Watched her lose.

"They asked me a question during my examination," she said. "Standard form. *Do you believe the gods are owed worship?* Every knight answers yes. It's the yes that makes the oaths make sense. Protection flows down from the temple, devotion flows up, the gods stand at the top and hold the roof on." She paused. "I said no."

"No?"

"I said the gods aren't owed anything. I said worship that's compelled by law isn't worship, it's tax. I said—" and here her voice did something he had never heard it do, cracked, just slightly, along an old seam — "I said if the gods need to be fed by force, then maybe they're not gods. Maybe they're just large, hungry animals wearing crowns, and someone should say so out loud."

The silence after was enormous. Somewhere far off in the root-cathedral, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"They stripped my rank for it. Took my colors. My sister stopped writing." She laughed, short and bitter. "My *heresy*. That people should be free to kneel or not kneel. That faith given at sword-point is worthless." She turned to face him fully, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "And then I met you. A man hiding his magic in a city that outlawed wonder, doing small illegal kindnesses for desperate people, and I thought — *there. That's what devotion looks like when nobody's forcing it.*"

"Sef—"

"Let me finish. I've been finishing nothing for three years." She drew a breath. "You asked me once why I burned my warrant. I told you it was because the temple was wrong about you. That was true. But it wasn't the whole truth." Her chin lifted, the way it did when she was walking into a fight she expected to lose. "The whole truth is that I've believed what I believe since before I met you, and meeting you didn't change it. Meeting you just made it cost something. And I paid. Gladly. And I would pay again."

Above them, among the ten thousand steady flames, something stirred. Yara felt it before he saw it — a pressure behind his ribs, the same hook-tug he'd felt the night the candle burned blue, but softer now, wider, a tide rather than a hook. The lanterns nearest them had begun to lean. Barely. A degree of tilt, no more, their cords bowing inward like stems toward sun.

They were listening.

"They can hear us," Yara breathed.

"Maybe they're lonely," Sef said. "Hanging in the dark for a hundred years while the world forgot this place existed." She looked up at them, at the leaning lights, and her expression broke open into something tender and appalled. "Gods. Look at them. They're *straining*."

Yara looked. She was right. All through the vast dark vault of roots, the lanterns had begun to lean — thousands of them, a slow silent inclination, every stolen life bending toward the two small figures standing in the earth below. Toward voices. Toward being spoken to. Toward, perhaps, the simple unbearable novelty of being witnessed.

His throat closed. When he spoke, it came out barely above a whisper.

"I spent three years making myself invisible. Being small. Keeping my head down, my magic hidden, my name out of mouths." He shook his head slowly. "And down here there are ten thousand souls who would give anything just to be *seen*, and I understand them completely. I understand them better than I've ever understood anything."

"Yara."

"And I think—" His voice caught. He pushed through it. "I think that's why I map. Why I draw roads to places no one's ever been. Because a place that's unmapped might as well be a soul in a lantern. Known by nothing, held by nothing, burning alone in the dark for no one." He looked at her. "I map things so they're real. So somebody, somewhere, knows they exist."

Sef had gone very still. The kind of stillness she kept for the moment before violence, except there was no violence anywhere near her now, only the thing underneath her stillness trying to get out.

"You make people real too," he said. "Did you know that? The way you look at them. The widow in Grayharbor — you crouched down to her level. You asked her son's name. Nobody does that. Knights don't do that." He stepped closer. Close enough to see the lantern-light reflected twice in her eyes, once in each, like two small steady fires. "I've watched you do it for weeks and I keep thinking, *there it is, there's the heresy, that's the actual crime.* Not the words at your trial. Just — caring about people the temple never taught you to care about."

"Stop," she said. It came out strangled.

"Why?"

"Because if you keep going I'm going to do something we'll both regret."

"Regret?" He laughed — helpless, startled, the sound escaping before he could weigh it. "Sef. We are standing in hell. Beneath the world. Surrounded by the stolen dead, hunted by your former brothers, owing my soul to a regent who wears faces like coats. What exactly is left for us to regret?"

Her composure broke.

Not all at once — in pieces, the way ice breaks, along seams that had been there all along. Her hand came up and closed in the front of his shirt, and she pulled him in, and the kiss landed like something that had been waiting a very long time and had run out of patience: fierce, ungraceful, tasting of road-dust and iron and the salt at the corner of her eye. His hands found her shoulders, her jaw, the short rough hair at the nape of her neck, and he kissed her back with the particular desperation of a man who has spent three years being small and has just, for one moment, stopped.

Around them, the lanterns blazed.

He felt it happen — felt the light change against his closed eyelids, felt the warmth bloom outward through the cathedral of roots like dawn arriving all at once. When they finally broke apart, gasping, foreheads pressed together, he looked up and his breath left him entirely.

Every lantern in sight was burning brighter. Not the dim honey-gold of before — brighter, whiter, alive, ten thousand souls flaring like a field of stars answering some signal only they could hear. The cords hummed. The great roots overhead seemed to shiver. And the light fell on Sef's upturned face and Yara's own hands and the soft dark earth, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and it was the worst, because he knew what it was made of.

"We shouldn't be happy here," he whispered.

"I know." She was smiling anyway. She couldn't seem to stop. It transformed her face entirely — the knight's iron mask gone, and underneath it someone young and astonished and lit from within. "Tell me to stop being happy, then."

"I can't." He touched her cheek. "I've tried. Since Grayharbor. Believe me, I've tried."

Somewhere above them, deeper in the tangle of roots, a bell tolled once — low, resonant, patient. Then again. Not an alarm. An announcement. Somewhere in the Undercourt, something had noticed two intruders standing beneath a dim lantern, and had decided to receive them.

Sef's smile faded into something steadier. Her hand found his and gripped it, hard, grounding them both.

"The regent knows we're here," she said.

"Yes."

"Whatever it offers you — whatever face it wears when it offers —" She turned to him, and the flare of the lanterns lit her eyes like coals. "Remember this moment. Remember that you're loved by someone who chose you over everything she was built to be. Whatever it puts in front of you down here, whatever it promises or threatens — *nothing it says outweighs that.*"

Yara looked up one last time at his own soul, hanging dim and patient on its cord, waiting for him like a letter unopened for thirty years.

"Nothing," he agreed. "Come on. Let's go meet whatever's wearing my mother's face."

Hand in hand, lit by ten thousand borrowed lives, they walked toward the tolling of the bell — and behind them, in their wake, the lanterns leaned after them like flowers toward a sun that had finally, finally come home.

* * *

The bell led them down.

The roots narrowed as they descended, braiding together into archways and colonnades, and the hanging lanterns grew sparser, older, their light deepening from gold to amber to a color like the last coal of a dying fire. The air grew warmer. Somewhere below, water moved — not the uphill river they had followed into the dark, but something slower, thicker, with a sound like breathing.

Sef walked with her sword drawn now. Yara walked with his satchel clutched to his chest, and inside it, against his ribs, the maps of nowhere — his mother's hand and his own, inked roads leading into the heart of the dark. He had not planned to bring them. He had not planned anything. But his feet knew the way, the way they had known the paths of Ravensworth, and he was beginning to suspect that everything he had ever drawn, he had drawn from memory of this place.

At the mouth of the final passage, the roots opened into a hall of living wood, and upon a throne of woven branches sat a figure whose face he knew better than his own.

"Yara," said his mother, in his mother's voice, with his mother's tired, loving smile. "You've grown so much. Come here. Let me look at you."

Beside him, Sef's grip tightened on her sword.

And Yara — heart hammering, eyes burning, thirty years of grief rising in his throat like floodwater — stepped forward into the light.
