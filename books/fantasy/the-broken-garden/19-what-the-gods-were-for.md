## 19. What the Gods Were For

The god was chained in a room that had no right to exist.

They found it by accident — or by inheritance, which in Yara's family might have been the same thing. A seam in the Undercourt's root-cathedral wall, thin as a knife's edge, that opened only when Yara pressed his palm flat against it and *asked*. The stone had known his blood before he did. That was becoming a familiar humiliation.

Beyond the seam: a corridor of black basalt, older than the roots above it, older than the court itself perhaps. And at its end, a chamber where something vast hung in chains of woven prayer.

Sef stopped three steps inside the door and could not make her feet go further.

She had been raised on the theology of chains. The Temple of the Ninefold Dawn taught that the old gods were bound for humanity's protection — that the outlawry, three generations ago, had been an act of mercy performed by mortal hands with mortal courage. She had memorized the liturgy of the Binding at seven years old. She had sung it every dawn of her novitiate, kneeling on cold marble: *We bound you not in hatred but in love, for your hunger was greater than our giving.*

She had believed it the way she believed in sunrise.

The thing in the chains had been beautiful once. Even now, half-starved and mad, there was a terrible grandeur to it — a body like a stag's if a stag had been dreamed rather than born, antlers hung with dead festivals, eyes the color of harvest moons going out. The chains did not bind its flesh. They bound its *name*. Sef could see them for what they were now, this deep in the world: each link a prayer, hammered flat and riveted shut, so that the god could not remember what it was called and therefore could not be what it was.

"Little knight," it said, and its voice was a thousand voices saying grace before a meal no one would eat. "Little knight of the new temple. Come to see what your dawn is built upon?"

"Sef." Yara's hand found her elbow. "You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I've come too far to look away now."

* * *

It told them its name first, or tried to. The chains caught the syllables and strangled them; what emerged was a sound like wind through a wheat field, like a congregation breathing. It laughed at its own failure, and the laugh shook dust from the ceiling in fine gray veils.

"They took even that," it said. "But I remember the taking. Would you hear it, little knight? It is a short story. Most true stories are."

"I'm listening."

"The Ravensworths fed us." The god's antlers dipped, the dead festivals clinking softly. "That was the covenant, in the first age. Worship given freely, worship received freely. We gave back rain and ripe fields, safe births, doors that opened for the honest traveler. It was not a transaction. It was a *marriage*. Do you understand? We were wed to the people of the upper world, and the Ravensworth line stood between as the officiants — the priests who held the bowl."

Yara had gone very still beside her. Sef felt him stop breathing.

"And then," the god continued, "the bowl became a cup. And the cup became a chalice. And the chalice became—" its ruined voice cracked across the chamber, "—a *mouth*."

* * *

It showed them.

Not with words. With memory, poured into the room like water into a basin, and Sef had no choice but to kneel at the edge and look down into it.

She saw the Raven Throne in its living years: a hall of white stone above ground, sunlight through high windows, and the Ravensworth kings and queens standing before their assembled gods with arms raised — and the worship of ten thousand faithful flowing through them like current through wire. She saw how it was meant to work. The people prayed. The gods received. The Ravensworths merely held the channel open, honored servants, and took nothing but the honor.

Then she saw the slow rot of centuries. A king who began to taste the worship as it passed through him — just a sip, just to know what his flock felt. A queen who found she could hold the channel closed, dam it, and drink her fill while the gods starved and the prayers went unanswered and the people learned to pray harder because their gods had gone quiet. She saw famine years when the rain did not come because the god of rain was being bled dry in a white hall by a man with a raven sigil who had grown fat on devotion. She saw the gods begin to change — to grow hungry in ways hunger was never meant to bend them, reaching down toward mortal souls because the free offerings had been cut off at the source.

And she saw the Binding. Not the Temple's version. The truth of it.

The last Ravensworths, feeling the age turn against them — feeling the first uprisings, the first reformers with torches at the gates — had done the unforgivable arithmetic. If they could not keep the feast, no one would. They had taken the gods' own names, the names entrusted to their keeping since the first covenant, and hammered them into chains. They had sealed the Undercourt. They had gone silent themselves, scattered their heirs into hiding like seeds ahead of the winter, and left the world to believe the gods had simply *died* — or worse, that the gods had been bound by righteous mortal hands.

The Temple of the Ninefold Dawn had been built on the empty stage after the play ended. Its founders had inherited a lie so old it had become architecture.

"They did not bind us to save you," the god said, and the memory-basin drained away, leaving Sef kneeling on cold stone with her hands shaking. "They bound us to keep you. A shepherd does not lock the flock in the barn because wolves are coming, little knight. He locks the barn because he intends to be the only one who eats."

* * *

"No."

Sef heard herself say it before she knew she was going to. The word fell into the chamber and lay there like a dropped coin.

"No. The outlawry — the reformers burned the temples, they broke the idol-cults, they—"

"They burned what the Ravensworths let them see," the god said gently, and the gentleness was worse than cruelty. "Your reformers were righteous, little knight. Never doubt it. They saw the hunger and they fought it, and many of them died for it, and their courage was real. But they were fighting the mouth without knowing the hand that fed it. And when the fires cooled, the men who built your new temple took the ashes and made a doctrine: *the gods are dangerous, the gods must be bound, wonder must be licensed.* They built a church on a foundation the Ravensworths poured. You have spent your life guarding a prison whose walls were raised by the jailers' own kin."

Sef looked at Yara.

He was standing very straight, very pale, his cartographer's hands hanging loose at his sides, and she watched him arrive at the same place she was arriving at, from the opposite direction. He was the heir. She was the sword. The throne and the temple, the crime and the cover-up, the wound and the bandage tied over it by people who never knew they were binding anything at all.

"My order," she said. Her voice sounded foreign. "The knights. My vows. Everything I—"

"Was real," the god said. "Your faith was real. Your love was real. That is what makes it unbearable, yes?"

* * *

They walked back along the basalt corridor in silence. Yara kept glancing at her — she could feel it, the way you feel weather changing — and finally, near the seam in the wall, he said her name.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Please. Just — give me a moment."

"You can't believe it."

"Can't I?" She laughed, and hated the sound. "Yara, I've seen the archives down here. I read your mother's contract. I've watched a river eat my sister's face out of my memory. At what point exactly am I permitted to believe the next terrible thing?" She pressed her back against the cold stone. "I need it to be a lie. I need the Temple to have been — if not good, then *deceived*, honestly deceived, deceived the way we all were. Because if the founding generation knew—"

"They didn't. Probably." Yara came closer, careful as a man approaching a wounded animal. "Sef, the Binding was four hundred years ago. Whatever the first temple-builders suspected, it's long buried. The knights who came after — you — you guarded people. You actually did that."

"With chains." She pushed off the wall and faced him. "Every seal I ever set, every outlaw charm I confiscated, every hedge-witch I dragged before an inquisitor — I thought I was protecting the flock. I was maintaining a cage built by your ancestors so that *they* would always have the only key." Her breath shuddered. "And now the key has a face. And the face is—"

She stopped. The word she'd almost said sat between them, enormous.

Yara said quietly: "Say it anyway."

"You're the heir to everything I swore against." Her eyes stung. "Do you understand what that means? Not the man — never the man. But the blood. The Throne. When the gods wake and kneel to you, when Grayharbor's magic erupts, when every chain your family forged comes undone — I will have helped do it. Me. Oathbreaker twice over. And the worst part, Yara, the part I cannot—" her voice fractured, "—the worst part is that watching you learn all this, watching you carry it, I still *love you*. The truth didn't even slow it down. What does that make me?"

"Human," he said. "It makes you human, Sef. Which is more than either the Throne or the Temple ever managed."

But she saw it in him too, now — the fracture running through him that matched hers. He had learned in the archive that his mother sold him to save him. Now he had learned that the family she saved him *from* was his own. There was no clean side of his history to stand on. Every road home led through guilt.

"The god said the reformers were righteous," she said at last, mechanically, grasping for solid ground. "That the outlawry—"

"Was necessary and also built on a lie. Both things." Yara rubbed his eyes. "Welcome to my entire life, Sef. Both things. Always both things."

* * *

She went back alone that night. She told herself it was to ask the god more questions — dates, names, evidence, the cartography of truth she needed before she could rebuild a single wall of her faith. That was partly true.

Mostly she went because she could not bear for Yara to see her pray.

She knelt outside the chain-chamber, in the corridor, where the half-mad god could hear her but not see her, and she did the thing she had done every dawn since she was seven years old. She recited the liturgy of the Binding. *We bound you not in hatred but in love.*

The chains rustled. The god's voice came soft through the doorway, amused and grieving at once:

"Little knight. Say it louder. The one who wrote those words was a Ravensworth. Let him hear you from wherever he rots."

Sef knelt a long time in the dark, and the liturgy would not finish. The words kept breaking in her mouth like ice under too much weight, and beneath the breaking she could hear something else rising — not doubt, doubt she had known all her life and managed like any other tool, but something larger. A vertigo. The sense of standing on a floor that had always been floor and learning it was a lid.

When she finally rose, her knees ached and her decision had not arrived, but its shape had. She could feel it waiting in her like a blade in a sheath, not yet drawn, not yet named.

She found Yara in the lantern-hall, standing beneath the thousand hanging souls, looking up at his own dim unlit lantern with an expression she recognized now as the beginning of an idea she would hate.

"There's something I have to do," he said, without turning. "Something about the price. Don't ask yet. I need a day."

"Yara—"

"A day, Sef." He turned then, and smiled at her, and it was the smile that frightened her — too calm, too settled, the smile of a man who has already signed something in his head. "One day. Then whatever happens, happens."

Above him, among the hanging lights, his lantern flickered — once, faintly, like a candle sensing a draft from a door not yet opened.

Sef stood in the root-cathedral with her broken faith in one hand and her unbroken love in the other, and understood that soon — very soon — she would be asked to choose which one she carried out of this place.

She did not yet know that the choosing would be made for her, in the worst way possible, before another sun rose over the world above.
