## 21. The Bargain Struck

The lanterns did not sleep.

Yara had learned this in his first night beneath the mountain, lying on a root-bed that breathed like a sleeping animal, staring up into the cathedral dark where a thousand souls hung burning with the lives their owners never lived. They flickered, but they did not go out. They could not. A soul was not a flame; it was a debt of light, and debts did not rest.

He counted them again. It was the only prayer he knew how to make anymore.

Sef had been gone three days. He knew because time moved strangely here and he had forced himself to measure it anyway — one hundred and eight times he had watched the slow tide of sap rise and fall in the walls of the great root-cathedral, and the sap rose once for every hour above, and so: three days. She had walked out of the Undercourt alone, up the river that flowed uphill, past the garden that remembered his feet better than his mind did, and she had not looked back, because looking back was a kindness neither of them could afford.

*Unable to help the man she loves unmake the world she swore to heal.* That was what she had said, or near enough, standing among the hanging lights with her burned warrant still ghosting ash on her fingers. Both sides right. That was the cruelest part. If she had been wrong, he could have hated her, and hatred was a rope you could climb.

He sat alone in the antechamber of the regent's hall, on a bench grown from pale wood, and waited to be summoned, because the regent always summoned him now. It had stopped feeling like an audience and started feeling like a courtship, and Yara understood courtships. He had spent three years selling charms in Grayharbor's night market; he knew how a seller smiled, how a buyer leaned in, how the moment before agreement tasted like copper and possibility.

The doors opened by themselves. Doors here always did. He thought, absurdly, of the mouse-god and its small promise, and pushed the thought away like a splinter too deep to dig.

* * *

The regent wore his mother's face.

It had worn other faces in the days since — a gray-eyed woman with a scholar's hands, a child with sap-dark curls, once, horribly, a younger version of Yara himself — but it always came back to his mother. It knew. Of course it knew. The regent was old enough to have eaten centuries, and cruelty that old does not announce itself; it simply arranges the furniture of your grief until you sit down in it willingly.

"You have decided." The regent stood at the far end of the hall, among the roots, wearing Miriel Ravensworth's face and a gown of woven shadow that might have been mourning-wear in some century long drowned. Its voice was his mother's voice. He had stopped flinching. Mostly.

"I've come to renegotiate."

"Renegotiate." The regent turned, and the movement was wrong in a way Yara could not name — too smooth, as if the body were a garment being rotated rather than a person walking. "That is a merchant's word."

"I'm a merchant."

"You are a king who has not yet admitted it." The mother-face smiled, and it was his mother's smile, warm at one corner, and Yara felt the hook behind his ribs — the same hook the blue candle had set there, the homesickness for something he could not name — twist gently, expertly. "Sit. Let us hear the merchant's terms."

He did not sit. Sitting would take longer, and he had rehearsed this speech standing, walking the root-corridors all night, and if he sat down the rehearsal would fall apart like wet paper.

"My soul," he said. "You hold my lantern. Dim, unlit — you told me it burns with the life I haven't lived yet, and there isn't much light in it because I made myself small. Three years of being small." His voice held. He was almost proud of it. "I want it back. Not bought. Returned."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange I give you a soul freely given. Mine. All of it — not the scrap in the lantern, not the contract-debt my mother signed. Me. Willing. Whole. Signed in blood and witnessed by whatever passes for law down here."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against his skin like deep water.

The regent came closer. Up close, the borrowed face was perfect and imperfect at once — the lines around the eyes were right, the small scar on the jaw was right, but the eyes themselves were lamps behind painted glass, and no amount of craft could put a person behind them. "Do you understand," it said softly, "what you are offering?"

"A crown, a god, or a willing soul. Your own words. I can't give the crown — I don't want it, and wanting matters, apparently, in your ledgers. I can't give a god; I don't command any yet, whatever my blood says about inheritance. But a willing soul—" He made himself say it plainly, because plainness was armor. "Mine is worth more than most. You said so yourself. Raven blood. You want me for reasons beyond the contract. So here is beyond the contract."

"You would feed this court for five hundred years." The regent's voice had changed — not warmer, nothing so human, but *interested*, the way a tide is interested in a sandcastle. "A single soul, given freely, from the line that bound us. Do you know what the lanterns burn on, little cartographer? Not punishment. Punishment is cheap. They burn on *longing*. On the weight of unlived lives. And yours—" the mother-face tilted, studying him like a map, "—yours is very heavy. You have wanted so much and permitted yourself so little. That is a rich fuel, Yara Ravensworth. That will burn beautifully."

"Then we have an accord."

"Not yet." One hand rose — his mother's hand, ink-stained at the fingertips, because his mother had been a mapmaker too, that was where he'd learned it, that was the one true thing under all the lies — and touched his cheek. He did not pull away. Pulling away would cost more than it bought. "Tell me why. Merchants always tell why. It lets me know what I am really buying."

Yara looked past the regent, up into the hanging dark, at the thousand small fires.

"Because they're people," he said. "Every one of them. Sold, stolen, gambled away, but people. And I found mine down here and it made me sick, and then I found everyone else's, and it made me sicker, and I keep waiting for the sickness to pass and it doesn't. My mother sold me to save me. Fine. I've read the archive; I know; Sef read it to me twice because I couldn't stop her. But she saved one child by feeding this place, and this place has eaten ten thousand children since, and somewhere in that arithmetic there's a sum I can actually pay." He swallowed. "I'm not doing it for them, exactly. I'm doing it because I looked at my own lantern and realized the life inside it wasn't worth what it costs everyone else to keep me alive. That's the why. Is it noble enough for your ledger?"

"It is honest enough," the regent said. "Honesty is rarer than nobility. Nobility I can counterfeit." The hand fell away. "But hear the terms before you sign, merchant, because I do not sell to the uninformed, and uninformed buyers come back later with grievances."

"Terms."

"The lantern returns to you. Unlit becomes lit — the life you have not lived flows back into you, and you will feel it, all of it, every year you were meant to be more than small. It will hurt. Light always does, when it arrives all at once."

"Accepted."

"Your name leaves our books. No collector may touch you. The debt your mother signed is paid, discharged, ash."

"Accepted."

"And in return, your soul — the whole of it, the living whole, not the potential — passes to the court upon your death. Not before." The regent's smile widened by a degree, and something in the width of it snagged on Yara's attention like a fishhook on a sleeve. "Freely given means freely timed, little cartographer. We do not take you tonight. You will live. You will go up. You will be what you were always going to be. And when you die — tomorrow or in eighty years — everything you are comes home to us, and burns, and feeds ten thousand lanterns for five centuries." The lamp-eyes glittered. "Is that not generous? Most sellers must die badly. You need only die at all."

And Yara, who had survived three years by reading the fine print of Grayharbor's worst alleys, who had caught a widow's nephew trying to salt a luck-charm with iron filings, who had built a whole life on noticing the wrongness in a thing before it bit —

— heard the generosity, saw the smile, and was too tired, too grieving, too full of Sef's absence and his mother's face and the thousand small fires, to ask the question.

Later — in another chapter of his life, on a surface he could not yet imagine, with a name slipping from minds like water through fingers — he would lie awake and find it. *What happens to a soul given freely, when the giver becomes something the court never priced? What does a Raven soul weigh after it wakes?*

But that was later. Now he only said:

"Accepted."

* * *

They sealed it in the archive, because the archive was where contracts lived, and the archive of the Undercourt was not shelves and paper. It was a root-chamber whose walls were scarred with signatures going back further than the city above had existed — names in blood, in sap, in things darker than either, layered like sediment. Yara's mother's signature was there, from the night of his birth, small and hurried and desperate: *Miriel Ravensworth, one soul, male infant, in exchange for concealment.* He had looked at it every day for a week. He did not look at it now.

The regent produced a stylus of black bone and a shallow bowl of silver sap.

"Blood," it said. "Yours. Freely given means marked freely — no coercion, no desperation." The mother-face regarded him with something that was almost tenderness, which was the worst thing it had done yet. "Say the words after me. And mean them. The roots will know if you don't."

Yara opened his palm over the bowl. The cut was small; the regent offered a knife of folded shadow and he took it because refusing would prolong this, and he had learned in the night market that the fastest deal is the safest one.

"I, Yara Ravensworth—"

"I, Yara Ravensworth."

"—of the line of the Raven Throne—"

"—of the line of the Raven Throne." The words tasted strange, like fruit from a country he'd never visited.

"—give my soul to the Court Beneath the Mountain, freely, wholly, upon my death, in exchange for the return of my lantern and the discharge of all debts against my name."

He said it. He meant it. The roots drank the blood from the bowl, and somewhere far above, he felt — he could not explain how, any more than he could explain how he'd mapped the collapsed mine from dreams — a door open inside him. Not a door closing. An opening. Something vast turning its attention toward him through a gap he had just cut in his own walls.

The regent exhaled, and the sound it made was the sound a starving man makes at bread.

"Oh," it said, in his mother's voice, soft with genuine delight, and the delight was the most frightening thing in all the deep places of the world. "Oh, you wonderful, foolish boy. Do you feel that? The court feels you already. Five hundred years. Perhaps six. A Raven soul, *willing*—" it laughed, low, and the roots around them shivered with it, and a thousand lanterns overhead brightened as if they had heard good news. "Your mother bought you a hiding place. You have just bought us a feast. Come. Take your lantern. A bargain is a bargain, and I keep my bargains — it is the only religion I have left."

* * *

His lantern hung low on the western wall of the cathedral, where the dim ones gathered — the souls sold young, sold whole, sold before they'd had time to live much worth burning. When the regent lifted it down and put it in his hands, it weighed nothing and everything, the way a closed letter weighs nothing and everything.

"Open it," the regent said. "It knows its name."

Yara broke the seal of shadow with his thumb.

Light came out.

Not gold — blue, the blue of the widow's candle, the blue of old magic moving, and it poured into him through his palms and his chest and the hook behind his ribs, and it *hurt*, exactly as promised, exactly like light arriving all at once. He saw — not with his eyes, but with the mapmaker's sense that lived deeper than eyes — the life he had not lived unrolling like a chart: the boy he might have been on the Raven Throne, the roads he might have walked, the years he had amputated from himself to stay small in Grayharbor. All of it came home. All of it fit. He knelt on the root-floor of the cathedral of souls and shook, and the regent stood over him wearing his dead mother's face and watched with an expression he was not yet wise enough to read as hunger.

When he could stand, the regent pressed the empty lantern back into his hands.

"A gift," it said. "Keep it. Hang it in your window, when you have a window. Empty lanterns make such lovely promises about the future." And then, as Yara turned to go, as his feet found the uphill path with the terrible certainty that had carried him through every mile of this descent, the regent added — lightly, almost as an afterthought, the way merchants add the clause that ruins you:

"You'll want to hurry, little cartographer. The blood remembers what it's owed. Now that your lantern is lit and your name is off our books, the throne will come looking for its heir. It has been waiting a long time. And it does not wait *gently*."

Yara climbed.

Behind him the thousand lanterns burned, and one of them — he did not see which, he would never know which — guttered once, like a held breath, like a warning, and went on burning.

Above, impossibly far above, something ancient felt a Raven soul become whole, and began to wake.
