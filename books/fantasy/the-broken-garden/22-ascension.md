## 22. Ascension

The last thing the Undercourt gave him was a door.

It stood alone at the end of the root-cathedral's long nave, where no door had any right to be — plain gray wood, iron-banded, utterly ordinary, as if the regent had grown it from Yara's own memory of every back entrance he'd ever slipped through. Beyond it, faintly, he could smell salt and tar and frying fish. Grayharbor.

"Walk through," the regent said, wearing his mother's face and smiling with his mother's mouth, which was the cruelest thing about her. "The debt is paid, little cartographer. Your soul is yours again. What you do with it now is no business of the court beneath."

Yara looked at her for a long moment. He wanted to say something final, something that would close the account between them forever. What came out was quieter and worse.

"You'll keep them safe. The lanterns. You swore it on the roots."

"I keep all my bargains." Her borrowed eyes glittered. "That has always been the problem with my kind, hasn't it? Ask your ancestors."

He walked through the door.

* * *

Grayharbor hit him like a wave he had mapped but never swum.

Night market noise, gull-cries, the groan of ships at their moorings, the particular sour-sweet stink of the fish quarter at low tide — all of it ordinary, all of it unchanged, and none of it able to touch him. That was the first wrongness. For three years Yara Ravensworth had moved through this city like a man wading through cold water, flinching from every lantern, every temple bell, every uniformed shoulder. Now he stood in the mouth of Cooper's Alley and felt nothing press against him at all. The city seemed to part around him the way water parts around a keel.

The second wrongness was the light.

Every charm-lamp in the night market was burning blue.

Not the widow's candle — not one candle gone strange, as it had been that first night that unraveled everything. All of them. The luck-charms in Tallow Row, the hearth-blessings over the fishmongers' doors, the cheap painted wards nailed above the cribs on Rope Street: every scrap of hidden, smuggled, whispered magic in Grayharbor had caught fire at once, and it burned the old color, the deep color, the blue of the hour before dawn when the world still remembered what it was before the temples renamed it.

People were coming out of their houses to look.

An old netmender stood in her doorway with her hands raised, staring at the blue glow pooling in her palms like held water. She had bought a blessing from Yara two winters ago, ten coppers, a trinket to ease her husband's cough. The blessing was waking up in her hands, and she was laughing — or weeping; from across the alley it was impossible to tell, and perhaps it made no difference.

Yara's chest went tight. *This is me,* he thought. *I did this.* Not by working any rite. Simply by walking out of the Undercourt with his soul whole inside him, like a lamp carried into a dark house, and everything flammable turning toward the flame.

Behind his ribs, where the hook of the gods' attention had lodged since that first blue candle, something shifted its grip. It did not pull anymore. It *knocked.*

"No," Yara said aloud, to the air, to whatever listened. "No, I didn't come back for—"

The sky answered.

Over the harbor, the fog tore open along a seam no wind had made, and through it came a sound Yara had no word for and his body recognized anyway: a vast slow exhalation, like the sea drawing breath before a wave, like a mountain settling after centuries of holding still. Every dog in Grayharbor began to howl. Every bell in every temple — the great bronze Throat of the High Temple on its hill, the little cracked handbells of the street shrines — rang without being touched, one clear note each, all together, all in tune.

And the bound gods woke fully, and knelt to him.

He felt it happen the way a mapmaker feels an earthquake: as lines moving. Somewhere north, under the drowned barrow-fields, something enormous turned over in its sleep and then rose, and its rising bent the world gently toward a point, and the point was him. Something in the river estuary uncoiled. Something in the mountain's roots stretched limbs it had folded for a hundred years. They had been awake before — stirring, muttering, answering prayers in small dangerous ways since the first blue candle. But now the binding his ancestors had laid on them frayed through, thread by thread, and what remained was not restraint but *recognition.*

They knew who he was. The last heir of the Raven Throne. And they knelt.

Yara felt each act of kneeling as pressure, as weight, as a hand pressing down on the crown of his head — five of them, seven, more, names rising in his mind unbidden in languages dead before the city was built: the Tide-Mother. The Lantern-Eater. The god of small doors, whose promise sat in his pocket like a seed. Each one bowing. Each one *waiting.* Not demanding. That was the horror of it. They were waiting for instruction, the way hounds wait, the way armies wait, and the throne they knelt to was empty except for a tired man in a stolen coat standing in Cooper's Alley with fish-guts on his boots.

"Get up," he whispered. "All of you — get *up.*"

They did not get up. Gods are literal creatures. He had not commanded them to rise; he had only begged, and begging is not sovereignty, and so they stayed kneeling, patient as stone, and their patience pressed down on him harder than any command ever could.

A woman screamed somewhere near the wharf. Then another. Then the whole harbor district was screaming and cheering at once, because the sea itself had begun to glow — a long luminous seam running out past the breakwater, mile upon mile, as if someone had drawn a border on the water in light. Fishermen were falling to their knees on their decks. In the temple square, the great statue of the Nameless Saint, the faceless marble figure that had watched Grayharbor for three hundred years, cracked from crown to foot with a sound like ice going out of a lake — and did not fall. It leaned. It bowed its headless neck toward the low town, toward the alleys, toward Yara.

Toward him. Everyone would know, eventually, that all roads of that night led to him. He already knew it. He could feel himself becoming visible — the smallness he had cultivated for three years, the careful gray life, sloughing off him like ice in spring.

Yara turned and walked, fast, away from the water, away from the light, hood up, shoulders curled, an old habit his body performed while his mind raced ahead of him down dark streets. It didn't help. Nothing would help. He had traded a future for a past in the court beneath the mountain, and the past, it turned out, had teeth.

* * *

He made it four streets before the city finished breaking open.

It began with the charms — his own work, sold over three years to half of Grayharbor's desperate. A fever-charm on a child in Tanner's Lane burned through the sickness and kept burning, gold light pouring from a sleeping boy's window until the whole lane looked down into a shaft of noon. A love-knot Yara had charmed for a fisherman's daughter, a joke-work, barely magic at all, flared so bright with true feeling that the girl's whole family woke weeping with a happiness none of them could name. Wards he had drawn as mere camouflage — chalk marks meant to look like superstition while hiding real power beneath — shed their disguises and showed themselves: spirals, sigils, doors.

Doors. Doors opening everywhere. That was his doing too, though he hadn't done it yet — the mouse-god's bargain rippling backward and forward through time, seals his ancestors had set on the world groaning loose all at once, magic that had been dammed for a century bursting every levee. In the tannery district a well ran upward for eleven seconds, silver water climbing into the night air and hanging there like a curtain before it fell as rain that smelled of forests no ship had ever sailed to. On Bell Street, a dead man sat up in his coffin at his own wake, apologized to his widow, and lay back down, satisfied. The wake became, by all later accounts, the best party Grayharbor had seen in a generation.

Yara walked through it all and felt himself drowning.

That was the only word for it, and he clung to it because it was honest. This was triumph — he understood that distantly, the way a drowning man understands the sun on the surface far above. His soul was his own. His mother's bargain had protected him, and her protection had held, and he had come back from the land of the dead with everything intact. Any ballad written about tonight would call it victory. But triumph, he discovered, feels exactly like drowning when it happens to you instead of around you: the same crushing pressure, the same inability to draw a full breath, the same sense of the surface receding.

Because power was not what he had wanted. He had wanted a name he could say aloud. He had wanted Sef. He had wanted the garden.

And now every god below the world was kneeling to him, and every temple above it would be reaching for knives.

He heard the first temple horns before he reached the tanner's stair — three long notes, the mustering-call, answered from the High Temple on the hill and then, terribly, from the garrison towers near the harbor gate. The Order had not survived a thousand years by being slow. Somewhere in those horn-calls was a name being spoken over war-tables. His name. *Ravensworth.* A name that had been ash for a century, suddenly the only word in the city.

He climbed. The tanner's stair switchbacked up the cliffside to the roof-tops of the terraces, and from the third landing he stopped, despite himself, despite the horns, and looked back at the harbor.

Blue fire on black water. The torn fog. The leaning saint. Ten thousand people in the streets, afraid and awed and alive in ways the outlawry had forbidden for a hundred years — a grandmother making light with her bare hands to show her grandson, a priest of the small shrine on Rope Street standing in front of his own altar with his vestments torn open, weeping at the old carvings beneath the whitewash, carvings of gods the temple had ordered forgotten. Everything Yara had spent three years smothering in himself was happening to everyone at once, everywhere, and it was beautiful, and it was going to kill people, and both of those things were true in the same breath.

*I am the most powerful person alive,* he thought, and the thought fit him like a coat cut for a larger man. *And I have never been less free.*

Behind his ribs, the kneeling gods waited. Above him, the horns called. And somewhere in the middle distance — he could not see it, but he felt it the way he felt weather coming, the way he had always felt it, the gift that had never once let him down — something else was moving. Two somethings. One was a garden, miles inland, waking from a hundred years of stone, about to bloom.

The other wore temple armor and walked with a limp she tried to hide, and she was coming toward him through the burning blue city, and he could not tell — even with everything he now was, even with the sight of the gods pooled in him like light — whether she was coming to save him or to kill him.

Probably both. It was Sef, after all. It had always been both.

Yara pulled his hood lower, and climbed toward the rooftops, a king in exile in the only country he had ever loved, drowning in triumph, hunted by morning.
