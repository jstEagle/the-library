## 23. The Broken Garden Blooms

Yara felt it before anyone told him — a pulling at the base of his spine, like a fishhook set behind his navel and reeled slowly eastward. He had been awake for three days since the gods knelt, three days of Grayharbor erupting into wonder and terror in equal measure, charms flaring blue in shop windows, fountains running with light, old women weeping in doorways because their dead husbands' voices had come back on the wind. He had spent those three days being the most powerful and most hunted person alive, and he had not slept, because every time he closed his eyes he saw the throne.

Not the Raven Throne itself. He had never seen it. He saw what it would be: cold stone, high seat, a thousand years of worship pouring into him like water into a cracked jar that could never fill. He saw himself becoming his ancestors. That was the thing no one warned you about inheritance — that it was not a gift but a gravity, and gravity did not care whether you wanted to fall.

So when the hook behind his spine began to pull, he let it.

"You don't have to go," said Marta, the widow from the night market, standing in the doorway of his workshop with her son peeking around her skirts. The boy's color was good now. The candle had done its work, whatever the candle had been — a conduit, an antenna, a prayer Yara had made without knowing to whom. "Everyone's saying the garden bloomed. Everyone's saying it's yours."

"Everyone's right," Yara said, folding maps into a leather case with hands that would not quite steady. "That's the problem."

He took the coast road out of Grayharbor at dawn, alone, wearing a cartographer's plain coat instead of anything that might mark him as what he now was. It didn't matter. Word traveled faster than feet. By the time he crossed the first ridge above the valley where Ravensworth sat, he could see the road below him was black with people.

* * *

The garden had indeed bloomed.

It should have been beautiful. Some part of him — the part that still drew maps for the joy of drawing, the part that had once charmed a candle just to see a widow smile — some part of him looked down at the valley and felt the old ache, the homesickness for something he could not name, swell up like a tide.

Ravensworth lay in a bowl of hills, and at its heart the petrified garden had flowered overnight. For a century the estate had been a ruin of gray — gray walls, gray hedges, gray roses frozen mid-bloom like a scream turned to stone. Now color had broken across it like a fever breaking. Roses the size of cabbages, red as arterial blood. Hedges heavy with white blossoms that glowed faintly in the morning shade, as if each flower held a coal of the old magic. Ivy that had been mineral for a hundred years had gone green again and climbed the broken towers as if trying to pull them back together. And through it all moved hundreds of people — pilgrims in rough wool, kneeling among the beds; the curious; the desperate; the sick, carried on litters, laid against the roots of trees that had been stone yesterday and were flesh today.

The gods had woken when his claim activated. And the land remembered whose it was.

Yara stood on the ridge for a long time, letting the wind push at him, and hated it.

He hated it the way you hate a face in a dream that turns out to be your own. Every stone of the place was beautiful and every stone was a lie. This was the house that had sent assassins after a newborn because a prophecy said the Raven line would end. This was the ground his mother had run across with him in her arms, trading his soul for his life, buying him one small ordinary existence in a city that outlawed wonder so that wonder would never find him. And wonder had found him anyway, because wonder was his name, his blood, his inheritance — and the garden knew it before he did.

"They're saying the heir has come home," said a voice beside him.

Yara didn't startle. He had stopped startling two days ago, when a god the size of a cathedral had pressed its forehead to the mud of Grayharbor's main square and called him *lord*. But he turned carefully, hand drifting toward the chalk in his pocket — chalk and blood, door-paint, the only weapon that had ever truly saved him.

The man on the ridge wore pilgrim's gray, but he stood like a soldier, and under the hood his eyes were temple-gray too. Inquisitor. One of Sef's former order, or something shaped like it.

"I'm a mapmaker," Yara said. "I came to survey the road damage."

"Of course." The man smiled without warmth. "Then you'll want to know the roads are damaged by four hundred pilgrims, nine inquisitors, and — by my count — at least three men who are not pilgrims or inquisitors, who arrived separately, do not pray, and watch the gates." He paused. "Assassins, I'd wager. Temple bounty on the last Raven is rumored to be... substantial."

"And you're telling me this why?"

The inquisitor studied him. "Because Knight-Commander Andric vouched for you once, before she burned her warrant. And because I watched her stand in front of civilians during the Night of Unbound and take a god's blow meant for a child." His jaw tightened. "She was the best of us. If she believed you were worth saving, I am willing to be wrong about you. Once." He stepped back, merging with the ridge's shadow. "Go in by the postern gate, mapmaker. The front path is for miracles, and you are not one. You're a debt coming due."

* * *

The postern gate opened at his touch.

That was the worst part, Yara thought, picking his way through the servants' corridors of Ravensworth — corridors he had never walked, lit by windows he had never cleaned, past doors that swung wide for him without being asked. The house knew him. The whole estate knew him, root and stone, and welcomed him with the dumb adoring hunger of a dog that has waited a hundred years at a grave.

The halls were stripped bare — the Ravensworths had taken everything portable when the line fell, and scavengers had taken the rest — but the bones remained, and the bones were grand. Vaulted ceilings furred with new ivy. A great hall whose hearth was large enough to roast an ox whole, its mantel carved with ravens in flight, every bird's eye worn smooth by centuries of superstitious touching. Portraits had been cut from their frames long ago, but the frames remained, dozens of them, empty rectangles marching down the gallery like open mouths.

Yara walked the gallery slowly. In one frame, the wall behind had been protected from damp, and he could see the ghost of what had hung there: a woman in green, dark-haired, holding —

His chest seized.

He knew that pose. He had seen it in the regent's borrowed face beneath the mountain, in the archive where Sef read the truth of the bargain. His mother. This had been her portrait. Someone had cut her out of her own house, and the house had kept her shape anyway, waiting.

"Stop it," he said aloud, to the hallway, to himself. His voice echoed strangely, and somewhere far off in the walls, something answered — a settling, a sigh, the sound of a house adjusting itself around its master. "I'm not staying."

But even as he said it, he climbed the stairs his feet already knew, and stood in the nursery where the prophecy had been spoken over his cradle, and felt the floorboards warm under his boots like a living thing glad of his weight.

* * *

By afternoon the garden had swallowed him whether he willed it or not.

He went out because the crowds were pressing closer to the house, and someone needed to keep them off the oldest beds — the ones still half-stone, half-bloom, caught mid-transformation like creatures surfacing from deep water. He went out because a girl had fallen into the rose trench and cut her arm, and when he knelt to help her, the cut closed under his fingers before he'd done anything at all, and the crowd around them went silent, and then went to its knees.

"No," Yara said. "Get up. Please. I'm not —"

"*Lord Ravensworth*," someone breathed, and the word ran through the garden like fire through dry grass, and four hundred people bowed in the flowers.

He fled to the far end of the estate, past the orchard, to the low wall where the grounds fell away toward the river, and there he found the graves.

Small ones. Dozens. Servants, mostly, from the look of the worn markers — names half-legible, dates clustered in bad years. And at the end of the row, apart, one larger stone, unmarked except for a single raven carved deep enough to hold rainwater.

He didn't need a name on it. He knew whose it was the way he'd known the paths through the Undercourt, the way he'd known the shape of rooms he'd never entered. Blood knew. The Raven line had fed on worship for a thousand years, the chained god had told Sef, and maybe that was true, maybe his ancestors had been leeches in crowns — but they had also been buried here, in this dirt, under this ugly beautiful sky, and one of them had loved him enough to damn herself.

Yara sat down in the wet grass beside the raven stone and put his palm flat on it.

"I hate this place," he told the grave. His voice came out cracked. "I hate every stone of it. It's a monument to everything my family did wrong. It's a trap with flowers on it. They're going to kill me for it, you know. The temple, the throne-hunters, half the world. All because I was born under your roof." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "You bought me a life with my soul and it wasn't even enough. Nothing's enough. I could give away everything I have and they'd still come, because the blood doesn't wash out, does it? It's in the ground here. It's in the *garden*."

The garden, behind him, rustled though there was no wind.

And Yara understood, sitting there, that the garden was not celebrating him. It was *feeding*. The old magic of Ravensworth had been bound for a century, starved, petrified — and his claim had unbound it, and now it drank the sunlight and the attention and the prayers of four hundred pilgrims and grew fat and hungry, and it pulled at him with that fishhook feeling because he was its wellspring, its heir, its next meal if he ever sat down on the throne and let it have him.

His family hadn't just fed on worship. The *land* had. The whole estate was a mouth.

He got up off the grass very fast.

* * *

They came for him at dusk.

He heard them before he saw them — the particular quality of silence that armed men make, the way birds go quiet in a hedge. Yara was in the gallery again, don't ask him why, maybe because the empty frames were honest in a way nothing else in the house was. Three shapes detached from the doorway, moving with the loose economical grace of professionals, and the lead one said, in a pleasant Grayharbor accent, "Master cartographer. There's a purse with your name on it. Come quietly and it spends better without holes in it."

Yara's hand found the chalk in his pocket. His other hand was already bleeding — he'd split the skin on a rose thorn an hour ago, and he'd kept the blood, because he kept everything now, because everything was a tool.

"I'd rather not," he said.

The three men drew steel. And the house objected.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible thing. No thunder, no eruption of power. The gallery simply *changed its mind about them*. The floor tilted two degrees — imperceptible until you tried to lunge, and then your lunge became a stumble. A door that had stood open all day was suddenly shut, and locked, and the lock was old iron that groaned like something waking. The shadows under the empty frames deepened and reached, and one assassin screamed — actually screamed, a grown killer in good boots — and bolted for the window, and the ivy caught him. Held him. Not crushing. Just holding, patient as a web, while the other two backed away from a man who had not moved, who stood with chalk dust on his fingers and an expression on his face that he hoped looked like mercy and suspected looked like horror.

"Tell whoever sent you," Yara said quietly, "that the purse isn't big enough. Tell them the price keeps going up, and I'm not the one setting it."

The ivy opened. The men ran. The house sighed around him, satisfied, possessive, and Yara stood alone in the gallery of empty frames and felt, for the first time since the gods had knelt, something close to fear — not of the assassins, not of the temple, but of the slow realization settling over him like snow:

He could not leave this place. Not really. Wherever he went, it would grow around him. Grayharbor, the Undercourt, the far side of the world — the Raven inheritance was not a location. It was him. He was the broken garden, and it bloomed wherever he stood, and things came to feed on the bloom, and things came to kill the gardener, and there was no map out of it because he *was* the territory now.

He sank down against the wall beneath his mother's empty frame and put his head in his hands.

That was how the messenger found him — hours later, a boy of perhaps twelve, breathless from the ridge road, carrying a folded paper sealed in temple wax. The boy wouldn't come inside the gate. He held the letter out at arm's length like something that might bite, said "Knight-Commander said you'd know it's really from her," and fled.

Yara broke the seal.

Sef's handwriting, sharp and slanting, hurried, as if written fast and sent faster:

*Yara. The temple knows the garden bloomed. The high altar has ruled the last Raven must die — they voted within a day of the Night of Unbound, and my name was the first they offered the commission. I asked for three days. They gave me three days.*

*I am not writing to warn you to run. You can't outrun this and we both know it, and I know you, and you won't.*

*I'm writing because I'm coming to Ravensworth. Not as the temple's blade. As me. Whatever that's worth now.*

*Three days. Make them count.*

*— S*

Yara read it twice. Then he looked up at the empty frame above him, at the pale ghost-shape of a woman in green holding a child that had been painted out of history, and he laughed — a small, wrecked, genuine laugh, the first in days.

Three days. The temple was sending its best killer, and its best killer was the only person alive he wanted to see.

Outside, the broken garden went on blooming in the dark, glowing softly, hungrily, like a lamp left burning for someone who might never come home — and Yara got up, and found paper and ink in a house that provided them before he asked, and began, in the gallery of his murdered family, to draw a map of everywhere he might still run.

Just in case she was late.
