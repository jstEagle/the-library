## 24. The Temple's Ultimatum

The summons came at dawn, carried by a boy on a lathered horse who would not meet Sef's eyes.

She read it standing in the doorway of a roadside inn forty miles from Grayharbor, the paper trembling not from wind but from her hand. The seal was black wax pressed with the sun-and-sword of the High Temple — not the regional chapter's mark, which she had seen ten thousand times, but the Primarch's own. She had only ever seen that seal twice before in her life. Once when she was elevated to knight-errant. Once when they stripped it away.

*Come home,* the letter said. *All is forgiven that can be forgiven.*

The words were chosen with a surgeon's care. *That can be forgiven.* Everything else was an invitation to imagine what could not.

Sef burned the letter in the inn's cold hearth and rode anyway. There had never been any question of not riding. That was the trap of duty — it did not need to compel you. It only needed to wait, patient as stone, until you came back to it on your own feet, calling your surrender a choice.

* * *

The High Temple of Vael Domarre had been built by people who wanted to make smallness feel like sin. Its nave rose so high that the clerics' incense smoke vanished before it reached the ceiling, ascending into darkness like prayers no one expected answered. Sef walked its length in her travel-stained coat, swordless — they had taken her blade at the gate, politely, with both hands, as one accepts a burden from the grieving — and felt eleven years fall away. She had been nineteen when she first crossed this marble. She had known everything then. It was the last time she had known everything.

They made her wait three hours in a side chapel, which was also deliberate. She used the time to study the frescoes: the Nine Ascended, crowned in light, and beneath their feet the painted figures of the Old Powers, bound and falling, their faces scraped away by some pious generation. She had knelt before these images every day of her childhood without once wondering what the gods had looked like before someone decided their faces were too dangerous to keep.

Now she knew one of them. Half-mad, chained beneath a mountain, he had wept when he told her what her church had done — no. What her church had *inherited*. The Ravensworth line had bound the gods, fed on them, grown fat on worship like ticks on a wound. And the temple, when the binding was done, had simply moved into the empty house and called the silence holiness.

Her heresy, the tribunal had called it. *Believing the gods should be free.* They had said it like a diagnosis.

"Sef Andric."

The voice came from behind her, and she turned to find the Primarch himself standing in the chapel door. Aurelian Voss was smaller than his reputation — a narrow man in plain gray, with the kind of face that looked kind until you noticed it never changed. He had been her examiner, once. He had told her she had the finest instinct for the divine of any novice in a generation. He had also signed the paper that unmade her.

"Your Holiness." She started to kneel. His raised hand stopped her halfway down, and she understood: this meeting was to be conducted between equals, which meant it was a lie from the first breath.

"You look well," he said, "for a woman who has spent a season consorting with abomination."

"I've spent a season learning things the temple taught me not to see."

"Yes." He moved past her to the frescoes, hands folded, studying the scraped faces as if reading something written there. "You know, I argued against your degradation. Did you know that? I told the tribunal: this one has not lost her faith. She has misplaced it. There is a difference, and the difference is recoverable." He turned. In the colored light from the window his eyes were almost gentle. "I am pleased to report I have been proven right."

"Have you."

"The world is breaking open, Sef. You've seen it. We all have. Magic crawling out of gutters in cities that outlawed it a century ago. Gods answering prayers through candle flames. And above it all—" he let the pause stretch, savoring it, "—a garden in the north that bloomed overnight after two hundred years of stone. Ravensworth. The name alone should chill you, even now. Even fallen."

It did more than chill her. It went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor, because she had stood in that garden. She had watched Yara walk paths his feet remembered and his mind did not, and she had seen the petrified roses split open overnight, color flooding back into them like blood returning to a limb. She had held his hand among the flowers and thought: *this is what the world could be.* And then she had left him, because love and faith had pulled in opposite directions, and she had chosen — God help her, she still didn't know what she had chosen.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"Want?" The Primarch smiled. "I want what the temple has always wanted. Order. A world where the divine serves the faithful rather than devouring them. And right now, the single greatest threat to that order is a man who does not yet understand what he is — or worse, one who is beginning to." He stepped closer. "Yara Ravensworth. Last heir of the Raven Throne. The binding runs in his blood, Sef. Every god that wakes, wakes *through him*. Every miracle in Grayharbor traces back to him like water to a spring. Cut the spring, and the flood stops."

"And if you cut him," she said quietly, "the gods don't stop waking. They wake *unbound*. Unled. Hungry. Is that what your order wants?"

Something flickered across the Primarch's face — not doubt, but the place where doubt had been surgically removed. "We have contingencies."

"You have contingencies for the end of the world."

"We have contingencies for everything. It is why there is still a world." He gestured, and from the shadow of the doorway a clerk approached bearing a cushion, and on the cushion lay a sword.

Not just any sword. *Vigilance*, the knight-errant's blade they had taken from her the day of her fall, its hilt wrapped in fresh white leather, its edge honed to a brightness that hurt to look at. Beside it lay a scroll sealed in black wax, and a ring — the signet of full restoration, rank, name, and honor returned entire.

"Full restoration," the Primarch said. "Every charge struck from the record. Your oath renewed before the whole conclave. Knight-Captain, if you want it — the youngest in the temple's history. And all we ask in return is the one thing you are uniquely suited to give." He lifted the scroll and held it out to her. "The last Raven. Dead by your hand, witnessed and sworn. Bring us his head or his heart's blood, and come home."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere far above, incense smoke climbed toward a ceiling no prayer had ever touched.

Sef looked at the sword. Eleven years of her life hung on that hilt — every dawn devotion, every hard-won mastery, every night she had lain awake certain she was doing right. They were offering to give her back to herself. That was the cruelty of it, and the genius. Not a bribe. A resurrection.

And the price was a man who mapped impossible roads, who paid double at rivers so other people could keep their memories, who kissed her among a thousand hanging souls and made even damnation feel like a kind of grace.

"He saved people," she said. "In Grayharbor. In the Undercourt. Whatever he is—"

"Whatever he is," the Primarch interrupted, softly, "he is the last pillar of a tyranny that enslaved the divine. Sef. Look at me." She looked. "I know what he was to you. The tribunal knows. Everyone in this building who matters knows. Do you imagine we chose you despite that?" He let the question hang, then answered it with terrible kindness. "We chose you *because* of it. A stranger's blade makes a martyr. A lover's makes a lesson. When the last Raven dies at the hand of the woman who loved him, every soul in this world tempted by the old magic will understand exactly what it costs. Your love will save thousands. Isn't that worth more than one man?"

The words went into her and found nothing to argue with, because every word was true in the way doctrine is true — true within its own walls, and the walls were the world, as far as she had ever been taught.

She took the scroll. Not the sword. Not yet. Just the scroll, the weight of it no heavier than a letter, heavier than a mountain.

"Three days," she said.

The Primarch's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The conclave marches in five. The host assembles at the border of the Ravensworth lands."

"Then you can spare me three." She met his eyes and kept her voice level, and marveled somewhere distant at how steady it sounded, how completely it hid the screaming. "Give me three days. If I do not return with proof of his death, send your knights. I will not run. I want you to understand that. I am not asking time to flee in. I'm asking time to finish something."

"Finish what?"

She looked at Vigilance on its cushion, bright and patient and ready to be hers again.

"A conversation," she said. "One I left unfinished in a garden."

* * *

They gave her the sword at dusk, because she asked for it and because refusing would have meant admitting they feared her, and the temple never admitted fear. She buckled it on in the pilgrims' yard outside the walls, feeling the familiar weight settle against her hip like a hand she'd forgotten she missed, and hated how good it felt. That was the thing about coming home. Home fit. That was how it got you.

She rode north out of Vael Domarre as the bells rang compline, and did not look back at the city, because looking back was a habit of people who had already decided.

Three days. She had asked for them honestly — that was the worst part, that she had not lied to the Primarch, because she wasn't sure anymore what the lie would be. Three days to kill him? Three days to warn him? Three days to stand in front of Yara Ravensworth with a restored sword on her hip and find out which woman rode north: the knight who had burned her warrant and followed him into the dark, or the templar who had walked away from him in the Undercourt because the truth of her own church was more than her faith could carry?

Both women were real. Both had ridden this road. They shared one body the way a house shares its walls with a fire.

By midnight she made camp in a hollow below the road, and sat awake with Vigilance across her knees, watching the stars wheel over the northern hills. Somewhere out there, past those hills, a garden was blooming that should have stayed stone. Somewhere in it, a man who had traded his soul for strangers' freedom was learning to hate every stone of his inheritance, waiting — she knew he was waiting; he had always been waiting, since the day she left without goodbye — for her to come back or stay gone.

He would open the gate for her. That was the unbearable thing. However she came — armed or unarmed, in love or in judgment — he would open the gate, because he was the kind of man who opened doors, even the ones that killed him.

Sef drew the sword and looked at her reflection in the blade, broken up by the firelight into pieces.

"Three days," she told the woman in the steel.

Then she sheathed it, kicked dirt over the fire, and rode north through the dark, toward the garden, toward the man, toward whatever waited at the end of the road — knowing that behind her, five days out, the temple's host was already marching, and that worse things than knights marched behind it.
