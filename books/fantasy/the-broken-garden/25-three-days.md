## 25. Three Days

She came at dusk, when the petrified garden went the color of old honey and the pilgrims had gone home to their tents beyond the walls.

Yara saw her from the gatehouse and knew at once that something was wrong — not because Sef Andric was there, but because of how she stood. She did not come up the road like a woman arriving. She came like a woman taking her last look at a place she expected never to see again, walking slowly, head bare, sword left behind in its scabbard with the strap unbuckled. A knight who meant to fight buckles her sword. A knight who meant to die does not bother.

He met her at the gate before she could knock on a door that no longer had locks. His ancestors had sealed them all; his blood had opened every one.

"You're not carrying your blade," he said.

"I'm not carrying my warrant either." She held up her hands, empty, palms out — the gesture of surrender, or of showing him she had nothing left to hide behind. "I need you to listen to me, Yara, and I need you to not argue until I'm finished."

"That's two impossible things."

"Yara."

The sound of his name in her mouth stopped whatever cleverness he had queued behind his teeth. He had heard her say it a hundred times down in the dark, but never like this — never stripped of irony, never afraid.

"The temple marches," she said. "Not inquisitors this time. The full host. Nine hundred knights, three hundred battle-priests, and the Choir of Unmaking itself has been given dispensation to sing. Highfather Vess signed the order four days ago. They'll be at the Grayharbor road by week's end." Her jaw worked. "And they are the *merciful* part of what's coming."

"Behind them," Yara said quietly. He had felt it for days, a pressure under the soles of his feet, the mountain's attention turning toward the surface like an eye opening.

"Behind them," Sef confirmed. "The regent is done waiting. Whatever bargain you struck below — it's coming due, and it isn't coming alone. My contacts in the temple's lower archives found records going back six hundred years. Every time a Raven heir was crowned, within a season the Undercourt surfaced somewhere. Flooded valleys. A village swallowed whole in the reign of the third Ravensworth. The mountain *opens*, Yara. And it opens where the heir stands."

The garden breathed around them — the impossible, blooming garden, roses and nightshade and things with no surface names, all grown overnight from petrified stone. Somewhere among the hedges the widow's son was laughing at something, high and bright, one of the dozen strays who had attached themselves to Ravensworth since the flowering.

"So I should run," Yara said. "Take the boy, take whoever will come, and go somewhere the mountain can't find me."

"No." Sef stepped closer. "Because I've read those same archives, and there's a second pattern. Every time the court surfaced, it was because the heir ran. It hunts its own blood. The only times it stayed closed were the reigns when the Raven sat the throne and held the door shut from above." She looked away, toward the darkening hills. "I spent three days trying to find you another answer. There isn't one. You stand, or everything between here and Grayharbor drowns in roots."

"And the temple?"

"The temple doesn't care about drowning villages. The temple cares that a Raven lives." Her voice cracked, just barely, on the last word. "Vess offered me restoration. Full rank, absolution, my name carved back into the rolls of the Order. All I have to do is put a knife in the last heir of the Raven Throne."

The words fell into the space between them and neither moved to pick them up.

"How long," Yara said finally, "did you ask for?"

"Three days."

* * *

They spent the first day pretending the world was ordinary.

It was Yara's idea, though he never said so aloud, and Sef understood without being told — he saw it in the way she let herself be led, unresisting, when he took her hand after breakfast and walked her out into the garden as if there were no armies on any road. The morning was cool and gold-edged. Mist still clung to the low hedges. The widow's son, Tam, trailed them at first until Yara gave him the solemn task of watering the eastern beds, and then it was just the two of them among flowers that had no right to exist.

"Do you know what this one is?" Yara crouched by a vine heavy with silver-blue blossoms. "Because I don't. I've mapped forty realms and I've never seen it anywhere but here."

"It looks like something that would kill you beautifully."

"It probably would." He touched one petal and it chimed, faintly, like a wet finger on glass. "My mother planted these. Or her mother. Someone in the long line of people who built all this and fed on worship and bound gods and did every terrible thing the archives say." He looked up at her. "And someone in that line also grew *this*. Somebody knelt in the dirt with their hands in the soil and made something lovely in the middle of all that rot. I keep trying to hold both of those truths and I can't. So I water the flowers instead."

Sef lowered herself beside him, armor-less in a borrowed linen shirt, and for a while she said nothing. Then: "When I was noviced, my teacher told me holiness was a wall. You build it stone by stone, rule by rule, and it keeps the world's filth out." She picked a fallen petal, turned it over. "She was wrong. Holiness is a garden. It dies if you stop tending it, and half of what grows in it is weeds, and you love it anyway."

"That's the most heretical thing you've ever said."

"I've been assured I'm good at heresy." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, and Yara thought: *I want to keep this. Whatever happens, I want to keep exactly this.*

They worked until noon. He taught her the names of things; she turned out to have a gardener's hands, patient and sure, and she laughed — actually laughed — when a snail made off with a cutting she'd been babying. Tam brought them water from the well and pronounced judgment on their rows ("crooked, but with heart"). In the afternoon Yara showed her the gatehouse where he'd begun carving names, and she ran her thumb over the two grooves already cut there — *YARA* and *SEF*, side by side, done in some reckless hour he'd never explained and she'd never asked about.

"When this is over," she said, "if there is an over — I want to finish these letters properly. Yours is missing its crossbar."

"Noted. Assuming we survive the week."

"Assuming nothing." She caught his wrist. "Say it plainly. We finish the letters."

"We finish the letters," he agreed, and the lie in it — the *if* hiding under the words like a snail under a leaf — hurt less than the truth would have.

* * *

The second day, she gave him her truth, and he gave her his.

It began with rain — soft summer rain that came down through the broken roof of the old conservatory, pattering on glass gone green with age. They sheltered there among dead orchids and living moss, and Sef sat on a workbench built for hands a century buried, and started talking the way people talk when they've decided not to stop.

"My sister's name was Lira." Yara went very still; he was one of perhaps three people alive who knew what the river had taken from her. "I don't remember her face. I remember that she existed, that I loved her, that she died of fever when I was nine and I prayed so hard over her body that the temple marked me for sensitivity to the divine. But her face—" She spread her hands. "Gone. Paid at the toll, so that I could keep—" She stopped. Looked at him. "So that I could keep *you*. You paid double that day. I didn't know until later. You stole memory out of your own head so I could keep something of hers."

"You were going to give everything," Yara said. "You'd have walked through that river empty."

"Yes. And you decided I wasn't allowed to." Her laugh had no humor in it. "That should have made me furious. It did, for about a day. And then I realized what it meant, and I've been terrified of what it means ever since."

"Sef—"

"Let me finish. I get three days and I'm going to use all of them." She slid off the bench and stood close enough that he could see the rain caught in her lashes. "My heresy was never doubt. Doubt is cheap; half the Order doubts. My heresy was believing the gods should be *free* — free to be loved or ignored, free to fail, free to be small. And then I learned that the family of the man I— " the word snagged, and she pushed past it, "—the man I love is the reason they were chained. Your blood built the cage, Yara. Every chain in the deep places was forged by a Ravensworth."

"I know."

"And I love you anyway. That's my heresy now. That's the one that will burn me." Her voice dropped. "I've made peace with it. What I haven't made peace with is what happens in five days, when nine hundred knights arrive and the mountain opens and you're standing on the fault line between everything I ever served and everything I ever wanted."

He reached up and touched her face, tracing the scar along her jaw that she'd gotten at the ford, the one she refused to have healed even when healing was legal again.

"Then let me tell you my truth, and we'll be even," he said. "I'm not going to sit the throne."

Her breath caught.

"I mean it. Not as defiance, not as sacrifice theater. I've thought about it every way there is. If I take the Raven Throne, I become the cage. I become the thing my family was — the binding, the hunger, the god-fed line. Even if I rule gently, even if I rule *well*, the throne itself is a mechanism, and I'd be its hand. And if I run, the court surfaces and drowns the coast." He exhaled. "There's a third door. There's always a third door; I'm a cartographer, finding doors is the entire discipline. But I need time to draw it, and time is the one thing nobody's selling."

"How much time?"

"Three days," he said, and they both heard the shape of the joke, bitter and small, and Sef leaned her forehead against his and stayed there while the rain filled the silence.

That night they did not sleep apart. Nothing needed saying about it; the decision made itself the way rivers decide, by gravity. They lay in the narrow bed in the gatehouse room, her back against his chest, listening to the wind move through a garden that should have been stone. Neither slept much. Neither pretended otherwise. At some hour past midnight she felt him awake and said, without turning, "Tell me the map again. The one you're drawing. The third door."

So he did — whispering into her hair about known and unknown, about how a soul cannot be held by what is truly known, about roads drawn so completely that captivity becomes impossible, and she followed him through it the way she'd followed him through the dark under the world, trusting the voice more than the path. When he faltered, she prompted. When he doubted, she said *again*, and he began once more.

It was the closest thing to prayer either of them had performed in years.

* * *

The third day, the scouts' horn sounded at dawn — one of Tam's older friends, posted on the ridge, signaling dust on the Grayharbor road. Not the temple yet. Something smaller, faster, moving ahead of the host.

Sef was armored within minutes, borrowed mail fitted to her, sword belted and buckled this time, and Yara watched her become a knight again in the span of a breath and grieved it.

"Riders," the scout reported. "Six. Temple colors, but wrong — no insignia of rank. And they're not riding toward the host. They're riding here."

"Vess's advance knives," Sef said grimly. "Sent to make sure the restoration offer got answered. Or to do the work if it didn't." She looked at Yara, and everything unsaid passed between them in a single glance. "Get the children inside the inner walls. Wake the widow. I'll meet them at the gate."

"You'll meet six knights alone?"

"I'll meet six knights *at a gate defended by the last heir of the Raven Throne*, whether he likes it or not." She was already moving. "Stay behind me. Stay *visible*. Let them see what they'd be killing."

The riders came up the road through the morning mist, and halted out of bowshot, and their leader spurred forward alone — a broad woman with a burned face and the flat eyes of someone who had stopped seeing people as people years ago. Inquisitor-Captain Dree. Sef had named her once, in the dark of the Undercourt, in a list of names spoken like curses.

"Sef Andric." Dree's voice carried across the grass. "The Order offers you one final grace. Stand aside. Deliver the abomination, and the Choir will strike your name from the heresy rolls instead of carving it from your skin."

"He's not an abomination," Sef called back, level and clear. "He's the only thing standing between your army and the mountain opening under it. Ride back and tell Vess that if the host sets foot on this ground, she'll be fighting the Undercourt, not one man. Tell her the heir offers parley. Real parley. Before week's end."

Dree smiled with the burned half of her face. "The heir offers nothing. The heir *is* offered." Her gaze slid past Sef, found Yara at the gate, and lingered with a hunger that had nothing human in it — and beneath it, faintly, Yara felt something else looking through her, something old and root-bound and patient, wearing a temple knight's eyes like a mask.

*Come home, little map,* said a voice that was not Dree's, directly into his skull, in his mother's cadence. *The regent grows tired of waiting. Three days were the knight's. Yours are spent.*

The riders wheeled and galloped back down the road. Sef watched them go, then turned to him, and whatever she saw in his face drained hers of color.

"What did it say?" she asked. "What did *she* say?"

Yara looked east, where the dust of the temple host was beginning to rise against the horizon like a second dawn — and south, where the mountain loomed, and where, very faintly, he could feel roots shifting in the dark like a sleeper turning over.

"She said my three days are spent," he answered. "And I think she's right. Sef — the ground's been humming since dawn. Listen."

He took her hand and pressed it flat against the gatepost. Beneath the wood, beneath the soil, beneath the whole sleeping garden, something vast below the world had begun, very slowly, to climb.
