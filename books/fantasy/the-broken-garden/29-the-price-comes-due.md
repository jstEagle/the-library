## 29. The Price Comes Due

It began with the birds.

Yara noticed it on the third morning after the Night of Unbound — the sparrows that had colonized Ravensworth's eaves would not land near him. They circled the broken fountain where he sat with his charts spread across the stone, and when he rose they scattered as if a hawk had passed overhead, though nothing moved in the pale gold light but dust.

He told himself it was the gods still loose in the air, their unbinding making every living thing skittish. He was very good at telling himself things. Three years of hiding his magic in a city that outlawed wonder had made him a scholar of comfortable lies.

The second sign came at noon, when the widow's son — Tam, eight years old, healed by a candle that burned blue — looked straight through him and asked Sef who she was talking to.

Sef did not laugh it off. That was the first thing Yara truly understood about what was happening: she didn't laugh, didn't make a joke of the boy's blank stare, didn't say *children are strange*. She went white to the lips and gripped Yara's wrist hard enough to bruise, and her eyes said what her voice would not yet say aloud.

*It's starting.*

* * *

They found the first vanished footprint that evening, in the long gallery of Ravensworth where the portraits of his ancestors hung like accusations. Yara had walked the corridor an hour before, tracking ash from the garden across the marble, and now the tracks were gone — not swept, not faded, but *unwritten*, the marble smooth and unmarked as though no one had ever crossed it. Except for one print. One, preserved in gray ash, exactly halfway down the hall, as if whatever force was collecting him had paused there to consider, then decided it could wait.

"It's early," Sef said. Her voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant fear wearing armor. "The bargain wasn't due. You gave your soul freely, yes, but the regent agreed to terms — the court fed, the city spared, and you lived out your natural span before—"

"Contracts beneath the mountain aren't measured in years." Yara crouched over the single footprint. His own boot, his own stride, already becoming rumor. "They're measured in weight. And I've been getting lighter."

"What does that mean?"

He didn't answer immediately. Instead he held up his hand toward the nearest window, toward the last slant of sun, and Sef saw it — saw the light pass through his fingers the way it passes through smoke, catching at the edges, dimming where it should have thrown shadows sharp as knives.

His shadow was wrong. Thinner than it had been that morning. Fainter at the hem, like a garment worn through.

"The regent isn't stealing me all at once," Yara said quietly. "That would be theft, and theft can be contested, even down there. Especially down there. It's *collecting* me. Installments. A little each day, each hour, so that by the time I'm gone, no one will be able to say when it happened or point to the moment of taking." He laughed, and the laugh sounded borrowed. "It's elegant. My mother would have hated it. Or admired it. With her it was always hard to tell."

Sef crossed the gallery and took his face in both hands — a knight's grip, blunt and certain, the way she did everything. "Then we go back down. Tonight. Now. We walk into that cathedral of roots and we put a knife against the regent's throat until it gives back what it took."

"You can't hold a knife to a concept, Sef."

"I've been wanting to try for years."

Despite everything — despite the thinning light and the vanishing footprints and the boy who hadn't known his name — Yara smiled. That was the trap of loving someone: even at the edge of erasure, they could pull a smile out of you like a splinter.

"There's something worse," he said. "Something I haven't told you. Last night I tried to write my name in the margin of my mother's atlas. The old hand, the Raven script — I've written it since I was a child, it's in my blood deeper than the magic is." He turned his left forearm over. There, where he had inked his signature a hundred times in practice, the skin was bare. Not scarred. Bare, and smooth, as if the name had never been written there at all. "It won't stay. Ink, blood, carved into wood — nothing holds it. I'm being unwritten, Sef. From the outside in. First the record, then the memory, then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Then the man."

* * *

Grayharbor knew him, and then it didn't, and the speed of it was the cruelest part.

On the fourth day, Marta the chandler — who had bought charms from him for three years, who owed him eleven coppers and a grudge — served another customer while Yara stood at her counter and never once raised her eyes. On the fifth day, the night market opened its lanterns along the harbor road and no stall made room for him, because no stall remembered there had ever been a cartographer who worked the corner by the fishmonger's, selling luck to people who couldn't afford to believe in it.

He stood in the crowd that had been his congregation and felt it flow around him like water around a stone that had always been there and would always be there and required no thought at all. A drunk sailor leaned on him without apology or awareness. A child ran through the space where his body was and only stumbled a little, frowning, as if the air had gone briefly thick.

"They're not ignoring me," he told Sef that night, in the room above the chandlery that no longer, technically, belonged to anyone. "That's what I keep getting wrong. They're not choosing not to see me. The knowledge of me is simply... draining out of them. Like color from an old tapestry. Marta looked right at me today and her eyes went *through* me. There was nothing to look at. I'm becoming a gap shaped like a man."

"Then I'll be the exception." Sef sat sharpening her sword on the windowsill, an old habit, the rhythmic whisper of steel the only sound. "I remember everything. Every day. Your name, your face, the way you talk when you're frightened — faster, like you're outrunning the sentence. I remember the mine, and the river that runs uphill, and the toll, and what you paid so I wouldn't have to. I remember all of it."

"For now."

"*For now* is a coward's phrase. Say something better."

He looked at her — this woman who had burned her warrant for him, killed for him, lost her sister's face in a river for him, and loved him against every law her life had been built on — and he tried to find something better to say, and found instead the truth, which was worse.

"Sef. When it reaches you — and it will, the regent is thorough, it collects debts from everyone who owes — I need you to promise me you'll let it happen."

The whetstone stopped.

"No."

"You don't know what you'd be carrying. To be the only one—"

"I know *exactly* what I'd be carrying." She set the sword down with deliberate care, the way she did when violence was close, and turned to face him fully. "I carried my sister's death for six years before the river took even the memory of her face, and I am still standing. I carried my oaths after I broke them, and I am still standing. I will carry you, Yara Ravensworth, for as long as there is anything left of you to carry, and then I will carry the shape of you, and I will not thank you for asking me to put it down." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, a hairline fracture in temple-forged steel. "You want to protect me by making me forget you. That's not protection. That's just dying alone, and I forbid it."

"You forbid it," he repeated softly.

"I'm told I'm still a knight of something. Possibly of you."

And because he could not argue — because some traitorous part of him sang at being claimed so fiercely by someone sworn to destroy what he was — Yara crossed the small room and kissed her, and for a while the fading stopped mattering, or seemed to, which in a city full of answered prayers was nearly the same thing.

* * *

The regent came on the seventh day, wearing his mother's face.

It did not surface, as it had before the crowds of Grayharbor. It simply appeared — in the reflection of the window while Yara worked on the new atlas, in the steam above his tea, in the dark of the garden where the petrified roses had begun, impossibly, to bloom again. Always at a distance. Always smiling with his mother's mouth, which was also his mouth, which was a cruelty the regent had discovered early and refined ever since.

*"You've noticed,"* it said, from the well of the garden dusk, in a voice like roots growing through stone. *"Good. I dislike debtors who pretend surprise."*

"The terms were clear," Yara said. He kept his hands steady on the gatepost. Beside him, Sef had gone rigid, her hand finding his. "The court feeds. The city stands. And I live my span before the soul passes. You're collecting early. Even your own kind call that theft."

*"My own kind,"* the regent mused, and the mother-face tilted with something almost wistful. *"Do you know how long I have worn faces, little heir? Longer than your line has worn crowns. I have watched empires of root and rot rise and fall beneath the mountain, and in all that time I have learned one law older than contract, older than court: that which is freely given may be taken when the giver ceases to give it freely."*

"I haven't ceased."

*"Haven't you?"* The smile widened, and it was terrible because it was familiar. *"You spend your days trying to give yourself away — to the souls below, to the city above, to the knight beside you. A soul given in ten thousand pieces is still given, Yara Ravensworth. I am merely gathering what you have scattered. Call it tidying."*

"Then take me," Yara said. "All at once. Here, now, and leave the rest alone — his memories, her memories, the widow's son who says my name in his prayers. Take the whole debt in one hand and be done with it."

For a moment — one long breath of garden air heavy with impossible roses — the regent's borrowed face went still, and behind his mother's eyes Yara glimpsed something vast and hungry and old turning over, considering.

*"No,"* it said at last, gently, which was worse than any threat. *"Where would be the worship in that? A god fed in a single meal grows fat and lazy. But a meal that lasts..."* The image of his mother reached out, and though its fingers never touched him, the cold of them sank into his chest anyway, into the place behind his ribs where the hook of returning magic had first caught him a lifetime ago. *"Every day a little less of you. Every day the world agrees a little more that you were never here. And when the last piece comes due — when even she—"*

It looked at Sef.

*"—when even she begins to wonder why she grieves — then the debt is paid in full, and beautifully, and no one will ever be able to say it was robbery."*

The garden went dark. The regent was gone. And Yara found himself on his knees among the blooming roses he had planted from thirty mapped worlds, watching his own hands flicker like candleflames in wind.

* * *

By the ninth day, half of Grayharbor had forgotten him entirely, and the other half had never known.

The maps were the worst. He went to his workshop above the chandlery — the hidden room behind the false wall, the sanctuary of his double life — and found his own annotations fading from the charts. The routes he'd dreamed into the Undercourt, the doorways he'd painted onto walls with blood and chalk, the marginalia in his careful cartographer's hand: all of it going pale, going ghostly, going the way of the footprint in the gallery. His work was being erased alongside him. In a month, perhaps less, there would be no evidence anywhere in the world that Yara Ravensworth had existed — no chart, no charm, no name in any ledger, no grief in any heart.

No heart except one.

He knew it because he tested it, shamefully, secretly, the way a drowning man tests a rope. He would stand behind Sef in the kitchen of Ravensworth and say nothing, and wait, and count the seconds until she turned and said, without looking up from the bread or the blade or the map she was studying:

"You're doing the frightened-fast talking again. In your head. I can hear it from here."

Or: "Your shadow's thinner today. Eat something. I don't know if it helps, but I can't watch you fade on an empty stomach."

Or, once, at three in the morning, when he woke gasping from a dream of the cathedral of roots and the thousand hanging lanterns of stolen lives: "Tell me again." And he would tell her — the Undercourt, the regent, the bargain, the vault where his blood had opened the truth of the Raven Throne — and she would listen with her whole body, her eyes never leaving his face, hoarding him the way misers hoard gold, and when he finished she would say, "Again tomorrow. And the day after. As many tomorrows as you have, and then all of mine."

She wrote it down too. That was her idea, on the eleventh day, when his signature had vanished even from the letters he'd signed for Tam's mother. She filled a journal in her blunt soldier's hand — *Yara Ravensworth. Cartographer. Born under a bad star to a woman who loved him enough to damn him. Left-handed. Afraid of deep water since the river took my sister's face and gave back mine. Laughs at his own jokes before the punchline. Kisses like an apology and a dare at the same time.* Page after page. An atlas of one person, drawn by the only witness left.

"Why?" he asked her, watching her write by candlelight, watching the flame throw his almost-shadow against the wall.

"Because paper remembers longer than people," she said. "And because if the world is going to erase you, then the world can bloody well fight me for every word."

* * *

On the thirteenth day, the innkeeper of the Drowned Bell — who had never met Yara, who knew him only as the stranger Sef sometimes spoke of — squinted at the space beside her and asked, kindly, if she was traveling alone.

Sef went very quiet.

That night she did not sleep. Yara woke near dawn and found her sitting upright in the narrow bed, the journal open on her knees, reading herself their entire history in a whisper — the night market, the blue candle, the warrant burning, the uphill river, the toll, the lanterns, the kiss witnessed by a thousand borrowed lights — and her voice never wavered, but her hands shook so badly the pages rattled.

"It touched the edges of me today," she said, when she saw he was awake. "At the inn. For a moment — one moment — I thought, *why am I talking to an empty chair*, and then I caught myself, and I held on." She looked up, and her eyes in the pre-dawn gray were the most frightening thing he had seen in all his journeys beneath the world, because they were determined. "But it's testing me now, Yara. Learning where I'm weakest. And I am weakest exactly where you'd want me to be — I love you too much to lose you cleanly, so it will try to make losing you feel like relief. Like peace. Like a wound finally allowed to close."

"Then we run," he said. "There are doors I've painted that the regent doesn't know about. Realms I've mapped that even the old gods forgot. We disappear into the margins of the world, you and I, and I fade slowly somewhere beautiful, and you—"

"And I spend fifty years talking to a chair." She closed the journal. "No."

"Sef—"

"*No.*" She rose, and pulled on her boots, and began buckling on her sword with the crisp economy of a woman preparing for battle, because she was. "I've spent two weeks watching you plan our surrender. Every option you offer ends with you politely evaporating and me learning to live around the hole. So here is my option, and it's the last one I'll hear tonight: we go back down. Through the collapsed mine, past the garden, past the mousehole, down the river that runs uphill — I remember the way, all of it, every turn, because remembering is the one thing I do better than anyone alive. We walk into the cathedral of roots, and we find the regent wearing your mother's face, and we end this contract the way contracts should have ended from the beginning."

"With what? I have nothing left to bargain with. That was the whole point of the bargain."

"You have me." She said it without ceremony, without romance, the way she reported terrain. "A disgraced knight of a temple that wants me dead, carrying a sword blessed by gods who are currently loose and unbound and possibly grateful to the man who freed them. You have a thousand souls in that court who owe their freedom to you. You have a mouse-god holding a promise. And you have—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, knight to the last, "—you have one witness. One person in all the world who remembers you completely. The regent's whole strategy is erasure, Yara. Erasure fails the moment even one memory stands against it. So we make sure I stand. Down there, where the debt was made. In front of the court itself."

Yara looked at her for a long time. The candle between them guttered, steadied, guttered — and in its light his shadow flickered on the wall like a signal fire burning low.

"If we go down," he said slowly, "and it takes the rest of me before we reach the court—"

"Then I will carry you the last of the way," Sef said. "In here." She pressed her fist, once, against her own sternum. "You taught me the realms below, Yara. Let me teach the Undercourt something the Ravensworths never learned: that a soul is not only what a person keeps. It's what someone else refuses to let go."

Outside, far beneath the sleeping garden, in the cathedral of roots where sold souls hung like lanterns, something ancient turned in its sleep and smiled with a dead woman's mouth — and began, patiently, hungrily, to count what remained.

Thirteen days. One witness. One road, going down.

Sef picked up the journal, and the sword, and Yara's hand, and led him toward the door.
