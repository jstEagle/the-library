## 32. The Court Revolt

The lanterns woke before the regent knew.

Yara felt it first as a pull behind his ribs — the same hook that had snagged him in Grayharbor a lifetime ago, back when a candle burned blue and the world still had edges. But this was different. This was not one prayer tugging at his name. This was a thousand.

He was fading. He had been fading since the river of forgetting took its toll and the regent collected early, and now he hung in Sef's arms like a coat someone had forgotten to take out of a closet — present, but no longer quite *his*. His footprints vanished behind them as they crossed the root-hewn floor of the Undercourt. His name had gone soft in his own mouth, a word spoken through water. He could not remember the color of his own eyes, only that Sef looked at them sometimes as if they mattered.

But the lanterns remembered.

They hung from the vaulted roots of the cathedral by their chains of shadow, each one burning with the life its owner had never lived — the wedding not attended, the child not raised, the song not sung, all of it compressed into light and left to burn for the court's appetite. For a century they had hung in patient horror. For longer than that. And now, as Yara passed beneath them, guttering like a candle in a draft, every flame leaned toward him.

*There,* the light said, in the wordless way that light says things. *There is one of us walking.*

"Yara." Sef's voice came from very far away and very close at once. She had stopped walking. Her arms tightened around him, armor creaking, and he realized she was shaking. "Your hands."

He looked down. His fingers were going translucent at the tips, the way frost thins on a window before it melts. Through them he could see the pale glow of the nearest lanterns, and stranger — the lanterns could see him. Their flames had turned, all thousand of them, like faces in a crowd.

"They're looking at me," he said. His voice sounded borrowed.

"You're *shining*, Yara." Sef set him down against a root as thick as a watchtower, and her hands stayed on his shoulders as if she could hold him to the world by grip alone. "You're — gods help me, you're lit up like — "

"Like them." He almost laughed. It hurt to laugh; everything hurt now, the way things hurt when they are nearly over. "No. Not like them. They're burning with lives they never got to live. I'm burning with the one I'm losing." He flexed his translucent fingers. "It must look the same from the outside."

"It doesn't." Sef's jaw was set the way it set before battles, before she burned her warrant, before she killed for the first time since her fall. He had learned to read that jaw. "It looks like a man being *stolen*."

Above them, at the crown of the cathedral where the roots braided into a throne, the regent rose.

She wore his mother's face. That was the cruelty of it — had been the cruelty from the beginning — the warm brown eyes, the laugh-lines, the small scar above the brow that Yara did not remember earning because he had never seen his mother wear it until he walked into this place. She stood among her roots with her hands folded, serene as a portrait, and her voice carried down through the cathedral the way cold carries down a stairwell.

"You should not have come back, little mapmaker. A debt paid cannot be re-litigated. The contract was signed in good faith — yours freely given, my collection honestly made."

"He didn't give it," Sef snarled, and her hand went to a sword that would do no good here, and she drew it anyway, because she was Sef. "You *took* it. You took it while he slept, you took it before the bargain was even — "

"The letter of the law," the regent said gently, in his mother's voice, "does not concern itself with your feelings, knight."

And that was when the first chain snapped.

* * *

It did not sound like metal. It sounded like a bell struck underwater — a deep, round note that Yara felt in his teeth and in whatever remained of his soul. Every head in the cathedral turned toward the sound: the regent, Sef, the pale collectors frozen mid-glide along the walls like moths pinned to velvet.

One lantern had torn loose from its root-hook. It swung slowly at the end of its broken chain, and its light — which had been the color of an unwritten letter, gray and patient — flared gold.

Then a second chain broke. Then ten. Then a hundred, and the sound became not bells but rain, a storm of snapping shadow-ropes, and the cathedral of roots filled with falling light.

"What's happening?" Sef had pulled Yara half-upright, sword between him and the room, though the room was not what threatened him. "What did you *do*?"

"Nothing." He was staring, mouth open, at the impossible. "I didn't — Sef, I can't even remember my own surname right now. I couldn't conjure a spark."

But he could see it, even if he couldn't cause it. The lanterns were drifting toward him. Not falling — *drifting*, each flame leaning into motion like a swimmer, and where they passed, the roots blackened and recoiled. The court had fed on these lights for centuries. The court had never once asked them how they felt about it.

A lantern drifted close enough that Yara could see into it. Not the flame — the life inside it. A woman's hands kneading bread. A doorway opening onto summer. A voice calling children home across a field at dusk. All the moments that had been harvested instead of lived, still whole, still waiting, still *hers*.

"She wants her weight back," Yara whispered. He understood it suddenly, completely, the way he understood maps — all at once, top to bottom. "That's all any of them want. They were sold short. Every one of them. And I'm — " he looked down at his fading hands, at the thin gold light leaking through his skin — "I'm the proof. I'm the debt come due, walking. They can feel me the way a scale feels the last weight dropped onto it."

The regent had stopped smiling.

It was a small thing, that cessation, but in the cathedral of roots it landed like an avalanche. For a hundred years — for longer — she had worn serenity like armor, and now it cracked, and beneath it Yara saw something old and hungry and *afraid*.

"Return to your hooks," she said, and her voice was no longer his mother's. It was older. It was dry as pith and deep as taproot, and it filled the cathedral the way rot fills a wall — everywhere at once, unseen. "You are property. You are *harvest*. You are the court's stored winter, and winter does not argue with the granary."

The lanterns answered with light.

Not words. Light — a rising chord of it, a thousand colors Yara had no names for, blazing off the drifting souls in waves that made the shadows scream and shrink against the walls. The collectors folded themselves smaller. The roots blackened further. And in the middle of it all, Yara stood with Sef's arm braced around him, glowing like the last coal of a fire that had once warmed a kingdom, and he began — without deciding to, the way one begins to weep — to laugh.

"What?" Sef demanded. "What is funny?"

"Not funny." He wiped his eyes with a hand that was more light than flesh now. "Relieving. Sef — I spent three years being small. Three years hiding what I am in a city that outlawed wonder, selling luck-charms and keeping my head down, and I thought — I thought that was the shape of my whole life. Small, and hidden, and lucky to be alive." He gestured at the burning cathedral, at the uprising of everything stolen. "And it turns out I was never small. I was just *unpaid*."

* * *

The revolt found its rhythm the way floods find their course — not chosen, but inevitable.

The lanterns did not fight the way soldiers fight. There was no line, no charge, no clash. They simply *went where they willed*, and the court discovered that everything it had built depended on wills other than its own. The root-thrones crumbled where souls refused to sit in them. The shadow-chains, which had held ten thousand captives across ten centuries, unraveled from the hook-end outward, because a chain is only as strong as its consent, and the consent had been withdrawn all at once, like a tide going out.

Sef fought anyway. She could not help it; it was written too deeply into her. When two collectors peeled themselves off the wall and flowed toward Yara — toward the light that was gutting their master's larder — she met them in the open ground with her blade trailing temple-fire, and she cut them apart the way one cuts smoke, which is to say imperfectly, painfully, and again and again until they stayed cut.

"Behind you!" she shouted, and spun, and severed a root-tendril that had come whipping down from the vaults to snatch at Yara's ankle. The tendril bled sap that glowed like amber and shrieked in a register below hearing.

"Sef." Yara caught her arm. His grip was cold. It frightened her more than the tendrils did. "Save your strength. They're not fighting for us. Look."

She looked.

The lanterns had formed no ranks, but they had made a shape nonetheless — a slow spiral around the cathedral's central aisle, thousands of lights turning like a galaxy deciding its own rotation, and at the center of the spiral, at the eye of it, stood Yara. Not because they obeyed him. Because they *recognized* him. He was the debt. He was the scale tipped. He was the living proof that the court's arithmetic had always been theft, and they orbited him the way truth orbits a confession.

"I'm not leading this," Yara said quietly. "I want that understood. By you. By them. By whatever's wearing my mother's face up there." He raised his fading voice, and it carried strangely, resonating in the light around him as if the souls themselves gave it echo. "I didn't call you! I don't command you! Whatever you choose tonight — choose it *yourselves*!"

For a moment the cathedral held its breath — if a place of roots can be said to breathe.

Then, from high among the vaulted dark, a single lantern detached from the spiral and rose. It was small. Its light was the color of a lullaby. It climbed past the blackened hooks, past the crumbling thrones, up toward the regent's roost — and there it stopped, hovering, and its light bent into something almost like a pointing finger.

The others followed. One by one, then hundreds at once, the souls of the sold rose off their spirals and lifted toward the crown of the cathedral, a column of dawn built in a place that had never had a morning. The regent stood at the top of it, his mother's face tilted up, her composure finally, fully gone — and Yara saw her raise one root-woven hand as if to ward off daylight itself.

"You are *nothing*," she thundered, in the old dry voice, in the mother voice, in both at once, the mask slipping and catching and slipping again. "You are stored grain. You are the court's *winter* — "

"We are the harvest," said a voice from the light.

Yara knew it. Gods, he knew it. It was the widow's voice — the widow from Grayharbor, whose candle had burned blue, whose son had woken healed — except the widow had never been to the Undercourt, and yet here was her timbre, her cadence, coming from a lantern whose inner life showed a woman kneeling beside a sickbed, praying to gods everyone said were dead. A soul sold long ago, in another city, by another desperate mother. Perhaps every desperate mother's bargain ended here. Perhaps the court was built entirely of love, weaponized.

"You called us *stored grain*," the light said, and now a hundred voices spoke with it, and a thousand, a chorus of every un-lived life. "But grain remembers the field."

The column of souls surged upward.

* * *

What happened at the crown of the cathedral, Yara watched from below, held upright by Sef and by something in himself that refused, stubbornly, to finish fading while there was still a thing left undone.

The regent fought. Of course she did. She was the first Raven ancestor who had refused death, if the archives spoke true, and refusal was the deepest root she had. She tore power out of the cathedral walls and hurled it downward in sheets of darkness; she unmade three lanterns entirely, snuffing three un-lived lives back into nothing, and the chorus screamed with grief that cracked roots for a mile in every direction. She wore his mother's face while she did it, and that was the worst of it, that was the wound under all the wounds — every blow struck by the face that had sung him to sleep in a house he could not remember.

But the light did not stop coming.

It could not stop coming. That was the arithmetic of it, the beautiful, terrible arithmetic that Yara understood better than anyone alive, because mapping had taught him: a lie has weight, and so does a truth, and when you pile enough truths onto the pan of a scale, the lie rises whether it wills it or not. Ten centuries of stolen lives pressed upward. The regent's darkness was vast but finite. The light was everything the court had ever taken, and it had all arrived at once, and it wanted its weight back.

Her defenses went the way ice goes underfoot — silently, structurally, all at once. One moment she stood crowned in shadow at the summit of her cathedral; the next, the summit was bare, and she was falling, tumbling down through the column of ascending souls like a stone dropped through sunrise, and the light parted around her and let her fall, because the light was not cruel. It simply no longer had any use for her.

She landed twenty feet from Yara, in the trampled dark at the cathedral's heart, amid the wreckage of her own roots.

Sef moved instantly — sword up, body between the fallen regent and Yara, every line of her saying *not him, not while I stand*. But the regent did not reach for power. She lay among the broken roots, and the shadow was draining out of her like blood from a wound, and as it drained, the face she wore began to change. The borrowed features softened, sagged, ran like wax — his mother's face dissolving, and beneath it something else surfacing, something older, something that had been wearing masks so long it had perhaps forgotten its own —

"No," Yara said.

He stepped forward. Sef grabbed for him and missed; he was lighter now than a man ought to be, more light than meat, and he crossed the distance to the fallen regent and knelt beside her before anyone could stop him.

Because he knew that face coming up beneath the mask. He had seen it in the archive, in the original contract, in the ink of his mother's signature. He had seen it in the petrified gardens of Ravensworth, carved into a fountain that no longer flowed. He had seen it every time he looked in a mirror and wondered why his cheekbones felt like an inheritance.

The regent looked up at him with eyes that were neither his mother's nor a monster's, and smiled with the first honest expression she had ever shown him, and said, in a voice like the last leaf letting go:

"Hello, little heir. You have your grandmother's stubbornness."

Around them the cathedral burned cold — blue-white flame crawling up the roots, consuming nothing, illuminating everything, the fire of a thousand freed lives refusing to go out. Above, the lanterns streamed upward through a fissure Yara had not noticed before, a crack in the vault of the world, pouring toward the surface like reversed rain.

And the regent — the first Raven, the root of his whole poisoned tree, the thing his mother had sold him to escape — reached up one fading hand toward him, and said:

"Before you decide what I deserve... let me tell you why I refused to die."
