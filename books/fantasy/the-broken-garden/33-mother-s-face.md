## 33. Mother's Face

The roots of the Undercourt had stopped burning cold and started burning *hungry*.

Yara felt it through Sef's arms as she carried him down the last stair into the cathedral of the world — a tremor in the wood and stone, like a great beast turning over in its sleep. The hanging lanterns swung on their long stems of shadow, ten thousand souls swaying in a wind that did not exist, and their light guttered toward him. Toward what was left of him.

He could no longer feel his feet. That had happened somewhere above, during the descent, and he had not told Sef because telling her would make it real, and because her face — when she thought he wasn't looking — had already gone to a place he couldn't follow. She carried him the way she had once carried wounded brothers off a battlefield: efficiently, without tenderness, tenderness being a luxury for people with time.

He was running out of time. He knew that the way you know weather coming. His edges were going soft. When he tried to remember the color of his own front door in Grayharbor, he got a shape instead of a color. When he reached for his mother's voice, he found only the space where it should have been, a silence shaped exactly like her.

"Almost there," Sef said. Her breath came hard. The stair had been long, and she had been carrying more than his weight for hours now — she had been carrying all of him, every memory he'd dropped along the road like coins from a torn pocket. "The dais. I can see the dais."

"I know." His voice sounded strange to him. Thinner. "I remember it."

"Do you?" A crack of bitterness. "Because yesterday you didn't remember my name until I said it twice."

He had no answer for that. She set him down at the edge of the dais anyway, gently, gentler than her words deserved, and propped him against the root-pillar so he could face the heart of the court.

She stood waiting there. Of course she did.

His mother.

Not his mother — he knew that, he had known it since the archive, since Sef read the bargain aloud in a voice that shook only once — but the regent wore her face the way a thief wears a stolen coat, and after thirty chapters of forgetting, after footprints vanishing behind him and his name slipping out of minds like water through fingers, Yara looked at that face and felt the whole of his grief arrive at once, late and enormous, like a tide that had been traveling toward him since he was six years old.

"You came," the regent said. Her mother's mouth curved. Her mother's eyes — brown, warm, creased at the corners from laughing — did not laugh. They were old behind the mask. Old as the first grave. "I wondered if you would make it before the last of you went out. You're dimmer than when you left us, little heir. Dimmer even than the night we hung your lantern."

"My name," Yara said.

It cost him something enormous to say it. He felt the word tear loose from whatever was still holding him together. But he said it, and the cathedral heard it, and ten thousand lanterns flared.

"Yara Ravensworth."

* * *

Sef's hand went to her sword. There was nothing left of the temple in the way she drew it — no ritual, no blessing, just the flat practical motion of a woman who had learned that the gods' house was full of wolves and that prayer was slower than steel. She planted herself between Yara and the dais, and the regent regarded her with mild, ancient amusement.

"The oathbreaker. Still here. Still carrying him." The regent tilted her mother's head. "You know what he is now, don't you? You've watched him come apart piece by piece, and you stayed. That's either love or stubbornness, and in my long experience they weigh the same."

"Sef," Yara said quietly. "Let me do this."

"He'll unmake you—"

"He already has." He tried to smile. It half worked; he could feel it working on only one side of his face, as if the other side had already been paid out. "That's rather the point. Let me talk to her. To it. Please."

A long moment. Then Sef stepped aside — one step, no further, sword low but not sheathed — and Yara pushed himself up the pillar until he was standing, mostly standing, held upright by will and wood.

He looked at his mother's face.

"I was six," he said. "When you died. Do you know what I remember? Not your face. Isn't that a terrible thing? I remember the smell of ink. I remember you grinding pigment at the worktable while I pretended to sleep, because pretending to sleep meant you'd stay in the room. I remember your hands. Ink to the second knuckle, always. And I remember—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, because he was done swallowing things, "—I remember the night the men came. I remember you putting me in the cellar with a candle and telling me to count to a thousand. I counted to four hundred and eleven and then the screaming started, and I have spent twenty years being angry at myself for stopping at four hundred and eleven."

The regent said nothing. The mask held perfectly still. That was how he knew it was a mask; his mother had never once in her life held still while someone grieved in front of her.

"And then I learned," Yara went on, "that the screaming wasn't what I thought. That you'd sold me first. That the men who came weren't there for me at all — or they were, but they never found what they were looking for, because you'd already hidden it. Me. My soul. You gave it away to save it." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet and cracked. "Do you understand what that did to me? All these years I thought I was abandoned. Sold. A transaction. I built my whole life small so I'd never be worth taking again. Three years in Grayharbor being *small*, and the whole time — the whole time you were the bravest person who ever loved me, and I didn't know, and you were dead, and I couldn't even hate you properly because hating you required knowing why, and nobody would tell me why."

"You're angry," the regent said, in his mother's voice, and the sound of it went through him like a blade finding an old scar.

"Yes."

"At her."

"No." He steadied himself against the pillar. His hand sank slightly into the wood; he was becoming translucent at the edges, a map of a man. "At *you*. For wearing her."

For the first time, something moved behind the borrowed face. Not anger. Interest. The regent leaned forward on the root-throne, and the shadows of the cathedral leaned with it, ten thousand lanterns tilting on their stems.

"She's dead, little heir. Dead a century and more, dust in a garden you can't even remember. This face is the closest thing to her left in any world. Would you rather look at what I really am?"

"Yes," Yara said.

Silence.

"Take it off," he said. "Take her face off. You've worn her to get what you wanted from me — my soul, my name, my inheritance, my fear. Every kindness you ever showed me wore her skin. When you told me the terms of the bargain, you used her mouth. When you offered me the crown, you used her eyes. You made me say goodbye to my mother over and over, in a dozen conversations, because each time I looked at you I had to lose her again." His voice rose, and the lanterns rose with it, light climbing like a tide. "You took the worst day of my life and made it a costume. So take it off. If you want what's left of me, let me see what's asking for it."

The regent studied him for a long moment. Around them the cathedral creaked and groaned; somewhere far below, in the deep vault where his blood had opened the Raven Throne's seals, something old shifted in its chains. The revolt of the souls had begun hours ago — a stirring, a swelling, lantern-light pulsing out of rhythm — and the regent knew it, and Yara knew the regent knew it, and neither of them mentioned it, because between them there was only this one unfinished thing.

"You grieve her," the regent said slowly, "as if grief were a door you could walk through and be done. But grief is a country, little heir. I have lived in it longer than your line has lived in its throne. You do not finish grieving. You only learn the roads."

"Then I'll map them," Yara said. "It's what I do."

And something flickered across the mother's face — something that might, in another creature, have been respect.

* * *

Sef watched from three steps away, sword bare, and understood that she was witnessing two sieges at once.

The visible one was Yara against the regent. The other was Yara against himself, and it was the harder fight, because he was fighting with a self that was actively leaving him. She had carried him down through the dark narrating their story — the night market, the blue candle, the painted door, the river that took her sister's face, the kiss among the lanterns — holding him real by sheer repetition, and she had thought that was the hardest thing she would ever do. She had been wrong. The hardest thing was standing here now, close enough to strike, doing nothing, because this battle was not hers and interfering would be the one cruelty that could actually break him.

But oh, it cost her. Because the regent wore a dead woman's face, and Sef knew about dead women's faces. She had knelt before her sister's grave-marker for a year after the river took her memory of it, praying to a face she could no longer picture, and she knew exactly the particular hell Yara stood in now: to need the face and know it was false, to ache for it and be ashamed of aching.

"Take it off," Yara said again, quieter now. Almost gentle. "Please. Let me bury her. Really bury her, this time. I never got to. I was counting in a cellar."

The regent rose from the throne.

And the mask began to come apart.

It did not tear. It *drained*. The warmth went first, out of the cheeks, the brown draining from the eyes like water from a basin. Then the structure followed — the lines softening, the familiar architecture of cheek and brow losing its conviction, features sliding like wax in sun, like ink in rain. Sef heard Yara make a small sound and forced herself not to move, not to go to him, because he had asked, and some doors a man has to open alone.

What stood revealed beneath was not monstrous. That was the horror of it. It was simply *old* — a figure like a person the way a ruin is like a house, tall and hollowed, its outline uncertain, its interior showing faint lights that might have been stars or might have been the lanterns of souls seen through translucent flesh. It had no face. Where the face had been there was a smoothness, and in the smoothness, faintly, like a watermark, like a signature pressed too deep into paper to ever fully erase —

— a raven.

"You see," the regent said, and its voice now was every voice it had ever borrowed, layered, genderless, patient as sediment. "This is what asks for you. Not her. Never her. She has been at rest for a hundred years, and my wearing of her disturbed that rest, and for that disturbance I offer no apology, because I do not regret it. Regret is for beings with futures. I am the past, little heir. I am the first Raven, the one who would not lie down, the one who built this court so that death would have a back door and I would hold the key. Your mother's face was a tool. Your soul is a meal. Your throne is my chair, and you are late for your own seating."

"I know," Yara said.

And then he did the thing that Sef would remember for the rest of her life, in a life that was about to become very long and very strange.

He wept.

Not the desperate, ragged weeping of a man being erased. Something else. He slid down the pillar — she started forward and stopped herself — and knelt on the cold root-floor of the Undercourt, in full view of the regent and ten thousand hanging souls, and he put his face in his ink-stained hands, and he grieved.

He grieved the mother who ground pigment at midnight. He grieved the candle in the cellar and the count that stopped at four hundred and eleven. He grieved the boy who grew up thinking himself sold, and the man who grew up thinking himself small, and the cartographer who mapped other worlds because he could not bear to map the empty room where his childhood should have been. He grieved loudly, ugly, without dignity, the way children grieve, the way no one had ever permitted him to grieve, and the sound of it filled the cathedral of roots, and the lanterns — the ten thousand stolen souls — flickered in answer, one by one by one, like a congregation lighting candles.

Sef knelt beside him. Not touching. Just near. A witness. That was all he needed her to be, and all she could give, and it was enough.

And when it was done — when the storm passed through him and out, when the last sob shook him and stilled — Yara Ravensworth lifted his head, and Sef saw what grief does when it finally finishes its work. It doesn't leave you empty. It leaves you *clear*.

He stood. He did not use the pillar.

"Thank you," he said to the regent, and meant it, and the meaning of it struck the ancient thing harder than any blow Sef could have landed. "You gave me back the worst night of my life so I could finally live through the end of it. That was cruel, and it was also the only gift anyone could have given me. Both things are true. I'm done pretending otherwise." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and his hand came away streaked with ink — his own blood-ink, the cartographer's medium, welling up through his fading skin. "My mother hid me. She didn't sell me. She *hid* me, the way you hide a lamp under a bowl, and everything she did, she did so that I would survive to stand here. Well. Here I am."

He raised his ink-dark hand, and the nearest lanterns flared toward him like flowers toward sun.

"You wanted a Raven. Someone to sit your chair and feed your court and keep the back door of death locked from the inside. But you made a mistake a hundred years ago, regent, and you've been making it ever since." He smiled, and it was not his mother's smile, and it was not the Raven Throne's smile. It was entirely, irreducibly his own. "You assumed I'd want to be a king. You never once considered that I'm a *mapmaker*. And a mapmaker's whole faith is this: nothing hidden stays safe. Nothing known can be owned. You kept this place unmapped so you could keep it hungry — and I am going to draw it. Every root, every stair, every lantern, every lock. I'm going to put the Undercourt on the page, and the moment the world knows you, you will never hold another soul again."

The raven-marked face regarded him. And beneath them, in the deeps, the chained god stirred. Above them, the ten thousand lanterns burned brighter, leaning, leaning, a cathedral of prisoners turning toward one small, fading, incandescent man.

"You are nearly gone," the regent said. "You cannot even hold your own name past sundown. What will you draw with?"

Yara opened his hand. The ink in his palm glowed.

"Everything I have left," he said. "Which is exactly enough. It was always going to be exactly enough — my mother saw to that."

He turned to Sef, and his eyes were clear, and for one heartbeat they were wholly, perfectly present, and she memorized them the way she memorized coastlines.

"Hold me steady," he said. "I'm going to need a surface to draw on."

Behind the regent, silent as falling snow, the first of the hanging lanterns tore itself free.
