## 36. The Empty Throne

The Raven Throne had been waiting for him.

Yara felt it before he saw it — a pull in his blood like the hook behind his ribs from that first blue candle, but vaster now, patient, ancient. It sat at the heart of the Undercourt's deepest vault, past the burning cold of the revolt's aftermath, past the drifting ash of the regent's dissolution, in a chamber where the roots of the world grew inward instead of down.

It was not gold. He had expected gold, or the black iron of Grayharbor's temple, or something cruel and beautiful. Instead it was living wood — pale as birch, dark as bog-oak, grown into the shape of a seat by centuries of careful, hungry tending. Ravens had been carved into its arms, hundreds of them, each with eyes of polished obsidian, and every single one was watching him.

The gods stood around it in a half-circle.

There were eleven of them left in this place — he could feel the number the way he felt the weight of ink on a page. Some wore shapes: a woman made of river-water who never quite held still; a broad-shouldered figure whose skin was bark and whose hands were full of small green shoots; the mouse-god, enormous now, tall as a door, whiskers trembling with something between hope and terror. Others had no shape at all, only presence — pressures in the air, warm spots in the cold, the sense of being looked at kindly by something too large to have a face.

They had followed him up from the burning cathedral of roots. They had watched him map their prison into the atlas of the world, stroke by stroke, until the Undercourt was *known*, and what is known cannot hold souls captive. They had watched ten thousand lanterns go free.

Now they watched him look at the throne.

"You are the last Raven," said the river-woman. Her voice was the sound water makes over stones, constant and unhurried. "The line is not ended. It is *here*."

"I'm the last one who didn't want it," Yara said.

The bark-skinned god laughed — a sound like wind through a young forest. "That has never once mattered in the history of thrones."

* * *

Sef stood at the vault's mouth, and she did not come closer.

She had not spoken since the souls went free. Yara kept finding his eyes going to her — checking, the way you check a wound — and each time she was there, solid, real, her broken knight's sword still drawn and lowered at her side, ash in her cropped hair. She had fought beside temple knights two nights ago who would have killed him without hesitation. She had chosen him anyway, again and again, and neither of them had yet found words for what that cost.

Now she watched the gods offer him everything, and her face was the face of a woman standing on ice, listening to it speak.

"Sit," said the mouse-god. Its voice was small even at that size, squeaking and vast at once. "You promised us doors. You opened them. But doors need a keeper, little cartographer. The world above is waking up to what your family did to us, and they will be afraid, and fear makes chains faster than any smith. Sit on the throne and we will kneel, and no one will ever bind a god again."

"Because I'd be the one holding the leash," Yara said quietly.

The mouse-god's whiskers drooped. "Yes."

He walked toward the throne. Not because he meant to sit — because he needed to see it properly, the way he needed to see a coastline before he drew it. Up close, the carved ravens were not decorative. Each one bore a name beneath its feet, etched so fine he had to lean in: *Veyra. Aldous. Maren. Corvane.* His family. Generations of them, grown into the wood, feeding it.

And there, near the base of the right arm, small and fresh as if cut yesterday:

*Roswyn.*

His mother's name. The letters were shallow. Incomplete. She had touched the throne — he understood that suddenly, viscerally, the night she sold his soul, the night she came down here to bargain with monsters to keep assassins from her son — and the throne had begun to take her, and she had pulled away, and run, and spent the rest of her life in Grayharbor selling charms and hiding her magic and never once telling him why she flinched at ravens.

The throne wanted a Raven. It had been starving for one for a hundred years.

"No," Yara said.

The word fell into the chamber and the roots themselves seemed to lean in.

* * *

"No?" The river-woman's current quickened. "Child, do you understand what is offered? Your ancestors bound us and fed on our worship until we were cattle. You unbound us. You freed the stolen dead. The debt of your bloodline is paid a hundred times over — sit, and rule *well*, and be the thing your family should have been."

"That's exactly the problem." Yara turned to face them all, and he was aware of Sef at the edge of his vision, aware that his voice would carry to her, and he found he wanted it to. "You're offering me my ancestors' chair and calling it a kindness. But look at it. Look at what it's *for*. It doesn't want a king. It wants a feeder. Every Raven who sat there didn't rule — they *ate*. Worship went in one end and power came out the other, and the gods got farmed, and the people got prayed at, and when anyone objected—" he gestured at the names in the wood, "—there were assassins. There were sold souls. There was all of this."

"The throne can be remade," said the bark-god. Shoots sprouted from its knuckles as it spoke, eager, hopeful. "We would remake it. A seat of counsel. A—"

"It can't." Yara laid his hand flat on the armrest, on the pale wood, and felt the hunger underneath, old and patient and bottomless. "I'm a cartographer. Do you know what that means? It means I know that a map is not the territory, and a chair is not a covenant, and you cannot redraw a thing by drawing it differently. This throne was built to make one person the hinge of the world. As long as it exists, someone will sit in it. If not me, then whoever is strong enough or desperate enough or frightened enough. And the world will bend around them again, and we'll be back here in another hundred years, freeing lanterns."

He stepped back from it.

"So I'm not declining the crown," he said. "I'm declining the *idea* of it. And I'm asking you to decline it with me."

Silence. Eleven presences considering him. Somewhere in the vault, water dripped from roots into darkness, keeping time.

"What do you propose instead?" the river-woman asked at last, and her tone had changed — less current now, more caution. Rivers, Yara thought, were wise about dams.

"Accountability."

The word sounded small in the vast chamber. He made himself stand inside its smallness.

"Not worship. Not obedience. Not a throne with a leash in either arm. Here's what I know how to do: I draw things so they can be *seen*. That's all magic ever was, underneath — my mother's charms, the temple's rites, your miracles. Making the unseen visible. So here is my counter-offer, and I warn you it's harder than kneeling."

He looked at each of them in turn, shapeless and shaped alike.

"Come up. Into the light. Walk among the people you were worshipped by, without temples, without tithe, without priests speaking for you. Let them see what you actually are — petty and vast and kind and cruel and *mortal-adjacent*, all of it. And let them choose. Day by day. Prayer by prayer. If a farmer wants rain and asks you nicely and you give it, that's a relationship. If she's too frightened not to ask, that's just the old farm with new fencing. No more binding — yours or ours. No more feeding. You get *witnesses*, not worshippers. And some mornings, most mornings maybe, nobody will pray to you at all, and you'll have to decide whether you're still worth believing in."

"That is a terrible bargain," said the mouse-god.

"Yes," Yara agreed. "For everyone. That's how you know it's honest."

* * *

The bark-god came first.

It crossed the chamber slowly, joints creaking like an old orchard, and stopped before Yara, and looked down at him with eyes the color of new leaves. Then it did something no god had done in a century, as far as Yara knew: it extended its hand, palm open, level with his shoulder. Not to be kissed. To be shaken.

"A god of small growing things," it rumbled, "has waited a long time to be judged by what it grows rather than what it's fed. I accept. I will plant myself in your broken garden, cartographer, and let the gardeners judge me."

"One condition of mine," Yara said, taking the hand. Its grip was gentle the way mountains are gentle. "No miracles in the planting. Seeds and soil and weather like everyone else gets. If you're going to live among people, start by needing them."

The bark-god's laugh shook loose dust from the vault ceiling. "Oh, you are *nothing* like your ancestors."

"That's the first kind thing anyone's said to me all week."

The river-woman took longer. She circled him twice, water whispering, and he had the sense of being weighed — not his soul this time, which was finally, blessedly, entirely his own, but his word.

"If they stop praying," she said, "we thin. We have always been fed by belief. Even freely given, belief is food. You are asking us to be hungry forever."

"I'm asking you to be *honest* about being hungry. There's a difference between a guest at a table and livestock in a pen. You've been in the pen so long you've forgotten there's a difference."

Her waters stilled. For a moment she was almost a woman — a face surfacing, tired and old and younger than her years.

"My springs dried up when your family bound us," she said. "I have been a trickle for a century. A trickle does not need much. I accept — and I will hold you to it, heir of Ravensworth. When the people forget me, I will not take it back by force. And neither will you take back your freedom when the throne-starved come begging you to sit."

"Agreed," Yara said. "May we both starve honestly."

Three accepted that day in the vault. The others drifted away — not angrily, mostly. One presence simply withdrew upward through the roots like heat leaving a stone, and Yara felt it go with a grief that surprised him. The mouse-god was the hardest. It stood before him a long time, huge and trembling, and Yara thought it would refuse, and braced himself to lose the first friend he'd made below the world.

"You promised me doors," it said finally. "All doors, opened."

"And I'll keep that promise. Doors aren't worship. A door doesn't care who walks through it."

The mouse-god's whiskers twitched. Slowly, enormously, it began to shrink — down and down, past man-height, past child-height, until it was small and brown and ordinary, sitting on the cold stone floor and looking up at him with black bead eyes.

"Then I'll be a door-god," it squeaked. "Not a god *of* doors. A god who lives in them. In the gaps. Where things pass through." It groomed one paw, embarrassed. "Mice have always known how to live in the walls of bigger creatures' houses. Perhaps it's the humans' turn to learn it from us."

It scampered up onto Yara's shoulder, settled into the crook of his neck like it had always lived there, and whispered, very close to his ear: "Take me up. I want to see the sky before I decide if I believe in it anymore."

* * *

Five remained in the vault when the others had gone.

They had no shapes Yara could read, and their silence had a different texture than acceptance — colder, older. The pressure in the air thickened. On the throne, the carved ravens' obsidian eyes seemed to brighten.

"You would make us beggars," said a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere, from the roots, from the dark between the roots. "Crawling among mortals for scraps of notice. We were worshipped when your line was young. We will be worshipped again, somewhere, by someone. There is always someone willing to kneel for power."

"I know," Yara said. "That's the horror of it. And those someones will find you, and you'll build a new court in some unmapped dark, and someday a cartographer will come looking for you." He shrugged. "I can't stop you. Nobody can, anymore. That's rather the point."

"We do not accept your terms."

"I know." He met the darkness where the voices lived, unflinching, though sweat ran cold down his spine. "Go, then. Freely. That's the whole of the law I'm leaving behind me: everyone goes freely. Even the ones walking away from me. *Especially* them."

The five rose like smoke and poured upward through the root-vault's high dark, and the chamber was suddenly, enormously empty — emptier than it had been in a thousand years, perhaps. The throne stood alone in the middle of it, pale wood and watching birds, with no one left to hunger for.

Yara looked at it a long moment.

Then he took out his kit — the same battered leather case he'd carried through three years of night markets, chalk and ink and bone pens — and he knelt at the throne's base, and he began to draw.

Not wards. Not bindings. A map. The throne itself, rendered true: every raven, every name, the hunger in the wood drawn as plainly as coastlines, so that anyone who ever came upon this seat would *see* it, would know exactly what it was built to do, and could not pretend otherwise. He worked until his fingers ached and the candle-stub of daylight in the vault had shifted, and when he finished he sat back on his heels and looked at the truth of the thing rendered in his own steady hand.

"There," he said softly. "Known. And what is known can't lie."

He left it there in the empty vault, mapped and named and unfed, the emptiest throne in any world — a monument, an warning, and a dare.

* * *

Sef was waiting at the vault mouth, as he'd known she would be. She hadn't come in. He understood: some rooms you don't enter unless invited, and she had spent her whole life being sent into rooms uninvited.

"Five refused," he said, climbing toward her. "Four walked. One's on my shoulder pretending to be a scarf."

"I heard." Her voice was rough. She was looking at him strangely — the way she'd looked at the maps in his workshop, that first night, ink and secrets. "You turned down a throne."

"I turned down a feeding trough with good posture."

"Yara." She caught his arm as he reached her. Her grip was careful, the way she'd held him after the hounds, after the killing, after every moment he'd come apart and she'd been the one who stayed. "Do you understand what you just did? Not the theology. The *politics*. The temple is going to hear that the last Raven refused the old gods' crown and demanded they be *accountable* — accountable, like a tax collector — and half of them will call it heresy worse than mine, and the other half won't have words for it yet, but they will. Eventually they will."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a warning." But she was almost smiling, tired and fierce, and her thumb moved once against his sleeve. "You've just founded something, whether you meant to or not. Movements don't wait for permission."

Above them, the long climb back to the surface waited — the uphill river, the memory-toll (he still owed Sef the truth of what he'd paid there), the collapsed mine, the gray light of Grayharbor where a city full of outlawed magic was waking up all at once and would want explanations. Wanted *him*.

The mouse-god stirred against his neck. "Little cartographer," it squeaked softly. "The air tastes different up ahead. Thin. Bright. Full of people saying your name."

Yara closed his eyes. Somewhere above, dawn was coming to a city that had forbidden wonder, and every candle in it was probably burning blue.

"They shouldn't say my name," he murmured. "I gave it away once already. Apparently it keeps coming back."

"Names do that," said Sef. She shouldered her sword, and held out her hand — palm open, level with his shoulder, the way a god offers a covenant and a person offers everything else. "Come on, heir of nothing. Let's go home the long way. I want to see the sky before I decide if I believe in it anymore."

He took her hand, and they climbed toward the light, and behind them in the dark the empty throne kept its vigil of carved birds — watching nothing, fed by no one, waiting for no one, which was, Yara thought, the most honest prayer any god had ever answered.
