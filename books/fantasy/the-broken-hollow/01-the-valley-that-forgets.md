## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Hollow woke the way it always did: slowly, and without ambition.

Edmund Thornbury stood at his window in the gray hour before dawn, watching mist pour down the valley walls like milk poured into a bowl. The fog came off the high ridges every morning, pooled in the fields, and burned away by midmorning, and no one in the valley thought anything of it anymore — if they ever had. It was simply what mornings did here. Everything here was simply what it did. The millwheel turned because it had always turned. The bell in the guildhouse tower rang seven times at seven o'clock because it had rung seven times at seven o'clock since before anyone could say why seven.

He had asked once, when he was small enough to still ask questions. *Why seven?*

His grandmother had looked at him for a long moment with her sharp cartographer's eyes, and then she had said, "Because eight would be showing off," and laughed until she coughed, and he had loved her so fiercely it frightened him.

She didn't laugh much anymore.

* * *

The Guildhall smelled of dust and lamp oil and old paper going soft at the edges, a smell Edmund loved more than any person could reasonably love a smell. He lit the lamps himself now. There were only four of them left — four members of the Cartographers' Guild, which had once, according to the ledgers, numbered three hundred strong, with chapter houses in nine cities whose names appeared in no other book Edmund had ever found.

Four. And of those four, one was dying by inches in the room above the map archive, and one was Deacon Hale, who was eighty-one and spent most meetings asleep, and one was Marta Voss, who attended faithfully but had confessed to Edmund over cider last autumn that she no longer remembered what the guild's oath actually said, only that there had been one, and that it had mattered.

"And you?" he'd asked her. "Do you remember yours?"

"I remember that I meant it," she said, which was either the truest answer or the saddest one, and he hadn't pressed.

That morning he climbed the tower stairs two at a time, as he did every morning, and rang the bell seven times, and stood afterward in the cold belfry looking out at the valley while the sound died away over the rooftops.

The Hollow was beautiful. He wanted to be honest about that, even in the privacy of his own resentment. It was a green hand cupped around a village, ringed by ridges of dark pine, with the river Liss running through the middle of it like a silver stitch. In autumn the hills went copper and gold. In winter the whole valley held its breath under snow. Travelers — the few who still passed through, wool merchants mostly, driving their carts along the old road that skirted the eastern ridge — always remarked on it. *What a lovely little place,* they said. *So peaceful.*

Peaceful. Yes. Like a held breath is peaceful. Like a locked room is peaceful.

Because that was the other thing about the Hollow, the thing the travelers never knew: nothing crossed those borders that shouldn't. The border stones stood at eleven points around the valley's rim — squat gray monoliths older than any record, carved with spirals that no chisel had made and no scholar had explained. And when magic came near them, they hummed.

Edmund had heard it exactly twice in his life. Once when he was nine, when a tinker woman came through with charms for sale and her wagon rattled past the western stone and the air around it shivered with a low note, felt in the teeth more than the ears, and the tinker's charms all went dead as river stones before she reached the village gate. She'd wept, quietly, packing up her useless wares, and no one had comforted her because no one quite understood what she was grieving.

The second time was last spring, and Edmund had been alone at the northern stone, sketching, when the hum started — and this time something on the far side of the stone had *pushed back*, a pressure like a hand laid flat against the inside of his skull, curious and cold, and then withdrawn. He had not told anyone. He had drawn the sensation instead, which was what he did with everything.

That was the secret. His secret, kept even from Marta and sleeping Deacon Hale and his fading grandmother: Edmund drew maps of places he had never seen.

They came to him at odd hours — at the washbasin, at the bell rope, halfway through mending a shutter — a pressure behind the eyes and then an image, complete and insistent: a harbor city built on terraces above black water; a desert crossing marked with the bones of a road; a mountain pass where the snow never melted though the sun beat down on it. He never knew what the places were. He had no reason to know them. He had never left the Hollow, not once in twenty-two years, not beyond the market fair at Millbrook that lay just outside the stones and barely counted.

But his hands knew them. He'd fill page after page of his hidden folio with coastlines and rivers and streets, and the drawings had a rightness to them that frightened him — bridges where bridges needed to be, fords where the terrain demanded fords. Maps, his grandmother had taught him, were arguments about the world, and a good map won its argument against the world itself. These maps argued and won, and Edmund had never stood in a single street they described.

He kept the folio wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard in the archive, under the great table where the Master's atlas lay open to a page no one had updated in forty years.

* * *

He brought her breakfast up at eight, as he did every day: bread, honey, tea gone lukewarm from the walk up the stairs. Ilsa Thornbury sat in her chair by the window with a blanket over her knees, watching the mist burn off the fields.

"Good morning, Grandmother."

She looked at him, and for a moment — a long, terrible moment — her face was politely blank, the face one gives a stranger at a door. Then it cleared. "Edmund. You've let your hair get long again."

"You say that every day."

"Do I?" She smiled, faint and puzzled at herself. "Well. It's good advice, wherever it comes from."

He set the tray down and knelt beside her chair, and she put her hand on his head the way she used to when he was a boy, fingers moving through his hair in the old absent rhythm. Her hands had drawn the last true survey of the Hollow, he knew. Every contour line, every bend of the Liss, inked in a script so fine it looked like stitching. She had been the finest cartographer of her generation, maybe of any generation the guild had produced, and now some mornings she looked at her own hands as if trying to remember what they were for.

"What are we doing today?" she asked.

"Guild meeting. Thursday."

"Is it Thursday?"

"It's Thursday."

"Who attends, still?"

"Marta. Deacon Hale, if we wake him. You, if you're willing."

Her brow furrowed. He watched her reach for something and fail to grasp it, watched the small humiliation of it move across her face and be mastered, because she was too proud to let it show for long. "There was an oath," she said slowly. "We swore an oath. All of us. I can feel the shape of it, Edmund, like a word on the tip of the tongue. Something about... promises." Her grip tightened on his shoulder. "A map is not a picture. A map is a—"

"A promise," he finished softly. "You told me. When I was ten. You said a map is a promise a place makes to itself."

"Did I?" She released him and turned back to the window, and the mist was nearly gone now, and the valley lay below them green and complete and sealed. "I must have been smarter then. I seem to have given so much of myself away over the years. Little pieces. I keep finding the edges of the holes."

Edmund's chest ached. "Grandmother—"

"There's a word I've lost," she said, conversational, terrible. "It began with a vowel. It meant the feeling of a life being too small for the person living it. I looked for it everywhere. It's gone." She patted his hand. "Eat your breakfast, dear. You'll want your strength for whatever it is you're not telling me."

His blood went cold. "I don't—"

"No," she agreed mildly, eyes still on the valley. "You don't."

* * *

He worked through the afternoon in the archive, cataloguing accessions that were decades old because there were no new accessions, because nothing new ever entered the Hollow. The light moved across the floor. Somewhere below the floorboards his secret folio waited with its impossible cities, and above him his grandmother's clock ticked, and out along the rim of the world the border stones stood in their circles of dark grass, silent, humming nothing, holding the valley like a jar holds water.

At dusk he climbed the tower to ring the evening bell — six times at six, because seven would be showing off — and as the last note faded he saw it.

Down at the western edge of the valley, where the road ran close to the stones, a figure was stumbling along the ridge path in the failing light. Moving wrong. Lurching, falling, rising. And even at this distance, even in the purple dusk, Edmund could see that the man was leaving a trail behind him that glowed — faint, flickering, wrong — like embers scattered on dark cloth.

Like light bleeding out of a wound.

The bell rope was still warm in Edmund's hand. Below him the village lamps were coming on one by one, small and golden and utterly unprepared, and the figure at the border staggered once more, fell to his knees at the base of the western stone —

— and began, slowly, to crawl toward the Hollow.
