## 2. A Stranger at the Border Stones

The border stones began to hum an hour before the sun went down.

Edmund heard it first as a wrongness in his teeth — a low vibration that lived in the fillings of the world, in the mortar of the mill wall, in the water bucket by the door. He had grown up with that hum the way other children grew up with church bells: as a fact of the landscape, unremarkable until it changed. The stones sang when magic came near, his grandmother had told him once, and then she had laughed at her own sentence, because in twenty-two years of Edmund's life the stones had sung perhaps four times, and never for longer than a breath.

This was not a breath. This was a sustained note, rising.

He was on the eastern path with a basket of late apples, the ones too bruised for keeping, meant for the pigs. He stopped. The hum climbed through his boot soles and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and the hairs on his forearms lifted though the evening was warm. Above him the sky was doing its usual dusk work — bruising violet along the ridgeline, gilding the last clouds — but below it, along the line of standing stones that marked the Hollow's edge, the air had begun to shimmer. Not heat-shimmer. It moved against the wind.

Edmund set down the basket.

He should have gone for help. That was the sensible thing; that was what the guild's few remaining rules said, in the careful hand of some long-dead clerk: *In the event of disturbance at the boundary, withdraw and report.* But the guild had eleven members now, and three of them were bedridden, and the nearest was old Tam Weller, who had forgotten which end of a compass pointed north and wept about it every morning. And besides — Edmund would tell himself this later, turning it over like a coin he couldn't quite read — besides, the humming sounded less like a warning than like a door being opened after a very long time. It sounded almost glad.

He walked toward the stones.

* * *

The man came through on his hands and knees.

There was no other way to describe it. One moment the space between the two central monoliths was empty evening air; the next, something was pushing through it, slowly, the way a drowning man pushes through the surface of a lake — and the air resisted. Edmund watched it bend around the stranger's shoulders like a membrane, watched it cling and stretch and finally tear with a sound he felt rather than heard, a soft wet whisper, like a page being pulled from a binding.

Then the man collapsed onto the grass inside the border, and Edmund saw that he was bleeding light.

Not blood. He understood that immediately, in the way one understands fire is hot — not by reasoning but by some older knowledge waking up in the blood. What seeped from the wounds in the man's side, from the gash across his palm, from a dozen smaller hurts Edmund could not count, was luminous. Pale gold, then white, then a color Edmund had no name for, a color like the moment just before remembering something. It pooled in the grass and did not soak in. Where it touched the ground, small flowers opened out of season — tiny white things, five-petaled, blooming and browning and blooming again in fast-forward, living whole lives in seconds.

The man lifted his head. His eyes found Edmund with terrible effort, and they were the most ordinary eyes Edmund had ever seen — brown, tired, human — which somehow made everything else worse.

"You're—" The voice cracked like dry wood. "You're guild."

"I'm—" Edmund took a step back, then hated himself for it and stepped forward instead. "I'm Edmund. Thornbury. Are you— who did this to you?"

The man laughed, or tried to. What came out was mostly blood-light, spilling past his lips in a thin bright thread. "Doesn't matter. Doesn't— listen." He was fumbling at his chest, at a leather satchel strapped there, and his fingers kept missing the buckle because they were going translucent at the edges. Edmund could see the grass through them. "Listen to me, boy. Take it. You have to take it."

"Take what? I'll get help — there's a healer in Millbrook, if I run—"

"No." The word came out with such force that the light around the man flared, and the border stones rang like struck bells. "No healers. No time. It's already—" He swallowed. "It's eating me. What's left. Please."

He got the buckle open. From the satchel he drew a folded square of something that was not paper and not cloth — it moved when he held it, shifting in his grip like a living thing adjusting its position, and even from six feet away Edmund could see lines crawling across its surface, redrawing themselves, patient as insects.

A map.

Every child raised in the Hollow knew what a map was. Edmund more than most; he'd been drawing them since he was seven, maps of cities he'd never seen, coastlines he'd never walked, filling ledger after ledger with places that existed only in his head and in his grandmother's increasingly worried silences. He knew maps the way he knew his own heartbeat.

He had never seen one move.

"It chose you," the man said. "Before I crossed. It's been pulling toward the valley for nine days. Toward—" he coughed, and the cough cost him visibly, dimming him like a lamp turned down, "—toward whatever's left of the oath here. You felt it. Don't lie. You felt the stones."

Edmund had. All day, if he was honest — not the hum, but something under it, a tugging behind his navel, a sense of being looked for. He said nothing.

The man dragged himself closer. Up close, Edmund could see that the light bleeding out of him wasn't random — it came off him in patterns, faint spirals and branching lines, like the veins of a leaf, like the roads on a map. Like the man himself was being unwritten.

"My name was Aldric Vane," the stranger said. "Royal Cartographers' Guild, third chair. Remember the name or don't. It won't matter soon." He held out the folded map with both ruined hands. "Take it. Swear the words."

"What words?"

"The words." Aldric's face twisted — frustration, or grief, or both. "They didn't teach you. Of course they didn't. They let it all go." He closed his eyes, and when he spoke again his voice changed — steadied, deepened, taking on the cadence of something recited ten thousand times in a hall Edmund had never entered:

"*By ink and intention, by line and by vow — what the map holds, the hand holds. What the hand carries, the heart carries. Until the last road is walked or the last promise kept.*"

The map flared in his grip like a coal breathed upon.

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "I don't know what that is. I've never— my grandmother never taught me any—"

"She didn't know it either," Aldric said gently, terribly. "That's the point. That's why it has to be you. The ones who forgot are free of it. The ones who never learned—" he pushed the map toward Edmund's chest, "—can still choose."

And Edmund, God help him, reached out and took it.

* * *

Later — much later, sitting in the dark with his back against the coldest border stone — he would try to reconstruct exactly what happened in those final minutes, and fail, because memory refused to hold it in order. There was the weight of the map settling into his palms, heavier than paper had any right to be, warm as a sleeping bird. There was the way it unfolded itself without being asked, edges creeping open like a moth testing wings, revealing a country Edmund did not recognize rendered in lines of silver that moved, that breathed, that *corrected themselves* — a river sliding a mile east, a forest thickening, a city assembling itself district by district like a thought becoming certain.

There was the moment the map touched his bare skin and *bit* — not painfully, but decisively, the way a ring bites a finger sized for it. A circle of warmth spread across his right palm, and when he turned his hand over, shaking, there was a mark there: a fine spiral of silver lines, intricate as frost on glass, sunk into the skin itself. Not ink. Not scar. Something between.

"It's bound you," Aldric whispered. He was lying flat now, and the light was leaving him faster, streaming upward in threads that dissolved before they reached the darkening sky. "Good. Good. It needs a hand. Maps are promises, boy, and a promise needs someone to keep it."

"Aldric—" Edmund heard his own voice crack. "What does it want? What am I supposed to do?"

"Follow it." The man's eyes were fixed on the sky, or on something in the sky. "It knows the way. It always knew the way. We just stopped listening." His translucent fingers twitched against the grass, and the flowers bloomed around them in frantic rings. "There's a woman in Vael. Sera. My sister. She'll know— she keeps the records, she'll have seen the blanks, she'll—"

"Blanks? What blanks? Aldric, slow down, tell me what's happening—"

But Aldric Vane was looking at Edmund now, really looking, and what was in his face was not fear. It was something worse. Pity, maybe. Or hope.

"He's still in there," the stranger said.

"What? Who? Who's still in where?"

But the light had almost finished leaving him. It went the way a tide goes — not all at once but in reluctant pulls, each wave dimmer than the last — and the flowers around him slowed their blooming, and the border stones' song softened note by note into silence. The last thing that left Aldric Vane was his eyes' light, and it went so gradually that Edmund could not say when the man stopped seeing him, only that at some point the brown tired gaze was aimed through him at nothing, and then it was aimed at nothing at all.

The body stayed. The body was ordinary — a man in travel-stained clothes, thirty years old or so, dead on the grass of a valley that magic could not enter. Except that where he lay, the grass had grown silver at the roots, and the little white flowers kept opening and closing around him like slow applause, and would keep doing it, Edmund understood without knowing how he understood, until someone buried him properly.

Edmund sat down hard in the dark and looked at his hand.

The spiral mark pulsed once, twice, in time with his heartbeat. And the map — the map had folded itself closed again, neat as a letter, resting in the crook of his arm as if it had always lived there. When he tried to set it on the ground, his fingers would not open. When he tried to stand and walk away, his feet turned him back after three steps, gently, inexorably, like a current in deep water.

Above the ridge, the first stars were coming out. Somewhere beyond them, beyond the hills he had never crossed, lay a city called Vael, and a woman named Sera, and blanks — blanks in records, blanks on maps, a king still in there, still in somewhere, waiting.

The map tugged.

It was not violent. It was not cruel. It was the pull of a thing that had waited a long time and finally been given a hand to hold — and Edmund Thornbury, twenty-two years old, keeper of bruised apples and impossible coastlines, sat against the humming stone with a dead man cooling beside him and a promise biting into his palm, and felt, beneath the terror, beneath the grief, the first unmistakable stirrings of wonder.

The map tugged again. Patient. Certain.

Toward home, it seemed to say. And then away from it.
