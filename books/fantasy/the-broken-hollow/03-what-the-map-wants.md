## 3. What the Map Wants

Edmund did not sleep.

He tried. He lay in his narrow bed with the shutters closed against the last gray light of dusk and the map folded beneath his pillow, and sleep would not come — not because of grief, though the stranger's death sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, but because the map *pulled*.

It was not a violent thing. That was what unsettled him most. It tugged at him the way a river tugs at a wader's ankles: patient, indifferent to his consent, utterly certain of its own direction. Toward the valley's edge. Toward the border stones. He could feel it even through wool and feather ticking, a faint lateral pressure behind his sternum, as if some part of him had already begun walking and was waiting for the rest to catch up.

Near midnight he gave up and lit the stub of candle on his desk. The map lay where he'd left it after prying it from the dead man's hands — no small task; the stranger's fingers had clung like roots. Edmund had wrapped it in linen and carried it home through streets gone quiet, past windows where neighbors were already forgetting the day, and now he unfolded it again because he could not stop himself.

The paper was warm.

Not fever-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm long after the sun has set, holding something it refuses to give back. And when he smoothed it flat across the desk, the ink moved.

He had told himself, walking home, that he'd imagined it. Grief did strange things; the Hollow taught its people that much. But there was no mistaking it now: the fine black lines of rivers and roads shifting like weeds in current, coastlines breathing, a mountain range slowly migrating northward by degrees so small he might have blinked and missed them. And scattered across the whole living surface of it, pale patches — places where nothing was drawn at all, where the paper showed through blank as fresh snow.

One of those blanks sat near the map's western edge. It was shaped, roughly, like a valley.

Edmund's mouth went dry. He knew that shape. He had drawn it a hundred times in the margins of his notebooks, in the dust of the guildhall floor, in the steam on the kitchen window: the Hollow, rendered from above, the long lens of it cupped between two ridgelines. Except here it was empty. Here, the place he had lived every one of his twenty-two years was a hole in the world.

The pull behind his sternum tightened, gentle and absolute, like a hand closing around his heart without squeezing.

"You want me to go," he said aloud, and felt foolish, and said it again anyway. "You want me to go *out*."

The candleflame leaned, though there was no draft. Outside, far off toward the border stones, something hummed — low, sustained, the sound the stones made when magic came near, a sound most people in the Hollow slept through their whole lives without hearing once. Edmund heard it now clearly, and understood that it was not warning the valley about the magic approaching.

It was warning the magic about the valley.

Dawn found Edmund standing in his grandmother's doorway with the map rolled under his arm, watching her move through her morning rituals in the blue half-light. Maren Thornbury was seventy-nine years old and had been losing herself for six years, slowly, the way a shoreline loses ground — a name gone here, a decade gone there, whole seasons washed away overnight. Some mornings she knew him. Some mornings she called him by his father's name, or by no name at all, and asked politely if he was the new apprentice.

This morning she looked up from grinding her tea, and her eyes went straight to the roll of paper under his arm, and everything in her face changed.

"Where did you get that." Not a question. A door slamming.

"A man came across the border last night." His voice sounded very young to him. "He was dying. He bled light, Gran. Actual light. He put this in my hands and said—" He made himself say it, made himself shape the syllables the stranger had pressed into his ear with his last breath, words in an old cadence Edmund had never been taught but which had somehow lodged in him whole, complete, as if they'd been waiting in his mouth all along:

"*By line and oath and open hand, I bind my road to yours until the land is mended or the maker ends.*"

His grandmother set down the pestle. She did it carefully, the way people do things carefully when the alternative is dropping them. Then she crossed the room, took the map from him, and unrolled it on the table, and Edmund watched sixty years of composure leave her face in the space of a breath.

She went pale. Not shocked-pale. *Recognizing*-pale — the pallor of someone seeing a face they buried long ago rise up out of a river.

"Where did you learn that phrase?" she whispered.

"He said it to me. Gran, I've never—"

"No." Her hand hovered over the moving ink without touching it, the way one hovers over a sleeping animal. "No, you wouldn't have. We stopped teaching it before you were born. We stopped teaching it because of *this*." She turned to him then, and her eyes — her fading, unreliable, beloved eyes — were clearer than he had seen them in years, as if the sight of the map had burned away some of the fog. "Sit down, Edmund."

He sat. She remained standing, both hands flat on the table now, framing the map like a woman framing a verdict.

"You think the guild made maps," she said. "Places. Mountains, rivers, roads. Little pictures of the world so travelers wouldn't get lost. That's what we let people believe, when the guild started shrinking. It was easier than the truth." Her thumb traced the edge of the paper, following a border that wasn't a border at all. "The Cartographers' Guild didn't make maps *of* places, child. We made maps *to* them. Promises. Every line we drew was a vow spoken to the land itself — *I will remember you. I will carry your shape in my keeping. Whatever is done to you, someone will hold the true form of you against the day it's needed.* And the land—" her voice caught, steadied, "—the land agrees. That's the binding. That's why the paper moves. You're not looking at ink. You're looking at a covenant, and covenants are alive as long as both parties keep them."

Edmund looked at the blank valley-shaped hole near the western edge. "Then why is the Hollow empty?"

"Because we broke our side." She said it flatly, without excuse. "When the last Masters died and the rest of us forgot our purpose — really forgot, not mislaid it, *forgot*, the way this valley forgets things that cross too near its nature — the promises lapsed. The land released us. Or we released the land. I honestly no longer remember which, and that is the cruelest part." She laughed, short and terrible. "Your valley isn't on the map, Edmund, because the map gave up on the Hollow decades ago. And the Hollow, being what it is, returned the favor."

"The man last night. He said an oath-phrase. He bound something to *me*. I didn't swear anything, Gran, I didn't agree to—"

"You didn't have to." She finally touched the map, one fingertip, and the lines beneath it shivered toward her like plants toward sun. "That phrase is the old receiving-oath. *I bind my road to yours.* When a Cartographer dies with a promise unfinished, the map seeks the nearest hand that could finish it. Blood helps. Inclination helps." Her gaze flicked to the margins of his notebooks stacked by the window — the maps of cities he'd never seen, coasts he'd never walked, all of them accurate, all of them impossible. "Yours has been ready since you were nine years old, drawing Cath Vaelia's harbor in the dust. We all pretended not to notice. I pretended hardest of all."

"Gran—"

"It will take you out of the valley." She said it simply, and the simple saying of it was worse than shouting. "It's already pulling you. Don't lie to an old woman and tell you can't feel it. And Edmund — listen to me, because I may be clear now and fogged by supper — outside these stones, the magic will have you. All of it. Everything this valley has kept off you for twenty-two years, every current and creature and covenant, and the map will steer you through it like a leaf steered by floodwater. There are things out there that eat memory. Whole districts of the world going dark. If the man who carried this died of it—"

"He said something else. At the end." Edmund's throat worked. "*He's still in there,* he said. Over and over. I don't know who."

His grandmother was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gentled into something he hadn't heard from her since childhood: the voice she'd used for storms and fevers and other unstoppable things.

"Then someone is trapped inside whatever wears that kingdom's crown, and the map means to dig him out, and it has chosen you as its spade." She pushed back from the table. "Wait here."

She went to the cedar chest at the foot of her bed — the chest Edmund had never once seen opened — and knelt before it, and her hands shook as she worked the iron latch. From beneath folds of cloth gone soft with age she lifted a compass.

It was small, brass gone green at the seams, its glass clouded like a cataract eye. But when she set it in his palm, the needle spun wildly, wildly, and then settled — pointing west. Pointing, Edmund realized with a chill that started in his spine and spread outward, at the blank valley-shaped hole in the map. At the Hollow. As if the needle knew where its maker's heart was buried.

"Your grandfather's work. Mine, after." She closed his fingers over it. "It doesn't point north. It points at what you've promised. Right now that's the Hollow, because the oath hasn't fully taken you yet. When it does—" she smiled, and it broke his heart, that smile, proud and grieving in equal measure, "—it'll point wherever the mending is. Follow it when you're lost. Trust it over the map, if they ever disagree. The compass remembers what I taught it, even if I don't."

"Gran, I can't just leave you."

"You can, and you will, and here is how I know." She took his face in both her hands, and her palms smelled of tea and lamp oil and seventy-nine years of this small safe valley. "Because I have spent six years forgetting, Edmund, and I will tell you the one thing the forgetting has taught me. A life is not made smaller by what you give away. Only by what you refuse." Her thumbs brushed his cheekbones. "Go and keep the promise. Come back and tell me what the world looks like. Even if I don't know your face when you do — tell me anyway. Stories get in under the fog. They're the last thing to go."

He held her then, hard, the map's pull singing through him like a plucked wire, and she held him harder.

At the door, packed and trembling, he paused. "What do I do if it asks me for something? The map. The oath. What if it wants more than I meant to give?"

His grandmother stood in her doorway in the early light, small and straight-backed and already receding from him in every way but one.

"Then you'll learn what every true Cartographer learns, my love," she said. "A promise is only worth what it costs the promiser. Now go. The land is waiting to hear from you."

And the compass needle swung gently in his palm — away from the Hollow, away from home, out toward a kingdom going dark — and Edmund stepped off his doorstep and followed it.
