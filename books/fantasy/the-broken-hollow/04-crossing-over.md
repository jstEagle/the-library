## 4. Crossing Over

The border stones hummed at dusk the way they always had — a low, patient note like a finger run around the rim of a wet cup — but tonight Edmund heard them differently. Tonight he stood on the wrong side of them.

He had said goodbye badly. His grandmother had pressed her compass into his palm that morning with hands that shook not from age but from something closer to fear, and she had told him, "The guild didn't make maps of places, boy. It made promises to them. Go keep one." Then she had forgotten, for a moment, who he was, and asked him kindly if he was the postman's son, and he had kissed her papery cheek and walked out before his courage could curdle into cowardice.

Now the stones rose around him in their ancient ring, lichen-eaten and patient, and beyond them the world went strange at the treeline — a shimmer like heat off summer stone, except it was autumn and cold. The map against his chest pulled. Not gently. It tugged at the bones of him, at the oath it had written into his palm in letters only visible when he bled, and the pull was the pull of a river for a leaf.

"Right," Edmund said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud was how you kept yourself from forgetting they were real. The Hollow taught you that. The Hollow forgot things; its people learned to speak their lives back into existence daily, names and errands and griefs repeated like prayers.

He stepped between the two leaning stones.

Magic hit him like weather.

There was no other word for it, though he would spend years searching for one. It came over him all at once, from every direction, a pressure and a temperature and a taste — ozone and honey and iron, sweet and wrong and wonderful. His knees buckled. The grass beneath him blazed green so violently green it hurt to look at, every blade distinct and singing its own small note of being-alive. Above him the sky moved. Not clouds moving across sky — the sky itself, a slow turning of colors like oil on water, violet bleeding into gold bleeding into a blue so deep it seemed less a color than a place.

Edmund vomited into the singing grass and lay there shaking.

In the Hollow, magic could not cross the border. He had grown up in a world of plain physics, of wood and water and weather that meant nothing except rain or snow. He had thought, in his secret heart, that the world outside was simply more of the same — bigger, perhaps, wider roads and taller towers, but made of the same honest stuff.

He had been wrong. The world outside was *alive*, and it did not care about him at all. That was the terrible beauty of it. A hawk turned overhead trailing faint ribbons of light from its wingtips. A stream nearby ran uphill for three feet before remembering itself and laughing — actually laughing, a sound like bells swallowed by water — as it corrected course. Everything here was saturated, humming, drunk on its own existence.

And everything here could see him. He felt it: a thousand small attentions turning toward the dry, silent, hollow-shaped hole where he stood, the one place in all this rioting life where magic simply wasn't. He was a silence in a symphony. He was a footprint in a field of flowers.

The map burned through his shirt.

He got up. He walked. There was nothing else to do; the map had decided, and the map was stronger than he was. It hauled him north through the night like a fish on a line, and Edmund stumbled after it through country he could not have described later if his life depended on it — through forests where the trees grew in spirals, past villages whose windows glowed with light that had no fire behind it, along roads that shifted subtly when he wasn't looking, as though the land were rearranging furniture.

He tried to mark his way. Old habit, grandmother's training: note landmarks, count paces, fix bearings. But the landmarks wouldn't hold still, and the compass she'd given him spun slowly, dreamily, pointing now at him instead of north, as if it found him more interesting than the pole.

"You're useless," he told it, and it ticked once, affronted.

Dawn found him on a hilltop, and below him sprawled Vael.

Even drained, even half-mad with exhaustion and magical sunburn — for that was what it felt like, a sunburn of the soul, every nerve scraped raw — Edmund stopped and stared. The city filled the valley floor like spilled mercury: white walls, slate roofs, a hundred towers, and above them all the castle, vast and black-stoned, crowned with spires that caught the early light and threw it back in colors the sky hadn't asked for. Rivers of traffic poured in through the gates. Bells rang somewhere, layered bells, calling the hour in harmonies.

It was the largest thing he had ever seen. It was also, he realized with a slow cold turn of his stomach, wrong. He couldn't have said how until he looked longer — then he saw it. Whole districts of the city sat dim. Not ruined, not abandoned. Dim. As if someone had gone through with a damp cloth and wiped the color off. People moved through those streets, but slowly, pleasantly, like sleepwalkers at a festival.

The map pulsed against his chest, once, hard, like a heartbeat. Like recognition. Like grief.

*That,* it seemed to say. *That is what I am for.*

* * *

They took him at the gate.

He should have expected it. He was filthy, bloodied — his palm had split again in the night, the oath-letters weeping red — and he was carrying papers sealed with a crest that made the gate-sergeant go still as a dead man. But mostly it was his eyes, he thought later. He couldn't stop looking up. In the Hollow there were no buildings taller than a granary, and Vael's walls went up and up, and he stood craning like a fool while the crowd flowed around him, and the guards flowed too, four of them, gray-eyed and unhurried.

"Traveler." The word was polite. The hand on his shoulder was not. "Where are you coming from?"

"The Hollow," Edmund said, and watched the word land. The sergeant's face didn't change, but the faces of the other guards flickered — a twitch, a blink, like men hearing a name from a dream.

"There's no Hollow on the census rolls."

"It's west. Past the—"

"There's *nothing* west." The sergeant smiled. All four of them smiled, at the same moment, with the same mildness, and Edmund understood with animal clarity that the smile was not theirs. Something wore these men the way his map wore ink. "You'll come with us. Someone will want to look at your papers."

They took the map. That was the worst of it — rough fingers pulling it from inside his coat, and the pain that shot up his arm was so sudden and total that he screamed, actually screamed, in the middle of the gate square, and the gray-eyed guards exchanged a glance of mild interest while the crowd stepped around him without curiosity. Nobody stopped. Nobody stared. In the Hollow, a scream would have brought half the village running. Here it slid off the morning like rain off oiled cloth.

They put him in a cell below the gatehouse and gave the map to an officer, and Edmund heard the officer's footsteps stop halfway down the corridor, and stop, and stop, and then quicken — hurrying upward, toward the castle, carrying his promise away from him in someone else's hands.

He sat on the straw in the dark and shook.

This was the part he had not planned for, because he had not really planned at all; he had been dragged out of his life like a root torn from soil, and now he lay in the dark of a foreign kingdom with his gift confiscated and his palm throbbing and magic pressing against the cell walls like a tide against glass. He could feel it out there. Even muffled by stone, it seeped through — the alive-ness, the vast indifferent attention of the world. And threaded through it, faint but unmistakable, a wrongness. A hunger-smell. The same flavor the dim districts had. It came from the direction of the castle, steady as smoke from a chimney.

Something up there was eating.

"He's still in there," Edmund whispered to the dark, the dead stranger's last words, because saying things aloud was how you kept them real. "Whoever you are. You're still in there."

"I know," said a voice from the next cell. "I've been trying to prove it for three years."

Edmund lurched upright. The wall between the cells was old, mortar crumbling, and through a knothole-sized gap a sliver of lamplight fell across a woman's eye. Just an eye, sharp and dark and exhausted, watching him through the gap.

"You heard me?"

"This whole block hears everything. The stones are thin and the guards are deaf — that's not a figure of speech, incidentally. They're becoming literally deaf. To certain things." The eye narrowed. "You said 'the Hollow.' At the gate. They wrote it down and then the paper went blank within the hour — I've seen it happen. Names of places the crown doesn't remember don't stay written. So tell me, traveler, since your name won't survive the night either: how does a man walk out of a place that isn't on any map, carrying a guild seal that hasn't been used in a century?"

Edmund's mouth went dry. "You know the seal?"

"I'm the royal archivist. Was. Before I became a traitor, which happened around the time I started asking questions like *why are the census maps going blank.*" A dry, humorless breath. "My brother carried that same seal. He left eight months ago to find the Cartographers' Guild. Last week his body washed up in the canal with his eyes burned out and a message folded into his hand that took me four days to decode. It said: *find the one the map chooses.*"

The gap in the wall was very small and very bright and Edmund could not look away from it.

"So," the woman said quietly. "Did it choose you?"

His palm throbbed. Somewhere overhead, the stolen map was being carried deeper into the castle, toward the hunger-smell, and every second it got further from him the ache in his chest sharpened like a wire drawing tight.

"Yes," Edmund said. "It chose me. And I think it's about to be fed to whatever wears the crown."

Silence. Then, through the gap, the eye came closer, and the voice that followed had lost its exhaustion and found something harder underneath, something forged and ready.

"My name is Sera. My brother died getting you here, whether he knew it or not. So tonight you're going to tell me everything — and tomorrow, cartographer, you and I are going to get your map back."
