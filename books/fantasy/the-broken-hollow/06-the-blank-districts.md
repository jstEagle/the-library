## 6. The Blank Districts

The map had been pulling for three days.

Edmund felt it in his palm the way a fisherman feels a line go taut — not pain, exactly, but insistence, a current running up through the bones of his hand and into his wrist. He had learned to read its moods. When they walked toward Vael's heart it lay quiet as pondwater. When they turned east, toward the river districts, it tugged like something drowning.

"It wants us to go to Marrowfen," he said, on the morning they finally obeyed it. He held the map open across his knees on the archive floor, where Sera kept a cot among her forbidden books. The district sat at the city's eastern edge, hunched against the river like an old man against a wall. On the map it was not blank. That was what had frightened him at first — he'd expected another white wound, another place eaten clean. Instead Marrowfen was drawn in fine gray lines, faded almost to nothing, like ink washed once too often. A district remembered by no one, rendered in the color of forgetting itself.

Sera stood over him with a candle, and the light made hollows of her face. She hadn't slept. He could tell because she'd stopped braiding her hair, and because she was holding her brother's letter again — the last one Tam had sent from the guild, three winters gone. She folded it away when she saw him looking.

"Marrowfen doesn't exist," she said. "I mean that literally. I've checked every census since the Founding. There is no Marrowfen. There's a blank space on the tax rolls between Ferrier's Walk and the river, and the clerks write *uninhabited* in the margin, and none of them find it strange." Her voice did the thing it always did when she was frightened — went flat and precise, an archivist reading from a ledger of horrors. "Two hundred years of records, Edmund. And a hole in them shaped like four thousand people."

"Then how do you know the name?"

"I don't." She looked at the map, at the gray ghost-lines. "The map does."

* * *

They went in at dusk, through the Ferrier's Walk gate, past guards whose eyes had that faint gray wash Edmund was learning to hate — the color of men who had forgotten why they were standing there but not that they were supposed to be standing there. No one stopped them. No one looked at them at all, really. Sera wore her archivist's sash, and the sash was a kind of spell: people saw authority and let their eyes slide off everything else.

The streets changed gradually, the way a fever comes on. First the lamps were unlit — not broken, just cold, as if no one had thought to ask them for light. Then the shutters hung crooked, unmended. Then the smell: river-mud and cold ash and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in a bowl. Edmund's skin prickled. In the Hollow, magic couldn't cross the border stones; here it soaked into everything, and this close to the crown-thing's feeding ground he could feel it pressing against him like deep water.

And the people.

They came out of doorways as the light failed, drifting into the streets without purpose or hurry. An old man stood at a corner turning his hat over and over in his hands, smiling at nothing. A woman swept a step that was already clean, the broom whispering back and forth, back and forth, her smile fixed and gentle as a painted saint's. They were not miserable. That was the worst of it. They were *content* — placid as livestock, warm as banked coals — and their eyes, all of them, all of them, were gray.

A man drifted close enough to touch. Edmund caught his sleeve. "Excuse me — can you tell me where we are?"

The man smiled. It reached his eyes and died there. "Of course," he said warmly.

"Where are we?"

"Away," the man said, after a pause so long a moth crossed the space between them twice. "We're away."

"What's your name?"

The smile flickered — the first true thing Edmund had seen on any face here — and then smoothed over. "I'm sure I have one," the man said pleasantly. "It'll come back to me."

He wandered off, still smiling, still turning nothing over in his empty hands.

* * *

Sera had stopped walking.

She stood beneath a leaning lamppost with her arms wrapped around herself, and when Edmund reached her she was shaking. Not sobbing. Shaking, small and continuous, like a wire under strain.

"My brother came here," she said. "Three winters ago. The records said a survey team was sent to the eastern districts to investigate the census irregularities. Four archivists. Tam was one of them." Her voice stayed level; only her hands betrayed her. "Three came back. They reported nothing wrong. They smiled the whole time they reported it. I watched them do it and I thought they were lying." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "They weren't lying. They just couldn't remember what they'd seen. And Tam—" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "Tam didn't come back, and nobody remembers he left, except me. Because I keep the records. Because remembering is my whole wretched job, and even I—" 

She stopped. Breathed. Edmund waited, because his grandmother had taught him that waiting was half of listening.

"Even I only remember because I wrote it down," she finished quietly. "In my own hand. Every night since. *Tam left. Tam has not returned.* If I stop writing it, will I stop knowing it?"

Edmund took out the map.

It had changed again. The gray lines of Marrowfen had thinned further since morning, fading toward white, and along the riverbank a new street had vanished entirely — he could see the ghost of it, the way you see an afterimage when you close your eyes. The crown-thing was still feeding. Still hungry. And beneath the fading lines, faint as breath on glass, letters had begun to surface in the margins, in a script he didn't know and yet could somehow read:

*what is taken can be carried*

*what is carried can be given back*

*but only by a hand that holds it*

He showed Sera. She read it three times, lips moving.

"That's guild scripture," she whispered. "Tam had a book of it. I burned it after — after the funeral that wasn't a funeral, because there wasn't a body, because there wasn't anything." She gripped his wrist, hard, above the bound map. "What does it mean? *Carried*? Carried how?"

"I don't know," Edmund said. Which was true, and also not true, because some part of him already knew, the same part that had drawn maps of places he'd never seen, the part that understood what maps were before his grandmother ever told him. A map was not a picture of a place. A map was a promise to a place. And a promise required someone to hold both ends.

* * *

They found the child near the well.

She was perhaps six, sitting on the well's stone lip with her feet swinging, watching the dark water with perfect contentment. She was the only person in Marrowfen who wasn't smiling, and Edmund understood why before he understood anything else that night: she was too young to have many memories to lose, and so whatever had been taken from her had left a smaller hole, and smaller holes hurt more. She looked at them approaching with the grave, patient attention of the very young.

"Hello," Edmund said, crouching to her height.

"Hello," she agreed politely.

"Are you waiting for someone?"

"For my mama. She's sweeping." A pause. "Or someone is sweeping. I think it's my mama." The grave look wavered. "I can't remember her face. Isn't that funny? I know I have one. A mama. But when I try to look at her inside my head, it's like the water here. You look down and you see yourself instead."

His chest tightened until breathing was work. Behind him, Sera had gone very still.

"What's your name?" he asked gently.

She considered this with real effort, brow furrowed, swinging feet gone still. "I had one," she said at last. "It fit right here." She touched her own chest, small hand flat over her heart. "But it's gone soft. Like bread left out. I keep reaching for it and my hand goes through."

*what is taken can be carried*

Edmund looked down at the map. At his own hand, bound to it, the oath he'd never sworn humming in his palm like a second pulse. He thought of his grandmother, who was losing herself room by room, memory by memory, and how he would have given anything — anything — to carry some of hers for her, if carrying were possible.

Maybe it was.

"I'm going to try something," he told the child. "It might feel strange. Like someone reading over your shoulder. Can you be brave for a moment?"

She nodded solemnly, the way children nod when adults ask impossible things.

He laid his free hand over hers, on the spot where she'd said her name used to live, and opened himself to the map.

It rushed him.

Not the tug of a current this time but a flood — the map pouring *through* him, using him as a channel, and into that channel came... her. Fragments of a small life: the smell of river mud and bread; a woman's laugh, low and quick; a red door with a chipped bottom panel; a lullaby in a minor key about a boatman; and then, rising through all of it like a fish surfacing, a word, a name—

*Wren.*

It settled into him. Not as knowledge — as weight. He was suddenly carrying her name the way you carry a sleeping child, careful of its head, aware of it in every step. He could feel it resting somewhere behind his ribs, warm and alive and *hers*, and he understood with terrible clarity that he could walk away with it, that it would live in him as long as he lived, that the crown-thing could never take it now because it was no longer hers to lose.

"Wren," he said aloud.

Her whole body changed. It was like watching frost reverse — like watching winter run backward into spring. Her eyes cleared from gray to a brown so dark it was nearly black, and she gasped, both hands flying to her mouth.

"That's mine," she breathed. "That's *mine*. Wren. I'm Wren." And then, faster, tumbling: "My mama's name is Odile and our door is red and she sings the boatman song wrong, she always gets the third verse wrong on purpose to make me laugh—" She stopped. Looked at him with those enormous dark eyes, and the look in them was the first wholly human thing Edmund had seen in Marrowfen. "You're holding it for me. I can feel it. It's warm."

"Yes," Edmund managed. His voice cracked on it.

"When will you give it back?"

*what is carried can be given back*

"When we make it safe," he said. "When the thing that took it is dead. I promise." And the words sank into his palm, into the map, into whatever old machinery of oath and binding ran beneath the world — and the map drank the promise like rain on drought-cracked earth.

Wren nodded, satisfied, six years old and trusting, and ran off toward the sound of sweeping, calling *"Mama! Mama, I remembered!"* — and Edmund heard a broom stop, heard a woman's voice rise in confusion and dawning, broken joy, and he had to turn away because his eyes were burning.

* * *

"She'll lose it again," Sera said later. They had withdrawn to the ruins of a granary on the district's edge, out of sight of the drifting, smiling crowds. Through the broken roof, the stars wheeled, indifferent. "Whatever you did — it bought her hours. Maybe a day. The hunger is still here. You felt it, the whole time you held her name — the pressure, like something circling." She hugged her knees, chin resting on them, staring out at the gray-eyed town. "You gave a drowning girl one breath of air, Edmund."

"One breath is more than she had." He flexed his aching hand. Holding Wren's name had cost him something — he could feel the absence vaguely, like a tooth socket, though he couldn't say what had filled the space before. The map took its toll for its miracles. He was beginning to understand that everything in this kingdom did. "And it proved it works. The scripture, the map, all of it. What's taken can be carried. What's carried can be given back." He looked at her. "Every name in this district. Every stolen year. It's not gone, Sera. It's *stored*. Somewhere. All of it — four thousand lives, your brother's memories, three winters of a king—"

He stopped. Sera had lifted her head slowly, and in the starlight her face had gone pale as paper.

"The nest," she whispered. "Tam's last letter — the one fragment I recovered from his rooms. I thought it was nonsense. I thought grief had made me see patterns." She fumbled at her collar and drew out a scrap of paper, soft as cloth from handling, and read by starlight: "*It keeps what it takes. Below. It keeps everything, below, it is building something—*" Her voice failed. She pressed the scrap flat against her chest. "It's not eating them. Eating is destruction. It's *hoarding*. Everything the crown-thing has ever taken is still *there*, Edmund — kept, below the castle, in the dark, like a dragon on gold."

"And if it's kept," Edmund said slowly, feeling the map warm against his palm as though it had been listening all along, "then it can be reached."

Far off across the rooftops, toward the castle hill, a bell rang the hour — and beneath its bronze voice, faint but unmistakable, Edmund heard the map begin to hum. Not the tug of a current this time. A drawing-tight. A promise being aimed.

It knew the way down.
