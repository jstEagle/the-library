## 7. Audience with the Hollow King

The petition that bought us into the palace was a lie written in perfect truth.

Sera had spent three days composing it, hunched over her desk in the safehouse with ink to her elbows, drafting and redrafting until she held up a single sheet and said, "There. A request for archival verification of land grants in the Ashvale district." She said it flatly, but I saw what she'd done — Ashvale was one of the blank places, one of the districts the crown had eaten down to its stones, and asking after its records was like asking a thief to account for his pockets. The petition was honest. That was its genius. No forger could be caught in a document that contained no falsehood.

"They'll let an archivist verify records," she said. "It's beneath suspicion. Bureaucracy is the best disguise ever invented — nobody looks twice at a clerk."

"And me?"

"You're my assistant." Her mouth twitched. "Try to look forgettable."

I had spent twenty-two years trying not to be noticed in a valley of forty people; I thought I could manage it in a city of thousands. I was wrong about the scale of the difficulty but right about the skill. The palace swallowed us the way the Hollow swallowed sound — completely, without echo.

We passed through gates where guards stood so still that frost formed on their pauldrons without their seeming to feel it. Their eyes were gray. Not blind-gray, not old-man-gray — gray the way river stones are gray, worn smooth by something passing over them again and again. Sera walked through them without flinching, and I understood that she had been walking through them for three years, carrying her grief like a lantern, looking at those worn-smooth eyes and seeing her brother's face in every one.

The map against my ribs had been quiet since we entered the city, quiet as a held breath. Now, as we climbed the great stair toward the royal apartments, it began to warm.

* * *

The audience chamber was smaller than I expected, and colder.

It was a long room paneled in pale wood gone silver with age, hung with tapestries so faded they were more memory than image — you could see where hunts had once raced through forests that had bleached to ghosts. At the far end, in a chair that was a throne only because someone had once decided it was, sat the king of Vael.

He was not what I expected. I think I expected a monster — something with too many teeth or too few shadows. Instead there was a man of perhaps fifty, thin as winter branch, dressed in plain wool with a circlet of iron on his brow. The crown-thing did not wear gold. Gold would have announced itself. It wore iron, and it sat very still, and it looked at us with eyes the exact color of the guards' eyes below, and for one merciful moment I thought: perhaps he is simply ill. Perhaps this is all a sickness and Sera is chasing a fever with knives.

Then he spoke, and the voice was wrong. Not monstrous-wrong. Empty-wrong. The way a house sounds when everyone has left it.

"Archivist Vael-Serane." He pronounced her full name carefully, like a man handling borrowed tools. "You bring a petition regarding Ashvale."

"Yes, Majesty." Sera bowed the precise depth required. "The district's records are incomplete."

"The district's records are gone," the king said, "because the district is gone."

The silence that followed had weight. Sera kept her bow. "Gone where, Majesty?"

And the king laughed.

It was a terrible thing to hear, because underneath the laugh — buried in it, tangled in it like a drowning man under ice — was another laugh, a real one, human and startled and afraid. Two men laughing in one throat. The second one was screaming.

"Where indeed," said the king, or the thing, or both. "Come closer, archivist. Both of you. Closer. I am told I do not receive enough visitors. I am told—" and here the voice stuttered, snagged, changed register mid-word, dropping half an octave into something rawer, "—*I am told nothing, no one comes, please*—"

"Majesty?" Sera said carefully.

The king's hands gripped the arms of the throne. His knuckles whitened. And his face — I watched it happen, watched it the way you watch weather change — his face moved. The gray drained from his eyes like silt from floodwater, and what was left underneath was brown and wet and desperate, and it was looking at me.

"You're one of theirs," the man inside said. His voice cracked on every word. "The guild. I can smell the oath on you. Boy. Boy, listen to me, I don't know how much time—"

"Your Majesty, perhaps we should—"

"*Listen.*" He wasn't shouting. That was the worst part. He was begging, quietly, precisely, the way men beg who have learned that volume costs them hours afterward. "Three winters. Three winters since I dreamed. Do you know what it eats? Not the body. The body it leaves. It eats the *having-been*. My mother's face — I had it yesterday, I swear I had it yesterday, and now there's a shape where it was. My daughter's name. I wrote it on my arm in the dark so I wouldn't lose it and now I look at the letters and they're just letters." He pulled back his sleeve. There were words inked there, dozens of them, layered and rewritten until the skin was a palimpsest of desperation. Half of them, I realized, were names. Half of them meant nothing to him anymore.

"My brother came for you," Sera whispered. "Tomas. He went to find the guild."

Something moved behind the king's eyes — recognition, or the ghost of it. "Tomas." He said the name like a prayer he was testing to see if it still worked. "Yes. Yes, Tomas. He crossed the border. He found the maps. He was going to bring back the first promise, the broken one, the one that started all of this—" The gray began creeping back in at the edges of his irises, slow as dusk. "Boy. Cartographer. Finish it. Whatever he started, whatever he paid for — finish it. Don't let it make me into a country it owns. Don't let me forget that I'm—"

The word *me* never arrived. The gray closed over the brown like water closing over a stone, and the king of Vael sat back in his chair, smoothed his plain wool sleeves, and said in the empty-house voice:

"You will find the archives satisfactory, I trust. Ashvale has always been a productive district."

* * *

I don't remember leaving the chamber. I remember the corridor afterward, the tall windows and the light coming through them gray as the guards' eyes, and Sera gripping my arm hard enough to bruise.

"He surfaced," she breathed. "Did you see — three years, Edmund, three years and he *surfaced*, he knew Tomas's name—"

"He knew because it ate Tomas's memories too." My hand throbbed. I looked down. Through the glove I'd taken to wearing, the map was glowing faintly, a heat like a coal wrapped in linen. "Everything it takes goes into the nest. Everything it takes is *there*. Sera — if the nest holds the king's mother's face, his daughter's name, everything — then unbinding isn't just breaking a spell. It's giving it all back."

"Then we break it and give it all back."

"It's not that simple. Nothing that eats for three years gives up its meal politely." The map pulsed again, harder, and I bit down on a gasp. "It's pulling. It wants me to go somewhere."

"Where?"

I unwound the glove. In the middle of the audience chamber, with guards at either door, I unfolded a bleeding map on a palace corridor floor, and we both looked down at what it wanted.

The palace itself was drawn there now — it hadn't been before; the map redrew itself nightly, hungrily, adding the world as it learned it — and beneath the drawn foundations, beneath even the cellars marked in the archivists' plans, there was a space. A chamber that existed on no plan Sera had ever catalogued. The map showed a route to it: down through the old cisterns, along a passage that bent in ways architecture shouldn't bend, to a small round room directly beneath the throne.

And in that room, rendered in ink that moved like something breathing, was a shape. A hoard. I thought of dragon-hoards in the old stories, gold piled to the rafters, and this was that — except the treasure was names. Faces. Years. Little glinting fragments of having-been, stacked and sorted and *kept*, and at the center of it all, folded with terrible care, a map older than any I had ever seen. The first promise. The broken oath.

"That's where it nests," Sera said. Her voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when she talked about Tomas. "Under the throne. Under *him*. It sits beneath him every day and drinks."

"It's showing me the way." The burning in my palm had become almost unbearable, and yet underneath the pain was something else, something I recognized from the night the stranger died at the border stones: reluctant wonder. The map wasn't dragging me anymore. It was *asking*. "Sera, why would it show me the way to its own—"

"Doors open both ways," said a voice behind us.

We turned. The guard had come up the corridor without sound — impossible, the floor was marble, and yet there he stood, gray-eyed, unhurried, with two more fanning out behind him. The nearest one looked at Sera, and something in his expression made my stomach drop, because it wasn't blankness. It was attention. Focused, deliberate, *directed* attention, and I understood in one cold flash that the thing downstairs had heard every word, had felt the map unfold above its hoard like a hand reaching toward a wound, and had sent its worn-smooth-eyed servants to collect the threat.

"Archivist Serane," the guard said. "His Majesty requests your continued presence. Indefinitely."

"Sera—"

"Edmund." She didn't look at me. She was already squaring her shoulders, already becoming the bureaucrat, the harmless clerk, and beneath that mask her eyes found mine for half a heartbeat and gave me an order no rank could convey: *run, finish it, don't you dare come back for me.* "Go file the petition," she said aloud. "I'll handle the records."

They took her arms. She didn't struggle. Struggling would have confirmed something, and Sera Vael-Serane had survived three years in a hollowed kingdom by never confirming anything — but as they turned her away, she said, clearly, in the flat tone of an archivist citing a source: "Maintenance tunnels, third cistern. The plans are in the sealed wing. Section nine."

A message. A route. A goodbye.

The guards carried her off down the gray corridor, and I stood alone in the palace of the hollowed king with a burning map in my hand and a choice splitting me down the middle like a border stone.

Every instinct I owned screamed at me to follow her. Every year of the Hollow, every lesson of my grandmother's, every ordinary cowardly fiber of my small safe life said: *you are one person, you are a cartographer, you draw places, you do not descend into them.*

But the map had stopped asking.

It pulled.

And beneath my feet, deep beneath the marble and the cellars and the bending passages, something ancient shifted in its hoard of stolen years — and waited for me to come down and make the trade.
