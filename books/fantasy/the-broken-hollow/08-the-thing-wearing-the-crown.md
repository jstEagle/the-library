## 8. The Thing Wearing the Crown

They took her boots first.

Sera never learned why. The guards — gray-eyed, gentle in the way of people who had forgotten they were capable of cruelty — unlaced them and carried them away like relics, and after that the stone floor of her cell was all she knew of the world beneath her feet. Cold that seeped upward through the soles of her stockings until her thoughts themselves felt chilled, slowed, thick as tallow.

Three days. Or four. Time went strange without windows. She measured it instead by the torchlight in the corridor's grate, which came twice a day and dimmed twice a night, and by the sound of the castle drinking — a great gurgle somewhere below, as if the foundations themselves swallowed.

On what she decided was the fourth day, someone knocked on her door.

Not the flat boom of guards. A knock she knew. Two quick, one slow, the rhythm Tam had invented when he was nine years old and sneaking into the archive after hours to bring her honeycakes wrapped in a handkerchief. *Two quick, one slow, so you know it's me and not the archivists.*

Her heart did something foolish and hopeful before her mind could stop it.

"Come in," she said, though there was no lock on her side of the door, no bolt, nothing she controlled.

It opened, and her brother walked in.

He looked exactly as he had the last morning she'd seen him — the morning before he'd crawled across a valley border three hundred miles away and died with a stranger's name in his mouth. Same crooked collar. Same ink stain on his right knuckle that never washed out because he licked his pens like a savage. Same tired smile, the one that said *I've done something stupid again, but listen, it worked.*

"Tamsin," she breathed.

"No," said the thing wearing him.

The smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the worst part. Grief moved through her like a wave breaking, and the face did not move at all, did not flicker, held its shape the way a mask holds its shape — perfectly, patiently, from the outside.

"You're not—"

"I'm not," it agreed, in his voice, with his slight rasp on the vowels. "But I have him. All of him. Every year." It sat down on the stool the guards had left, folded its hands — *his* hands, ink stain and all — and looked at her with eyes that were gray only at the very center, like wells with light still caught near the top. "Would you like to see?"

"I'd like you to rot."

"That's grief talking. I understand grief. I've eaten a great deal of it." It tilted its head, and the gesture was so precisely Tam — the way he listened, chin down, giving you his whole attention — that Sera had to grip the edge of her cot to keep her hands from shaking. "I don't want your loyalty, Archivist. Loyalty is bitter. I want something much simpler. The cartographer boy is going to come down into my house. He'll come tonight or tomorrow night; the map is pulling him the way a river pulls a stick. When he comes, he will try to take the first promise back."

"The oath-map."

"You know of it. Of course you do. You're your father's daughter; you read everything they tried to burn." Something almost like admiration crossed the borrowed face. "Here is my offer. Tell me where he will enter. Tell me what the map has shown him. And I will give Tamsin back to you — not the body, the body is spent, but the memory entire. Every birthday. Every argument. The lullaby Mother sang wrong on purpose. You would carry him whole inside you for the rest of your life, instead of the ragged half you have now. Isn't that what you archivists want? Everything preserved? Nothing lost?"

Sera discovered she was crying. She hadn't noticed it start. The tears were cold on her face, and she wiped them with the heel of her hand and made herself look at the thing squarely.

"When you eat a memory," she said, "what happens to it?"

"It lives in me."

"And the person it belonged to?"

The thing paused. For just a moment, the mask seemed to consider whether this question deserved honesty, and she understood — with a clarity that felt like falling — that it was going to answer truthfully, because lying was effort and it was very old and very tired.

"They feel lighter," it said. "That is not a kindness. Lightness is only the absence of weight. They smile more. They cannot say their own names. But they are not in pain, Sera. I am careful about pain. Pain sours the flavor."

"You're describing a graveyard."

"I'm describing hunger." And here the voice shifted — still Tam's timbre, but underneath it something vast and hollow spoke, the way wind speaks through an empty granary. "I was made, do you understand? Made by your guild, long ago, to hold what kingdoms could not carry. Promises need a keeper. Memories need a vault. I was the vault. And then the guild broke its promise to me — sealed me under this stone, gave me nothing, expected me to sleep forever on an empty stomach. Three winters ago the king dreamed too close to my door. I tasted him. Just a taste." Its hands opened, palms up, an old man's gesture of helplessness wearing a young man's fingers. "Do you know what it is to be hungry for two hundred years and then taste? There is no malice in me, girl. There is only appetite, and appetite does not hate what it eats. Appetite loves it. That is the horror of appetite. I love every name I have taken. I love them completely, and I will consume them completely, and both things are true."

Sera sat very still. Partly from fear. Partly because some buried archivist instinct in her was *transcribing*, taking down the confession of a monster in the sealed wing of her own mind, filing it under truths the kingdom had forgotten.

"You killed my brother."

"He came to me freely. He swore the old words. He offered himself as courier — a map must be carried out by a living hand or it goes blank, that was always the covenant. He knew the price. He asked me, at the end, to tell you—" The thing stopped. Adjusted. Tried again, and this time the voice wavered, and Sera could not tell whether the wavering was craft or something real bleeding up through the mask. "He asked me to tell you the archive was right. About everything. And that he was sorry about the honeycakes. He stole them. He never told you. He thought you should know, in case it mattered, in case it changed how you remembered him."

She made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from herself.

"So." The thing stood, smoothing Tam's coat, and the mask settled back into its perfect patience. "The offer stands. His memories, whole, in exchange for the cartographer's route. You have until the torches dim. Think of it as an archival decision, if that helps. Which record matters more? One dead boy's past, or one living stranger's future?" At the door it paused, hand on the frame, and without turning around said, in a voice suddenly small and young and entirely, unbearably her brother's:

"Serry. Two quick, one slow. Please."

Then it was gone, and the door closed, and Sera sat alone with the cold rising through the floor and her brother's last words sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

*He never told you. He thought you should know.*

She pressed her fists against her eyes. And slowly, horribly, she began to laugh — because she saw it now, saw the shape of the trap, saw that the thing had miscalculated in the only way a hungry creature can. It had given her a true memory. Freely. And a true memory, freely given, was worth more than any betrayal.

She got up off the cot. Her bare feet found the seam between two floor stones, the one she'd been worrying at with a bent nail since the second day, and she began, quietly, methodically, to dig.

Above her, faint as breath, she heard the castle groan — and somewhere far below, something vast turned over in its sleep, listening for footsteps that were already falling.

Edmund climbed down into the dark with the map burning against his palm.

The way in was a well shaft behind the old armory, older than the armory, older than anything — the stones fitted without mortar, slick with a moisture that wasn't quite water. The map had redrawn itself twice since the throne room. First it had shown the castle as a body, corridors for veins. Then it had peeled the body open, and now it showed only the descent: a spiral of ink tightening toward a single point beneath the deepest foundation, drawn in strokes that trembled slightly, as if the pen itself were afraid.

His hand hurt. It had hurt since the king's chamber, since the moment the map had flared white-hot and burned its new route into itself — and into him. The skin of his palm had gone smooth and pale where the binding sat, like scar tissue that had always been there.

Down. Rung after rung after rung. The air changed as he descended — warmer, then strangely cool, then warm again, breathing around him like the throat of some enormous sleeping animal. Twice he passed doorways bricked shut, and each time the map tugged harder, and each time he kept climbing down, because the blanks on the map were not doorways. They were stomachs.

At the bottom there was no bottom. There was a room.

No — not a room. Edmund stood at the edge of a space so large his eyes refused it, a cavern beneath the castle the size of the Hollow itself, and it was full.

Names. Faces. Years.

They hung in the dark like lanterns — thousands upon thousands of small soft lights, drifting in slow constellations, and each one hummed faintly with a human sound. A woman laughing at a wedding. A boy reciting the names of rivers. An old man's hands teaching smaller hands to knead bread. Whole lives, suspended, glowing gently, tended like orchards. Edmund's breath fogged in the cold and he understood that he was standing inside the thing's hoard, inside its larder, inside its *heart*, and that every light here was a person somewhere above walking the streets smiling, unable to say their own name.

And at the center, on a plinth of black stone, lay a map.

Older than any map he had ever seen. Older than his grandmother's oldest plates. It was spread open and weighted at the corners with four stones that hummed the same note as the border stones of home, and even from thirty feet away Edmund could read the seal at its base — the compass rose and the open hand, the mark of the Cartographers' Guild in its first century, before the valley, before the forgetting.

The first promise. The guild's founding oath, written onto vellum, given to the deep places of the world: *What is entrusted, we keep. What is kept, we return.*

Broken. Sealed away. Left to starve in the dark for two hundred years.

Edmund's legs carried him forward without his permission. The lights parted around him like a crowd making way, and their humming grew louder, and some of the nearest ones drifted closer, curious, brushing against his shoulders with touches like moth wings — and each touch showed him a flash of someone else's life, a harvest festival, a funeral, a first kiss in a hayloft, none of them his, all of them *real*, and he staggered under the weight of strangers' joy.

The oath-map lay waiting. As he reached for it, every light in the cavern went dark at once.

In the sudden black, Edmund heard the thing speak — not from any direction, but from everywhere, from the walls, from his own burning hand — and its voice was not Tam's voice at all. It was older. It was patient. It was terribly, achingly empty.

"*You brought it back,*" the dark said, and the hunger in it was bottomless. "*After two hundred years, little cartographer — you brought the promise home.*"

The map in his hand flared white.
