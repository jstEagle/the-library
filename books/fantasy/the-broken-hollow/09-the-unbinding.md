## 9. The Unbinding

The stairs beneath the castle did not feel like stone. They felt like the inside of a throat.

Edmund descended with the map pressed against his chest, its edges warm as a living thing, and with every step the dark around him thickened until it was less an absence of light than a presence of it — a gray that breathed. The walls were slick with something that was not quite moisture and not quite frost, and where his fingers brushed them, they came away smelling of old paper and rain on dust. Of archives. Of everything that had ever been written down and then unwritten again.

The map pulled him downward the way his grandmother's compass had once pulled toward true north: patiently, inevitably, without any interest in what he wanted. He had stopped fighting it somewhere around the third landing. The burn in his palm had become a kind of conversation, wordless and one-sided, and he had learned to listen the way she had taught him to listen to a river before drawing it — *the land tells you where it goes, Edmund; your job is only to keep up.*

*Keep up,* he told himself now, and kept going down.

He could hear the feeding before he saw it. Not a sound, exactly — more an absence of sound, a silence so complete it rang. It came up through the floors like cold through a cellar wall, and Edmund understood, with a lurch, that this was what a stolen memory sounded like from the outside. This was the noise of a kingdom forgetting itself.

* * *

The nest opened around him like the chamber of a heart.

It was vast — a cavern hollowed out beneath the throne room itself, its ceiling lost in gray murk — and every surface of it was covered in names. Not written names. *Worn* ones. They hung from the walls in drifting strands, pale and gossamer, thousands upon thousands of them, and each strand shimmered faintly with the shape of what it held. Here a woman's face, laughing, gone before Edmund could see whose. There a summer, whole and golden, its wheat fields rippling. A christening. A funeral. A first kiss under a bridge in a district that no longer appeared on any census roll. The hoard turned slowly in air that did not move, and the sight of it made Edmund's eyes ache, the way staring at the sun through closed lids aches.

At the center, on a plinth of fused glass — sand melted by nothing Edmund could name — lay the original oath-map.

He knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting. It was older than the guild's ruin, older than the Hollow, drawn on skin that might have been vellum and might have been something else, and across its face ran a single line of script in the old guild hand:

*We promise to remember you.*

Broken. The promise was broken — torn clean through the middle of the vow, the way you tear a contract when you mean to be rid of it. And from the tear, all of this had grown. All of this hunger.

Edmund reached for it, and the map against his chest flared white-hot, and the whole cavern turned its attention to him.

Not the strands. Something behind the strands. He felt it wake the way you feel a storm change direction — a pressure shift, a gathering — and then the gray silence found its voice, and it spoke with a thousand mouths that were all the same mouth.

***A cartographer.***

The voice was not cruel. That was the worst of it. It was patient, and vast, and terribly hungry, and there was nothing in it but appetite, the way there is nothing in winter but cold.

***You carry a promise. I have eaten so many promises. They taste of—*** the strands shivered, and for one dizzying moment Edmund tasted it too: ink, and beeswax, and his grandmother's kitchen, ***—of keeping.***

"I'm not here to feed you," Edmund said. His voice sounded very small in all that gray. "I'm here to take it back."

***Everything is taken eventually. Kings. Kingdoms. Names. I do not steal, little maker. I am what remains when remembering stops.***

"It's not stopping," Edmund said, and thought of Sera in her cell below the waterline, thought of her brother dying at the border stones with the words of an oath he'd never been taught still wet on his lips. "That's rather the point of me."

And he turned and climbed.

* * *

The throne room doors were open.

Of course they were open. The crown-thing wanted him here; the whole castle had been bending toward this moment for three days, guards with gray eyes stepping aside without seeing him, corridors rearranging themselves like the pages of a book being thumbed toward its ending. Edmund walked in with the map burning against his ribs and the original oath-map wrapped in his coat, and the doors swung shut behind him with a sound like a held breath released.

The king sat on his throne beneath the high windows, and dawn was coming up outside them, the first honest light Vael had seen in three winters, and it fell across the king's face and did nothing for him at all.

He was almost gone. Edmund could see it now, see it clearly, the way you can only see a thing when it is nearly too late. The man beneath the crown was thin as a candle burned to its socket. His hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, the posture of a beggar or a sleeping child. His eyes were gray — not the guards' faint gray, not a tinge, but full gray, the color of the blank districts, the color of the map's empty places.

But his lips were moving.

Edmund crossed the floor slowly. The crown sat on the king's brow like a spider settled on a flower, and it was beautiful, horribly beautiful, wrought gold twisting into shapes that hurt to follow, and as Edmund drew near he heard what the lips were saying.

"*Run,*" the king whispered. "*Boy. Cartographer. Run. I cannot hold it much longer — it is eating the last of me — run, and tell my people I tried—*"

"I'm not running," Edmund said.

The gray eyes flickered. For half a heartbeat, something human looked out of them — terror, and shame, and underneath both, a stubborn ember of a man who had once, perhaps, been worth following. "*Then finish it,*" the king breathed. "*My brother began it. He died crossing your valley to find the guild. Finish what he started.*"

"So he *was* guild," Edmund said softly. "He swore the oath."

"He swore—" The king's face crumpled. "*He swore to remember me.*"

And the crown moved.

It was not gradual. One moment it sat; the next it *flowed*, gold pouring upward off the king's brow like smoke reversing, rising, spreading, filling the air beneath the vaulted ceiling with a shape too large for the room — a shape made of every stolen strand at once, faces and summers and names braided into a single vast gray hunger, and the last of the king's light guttered like a lamp out of oil.

***YES,*** said the crown-thing, in a voice that shook dust from the rafters. ***THE PROMISE-BEARER COMES TO THE FEAST. GIVE ME WHAT YOU CARRY, MAKER. GIVE ME THE MAP AND THE MAN AND THE MEMORY, AND I WILL LET YOUR VALLEY KEEP ITS QUIET FOREVER.***

"You don't want the map," Edmund said. His heart was slamming against his ribs, but his voice, miraculously, held. "You want what's bound to it."

He unbound his sleeve. He held up his right palm, and the guild seal glowed there, sunk into the skin, the mark the stranger's dying grip had left — the oath he had never spoken aloud, binding him to a promise he hadn't chosen.

"The unbinding," Edmund said. "I know how it works now. I've known since the blank district, since the child. Magic in this world can't take a memory without consent. That's your law. That's the crack in you. Everything you've ever eaten, you've eaten because someone *let go* — because they forgot how to hold on, or were made to. But a freely given memory—" he stepped forward, into the shadow of the crown-thing, into the cold of it, "—a freely given memory has weight. Equal weight to any binding. Even yours."

***GIVE IT THEN,*** the crown-thing said, and Edmund heard, beneath the vastness, something new. Eagerness. ***GIVE ME SOMETHING SMALL. A CHILDHOOD. A FACE. YOU HAVE SO MANY.***

"No," Edmund said. "You misunderstand. I'm not giving you *a* memory."

He knelt, and laid the original oath-map on the floor between himself and the throne, and set his own bleeding map beside it, and pressed his glowing palm flat against both.

"I'm giving you the binding."

* * *

He had expected it to hurt. Everyone expects the great sacrifices to hurt, and in stories they are always described as pain, as fire, as tearing.

It was none of those things. It was *quiet*.

It began with the hum. The border stones' hum, low and constant, the sound that had meant *home* to him for twenty-two years, the sound he had never once heard consciously because it had simply always been there, the way a heartbeat is always there. He felt it rise in him, gather, and leave — pass out through his palm into the maps, into the broken promise, and mend it. *We promise to remember you.* The torn script knit itself whole, and the strand-hoard shivered like a forest in wind.

Then his grandmother's voice. Every version of it. Scolding him for smudging his ink. Singing off-key on festival nights. Saying, in the last lucid hour he'd spent with her, *you were always meant for larger paper, love.* He gave them all away, and each one went gently, the way a bird leaves a branch — no struggle, just flight.

Then the valley itself. The smell of the forge-smoke in autumn. The particular green of the hills after rain. Twenty-two years of small safe days, of a life too small for him that he had loved anyway, that he had *chosen* even in his suffocation, because it was his. He gave it freely. That was the whole of the magic, he understood dimly, even as it emptied him: not the size of the memory, but the freedom of the giving. The crown-thing had devoured a kingdom and grown no stronger than a parasite, because everything it took was taken. What Edmund offered was given, and so it outweighed all of it — a single freely surrendered life set against a hoard of thefts, and the scales of whatever ancient law governed such things tipped, and tipped, and broke.

The crown screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a starving animal finding the food was ash, the sound of three winters of stolen feasting unraveling at once. The vast gray shape convulsed above the throne, and the strands tore loose from it — thousands of them, names and faces and summers, streaming free like birds from a burning rookery — and they did not fall. They flew. Out through the high windows, out over the waking city, home to the mouths and minds that had once held them, and Edmund heard, distantly, impossibly, the whole of Vael begin to weep and laugh at the same time, the way a kingdom does when it remembers itself.

The crown cracked down the middle.

Gold rained across the throne room floor in pieces that smoked and shriveled, and the gray poured out of the king's eyes like water from a breached dam, and Aldric of Vael — Edmund would learn the name later, would learn all of him later — slid from his throne onto his knees on the broken gold, and wept like a child, and lived.

Edmund knelt where he had knelt, palm still pressed to the maps, and felt the last of it go.

The Hollow. All of it. The border stones' hum, the last echo of his grandmother's voice, the green of the hills after rain — gone, passed through him like a tide going out, leaving smooth and empty sand. He searched for the feeling of home and found a shape where it had been, the way you find the dent left in a chair. He knew he had belonged somewhere. He could not remember belonging anywhere.

The map beneath his hand had gone blank. Every city, every road, every stolen district — wiped clean, its work finished, its promise kept.

Except for one place.

In the lower corner, in a hand Edmund recognized with a jolt that was almost grief and almost joy, in his own careful cartographer's script, someone had drawn a small valley ringed with hills. No labels. No border stones marked. Just the shape of it, and beneath it, in letters that trembled slightly, as if the hand that wrote them was learning them for the first time:

*Home.*

The king was saying something — *who are you, boy, who are you, what did it cost you* — and the doors were bursting open, and Sera was there, gray-faced and furious and alive, having fought her way up from the cells with an archivist's knowledge of every forgotten door in the castle, and Edmund looked up at all of it — the weeping king, the morning light, the friend he could not yet remember meeting properly — and held up the map.

"There's one place left to deliver," he said. "I think it's mine."
