## 10. Home, Redrawn

The king of Vael woke on a morning in late spring, and the first thing he did was weep.

Edmund stood in the doorway of the sickroom while the man who had been hollowed for three winters clutched his own hands as though they were strangers returned from a long war. The gray had gone out of his eyes. What remained was brown and wet and human, and it looked at Edmund with an expression he would spend the rest of his life trying to name — gratitude braided with grief, the look of a man who knew that someone had paid for his waking and could not remember what the coin had been.

"You," the king said. His voice was rust and river water. "I know you."

"No, Your Majesty." Edmund's voice came out steadier than he felt. "You don't. That was rather the point."

The physicians fluttered at the edges of the room like moths that had forgotten what light was for. Three days since the crown had shattered in the throne room, three days since the thing that had worn it starved screaming into nothing, and still the castle moved through its corridors like a body remembering how to breathe. Servants wept over laundry they suddenly recalled folding for their mothers. Clerks sat in the archive among Sera's rescued ledgers, reading names aloud just to hear them exist again.

The king dismissed them all with a flick of fingers that were still learning to be his own.

"Sit," he said. "Please. I have spent three years unable to ask anyone for anything. Let me practice."

Edmund sat.

"I am told you gave something up for me." The king leaned forward. "No one will tell me what. They say even they don't know — that the memory went where memories go when they are freely given, and no one can follow it there. But I know its weight, Thornbury. I felt it leave you. It was heavy. It was *home*-shaped." His voice cracked on the word. "So I am offering you one. Stay in Vael. Take a seat at court, a house on the Lantern Row, whatever title pleases you. The kingdom owes you a place to belong, and I intend to pay my debts."

Edmund looked at his hands. The scar across his right palm — where the map had bound itself to him, where it had burned like a brand every mile of the road to Vael — had faded to a pale thread, almost invisible now. Almost.

"Thank you," he said. "Truly. But I have somewhere to be."

*Do you?* asked a small voice inside him, the voice that had lived in the Hollow for twenty-two years and knew every stone of it by heart except that he no longer knew anything by heart at all. *You gave it away. You don't remember your grandmother's face. You don't remember the hum of the border stones or the smell of the guildhall or why you ever wanted to leave.*

He did not answer the voice. He had learned, in the long weeks since the unbinding, that some questions were not meant to be answered but carried.

* * *

Sera found him packing in the guest chamber that evening, which is to say she let herself in without knocking, because she was Sera, and sat on the windowsill with her boots swinging like a girl half her age.

"The Lantern Row house has a very good view," she said. "And the king is not a man who offers twice."

"I know."

"And yet you're folding shirts like a man leaving."

"I'm told I fold shirts like a cartographer," Edmund said. "Every crease a contour line."

She laughed — short, surprised out of her — and then didn't laugh anymore, and the room got quiet in the way rooms do when two people are both trying not to say the true thing first.

"Come with me," she said finally. "That's the less formal offer. No houses, no titles. Just — come. Or stay. Or write to me. I've spent three years learning how to grieve a brother, and then I got him back for exactly one day before—" She stopped. Her brother Tam had died at the border stones with a bleeding map in his hands, and no restoration of the kingdom's memory could give Edmund back the words to say about that, because he had never known them. "I'm bad at this," she finished. "Archives are easier than people. In archives everything stays where you file it."

"Nothing stays anywhere," Edmund said. "I've seen what happens when things stay too still. They get eaten."

She slid off the sill and crossed the room and took his face in her hands, and kissed him once, thoroughly, like an archivist stamping a seal — *this document has been read, this document matters* — and then stepped back before either of them could make it into more or less than it was.

"That's filed," she said. Her eyes were bright and furious and dry. "Under pending. Go home, Edmund Thornbury. And when you get there, if the valley feels like a stranger's country — write to me anyway. Strangers make the best correspondents. All the good gossip, none of the obligations."

He laughed, and it hurt, and he left Vael at dawn.

* * *

The map went blank the night the crown shattered. Every district restored, every stolen name returned to its owners, and then the whole vast parchment had faded to clean white paper — except for one shape in one corner, small and stubborn, drawn in ink that was unmistakably his own hand: the Hollow. The valley he had surrendered. The border stones, the river bend, the crooked little square of the guildhall, all rendered in lines he did not remember drawing and could not deny drawing, because no other hand in the world curved quite that way at the river fork.

It was the map that led him home. He followed roads he'd traveled ten thousand times and recognized none of them, and the map rode in his satchel like a compass pointing at his own lost life, and on the last evening, as the hills rose around him in shapes that should have been familiar and were only beautiful, he understood that this was what his grandmother had meant all those years ago — *the guild didn't make maps of places, boy, it made promises to them* — and that a promise does not require the promiser to remember making it. Only to keep it.

The border stones hummed as he passed between them. He felt it in his teeth, in the scar on his palm: a low sweet note, the sound magic made when it was kept outside, the sound of a door held shut by people who believed doors mattered. He had given away the memory of that sound. His body had kept it anyway, the way a hand keeps the shape of a pen.

The guildhall stood where the map said it would, lamp-lit against the dusk, and there — small and straight-backed on the step, wrapped in the grey shawl he had drawn a dozen times without knowing whose shoulders it belonged to — sat his grandmother.

She was looking at the road. She had been looking at the road, he understood later, every evening for months, though she could not have said why; her memory had gone soft as wet paper, names slipping through it, faces blurring, whole years pooling and draining away. But she looked at the road.

She saw him coming up the hill in the last light, a stranger with a stranger's gait and dust of foreign kingdoms on his boots, and she rose, and her old hands went to her mouth, and she said:

"There you are."

Not *who are you*. Not *can I help you*. There you are, the way you greet someone who is late, someone expected, someone whose absence had become so ordinary you'd stopped noticing it until the moment it ended.

"I—" Edmund's throat closed. He had rehearsed nothing, because there was nothing to rehearse; he did not know this woman, and he loved her, and both of these facts were equally true and equally unbearable. "I'm Edmund. I'm—"

"I know who you are." She came down the steps slowly, one hand trailing the rail, and took his chin in her fingers the way she must have done when he was small enough to lift, and studied his face with eyes clouded by forgetting and somehow not clouded at all. "You've been gone a long time, love. You look taller. Did you eat? Don't answer that, you have the look of a boy who forgets to eat when he's drawing." She tucked her arm through his, proprietary, certain, and turned him toward the hall. "Come in. I'll put the kettle on. You can tell me where you've been, and I'll pretend to remember it after, and we'll both be kind about the pretending."

He walked beside her into the guildhall, into the smell of ink and woodsmoke that his map had promised him, and if his eyes burned, well, the dusk was smoky, and grandmothers do not comment on such things.

* * *

They came to him slowly, the new ones. At first only Tam's grave-marker needed tending, and Sera wrote asking him to see it done properly, and he did, carving the guild seal above the name with hands that remembered the chisel's weight. Then a shepherd's daughter who had always drawn the flocks in the dirt and wanted to know why the shapes pleased her. Then a miller's son, then two sisters from the far side of the valley, then a quiet boy who arrived one morning with a bundle of maps he'd copied from Edmund's own early work, back when Edmund drew places he'd never seen, and asked, breathless, *are these real, can we go to them.*

The guild had numbered four when he left. By the second winter it numbered eleven, and the guildhall smelled of fresh shavings and new ink, and Edmund stood before them on cold mornings and taught them the only lesson that mattered, the inheritance his grandmother had handed him along with her compass and her blessing:

"A map is not a picture," he told them, walking the length of the great table where the master chart lay open under lamplight. "Anyone can copy the shape of a thing. A cartographer makes a promise. When you draw a river, you promise the river will be found where you say. When you draw a village, you promise the village exists — that its people are real, that their names are worth ink. And when you draw a person—" he touched the pale scar on his palm, and eleven young faces watched the gesture with the reverence children reserve for mysteries they intend to solve someday, "—when you draw a person, you promise they can always find their way home. Even if home has to find them first."

His grandmother sat by the fire during lessons now, listening without following, and afterward she would pat his arm and say, *you'll make a fine Master Cartographer someday,* and he would say, *yes, Gran,* and neither of them pretended otherwise, and it was, he thought, the kindest ritual the valley had ever taught him.

* * *

On the last page of his book — the great ledger he kept now, part atlas, part oath-record, part letter to whoever came after — he saved the final leaf for the Hollow itself. Every other place he had mapped since returning: the kingdom of Vael redrawn whole, the blank districts re-inked street by street from Sera's letters, the roads between, the mountains, the coast. Everything but home.

One winter night, with the fire low and the apprentices long abed, he dipped his pen and began.

He did not remember the valley. That was the truth of it, plain as any line he'd ever drawn: the memory was gone, freely given, unrecoverable, spent like a coin to buy a stranger's king. He could not close his eyes and see the border stones. He could not hear his grandmother's voice as it had been before the forgetting took her, or taste the river water, or feel the particular suffocating smallness of the life he had once been desperate to escape.

But his hand set the pen down at the valley's northern edge, and his hand knew.

It drew the border stones in their crescent, each one tilted at the angle he had once known without thinking. It drew the river's bend with the exact curve no other hand in the world made. It drew the guildhall, the crooked lane behind it, the hill where a woman in a grey shawl had sat watching the road every evening for months, waiting for a stranger she somehow knew was hers. Line followed line, sure and unhurried, and Edmund watched his own fingers move like a man reading a letter from someone he used to be, and by the time the last stroke dried he was crying without any memory of why, and laughing too, because the page before him was warm with a belonging he had not been given back but had *chosen*, chosen on a throne-room floor, chosen again with every mile of the road home, chosen now with every line —

And that was when he saw it.

There, at the valley's southern edge, where the map showed the border stones running smooth and unbroken — a gap. A single stone missing from the crescent, drawn in fainter ink, as though his hand had hesitated. As though his hand knew something about that stone that Edmund did not.

He bent closer. Beneath the gap, in letters so small he had to hold the page to the firelight, his own handwriting whispered a single line he had never written, or had written long ago and given away:

*This stone remembers you. Come find out why.*
