## 1. The Keeper of Nothing

The moon rose over Thornfell like a coin dropped into a beggar's cup—thin, tarnished, and worth less than it looked. Ansel Voss watched it clear the eastern ridge from her perch on the gatehouse wall, a tin cup of pine-needle tea cooling in her hands, and thought, as she did most nights, that there were worse views to grow old with.

There was no one to share the thought with. There never was. But the thinking of it had become its own small ritual, the way some people touch iron or whisper to saints: *there are worse views.* She said it to the mountain, and the mountain, being stone, kept her confidence.

Below her, the Hollow Gate filled the notch between two shoulders of the massif like a held breath. It was not a gate in any way a traveler would recognize—no doors, no arch, no portcullis rusted open or shut. It was a pass, three hundred paces wide at its throat, walled on either side by cliffs the color of old bruises, and the road through it simply stopped. Not blocked. Stopped. The paving stones ran up to an invisible line and then became ordinary scree, as though the masons had laid them toward something they lost faith in halfway. Travelers who somehow found their way this deep into the high country—and almost none did, anymore—would stand at that line, feel the air go strange against their skin, and turn back without ever knowing why. They would tell themselves the pass was haunted, or collapsed, or merely dull. The oath saw to it that they never told anyone anything more interesting than that.

Ansel finished her tea and went down to make her rounds.

She had walked these rounds for nineteen years, since she was eleven years old and her mother's hands had gone too stiff with the ache in her joints to climb the eastern stair. Her mother had taught her the gate the way other mothers taught prayers: by repetition, by rote, by walking it together until the walking became the knowing. *Feel before you look,* her mother used to say, palm flat against the cold rock. *Stone tells you what it's carrying if you're patient enough to listen.*

Ansel was patient enough. She had made herself patient enough, because patience was the only inheritance her order had left to give.

The order itself was nearly nothing now—a name in a ledger nobody read, a rulebook she kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath her bed, herself. The Oath of Unmarked Service required three things. First: keep the gate. Second: speak of it to no one. Third—and this was the one that shaped everything else, the one that had hollowed out her mother and her mother's mother before her—*be unseen.* Not hidden exactly. Unremarked. A keeper should be the kind of person eyes slid off, the kind whose face was forgotten before the door closed behind her. Ansel had been practicing invisibility so long she sometimes wondered whether she'd gotten good at it or whether she'd simply become what she practiced: a woman of no particular height, no particular coloring, with a voice pitched to be forgotten mid-sentence. In the valley markets, twice a year, when she came down for salt and lamp oil, merchants took her coin and forgot her purchase before she reached the next stall. Once, as a girl, she had stood directly in front of a neighbor for a full minute waiting to ask about flour, and the woman had looked straight through her and called out to someone else across the square.

Her mother had called that a blessing. Ansel had learned to call it that too. Most days she even believed it.

She started her rounds at the western anchor, as always. The gate's power was anchored at four points—one at each end of the stopped road, and two set back in the cliff faces where only she knew to look—and each anchor had to be checked nightly, felt rather than seen. She pressed her bare hand against the first anchor stone, a slab of granite veined with quartz that glittered faintly even in starlight, and listened with her skin.

Steady. The stone hummed the way it always hummed, a vibration below sound, like the memory of a bell rung a hundred years ago. Whatever the gate held back—and her mother had told her only that it held, never what—the holding was constant. Faithful. The oldest thing in her life, more constant than her own heartbeat.

She moved along the stopped road, boots crunching on frost, checking the drainage channels her great-grandmother had cut and that every keeper since had kept clear. The night was doing what Thornfell nights did: gathering its cold close around the stone, breathing frost onto every surface. Her breath plumed. Somewhere above, wind worried at the crags with a sound like slow tearing cloth.

That was when she found the tracks.

They came up the smugglers' path from the south—the narrow goat-trail that switchbacked down through the black pines toward the low valleys, the path her order pretended not to know existed because acknowledging it would mean acknowledging the traffic it carried. Smugglers used the trail maybe four times a year, moving brandy or untaxed wool or occasionally people who needed to be somewhere without questions. Ansel always knew when they came. She knew every path through Thornfell the way a shepherd knows the faces of his sheep—not just the smugglers' road but the hunter's cut above the frozen falls, the ledge route under Raven Crag that only held in dry weather, the old pilgrim stair swallowed by juniper that she alone had cleared foot by foot over six summers. She knew which paths flooded in thaw, which drifted shut by midwinter, which ones bore weight and which would drop a man screaming into a ravine. It was, if she was honest with herself, the one pride she permitted: that no living soul knew these mountains better. The inquisitors' maps showed three roads through Thornfell. Ansel could have shown them thirty, and eleven of them didn't exist on any map drawn by anyone but her.

But these tracks were wrong.

She crouched over them, holding her lantern low. Four sets of boot prints, heavy-laden, coming up the smugglers' path—but instead of turning west at the fork where the safe route bent away from the gate, they continued east. Straight toward the stopped road. Toward the line where the world went strange.

No smuggler came here. Ever. The smugglers had a whole folklore about the Hollow Gate, garbled and half-understood, and every version of it agreed on one point: you didn't go near the place where the road stopped. Even the desperate ones, even the ones who'd heard rumors of treasure in the high country, gave it wide berth. Ansel had once watched from cover as a smuggler crew argued about it in the fork, and the youngest among them—a boy of maybe sixteen—had looked at the stopped road and gone gray as porridge, and the whole crew had turned around rather than camp within a mile of it.

Four sets of prints, walking straight at it. And here was the second wrongness: the prints were *old*. Frost had grown inside them, feathering the edges, which meant they'd been made at least a day ago—yet Ansel had walked this exact stretch last night and this morning, and there had been nothing. Nothing. She did not miss things on her rounds. Missing things got keepers killed; her mother had said that too, though she'd never explained what killed them.

And the third wrongness, the one that raised the hair on her arms despite the cold: the tracks didn't come back. Four men had walked up the path toward the gate, and no prints led away. Not back down the trail. Not around the stopped road. Nowhere.

Ansel stood very still for a long time, lantern raised, listening to the wind tear slowly at the crags.

Then she noticed the stillness.

It took her a moment to name it, because it was an absence rather than a presence—the way you notice a clock has stopped only after the silence has already grown teeth. The stone under her feet had stopped humming. All along the stopped road, the anchors should have been singing their subterranean note, steady as they'd been for a hundred years and more. She pressed her palm flat against the paving at the invisible line, and felt—nothing. Not weakness. Not failure. *Stillness*, vast and deliberate, like a held breath, like a household going quiet because someone is listening at the door.

The gate wasn't failing. The gate was *waiting*.

Ansel snatched her hand back as if the stone had bitten her. Her heart was suddenly loud in her ears, absurdly loud, loud enough that she glanced over her shoulder at the dark mouth of the smugglers' path, half expecting to find someone standing there watching her jumpiness with amusement.

Nothing. Just the pines, just the frost, just the moon climbing higher and thinner.

She finished her rounds quickly, badly, hating herself for hurrying. At the western anchor the hum returned, steady and faithful, and she clung to that steadiness like a rope over a drop. Probably the stillness was nothing. Probably it was a fluctuation, a weather-thing, some interplay of frost and season her mother would have understood and explained in that calm rasp of hers. Probably the tracks belonged to smugglers who had turned back in the dark and descended by some other way, though she couldn't imagine which, because there wasn't one, because she knew every path—

Probably.

Back in the gatehouse, she banked the fire, sat at her table, and allowed herself the evening's one indulgence: the map. It hung on the far wall behind a curtain, and it was hers alone, drawn in her own hand over nineteen years, amended and corrected until the original ink was mostly memory. Every path in Thornfell lived on it, including the ones that existed nowhere else. She looked at it now the way other people looked at photographs of family.

On the table beside her lay the thing she hadn't let herself think about on the rounds: the token. Because there had been a fourth wrongness tonight, one she was keeping carefully in the pocket of her attention where she wouldn't have to examine it. Wedged in the crack of the eastern anchor stone—wedged deliberately, placed by a hand, because frost doesn't wedge things and gravity doesn't lift them—she had found a small disc of black horn, stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize: a crown, broken cleanly in half, its halves separated by a lightning-shaped fissure.

Someone had climbed to the eastern anchor. Someone had known where it was. No one alive knew where it was except Ansel, and the dead of her order, and whatever was on the other side of the gate.

She turned the token over in her fingers. It was warm. Horn doesn't hold warmth. She set it down on the table, told herself she would throw it into the ravine in the morning, and knew she wouldn't.

Outside, the wind changed direction, and for a moment—just a moment, just long enough—the stillness in the stone seemed to lean toward the gatehouse like a listener leaning toward a window.

Ansel Voss, keeper of the Hollow Gate, keeper of nothing, blew out her lamp and sat in the dark, holding a piece of a broken crown, and waited for dawn the way you wait for a verdict.

* * *

Dawn came gray and grudging, and with it, sanity—or so she told herself. Daylight shrank the night's terrors to their proper size. Tracks could be misread. Frost lied about ages. Stillness in stone was probably a seasonal thing, a deep-winter thing, and tonight she would check her mother's journals and find the explanation in her mother's calm handwriting, and everything would slot back into its groove.

She spent the morning on the foundation repair she'd been putting off for a week: the cracked stone at the gate's southern footing, where meltwater had worked into a seam and frozen and widened it, the patient vandalism of water and cold. It was honest work, good work, work with a beginning and an end, and she threw herself into it gratefully—prying loose the shattered facing, chiseling out the ruined mortar, hauling fresh sand and lime up from her stores. By midday she had the cavity opened and cleaned, a neat rectangle of exposed inner masonry, and she was mixing mortar with her sleeves rolled up and frost-mud to her elbows when her trowel struck something that rang.

Not stone. Stone thuds. This *rang*, a thin metallic note, and Ansel went still with her trowel hovering, listening to the echo die.

She dug carefully after that. The object was a lead box the size of a prayer book, soldered shut, set into the masonry at a depth that meant it had been placed when the gate was built—or repaired, at least, and the repairs were older than her order's records, such as they were. Her heart had begun doing something complicated in her chest. Keepers before her had left caches; she knew of two, small things, spare tools and a vial of anchor-balm. But those were marked. This had been buried. Hidden. From her order, perhaps. Or for it.

She broke the solder with a chisel tip, working slowly, making herself be careful, and lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a book.

It was small, bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, and it was heavier than it should have been, the way certain books are heavy, the way a full stop is heavy. She unwrapped it with hands that had stopped being entirely steady. The cover bore no title. The spine was unbroken—it had never been opened, or it had been opened so long ago the leather had forgotten how. On the first page, in a cramped antique hand, was a single line:

*For the keeper who comes after, when the date arrives.*

Ansel turned pages with fingers gone clumsy. The book was a chronicle, or part of one—entries in at least three different hands spanning decades, observations, measurements, warnings, all of it in language stiff with age. And at the end, on the final written page, one entry stood apart, fresher, the ink still brown-black, the letters precise as engraving:

*Tonight the seal begins to fail. Let the keeper of the Hollow Gate take heed: what was locked away was never destroyed, and it remembers the way out.*

Beneath it, a date.

Ansel read the date once. Then again. Then a third time, her lips moving, though there was no one to see and nothing to say.

It was today's date. Tonight's date. Written in a book buried in her gate's foundations before her grandmother's grandmother had drawn breath.

The wind moved through the pass, and far below in the stopped road, the stone was silent—still holding, still waiting, stiller than it had ever been in nineteen years of her listening.

Ansel sat down hard on the frost-stiff ground, the prophecy book open in her lap, and understood that her life, which had fit her like her own skin for thirty years, had just been measured for something else entirely.
