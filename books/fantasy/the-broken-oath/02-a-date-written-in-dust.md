## 2. A Date Written in Dust

The crack announced itself the way all trouble did at the Hollow Gate: quietly, and only to someone listening.

Ansel Voss had been listening for eleven years. She heard it first as a wrongness in her boot-steps during the evening circuit—a faint hesitation in the stone beneath the third flag of the western stair, a sound like a held breath. By the time she'd fetched the lantern and knelt, the frost had already widened it into something visible: a hairline fracture running from the foundation stone's crown down into the earth, thin as spider silk but going deep. She could tell by the way the cold breathed out of it.

"Who's been walking on you?" she murmured, pressing her palm flat against the granite. It was an old habit, talking to the gate. There was no one else to talk to, and the gate never minded.

The Hollow Gate was not, despite its name, much of a gate anymore. It was a pass—a throat of broken country between two shoulders of the Thornfell mountains, where the trail narrowed to a width that would admit a cart if the cart's driver were brave and the cart were narrow. The wall that had once blocked it had been raised and razed so many times that its bones lay scattered along the defile like the vertebrae of some enormous patient animal. What remained of the formal gate was the archway itself, half-swallowed by lichen, and beneath it the foundation stones—seven of them, each taller than Ansel, each older than any kingdom currently claiming the land above them.

Her order had kept this place since before the maps changed. The Order of the Quiet Threshold, though nobody outside the order called them anything at all, because that was rather the point. Keepers of the Hollow Gate swore the Oath of Unseen Service: to tend, to watch, to mend, and to be forgotten. No name carved in stone. No record in royal archives. When a keeper died, she was buried unmarked in the scree below the pass, and a new one came up the mountain as if summoned by the stones themselves. Ansel had never learned who chose her. She had simply woken one morning knowing the paths through Thornfell the way she knew the backs of her own hands, and she had walked up here, and she had stayed.

Eleven years of nights like this one. She found the rhythm of it good, mostly. The mountain gave her weather instead of conversation, and she had learned to read its moods—the way wind combed east meant snow within three days; the way the pines went silent all at once meant something large moving below, usually elk, sometimes worse. She kept bees in summer and salted meat in autumn and in winter she sat by her stove and mended her own clothes and told herself, in the firm voice one uses with a child who protests too much, that this was enough. That solitude was not loneliness. That being unseen was not the same as being nothing.

Most nights she believed it.

Tonight she knelt in the frost with a lantern hooked over her shoulder and a crack under her fingers, and the gate felt different. She couldn't have said how. The stillness, maybe. Thornfell was never truly still—there was always water somewhere under the ice, always stone settling, always wind worrying at the high crags. But tonight the silence had a quality of attention to it, as if the whole pass were holding its breath around her, waiting to see what she would do about the crack.

"All right," she told it. "All right."

She went back to her hut for tools.

* * *

Mending foundation stones was work most keepers did perhaps twice in a lifetime, and Ansel had done it once before, on the easternmost stone, when she was new and eager and had used far too much mortar. The craft was older than her order's written records: you did not fill a crack in the gate's foundations—you persuaded it closed. Lime and ash and river-sand mixed with melted snow, worked thin, fed into the fracture a finger's width at a time while the stone drank. Rushing it made the stone reject the binding. She had learned that the hard way, and the eastern stone still bore a pale scar where her impatience had been refused.

So she built her fire beside the western foundation stone and set the mortar pot to warm, and while it warmed she cleared the crack's mouth with a bone pick, flicking out grit and ice and a century of accumulated dust.

It was the dust that stopped her.

Not because dust was strange—it gathered everywhere in the pass—but because this dust was *organized*. It lay in the crack in fine, even layers, like sediment in a bottle left undisturbed, and between two of the layers ran a seam of something darker. She picked at it. It flaked. She rubbed it between her fingers and held it to the lantern light.

Ash. Not wood-ash. Bone-white, fine as flour, the kind of ash that came from burned paper—or burned vellum—and had sifted down through stone over a very long time.

Ansel sat back on her heels. Her breath hung in front of her, silvering in the lantern glow.

Someone had burned something inside this stone. Or near enough that the ash had migrated. And then someone had sealed the crack after—not mortared it, not the way she would have, but sealed it, with a plug of stone fitted so precisely that a hundred years of frost-heave had been needed to loosen it again.

She should have gone for the archive key. She knew that even as she reached instead for her smallest chisel. The protocol for anomalies in the gate's fabric was written in the front of every keeper's ledger: report, document, consult. But the archive was a day's walk down the southern trail, and the crack was open now, drinking cold air into the gate's bones, and whatever was plugged into it was directly in the path of the fracture. If she didn't clear it, she couldn't close the crack. And if she couldn't close the crack—

Well. She was the keeper. This was hers to mend.

The chisel found the seam of the plug on the fourth try. It wasn't mortared in place; it was *keyed*, cut with a taper that let friction do the work of a century. She tapped it gently, angling for purchase, and the plug shifted with a sound like a sigh, and slid free into her gloved hand.

Behind it, the stone was hollow.

Not naturally. The cavity was too regular, its walls too smooth—a niche cut into the heart of the foundation stone, perhaps a hand-span deep and wide as two of her palms together. The lantern light fell into it and found cloth: oilcloth, wrapped tight around something book-shaped, tied with cord gone brittle as straw. The wrapping crumbled when she touched it, and underneath, dry as a moth's wing, lay a book bound in grey leather with no title on its spine.

Ansel did not move for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind worried the crags, and the sound came down the pass like a voice calling from very far away.

Then she lifted the book out, and the weight of it—so slight, so absurdly slight for what it would come to mean—settled into her hands.

* * *

She carried it to the fire rather than opening it there in the wind, and she told herself this was prudence, protecting old vellum from the cold, and not that some part of her wanted walls around her before she looked inside. Her hut was small: one room, a stove, a bed, shelves of ledgers in her own hand and her predecessors' hands going back further than the paper could be trusted. She set the lantern on the table, laid the book down beside it, and washed the stone dust from her hands with snow-melt so cold it burned, taking far longer than washing required.

Then she sat, and opened it.

The pages were vellum, fine-grained, and the handwriting was a formal ecclesiastic script she recognized from her order's oldest records—the hand of a trained scribe, patient and exact. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood. There was no title page. The book began mid-thought, as if it were one volume of several:

*...and the seventh seal is not stone, though it is laid in stone, and the seven keepers shall each keep their stone as a man keeps a vow he does not fully understand.*

Ansel turned pages slowly. It was a chronicle of sorts, or the skeleton of one—entries dated in a calendar she half-recognized, the old reckoning that predated the current kingdoms' standard. Entries about weather. About mortar mixes. About the placement of the seven stones and the words spoken over each. Mundane things, keeper's things, recorded with a care that made her chest ache with recognition. Whoever wrote this had been her, a hundred years ago. Had knelt where she knelt. Had talked to the stones because there was no one else.

And then, threaded through the mundane entries, other entries. Stranger ones.

*The third stone hums at moonset. I have checked my instruments twice.*

*I dreamed of a door standing in a field of ash, and behind it not darkness but a country, green and whole, and I woke weeping and do not know for whom.*

*The founders' oath says "keep." It does not say "keep out." I have begun to wonder if these are the same word.*

Ansel's finger paused on that last line. Outside, the wind rose a register, and the hut's shutter knocked once against its frame, and she realized her heart was going faster than the walk up from the gate could account for.

She kept turning pages. The entries grew sparser as the years passed—the scribe aging, the hand stiffening. Gaps of a year, then two. Then, near the end, the dates resumed in a shakier script, and the entries shortened until they were hardly more than marks:

*Still sealed.*

*Still sealed.*

*Cold this year. The stones sing more than they used to. Or perhaps I hear them better now.*

And then the final entry.

Ansel knew it was final before she read it, because the page facing it was blank, and the blankness had a terrible deliberateness to it, like a held breath. The last written page bore a single date, inked larger than any other entry, centered on the page with the care of a scribe who understood exactly what he was writing.

She read it once. Then she set the book down flat on the table, stood up, walked to the window, and looked out at the ordinary night—the stars, the black shoulders of the mountain, the faint orange of her own fire still burning down by the gate—and made herself breathe.

Because the date was tonight's date.

Not approximately. Not in a way that could be reasoned with or explained away by calendar drift, though her mind tried both, frantically, the way a man pats his pockets for keys he can see lying on the table. She had copied the old reckoning into her own ledger years ago, out of boredom, out of the same quiet pride that made her know every path through Thornfell. She knew the conversion. Tonight was the night. A hundred years ago, a dying scribe had sat in this room or one like it and written tomorrow's date—no, *tonight's* date—in a book and buried it in the heart of the gate's foundation, and the frost had taken a century to deliver the message, and it had arrived.

She sat back down. Her hands were steady. She noted that with distant interest, the way one notes the behavior of a stranger.

The final entry was four lines long. Three of them were the date. The fourth, beneath it, in the shaking hand of a very old man or woman who had run out of time to be careful:

*On this night the seal begins to fail, and the keeper will find this book, and the keeper will understand that the gate was never meant to hold forever — only until someone came who could choose. May the keeper choose better than we did.*

*— the keeper*

Ansel read it five times. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself. The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse she had never managed to evict went about its small, indifferent business.

*The keeper.* Not a name. Of course not a name—her order left no names. But the word landed on her like a hand on her shoulder, and she understood, with a dread that opened in her the way the crack had opened in the stone, slowly and then all at once, that the sentence was not addressed to some future keeper in the abstract. The book had waited a hundred years behind a keyed stone in the exact path of a fracture that would need mending. It had not been hidden. It had been *addressed*.

She looked at her own reflection, faint and wavering in the dark window glass: a woman alone in a hut on a mountain, invisible by oath, necessary to no living soul, keeper of a gate whose purpose she had recited every morning for eleven years without once asking what, precisely, she was keeping. *Keep. It does not say "keep out." I have begun to wonder if these are the same word.*

"What did you do?" she asked the dead scribe softly. "What are you making me do?"

The book said nothing. Books were good that way. But the mountain, when she listened—took a breath, and held it, and the silence coming down off the crags had that quality of attention again, sharper now, closer, as if the pass itself had leaned in to hear whether she would say yes.

She wrapped the book in her spare cloak and carried it back down through the freezing dark to the gate, because it seemed suddenly unbearable to leave it anywhere but at the stones' feet, and she fed her mortar into the crack a finger's width at a time while the stone drank, and the fracture closed under her hands as smoothly as a wound knitting, and she did not feel the satisfaction she always felt. She felt watched. She felt, for the first time in eleven years, that the gate was not hers—that she was its, the way a letter is its address, the way a message is the property of whoever finally reads it.

Near midnight she banked the fire and started back up the trail to her hut, and that was when she saw the light.

Down-valley, where no light had any business being—a point of orange bobbing among the black pines, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who knew these trails. Who knew *her* trails. Ansel stood in the shadow of the archway with the mended stone warm at her back and the book heavy against her ribs, and watched the light climb toward her, and understood that whatever the hundred-year-old page had begun, it had not waited for morning to arrive.

Someone was coming up the mountain. And they were coming straight to her door.
