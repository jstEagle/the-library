## 4. Inquisitors on the Pass

Corvin Hale smelled the smoke before he saw the fire, and he knew by the smell that it wasn't cookfire smoke. Cookfires smelled of pine resin and old fat, of beans gone soft in a pot, of the particular sweetness of a camp at rest. This smoke was sharp and chemical, reeking of lamp oil and scorched canvas, and underneath it ran a thread of something worse—burnt paper, burnt leather, burnt things that had once been someone's whole life folded into a pack.

He stood on the ridge above the valley with the dawn coming up grey behind him and watched three smugglers' camps burn.

The first was Doss's old site down by the river bend—the low tents half-sunk into the willows where men who moved contraband through Thornfell had wintered for thirty years without so much as a tax collector finding them. The second was the tannery camp under the scree slope. The third he didn't recognize, which meant it was new, which meant someone had been moving through this valley recently enough to pitch fresh canvas, and the inquisitors had found them anyway.

Soldiers moved between the fires in disciplined lines, black-and-silver livery catching the early light. Inquisitorial colors. Not the garrison troops who occasionally blundered up from the lowlands and got lost, not the customs men who could be bought with a bottle and a wink. These were the Crown's own hounds, and they had come up the pass in winter, which meant they had come for something specific.

Corvin crouched in the shadow of a granite outcrop and did what he had done his whole life: he counted.

Twelve soldiers visible. Two officers on horseback, one of them wearing the pale grey cloak of a ranking inquisitor. A supply train of six mules—which meant they intended to stay. And there, at the edge of the burning camps, a knot of prisoners kneeling in the frost, maybe eight or nine figures, heads bowed while an officer walked the line of them slowly, speaking. Corvin couldn't hear the words at this distance, but he knew the posture of interrogation when he saw it. He'd worn both sides of it.

*They're asking about the gate.*

The thought arrived cold and certain, the way the truest thoughts always did. He'd known since the letter came—a scrap of paper passed hand to hand through six pairs of smuggler palms until it reached him in a tavern in Marrow's Ford, unsigned, written in a cramped and careful script that seemed to press too hard into the page, as though the writer feared the words would escape if written lightly:

*The seal is thinning. The keeper does not know. Send someone she will listen to. Tell them: the date is close.*

*She does not know.* He'd laughed at that, alone in his rented room, laughing until it turned into something else. The keeper of the Hollow Gate didn't know what she kept. A century of kings and councils and treaties had conspired to erase a country from every map and memory, and the woman standing watch over the last door of it thought she was guarding a mountain pass against bandits.

He'd come anyway. Of course he'd come. Not for the letter-writer, whoever they were, trapped behind a seal most people couldn't even imagine. Not entirely for the coin the letter promised, though Corvin Hale had never yet refused coin and wasn't going to start a habit of it now. He'd come because the letter had said *she does not know*, and some stubborn splinter in him had lodged there and wouldn't work loose.

Now he watched the inquisitors burn the valley, and the splinter twisted.

* * *

By midmorning he had worked his way down off the ridge along routes no soldier would find—the goat paths, the drainage cuts, the seam of broken ground where the mountain had shrugged off its own skin centuries ago and left a corridor of rubble only smugglers bothered to memorize. He moved the way he'd learned to move as a boy in the river towns: never in a straight line, never twice over the same stone, always leaving the eye nothing to hold.

The prisoners were being marched down-valley toward the fort road. He saw them pass below him, single file, hands bound. One of them—an old woman with a tanner's forearms—turned her head at the bend and looked back up at her burning camp for one long moment before the soldier behind her shoved her onward. Corvin pressed himself flat against the rock and made himself watch. It felt like the least he owed her, watching. He'd drunk at her fire two winters past. She'd called him *little wren* because he ate fast and quiet.

By noon the camps were ash and the soldiers were establishing themselves in the valley mouth, pitching proper tents, stacking timber for a watchtower. They weren't passing through. They were settling in like a disease settling into a lung.

And that was when Corvin understood how bad the situation truly was—not because of the camps, not even because of the prisoners, but because of geography. Because of where the inquisitors had chosen to dig in.

The valley mouth sat below the eastern approaches to the Hollow Gate. Any traffic to the gate—any supply run, any visit, any solitary figure carrying firewood home along the high path—would have to pass within sight of the new watchtower. And the keeper lived *there*. On the mountain. Within walking distance of the thing the Crown's hounds had climbed through winter passes to find.

Did she know they were coming? Had she seen the smoke? Could she see it from wherever she kept herself, up in that stony silence?

He realized, with a lurch, that he didn't know where she lived. Three days he'd been in Thornfell, following the letter's directions to the gate itself, and he had never actually laid eyes on the keeper. He'd found her footprints instead—small, deliberate prints in old snow, spaced with the evenness of someone who had walked these paths ten thousand times. He'd found a cairn she maintained, stones stacked with a precision that bordered on tenderness. He'd found a shrine niche cut into the rock above the pass, holding a candle stub and a sprig of rowan gone brown with age, refreshed often enough that the wax layers were thick as tree rings.

Someone lived up here alone and kept the paths like a garden. Someone had spent her whole life becoming invisible among these mountains—and now the loudest, hungriest institution in the kingdom had arrived at her front door asking questions about her life's work.

*I have to warn her,* he thought, and then immediately: *she'll put a knife in me.* Both thoughts were equally true, and neither changed anything.

* * *

He found her at dusk, and it nearly went badly, exactly as predicted.

She came down the high path with a bundle of kindling on her back and a hatchet at her belt, moving with that same metronome evenness he'd read in her footprints. Corvin had planned to announce himself from a distance, hands wide, voice friendly. What actually happened was that he stepped out from behind a boulder, said "Evening, Keeper," and found a hatchet's blade resting against his throat before he'd finished the second word.

She was smaller than he'd expected. Younger, too—or she looked younger, in the way of people whose faces hadn't learned to perform for anyone. Grey eyes, wind-chapped cheeks, hair the color of dry grass tied back with a strip of leather. She held the hatchet the way a farmer holds a tool rather than the way a fighter holds a weapon, but her grip didn't shake, and her eyes went over him with a thoroughness that catalogued everything: his boots, his knife, the smuggler's wax seal cord at his wrist, the lie of his pack.

"You're the one leaving tracks on my western path," she said. Her voice was flat and unhurried, the voice of someone stating weather.

"Guilty."

"You walk heel-first. Loud." The blade pressed a fraction closer. "Why are you on my mountain?"

*My mountain.* As if the mountain were hers, or she were its—as if the distinction mattered to either of them. Corvin kept his hands open at his sides and chose honesty, mostly because every lie he considered, he could hear her seeing through.

"There are inquisitors in the valley," he said. "Crown hounds. Twelve soldiers, two officers, one of them ranked. They burned three camps this morning and took prisoners down the fort road. They've pitched for winter at the valley mouth, and their watchtower will look straight onto your gate path." He paused. "I came to tell you before they told you themselves."

Something flickered across her face—quick, quickly mastered. Not fear exactly. Recalculation. He watched her weigh his words against the smoke she might or might not have seen, the ash drifting on the wind, whatever instincts a lifetime of solitude had honed in her.

"I saw the smoke," she admitted finally. The hatchet lowered an inch. "I assumed bandits fighting bandits."

"Inquisitors don't fight bandits. They harvest them." He rubbed his throat where the blade had kissed it. "They're asking questions, Keeper. About the Hollow Gate. Which means somebody important has remembered it exists, and that is never good news for anyone involved."

"Why?" The word came out sharp. "It's a sealed pass. There's nothing behind it but rock and old snow. I keep it clear, I keep the boundary marks, I keep people from breaking their necks on the approaches. That's all it is."

And there it was. Corvin looked at her—at this woman with frost in her lashes and a lifetime of the mountains in her stance—and felt the splinter in him twist again, hard enough to hurt. She believed it. That was the terrible thing. She believed it completely, the way you believe the floor beneath your feet. Somewhere in a crumbling archive or a dead woman's instructions, her order had taught her a story about a sealed pass, and she had built her entire existence inside that story without ever once being told what the walls were for.

She doesn't understand what she's guarding.

"They sent twelve soldiers and a ranked inquisitor to a pass with nothing behind it but rock and old snow," he said carefully. "Does that seem proportionate to you?"

Her jaw tightened. He watched her want to argue and watched her fail to find the argument. Above them the first stars were pricking through, and somewhere far down the valley, faint on the wind, came the sound of hammering—inquisitors building their tower, nail by nail, a stake driven into the mountain's flank.

"Who are you?" she asked. "Truly."

"Corvin Hale. Smuggler, guide, occasional liar." He gave her the grin that usually disarmed people and saw it bounce off her like rain off slate. "Sent by someone who wants you to know the truth about your gate before the inquisitors decide it for you."

"What truth?"

He hesitated. Three days he'd carried the letter's message, rehearsing ways to say it gently, and now, face to face with her hatchet and her flat grey eyes, every gentle version died in his throat. Because he could see it already—see how the truth would land on her like a rockfall, burying the small quiet life she'd built word by careful word.

"The truth that the gate isn't keeping anyone out," he said quietly. "It's keeping something in. And whatever it is, it's waking up."

The wind moved through the pines above them, long and low, almost like breath. Ansel Voss—for he knew her name now; the letter had given it, and the way she carried herself confirmed it—stood very still, kindling bundled on her back, hatchet hanging forgotten at her side, looking at him the way people look at a door they never knew was in the wall.

"That's a lie," she said at last. But her voice had changed. It had cracked open, just slightly, the way ice cracks before it gives.

"Maybe," Corvin said. "But ask yourself why the Crown is afraid of a door it swears leads nowhere. And then ask yourself why nobody ever told *you*." He reached slowly into his coat—she tensed, hatchet rising—and drew out the token: a small disc of greenish metal, stamped with a crown broken in half, warm from riding against his chest. He set it on the flat stone between them and stepped back.

"When you're ready to know more, light a fire on the high shelf above the gate. I'll see it from the ridge." He turned and melted back toward the shadows, and just before the dark took him, he heard her voice behind him, barely more than breath, aimed at no one:

*"What am I guarding?"*

Corvin climbed the ridge in the dark with the inquisitors' torches glittering in the valley below like a scatter of fallen stars, and he found that his hands were shaking—not from the cold. He'd come to deliver a warning to a stranger. Now he understood it was worse than that. The hounds were at her door, the seal was failing, the date in that buried book was closing like a trap—and the one person alive who could stand at that gate and choose what happened next didn't even know there was a choice to make.

He looked back once. High on the black shoulder of the mountain, a single point of light bloomed: she had lit her fire after all.

Or perhaps, he thought, climbing faster, she had simply lit the first candle of a very long night—and somewhere beneath the snow, something ancient had felt the flame and begun, at last, to answer.
