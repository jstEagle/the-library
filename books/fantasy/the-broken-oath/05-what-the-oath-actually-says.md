## 5. What the Oath Actually Says

The archive of the Order of the Hollow Gate sat three days' walk from the pass itself, down in the green throat of a valley where the Thornfell mountains softened into foothills and the air forgot how to bite. Ansel had made the journey twice in her life: once at seven years old, when her mother had carried her through the snow to be sworn, and once at nineteen, when she had come to replace a rotted door hinge and stayed only long enough to do it.

She had never wanted to come back.

The building announced itself before she saw it—a smell of wet stone and older paper riding the wind up the valley path, like something breathing under a blanket. Then the trees opened and there it was: a long, low hall of grey granite sunk half into the hillside as though the mountain were slowly swallowing it and could not be bothered to finish. Moss climbed the eastern wall in a green tide. The slate roof sagged in the middle where a beam had given up decades ago. Ivy had taken the western face entirely, so that in summer the archive looked less like a building than a grave with windows.

Ansel stood at the edge of the tree line for a long moment, her pack heavy on her shoulders, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat.

*Come back when you understand what you've read,* Corvin Hale had said—or had not said; he had simply looked at her with those quick dark eyes while the fire spat between them, and the look had said it for him. *You keep a gate you've never questioned. That's not keeping. That's sleepwalking.*

She had turned him away. She had kept his token—the small iron key on a leather cord, worn smooth by someone else's hand—and told herself it was evidence, nothing more.

Three days later, here she was.

The doors were oak, banded with iron gone orange with rust, and they opened at her touch with a groan that seemed to come from the foundations themselves. Inside, the great reading room stretched away into shadow, its vaulted ceiling lost in gloom above banks of shelves that leaned against one another like tired old men. Dust hung in the shafts of light falling from high, filthy windows—not drifting, just hanging, as if even the dust here had sworn an oath of stillness.

"Hello?" Her voice came back flat and wrong, swallowed by the room.

No answer. There would be no answer. The order had never been large—her mother had told her that much—but it had once held dozens: keepers, scribes, wardens who walked the passes. Now the rolls named four living members, scattered across the mountains like coins dropped in snow, and Ansel suspected she was the only one who still performed her duties in full. The others sent their yearly attestations by courier and otherwise let the silence lie.

She lit one of the lamps near the entrance—it caught reluctantly, the oil old and thick—and began to walk.

Her boots left prints in the dust of the central aisle, and she found herself pausing to look at them. In the Hollow Gate's corridors, her feet left no mark at all. That was part of the oath's working: *unseen, unmarked, unremembered.* She had walked past travelers on the pass a hundred times and watched their eyes slide over her like water over oiled stone. She had stood in the doorway of a smugglers' inn while men planned robbery three feet from her face, and none of them had ever known a keeper stood among them.

It should have felt like power. Mostly it felt like standing behind glass.

The founding documents were kept in the eastern alcove, in a cabinet of blackened walnut behind a door whose lock had seized sometime before Ansel was born. She did not bother with the lock. She put her shoulder to the wood and the whole thing gave with a crack that echoed through the hall like a shot, and she stood wincing in the ringing quiet, waiting for some warden to materialize and scold her.

Nothing came. Only the dust, hanging in its shafts of light.

Inside the cabinet, the founding charter lay in a lead-lined box, exactly where tradition said it would be. Ansel lifted it out with both hands. It was heavier than she remembered—or perhaps she had never actually held it. At her swearing, the charter had been read aloud over her by the last warden of the valley, an ancient woman named Odile Voss, no relation, whose voice had cracked like river ice and whose eyes had never once met hers.

Ansel carried the box to the great reading table beneath the window, set it down, and unwrapped the oilcloth from the book inside.

The charter was bound in white leather gone the color of old teeth. Its clasps were silver, tarnished black. She opened it, and the smell rose up to meet her—dust and tallow and something faintly sweet, like flowers pressed a century too long.

The first page bore the oath.

She knew this page. Every keeper knew this page, or thought they did. She had heard it spoken at her swearing, had repeated it word for word with her small cold hand on the cover:

*I swear to stand watch at the Hollow Gate. I swear to pass unseen, to speak unheard, to be unremembered by all who cross my path. I swear fidelity to the seal and silence to the world, until the mountain releases me or the mountain falls.*

That was the oath. That was all of it. She had carried those words for twenty years like stones sewn into her coat.

But now, in the grey light of the archive, with the lamp turned up and her eyes grown sharp from years of reading weathered trail-signs and faded boundary markers, Ansel looked at the page properly for the first time in her life—and saw that the oath did not end there.

The text continued. It continued for two more pages.

Her mouth went dry. She turned the leaf with fingers that had begun, very slightly, to shake.

*And I swear to hold fast the inner door,* the next passage read, in the same formal hand, the ink browned but perfectly legible, *that which was shut in the Year of the Falling Crown, that nothing which waits beyond it may come forth into the world of the living. I swear that the Gate is not a wall but a lid. I swear that what lies beneath the mountain is not banished but contained. I swear to know this always, and to speak of it to no one, and to die knowing it, as every keeper before me has died knowing it.*

Ansel sat down. The chair scraped; the sound went off into the dark of the hall and did not return.

*A lid.* Not a wall. Not a border-post against smugglers and refugees and the weather. A lid, on something that waited.

She read the passage again. Then again, a fourth time, hunting for some other meaning, some metaphorical softness in the words that would let her fold them up and put them away. There was none. The language was plain as a hammer. The gate kept something *in*. Whatever the seal was failing to hold—the singing she had heard under the ice, the stillness in the stone, the date written in a dead man's book—all of it was on the inside, pressing outward, and had been for a hundred years.

And no one had told her.

She laughed, once, and the laugh came out broken. Of course no one had told her. That was in the oath too, wasn't it? *To speak of it to no one.* The order had not merely hidden the truth from the world; it had hidden it from its own keepers, generation after generation, handing each of them half an oath and calling it whole. Her mother had died without knowing. Odile Voss had died without telling. Ansel had spent her entire adult life guarding a prison while believing herself a shepherd of an empty pass.

She thought of the smuggler tracks in the snow, and felt her cheeks heat with shame—not for missing them, but for the pride she'd taken in the pass's emptiness. *My gate keeps nothing out because there is nothing to keep out.* She had said those words to herself a thousand times, comfortably, the way a person pats a wall in the dark. The wall had never been a wall.

Outside, wind moved through the valley, and somewhere far off a branch cracked under its weight. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary world. But everything ordinary had changed shape since she'd opened the book in the gate's foundation, and now it changed again, tilting, refusing to sit still.

She forced herself to keep reading.

The remainder of the charter was administrative—rotations, provisions, the rite of succession, formulas for the renewal of the invisibility-working at each new moon. Useful, once. Dead letter now. Ansel skimmed it with the mechanical attention of someone checking a rope bridge they already intended never to cross again.

It was the marginalia that stopped her.

The charter's final pages were annotated in a different hand—smaller, sharper, slanting hard to the right as if the writer had been in a hurry or leaning into a wind. The ink was older than the rest, or perhaps younger; she couldn't tell. The notes crept up the margins, crowded into the gutters, spilled onto the flyleaves. Whoever had written them had not been able to leave the text alone.

The name signed at the bottom of the last page was *Ysolde Marran, First Keeper.*

Ansel went very still.

Every keeper learned the name Ysolde Marran the way children learned the names of saints. The first keeper, the founder of the order, the woman who had supposedly sealed the Hollow Gate with her own hands in the country's last terrible year. There was a statue of her—or had been—in the archive's entry hall, though ivy had eaten it so thoroughly that Ansel had walked past it an hour ago without recognizing more than a shape. Ysolde the steadfast. Ysolde the faithful. The exemplar every keeper was raised on, the standard against which all their small failures were measured.

Her marginalia read like a different woman entirely.

Beside the passage about the inner door, in that sharp slanting hand: *We did not seal a horror. We sealed a people. Write it plainly, whoever reads this, though they burn me for it: the door out of a country that no longer exists does not open onto hell. It opens onto Veyland.*

Ansel read the words four times. They did not change.

*Veyland.*

The name meant nothing and everything. It was one of those words that lived at the edges of things—nursemaids' rhymes about a kingdom under the hill, a curse her father used to mutter over bad luck, *Veyland take it*, without either of them knowing why. Maps did not show it. Histories did not mention it. Somewhere in the deep past of the mountains, a country had been erased so completely that even its erasure had been erased, leaving only nursery nonsense and a keeper's oath as fossils.

*The door out of a country that no longer exists.*

She turned the pages with exaggerated care, as if the charter might flinch. More marginalia followed, dense and urgent:

*They called it mercy, what we did. The treaty-men and the crown-men and the college-mages, all wringing their hands and speaking of peace. Peace. We took a nation of living souls and folded them out of the world like a letter no one wished to answer. And then we built an order of blind women to sit on the fold.*

And below that, smaller, the ink thinner, as if written late at night by a weakening hand:

*The seal was never meant to last forever. Nothing lasts forever. I built the gate to buy time, not to bury them eternally. If you are reading this, keeper, know that the day will come when the working thins. On that day you will have a choice, and I am sorry—I am so sorry—that I could not make it for you. I only ask that you remember they are people. Whatever comes through the door, remember it first.*

Ansel closed the charter.

For a long moment she simply sat in the dusty light, listening to her own heartbeat, feeling the architecture of her life rearrange itself around her with a soundless, terrible click.

Everything she was—the invisibility, the solitude, the twenty years of unmarked footprints and unseen vigils, the contentment she had nursed like a coal in cupped hands—all of it rested on an oath she had never fully read, sworn to a purpose she had never been told, authored by a woman the order had turned into a saint precisely so that no one would read what she'd actually written.

Betrayal was too clean a word. Betrayal implied an enemy. This was worse: it was inheritance. The lie had been handed to her swaddled in duty, warmed by her mother's love, blessed by Odile Voss's cracking voice, and she had carried it gratefully, proudly, all her life. You could guard yourself against a thief. No one taught you to guard against your own cradle.

And yet—

She opened the charter again, to the last page, and read Ysolde Marran's final note once more. *Remember they are people.*

The old keeper had not asked her to maintain the seal. Had not asked her to destroy it. She had asked her to *remember*, and to choose, and had apologized for leaving the choosing to her. Of all the cruelties buried in this book, that was the one that undid Ansel: the apology. A century dead, and Ysolde Marran was sorry.

Ansel pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark.

When she lowered her hand, the light through the window had gone amber. Hours had passed. The dust still hung in the shafts, patient as ever, and the great hall held its silence around her like a held breath.

She repacked the charter in its lead box, but she did not return it to the cabinet. She wrapped it in oilcloth alongside the prophecy book, and the two volumes rode together against her chest, one naming the day, the other explaining it, and both of them heavier than paper had any right to be.

At the door she paused and looked back at the reading room—at the leaning shelves, the hanging dust, the ivy-eaten statue of a woman who had tried, in cramped and desperate ink, to tell the truth to anyone brave enough to open a locked door.

"You could have shouted," Ansel said quietly, to Ysolde, to the order, to all of them. "One shout. A hundred years of shouting."

The archive said nothing. It was very good at saying nothing. It had been practicing for a century.

She stepped outside into the cold, and the doors boomed shut behind her, and she stood a moment on the mossy steps breathing the honest wind, trying to decide what a keeper did with a truth like this. Go back to the gate. Stand her watch. Pretend the words weren't burning a hole through her.

Or find someone who could read the rest—who could tell her what *Veyland* had been, and what waited behind the inner door, and whether the smuggler's iron key fit anything in this story besides trouble.

Corvin Hale had known her name. He had known things about the gate no outsider should. He had claimed, with that crooked half-smile, to have been sent by someone inside.

Ansel tightened her pack straps, turned her back on the archive, and started down the valley toward the lowlands—toward wine cellars and fugitive scholars and every road she had sworn never to walk.

Somewhere behind her, in the drowned dark beneath the Hollow Gate, something that had waited a hundred years went on waiting. But not, she understood now, patiently.

Not patiently at all.
