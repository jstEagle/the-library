## 7. Burning the Archive

Maren Quill had spent thirty years learning that knowledge did not burn easily—but paper did.

She thought of this absurdity often, and she was thinking of it now, at three o'clock in the morning, standing in a wine cellar beneath the Hollow Barrel tavern with a candle in one hand and the collected marginalia of a dead court historian in the other. The cellar smelled of oak tannin and mouse droppings and old ink, and it held—by her last honest count—eleven thousand pages of scholarship on the fall of Veyland. Every scrap of it contraband. Every scrap of it hers.

"Read it again," Ansel said behind her.

The keeper had been in her cellar for six hours now, and Maren still hadn't decided what to make of her. The woman moved like someone accustomed to being unseen: no wasted motion, no sound where sound could be avoided, a habit of standing near walls rather than in front of them. She had introduced herself with only her first name, as if her surname were something she'd misplaced decades ago and hadn't missed.

Maren turned back to the prophecy book, its vellum soft as moth wing under her fingers. "The final page. Tonight's date." She cleared her throat and read aloud, translating from the Old Court script as she went, the way she'd done a hundred times since the keeper had appeared at her door with a book that should not exist:

*"When the thirteenth keeper counts the years and finds her own among them, the seal will thin as breath on glass. The door out of a country that no longer exists will remember itself. And the dead of Veyland—"*

She stopped.

"And the dead of Veyland," Ansel said quietly, "*what?*"

"That's where the page tears." Maren touched the ragged edge. The tear was old—the vellum had yellowed unevenly around it, which meant it had been damaged before the book was hidden, before the gate's foundations swallowed it. Someone had torn the ending out deliberately. "Someone didn't want the last line read."

"Convenient."

"Or merciful." Maren set the book down on the reading table she'd built from two wine crates and a slab of slate. Around them, the cellar rose in shelves: ledgers disguised as shipping manifests, treatises bound in false covers labeled *Agronomy of the Southern Vale* and *On the Keeping of Bees*. A century of scholars who had refused to let a country be erased, each one hiding their work inside the skin of something dull enough to survive. She had inherited the collection from her teacher, Aldous Wren, who had inherited it from his, all the way back to the treaty that unmade Veyland—and she had added eleven years of her own before the court stripped her title, her post, and very nearly her freedom.

"The dead of Veyland," Ansel said again. "You keep using that word. Dead."

"Because that's what the histories say. The kingdom fell, the people died or scattered, the victors wrote it down and salted the memory with magic so thorough that maps made after the treaty show open grassland where Thornfell's western peaks should stand." Maren pulled off her spectacles and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I've spent my career documenting an erasure, Keeper. I know how it works. First you kill the people. Then you kill the proof they lived. The second part is easier. It's just paperwork."

"You don't believe they're dead."

It wasn't a question, and Maren resented, briefly, how quickly this stranger had found the soft place in her scholarship. She put her spectacles back on and looked at the prophecy book instead of at the woman. "I believe the seal your order guards was never meant to hold anything *out*. I believe the founding oath is a lie of grammar—a lie told by putting the wrong verb in the right sentence. And I believe that whatever is behind the Hollow Gate has been waiting a hundred years for tonight, and I would very much like to know what, but the person who tore out this page took that answer with them."

* * *

They worked until the candle guttered low, cross-referencing the prophecy against Maren's own translations of the treaty documents. It was good work. It was the first good work she'd done in three years, since the inquisitors had emptied her chambers at the court and she'd fled south with nothing but her notes sewn into the lining of a coat. Ansel had a scholar's patience hiding under all that mountain silence—she read slowly, but she read *closely*, catching contradictions Maren had glossed over, asking the blunt questions that trained academics had learned to be embarrassed by.

It was Ansel who noticed the third thing in the wall.

Maren had stepped up to the shelf for the Wren codex, and the keeper had gone still—that particular stillness Maren was beginning to recognize as the woman's version of shouting. "This stone's been moved," Ansel said. Her fingers traced the cellar wall where the shelving met the foundation. "Recently. Within the year. See the dust line?"

"I built those shelves myself. There's no stone behind them, there's the—"

But Ansel was already pulling the shelf away from the wall, silently, muscles that looked like they belonged to someone twice her size moving a hundredweight of oak and books like a kitchen chair. Behind it: a section of foundation stone sitting proud of its neighbors by the width of a knife blade, mortar crumbled around its edges.

"It's a niche," Maren breathed. "Like the one you found at the gate."

Ansel worked the stone loose with a knife point. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a thin volume—not vellum but paper, cheap paper, the kind used for letters and confessions. Maren unwrapped it with hands that were not quite steady.

The handwriting was cramped, urgent, slanting downhill across the page like a man writing while walking. The date at the top was ninety-nine years old.

"It's a letter," she said. "Addressed to... 'the keeper who finds this.'" She began to translate, and the cellar seemed to grow colder with every line. "'I was the eleventh keeper of the Hollow Gate. I write this because my successor has been given the shortened oath, as I was, as we all are now. The full text survives in only three places that I know of. If you have found this, you have already begun to suspect. So I will tell you plainly what my teacher never told me: the gate does not protect the world from Veyland. The gate protects the world from *knowing* what was done to Veyland.'"

Maren read the rest in silence, her lips moving, her chest tightening. The letter spoke of the last days of the war—of a kingdom that had surrendered, not fallen; of a treaty signed not in defeat but in terror, because Veyland's rulers had possessed something the victors wanted and could not take by force; of a sealing worked not upon a gate but upon a *country*, folding its roads and valleys out of the world like a letter folded shut. Of the people still inside when it happened.

"How many?" Ansel asked, when Maren finally lowered the pages.

"The letter doesn't give a number. It says—" Maren's voice caught, and she hated that, hated the tremor in it, and pushed through anyway. "It says the census records of Veyland listed four hundred thousand souls in the year before the treaty. And it says the sealing was worked at night, without warning, and that the signatories agreed to record the whole nation as casualties of the war. Four hundred thousand people, Keeper. Not killed. *Filed.*"

The candle flame stood perfectly still between them. Somewhere above, through the floorboards, the tavern's last drunk was singing something tuneless and far away.

"My order swore to guard this," Ansel said at last. Each word came out flat and separate, like stones laid one by one. "Thirteen generations of keepers. We fast on the anniversary of the sealing. We call it the Day of Ashes. We mourn the *dead*." She laughed once, and it was the worst sound Maren had heard in years. "We've been holding a funeral for a prison."

* * *

It was the smell that saved them—or rather, the absence of it.

Maren had lived above and below the Hollow Barrel for three years, and she knew its smells the way Ansel knew her mountain paths: the sour-sweet breath of the casks, the tallow, the wet stone. So when, near dawn, the cellar air went thin and clean in a way it never did, she lifted her head from the letter like a deer scenting smoke.

"What is it?" Ansel whispered.

"Someone's opened the trapdoor." Maren pinched out the candle. In the sudden dark, her heart was a hammer. "Nobody opens it before six. Tam the barman sleeps with the key under his tongue, near enough."

Boots on the stairs. More than one pair. The particular cadence of men moving in formation, which Maren had heard once before, three years ago, through the doors of her own chambers at court.

"Inquisitors," she breathed. "How—"

"The smuggler," Ansel said, and there was something raw in it. "Corvin Hale. They followed him. Or he led them." A pause. "Doesn't matter which. Can you carry armloads?"

"Yes."

"Then choose fast. Three books. No more."

Three books. Out of eleven thousand pages. Out of a century of dead scholars who had hidden their life's work inside beekeeping manuals so that someday, someone, might know the truth. Maren's hands hovered over the shelves and shook, and for one terrible moment she could not move at all—could not choose, could not abandon any single spine to the flames she could already smell starting somewhere above them, hot resin and pitch, the inquisitors' preferred accelerant, she remembered that too—

"Maren." Ansel's hand closed on her shoulder, steady as bedrock. "Choose like a scholar. What can't be rewritten?"

That unlocked her. Maren moved.

The prophecy book, of course—the primary source, irreplaceable. The Wren codex, containing the only surviving transcription of the full founding oath. And then, with a sob she didn't bother to swallow, the slim, hand-bound volume of her *own* eleven years—her translations, her reconstructions of Veyland's geography, her maps of a country that maps had forgotten. Not because it was worth more than the others. Because it was hers, and she was allowed, just this once, to be small about it.

She stuffed the three volumes into a satchel. Above them, floorboards groaned under marching feet, and a voice—female, cold, carrying—said something Maren couldn't make out, followed by the crash of furniture.

"The wine racks," Ansel said. "Is there another way out?"

"There's the coal chute, but it comes up in the yard—"

"Where?"

"Behind the tavern, but they'll have the street—"

"They'll have the front. Nobody watches a coal chute." Ansel had already found it, wedged in the corner behind the last rack, a wooden hatch furred with cobweb. She wrenched it open and black night air poured in, tasting of frost. "After me. Fast and quiet. Don't look back at the cellar."

"I built that cellar," Maren said, and her voice cracked on it. "Thirty years, Keeper. My teacher's notes. His teacher's. Everything anyone ever risked to remember—"

"It'll burn in four minutes and grieving takes longer, so we'll do it walking." Ansel's voice was gentle and iron at once. "In. Satchel first. I'll catch you."

Maren went. The chute scraped her shoulders; the frost bit her palms; Ansel's grip on her wrist was the only solid thing in the world. They came out in the yard behind the Hollow Barrel, beneath a sky going grey at the edges, and the windows of the tavern above them had begun to glow orange from within.

* * *

They ran.

Not far—Ansel knew these streets too, apparently, or at least knew how to move through them unseen, hauling Maren down a drainage alley, across a frozen garden, along the backs of houses where laundry lines hung like bunting for a funeral. Twice they stopped in doorways while patrols passed close enough to hear the creak of leather armor. Once, from a rooftop two streets over, Maren saw the inquisitors' lanterns swarming the Hollow Barrel like hornets, and saw figures carrying armfuls of books out to a bonfire in the square—not to save them. To sort them. To decide what burned publicly and what vanished into the inquisition's own vaults, which was, in its way, a worse fate.

She watched a century of scholarship go up in sparks and could not look away.

"There," she said numbly, as a gout of embers rose over the rooftops. "That's Aldous Wren's translation of the Treaty of Severance. He copied it out by hand three times and hid all three copies and they're burning one right now and I don't even know if the others survived him." Her breath fogged and tore away. "Eleven thousand pages, Keeper. Do you understand? I catalogued every one. I know them like faces. And some clerk with a list is deciding which ones count as heresy and which ones count as fuel."

Ansel said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, and it was the voice of someone talking to herself as much as to Maren.

"I used to think my invisibility was a discipline," she said. "The oath of the unseen keeper. Walk softly, live small, let no one learn your face or your name. I thought it was devotion." She watched the firelight flicker on the clouds. "Tonight I learned what my order actually was. Thirteen generations of jailers pretending to be priests, and the vow of silence wasn't humility—it was a muzzle. They made us invisible so we'd never see clearly enough to ask questions."

"And now you have."

"Now I have." Ansel turned to look at her, and in the ember-light her face was transformed—not the quiet, self-contained keeper from the cellar anymore, but something harder and more awake. "There's no going back, is there. Even if I walked home tonight and swept my hearth and pretended the book was never found—they'd find me. Your inquisitors follow threads. Corvin Hale knows my name. You know my name. The gate's foundations are broken open for anyone to see." A pause. "I spent thirty years being no one. I think I just stopped."

Maren hugged the satchel to her chest, feeling the spines of the three survivors through the canvas—the prophecy, the oath, her own maps. Three books against a burning library. It should have felt like defeat. Instead, standing in the freezing dark with ash beginning to sift down like dirty snow, she felt something coalesce in her that had been scattered for three years: purpose, drawn tight.

"They didn't get everything," she said. "Do you understand what we're holding? The full oath. The prophecy. And my geographic work—which means that if there is a true door into Veyland, a way in that isn't your Hollow Gate, then between the three of us and these three books, we may be the only people alive who can find it."

"The door out of a country that no longer exists," Ansel murmured. "The letter called it that."

"Then we'd better hope the country remembers where it put it." Maren pulled her coat tighter and glanced east, where the sky was lightening over the black teeth of Thornfell. "Because the inquisitors will come for you next, Keeper, and they won't stop at your gate. They'll want the foundations collapsed and the pass sealed and every witness buried under the rubble. Which means we need to move tonight. Tonight, or—"

She stopped.

Down the lane, unhurried, a figure had detached itself from the shadow of a hay barn and was walking toward them with the loose-hipped swagger of a man entirely at home in places he shouldn't be. Frost silvered his stubble. One hand was raised in a gesture that was half greeting, half surrender.

"Well," said Corvin Hale, "you both look like you could use a smuggler."

Ansel's hand went to her knife.

Maren, watching the man's face—watching the guilt and the desperation tangled together in it like rope—had the sudden, sinking certainty that whatever story he was about to tell them, the fire behind them was only the first thing they would lose.
