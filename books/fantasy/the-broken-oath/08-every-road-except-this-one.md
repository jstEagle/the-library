## 8. Every Road Except This One

The smoke was visible from three ridges away.

Ansel stood at the crest of the Wolf's Tooth with the wind pulling her hair sideways and watched it rise from the valley where she had lived for eleven years—a thin gray thread unwinding into a sky that had never once, in all that time, held anything worth watching. Her cabin. Her archive of maps and ledgers. The little stone hut where she kept her mother's kettle and her father's whetstone and every small object that made a life recognizable as one's own.

Burning. All of it burning.

"They work fast," Corvin said behind her. He said it without apology, which she was beginning to understand was simply how he said everything.

Maren had both hands pressed over her mouth. She had lost her cellar last night—her books, her twenty years of scholarship, the marginalia of dead men—and now Ansel was losing the only home she'd ever chosen. The symmetry of it felt almost deliberate, as though some author were being cruelly tidy.

"We should keep moving," Ansel said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

She turned her back on the smoke. That was the hardest part—not the leaving, but the turning, the physical act of rotating her body away from everything warm she owned. She had spent her whole life learning to be unseen; she had never learned to be unmoored.

"Which way?" Maren asked, hugging the three salvaged books against her chest like children.

Ansel looked at the mountains. And for the first time since she'd taken the oath at seventeen, the question didn't frighten her. Because this—the paths, the passes, the secret veins of Thornfell—this was the one thing they could not burn.

"There are forty-one ways through these mountains," she said. "The king's maps show six. The smugglers know maybe fifteen. I'm going to show you the other twenty-six."

* * *

They walked the rest of that day along a road that did not exist.

That was not poetry. Ansel had checked, years ago, out of a keeper's thoroughness: the surveyors' maps in the royal archive marked this entire slope as impassable scree, a wall of broken stone too steep for goats. The surveyors had come up from the south in high summer, seen the scree, and turned back. They had never climbed another two hundred paces to find what lay above it—a shelf of solid granite, wide as a cart road, running the spine of the mountain like a hidden sentence between two lines of text.

The smugglers called it the Ledger. Ansel had never asked why. She suspected Corvin knew.

"You've walked this alone," he said, falling into step beside her. It wasn't a question. He had a way of making observations sound like accusations, or maybe the reverse.

"Eleven years."

"And you never thought to tell anyone? Sell the route? A road like this is worth more than gold in certain markets."

"It's not mine to sell." She stepped over a gap where frost had split the granite, testing the far edge with her heel before trusting it. "My order keeps the gate. That includes keeping the ways to it. If I sold one road, I'd have to watch every traveler who used it, forever, wondering which one would bring fire to my door." She glanced back at the smoke, already fading behind the shoulder of the ridge. "Seems I brought the fire myself."

Corvin was quiet for a few steps. Then: "You didn't light it."

"No. But I'm the reason it's lit." She kept walking. "There's a difference, but it's a thin one, and thin things break under weight."

Maren, trailing behind with her books and her bruised dignity, called up: "For what it's worth, the inquisitors would have found the gate eventually regardless. Serane Doss has been hunting Veyland references for nine years. You didn't cause this, Keeper. You just happened to be standing where the lightning was already aimed."

Ansel didn't answer. But she noticed, with a small private surprise, that the words loosened something in her chest anyway.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of rock that smugglers called the Priest's Hat, because from the valley below it looked like a bishop's mitre tilted in prayer. Ansel built the fire small and screened it with a curved sheet of slate she'd cached there years ago—one of nineteen such caches strung through the mountains, each holding dry wood, oilskin, a clay jug of water. She had prepared, all her solitary life, for companions who never came. Now the preparation finally paid its dividend, and the irony of it sat in her throat like a stone.

Maren slept first, curled around her books, her breath going long and even within minutes. A woman who could sleep anywhere after a lifetime of hiding—or perhaps because of it. Ansel watched her and felt an unfamiliar ache, half tenderness and half grief.

Corvin sat across the fire, cleaning his boots with a knife.

"You knew about the caches," Ansel said quietly.

"I guessed. Smugglers always cache. Yours were better than most." He didn't look up. "You want to ask me something. You've wanted to ask it since the night at your fire. Ask."

She fed a twig to the flames. "Who sent you? Really. Not the story you told me at the gate—the true one."

"The true one is mostly the same story with worse lighting." He set down the knife. "I was born in Lowmark, on the coast. My grandmother came from somewhere she never named. She sang songs in a language nobody around us spoke. When I was a boy she told me our family came from a country that fell off the map, and I thought it was a story old women tell. Then I started running cargo through Thornfell, and I heard the stories the mountain people tell instead—about the Hollow Gate, about the keeper who walks like a ghost. And I started asking questions I shouldn't have." He shrugged. "Someone inside answered."

"Inside." Ansel let the word sit. "You mean beyond the seal."

"I mean exactly what I said."

"The country is gone, Corvin. Erased. Unmade. Maren told me—the treaty, the magic, a century ago. There is no inside."

"Then someone is singing from nowhere," Corvin said, "and they know my grandmother's name."

The fire popped. Above them, through the gap between the Priest's Hat and the mountain's shoulder, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. Ansel thought of the prophecy book with tonight's date inked in dust-dry ink a hundred years before she was born, and of the last line naming the keeper, and of how every road she'd ever walked had apparently been leading here, to a cold hollow, listening to a smuggler claim kinship with ghosts.

"Get some sleep," she said finally. "We cross the Saddle tomorrow. It's exposed for half a mile and the wind will strip the flesh off anyone careless."

"Yes, Keeper," Corvin said, and there was something in his tone—not mockery, exactly. Recognition, maybe. She decided not to examine it.

* * *

The Saddle nearly killed Maren.

It was Ansel's fault, partly—she had misjudged how a scholar would handle exposure, had assumed that anyone who'd survived the inquisitors this long would have a mountaineer's nerve. But Maren froze halfway across the knife-edge ridge, the wind screaming over the drop on either side, and simply stopped, arms locked around her books, eyes fixed on nothing.

"Maren." Ansel worked her way back along the ridge, feet finding the worn pockets in the stone without looking. "Look at me. Not down. At me."

"I can't—" Maren's voice tore away in the wind. "I can't see the path, it's—"

"You don't need to see it. I can see it. I've crossed this ridge sixty-two times, in fog, in ice, once with a broken wrist. Every footfall is in my body." Ansel took her hand. It was freezing and trembling. "Put your hand on my shoulder. Walk where I walk. Close your eyes if you have to. I will not let you fall. That is not a comfort, it is a fact, and facts hold better than comforts on a ridge."

Something shifted in Maren's face—distrust wrestling with exhaustion, and exhaustion winning. She gripped Ansel's shoulder. They crossed the remaining quarter mile that way, step by step, while the wind tried to pry them off the world, and when they reached the sheltered side and Maren collapsed onto safe stone, laughing shakily, tears freezing on her cheeks, Ansel realized her own hands were shaking too.

Not from fear. From something else. From the strange, alarming warmth of having mattered to someone. Eleven years of keeping a gate no one thanked her for, and this—this single hand on her shoulder—was the first time anyone had needed her *visibly*, had needed her and known her name while needing her.

Corvin dropped down beside them, breathing hard, grinning like a wolf. "The great invisible keeper, reduced to a walking handrail."

"Shut up, Hale."

"Yes, Keeper."

But he said it gently this time, and helped Maren up, and took the heaviest pack without being asked, and Ansel walked on ahead so neither of them would see her face do whatever it was doing.

* * *

On the fourth day, the soldiers found their trail anyway.

Not them—their trail. Ansel spotted it from a bend in the Gullet, a narrow defile where their path ran beside a stream: boot prints on the muddy bank, fresh, a patrol of six moving fast, cutting the route ahead of them. Someone was reading their passage the way she read the mountains—which meant someone had taught the inquisitors smuggler-craft, or bought it.

She backtracked silently and found the others in the lee of a boulder.

"Patrol ahead. Six. Moving toward the ford." She crouched, drew in the dirt with a stick: the defile, the stream, the fork. "If we take the low road we meet them head-on in an hour. If we climb here—" she scratched a line up the canyon wall, "—there's a chimney route. Hard climbing. Slow with the packs."

"Or," Corvin said, studying her map in the dirt, "we let them think they've caught us."

Ansel looked up sharply. "Explain."

"Smuggler's rule: a trail is a message. Right now ours says *three fugitives, heading for the ford, predictable*. So we write a different message." His finger moved in the dirt. "We leave the packs' weight in stones wrapped in oilskin at the fork, drag marks toward the low road, double back through the chimney, and let them chase a ghost down the valley while we're already two ridges north. Being known isn't the same as being caught, Keeper. Half of staying free is letting them be certain of the wrong thing."

"That's deception."

"That's Tuesday," Corvin said. "Deception is just hospitality for people you don't like."

Despite herself—despite the smoke still hanging somewhere behind her, despite the prophecy, despite everything—Ansel felt the corner of her mouth move. "My order would call that dishonorable."

"Your order also called erasing a country honorable, so forgive me if I don't consult them."

She couldn't argue with that. She stacked stones in oilskin instead, and dragged their spare cloak through the mud toward the low road, and felt, doing it, less like a traitor than she'd expected—more like a keeper of a different kind. Keeping them. Keeping herself. The word was the same; only the object had changed.

The chimney climb took two hours and cost Maren a torn sleeve and most of her composure, but by dusk they were two ridges north, watching from a height as torchlight bobbed along the valley floor, chasing stones in a cloak.

"Beautiful," Corvin murmured. "They'll report three fugitives moving south. By the time anyone checks north, we're past the Watcher's Stair." He glanced at Ansel. "You knew these tricks?"

"I knew how to be unseen. That's different. Unseen is just... absence. What you do is presence, misused." She frowned at her own words, trying to shape the thought. "I spent eleven years being nobody. You spend your life being somebody the wrong people underestimate. I used to think those were the same skill."

"And now?"

"Now I think yours is harder." She said it plainly, because it was true, and because saying true things to these two was becoming a habit she hadn't authorized. "And more useful."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment, firelight moving in his eyes, and for once had nothing clever to say.

* * *

The sixth night was cold enough that they risked a real fire, deep in a cave behind the Weeping Wall, where water seeped through the stone in a hundred silver threads and the smoke rose through a crack in the roof that had been venting smugglers' fires for a century. Maren had recovered her humor somewhere on the fifth day, somewhere between the Watcher's Stair and a meal of smoked trout Corvin caught bare-handed from a stream, and now she sat with her boots off, one of the salvaged books open on her knees, reading aloud—not scholarship, just an old poem she'd memorized as a girl, something about a shepherd counting stars instead of sheep.

Corvin listened with his head tipped back against the stone. Ansel mended a strap on her pack, badly, and listened too.

"When did you last sit at a fire with other people?" Maren asked her suddenly, mid-poem, the way scholars ask things—as though the question were merely another line of text.

Ansel considered lying. The habit of invisibility went deep. But the fire was warm and the water was singing in the walls and the lie wouldn't fit through any of it.

"Never, actually. My order takes the oath at seventeen. After that, contact with others is..." She searched for the word the oath used. "*Dilution.*"

"Dilution," Maren repeated, horrified. "Of what?"

"Purpose, I think. Possibly self." Ansel pulled the strap tight. "We were taught that a keeper who loves anything outside the gate will eventually choose it over the gate. So we were given nothing to love. It seemed reasonable at seventeen."

"And now?" Corvin again, quiet.

"Now I think it was a cage dressed as a calling." She looked into the fire. "I thought I was content. I had the paths and the weather and the work, and I told myself the silence was peace. But peace doesn't flinch when it ends. Contentment doesn't leave a hole shaped exactly like two annoying people arguing about poetry." She heard herself and stopped, ears going hot. "That came out wrong."

"It really didn't," Maren said softly.

Corvin threw another branch on the fire. "For what it's worth, Keeper, I've run with crews of twelve and crews of two, and I've slept alone more nights than I can count, and I'll tell you what I know: being known isn't the same as being caught. I spent years confusing them. Thought every name attached to me was a rope someone could pull. But a rope goes two ways." He nodded at her, at Maren, at the small circle the fire made of the three of them. "You pull too."

Ansel looked at him—at this man who had nearly gotten her killed, who had then saved her life twice, who claimed kinship with a country that didn't exist and sang none of his grandmother's songs where anyone could hear—and felt the truth of it settle into her bones like heat into stone. Being known. Not caught. Known.

She had been the keeper of nothing for eleven years. Sitting in a wet cave with a disgraced scholar and a liar-smuggler, listening to water sing through a century of secrets, she understood that she had somehow become the keeper of something else entirely, and that she had no idea how to perform the office, and that she would learn.

Maren resumed the poem. Corvin closed his eyes. Ansel finished mending the strap, badly, and left it.

* * *

Near dawn, Corvin shook her awake, one finger to his lips.

Below the cave mouth, far down in the valley, lights. Not torchlight this time—lanterns, many, moving in disciplined formation. And rising above them, catching the first gray light, a banner she recognized from the heraldry books in her archive: the crowned eye of the Royal Inquisition.

At their head rode a figure in gray, and even at this distance, even without glass, Ansel felt the gaze of the woman sweep the cliffs below them like a hand searching a pocket for a knife.

"She's not following our trail," Corvin breathed. "Look at the bearing. She's not chasing where we went. She's heading where we're *going*."

Ansel followed his finger and felt the blood leave her face.

Southwest. Toward the deep passes. Toward the voices under the snow she had not yet heard but already, in her bones, dreaded.

Toward the door.

Serane Doss knew where they were bound—and she was going to get there first.
