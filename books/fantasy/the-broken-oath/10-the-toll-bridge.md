## 10. The Toll Bridge

The river came out of the mountains the way secrets do—quietly, and then all at once.

Corvin heard it before he saw it, a low grinding under the morning fog where the Greywash chewed at its banks. By the time they crested the last ridge of scree, the sound had swelled to a roar that filled his teeth, and there it was below: the Toll Bridge, strung across the gorge on chains older than any kingdom Corvin had ever robbed. The planks were black with a century of boot-grease and rain. The tollhouse squatted at the near end like a toad, its chimney smoking, its door open despite the cold.

"Smugglers' road," Maren said behind him, in the tone of a scholar cataloguing something distasteful. "I confess I expected more... architecture."

"You're standing on it," Corvin said. "This *is* the architecture. Everything else got burned."

He didn't look back at Ansel. He'd been not looking back at her for two days now, which was its own kind of looking. She walked the way she did everything—quietly, precisely, as if the ground had agreed to her passage in advance—and every hour he spent in her company made the thing he was carrying in his chest heavier. He'd been carrying it since the waystation at Marrow Ford, since the night he'd sat alone by the dead fire and worked out, with a smuggler's clean arithmetic, exactly what Serane Doss would pay for three names and a route.

He hadn't sold them yet. That was the honest version. He also hadn't decided not to.

*You'll know when you know,* his mother used to say, mending nets by lamplight. *A man always knows when he knows. He just doesn't like what he knows.*

The fog thinned as they descended, and Corvin saw the figure waiting on the bridge's first span, arms folded against the rail, watching them come with the patience of a spider who owns the web.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

"What is it?" Ansel's voice, low, already reaching for whatever she kept sheathed inside herself.

"That," Corvin said, "is Brannick Vail."

* * *

They had been rivals for eleven years, which in the smuggling trade was nearly a marriage. They'd run salt together out of Duncarrow before the inquisitors closed the coast. They'd split over a woman, then over money, then over the only argument that ever really mattered between two people in their line: what a man owed to the people walking beside him. Brannick had answered that question early and often. Brannick answered it with ledgers.

He hadn't changed. He stood on the bridge like a man standing on his own property—which, in every way that mattered on this river, he was—and he watched Corvin approach with those flat grey eyes that never hurried anywhere.

"Hale." Brannick's voice carried over the water without effort. "Word was you were dead."

"Word was right. I'm working on it."

Brannick smiled without warmth. Up close he was broader than Corvin remembered, silver coming into his beard, a scar through one eyebrow that Corvin didn't remember giving him and resented slightly for missing. Two of Brannick's people lounged at the far end of the bridge, crossbows resting easy across their knees, which meant they weren't resting at all.

"Toll's doubled since you last crossed," Brannick said. "Inquisitor patrols on the south roads. Risk premium."

"I know the rates, Vail. I helped write them."

"You helped write them eleven years ago." Brannick's gaze slid past Corvin's shoulder, unhurried, and Corvin felt him take inventory the way another man might breathe. The tall woman with the scholar's hands and no calluses worth mentioning. The other woman—grey-cloaked, watchful, moving like someone who knew every stone in these mountains by its private name. "Interesting company you keep these days. Interesting company for a dead man."

"They're paying customers."

"Mm." Brannick scratched his jaw. "Here's my trouble, Hale. I've had visitors. Crown men. Inquisitors, riding up from the valley with their little white badges and their little white fires. Asking after travelers. Three of them, they said. One of them a keeper off the Hollow Gate." His eyes came back to Corvin, and there it was—the ledger opening. "Now why would the crown care about a gatekeeper?"

Corvin kept his face still. Behind him, he heard nothing from Ansel at all, and that silence was louder than anything Maren might have said.

"Folk ask after all sorts," he said.

"Folk do." Brannick nodded slowly. "And I tell folk all sorts. Depends on the day. Depends on the price." He pushed off the rail and stepped closer, boots loud on the old planks, and dropped his voice to something only Corvin could hear under the river's roar. "Depends, mostly, on whether an old friend remembers what he owes me. Because you owe me, Hale. Duncarrow. You left me holding the manifest when the customs cutters came, and I did eighteen months in the Saltcells for your name. Eighteen months eating weevils and thinking about you."

"I paid that debt," Corvin said quietly. "The winter I pulled your brother out of the Whitefen with a broken leg on my back. Forty miles. You know I did."

"I know you did." For just a moment something moved behind the grey eyes—something old and tired. Then it was gone, shuttered, and the ledger was open again. "Debts don't cancel, Hale. They accumulate interest. So here's the rate. You walk your friends across free, today, no questions. And in exchange, next time the inquisitors come asking—and they will come asking—you send word ahead. Names. Route. Where they sleep." He smiled again, and it was worse than the first smile. "That's all. Just words. Words are cheap. You've always said so."

The river roared beneath them, patient as arithmetic.

Corvin looked at the far end of the bridge. At the mist rising off the Greywash. At the mountains northward, where somewhere above the snowline a seal was failing and a country full of exiles was pressing its palms against a door. And he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief: *this is the moment. This is the fork. Every man gets one, and here's mine.*

He could say yes. It would cost him nothing today. It would probably cost him nothing tomorrow. Serane would pay regardless; Brannick's word would simply make it cleaner, safer, a chain of custody for his own survival. And when the soldiers took them at some waystation down the line, he'd be miles away with his purse fat and his name unattached, and he'd drink to his own cleverness in some port town until the drinking stopped working, which it eventually did, it always did.

Eighteen months in the Saltcells. Weevils. A name spoken in a customs house while Brannick went to the wall for him.

*A man always knows when he knows.*

"No," Corvin said.

Brannick tilted his head. "No?"

"No." Corvin heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, because inside he felt like the bridge must feel with the river tearing at its chains. "Send your word. Sell your names. But not mine to sell, Vail. Not theirs either. Find another way to collect your interest, because I'm not paying that one. Not this time. Not for Duncarrow, not for the Saltcells, not for anything you've got in that ledger of yours." He pulled his purse from his belt and tossed it; Brannick caught it without looking. "There's the toll. Doubled. Count it. And then let us cross, because we're paying customers, and you've never once cheated a paying customer. It's the only principle you've got left."

For a long moment the only sound was the river.

Then Brannick laughed—a short, ugly, genuine bark of a laugh—and turned the purse over in his hand. "Eleven years," he said, "and you finally grow a spine. On a bridge. Of course. Couldn't have done it somewhere I could charge admission." He jerked his chin toward the far bank. "Cross. All of you. And Hale—" his voice dropped again, and this time there was no ledger in it, only something rawer, "—the inquisitors aren't asking after three travelers anymore. They're asking after four. Someone gave them a description of a keeper. Grey cloak, young, walks soft. They're calling her the Gate's Ghost." He shrugged. "I told them I'd seen no such woman. Consider that my contribution to your new spine. Don't waste it."

Corvin crossed the bridge. His legs felt strange, lighter than they should, as if the decision had been ballast he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Behind him, Brannick called out once more over the water, almost cheerful: "Give my regards to Veyland!"

Corvin stopped mid-stride.

He turned. Brannick was already walking away toward the tollhouse, but at the door he paused, and looked back, and winked.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the river, screened by pines, with a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Maren fell asleep mid-sentence over one of the rescued books, cheek on the page, ink transferring faintly onto her jaw. Corvin sat up keeping the fire alive and telling himself that was the reason he wasn't sleeping.

Ansel came and sat across from him without a word. She did that. She could fill a silence better than anyone he'd ever met by putting absolutely nothing into it.

"If you're going to say something," he said finally, "say it."

"You refused him."

"You were listening."

"I was listening." Her eyes caught the firelight. "At the far end of the bridge. I heard every word."

Corvin fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch. Here it was, then. The second fork, smaller than the first but sharper. He could lie—he was good at lying, it was practically a trade skill—but lying to her had started to feel like burning books. Wasteful. Ugly.

"He offered me a trade," Corvin said. "Free crossing today. Your names and route to the inquisitors later. I nearly took it." He looked up. "I want you to know that. Not the cleaned version. I stood on that bridge and I did the sums, and the sums said sell you, and I was this close—" he held up two fingers, a hair's width apart, "—to being the kind of man who does sums like that."

"Why didn't you?"

"The honest answer? I don't fully know. Part of it was an old debt I owed him, paid back wrong, and I couldn't stomach paying it wronger. Part of it was—" he gestured vaguely at the dark, at the mountains, at her, "—this. Whatever this is. You, and the scholar, and the singing country nobody's supposed to remember. Somewhere in the last few weeks I stopped being able to picture the man who sells you and still has to live in his own head afterward." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "My mother would say I finally learned what I know."

Ansel was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was even, and that was somehow worse than anger.

"At the waystation at Marrow Ford," she said. "Two nights ago. You were awake after Maren and I slept. You sat by the fire a long time. You had paper."

The fire popped. Corvin didn't move.

"I know what paper and a long night means on smuggler roads," Ansel said. "I grew up on a border. I'm not accusing you. I'm telling you I saw, and I said nothing then, and I'm saying nothing now except this: I watched you choose today. Both halves of you. The half that wrote the letter and the half that told Vail no." She rose, brushing pine needles from her cloak. "I'm choosing to believe the second half. Don't make me sorry."

She walked to the edge of the camp and stood facing north, into the dark, the way she often did—keeping a watch she never explained and never needed to explain, because she was a keeper, and keeping was the whole of her.

Corvin sat by the dying fire and felt the strangest sensation: that he had just survived something. Not the bridge, not Vail, not the crossbows. Something interior, some collapse that should have buried him and hadn't. He thought about the unfinished letter hidden in the lining of his pack, and decided—not tonight, maybe not ever—that it would stay unwritten.

Maren stirred in her sleep and murmured something in Old Veylander, a word Corvin's grandmother had used over the fish-kettles when he was small enough to sit at her feet. *Aelth.* Homecoming. Or homegoing. The language had never been clear on which direction home ran.

High above them, wind moved along the ridgelines of Thornfell, and Corvin could have sworn—for a heartbeat, just one—that the wind carried something underneath it. Thin. Far away. Almost like voices. Almost like singing.

He told himself it was the river.

The river was closer. The river made sense.

But he lay awake a long time anyway, listening to the mountains, and the mountains did not stop.
