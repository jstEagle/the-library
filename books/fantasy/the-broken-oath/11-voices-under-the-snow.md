## 11. Voices Under the Snow

The cold came up through Ansel's boots like a living thing, patient and old, and she had learned long ago not to fight it but to walk with it, the way one walks with a dog that knows the road better than you do.

Three days above the tree line now. The smugglers' roads had thinned to suggestions—here a cairn half-swallowed by scree, there a ledge worn smooth by generations of feet that had never been counted on any ledger—and then even those suggestions failed, and it was only her knowledge of Thornfell that kept them moving: which faces of stone shed their frost in the morning sun and which held it like a grudge, where the wind funneled into screaming channels and where it went strangely still, as if holding its breath around something buried.

She walked first, always. Maren followed with the three rescued books wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like children, and Corvin came last, watching their backtrail with the unhurried vigilance of a man who had spent his whole life being followed.

The snow began at noon on the third day, fine and dry as ash, sifting down out of a sky the color of unpolished pewter. It did not fall so much as hang, suspended between falling and not falling, and in that hush Ansel felt the mountain change its mood beneath her feet.

She stopped.

"What is it?" Maren's voice was muffled by her scarf, thin as a thread drawn through cloth.

Ansel didn't answer at once. She crouched, pulled off one glove, and pressed her bare palm flat against the snowpack. Beneath the cold of the surface there was another cold, deeper, and beneath *that*—

A tremor. Faint. Rhythmic. Not an avalanche settling; she knew the sound of a mountain shrugging off its winter coat, the deep groan and sigh of shifting ice. This was different. This was *small*, and repeated, and it came from below.

"Get off the ice," she said quietly.

Corvin was already moving, hand on her shoulder, steering her toward a shelf of bare rock that jutted from the slope like a rib. "There's a hollow under this stretch," he said. "Old melt channel. The smugglers call it the Whisper Bridge. We cross it fast or not at all."

"You've crossed here before."

"Twice." He said nothing else about the other times, and Ansel had learned not to ask Corvin Hale for the endings of stories until he was ready to give them.

They crossed in single file, quick and light, boots whispering on the packed snow. Halfway across, Maren stumbled—not on the ice, Ansel saw, but as if something had reached up through it and plucked at her ankle like a child tugging a hem. The scholar made a small strangled sound and then stood very still, head cocked, listening.

"Maren?"

"Wait." Maren knelt. Pulled off both gloves. Pressed her ear—actually her ear—to the snow, hair spilling white against white, and her face did something complicated. Wonder and horror fighting for the same ground.

"There are voices," she said. "Under the ice."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite spine the smugglers called the Old Man's Shoulder, a wind-scoured hollow where a fire could burn without announcing itself to the whole valley. Corvin built it small and fed it slow, feeding it like a secret, and the three of them sat close enough that their shoulders touched, because up here warmth was currency and no one pretended otherwise.

It started after full dark.

At first Ansel thought it was the wind. Wind in Thornfell did sing—it moaned through the passes in long minor notes, keened around the pinnacles, sometimes whistled through cracks in the stone so purely it sounded almost deliberate, almost like a flute played by something with fingers. She had heard every song the mountain had. She knew them the way a shepherd knows his flock's voices.

This was not wind.

It rose from below. From beneath the snowfield that stretched away from their camp in a pale sheet toward the high col, singing up through the frozen ground the way heat rises through a floor. A melody—or many melodies braided into one, out of phase with each other, overlapping, the way a village sounds when everyone sings the same hymn a half-beat apart. It had words. She couldn't make out the words, but she could hear that they were words, syllables rising and falling with intention, with *feeling*, and underneath the melody ran a pulse like a heartbeat slowed to the tempo of grief.

Maren sat bolt upright, books clutched to her chest. "That's Veylish," she whispered. "That's—I know the cadence. It's a lament form. They sang it at—" Her voice caught. "At funerals. At partings. It's in the grammar of the songs that survived the erasure, the ones people hummed without knowing why. *We were here. Remember us. We were here.*"

"It's coming from underground," Corvin said. His face was lit orange and shadow, and he looked younger than Ansel had ever seen him, and more afraid. "From below us. How far below us can a voice carry through rock?"

"I don't know," Maren said. "I don't think it's carrying through rock. I think—" She fumbled one of the books open, the middle one, the one whose maps lied, and turned pages with numb fingers until she found what she wanted. "I think we're standing on the seal."

Ansel felt the words land in her chest like a dropped stone.

"The Hollow Gate is forty miles south," she said. "I've kept it my whole life. I've walked every stone of it. There's nothing here."

"There's nothing *mapped* here," Maren said gently, and Ansel hated how gentle she was being, how much it sounded like pity. "Ansel. The gate isn't a door in a wall. It's a wall around everything. You've been guarding one hinge your whole life. But a seal that size—" she spread her hands, taking in the pass, the peaks, the dark — "a seal that size has a body. And bodies have thin places."

The singing swelled. Or perhaps they were simply becoming able to hear it, the way eyes adjust to darkness until the darkness turns out to be full of things. Ansel stood, without deciding to stand, and walked out beyond the firelight onto the snowfield.

The cold hit her like a wall, but she barely noticed, because now that she was standing directly on it, alone, with no fire to soften anything, she could feel it through her boots, through her bones: the pulse. Slow. Enormous. Patient in the way that only suffering can be patient, because suffering has no other choice.

And the voices. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Men and women and—she pressed her hand to her mouth—children, high and thin threads woven through the lower harmonies, singing a song about going home to a country that no longer existed, and the terrible thing, the thing that would stay with her for the rest of her life, was that they did not sound angry.

They sounded hopeful.

They sounded like people who believed someone might finally be listening.

* * *

She didn't remember walking back to the fire. She found herself sitting again, the heat of the flames almost painful against her chilled face, and Corvin pressing a tin cup of something hot and spirituous into her hands without a word.

"The date," she said.

Maren looked up.

"In the book. In the foundation niche. The last page—the date written there. Tonight's date. I thought—" Ansel stared into the fire. "I thought it marked when the door opens. When whatever's sealed gets out. That's what the oath implied. Containment fails, containment breaks, the thing inside comes out. Every story my order ever told follows that shape. The wolf gets out of the pen. The flood gets past the levee. Something *comes through*."

"And now?" Maren asked.

"Now I'm listening to a thousand people sing a funeral hymn under a glacier, and they don't sound like wolves." Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake as if they belonged to someone else. "They sound like they're waiting for the walls to come down so they can finally be let *out*. Not break out. Be let out. Like they know the difference. Like they've been polite about it for a hundred years."

The silence stretched. The fire popped. Somewhere far below, faint as memory, the singing continued, and Ansel realized she could no longer hear it as separate from the mountain itself—that it had become part of Thornfell's voice, that it had probably been part of it for a century, and that every keeper before her who had ever paused on a high pass and heard the wind sing strangely had heard this and called it weather.

"The prophecy doesn't mark the opening," she said slowly. "It marks the collapse. The seal isn't failing because something inside is pushing. It's failing because—" she searched for the words and found them, and they frightened her with their plainness — "because it was never built to last forever. Because whoever built it built it to hold for a hundred years and not a day more, and the hundred years are up, and everything inside is going to come through whether there's a door or not. Whether there's anyone ready or not. Whether what comes through is a monster or—"

"A country," Maren finished quietly. "What comes through will be a country."

"That's worse," Corvin said.

All three of them looked at him.

"A monster, you kill it or you run," he said, staring into the fire. His jaw worked. "But a country—a country has farmers and bakers and children and grudges and a king nobody's crowned and a hundred years of reasons to hate everybody who put them under the ice. You can't kill a country. You can't outrun it. It just—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the snow, at the stars, at the whole indifferent world — "arrives. And then everyone alive has to figure out what to do with it."

"You talk like you've thought about this," Ansel said.

Corvin was quiet for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face. "My grandmother used to hum a tune she wouldn't name," he said at last. "Said it was from her mother, who got it from hers. Said never sing it loud, never sing it where soldiers drink. Said our family came over the mountains before the maps changed, and the less the maps remembered us, the longer we'd live." He shrugged, a small hard motion. "I always figured it was smuggler superstition. Old families tell old stories to keep children careful." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and wet in the firelight. "It's the same tune. Under the ice. My grandmother's lullaby is what they're singing down there."

No one spoke. Above them the stars burned in their cold multitudes, and below them the dead—or the not-dead, or the whatever-they-were—sang their hundred-year hymn into the roots of the world.

* * *

Ansel did not sleep. Toward the smallest hours she climbed again to the edge of the snowfield, alone, and knelt, and did the thing her order had trained out of her so thoroughly that doing it felt like breaking a bone: she listened as a person listens, not as a keeper takes inventory.

The pulse beneath the ice had quickened. Barely—measurably, if you knew what to measure—but it had. She thought of a heart that had beaten slowly for a century, conserving itself, and had just now, tonight, sensed proximity. Sensered *her*. The keeper. The one named on the last page of a book buried in a gate's foundations before her grandmother's grandmother was born.

"They're not pushing," she said aloud, to the snow, to the dark, to whoever was underneath. Her breath fogged and hung. "You're not pushing, are you? You're pulling. You're pulling the seal apart from underneath, gently, the way you'd pull a blanket off someone sleeping. Because you felt me coming."

The singing shifted. She couldn't have sworn to it afterward—the change was too subtle, too far below the threshold of certainty—but it seemed to her that the melody bent, that the lament leaned toward her position on the slope like grass leaning toward a window, and that somewhere in the weave of voices one voice rose briefly above the others, clear and female and exhausted, singing a single line that needed no translation because some things predate language:

*Come.*

Ansel knelt in the snow with her heart hammering and understood, finally, with total clarity, what the date in the book meant. Not a door swinging open. Not a wolf escaping a pen. A dam reaching the end of its design, a hundred years of patience ending not in violence but in simple arithmetic—the seal had been built to fail, and fail it would, soon, catastrophically, and everything behind it would arrive in the world at once, uninvited, unprepared-for, unless someone opened a door instead. Deliberately. On purpose. While there was still a threshold left to cross.

And she knew, too, with the same clarity, what waited between her and that door. Serane Doss and her soldiers were two days behind them at most—Corvin had read the signs at the waystation ruins that morning, boot-prints and burned cook-fires, and had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet when the news was bad. The inquisitors would not come to open anything. They would come with sappers and charges and the righteous certainty that mercy means burial.

The deadline wasn't the seal's collapse.

The deadline was the race.

Ansel rose, brushed the snow from her knees, and turned back toward the fire where her strange little family waited—scholar, smuggler, and the last keeper of a lie—and behind her, faint beneath the ice, the singing followed her like a hand laid between her shoulder blades, guiding her forward into the dark.
