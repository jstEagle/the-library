## 12. The Inquisitor's Mercy

The smuggler's name was Ferren Brask, and he had been crying for the better part of an hour.

Serane Doss let him cry. She sat across the cell's single table with her hands folded and her ledger open, and she watched him weep into his bound wrists with the patience of a woman who had learned long ago that tears were a kind of testimony too—often more honest than anything spoken. The cell beneath the toll-house was cold enough that his breath fogged, and the lantern between them guttered in some draft from the old mine shafts below, throwing his face first gold, then gray, then gold again. A man made of weather.

"Take your time," she said.

"I don't— I told the sergeant everything. I told him."

"The sergeant asked you where your camps were." Serane turned a page she had not read. "I'm asking you something else entirely. I want to know about the keeper of the Hollow Gate."

That stopped the crying, or at least changed its texture. He looked up at her through swollen eyes, and there it was—the flicker she had been waiting for. Not confusion. Recognition, followed by fear, followed by the particular guilt of a man who has betrayed someone without meaning to.

"There isn't one," he said. "There's no keeper. There's just the gate."

"Ferren." She said his name gently, the way one might touch a bruise. "You've run the Thornfell passes for eleven years. You know every fire-ring on every ridge. And you're telling me that in all that country, no one tends the one sealed pass that every map marks with a warning?"

He swallowed. Outside, snow ticked against the barred window in small dry sounds, like insects dying against glass.

"She keeps to herself," he said finally. "Nobody sees her. Nobody *wants* to see her. That's the whole point of her, isn't it? The keepers are supposed to be invisible. Unnoticed. Like they're not even people."

"And yet," Serane said, "you know she's a woman."

The silence stretched. He understood then what he had done, and she watched the understanding move through him like cold water through cloth.

"It's not hard," he whispered. "You see her sometimes. At a distance. Buying salt at the valley market twice a year, and the merchants never remember her, and the dogs never bark at her, and—" He shook his head. "It's like looking at a hole shaped like a person. But holes have edges, Inquisitor. You can see the shape of what's missing if you look long enough."

Serane wrote that down. *A hole shaped like a person.* It was, she thought, as good a description of the keeper's oath as any scholar would manage, and this man had found it by accident, in the course of moving contraband through the high passes.

"What is her name?" she asked.

"I don't know it. No one does. That's the oath, they say. They give up their names when they take up the gate." He hesitated. "But the old men in my trade, the ones who ran these roads before me—they called her line the Vosses. Keepers of the Hollow Gate for four generations. Before that, who knows. Before the fall, maybe."

*Voss.*

She wrote the name down and felt nothing, which was itself remarkable, because names were supposed to mean something to an inquisitor. Names were hooks. Names were how you pulled a life out of the dark and held it up to the light until it burned. But this name landed in her like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without striking bottom, because she had heard it before—in another life, at another gate, from the mouth of another keeper who had also believed that silence was service.

"Thank you, Ferren," she said, and closed the ledger. "You'll be released in the morning. Your cooperation will be noted."

"You're not going to—" He stopped. Started again. "They said the Crown's inquisitors were burning camps. Killing runners. My brother's crew was on the White Larch road two nights ago and—"

"Your brother's crew burned caches of unlicensed goods and fled south," Serane said. "No one died. The Crown has no interest in dead smugglers, Ferren. Smugglers talk; corpses don't." She rose, tucking the ledger under her arm. "What the Crown has interest in is the gate. Tell your people: anyone who helps the keeper, shelters her, guides her, or carries word to her—that is a different matter entirely. That is treason against the peace of the realm, and it will be treated as such."

She left him weeping again, though differently now, and climbed the stairs out of the cell feeling the familiar weight settle over her shoulders—not guilt, never guilt, but the gravity of the work. Mercy had many shapes. Most people imagined it as a soft thing, a warm thing. Serane had learned that mercy could be a hammer, and that sometimes the kindest act in the world was to break something thoroughly before it could break worse things later.

* * *

Her tent that night smelled of tallow and wet wool, and the wind off the Thornfell peaks came down the valley in long slow breaths, like something enormous sleeping and exhaling.

She did not sleep. Instead she took out the second ledger—the private one, the one no clerk ever transcribed—and turned past years of entries to a page near the beginning, soft with handling, stained at one corner with something she had never been able to wash out.

*Ashgate,* the entry read. *Year 14 of my service. What came through came at dusk.*

She had been twenty-six. Ashgate was a minor seal in the eastern hills, so minor that it rated a garrison of six and a keeper so old he had forgotten his own oath-name and answered to whatever the soldiers called him. Serane had been posted there as a junior inquisitor, her first commission, full of doctrine and certainty. The gate was a low arch of black stone in a hillside, choked with ivy, and the keeper lived in a hut beside it and spent his days doing nothing, which had struck her then as the most profound waste of a life she had ever witnessed.

*Nothing happens at these gates,* her instructors had said. *That is what success looks like. You will serve decades and see nothing, and you must learn to call that victory.*

Then, on the last night of autumn, the arch had hummed.

Not cracked. Not broken. *Hummed*—a sound she still could not describe except as the sound a door makes when someone on the other side leans their weight against it. The old keeper had come out of his hut barefoot in the frost, and his face had been the worst part, worse than anything that followed. He had looked at the humming stone the way a father looks at a child's fever. He had known. He had known exactly what it meant, and he had said only one word:

"Soon."

It had come three nights later, at dusk, as the entry said. Not through the arch—the arch held, the arch always held—but through the hillside beside it, through forty feet of rock that had simply decided, for the space of ten heartbeats, to be elsewhere. Serane remembered standing in the garrison doorway with her sword half-drawn and watching the darkness pour out of the hillside like smoke running backward, and she remembered thinking, absurdly, *that's not possible*, and then remembering that the entire purpose of her order was to stand guard against precisely the things that were not possible.

Three of them came through. Only three. The histories would later record them as *manifestations*, *seepage events*, *incursions*—the clean language of clerks terrified of the truth. Serane had other words.

One of them killed Sergeant Hale and his two youngest men before it was brought down, and it had not fought like a beast or even like a man. It had fought like something *starving*. The second had gotten into the village. The third—

The third had gone to the keeper's hut, and the old man had opened his door to it, and Serane had arrived in time to see him reach out toward it with an expression she had never been able to file under any category her training offered. Not terror. Not madness. Something closer to *recognition*. As if the thing wearing the dark like a shroud had once had a face he knew, and he had been glad.

She had put her blade through it. Through both of them, in the end, because the keeper had thrown himself in the way, and grief and horror had made her fast rather than careful. She had stood afterward in the ruined doorway with blood freezing on her hands, listening to the hillside grind itself closed again, and she had understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only once or twice in a life—that she had just killed the only man who knew what those things actually were.

The inquiry had cleared her. Of course it had. Doctrine was clear: anything crossing a seal was an enemy of the realm, and enemies of the realm were to be destroyed. The tribunal had commended her restraint, her speed, her adherence to protocol. They had given her a commendation and a promotion and a new posting, and they had never once asked her the question that had kept her awake for thirty years since:

*Why did the keeper open his door?*

She had built her career on the answer she was permitted to believe: senility, enchantment, the corruption that seeped from long proximity to sealed things. But the true answer had followed her from posting to posting like a dog she couldn't lose. Because she had seen the third one's face, in the last instant, before her blade took it. Under the shroud of dark, for half a heartbeat, it had had a face. A woman's face. Gaunt, gray, exhausted beyond measure—and weeping.

Doctrine said the exiled dead wore the faces of the loved ones left behind, to lure the unwary. Serane had repeated that doctrine a thousand times in a thousand lectures. But at night, in the privacy of her skull, the question remained, patient as a stone in a shoe:

*If it was a lure, why did it look so relieved to be killed?*

* * *

In the morning, the reports came in from the northern sweeps, and with them, the piece she had been waiting for.

A wine merchant in the valley town, questioned about unusual purchases, remembered selling lamp oil and dried fruit in quantities suggesting travel—a sale made, he thought, to a woman. A woman who paid in old coin. Another witness, a shepherd, spoke of lights on the ridgeline above the Hollow Gate three nights running, and of strangers on the smugglers' roads: a big man with a scarred jaw seen walking openly where no honest walker had reason to be, and a thin, stooped figure who talked to himself in the cadence of a scholar reciting.

Serane laid the fragments out on her campaign table like a surgeon laying out bones, and let them tell her the shape of the body.

The keeper had left her gate. That was the first truth, and it was the important one, because keepers did not leave. In fourteen generations, the records showed, the keepers of the Hollow Gate had not traveled farther than the valley market. If the keeper was on the road, she had been driven out—or she had learned something. Either way, the seal was compromised, and either way, the clock was running.

And she was not alone. A smuggler who knew the passes. A scholar—the description matched, uncannily, the disgraced court academician Maren Quill, whose heretical papers on the erased kingdoms had been suppressed a decade ago and whose disappearance had been quietly celebrated in certain doctrinal offices. Quill. Of all the scholars in the world, the one who had written most dangerously about Veyland.

Serane stood at the map table for a long time, her fingers resting lightly on the mountains, on the blank space east of the Thornfell range where every cartographer for a century had drawn nothing but clean white paper and a printed legend: *TERRA INCOGNITA — BY TREATY, UNMAPPED.*

Veyland. Even the name was contraband. A kingdom that had not fallen but been *unmade*—its cities struck from record, its language proscribed, its people, by every official account, extinguished in the wars of the century past. Serane had read the restricted histories. She knew what the treaties said, and she knew what the treaties carefully did not say, and she knew that the Hollow Gate and gates like it were not fortifications against invasion.

They were lids.

And under the lids, something stirred. Her reports from the high passes grew stranger each week: shepherds' dogs refusing to go above the snowline; ice on the southern faces melting in midwinter; and now, from three separate sources, the same impossible detail—*singing*. Voices under the snow, voices in the stone, voices that the mountain folk blamed on spirits and the sensible dismissed as wind, but which Serane, who had stood outside a humming arch thirty years ago, recognized for what they were.

Pressure. The lid was bowing.

"Sergeant Ovist," she called, and the man appeared at the tent flap with the promptness of long habit.

"Inquisitor."

"Double the sweep on the western passes. I want the smugglers' roads watched, not burned—we need eyes, not ashes. And send riders to the sapper company at Fort Marren. Tell Captain Ilves I require his full complement and his demolition stores, and I require them within the week." She paused, her hand flat on the blank space of the map. "We are going to close a door permanently."

Ovist hesitated. He was a decent man, Ovist, decent men always hesitated at the right moments, which was why they stayed sergeants. "Ma'am—the keeper. If we find her first. What are your orders regarding the keeper?"

Serane considered the question honestly, because she owed honesty to the dead, if to no one else. Somewhere in those mountains was a woman who had given her name to a stone door and her life to silence, who had done everything right, who had kept faith with an oath older than the realm itself—and who was now, unknowingly, carrying the spark that might burn down everything. A hole shaped like a person. Ansel Voss, if the old records were right, fourth of her line, last of her line, alone in the high dark with a duty she did not understand.

Thirty years ago, Serane had killed a keeper because she had not understood in time. She would not make that error again. Understanding, this time, would come first. And after understanding—

"After understanding," she said aloud, "mercy."

"Ma'am?"

"Find her, Sergeant. Find her before she reaches whatever she's looking for. And bring her to me alive." She rolled up the map, and her voice went quiet, almost gentle, the voice she used for deathbeds and tribunals alike. "Whatever she thinks she's opening—it must not open. I have seen what waits on the other side of these doors. I have buried what they leave behind. If the seal fails, thousands die. If the gate falls, perhaps more. But if we collapse it first—if we bury the road so deep that not even memory can find it—then no one dies at all. Not her. Not the villages below. Not the things in the dark that only want to be let out."

She looked at the window, where the Thornfell peaks stood white and silent against the morning, beautiful as a lie.

"They call us butchers in the taverns," she said. "Let them. Destruction is mercy, Sergeant. It is the only mercy that cannot be argued with, because the dead do not suffer, and the saved never know what was spared them." She straightened her collar, and the inquisitor returned to her face like a mask settling into place. "Now go. We have a door to close, and somewhere in those mountains, a keeper who doesn't yet know she's holding the key."

Ovist saluted and went. Alone in her tent, Serane took out the private ledger one last time and, beneath the thirty-year-old entry, wrote a new line in her small precise hand:

*The keeper of the Hollow Gate is named Voss. She is young, and alone, and wrong about what she guards. So was I, once. Pray we are both merciful.*

Outside, far up the valleys, faint and intermittent, the wind carried something that might have been singing.
