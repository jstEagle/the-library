## 13. Betrayal at the Waystation

The waystation at Greyhollow had been a smuggler's refuge for longer than Corvin Hale had been alive, and probably longer than his father had been alive, and it had one rule that every soul who used it understood without ever writing down: *you don't sell a route through Greyhollow.* Not because of honor—smugglers had about as much honor as river ice, thin and treacherous and gone by morning—but because the whole trade ran on the certainty that some doors stayed shut. Sell one route, and every keeper on every pass started checking their ledgers twice. Sell two, and you were done in this country.

Corvin had sold theirs anyway.

He'd done it three nights ago, at the toll bridge, in the space between one breath and the next, when old Vetch had slid the paper across the counter and said, *just the road they're taking, boy, nothing but ink,* and Corvin had looked at the coin purse fat enough to buy him out of Thornfell entirely—out of the passes, out of the cold, out of the long slow dying that was a smuggler's retirement—and he had said yes. He'd told himself it meant nothing. That Serane Doss's soldiers would ride to the Hollow Gate and find an empty valley, that Ansel would be long gone by then, that Maren Quill would be halfway to somewhere with a new name and a false beard. Ink on paper. Nothing but ink.

He'd told himself that right up until tonight, sitting apart from the fire with his back against the waystation's crumbling wall, turning Vetch's coin over and over in his fingers until the grooves wore smooth against his thumb.

* * *

Greyhollow waystation was less a building than a wound in the hillside—a roof of slate slabs propped against the mountain's shoulder, walls of dry-stacked stone furred with lichen, a fire pit worn into a shallow bowl by a hundred years of fugitive fires. It sat in a fold of the hills where the wind went strange, curling back on itself, so that smoke rose and then drifted sideways along the ground like something crawling home. Ansel had chosen it. Of course she had. She knew these mountains the way other people knew their own hands, and she'd walked them into this hollow with the easy confidence of someone following a path only she could see.

She was asleep now, or nearly. Corvin could see her shape in the firelight, curled near the coals with her cloak pulled to her chin, her breathing slow and even. Even asleep she looked watchful somehow. Like the stone of the gate itself. He wondered if she dreamed about doors.

Maren slept badly, as always, muttering fragments of dead languages into her bedroll, one hand curled around the strap of the book-bag even in sleep. Three books saved from a burning cellar. A hundred years of scholarship reduced to ash and embers, and Maren carried what remained like a reliquary, like a corpse she couldn't bring herself to bury.

And Corvin sat in the dark with blood money in his fist, listening to them breathe.

That was the thing nobody warned you about betrayal. It wasn't loud. It didn't come with trumpets or a knife between the shoulders. It came quiet, in the small hours, wearing the face of practicality. *They'll never know. It won't even matter. You have to look after yourself; no one else will.* His mother had taught him that last part, before the fever took her, pressing her last coins into his hand with fingers already going cold. Look after yourself, Corvi. The world is a cliff face and everyone else is just weather.

He'd built his whole life on that. And it had worked, right up until a keeper with dust in her hair had stood in front of him at the Hollow Gate with suspicion in her eyes and asked him how he knew her name, and he had lied to her, and then—this was the part that still didn't make sense—he'd come back. He'd kept coming back. Somewhere between the gate and the cellar fire and the long cold roads where Ansel moved through Thornfell like water finding its level, the weather had turned into people.

He looked at the coin again. In the firelight, the stamp caught: the crowned tower of the royal mint. Clean money. Honest money. Money for selling a road.

Corvin closed his fist around it, and for a long moment he couldn't feel anything at all, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

He woke before dawn because something was wrong with the silence.

Not the absence of sound—the presence of it. The wrong kind. Greyhollow's wind did its strange backward crawl all night, every night; he'd slept here a dozen times and the hollow always whispered, always muttered, always breathed. Tonight it had stopped. The air lay flat and held, like the pause between lightning and thunder.

Corvin lay still in the dark and listened, and beneath the silence he heard it: the creak of leather. Many sets of boots on frost-stiffened scree, moving with the deliberate care of men who had been told exactly where to walk.

They were already in the hollow.

He didn't remember deciding to move. His body simply did what thirty years of smuggling had trained it to do—rolled away from the wall, low, silent, one hand finding Maren's shoulder and clamping over her mouth before her gasp could escape. Her eyes flew open, wide and white in the dark, and he put his lips against her ear.

"Soldiers," he breathed. "Inquisitors. Don't make a sound."

Her hand went to the book-bag instantly. Scholar to the bone. He almost laughed.

Ansel was harder. She slept like a person who trusted the mountain to warn her, and the mountain hadn't, and when Corvin shook her awake her first instinct was to fight—her elbow caught him hard under the ribs before his whisper landed. Then she went absolutely still. He felt her listening the way he imagined she listened to the gate: whole-bodied, patient, reading the dark.

"Twelve," she mouthed at him. "Maybe fourteen. Coming around the eastern rim."

Of course she could count them by sound. Of course she could.

"The south gully," Corvin whispered. "It's blind from the rim. We can—"

"South gully floods with meltwater." But she was already moving, rolling her bedroll with hands that didn't shake, and there was no time to argue about whether she knew a better way, because a boot scraped on slate above them and a voice called down into the hollow, flat and official and terribly awake:

"Light the pit. They're in there."

The world went orange. Torches bloomed along the eastern rim like a row of terrible flowers, and Corvin saw them clearly for one frozen instant: soldiers in the grey-and-crimson of the crown, crossbows leveled, and at their center a woman on foot with a pale scar running down one side of her face, watching the waystation with eyes that held no anger at all. That was the worst part. Serane Doss looked at the sleeping place where he'd betrayed his companions with the calm of someone confirming a sum on a ledger.

Because he'd given her the numbers. He'd given her the road. *Three travelers, one woman young, one woman old, one man. Taking the high route past Greyhollow, meaning to reach the door-country within the week.* Nothing but ink.

"Ansel," he hissed. "The back wall. There's a gap behind the slate—I've used it before—it drops into the scree field—"

"You've *used* it?" But she was already moving where he pointed, hauling Maren up, and Corvin understood with a sick lurch that she'd catalogued his knowledge of the waystation in the same instant she'd catalogued everything else, filing it away, weighing it, and that the weighing would come later. Right now there was only motion.

They went through the gap single file—Maren first, clutching her books, then Ansel, then Corvin—and dropped four feet into darkness as the first crossbow bolts punched through the space where they'd been sleeping, splintering bedrolls, thudding into stone. Behind them the waystation erupted with torchlight and shouting, and then they were running, sliding, falling down a slope of loose stone in the blue-black hour before dawn, and Corvin's heart was a drum and his lungs were knives and none of it hurt half as much as the thought that he had done this. He had drawn the map that brought them here.

A bolt skipped off the rock beside his head with a sound like a struck flint.

Then Ansel's hand closed on his collar and hauled him sideways, and the mountain opened.

He never would have found it—not in a lifetime of using these passes. A seam in the scree, invisible unless you knew the angle of it, widening into a slot canyon barely wider than his shoulders. Ansel pushed Maren through first, then Corvin, then slipped in after as boots crashed down the slope above them, and suddenly the torchlight was gone, cut off clean, and they were in a crack in the world where the air smelled of wet granite and old snow.

They climbed. For an hour, maybe more, hands bleeding on stone, breath smoking, while somewhere behind and below them the soldiers swarmed over a waystation that no longer held anyone. Ansel moved through the dark like she'd been born in it, and Corvin realized, with a strange floating clarity, that she wasn't even hurrying anymore. She was choosing paths the way a spider chooses silk. Every fork, every ledge, every tumble of fallen rock—they took the one she wanted, and the mountain swallowed their sounds behind them.

When the sky finally paled to grey, they came out onto a high shelf above a cloud-filled valley, and Ansel stopped, and turned, and looked at him.

* * *

She didn't shout. Corvin had expected shouting. He'd rehearsed shouting on the climb, composing defenses the way he composed alibis, quick and slippery and technically true. But Ansel just stood there in the early light with the clouds rolling below her feet and the wind pulling at her hair, and she looked at him the way she must have looked at the cracked foundation stone in the gate—with the particular attention of someone reading something written in a language she'd trusted her whole life.

"Maren," she said quietly. "Walk ahead, please. Follow the shelf east until the cairn. Don't go past the cairn."

Maren glanced between them, read the weather, and went. Her footsteps faded into the wind.

"They knew the hollow," Ansel said. "Not guessed it. Knew it. Knew we'd stop there, knew the layout, knew to seal the rim before dawn." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "There are perhaps six people alive who could have told them that. Two of us were asleep."

Corvin's mouth was dry. "Ansel—"

"I want to hear you say it." Still no anger. Just that steady, patient attention, the attention of the gate itself, of the stone that had watched a century pass without blinking. "I want to hear what you did, in your own words, because I've spent my whole life learning that the words people use about their own actions tell you more than the actions do."

So he told her. All of it. Vetch at the toll bridge, the paper, the coin purse heavy enough to buy a life out of Thornfell. He told it plainly because lying to her now, with the mountain standing witness, seemed like the one sin left that he couldn't commit. When he finished, he opened his hand and showed her the coin, the crowned tower catching the first true light of morning.

"That's why I doubled back," he said. "At the bridge I refused him. I told you I refused him. That part was true. But I'd already—" He stopped. Started again. "I sold the route first. Before I refused. Both things are true. I don't know how both things are true, but they are."

Ansel looked at the coin for a long moment.

"You woke before dawn," she said. "You heard them coming. You could have gone alone. The south gully, your own route—you know a dozen ways out of Greyhollow that don't include two women who can't move through scree like you can." Her head tilted slightly. "Why didn't you take them?"

"Because—" The word stuck. He made himself say it. "Because I watched you sleep by that fire and I couldn't remember who I was before I knew you. That's the truth. It's not a good truth. It's not noble. I sat there holding their bloody coin and I realized I'd already spent myself somewhere else, and the money was just metal."

The wind moved through the pass, carrying the smell of snow from the high peaks, and far below them the cloud sea turned slowly like something dreaming.

Ansel held out her hand.

For a heartbeat Corvin didn't understand. Then he did, and his stomach dropped, because she was asking for the coin, and giving it to her felt like handing over a piece of his own spine. He gave it anyway.

She weighed it once in her palm. Then she turned and threw it off the shelf, out over the clouds, and they both listened until the silence told them it had landed somewhere far below, in country no one would ever search.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "Not yet. Forgiveness is a door, and I don't open doors on the same day I find out they were broken." She met his eyes, and there was fury in hers after all—not hot, but deep, banked, glacial, the kind that moved mountains over centuries. "But you came back. You heard them coming and you came back for us instead of running, and I have read enough oaths in my life to know that what a person does when no one would blame them for doing otherwise—that's the truest page in the book. So we go on. And you carry this, Corvin Hale. You carry the knowing that I know. Every campfire, every road, every quiet morning. That's your price, and I'm charging it."

"Yes," he said. His voice came out rough. "Yes. Fairly charged."

"And when we reach the door," Ansel said, turning toward the east, toward the cairn where a small figure waited with her books, "you'll stand in front of whatever comes through it, and you'll speak the old tongue if it's in you, and you'll prove that the man who sold a road can open one too."

She started walking. After a moment, Corvin followed.

Behind them, far below in the drowned valley, something glinted where a coin had fallen—and above the peaks to the north, dark birds circled in a slowly tightening spiral, marking the place where soldiers regrouped, where a scarred woman studied maps by torchlight, and where the road ahead bent, at last, toward the door out of a country that no longer existed.
