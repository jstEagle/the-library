## 14. Where Mountains Remember

They climbed for three days into country that had no name on any map Ansel had ever memorized, which was itself a kind of miracle, because she had memorized all of them.

Not the royal surveyors' maps—those were pretty things, inked for courtiers who would never smell pine resin or feel a switchback bite through their boot soles. She meant the true maps, the ones smugglers scratched on hide and keepers like her carried in the skull: every goat trail in Thornfell, every collapsed mine shaft, every spring that ran sweet and every one that ran bitter with iron. Forty years of walking had written the mountains into her body. She could find her way from the Hollow Gate to the lowland fens blindfolded, in snow, in the dark before dawn.

And now Maren Quill was asking her to distrust all of it.

"Again," Ansel said, not bothering to keep the edge out of her voice. They stood on a shoulder of granite above the tree line, wind pulling at their cloaks, and Maren had the third book open across both forearms, its pages fluttering despite the leather straps she'd tied around them. "Explain it again. Slowly. As if I'm stupid."

"You're not stupid," Maren said. "You're precise. It's worse." She pushed her spectacles up her nose with one knuckle; they kept sliding in the cold. "The treaty men didn't just erase Veyland's name. They erased its *place*. Every cartographer in the empire was ordered to redraw the borderlands as though the kingdom had never sat there. Rivers were nudged. Valleys renamed. Whole ranges shifted a thumb's width eastward on paper."

Corvin Hale, crouched over their small cook fire a few paces down, snorted. "So the maps lie."

"The maps lie *consistently*," Maren said. "That's the important part. A lie told once is an error. A lie repeated by a hundred surveyors over a century becomes a kind of truth—a paper truth. But the land doesn't read decrees." She tapped the book. "The first keeper understood this. The mountains remember where they actually are. The rivers remember. The shadows remember. When you force a country to stop existing, the world develops—" she searched for the word, frowned, found it reluctantly— "*disagreements.*"

"So we look for where the land argues with itself," Ansel said.

"Where the light argues with the stone," Maren corrected. "At certain hours, in certain seasons, the sun strikes this region at angles that only make sense if there is a valley here that our maps say is not here. The peaks cast shadows that fall wrong. We walk toward the wrongness."

Ansel looked out at the wilderness below them: gray scree, white rags of old snow, the black stitching of pines in the folds of the high country. Somewhere south and west, two days' hard march, Serane Doss was moving with soldiers and sappers and enough blasting powder to murder a mountain. Corvin's contacts had brought word at the toll bridge, and the word had been simple: *she knows where you're going. She's racing you.*

That had been yesterday. The clock under everything—the date inked a century ago in a dead woman's hand—was ticking whether they walked or slept or bled.

"Then we'd better find some wrongness quickly," Ansel said.

* * *

They found the first wrongness at dusk, and it nearly broke something in her.

They had descended into a long U-shaped valley following a stream that Maren insisted was running a half-league west of where any honest map placed it. Ansel had checked against her own memory—the smugglers' charts, the keeper's routes, the oral geography passed down through nine generations of Hollow Gate wardens—and Maren was right. The stream was lying about its address. Water did not do that. Water obeyed gravity and nothing else, and yet here it was, carving a course through stone that should have been a league to the east, patient and unbothered, as if the correction of empires meant nothing to it.

But it was the shadows that stopped her heart.

The sun was going down behind the western ridge, and as it dropped, the peaks along the valley's north wall threw their shadows across the floor—and the shadows fell *south*. Not angled south, not bent by some trick of the terrain. They fell cleanly, correctly, mathematically south, the way shadows fall when the sun sets in the *east*.

Ansel stood in the blue cold of that impossible evening light and felt the ground tilt beneath her, though the ground was perfectly still. It was like hearing her own voice speak words she'd never said. Forty years of trusting the sun as the one honest thing left in the world, and here it was, complicit in a century-old crime.

"That's not possible," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"It's entirely possible," Maren said gently. "It's just not permitted. There's a difference. The treaty magic didn't move the valley—it moved the *agreement* about the valley. The stone kept its own counsel." She was writing as she talked, hunched against the wind, pencil scratching in a little notebook she guarded like a child. "Light is just information. The valley is still telling the truth about itself. The sun is simply delivering mail to an address the empire says doesn't exist."

Corvin had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when something mattered—which was rare enough that Ansel noticed. He stood looking at the falling shadows with his arms crossed, and when he finally spoke, his voice had an odd grain in it, almost reverent.

"My grandmother used to sing a song about a valley where evening came twice," he said. "I always thought it was nonsense. A smuggler's lullaby." He rubbed his jaw. "She sang it in the old tongue. I never knew what the words meant until Maren started teaching me."

"What did they mean?" Ansel asked.

He was silent a moment. "'The door is where the day forgets itself,'" he said. "That's the chorus, anyway."

Maren's pencil stopped moving.

"Well then," she said softly. "We're close."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen crag, and Ansel took first watch because she did not trust herself to sleep.

The stars were wrong too. She lay back against her pack and found the constellations she'd steered by her whole life—the Shepherd, the Broken Wheel, the seven cold sisters of the Frost Crown—and they were all present, all correct, except that the whole sky seemed very slightly rotated, as if some enormous hand had gripped the heavens and turned them a degree counterclockwise and let go. No one else seemed able to see it. She tested Corvin once, pointing, asking him what he made of the Wheel's position, and he squinted and said it looked fine to him, why?

Only she saw it. Or perhaps only she was honest enough about her own memory to admit what she saw.

*You know every path through Thornfell,* her order had taught her, *and therefore you belong to no path.* That was the oath of invisibility—not a spell but a discipline, a way of being so unremarkable that the world slid past you without friction. Keepers were not hidden. Keepers were *unnoticed*, which was crueler and more useful. And the price of forty years of unnoticedness was that she had no one to tell about the tilted sky. No one whose eyes she trusted more than her own trained, solitary, possibly-mad judgment.

Except that wasn't true anymore, was it. There was a disgraced scholar asleep ten feet away with pencil smudges on her cheek, and a smuggler with Veylander blood in his veins dreaming by a banked fire, and both of them would believe her. Both of them had already believed her about worse.

The loneliness hadn't lifted, exactly. It had changed shape. It had become something with weight and warmth, like a stone pulled from a fire and held in both hands.

Near dawn, the singing started again.

It came up through the rock, faint as breath on a window: voices beneath the ice of the high snowfields, layered and slow, a melody without beginning or end. Ansel had heard it first four nights ago and had nearly put a knife in Corvin for startling her while it played. Now she just listened. The exiled dead—or the exiled living; Maren's translation of the first keeper's confession had rearranged that horror into something worse, an injustice wearing death's clothing—pressed upward against a seal their great-grandparents had built, singing to keep their courage up, or to keep time, or simply because a hundred years of waiting needs a sound.

The song was louder here. That was the thing she wrote in her own notebook, in her blunt keeper's hand, beside a sketch of the valley and its backward shadows: *louder here than anywhere. The seal is thin where the land disagrees.*

* * *

On the fourth day they found the wound.

There was no better word for it, and Maren, who had a word for everything, agreed that no better word existed.

It began as a wrongness in the air ahead of them—a shimmer, subtle as heat off summer stone, though the morning was cold enough to crack their water skins. Then a wrongness in the sound: their footsteps, which should have echoed off the enclosing walls of the pass, fell dead, swallowed, as though the mountain had laid wool over its ears. Then a wrongness in the light itself, a faint doubling at the edges of things, so that each boulder seemed to have a ghost of itself hanging a hair's breadth out of true.

And then the valley opened.

It was small—a bowl of green tucked between shoulders of granite, maybe half a league across, holding a stream that ran in a perfect circle with no source and no mouth. Wildflowers grew in it out of season, pale gold and deep violet, nodding in a wind that touched nothing else. And the shadows—the shadows of the surrounding peaks lay pooled in the bowl's center like spilled ink, dense and motionless at high noon, when every shadow in the world should have been short and sharp beneath its object.

The shadows in the bowl did not shorten. They waited.

"Oh," Maren said. It was barely a sound. She took off her spectacles, cleaned them on her sleeve with hands that trembled slightly, put them back on, and looked again. "Oh, it's *beautiful*. It's a scar in the agreement. Do you see? The treaty magic couldn't fully overwrite terrain this old, so it—it folded it instead. Folded it out of the world's accounting. Everything here is still exactly where it was a century ago, and the world flows around it like water around a standing stone." Her voice cracked with something between laughter and grief. "A hundred years I spent in court archives, and the truth was sitting in a valley growing flowers."

Corvin walked to the edge of the shadow-pool and stopped as though he'd struck glass. He knelt. Pressed his palm flat to the ground. His lips moved—soundlessly at first, then in a whisper Ansel caught fragments of: the old tongue, the language of a country that no longer existed, spoken to soil that apparently remembered it.

"She's saying the same thing the song says," he reported, when he rose. His eyes were wet and he didn't bother hiding it. "Under the ice. Word for word. This valley is the other side of the door. This is where they'd come out."

Ansel stepped forward alone.

The others let her. Perhaps they understood—Corvin with his inherited blood, Maren with her borrowed books—that this part belonged to her. Nine generations of keepers had sworn the oath over the Hollow Gate, and whatever door stood in this valley, it was the twin of theirs, the far end of the same wound, and the last of that line was her.

She walked to the center of the bowl, to the edge of the pooled shadows, and the moment her boot crossed that invisible line the world *tilted*.

Not the ground—the ground was firm, flower-soft, real. But everything else lurched: the sky swung, the light doubled, and for one vertiginous heartbeat Ansel saw the valley twice—once as it was, green and blooming and wrong, and once as it must have been a century ago, terraced and walled, orchards on the slopes, smoke rising from chimneys, people. People everywhere, brown-skinned and bright-clothed, turning toward her across the intervening years as if she were the intruder in their ordinary afternoon.

Then it was gone. Flowers. Silence. The shadow-pool, patient as a held breath.

She stood shaking at the edge of understanding, and understood, finally, what Maren had been trying to teach her since the wine cellar: the door out of Veyland was not a place. Places could be mapped, and mapped things could be erased. The door was a *wound*—a disagreement the size of a kingdom, pressed flat and hidden, aching under a century of lies. You did not find it on a map. You found it by learning to read the land against itself, by trusting the shadows over the surveyors, the streams over the decrees, the singing under the snow over a hundred years of official silence.

Behind her, hooves—no. Boots. Many boots, on scree, descending the pass they'd climbed that morning.

Corvin reached her first, hand on his knife, face drained. "Smoke signal from the ridge," he said. "Two days early. Maybe three." He swallowed. "Serane's found the valley, Ansel. She's coming down with the sappers right now."

Maren clutched the books to her chest. The shadow-pool lay still and secret at Ansel's feet, full of a country's worth of waiting.

The singing beneath the earth swelled—once, briefly, joyfully—as if the singers, too, had heard the boots on the stone.
