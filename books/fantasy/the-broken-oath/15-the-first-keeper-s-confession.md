## 15. The First Keeper's Confession

The book did not want to be read.

Maren Quill had spent thirty years coaxing reluctant texts open—palimpsests scraped twice over, ledgers waterlogged in cellar floods, a treaty once burned and sewn back together with horsehair thread. She knew the way old paper resisted, the way ink retreated from the eye like a shy animal. But this was different. The final book—the third they had carried out of her burning cellar, wrapped in Ansel's oilcloth while the century of her life's work went up in cinders behind them—seemed to resist not out of age but out of *shame*.

It lay on the flat stone between them now, in the hollow beneath an overhang where the smugglers' road bent away from the valley, sheltered from the wind that came down off Thornfell in long white ropes. Corvin had built a fire small enough to hide in his cupped hands and fed it twigs one at a time, so that its light reached no farther than the circle of their knees. Ansel sat opposite, still as she always sat, as if stillness were a discipline she practiced the way other people practiced prayer. Snow ticked against the overhang above them. Somewhere below, too far to see, the world went on believing Veyland had never existed.

Maren peeled back the cover with both hands, the way she imagined one might peel back a bandage from a wound that had been healing wrong for a hundred years.

The binding was vellum over boards, cracked along the spine, stitched with cord gone the color of old teeth. The first forty pages were what she expected: inventories. Grain tallies. Lamp-oil. The number of candles allotted to each watch of the gate. A keeper's hand, careful and unlovely, recording the small arithmetic of solitude. She recognized that hand. It matched the marginalia in the archive copies of the founding oath—the same cramped letters, the same habit of pressing so hard the nib tore through in places, as though the writer could not decide whether the words mattered more than the page could bear.

*Edric Vane,* she thought. *First keeper of the Hollow Gate.* The name surfaced from her own scholarship like a body rising in dark water. She had written four essays about him. She had called him *the faithful warden* in two of them.

She turned the pages with a bone folder, because her fingers were shaking and she did not trust them.

* * *

It began, as these things always begin, with weather.

*"The winter of the sealing was mild,"* Edric Vane wrote, *"which I have come to believe was a mercy shown to us so that we would have no excuse."*

Maren read the line three times. Around her, the fire popped; Corvin shifted; Ansel's breath made a slow silver ghost in the cold. None of them spoke. There was a quality to certain silences, Maren had learned long ago in the court archives—a silence that meant everyone present understood, without discussion, that something irreversible was happening.

*"They told me the gate was built against a plague,"* the confession continued. *"Then they told me it was built against sorcery. Then, when I had sworn and been fed and given my lamp and my keys, they told me nothing at all, which is how I learned that the first two stories were also lies."*

Ansel made a sound—not quite a word. Maren looked up. In the firelight the keeper's face was very pale, and her eyes were fixed on the page as if it were a snake she had found coiled in her own bed.

"Keep reading," Ansel said quietly.

Maren kept reading.

*"On the ninth night I heard them singing under the stone. Not ghosts. Ghosts do not sing in parts. Ghosts do not know the words better than you do. I went down into the foundations with my lamp and stood on the seal-stone, and the singing came up through my boots and through my knees and into my chest, and I understood that whatever was beneath me was not dead, because the dead do not need to breathe between verses."*

Corvin had stopped pretending to tend the fire. His hands hung motionless around the stick he held, and his face had gone strange—not frightened, Maren thought. Worse than frightened. *Recognizing.*

*"I asked my superior, Warden-Captain Ilsette, what lay beneath. She said: 'A country.' I laughed. She did not. She said: 'You think we erased them with armies? Armies leave graves, and graves leave questions. We erased them with paper and with the working, and the working needed somewhere to put what it took. The gate is not a wall, keeper. It is a pocket turned inside out. Everything of Veyland that the treaty unmade—the people, the fields, the songs, the unborn—all of it is folded into the mountain, and your oath is the fold.'"*

Maren had to set the book down then. Her hands were not merely shaking; they were *ringing*, the way a bell rings after it has been struck, and she pressed them flat against the cold stone until the trembling slowed.

She had spent her career among the victors' papers. She had catalogued the treaties, the proclamations, the elegant diplomatic language by which a kingdom the size of a small sea-coast had been struck from every map and record within a single generation. She had always assumed the erasure was bureaucratic—brutal, yes, but bureaucratic. Names changed, borders redrawn, histories rewritten. People scattered and absorbed, or killed in the ordinary ways that killing is done.

She had never imagined erasure as a *craft*. As a working performed on reality itself, with a container built to hold the overflow.

"They didn't destroy Veyland," she said aloud, and her voice came out rusted. "They *filed* it."

* * *

The middle pages were worse, because they were practical.

Edric Vane had been a meticulous man, and shame had not loosened his hand. He recorded the ration schedules for the sealed population—which was to say, he recorded how the victors had decided, year by year, how much life to allow the people they had buried alive in the folds of a mountain. He recorded the deliveries: grain that arrived through some mechanism he described only as *the tithe-door*, lowered into the dark at intervals, never opened wide enough for anything larger than a sack or smaller than a family to pass.

He recorded the petitions.

This was the part Maren would carry for the rest of her life, she thought. Longer, if there was a longer.

*"They send up papers,"* he wrote. *"Petitions, properly drawn, in proper legal form, addressed to a crown that declared them never to have existed. They write on birch bark and on skins and once, heartbreakingly, on the back of a child's lesson page. They cite laws. They cite the old compacts. They ask for recognition, for trade, for the return of their exiles' names to the rolls. Last month they asked only to be acknowledged. This month they asked only to be counted. I am commanded to burn all such documents unread. I have begun instead to keep them. If I am ever brought before any judgment, human or otherwise, let this book be my testimony: they were never silent. We simply agreed, all of us, every keeper in every generation, to call their voices the wind."*

Ansel rose abruptly and walked to the edge of the overhang. She stood looking out at the snowfall with her back to them, and even from behind, Maren could see what the posture cost her—the rigid shoulders, the hands clenched at her sides, the controlled breathing of someone who has learned that breaking apart is a luxury scheduled for later, if ever.

"The singing," Ansel said, without turning. "Under the ice, in the high pass. You said it was the exiled dead."

"I said what the records said," Maren answered gently. "What the records said was written by the victors."

"And the prophecy book in the gate—" Ansel's voice was level in the way ice is level, right up until it isn't. "The date. Tonight's date. What does tonight actually mark?"

Maren looked down at the confession, at the final pages she had not yet dared, and felt the answer waiting there like a fall she had already begun.

"I think," she said, "we should finish the book first."

* * *

The last pages were written in a different season of the man's life. The hand had aged, grown looser, more urgent, as though Edric Vane had finally understood that no one else would ever say these things and that his remaining time was a candle burning at both ends.

*"Let me set down plainly what I spent fifty years refusing to set down plainly."*

*"There is no evil in the mountain."*

*"I have listened for half a century to what the oath calls the sealed horror, and I will tell you what I have heard: lullabies. Arguments. Weddings, by the sound of the drums. Once, a funeral, and the whole deep of the mountain sang the mourning verses so beautifully that I sat down on the seal-stone and wept like a fool. There are children down there who have grown old. There are children born down there who have never seen a sky, and when they sing of the sun they sing of it the way we sing of heaven—as a thing promised, withheld, believed in anyway."*

*"We did not imprison a monster. We imprisoned a nation, because a nation's existence embarrassed us. Because their fields were better than our fields and their law was older than our law and their king had signed a treaty our king regretted signing, and it is easier to unmake a people than to admit a mistake. So we called them a horror. We taught our children to shudder at the mountains. We built an order of keepers and swore us to invisibility—not to protect the secret from the world, but to protect the lie from examination. No one guards a truth this carefully. Truths can afford witnesses."*

*"And here is the last thing, the thing I would carve into my own bones if books could burn: the seal is failing. Not because they are breaking it. Because it was never built to hold living people forever—it was built to hold a memory, and memories fade, and what is fading is the working itself. When it fails—and it will fail, in my successors' time or their successors', the arithmetic is not difficult—it will not release a horror. It will release ten thousand starving, furious, grieving, *alive* human beings into a world that has spent a century learning to fear them as monsters."*

*"Unless someone tells the truth first."*

*"Keeper who comes after me—I know you. I was you. I know you love the paths and the quiet and the pride of being the one who knows. I know you think your oath is a wall and you are its stone. Hear an old man who was the first stone: your oath is a muzzle, and it was fitted to your mouth before you were born. Take it off. Tell the truth while there is still a world left to tell it to. Let them out as neighbors, not as invaders. It is the only ending to this story in which anyone survives it with their soul."*

*"I did not dare. God forgive me, I loved my quiet too. But I have written it all here, and hidden it where only a keeper would look, and prayed—yes, prayed, though the order forbids it—that whoever finds this will be braver than I was."*

Below that, one final line, in a hand gone suddenly frail:

*"The dead under the snow are not dead. Forgive us. We buried the living and called it peace."*

* * *

No one spoke for a long time.

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its bed of ash. Snow hissed softly against the stone above them. Maren became aware, distantly, that she was crying—not dramatically, not with any sound, just tears running steadily down her face and dripping onto the vellum, and she turned her head aside so they would not stain the pages. A scholar learns early to protect the text from her own grief.

Corvin broke the silence, and his voice was not the smuggler's drawl she had come to expect. It was quieter. Younger.

"What songs," he said. "What were the mourning verses. What were the *words*."

Maren looked at him. He was staring into the fire, and his jaw was working, and his hands—his clever, quick, dishonest hands—were perfectly still in his lap, which she had learned was how Corvin Hale looked when something true had hold of him.

"You know them," she said. It was not quite a question.

"My grandmother sang them." His voice was rough. "Called them hearth-songs. Said they came from over the mountains, from before. I thought—gods, I thought she meant folk tunes. Everyone's grandmother has folk tunes." He laughed, one short broken note with no humor in it at all. "She wasn't from *over* the mountains. She was from *under* them. Or her mother was. Someone walked out through the tithe-door, or someone was passed out, or—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I've been smuggling myself home for ten years and didn't know it."

Ansel had come back from the edge of the overhang at some point without Maren noticing. She stood at the firelight's border, and the light touched only half her face, and on that half was an expression Maren had no courtly vocabulary for. It was not horror anymore. Horror had burned off in the reading, the way fog burns off, and what remained underneath was harder and colder and cleaner.

Injustice. That was the word. Maren had spent her life among injustices filed and catalogued and footnoted, and she knew the difference between the ones that sadden you and the ones that *recruit* you. This was the second kind. She could see it taking hold of Ansel Voss the way frost takes hold of a window—silently, completely, from the edges inward.

"The prophecy date," Ansel said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. "The date in the foundation book. Tonight."

"The seal's collapse," Maren said. "Or the beginning of it. Edric's arithmetic was right—it's failing, and failing faster now, the way rot fails. The date isn't a door opening, Ansel. It's a dam cracking. And everything on the other side of it is *people*."

"People Serane wants to bury under a thousand tons of rock," Corvin said.

"People the world has been taught to kill on sight," Maren said.

Ansel was quiet for a moment. Then she crouched, took the confession from Maren's hands with unexpected gentleness, and closed it.

"Then we're out of time," she said, "and we're done being invisible. Which way?"

Maren looked at the fire, at the snow, at the two impossible allies the ruin of her life had left her, and gave the only honest answer she had.

"That depends," she said, "on whether we can find a door that isn't on any map—in a country whose geography was rewritten to make sure it couldn't be found. I'll need the land itself to testify. Mountains remember differently than rivers do. And Ansel—" she met the keeper's eyes, "—no one alive knows what these mountains remember better than you."

Far off in the high passes, faint beneath the wind, the singing rose again—and this time, none of them mistook it for ghosts.
