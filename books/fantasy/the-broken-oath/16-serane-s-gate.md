## 16. Serane's Gate

The valley had no name on any map Serane Doss carried, and she had carried every map the Crown possessed.

She stood at its mouth in the hour before dawn, breath smoking, and watched the light come down the eastern walls of Thornfell the way water comes down a drain—slowly, reluctantly, pooling last in the low places. The peaks above were wrong. She had known it from three miles out, from the moment the road bent and gave her her first look at them: they leaned. Not visibly, not enough that a surveyor would flag it, but the eye knew, the way an animal knows a snare before it sees the wire. The mountains here remembered being something else, and their remembering showed through like bone beneath skin.

"Ma'am." Lieutenant Brakk, at her shoulder, his voice pitched for her alone. "Sappers are asking how deep you want the charges."

"As deep as the mountain will allow," she said. "And then deeper."

He hesitated. He was a good officer—obedient, unimaginative, which in her experience amounted to the same thing—and he had learned that when she gave an order twice, she did not want to be asked about it once. He went back down the column, boots crunching frost, and left her alone with the valley and with the thing she had been carrying for thirty years, which weighed more than any of her soldiers' packs and could not be set down.

Below her, where the valley floor narrowed to a throat of grey stone, there was a door.

It did not look like a door. That was the first thing she had thought when the smuggler's confession had finally given her the location, cross-checked against three burned maps and one hanged man's dying directions. It looked like nothing at all—a seam in the rock face, a shadow too straight, a coldness in the air that made the frost grow inward instead of outward, ferning toward the stone as if the winter itself were being pulled inside. Her scouts had walked within ten paces of it and reported only "a wall, ma'am," and she had not corrected them, because she did not want them standing near it long enough to see what she saw.

She saw it because she had seen one before.

* * *

She had been nine years old, and the gate at Karst Hollow had been her father's responsibility, the way this one had belonged to keepers whose names the Crown had let rot out of its records.

Her father had been a warden of the third rank, which meant he checked the wards twice a day and drank once, in the evening, and never more than two cups, and between those disciplines he had raised a family in a stone house with blue shutters that her mother painted every spring. Serane could still smell that paint. Linseed oil and white lead, sharp and sweet, and her mother's hands stained ghost-pale for weeks, and her mother laughing, saying, *a house should look like it expects good weather.* They had expected good weather for years. The gate held; the wards sang their thin note at dusk; the world outside Karst Hollow went on believing that the hollow contained nothing but a shrine and some old stones.

Then, in the autumn she turned nine, the note changed.

She had not known then what the note meant. She knew now—the wardens' manual, which she had read cover to cover by candlelight in the years after, in the years when reading it was the only thing that kept her from walking into the sea—said the wards were tuned to the seal's integrity, and that a change in pitch meant a change in pressure, and that a change in pressure meant something on the other side was pushing. What the manual did not say, what no manual said, was how fast the pushing could become breaking. Her father had sent the signal. The relief wardens were four days out. The seal lasted two.

What came through was not an army. That was the detail no one ever believed, when she told it, and so eventually she stopped telling it. It was not an army, not a horde, not a flood of darkness such as ballads describe. It was eleven figures, gaunt and grey-wrapped, moving out of the broken gate into the blue evening with the terrible unhurried patience of people who have waited a very long time and intend to enjoy what comes next. She had watched them from the loft window while her mother shoved her down behind the hay and told her, in a whisper Serane would hear for the rest of her life, *don't look, don't make a sound, whatever you hear, don't—*

She looked. She heard.

They took the livestock first, methodically, and then they took the neighbors, house by house, and the sounds the neighbors made were sounds she would not write down even in her private journal, even now, even with thirty years and a rank and a reputation built deliberately on being the sort of woman who felt nothing. And then they came to the house with the blue shutters, and her mother stopped painting the world forever, and her father died in the doorway of his own gate with his warden's iron in his hand, having done everything right, having followed every rule, having been exactly the kind of careful, faithful, disciplined man the order required—and it had not mattered. Not a whit of it mattered. The seal failed and the careful man died and the girl in the hayloft lived because she was small and silent and the exiles had already taken eleven souls and seemed, in their grey patience, satisfied.

The relief wardens arrived on the fourth day and found her sitting in the yard, nine years old, arranging her mother's paintbrushes in a row.

They asked her what she wanted. Everyone always asked children that, as if grief were a shop counter. She had said: *I want to learn what my father knew.* And the senior warden, a tired woman named Odile Vetch who had seen too many hollow houses, had knelt down to her eye level and said the sentence that made her:

*"Then learn this first, girl. What your father knew didn't save him. Learn better than knowing. Learn ending."*

* * *

"Ma'am."

Brakk again. She surfaced from the memory the way a swimmer surfaces—reluctantly, into colder air. The sun had cleared the eastern ridge and lay along the valley floor, and the door-shadow at the throat of the stone had not moved, and the frost still grew the wrong way.

"The sappers have placed the first ring," Brakk said. "Twelve charges at the face. Corven says—" he glanced back toward the camp, where a squat man with powder-blackened hands was arguing with two corporals over a coil of fuse, "—Corven says the rock here is strange. Softer than it looks. He thinks it will take the charge well."

"It had better take it perfectly," Serane said. "Because we will get one attempt."

"You're certain they're coming? The keeper and her companions?"

"I'm certain the prophecy is coming true," Serane said, "which is worse."

She had pieced it together over six weeks of interrogation, and the picture assembled itself in her mind now with the grim satisfaction of a lock accepting its key. A keeper at the Hollow Gate—an actual keeper, of the old line, hidden all these years in plain sight like a coin under a floorboard. A disgraced scholar, Maren Quill, whose name had been on the Inquisition's watch-lists since the Purge of the Archives, carrying books that should have burned. And a smuggler, Corvin Hale, whose file was thinner than it should have been, which meant someone had paid to keep it thin, which meant he was more than he appeared. Three people walking toward a door that a hundred years of treaty and erasure had tried to unmake, guided by a book written before the ink on the treaties was dry.

And a date. Tonight's date.

She had read the confiscated pages herself, or what remained of them after the fire at Quill's cellar—her own people had set that fire, and she had signed the order, and she had felt nothing, or had instructed herself to feel nothing, which in her experience amounted to the same thing. The surviving fragment spoke of the seal failing, of the dead pressing outward, of a keeper who must choose. The Church scholars who examined it wanted the word *dead* interpreted spiritually. Serane had looked at the word for a long time in the lamplight and thought of eleven grey figures walking patiently through blue evening light, and had written in the margin of her report, in her flat clerk's hand: *the dead are whatever comes through.*

"Ma'am," Brakk said, and now his voice had changed, gone careful. "There's something you should hear. From the ice."

She frowned. "Ice?"

"High up. The scouts on the north ridge report singing. Under the snowpack." He swallowed. "They're asking permission to come down off the ridge."

Serane closed her eyes. Of course. Of course it was beginning already—the seal failing meant failing everywhere at once, thinning like ice on a spring pond, and sound traveled through thin places. She had read the old wardens' accounts. *Voices under the snow* was practically a formula in them, appearing in the final entries of gate after gate, in handwriting that deteriorated page by page until the last entry was often just a date and a single word: *breach*, or *gone*, or, in one memorable case, *run*.

"Permission granted," she said. "Everyone off the high ground. I want the whole company below the tree line by noon."

"And the sappers?"

"Working. All day if they need it. We detonate tonight, before moonrise, whether the keeper has arrived or not." She opened her eyes and looked down the valley at the unmoving shadow in the stone. "Especially if she hasn't."

Brakk saluted and went. Serane remained, because someone had to stand at the mouth of this valley and look at the door, and she had learned long ago that looking was a discipline like any other. You practiced it until it stopped hurting. It had never entirely stopped hurting, but it had stopped showing, and in her profession that was the same achievement.

* * *

At midday she walked down to the face itself.

Her officers had begged her not to; she had ignored them with the serene efficiency of long practice. The sappers flattened themselves against the walls as she passed, superstitious, and she did not blame them. The air near the door was wrong in a way the body understood before the mind did—too still, too cold, tasting faintly of stone dust and something older underneath, something like the air in a tomb that has been sealed well. Ten paces out, the frost grew inward. Five paces out, her breath stopped smoking, as if the door were drinking even that.

Up close, the seam in the rock resolved into carving. Faint, worn nearly to invisibility, but carving: lines of script running vertically down both sides of the shadow, in an alphabet she recognized from exactly one place—the margins of the oldest confiscated documents, the ones the archivists called *pre-treaty gibberish*. Not gibberish. Veylander. The language of a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own name survived only in the past tense.

She stood before the door of the country that did not exist, and she hated it with the clean, nourishing hatred of thirty years.

People misunderstood her hatred—they always had. They assumed it was fear wearing armor, or ambition wearing grief, or simple cruelty of the kind the Inquisition attracted like flies. Let them assume. The truth was simpler and worse: Serane Doss had stood in a hayloft at nine years old and listened to the cost of mercy, and she had decided, in the way only a child can decide, absolutely and without appeal, that she would spend her life ensuring no one else ever had to listen to that. If the price of that was burning archives, hanging smugglers, collapsing doors on whatever—or whoever—waited behind them, then the price was the price. Grief was not a wound to be healed. Grief was a weapon to be kept sharp, and she had kept hers honed for three decades, and it had never once dulled, and it had never once turned in her hand.

Except—

Except that lately, in the quiet hours, a thought had begun to visit her that she did not welcome. It had arrived with the interrogations, with the keeper's file, with the fragmentary prophecy, and it sat in the corner of her mind like an uninvited guest: *eleven figures.* Eleven. Not hundreds. Not an army. Eleven gaunt, patient exiles, who took livestock and lives and then stopped, satisfied, as though they had taken precisely what they needed and no more. Monsters did not stop at eleven. Monsters did not move with the unhurried dignity of people keeping an appointment. Monsters did not leave a small girl alive in a hayloft when leaving her alive served no purpose at all.

She crushed the thought each time it came, the way her mother had crushed spiders against the blue-shuttered windowsill. But it kept coming back, and it had brought friends: the memory of Odile Vetch kneeling in the dirt, saying *learn ending*; the memory of the wardens' manual's final chapter, which she had read forty times, titled *On the Necessity of the Gates*, which justified everything in language so careful, so lawyerly, so evasive about what exactly was being kept in and why, that a suspicious mind might almost think the men who wrote it had been embarrassed.

Embarrassed. She had laughed aloud, alone in her tent, when that word surfaced. Embarrassed. As if a century of sealing had been not protection but shame.

No. She put the thought down with the others. Whatever the gates had been built for, whatever politics had shaped them, the fact remained: things came through. Things had come through Karst Hollow, and her mother's hands had been stained with paint, not blood, right up until the end, and no scholarship, no prophecy, no gaunt exile with a sad story would ever make that right or make her wrong.

Tonight the door would die. Tonight the mountain would close its throat. And if the keeper and her smuggler and her scholar arrived to find rubble, then they would grieve, and their grief would be their own education, and perhaps someday one of them would kneel in the dirt before a nine-year-old somewhere and say *learn ending,* and the work would continue, because the work always continued, because the world never ran out of doors that needed killing.

"Ma'am!" A runner, skidding down the scree, young enough that his voice cracked on the second syllable. "From the north ridge—the singing, ma'am—it's louder. And Corporal Eves says—" the boy swallowed, eyes wide, "—she says the voices are words. She says one of them keeps repeating the same phrase, over and over, and the translator-scholar we brought says it's old Veylander, and it means—"

"Say it."

"*We know you're there. Please. We know you're there.*"

The valley was very quiet. Somewhere above, faint as a hairline crack in glass, the singing threaded down through the snowpack, patient, grey, unbearably human, and Serane Doss stood before the door of the country that did not exist and found, to her fury, that her hands were not quite steady.

"Detonate at dusk," she said. "Not tonight. Dusk. Move up every schedule we have."

Because the seal was failing faster than the sappers could drill, and because she had heard that phrase before—not these words, but that tone, that patient pleading—and the last time she had waited to be sure, the waiting had cost the world a woman who painted her shutters blue.

She would not wait to be sure. She would end it first, and grieve after, in the order she had always kept: the sharp work, then the dull ache. Dusk. The mountain would fall at dusk.

And far up the southern pass, unseen in the gathering cloud, three small figures had already entered the valley.
