## 17. The Choice of Three Oaths

The valley had a name once. Maren said so, tracing the shape of it in the ash of their fire: *Vaelmoor*, the low country, the green threshold, the place where Veyland's exiles had been marched before the door was shut behind them. Now it was only a bowl of stone and old snow between two shoulders of mountain, and at its far end, half-swallowed by scree and shadow, stood the wound.

Ansel felt it before she saw it—the way she had learned to feel weather in her teeth, or the approach of strangers along a pass. A wrongness in the ground's hum. The Hollow Gate had always sung to her, faintly, a note held for a hundred years by keepers who never let it waver. This was that same note, but broken. Fractured into harmonics that did not agree with one another. The land itself could not decide whether it was sealed or open, and neither, Ansel realized with a cold settling in her chest, could she.

She stood at the valley's mouth while dawn came up gray behind them, and she looked at the door out of a country that no longer existed.

It did not look like a door. It looked like a scar—a seam in the mountainside where the rock's grain ran contrary to every other line of stone around it, as if the world had been stitched here by someone in a hurry and ashamed of the work. Snow refused to lie on it. Wind bent around it. And beneath it, deep and rhythmic as a heartbeat heard through a wall, came the singing.

They had been hearing it for three days now. Not voices, exactly—more like the memory of voices, worn smooth by a century of pressing against the inside of the seal. But sometimes, when the wind dropped and the fire burned low, Ansel could almost make out words. Lullabies. Names. Once, horribly, a counting—someone counting children, over and over, the way a mother counts to be sure.

"They're closer," Corvin said beside her. He spoke quietly, the way he'd learned to speak around her silences. "The singing. Two nights ago you couldn't hear it past the fire. Now it carries across the whole valley."

"I know."

"And the seal?"

Ansel closed her eyes and listened the way her mother had taught her, palm flat against cold air, reading the gate's note the way other people read weather. The tone was thinning. Going reedy. Like a bell about to crack.

"Days," she said. "Maybe less. Maybe hours." She opened her eyes. "It won't fail gently, Corvin. The oath wasn't built to come apart. It was built to hold until it couldn't—and then to break all at once, like ice on a thawing river. Everything behind it comes through at once. Everything."

Corvin looked at the scar for a long moment. Behind them, Maren sat wrapped in both their cloaks, still coughing from the waystation smoke, her three rescued books spread on an oilcloth like patients on a table. She hadn't slept. None of them had, not really, not since the ambush—not since learning what Corvin had done, and what he had undone, and how narrowly both had missed killing them all.

"Then we choose," Corvin said. "That's why we came. That's why you came."

"Yes," Ansel said. And then, because lying to him had become harder than telling the truth: "I don't know how."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar, close enough to the scar that the singing threaded through everything they said, far enough that Maren insisted the first waves of whatever came through would wash past them. Ansel doubted there would be gentle waves. Every keeper's journal she had ever read agreed on one point: the seal was not a wall but a dam, and dams do not leak politely.

Maren laid out the first keeper's confession, open to the pages they had spent two nights deciphering, her fingers trembling with cold and something else. "Say it again," she said. "All of it. I want to hear it in your voice, not mine. I've been reading it so long I can't tell anymore whether I'm understanding it or just wanting to."

So Ansel read aloud, in the flat careful voice she used for oaths:

*"I was the first to swear, and I will be the first to say what we swore. We did not lock away monsters. We locked away a people whose fields fed our armies and whose existence shamed our kings. When the treaty unmade Veyland, it did not unmake the Veylanders. We wrote them out of the maps and then we wrote them out of the world, and we called the prison a mercy because the alternative was admitting what we had done. If any keeper reads this and feels the seal strain against her hand, let her know: what pushes is not evil. What pushes is the wronged, asking to be let out. May whatever god keeps count forgive us. I do not expect He will."*

Silence, except the singing.

"My order knew," Ansel said. Her voice came out strange, distant, like someone else's. "Every keeper for a hundred years. They knew, and they handed down the oath anyway, and they taught us to call it vigilance. My mother sang me to sleep with the litany of the seal. *Hold fast, hold true, hold the line.* She never told me what the line was holding back. Or maybe—" and this was the thought that had been circling her all night like a bird of prey—"maybe she didn't know either. Maybe each generation only got the oath, not the truth, and the truth died with the first keeper's confession in a niche in the foundation where no one would ever look."

"Until tonight's date was written in a book a century ago," Maren said softly. "Whoever wrote that prophecy knew the seal had a lifespan. Knew there would be a keeper standing here when it ran out. This moment was always coming, Ansel. You were always going to stand in this valley with a choice in your hands."

"That's not comfort, Maren."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Corvin crouched by the fire, feeding it a stick, and for once he had nothing glib to offer. That frightened Ansel more than the inquisitors had. Corvin Hale without words was a man facing something too large for charm.

* * *

She walked the valley alone while the others slept—or pretended to. It was what she did; it was the only ritual she had left. Keeper of the Hollow Gate, inspector of paths, reader of stone. Except the Hollow Gate was a day's march behind her, and this valley had never been hers to walk, and every step she took on ground that appeared on no map felt like trespass and homecoming at once.

The sun climbed. The singing grew louder. And near midday, from the eastern rim of the valley, she saw them.

Soldiers. A long thin line of them, descending in disciplined silence, and ahead of them a figure in gray who moved like someone who had walked these kinds of slopes before—inquisitor's robes pinned up for climbing, a satchel slung across her back that Ansel did not need Maren's scholarship to identify. Sappers' tools. Charges. Serane Doss had reached the valley first after all.

Ansel pressed herself into the shadow of a boulder and watched, and her heart did a strange thing: it did not race. It slowed. Because watching Serane descend toward the scar, Ansel understood suddenly and completely that there were not two futures waiting in this valley. There were three, and she had known them all her life without knowing it, the way you know a song before you've heard it played.

*Uphold the oath.* Do nothing. Let the seal run out its century and burst, and everything behind it—every exile, every soul, guilty and innocent and counting-children mothers—comes through in a single catastrophic tide, and the world above meets them with soldiers and terror and a war to finish what the treaty started. The oath kept. The country drowned in its own dead.

*Side with Serane.* Help her set her charges. Bury the door forever under a thousand tons of collapsed mountain, seal the innocent in with the guilty, end the singing with stone. Mercy, Serane would call it. Ansel had seen what escaped Serane's last gate. She understood the wound. But she had also read the first keeper's confession, and she knew that burying the innocent with the guilty was not mercy—it was the original crime, committed again, with better explosives.

*Or break the oath.* Open the door herself. Deliberately. On her terms, with words she would have to find, words no keeper had ever spoken because no keeper had ever dared. Let the exiles cross in order, in peace, seen and named instead of bursting through unseen and monstrous. And pay whatever price the breaking cost—an oath was not a rope to be untied; it was a root, grown through a keeper's whole life, and tearing it out would take things with it.

Three oaths. Keep the old one and kill a country twice. Swear a new one to Serane and become the first keeper to murder her own charge. Or break faith with a hundred years of mothers singing litanies, and trust that the wronged, given a door, will walk through it rather than tear down the house.

No keeper should carry this alone. The thought arrived with such force that Ansel stopped walking. That was the design, she realized. That was the cruelty hidden inside the kindness of her order. Solitude was not a side effect of the oath of invisibility—it was the mechanism. A keeper who belonged to no one could consult no one. Could be swayed by no one. Could stand at the door with the choice in her hands and have absolutely nowhere to put it down.

Her mother had died alone at her post. Her mother's mother likewise. A hundred years of women dying alone at doors, each certain she was the only soul in the world who understood the weight, each forbidden by the very vow she'd sworn from ever sharing it.

"Well," Ansel said aloud, to the valley, to the singing, to whoever had written tonight's date in dust a century ago. "You were wrong about one thing. I'm not alone. I brought a smuggler and a disgraced scholar, and I'm going to be the first keeper in history to ask them what they think."

* * *

Serane's column reached the valley floor by late afternoon and began setting camp with the crisp economy of people who intended to work through the night. Ansel watched from the rocks as sappers unloaded the charges, as Serane herself walked the length of the scar with her head tilted, listening, her face wearing an expression Ansel recognized because she had worn it her whole life: the look of someone listening to a sound no one else can hear, and being believed by no one.

"She'll fire them at dawn," Maren murmured. She had come up beside Ansel without sound; the scholar moved like a woman used to not being noticed, and Ansel thought, not for the first time, that they were more alike than either admitted. "Sappers prefer dawn light. And she'll want it done before the seal fails on its own—she's not stupid. She knows she's racing the collapse."

"Then we go down tonight."

"To do what?" Corvin's voice was carefully neutral. "Talk to her? She burned the smugglers' camps, Ansel. She interrogated my people. She's not a woman you argue with."

"No," Ansel said. "We're not going down to talk to Serane."

She turned to face them both, and the singing rose around them, and for the first time since she'd found the book in the foundation, the dread in her chest was quiet. In its place was something older and heavier and cleaner. Purpose, maybe. Or just the particular clarity that comes when every path but one has been taken away.

"We're going down to the door. All three of us. While her sappers are still drilling their holes, we're going to stand in front of the scar, and I'm going to do the one thing my order spent a hundred years making sure no keeper would ever know how to do."

Maren went very still. "Break the oath."

"Rewrite it." Ansel looked at the scar, at the snow that would not lie upon it, at the place where the world had been stitched badly and ashamed. "The first keeper confessed because she wanted someone, someday, to fix what she'd done. The prophecy named me because someone had to be standing here when the century ran out—with the truth in one hand and the choice in the other. Serane wants to bury them. The old oath wants to drown us. And those people under the snow have been counting their children for a hundred years."

She took a breath, and found, to her surprise, that her hands were steady.

"So I'm going to open the door. Deliberately. With witnesses. With words. And if the price of breaking an oath is what I think it is—" she touched the hollow place below her collarbone, where the invisibility lived, the quiet gift that had been her pride and her cage for her entire life, "—then I'll pay it. But I'll pay it my way. Not Serane's. Not the founders'. Mine."

Corvin studied her for a long moment. Then he grinned—that crooked, reckless grin that had gotten them thrown out of a toll bridge and saved their lives at a waystation, sometimes in the same hour. "Well then, Keeper. Best start thinking about your wording. I hear oaths are binding."

Below them, in the gathering dark, the sappers' drills began to bite into the mountain. And beneath everything, growing stronger with every passing minute, the singing rose—no longer worn smooth, no longer distant, but urgent now, almost articulate, a hundred years of patience finally daring to hope.

Ansel pulled her cloak tight and started down the slope toward the door, and behind her, in the ruins of a country that no longer existed, something that had waited a century for a keeper with a mind of her own began, very softly, to sing her name.
