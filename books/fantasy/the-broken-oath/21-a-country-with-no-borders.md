## 21. A Country With No Borders

The tent smelled of ink and wet wool, which Maren Quill had come to regard as the two truest smells of the new world. Outside, the refugee camp of Vey's Rest spread down the valley like a spilled satchel—hundreds of canvas shelters, cookfires smoking in the gray morning, children running between the rows with the particular ferocity of children who had spent a century underground and intended to make up for it. She could hear them even through the canvas. She could hear everything now. That was what being restored meant: everyone wanted to talk to her.

She dipped her pen, hesitated, and wrote:

*Day thirty-one since the crossing. The treaty delegation from Cael Marren arrived at dusk yesterday, four wagons and a herald with a voice like a struck bell. They have given the exiles a name for purposes of record—the Displaced of Veyland—which is a diplomat's way of saying we still do not know what to call you, but we would like this to be over by winter.*

She stopped. Crossed out *we*. She was not one of the Displaced; she had never been under the mountain. And yet for thirty-one days she had written their chronicle as though she belonged to them, and no one had corrected her, and she suspected no one would. A disgraced scholar spends years learning that history is written by whoever survives the burning. She had survived a burning. It seemed she had been adopted by its survivors in the bargain.

*The woman who wears the broken crown—she has asked us not to use her name until the treaty is signed, saying a name is a claim and claims must wait their turn—has begun holding audience each morning beneath the black pine at the camp's edge. She receives petitions the way rain receives the ground: patiently, without argument, letting each grievance soak in. Yesterday an old man wept before her for an hour about an orchard. There is no orchard anymore. There has been no orchard for a hundred years. She listened anyway.*

Maren set down the pen and flexed her hand. Through the tent's open flap she could see the pine, and the small crowd gathered beneath it, and beyond them the scar in the world where the Hollow Gate had stood—or rather where it had *not* stood, because that was the thing no one's eye could accept. The mountain had closed over the wound like water closing over a stone. Where Ansel Voss had held her ground against fire and falling rock, there was now only a green slope, unremarkable, already growing grass. Maren had walked up there twice and felt nothing, which frightened her more than feeling something would have. A country had poured through that slope. A keeper had nearly died holding the door. The earth had simply tidied itself.

Scholars, she thought, are trained to distrust tidy things.

She returned to the page.

*Corvin Hale has taken employment, if you can call it that, guiding the first parties of exiles over the southern passes to the lowland towns where work is being found for them. He walks openly now. I keep writing that phrase because I cannot get over it. He wears his smuggler's coat with the collar turned down, and he greets toll-keepers by name, and twice I have watched him stop on a road he once traveled only at night and simply stand there, looking at the view, like a man reading a letter he'd only ever heard read aloud.*

*He does not speak of the waystation. Neither does Ansel. I have decided this is not my chronicle's business, except to note that he came back, and that coming back is the whole of some men's redemption, and that Ansel let him walk beside her on the road down from the pass, which from her is a pardon sealed in wax.*

A shadow crossed the flap. Maren looked up expecting the herald's clerk, who had been pestering her daily for certified copies of the crossing records. Instead it was a girl of perhaps twelve, one of the born-under children, holding a battered tin cup as though it were evidence in a trial.

"You're the writer," the girl said. Not a question. Everyone knew the writer; the writer was the person you brought things to.

"I'm a chronicler," Maren said, "which is a writer with delusions of accuracy."

"The crown lady said you write down what happened. Under the snow." The girl's knuckles were white around the cup. "I sang. Down there. When they told us to sing so the ones outside would know we were alive. Did—" Her voice did something young and terrible. "Did anyone hear us?"

Maren put down her pen entirely. "I heard you," she said. "High in the passes, under the ice. We all did. It's in the chronicle. Day eleven of our journey: *voices beneath the snow, singing.*" She reached across the table and touched the girl's wrist. "You were heard. I made certain of it. That's what a chronicle is for."

The girl looked at her for a long moment, deciding whether to believe her, the way children decide—with their whole bodies—and then nodded once and left, and Maren sat back and discovered her eyes were hot and her hand was shaking slightly, and that she would need to write nothing further for a while.

* * *

She found Ansel at midday, as she had learned to find her: not at the center of anything, but at the edge of it, doing a small useful task with great concentration. Today the task was splitting firewood for the infirmary tent, and a queue of people had formed nearby hoping to speak with her, and Ansel was splitting wood with the grim efficiency of a woman who understood that axes discouraged conversation better than words did.

It had become the camp's strangest spectacle. The Keeper of the Hollow Gate—the woman who had broken a century-old oath aloud on a mountainside while demolition charges burned their fuses toward her—was famous. There had been a broadsheet in the lowlands, Maren had seen it: a woodcut of Ansel standing on a threshold of light, sword raised, looking nothing whatsoever like herself. People came to Vey's Rest partly for the exiles and partly, shamelessly, to see her. They left offerings at the green slope. Someone had tied ribbons to a fence post near where the gate's foundation stones had lain, and the ribbons multiplied weekly.

Ansel hated every ribbon individually.

"Maren," she said, without turning, because she always knew who was behind her—old habit, the habit of a woman who had spent her life listening for footsteps on stone. "Tell them I'm busy."

"They can see you're busy. That's why they're waiting. Waiting feels like devotion."

The axe bit into the block. "I was invisible for twenty years," Ansel said. "Do you know how much of my oath was just—that? Not magic. Not binding. Just practice. You make yourself small and quiet and after a while the world agrees to look past you." She straightened, wiped her forehead with her sleeve. "Now there's a fence with ribbons on it. Now the herald wants me at the signing ceremony. *At the front, Keeper Voss.* Me. At a front."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him someone has to watch the woodpile." She almost smiled. It was a real smile underneath, Maren could see it, but she kept it rationed like hardtack. "I'm learning to bear it. That's the honest answer. Some mornings I stand where they can see me and let them look, and it's like standing in cold water—I can do it, but I count."

"Count what?"

"How long until I can go back to being no one." She paused, and Maren watched her consider whether to say the next part, and then say it anyway, which was new for her too. "Except I don't want to go back. That's the strange thing. I spent twenty years content, or what I thought was content, and now I know the difference, and I can't unknow it." She gestured with the axe at the camp, the smoke, the children, the whole improbable settlement of a country that officially did not exist. "Being known isn't the same as being caught. Corvin told me that once, on a road, when I didn't believe him."

"And now?"

"Now I believe him. Most days." She set the axe down. "The herald also wants your books, by the way. Certified copies. The treaty needs a record of what Veyland was, and apparently you're the only scholar alive who's read the primary sources."

"Because they burned all the others."

"Because they burned all the others," Ansel agreed gently. "Which makes you not a disgrace anymore. Have you noticed? Nobody here says *disgraced court scholar.* They say *our chronicler.*"

Maren had noticed. She had noticed and had not known what to do with it, the way a woman who has carried a heavy pack for years does not know what to do with her shoulders when it comes off. "I'm writing it all down," she said. "Everything. The confession of the first keeper. What the oath really said. What Serane Doss did when she lowered her sword. Especially that. Future readers will need to know that mercy is possible at the end of a long anger."

Ansel was quiet a moment. "How is she? Serane."

"In the lowlands, under physician's care. They say she sleeps badly and asks about the exiles' rations." Maren smiled thinly. "Grief doesn't vanish, Ansel. It just changes what it guards."

* * *

Evening brought the herald's clerk again, and with him a complication Maren had been dreading: the question of the land itself. The green slope. The valley where the door had been. The treaty draft, the clerk explained apologetically, contained a clause declaring the site of the former Hollow Gate a protected memorial, off-limits to settlement, its exact boundaries to be determined by survey.

"Surveyed by whom?" Maren asked.

"Well—cartographers, madam. The Crown's cartographers."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. "Master Clerk, do you know why Veyland appeared on no map? Not because it was hidden. Because when the country fell, its geography was *rewritten*—mountains moved in memory, rivers taught to lie. The Crown's own scholars did it, and then the knowledge of how died with them, and now your cartographers will walk that slope with instruments that measure a world that agreed, a hundred years ago, to be wrong about this place." She tapped the treaty page. "You will draw boundaries around a grave and miss the body. If you want the truth of that ground surveyed, you don't send cartographers. You send someone who reads land against itself. You send me."

The clerk blinked. "That's—not within my authority, madam."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "But it's within the chronicle. Write it down."

After he left she sat with the lamp and the three rescued books—the last surviving texts of a burned archive—and opened the first keeper's confession to the page she had marked. *We did not seal a monster,* the old ink said. *We sealed a people. God forgive the hands that wrote the oath, mine included.* She had read it perhaps forty times now. She expected to read it forty more. Some sentences are load-bearing; you build a life on them.

Outside, the camp fires were coming alight one by one, and somewhere beneath the black pine the evening singing had started—the exiles sang at dusk now, freely, above ground, and the sound carried down the valley like water finding its old bed. Maren listened, and picked up her pen, and began the day's final entry.

*Day thirty-one, concluded. The singing was heard as far as the lowland farms tonight, according to a drover who passed through. He asked what country the song was from. I told him: none, now. All of them, soon.*

*The blank pages are ahead of us. For once, that does not frighten me.*

She was blotting the last line when Corvin Hale ducked into the tent, travel-dusted, grinning, and dropped a folded paper on her table. "Road news," he said. "From the south. You'll want this for your chronicle."

Maren unfolded it. It was a copy of a royal proclamation, fresh ink, official seal—and the first line stopped her breath.

*Whereas the nation of Veyland, having been found to exist...*

"Found to exist," she murmured. "Three words. A hundred years, undone in three words."

"There's more," Corvin said, and his grin had gone carefully neutral, the way it did when he was delivering something dangerous. "Read the last paragraph. About the survey of the gate lands."

She read it. Read it again. Then looked up at him, and he nodded slowly, and together they both turned toward the green slope, where—unbeknownst to either of them yet—a lantern was already climbing through the dusk, carried by someone who had gone up alone to sit where the gate had been, and who had found, half-buried in the new grass, a corner of pale stone bearing letters that had no business surviving the collapse.

Letters that spelled, in the old tongue of Veyland, a single word: *begin.*
