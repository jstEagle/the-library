## 1. The City That Forbade Wonder

Saltmere at night smelled of brine and tallow and the particular desperation of people pretending to be ordinary. Noor Winterbourne had learned to love that smell, or at least to stop flinching from it. Three years in the city taught a person that survival was mostly a matter of small surrenders performed so often they stopped feeling like surrender at all.

The night market opened when the Warden patrols changed shift — a gap of forty minutes between the dusk watch going off duty and the night watch rousing itself from whatever tavern held its sergeant. Forty minutes was enough. The stalls bloomed along Gullwing Lane like something surfacing for air: fishwives selling charms woven from kelp that were, officially, only decoration; a toothless man who sold "lucky" river stones that were lucky because he said so; old Marta with her jars of salt dyed seven colors, each hue meaning nothing at all, which was precisely why no one could arrest her for it.

Noor's stall sat at the lane's quiet end, under a leaning awning stitched from sailcloth. On it, arranged with a cartographer's precision, lay the harmless inventory: copper rings worn smooth by someone else's hands, knotted cords in sailor's colors, little folded paper birds that would flutter if you breathed on them just right — a trick of paper grain and patience, not magic, never magic. Saltmere had outlawed wonder after the Sundering, but it had not outlawed the *want* of it, and want was the one market that never glutted.

"You've got new stock," said a voice, and Noor looked up into the round, wind-chapped face of Odile Ferran, who kept the pie stall two down and knew everything that happened within three streets of the harbor.

"Paper," Noor said. "It's all paper."

"It's never all anything." Odile picked up one of the birds, turned it over, set it down as though handling evidence. "You fold them like you're drawing them. I watched your hands last week. Nobody folds a bird like they're tracing a coastline."

Noor smiled the smile they'd practiced in front of a cracked mirror until it fit their face like a well-worn glove. "I used to draw maps. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before I decided maps were more trouble than they were worth."

Odile snorted, bought three birds and a cord, and moved along, and Noor let out a breath they hadn't known they were holding. This was the work now. Not the folding, not the selling — the *smiling*. The daily labor of being a person with no history deeper than a bad marriage and a dislike of cold weather. Noor Winterbourne, charm-seller, insomniac, unremarkable. It had taken two years to build that person, brick by careful brick, and every day the mortar needed patching.

Under the stall's counter, hidden beneath a false bottom that had once held smuggled brandy, lay a leather case. In the case lay instruments that would have earned Noor a pyre: dividers tipped with star-metal, ink that shifted color with proximity to power, and a compass whose needle pointed not north but toward the nearest thin place — the nearest door between realms. It hadn't swung in three years. Saltmere's Wardens had seen to that, sealing or burning every threshold they could find, until the needle hung dead as a drowned man's finger.

But Noor still mapped.

Every night after the market closed, by candle stub, in a book bound as a ledger of accounts, Noor drew the city from memory — and then drew what the memory held beneath it. The doorway that had stood in the cellar of the Cordwainers' Hall before the Sundering, its arch shaped like a wave about to break. The threshold under the old lighthouse, sealed with lead and prayer-iron. The place where the river met the sea wall, where the water had once run silver on certain nights, where something on the other side had used to press its palm against the surface like a child pressing against a window.

Forty-one doorways. Noor had catalogued forty-one, and every page was a crime, and every night the pen moved anyway, because a mapmaker who stops mapping stops being a mapmaker, and then what was left? A person made of smiles and paper birds, hollow as a gull's egg.

*Belonging purchased at the price of truth,* Noor wrote once, in a margin, in cipher, and then scratched it out, and then wrote it again smaller.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble in Saltmere always did, with the sound of boots.

Not the loose, bored rhythm of the night watch — these boots came in step, in column, too many of them for Gullwing Lane. The market did what markets do: it didn't scatter, because scattering was an admission, but it went quiet in the way of animals deciding whether the shadow overhead is cloud or hawk.

Sergeant Hallow came around the corner first, lantern high, and behind him six Wardens in grey-and-white, and behind *them*, a wagon with a brazier mounted on its bed, coals already glowing. Noor's hands went still over the paper birds. Everyone in the lane understood the wagon. Everyone had seen it before, somewhere else, some other street, some other neighbor.

"They're saying Odile's kelp charms are charmed," whispered the boy who swept the lane, appearing at Noor's elbow the way he did when disaster came, drawn to it like gulls to gutting tables. "Ferran's stall. They're saying there's real working in it."

"There's nothing in it," Noor said. "Kelp and thread. I've seen her make them."

"So have they." The boy's name was Pip; he was eleven and had the flat, watchful eyes of a child raised under occupation. "That's not the point, is it."

It was not the point. The point was that the Wardens needed a fire every few weeks the way a stove needs wood, and tonight the wood was Odile Ferran, who had sold Noor eel pies every market night for two years and asked nothing back but conversation.

Hallow stopped at Odile's stall. He was not cruel — that was the terrible thing, the thing Noor had learned about order in Saltmere. He was tired and fair-minded and utterly convinced of his own decency, and he read from a paper in a clear voice while Odile stood with her flour-dusted hands folded, listening to herself be condemned for knots and seaweed.

"—found in violation of the Edict of Stillness, articles four and nine—"

"I tie them for luck," Odile said. "My mother tied them. Her mother. There's no working in a knot, Sergeant. There's just a knot."

"That's for the assessor to say." Hallow wouldn't meet her eyes. "You'll stand back from the stall, please."

"No."

"Madam—"

"I said no. I'll stand where I like. It's my stall."

One of the younger Wardens shifted, hand near his baton, and Noor saw the whole shape of the next minute unfold the way they once saw the shape of borders — the way pressure meets resistance and decides, in a heartbeat, how much blood will pay for it. And Noor did what Noor had done for three years, what had kept them alive and anonymous and safe: nothing. They looked down at their own hands. They became a bystander, a background figure, a charm-seller with no stake in any of it.

The coals came off the wagon. Odile's stall caught fast, sailcloth and pine and dried herbs going up with a roar that sent sparks swirling up past the shuttered windows. Someone screamed — not Odile; she stood silent, watching twenty years of her life curl into black lace — but someone further down the lane, a woman Noor half-knew, and the sound of it went through the crowd like a hook through a net, pulling everyone tight together and low.

And then Odile turned, in the orange light, and found Noor across the lane.

She didn't shout. She didn't accuse. She just looked, with an expression Noor had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a colleague left behind at a collapsing border — the look of a person confirming something they had long suspected about someone they had trusted. Then she turned back to her burning life, and Noor stood in the dark beyond the firelight with their heart hammering and their hands perfectly, deliberately empty.

The Wardens moved on. The bucket lines formed. By midnight the stall was a wet black scar on the cobbles and Odile was gone, taken to the assessor's hall or simply gone, and the market reopened around the scar as markets do, because hunger doesn't mourn.

* * *

Noor walked home the long way, along the sea wall, because the sea wall was the one place in Saltmere without eyes. Below, the water slapped the stones in the dark, and far out, where the horizon should have been, there was only fog — fog that had not lifted in three years, fog the fishermen called the Long Grey, fog that had come the season the Sundering finished its work and the realms pulled apart like torn cloth.

Noor stopped at the gap in the wall where, once, a doorway had stood. Page thirty-one of the false ledger. The arch had been shaped like a wave about to break; the Wardens had poured lead into it and posted a notice reading CLOSED BY ORDER, as if a door between worlds were a well or a road. Noor pressed a palm against the cold stone, feeling for the hum that wasn't there, the way a man touches an amputated limb.

"I'm sorry," they said aloud, to Odile, to the stone, to no one. The words fell flat on the wind. Sorry was the coin of the powerless, minted in abundance, worth nothing anywhere.

Here was the truth Noor carried home through the sleeping streets, up the stairs to the narrow room above the chandler's shop, where they lit no lamp: neutrality was not safety. Neutrality was just a slower fire. Every night they folded their paper birds and smiled their practiced smile and told themselves they were keeping their head down, keeping the past buried, keeping everyone safe — and every night the burial got a little deeper and the person doing the burying got a little less real, until some morning soon there would be nothing left at the bottom of the hole but a smile and a handful of ash.

They took out the ledger. They turned to a fresh page. Their hand hovered.

And that was when the compass moved.

Three years dead, the needle shuddered once against its glass like a moth against a window — and swung. Not toward the sea wall, not toward any of the forty-one sealed and numbered doors in Noor's memory. It swung inland, toward the hills above the city, trembling with a strength it hadn't shown since the Sundering, pointing at something coming.

Something walking toward Saltmere through the dark, carrying a piece of the world that had been broken.

Noor sat very still for a long time, listening to the needle tick against the glass, small and steady as a second heartbeat.

Then they reached for their boots.
