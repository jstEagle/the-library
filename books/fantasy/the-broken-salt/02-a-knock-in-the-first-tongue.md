## 2. A Knock in the First Tongue

The knock came at the hour when Saltmere forgot itself — when the lamplighters had gone home and the tide of the night market had drained away, leaving only the smell of fried dough and wet wool clinging to the alleys. Noor was hunched over the kitchen table with a cup of barley tea gone cold, copying from memory a doorway that no longer existed: the Moon Gate of Vess, which had stood between two realms like a held breath, before the Sundering took it and everything else.

Noor drew it anyway. Every night, one door. It was a discipline, or a penance, or a prayer — Noor had never decided which. The maps went into a locked chest beneath the floorboards, ink on vellum that would be worth a hanging if any Warden ever saw it. *Cartography of the living borders* was not a phrase used in polite company anymore. In Saltmere it wasn't used at all.

Three years of careful nothing. Three years of selling luck-knots and fever-charms that were only thread and dried lavender, harmless as bread, legal as rain. Three years of being *the quiet one on Lantern Row*, the charm-seller who kept to herself, whose stall never gave the patrols a reason to slow down. Noor Winterbourne had been very good at becoming no one.

The knock came again. Not loud. Almost apologetic.

Noor set down the pen and listened. Saltmere's nights were full of sounds that weren't knocks — shutters banging, drunkards singing off-key, the harbor bell counting the fog. This was different. This was small, and steady, and patient, the way a knock is when the person knocking has nowhere else left to go.

They opened the door on a child.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, maybe nine — it was hard to tell under the mud. She was caked in it, head to boots, as if she'd waded out of a riverbed and kept walking. Her hair was matted dark, her lips cracked, her eyes — her eyes were the problem. They were clear and level and utterly unafraid, which no child standing alone on a doorstep in Saltmere at midnight should have been. Behind her the street lay empty, lamplit fog rolling down toward the water, and there were no footprints in the frost behind her except her own, small and deep, coming from the direction of the sea wall. From the direction of the *old road*. The one that led nowhere anymore.

Noor knew every road that led nowhere anymore. Noor had drawn most of them.

"Lost?" Noor said, keeping the voice flat, market-day flat, the voice that invited nothing.

The girl looked up and spoke.

The sound that came out of her was not Saltmere's trade-tongue, nor the high speech of the Wardens' courts, nor the dockside pidgin of the Tidebound smugglers. It was older than all of them. Older than the kingdoms. It moved through the air the way cold moves through a wall — slowly, and all the way through — and Noor felt it land somewhere beneath the ribs, in the place where old training still lived even after you buried it.

*"Veth-ka ilun. Veth-ka sorren."*

And the words translated themselves in Noor's mind, unbidden, the way they always had — because the First Tongue was not learned so much as remembered, and Noor had spent three years trying to forget.

*The winter is falling. Find the oathkeeper.*

Noor's hand found the doorframe and gripped it.

"No," they said aloud, to the child, to the street, to whatever had sent her. "No, you've got the wrong door. There's no oathkeeper here. There's no one here at all."

The girl tilted her head, considering this, the way a bird considers glass. Then she opened her small muddy fist.

In her palm lay a shard of pale stone, no bigger than a knucklebone, veined faintly with light the color of dawn on snow. And against Noor's hip, inside the coat pocket where it had slept untouched for three years, something answered.

The compass.

Not a compass, really — though it looked like one, brass-cased, its face marked not with north but with nine points that corresponded to no sky. A border-finder. The tool of a realm-cartographer, useless since the Sundering, dead metal for three years, and now it was humming against Noor's ribs like a struck bell, the needle spinning and spinning and then snapping around to point — trembling, eager, *hungry* — at the shard in the child's hand.

Noor hadn't felt it move in three years. Hadn't wanted to feel it move. The hum went up their arm and into their teeth and they tasted copper and memory: a tower under a red sky, a stone the size of a heart breaking into pieces in their hands, Ilsa Vane's voice saying *do it, do it now, there's no time to be gentle*—

"Where did you get that?" Noor heard themselves ask. Their voice came out wrong. Rough.

The girl closed her fist over the shard and said, in that same terrible beautiful language, *"Sorren. Oathkeeper."* And then, with the grave patience of someone repeating herself to a fool: *"Veth-ka ilun."*

*Find the oathkeeper.*

"I'm not—" Noor started, and stopped, because the compass was still humming, and because the girl was looking at them with those clear eyes, and because somewhere under three years of careful anonymity a truth was stirring that Noor had starved but never killed: *you know what she is. You know what she carries. You have known since you opened the door.*

The winter is falling. Noor had felt it too, if they were honest — everyone in Saltmere had. Three years of winters that didn't end properly, springs that arrived late and left early, the harbor freezing further out each year until the icebreakers gave up. The Wardens called it weather. The priests called it penance. But Noor had mapped the flows between realms once, had drawn the warm currents of the covenant like rivers on a chart, and Noor knew what it meant when the rivers stopped flowing.

It meant the covenant was dying. And when it died fully, it wouldn't just be cold. It would be the Sundering again — but slower, and worse, and final.

"I can't help you," Noor said quietly. "There's nothing here. Do you understand? Whatever told you to come here, it made a mistake."

The girl did not look like a child who had been sent by a mistake.

She looked tired. That was the worst of it — underneath the eerie calm she was exhausted, swaying slightly on her feet, mud cracking in the folds of her neck, and some part of Noor that had gone soft in Saltmere's safety noticed that she must have walked a long way, and that the frost behind her held only one set of footprints, and that no one had come looking for her, and that she was eight years old, maybe nine, and alone.

Noor looked up and down Lantern Row. Empty. Fog. Somewhere far off, a Warden patrol drum sounded — two beats, pause, two beats — the rhythm that meant *all is well, remain indoors, wonder is forbidden.* All was well. Wonder was forbidden. The girl swayed on her feet.

"Inside," Noor said, before the better judgment could catch up. "Quickly."

* * *

She ate like a small wolf and fell asleep almost mid-bite, curled in the chair by the banked fire, the shard still clenched in her fist even in sleep, as if letting go of it had never once occurred to her. Noor covered her with a blanket and sat across the room and watched her and did not sleep at all.

The compass lay on the table between them, needle fixed, pointing at the child. Noor turned it away. The needle swung back. Noor put it in the drawer. Through the wood, faintly, it hummed.

*Tess,* the girl had murmured, half-asleep, when Noor asked her name. Not in the First Tongue — in trade-tongue, rough and rusty, as if she'd had a name in each language and had nearly forgotten both. *Tess.*

Noor sat with the cold tea and thought about doors.

That was the whole of cartography, really — people thought it was lines and distances, but it was doors. Where they stood, why they stood there, what they cost to open. Noor had spent a lifetime learning where the world kept its hinges. And now a door had knocked on *them*, mud-splattered, speaking the First Tongue, carrying a piece of the covenant-stone that Noor had last seen shattering in their own hands while Ilsa Vane screamed at them to hurry.

Three years ago Noor had run from that night. Run from the choice Ilsa demanded, run from the Wardens rising in the aftermath, run until running became a life, became a stall of harmless charms on Lantern Row, became *no one*. And it had worked. It had worked beautifully. That was the unforgivable part — how well it had worked, how easy belonging was, if you paid for it with everything true about yourself.

Now the past had walked out of the fog with one set of footprints and knocked politely at the door.

Outside, the patrol drums passed again, closer this time. Two beats, pause, two beats. And beneath them, so faint Noor might have imagined it, the compass hummed on in its drawer — steady now, no longer spinning, pointing through wood and darkness and three years of silence at the sleeping child, at the shard, at the frozen road that led back to everything Noor had buried.

*The winter is falling. Find the oathkeeper.*

Noor looked at Tess's small mud-stained fist, closed around a piece of the broken world, and understood with a dread like cold water that the words had never been an instruction meant for someone else.

Somewhere out in the fog, a Warden whistle blew — twice, sharp, much nearer than the drums.

Someone was already searching the streets.
