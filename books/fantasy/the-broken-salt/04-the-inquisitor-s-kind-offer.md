## 4. The Inquisitor's Kind Offer

The night market still smelled of smoke when Dain Marrow arrived, though the fire had been out for two hours. He stood at the edge of the ash-strewn square and let the smell settle into his coat, into his hair, the way he let everything settle — patiently, thoroughly, without flinching. Evidence was a language. Most people only ever learned to read it in panic.

The burned stall had belonged to a charm-seller named Odile Ferran. A minor offender. Salt-dried lavender bundled with knots tied in the old style. Nothing that could open so much as a window between worlds. The patrol had burned it anyway, because the law did not distinguish between a knot and a catastrophe, and because Sergeant Vell believed fear was a tool that never dulled.

Marrow disagreed. Fear dulled. Fear got tired, the way a dog got tired, and then it stopped barking at the right things. That was why he was here himself, past midnight, boots in another man's ashes, instead of letting the machine grind on without him.

"Inquisitor." Sergeant Vell approached, lantern held high, casting Marrow's shadow long across the scorched cobbles. "Witnesses are gathered. The ones who'd talk, anyway."

"The ones who'd talk," Marrow repeated mildly. "And the others?"

Vell's jaw tightened. "There's a woman keeps her shutters closed. Says she saw nothing. Says she sees nothing most nights."

"Name?"

"Ferra Quill. Sells fish. Or says she does."

Marrow nodded slowly. Everyone in Saltmere sold something they didn't sell. It was the city's whole economy, if you looked at it honestly: not salt, not fish, not charms, but concealment. A city built on the ruins of a broken covenant, trading in the one commodity that never spoiled.

He walked toward the witnesses.

* * *

They had put them in the customs house, six market-folk arranged on benches like goods awaiting appraisal. Marrow took off his coat before he began — a small ritual, deliberate. Inquisitors who interrogated in their coats were men hiding behind authority. He preferred to be seen: gray at the temples, ink stains on his fingers, the tired eyes of a man who read too much and slept too little. Harmless-looking. That was its own kind of instrument.

"You saw the stall burn," he said to the first witness, a young man with flour on his sleeves. "Tell me what you noticed."

"Noticed? It — it burned fast. Grease in the awning, maybe—"

"I don't mean the fire. I mean before it. What did you notice *before* it?"

The young man blinked. Marrow waited. Waiting was half the craft; people rushed to fill silence the way water rushed to fill a crack, and what poured out of them was always more honest than what they'd prepared.

"There was a cartographer," the baker said finally. "At the end of the row. Sold little things. Charms against headaches, that kind of thing. Harmless."

"Harmless," Marrow agreed. "Go on."

"They weren't. I mean—" The baker's hands twisted together. "I bought one once. For my wife's knees. And it worked, Inquisitor. Not like a poultice works. It *worked*. And afterward I couldn't stop thinking about how it worked, and I stopped going to the market."

"Why?"

"Because if that one worked, then all of them might. And if all of them might, then—" He stopped.

"Then the Sundering wasn't finished with us," Marrow said quietly. "Yes. Thank you. You've been more helpful than you know."

He moved down the bench. An old woman who sold candles. A boy who swept the square. Each of them gave him pieces, and he assembled the pieces the way he assembled everything, without hurry, until the picture stood complete in his mind:

A stall at the end of the row, unremarkable, deliberately unremarkable. Charms calibrated to be almost useless — strong enough to keep customers, weak enough to avoid attention. A hand-lettered sign with no name, only a symbol: a compass rose with one point missing.

Marrow knew that symbol. He had spent eleven years knowing it.

Realm-cartographers drew their marks with one point missing, because the fourth direction was not north or south or east or west but *between*, and to draw it fully was to invite it. He had seen the mark once before, in the archives beneath the Warden citadel, on maps recovered from the Sundering's last night. Maps drawn by a hand that had stood at the center of everything and then, by every record he could find, simply vanished.

Noor Winterbourne. Cartographer of the living borders. The youngest oath-sworn mapper in the history of the covenant, and the only one whose signature disappeared from the record three years ago as cleanly as if the page had been burned around it.

Marrow had spent three years learning to think of that name without hunger. Hunger made inquisitors sloppy. He had taught himself to hold the name the way a surgeon holds a scalpel: precisely, and only when needed.

Tonight it was needed.

* * *

He found Ferra Quill's shuttered window on his second pass through the fish-quarter, and he did not knock. He simply stood in the street below it, in the cold, and said, at a conversational volume, "The child came to your neighbor's door tonight. The one who sells headache charms. The child spoke, and the stone sang, and now there is a shard of the covenant-stone inside a house in Saltmere for the first time in three years."

The shutter did not move. But he heard breathing change behind it, and that was enough.

"I'm not going to break down your door, Mistress Quill. I'm going to tell you what happens next, and you can decide whether to be part of it or merely near it." He folded his hands. "Tomorrow, Warden patrols will search this quarter. They will be less polite than I am. Sergeant Vell believes burning is policy. I believe policy is what we hide behind when we're afraid, but I am only one man, and tomorrow morning I will be tired, and tired men let sergeants lead."

Silence. Then, low, bitter, through the slats: "You threaten like a priest, Inquisitor. All mercy and damnation in the same breath."

"I was trained by priests," Marrow said. "Before the Wardens, I studied to be one. I wanted to save souls. Now I save cities. It's the same work with worse hours." He paused. "The child, Mistress Quill. Mute, they say. Mud to the knees. Came from outside the walls, which means past the wards, which means the wards failed, which means the winter is worse than the Council admits. Do you understand what's coming? Because I do. I've read the dispatches from the northern farms. I've seen what the frost does to fields that were green in my lifetime. This isn't weather. Weather doesn't have a direction."

The shutter opened an inch. One eye, sharp and afraid.

"What do you want?"

"To talk to Noor Winterbourne. Alone. Before anyone else finds them." He stepped back from the window, into the lamplight, so she could see his face — the whole face, nothing performed. "Tell them that. Tell them Dain Marrow comes with an offer, not a warrant. And tell them—" he hesitated, then gave her the truth, because truth traveled better than threats, "—tell them I knew their mentor. Ilsa Vane taught me doctrine at the citadel, years ago. I owe her that much memory, at least. Winterbourne will understand what it means that I remember her kindly."

The shutter closed. Marrow stood a moment longer in the cold, then walked back toward the citadel through streets where every lamp burned behind glass and every door was double-locked, a city of people who had outlawed wonder and called the outlawing peace.

* * *

He was wrong about being alone with Winterbourne, in the end — or perhaps right, depending on how you counted. He chose the market itself, midmorning, when the stalls were open and the crowd was thick enough to make violence impolite even for Wardens. He bought a headache charm from the neighboring stall first, paid full price, and wore it visibly on his wrist as he approached the map-seller's table.

Noor Winterbourne looked up at him with the flat, careful eyes of someone who had rehearsed this moment for three years and hoped never to need the rehearsal.

"Inquisitor," they said. Their voice was steady. Their hands, resting on a display of harmless charts, were not.

"Master Winterbourne." Marrow set a folded paper on the table between them — not a warrant, just a map of the city, ordinary, official. "I'll be brief, because I know you value brevity, and because every minute we speak, we're both committing crimes. Yours is older. Mine is newer."

"Is there a point?"

"A child came to your door last night." He watched the stillness take Winterbourne — the particular stillness of prey deciding whether to run. "Don't. Please. If you run, Sergeant Vell runs faster, and Vell has already asked me twice this morning why we haven't burned your stall. I told him you were under investigation by my office, which buys you days, not weeks. So listen."

Winterbourne listened. That was the thing the records had never captured, Marrow thought — the quality of attention, the way the cartographer took in a room like surveying terrain.

"The child carries a shard of the covenant-stone. The stone your order broke. The stone Ilsa Vane ordered broken, on the last night, while the realms tore themselves apart around her." He kept his voice low, level, almost gentle. "I know what you were, Winterbourne. I've known since the day you vanished from the rolls. I've spent three years making sure no one else figured it out, because I believed — I still believe — that what you know is worth more than what you'd confess under torture. Torture gives us names. You give us *maps*."

"And what do you want for this generosity?" Winterbourne's voice was dry as old vellum.

"Clemency. Real clemency, sealed and signed. For the child above all." Marrow leaned forward. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, and I'll make it honestly, because I find honesty disarms people faster than any blade. Give me the shard. Give me the child's testimony — written, spoken, whatever the child can manage. In exchange, the child walks free under Warden protection. Fed, housed, schooled. No cells. No questions about where they came from. And you—" he met those flat gray eyes, "—you walk away. Resignation accepted. New name, new city, new life. The Wardens lose interest in a dead mapmaker. I've done it before. It works."

"And the shard goes to the citadel. To be locked away."

"To be *kept*," Marrow said, and here his voice changed — warmed, almost, with something that was not performance. "Winterbourne, I want you to understand me. I am not Vell. I don't burn stalls for pleasure. Every seal I've set, every door I've locked, every realm-border I've helped nail shut — I did it because I stood in the aftermath of the Sundering when I was nineteen years old and pulled bodies out of a river that flowed through two worlds at once, and some of the bodies were children, and none of them were whole. The covenant existed to keep that from happening again. Your order broke it. Perhaps you had reasons. Perhaps Ilsa Vane wept while she swung the hammer. It doesn't matter. What matters is that the wall is down, and the winter is coming through the gap, and the only question left is whether we rebuild the wall or stand in the gap arguing about architecture."

Winterbourne was silent for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was quieter. "You truly believe you're the merciful one."

"I believe mercy is a wall that holds," Marrow said. "Everything else is poetry."

He straightened, retrieved his map of the city, and turned to go. At the edge of the stall he paused, not looking back.

"You have until the frost reaches the harbor, Winterbourne. Three days, perhaps four. After that, Vell takes over, and Vell doesn't buy charms or make offers." He set a small iron token on the table — a Warden seal, good for one safe passage through any gate. "For the child. Whatever you decide. Children shouldn't pay for our choices."

He walked away through the market crowd, and behind him he felt Winterbourne's gaze like a drawn line on a chart — precise, measuring, and, he suspected, already plotting a course he would not be able to follow.

That night, in his office above the citadel archive, Marrow lit a single candle and opened a drawer he rarely touched. Inside lay a letter, twenty years old, in a hand he would have known anywhere: *If the stone must break, let it break clean, and let no one carry the guilt alone.*

Ilsa Vane's handwriting. Ilsa Vane's doctrine. And somewhere out in the frozen dark, he was increasingly certain, Ilsa Vane herself — alive, or something close to it, sustained by the dying covenant she had murdered.

Marrow closed the drawer and sat for a long time in the candlelight, a sincere man guarding a dying world, waiting to learn whether sincerity was enough.

Three days, he had given them. He wondered, watching the frost creep up the inside of his own window, whether either of them had three days left.
