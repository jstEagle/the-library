## 5. Salt and Ashes

We left Saltmere before the fourth bell, when the fog off the harbor was thick enough to drown a lantern.

I had done this before — the leaving. I knew the choreography of it: the bag packed in the dark, the coins counted twice, the door left unlocked so the room would look abandoned rather than fled. What I had never done was leave with someone else's small hand curled into mine, gripping so hard the bones ached.

Tess did not look back at the city. I did, once, from the top of the seawall stairs. Behind us Saltmere lay gray and sleeping under its law, its thousand chimneys breathing smoke that was permitted to be smoke and nothing more. Somewhere in that grid of streets, Inquisitor Dain Marrow was already awake, already asking his gentle questions. I could feel him the way you feel a storm front — not seen yet, but coming, and certain.

The map was older than my exile. I had drawn it three years ago in blood-ink, the kind that remembers what it has drawn and will not fade until its purpose is served. It showed a coastline south of the city that no Warden chart acknowledged: a ragged scar of inlets where the tide came in wrong, twice a day, out of some courtesy to people who had never signed Saltmere's covenant of fear.

The Tidebound.

Smugglers, the Wardens called them. Heretics. But I had met them once, years ago, buying passage for a woman whose magic was nothing worse than making bread rise true, and they had asked no questions and taken no coin she didn't have. Outcasts keep the oldest hospitality. They know how quickly anyone can become one of them.

"Where are we going?" Tess asked.

It was the first full sentence she'd spoken since the door. The First Tongue sat strangely in her mouth — not foreign but *archaic*, like hearing a grandmother's voice come out of a child. I understood it without wanting to; that was the curse of my training. Languages of power translated themselves in me whether I willed it or not.

"Somewhere the Wardens' maps have holes," I said.

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Holes are doors."

"Sometimes." I tightened my grip on her hand. "Don't tell the Wardens that."

* * *

We walked the salt road through the morning, and by noon I understood why Marrow wanted the child so badly.

The cold followed us.

Not the ordinary cold of the season — this was directional, purposeful, a thinning of warmth itself. Frost bloomed on the leeward side of fence posts in patterns I recognized from old survey work: hexagonal lattices, the signature geometry of covenant-stone. When Tess stumbled and caught herself against a milestone, the frost around it *retreated*, drawing back from her hand like a dog from a raised voice.

She was draining it. Or it was being drawn toward her. I couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like swallowed ice.

At midday we sheltered in the ruin of a tollhouse, and I fed her dried plums and half my bread while she studied my compass. The needle no longer pointed north. It pointed at her — had been pointing at her since the night she arrived, trembling against its glass housing like something alive and frightened.

"What broke the stone?" she asked suddenly.

My hands went still over the bread knife. "Who told you it was broken?"

"The winter did." She said it as if it were obvious. "The winter talks all the time. Nobody listens."

I looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the frost crept up the tollhouse window in those same hexagonal lattices, and I thought: *three years I told myself the Sundering was finished. Three years I sold lucky charms and drew maps of places I would never go, and called it peace.*

"Eat your plums," I said.

* * *

We reached the Tidebound coast at dusk, and the sea greeted us wrong.

That was the only word for it. The tide stood a hand's breadth higher than any chart allowed, held there — I realized as we picked our way down the shingle — by intention. Small magics, layered like fish scales along the shore: a charm here to hold the water, a ward there to blur the sight of passing patrol boats, everywhere the faint warm smell of working craft, like bread baking in a house you can't see.

A woman rose out of the rocks ahead of us, and I nearly put a knife in her before I saw she was seventy if she was a day, wrapped in oilskin, holding a lantern with a flame that burned blue.

"Noor Winterbourne." She said my name like a diagnosis.

"You have me confused with—"

"I don't." She lifted the lantern, and its blue light washed over Tess, and the old woman's face did something complicated. "Three years, cartographer, and you walk back into my tide carrying *that*. Come down before the cold does. And mind the third step; it lies."

Her name was Maren Oduya-Clay, though everyone called her the Harbormistress, and she led us down through a maze of tidal caves into a harbor that existed on no map because it existed only at low water — a bowl of black stone roofed with sky, where forty boats rode at anchor and fires burned on every deck, and children ran the rigging barefoot, and somewhere a fiddle was playing a tune in a mode I hadn't heard since before the Sundering.

Magic. Small, stubborn, domestic magic. A man mending a net murmured knots into it that would never slip. A girl coaxed a sick gull back to health with cupped hands and patience. Nothing grand. Nothing that would frighten Saltmere's Council. Everything that Saltmere had outlawed anyway.

Tess stopped dead at the top of the last stair and stared, and then she laughed — a bright, cracked sound, the laugh of a child remembering how — and the nearest fire flared up gold, and every head in the harbor turned.

"Well," said the Harbormistress dryly. "That's torn it."

* * *

They gave us a bunk in the stern-castle of a beached ketch called the *Mercy of Thieves*, and hot stew, and something better than either: the particular silence of people who have decided not to ask questions yet. Tess fell asleep between one spoonful and the next, the shard of pale stone clutched against her chest, and I sat watching her breathe while the harbor talked quietly around me.

Maren found me there an hour past midnight. She brought two cups of something bitter and warming and sat down across from me without invitation, which I was learning was her way.

"The child drains heat," she said. Not a question.

"So you've noticed."

"We've noticed the bay freezing at the edges. First time in living memory. And the fishing grounds north gone dead-cold, and my knees telling me a weather that isn't weather." She sipped. "You're the only person on this coast who might know what's actually happening, and you've been sitting there looking like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. So finish it."

I turned my compass over in my hands. The needle strained toward the sleeping child.

"When the realms were joined," I said slowly, "there was a covenant. A stone, sworn at the Threshold, that kept the doors open and the passages safe. Warmth moved between worlds along it. Life did. When it broke—" I stopped. Started again. "Everyone thinks the Sundering ended three years ago, when the last battle was fought and the Wardens took power and Saltmere outlawed magic. Everyone thinks the war is over."

"But?"

"But wars end. Covenants *die*. Slowly." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it did when I reported survey findings I wished weren't true. "The stone wasn't destroyed cleanly. Its shards are still trying to do their work, reaching for each other across the dark, and every year the reach costs more. The long winter isn't climate, Maren. It's a wound. The covenant is bleeding warmth out of every realm it ever connected, and it won't stop until either the shards are rejoined or the last of the old bond gutters out. And when it gutters out—"

"It takes everything it touched with it."

"Everything it touched," I said, "was everything."

The fiddle had stopped. Across the water, the banked fires made small orange constellations, and above them the stars were hard and cold and closer than they should have been — another symptom, I realized, another sign of the walls between worlds wearing thin. You shouldn't be able to see the other realms' stars. Soon you wouldn't see anything.

Maren was quiet for a long time. Then: "You helped break it."

It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse.

"Yes."

"And now the breaking has a face and small hands and follows you through the snow." She set down her cup. "My brother's son is out there, cartographer. Bram. He sailed north three years ago with Ilsa Vane's expedition and never came home, and I have lit a lamp for him every night since, and I will light it tomorrow, whatever you tell me. But if there's a way to stop this winter — a real way, not charms and not hiding — you'll find it. Because outcasts survive by knowing which way the tide turns before it turns."

She left me with the cold cup and the sleeping child and the compass straining in its case like a heart against a ribcage.

* * *

I woke before dawn to Tess standing at the rail, barefoot on frost-slick deck, holding the shard up to the gray light.

"Tess." I kept my voice soft. "Come away from the rail."

She didn't turn. "It's singing louder."

"Shards don't sing."

"This one does." She lowered the stone and looked at me, and her eyes in the pre-dawn gloom were the color of deep water over deep stone. "It sings the oath. The first one. The one they swore at the Threshold." She held it out to me, and the frost on the rail around her small fingers had arranged itself — I saw it clearly, and my stomach dropped — into letters. Old ones. First Tongue characters, precise as engraving, spelled out in ice:

*THE KEEPER COMES. THE KEEPER IS CHOSEN.*

"That's not about me," I said. My voice cracked on the second word.

Tess tilted her head, considering. Then she smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had ever seen on a child's face.

"It says *chosen*, Noor. Not *guilty*."

Behind us, across the black water, a lamp was burning in the Harbormistress's window — and beside it, a second lamp had just been lit, high on the cliff path, burning a color no fire should burn.

Blue. Warden-blue.

Someone had found the tide.
