## 6. The Traitor's Lantern

*Tess draws the harbor first. Always the harbor.*

She draws it in charcoal on the back of a salt-crate lid, because paper is for people who trust that tomorrow will come with clean hands. The Tidebound's harbor: crooked pilings, boats stitched together from three wrecks, lanterns hung on fishhooks so they can be cut loose and carried into the dark when the Wardens' skiffs come sniffing along the coast.

In her drawing, every lantern has a face.

This is how Tess thinks, though she does not know the word *think* in the way scholars mean it. Words went out of her the night her village burned — went out like a pinched wick — and what remained was the part of her that had always been there underneath: the seeing, the keeping, the making of marks. Her mother used to say she had a cartographer's eye before she could walk. Now the eye is all she is, and it is enough, mostly.

She draws Noor at the edge of the harbor too. Noor is hard to draw. Noor is a person made of doors that have been nailed shut, and charcoal cannot render a nail. So Tess gives Noor a lantern instead, hanging unlit at the hip, and hopes the picture understands what she means.

The shard hums against her sternum where it hangs on its cord. In the First Tongue — the tongue that lives in her mouth now like a second heartbeat, the tongue that came with the stone — the humming has a name:

*Vess-ka-illun.* The stone remembers being whole.

Tess presses her palm flat over it and adds one more mark to the drawing: a small figure beside Noor, holding a lantern that is lit. Herself. She is not sure yet what the light is for. She only knows that in every picture she makes lately, the light gets bigger.

* * *

*The word for stranger and the word for storm are the same word.*

*Karesh.* Tess learns this on the third morning among the Tidebound, when old Maren-the-netmender says it twice in one breath — once pointing at the fog rolling off the water, once pointing at a one-eyed smuggler nobody will sit beside. The Tidebound speak a gutter-trade tongue full of salt and shorthand, but their oldest words are borrowed from older things, and Tess collects them the way other children collect shells.

She writes them in her book of pictures. Not letters — letters are Noor's country, and she is learning to cross that border slowly, *N-o-o-r*, four strokes she practices until they stop looking like a fence. But under each letter-word she draws the thing itself, and above it she writes the First Tongue sound as best her hand can shape it, because the First Tongue does not like ink. It prefers breath. It prefers to be said into a room and watched to see what the room becomes.

That morning the room becomes afraid.

It starts small. A boy on net-mending duty stops mid-knot and stares north. A dog that never barks barks once. Then the lookouts come down from the mast at a run, and the whole harbor changes key — Tess feels it in the shard before she hears it in the voices, a low wrong note, *vess-ka-illun* souring into something like *vess-ka-dur*. The stone remembering being broken.

Maren grabs Tess by the shoulder and steers her behind the drying racks, saying words Tess doesn't know but understands anyway, because fear is the one language with no dialects.

Then the fog thins, and Tess sees them.

A column. Grey cloaks, grey skiffs beached neat as knives, and at the front — walking ahead of his own soldiers like a man strolling to market — a tall figure carrying a lantern with a shuttered flame. Even at distance, even through sea-haze, Tess can see the lantern is beautiful. Brass and glass, old workmanship, the kind of object someone keeps for decades. The kind of object someone carries because they cannot carry the person who gave it to them.

The man lifts his free hand, palm out, and calls across the water in a voice trained to carry without breaking:

"Bram Oduya! Warden Auxiliary, Third Column! I come under parley — no torches lit, no steel drawn!" A pause, and then, softer, almost pleading: "I'm looking for Noor Winterbourne."

Behind the drying racks, Tess feels Noor go still beside her. Not soldier-still. Something worse. The stillness of a door being opened after years of nails.

Noor whispers a name. It is not *Bram Oduya*. It is just *Bram*, said the way you say a word you thought was lost from your own language.

Tess looks up at Noor's face and begins, without deciding to, to draw it.

* * *

*There is a sign in the First Tongue that means both grief and gift.*

Tess draws the parley from the top of the net-loft, where Maren puts her because children are small and children are witnesses and the Tidebound waste nothing, not even that.

She draws Bram Oduya standing on the wet planks with his lantern held high, and she draws him honestly, which is the hardest way to draw anyone. He is not cruel-looking. That is the trap of him. His face is long and tired and kind around the eyes, the kind of face that has apologized to people sincerely and then done the thing again anyway. His grey cloak is Warden-issue but worn soft at the cuffs, like he sleeps in it, like he has been wearing it long enough that it stopped being a costume.

He talks for a long time. Tess catches fragments through the loft slats, and the shard translates some of it against her skin, warm and cold by turns.

"—three years I let them believe it," Bram is saying. "Drowned at the Sundering's edge. It was easier for everyone. It was easier for *you*, Noor. You got your little life. Your little stall. Your little silence." His voice cracks on the last word and Tess marks it — a jagged line in the margin, the sound of a crack in a wall. "But I didn't die, Noor. I *converted*. There's a difference, and the difference is that I've read every report, every atrocity ledger, every burned village from here to the Meridian. Magic did that. The covenant's slow rot did that. And you know what's coming — you of all people know — the realms are bleeding into each other through a thousand cracks and every crack is a war waiting for soldiers."

"I know what's coming," Noor says. Tess draws Noor's hands, which are shaking, and Noor's feet, which do not move. "I've known since the child arrived."

"Then give me the shard." Gentle now. Terribly gentle. "Give it to the Wardens and take the girl somewhere the sun reaches. Marrow will grant clemency — I've already bought it, it's written, your name isn't even on it. One stone for two lives, Noor. One stone for an end to it *all*. Sealed borders. No more Sundering. No more villages." He raises the lantern higher, and the shuttered flame inside throws a thin gold line across the planks between them, like a border drawn on a map. "I loved you. That hasn't changed. That's why I found you before Marrow did."

And here is the thing Tess will spend many nights redrawing before she gets it right: Noor *wavers*.

She sees it. She draws it. Noor's shoulders drop half an inch, and Noor takes one step toward the lantern-light, and the whole harbor holds its breath, and Tess's own hand goes still on the page because she understands, suddenly and completely, that Noor is considering it. Considering giving away the stone in Tess's chest. Considering buying safety with the thing that hums *find the oathkeeper, find the oathkeeper* against her ribs day and night.

The betrayal is not Bram's. Tess barely knows Bram. The betrayal is watching someone you trust stand in the light of someone who loves them wrongly, and lean.

Then Noor stops. Looks back — up, at the net-loft, at Tess, though Tess is sure she cannot be seen through the slats. And whatever Noor finds in that upward glance, it closes the door. Nails it shut. Maybe.

"No," Noor says. Quietly. Then louder, to the whole grey column: "No. You want to seal the wound because sealing is easy and healing is work. I ran from this for three years, Bram. I know exactly how heavy the easy choice is. I won't hand you the stone, and I won't hand you the child, and if your clemency is written down, burn it, because we're leaving tonight and we're not going anywhere your maps can follow."

Bram lowers the lantern. For a moment his kind tired face does something complicated, and Tess draws it three times trying to catch it — grief, yes, but also relief? Also something like envy, the look of a man watching someone make the brave choice he talked himself out of years ago?

"You always were the better cartographer," Bram says. Almost a smile. Almost. "Which means you know the White Meridian can't be crossed. Not in winter. Not by anyone."

"I know it can't be *mapped*," Noor answers. "Those aren't the same thing anymore."

Bram stands there a long moment, lantern guttering behind its shutter. Then he steps aside — actually steps aside, clearing the path to the water — and says, in a voice pitched only for Noor but caught anyway by a child with a cartographer's ear:

"You have until dawn. Then Marrow's column arrives, and I can't hold them, and everything I traded to find you first will have been for nothing. Take the north road, Noor. Please. Take the road I can pretend I didn't see."

* * *

*The last drawing of the night is not a drawing. It is a map.*

Tess works by shard-light while the Tidebound argue in low fierce voices below — sail now, hide now, scatter now — and Noor stands apart, staring north, holding a rolled tube of old blood-ink parchment like a man holding a snake he has decided to pick up anyway.

Tess draws the north road. She has never seen it; she draws it anyway, because the shard shows her things sometimes, images pressed against the inside of her skull like leaves in wax: white wastes, a line across the world, a hall with no roof. At the top of the map she writes the First Tongue sounds for the place the visions keep returning to, the syllables that taste like ice and iron:

*Thress-ka-vellum.* The Threshold. Where the oath was sworn.

Below it, because the stone insists, she writes the other phrase, the one that has been growing in her dreams all week, patient as frost:

*Vess-ka-illun. Vess-ka-dur. Vess-ka—*

The stone remembers being whole. The stone remembers being broken. The stone remembers—

She stops. Presses her hand flat over the shard. Listens.

Outside, far off along the coast road, a second set of lights has appeared. Many lights. Moving fast. Not Bram's lantern — something colder, something that marches in ranks. Dawn is hours away and the dawn is already coming.

Tess picks up her charcoal and adds herself to the map, small and lit, standing at the edge of the white. Then she tugs Noor's sleeve and holds it up, and watches Noor read her drawing the way Noor reads all maps — like a sentence written in a language that is about to decide whether to forgive the person reading it.

"The Meridian," Noor says softly. "You've seen it."

Tess nods.

Noor looks north, where the wastes wait, unmapped and hungry. "Then we'd better leave before my friend decides to be honest," Noor says, "and before his honesty runs out."

Above the harbor, the first snow of the long winter begins to fall — and it falls *upward*, drifting toward the stars, as if the sky itself were being pulled away.
