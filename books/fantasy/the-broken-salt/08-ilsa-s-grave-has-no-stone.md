## 8. Ilsa's Grave Has No Stone

The oath-hall had been a cathedral once, before the Sundering taught Saltmere to fear its own history. Noor saw it first as a smudge of ruin against the white horizon, then as a shape they knew too well — the broken ring of pillars, the stair that climbed to nothing, the great hollow where the covenant-stone had stood for a thousand years. They had drawn this place from memory a hundred times in the years since, in the margins of harmless maps, in the dirt with a stick when sleep would not come. They had never expected to see it again.

Tess saw it and stopped walking.

She stood hip-deep in the drifts, the shard of pale stone cupped against her chest like a bird she was afraid to startle, and her breath came out in long silver plumes. She looked at the ruin the way Noor had seen her look at locked doors: not with fear, but with recognition.

"I know," Noor said. Their voice had gone rough from three days of wind. "I know."

* * *

They had crossed the White Meridian in six days that felt like sixty. The wastes had done what the wastes always did — rearranged themselves around what travelers feared. On the second night Noor had dreamed of Saltmere burning and woken to find the snow around their camp shaped like streets, like doorways, like every threshold they had ever mapped and sealed. Tess had slept through it, her small hand fisted in Noor's coat, and in the morning the child had spoken her first full sentence aloud: *"The stone remembers us."* Three words in the First Tongue, thin and clear as ice cracking over a pond, and Noor had wept into their scarf where she couldn't see.

Now the hall rose above them, and the cold coming off it was different from the waste's cold. This was an old cold. A kept cold.

Noor climbed the stair with Tess behind them, one small mittened hand gripping the back of their belt. The pillars leaned like drunkards. Frost had grown up the walls in feathered sheets, and through the gaps Noor could see the sky going the color of pewter — the last light, or close to it. The final cold was coming. They could feel it in their teeth.

At the top of the stair, in the hollow where the stone should have been, there was a fire.

It was a small fire, stubborn, fed on broken pew-wood, and beside it sat a figure wrapped in a Warden-grey cloak gone white with frost, so still that Noor's first thought was *a corpse, someone left her here, someone—*

Then the figure turned her head.

Three years fell away like a dropped cloak. Ilsa Vane was gaunt as a winter branch, her hair gone entirely white, her lips cracked and dark. But her eyes were the same — grey, level, patient as surveying instruments — and they found Noor across the ruined hall with no surprise at all.

"You took the northern pass," she said. Her voice was dry paper. "I told you once you would. You always chose the longer way when you were afraid of arriving."

Noor could not move. Tess pressed closer against their back.

"You're dead," Noor said finally. "Bram said — the reports said—"

"The reports were mine." Ilsa smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that meant *you are being slow, cartographer; keep up.* "A dead woman asks fewer questions. Sit down. Both of you. You're letting the heat out, and I have very little heat to spare."

* * *

Noor sat. It was not a decision so much as a collapse. Tess wedged herself between Noor's arm and side, staring at the old woman across the flames with open, unguarded curiosity — the way children stare at things adults have already decided about.

Up close, Noor could see what sustained her. It was in the way the frost refused to settle on her hands, the way the fire burned without smoke, the faint light under her skin like a lamp behind vellum. The covenant's death throes were leaking out across all the realms, draining warmth from hearths and harbors and hearts — and here, at the point where the covenant had been sworn, the last of that dying power pooled like blood in the bottom of a wound. Ilsa Vane had made herself a vessel for it. Or it had made her one.

"How long?" Noor asked.

"Since the night we broke it." Ilsa fed a splinter of pew-wood to the flames. "The stone didn't want to shatter, Noor. Stone that old has opinions. When it broke, it broke *toward* me. All that leaving power had to go somewhere, and I was holding the hammer." She flexed her fingers, and Noor saw the fine white scarring across her palms, old burns in the shape of a shattered seal. "So I stayed. Someone had to mind the grave."

"There's no grave," Noor said. The words came out bitterer than they intended. "There's no stone. There's nothing here but you and a fire."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "That was rather the point."

And then, because there was no more running, because the wastes had stripped them down to whatever was truest, Noor asked the question they had carried for three years like a stone in the boot.

"Why? You were the one who taught us the covenant was sacred. You drew my first border-map. You held my hand at the Threshold and swore me in. And then you gave the order to break it, and you didn't even—" Their voice cracked. "You didn't tell us why."

Ilsa was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere in the ruin above, ice shifted and settled.

"Do you remember the war?" she said at last. "Not the Sundering. Before it. The year the gods noticed each other again."

Noor remembered. Everyone who had lived through it remembered, though few spoke of it. The old powers waking in their far realms, hungry after centuries of the covenant's careful distance. The borders thrashing like netted fish. The sky over four kingdoms turning the color of a bruise.

"It wasn't a war of armies," Ilsa went on. "Armies end. This was gods leaning against the walls of the world to see which would crack first. And I sat in council with the other oathkeepers, and I watched them vote — vote, Noor, as if a thing like that could be voted on — and I understood that none of them would do what needed doing. So I did it myself. I gathered the ones I trusted most, and I lied to them about how much time we had, and I broke the covenant-stone before the gods could use it as a door." Her eyes closed briefly. "Eleven realms went dark that night. Not destroyed. Sealed. Cut off from each other and from the old powers, forever, if forever is what I meant it to be."

"And the winter?"

"The winter is the bill coming due." She said it without flinching. "A broken covenant doesn't stop working. It dies slowly, and as it dies, everything it once held apart goes cold. I knew that. I accepted it. Cold is survivable, Noor. Gods are not." She opened her eyes and fixed them on Noor, and there it was — the full weight of three years, of every map drawn in hiding, every charm sold in the night market, every night Noor had lain awake hating her. "Let them die apart. That's all I ask. Let the realms go out like lamps in separate rooms, quietly, each in its own bed. Don't reforge it. Whatever Marrow has promised you, whatever the shard is whispering to the child — don't. Because the next stone will be forged by people, and people are exactly what the gods are waiting for."

Tess stirred against Noor's side. Slowly, deliberately, she pulled the shard from her coat and held it up, and in the firelight it glowed like a held breath.

Ilsa looked at it, and for the first time something moved behind her iron calm — something that might have been grief.

"That piece sang to you, didn't it," she said to the child. Not unkindly. "It sang to all of us, once. That's how it gets reforged. That's how the whole terrible wheel turns again." She looked back at Noor. "I am asking you to break the wheel. I am asking you to be crueler than I was, and kinder. Let it end."

* * *

Noor did not answer. There was nothing in them that could answer — only two truths grinding against each other like millstones, and between them, a child.

It was Tess who heard them first. She went rigid, her head tilting, listening to something below the range of human ears — and Noor heard it a moment later: voices on the wind, and beneath the voices, the creak and jingle of harness and steel.

Lantern-light bloomed at the base of the stair. A column of Wardens, white-cloaked, moving up through the drifts in disciplined silence. And at their head, two figures Noor knew: one tall and deliberate, carrying his office the way other men carried swords — Inquisitor Dain Marrow, his breath steaming, his face turned up toward the ruined hall with the expression of a man arriving at the conclusion of a long argument.

And beside him, holding a lantern high, a broad-shouldered man with a scarred jaw and Bram Oduya's familiar walk, lighting the Wardens' path as though he had done it all his life.

"Noor," Ilsa said quietly. She had not risen. She did not seem surprised. "Whatever you're going to choose — choose it now."

Marrow reached the top of the stair and stopped, taking in the scene: the dying oathkeeper, the fugitive cartographer, the mute child with the singing stone. His gaze settled on Noor, and there was no triumph in it. Only a tired, terrible sincerity.

"Master Winterbourne," he said. "We need to talk about mercy."

Behind him, Bram raised the lantern higher, and its light fell full on Tess's face — and faltered, just slightly, as if the man holding it had remembered something he'd spent three years trying to bury.
