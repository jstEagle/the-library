## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle of wax when Juniper Whitlock noticed his hand shaking.

He'd been copying debt ledgers for six hours, and the tremor was nothing new—every scribe in Hollowmere developed it eventually, a fine vibration in the fingers that came from pressing quill to vellum until midnight, night after night, year after year. His master, Brother Aldous, called it *the honest shake*, because it only afflicted men who did honest work. Juniper had never been sure whether that was comfort or threat.

But this was different. This wasn't fatigue.

Outside the scriptorium's narrow window, the sky over Hollowmere Valley was going out.

He set down his quill. Blotted the line he'd just finished—*Vessic, Marta: four crowns, eleven shillings, interest accrued through third winter*—and rose from his stool. The window was tall and thin as an arrow slit, cut for light rather than view, but from here he could see the whole bowl of the valley: the terraced fields gone grey with frost, the crooked rooftops of Lowtown, the temple spire rising at the center like a black finger pointed at heaven, and above it all, the wards.

You couldn't see the wards themselves. Everyone in Hollowmere knew that. You saw what they touched—the faint silver shimmer along the ridgelines where the valley walls met the sky, like breath on cold glass. The priests said it was the gods' protection made visible. The debtors said it was the lid on the pot. Both were right.

Juniper pressed his forehead near the glass and counted stars.

That was a habit too, though he'd never told anyone. When the copying got bad enough that his eyes crossed and the letters swam, he'd look up and count until his mind went quiet. There were always the same ones. The Shepherd's Crook, low over the western ridge. The Seven Sisters clustered tight above the spire. The pale blue one that sat alone in the south, so bright it seemed to hum. Forty-one stars, if you counted generously, which Juniper always did, because generosity cost nothing.

Tonight there were thirty-eight.

He blinked. Rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. Counted again.

Thirty-six.

They weren't being covered by cloud—he could see that now with a sick lurch in his stomach. Cloud would have dimmed them all evenly, a veil drawn across the whole sky. This was different. The stars were going out one by one, in no order he could follow, like candles snuffed by a wind moving backward through time. The Shepherd's Crook lost its handle. The Sisters became five, then three. And the lone blue star in the south—it didn't fade so much as *close*, the way an eye closes, and the darkness it left behind was somehow deeper than the darkness around it.

"No," Juniper said aloud, to no one. The word fogged the glass.

Behind him, Brother Aldous snored gently over a half-finished psalter, his own candle guttering. Down the corridor, the other night-scribes would be at their desks, bent over their work, copying prayers against the dark the way their masters had done for two hundred years. *Prayers keep the wards fed,* the temple taught. *Ink is devotion; devotion is fuel.* Every child in Hollowmere learned to write by copying ward-prayers before they learned to write their own names. Juniper had copied more of them than anyone alive. He'd once calculated, during a long winter of insomnia, that his hand alone had transcribed eleven thousand pages of holy text, and that if devotion were truly fuel, he should be able to feel it burning somewhere behind his ribs.

He felt nothing behind his ribs now except fear.

Because he knew something about the wards that the temple didn't teach children. He'd found it years ago, filed away in a ledger he wasn't supposed to read—a maintenance record, dry as dust, listing the wardline's anchor points. Nine anchors, ringed around the valley. And beside each anchor point, in the cramped notation of some long-dead warden, a single repeated instruction:

*Blood-tithe current. Verify lineage intact.*

Lineage. Intact. Juniper had turned those words over for years like a stone in his pocket, feeling for what was underneath. He'd never found anything. He was nobody. A debtor's son raised in the temple's care because his father's debts had outlived him, useful only for his handwriting, invisible as ink drying. Whatever bloodline the old record meant, it couldn't be his.

The thought had always ended there. It ended differently tonight.

The last star in the south went out, and the wards snapped.

There was no other word for it. Juniper felt it in his teeth first—a high, thin note, like a wet finger run around a crystal rim—and then in his chest, a sensation he could only describe as a thread pulled taut somewhere inside him suddenly *cut*. The shimmer along the ridgelines flickered once, twice, like a lamp running dry—

—and went out.

The valley walls stood naked against the dark for the first time in living memory. No silver breath. No lid on the pot. Just stone, and beyond the stone, whatever the stone had spent two centuries keeping out.

Every hair on Juniper's arms rose. The candle flames in the scriptorium leaned sideways, all together, all in the same direction, as if something enormous had exhaled.

Then the bells began.

* * *

They rang wrong. That was Juniper's first coherent thought as he joined the flood of bodies pouring into the temple courtyard—that the bells had a voice for every occasion, and none of them fit this. The dawn bell was bright. The debt-bell, rung when collections came due, was flat and greedy. The funeral bell tolled. But this sound was raw, arrhythmic, the ringers hammering without pattern, and it scraped down Juniper's spine like a nail.

Torches bloomed around the courtyard. The temple elders emerged onto the great steps in their grey robes, nine of them, arranged in their usual crescent, and High Elder Maren lifted her arms for silence she did not get for a long moment.

"The wards have failed," she said, when the noise finally broke apart enough for words to carry. "By now you have seen it. I will not soften it: the shield that has guarded this valley for two hundred years is broken."

The crowd made a sound Juniper had never heard before—not a gasp, not a scream, but a collective intake, hundreds of people inhaling at once, as if the whole valley had become one animal preparing to bolt.

"Until the wards are restored," Maren continued, "the gates are sealed. No one enters. No one leaves." Her gaze swept the courtyard, and Juniper watched it change—watched the grief in it harden into something more useful to her, something with edges. "And I know what you are asking. *How?* How could two centuries of protection fail in a single night?"

She let the question hang. Then her arm came up, slow and deliberate, and her finger swept across the crowd like a scythe.

"Because the covenant has been *starved*. Because faith has grown thin in certain houses. Because there are those among us—" the finger stopped, and Juniper's stomach dropped through the floor of the world, "—who have taken the temple's shelter while withholding the temple's dues."

Lowtown, Juniper thought. She means Lowtown. She means the debtors.

It was absurd. It was monstrous. Every soul in that courtyard owed something—to the temple, to a landlord, to a neighbor, to the past. Debt in Hollowmere wasn't a condition; it was the air. But Juniper understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching a fire decide which house to eat, that absurdity had never once stopped a mob.

"The ledgers will be reviewed," Maren announced. "Every account. Every name. Those whose devotion has lapsed will answer for it." Beside her, Juniper saw the grey-robed figures of the wardens already moving down into the crowd, lanterns swinging, and among them—tall, gaunt, unmistakable even at forty paces—High Warden Corvane, whose reputation needed no introduction because everyone in Hollowmere had heard someone scream in his keeping.

Juniper did the thing he had done his entire life. He made himself small.

He folded inward, dropped his chin, let his shoulders curve into the posture of a man carrying nothing worth taking. He had perfected invisibility the way other men perfected swordwork—through ten thousand hours of practice. Don't be interesting. Don't be remembered. Be the fourth scribe from the left, the one whose name is written correctly in no ledger because no one bothered to check the spelling.

It had worked for twenty-three years.

A lantern stopped in front of him.

"Scribe." Corvane's voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. Quiet meant he'd already decided something. "Whitlock. You keep the night ledgers."

"I—yes, High Warden."

"You copy the ward-prayers. Eleven thousand pages, they tell me." Corvane's face was a long vertical plane, all shadow and angles, and his eyes moved over Juniper like a man counting coins. "Devoted work. Tell me, scribe—when did you last see the wards shimmer?"

"Tonight," Juniper whispered. The truth escaped before he could weigh it. "I mean—I saw them go. From the scriptorium window. I was working."

Something flickered in Corvane's expression. Not belief. Interest. "Working. At the exact hour the covenant broke." He leaned closer, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Curious timing, Whitlock. Curious, that the man who has written more prayers than any soul in this valley should be awake to watch them fail."

"They failed because—" Juniper stopped himself. *Because the stars went out first. Because something cut a thread inside me. Because I felt it snap like it was tied to my own chest.* None of that was survivable to say aloud. "Because the ledger records say the anchors need verification," he finished instead. "I read it. Years ago. In the maintenance rolls."

Corvane went very still.

For three heartbeats, the only sounds were the bells and the torches cracking, and Juniper understood that he had just made a mistake of a size not yet measurable. He had made himself *interesting*.

"Verification," Corvane repeated softly. "Yes. We'll verify." He straightened, and his lantern swung away, and for one merciful moment Juniper thought it was over.

Then the gates rang.

Not the bells this time—the gates themselves, the great iron-bound doors at the valley's mouth, hammered from outside. Once. Twice. A third blow that Juniper felt through the flagstones, up through his boot soles, into the place behind his ribs where the cut thread still stung.

The whole courtyard turned toward the sound. The torches leaned again, all together, all the same direction, and this time there was no wind to blame.

From beyond the sealed gates, muffled by iron and oak and two hundred years of fear, came a voice. It was melodic and strange and utterly unhurried, and it spoke in the accentless tone of someone who had never once needed to raise it.

"Open your door, little valley. I've come a very long way to collect what's mine."

Beside Juniper, High Warden Corvane drew his sword without a word.

And on the inside of Juniper's left wrist, beneath the cuff he had hidden under sleeves his whole life, something answered the voice—with heat.
