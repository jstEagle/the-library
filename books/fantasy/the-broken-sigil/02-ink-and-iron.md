## 2. Ink and Iron

They came for him at the third bell, when the copying hall smelled of tallow and cold iron and the other scribes had gone to their pallets.

Juniper heard the boots first—two sets, heavy, unhurried. Wardens didn't hurry. Hurrying was for people who feared something, and wardens were the thing others feared. He set down his pen with a steadiness he did not feel and blotted the ledger line he'd been working: *grain tithe, autumn quarter, paid in full by the family Ashdown.* The last honest words he would write for some time, though he couldn't have known that then.

"Juniper Whitlock." The taller of the two wardens read it off a slate as if Juniper might have forgotten his own name. "High Warden Corvane will see you."

"Now?" The word escaped before he could cage it. "It's past the third bell."

"It's past the end of the world," the warden said. "Move."

The corridors of the temple were lit wrong tonight. Every sconce burned double its usual oil, and still the shadows pooled thick between them, as if the light itself were afraid to spread. Scribes pressed themselves against walls as Juniper passed. Old Master Ellery, who had taught him his letters, caught his sleeve for half a heartbeat.

"What did you do?" she whispered.

*Nothing.* That was the truth, and the truth had never felt so useless. "I don't know," he whispered back, and the warden's grip closed on his arm, and he was pulled forward into the dark.

* * *

The interrogation room beneath the temple had no windows, which meant no way to know whether the stars were still going out. Juniper thought about that constantly while he waited on the plain wooden stool—the only furniture besides Corvane's desk—with his hands folded in his lap like a good debtor awaiting an audit. His left wrist he kept turned inward, tucked against his thigh.

The birthmark sat there like a secret that had finally learned to whisper. It had never looked like this before. All his life it had been a faint brown smudge, the kind of thing a mother might kiss without comment. Tonight, when the wards snapped, it had woken. Now it was a sigil in truth—a knotwork star, sharp-lined, faintly warm, as if ink had been driven under his skin from the inside. He had scrubbed at it in the privy before they came. It hadn't faded. It had *pulsed*, once, like something acknowledging him.

Corvane entered without ceremony. High Warden of Hollowmere, keeper of the gates, owner—in every way that mattered—of four hundred debtors' lives. He was not a large man. He didn't need to be. He moved through rooms the way frost moves through a windowpane: quietly, and everything he touched went stiff.

"Scribe Whitlock." He sat, arranged three papers before him, and did not look up. "You've copied prayers for eleven years. Tithe ledgers for six. Your hand is considered the finest in the valley. Is that correct?"

"Yes, High Warden."

"Finest hands make finest witnesses." Still not looking up. "Where were you when the wards fell?"

"In the copying hall. Third bell. I was finishing the Ashdown entry."

"You saw nothing?"

"I saw—" Juniper's throat tightened. He had seen the stars go out one by one, like candles pinched by a patient hand. He had seen the sky over the wardline turn the color of a dead man's lips. But saying so felt like handing Corvane a knife and pointing at his own throat. "I saw the sky change. Everyone did."

"Everyone saw the sky change." Corvane finally raised his eyes. They were pale gray, the gray of temple slate, and they weighed things. "And what does a scribe feel, I wonder, when the wards snap? You've spent your life inside them. They're in your bones. When they broke, you must have felt it."

*I felt it like a thread pulling tight around my wrist,* Juniper did not say. *I felt it like something calling me home.*

"I felt afraid," he said.

"Mm." Corvane turned a paper toward him. A list of names. Debtors, all of them—families who owed the temple grain, labor, years. "The elders believe the wards were broken from within. Sabotage. Someone in the valley opened a door, and now the door won't close, and the things beyond are already testing the hinges. Do you understand what happens next, Scribe?"

"The gates are locked."

"The gates are locked. Curfew at dusk bell. Rations halved until we find the traitor." Corvane's voice never rose; that was the horror of it. "Four hundred people will go hungry this winter because one person chose to be clever. So I'm asking everyone the same questions, and I'm watching how they answer." He leaned back. "Show me your wrists."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, a bell rope creaked in wind that shouldn't reach this deep.

"My wrists?"

"Wardens are marked with the temple's brand. Debtors carry their tally-scar. Yours should show eleven years of service marks and your debt number. Show me."

Juniper's heart was a fist beating on a locked door. He extended his right wrist first—clean, familiar, the small crossed-lines scar of his tally. Then, slowly, he turned his left arm over, keeping the inner face angled away, showing only the outer edge where the skin was blank.

Corvane took his hand and rotated it anyway.

For one endless moment the sigil lay bare in the lamplight—the knotwork star, dark as old blood—and Juniper watched Corvane's eyes fix on it, watched the pale gray pupils narrow—

And the mark dimmed. Faded. Bled back into ordinary skin, a smudge, a nothing, a birthmark any mother might kiss.

Corvane stared at it. Then at Juniper's face. Then at the mark again.

"A stain," Juniper said, and heard his own voice as if from far away, calm and bored. "Ink. I've been at the ledgers since dawn. It doesn't wash out well anymore—the new batch is cut with ash."

Something flickered across Corvane's face. Not belief. Consideration. He released Juniper's wrist and made a small note on his papers.

"Eleven years," he said, "and I don't have a single reason to doubt you. That's either very good or very interesting, Scribe Whitlock. We'll find out which." He stood. "Return to the hall. If you remember anything—anything at all—you'll bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. Won't you."

It wasn't a question. "Yes, High Warden."

He was nearly at the door when the shouting started above.

* * *

They dragged the stranger in at the fourth bell, and Juniper, dismissed but not released, was herded against the corridor wall to watch.

She was tall and thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a traveler's cloak crusted with frost that did not melt indoors. Her hands were bound in iron that smoked faintly, hissing where it touched her skin, and she wore the burns without flinching, the way you'd wear rain. Her hair was white-blond, cropped short, and her eyes—

Her eyes were silver. Not gray. Silver, like moonlight given a pupil, like the stars that had gone out finding somewhere else to live.

The wardens threw her down the stairs ahead of them. She caught herself on bound wrists and rose to her feet in one motion, unhurried, as if the floor were hers and they were guests on it.

"Found at the wardline," one warden reported, breathless. "Walking straight toward the breach, sir. Didn't run when challenged. Cut Hask's pike in half with—" he faltered, "—with nothing, sir. Just looked at it."

"Name," Corvane demanded.

The stranger smiled. It was not a comforting smile. "You wouldn't be able to pronounce it. Your tongue is missing the sounds."

"Tongues can be removed."

"Then you'd have even less of me than you do now." She said it lightly, almost kindly, and the warden holding the chain took an involuntary step back.

Juniper should have kept his eyes on the wall. That was the rule of the valley: see nothing, be nothing, survive. Eleven years of practice. He was very good at invisibility.

But the stranger turned her head—deliberately, slowly, past the wardens, past Corvane—and found him against the wall.

Silver eyes met brown ones, and the sigil on Juniper's wrist woke like a struck bell.

It wasn't pain. It was worse: recognition. A warmth unspooling up his arm, into his chest, a feeling like hearing his own name spoken in a voice he'd never heard but somehow knew. The stranger's breath caught. He saw it catch. Whatever she'd come to the valley expecting, it wasn't this—it wasn't a shabby debtor-scribe pressed against cold stone, staring back at her with his whole hidden self suddenly, terribly visible.

"*There* you are," she said softly.

"Who is he talking to?" a warden barked. "Scribe! Did you speak to the prisoner?"

"No," Juniper said.

"He did not," the stranger agreed pleasantly. "We haven't been introduced. Though we're hardly strangers, are we."

"Take her below," Corvane said. His slate-gray gaze slid to Juniper and stayed there, weighing, reconsidering. "Cell nine. Double irons. And, Warden—" he didn't look away from Juniper, "—the scribe stays. I find I have more questions."

The wardens hauled her past him. As she passed, close enough that he could smell frost and something green underneath it, like sap, like spring held prisoner, she murmured—so low only he could hear:

"They'll ask you to lie for me eventually. Practice now. You're terrible at it."

Then she was gone down the stairs, silver eyes swallowed by the dark, and Juniper stood alone in the corridor with Corvane, with the fading warmth in his wrist, with the shape of his first lie still on his tongue.

"Interesting," Corvane said again, softer this time, and it sounded like a door closing.

Below them, muffled through stone, came the sound of the stranger laughing in her cell—light, unafraid, like someone who had already read the last page of the book.

Juniper realized his hands were shaking. He realized, too, that for the first time in eleven years, he wanted the shaking to stop—not because fear demanded it, but because defiance did.

Whatever she was. Whatever he was becoming. He was not going to let them write this story without him.

"Sit down, Scribe," said Corvane. "Let's try those questions again."
