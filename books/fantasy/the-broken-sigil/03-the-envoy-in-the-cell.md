## 3. The Envoy in the Cell

I told myself I was going back for my pen.

That was the lie I carried down the stair like a candle, cupping it against the draft, and I knew it was a lie the whole way because my pen was in my pocket. I'd felt its weight when Corvane's clerks finally released me—released me, as if interrogation were a service rendered—and sent me home with a warning to pray harder and copy faster. The temple wanted me docile. Docile men don't wander the undercrofts at midnight.

But the stranger with the silver eyes had looked at me like she knew me, and I had spent eleven years learning that being known was more dangerous than any crime. So of course I went toward it. Fear has always moved me backward.

The cells beneath the temple were older than the temple itself. The stones down there were fitted without mortar, dark with damp, and the air tasted of iron and old water—the same taste, I realized with a lurch, as the moment the wards snapped. Whatever ran beneath Hollowmere ran deepest here.

Two guards stood at the cell door. Both asleep. Not slumped, not snoring—standing, chins on chests, breathing slow as tide. I touched one man's shoulder and he swayed but did not wake, and my skin went cold all over, because men did not sleep like that. Men could be ordered not to sleep like that only by something that wasn't a man.

The lock was open. That frightened me worse than the guards. Someone wanted me to come in.

She sat cross-legged on the stone floor, wrists unbound, silver eyes catching what little light leaked through the grate overhead. Up close, in the dark, the wrongness of her resolved into details: her pupils were vertical, thin as script; her hair held a faint frost-sheen no candle could explain; and where her shadow should have stretched along the wall, there was instead a second silhouette, taller, antlered, that didn't move when she did.

"You came," she said. Her voice was low and dry, like a page turning. "I wondered if you would. Most debtors are broken early."

"I'm not—" I stopped. My whole life was sentences that started that way. *I'm not a debtor, I'm just poor. I'm not a prisoner, the valley just doesn't let you leave. I'm not important, please stop looking at me.* "Who are you?"

"Sylwen. Envoy of the Court of Standing Frost." She said it the way a priest says a name in liturgy—worn smooth by repetition. "And you are Juniper Whitlock, third-generation indentured scribe, born under the wardline in the hour the stars went out. Though your mother named you something else first, didn't she? Before they made her surrender it."

The floor tilted. Not really—but my body insisted it had. "My mother died when I was four."

"Your mother *fled* when you were four," Sylwen said, "and the temple wrote *died* in their ledgers because dead women make better lessons than escaped ones. You've been copying their lies since you could hold a pen. Haven't you ever wondered why a copying-house keeps a child indentured past every legal term? Why your debts never shrink no matter how many pages you fill?"

"Because interest compounds." I'd said it a thousand times. It sounded thinner each time.

"Because you're not paying a debt," she said. "You're being kept. Juniper Whitlock is collateral."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "Collateral for what? I copy prayers. I'm nobody. That's the entire point of me—that's how the system works, invisibility is survival—"

"Show me your wrist."

My hand moved before my mind agreed to it. Eleven years of hiding the birthmark, and one stranger's quiet command undid it all. I pushed back my sleeve. In the dark, the mark seemed to give off its own faint light: a spiral of interlocking lines, broken at its heart by a gap shaped like nothing so much as a keyhole.

Sylwen rose. This close, the antlered shadow behind her leaned with her, and I understood suddenly why the guards slept standing up, why the lock was open, why the whole undercroft felt like a held breath.

"That is half of the Sigil of Accord," she said. "The covenant between this valley and my realm, sealed nine hundred years ago. Your bloodline were the seal-keepers. The Whitlocks. When the covenant was struck, your ancestor gave his blood to bind the wards—not to keep magic *out*, whatever the priests taught you, but to keep the two realms in balance. Flow and return. Give and take. One sigil here, one sigil beyond the border, and between them, the valley lived warm and the court lived long."

"And now?" My mouth was dry.

"Now the temple lords stole the keystone—the heart of the sigil, the piece that let the halves speak to each other—and hid it beneath their own altar. They've been drinking the flow ever since. Siphoning the magic meant for both realms to feed themselves, growing fat and secret while the wards starved." Her vertical pupils narrowed. "Three nights ago, the last thread snapped. You felt it. Everyone felt it. And now winter is coming for both worlds, and it will not stop at the wardline, because there is no wardline anymore. There is only what your masters hoard, and what everyone else freezes for."

"My bloodline broke the covenant?" I whispered.

"Your bloodline *was* the covenant. And the temple has spent three generations making sure you'd never know it. Why do you think they keep debtors illiterate in everything but prayer-copies? Why do you think the Whitlock name appears in exactly one ledger, renewed year after year, never discharged?" She stepped closer, and the cold coming off her was clean, like snowmelt. "You weren't born into a cage, scribe. You were born into a lock. And they have kept you oiled and turned and ignorant of the door."

I sat down. Not gracefully—I simply stopped being able to stand. The stone was freezing through my trousers and I barely noticed. Everything I was rearranged itself around her words and found nothing left to stand on. Eleven years of small careful invisibility. Eleven years of believing the valley was a hard place and I was a lucky one to survive it. And underneath all of it, the whole time: a lock, waiting for a key, and me the key, filed down and shelved and forbidden to fit anything.

"They'll kill me," I said finally. "If they find out what I am."

"They already intend to. Why do you think Corvane questioned you twice about a birthmark he's seen in records older than his office?" Sylwen crouched before me, and up close her face was younger than her eyes, which were old the way rivers are old. "The lords want the last Whitlock under their thumb before winter finishes the job. You are the only living thing that can turn the keystone, Juniper. They can't use it without you. They can't destroy it without you. So they will own you or bury you, and either way the world ends cold."

"The stars went out," I said stupidly. "I watched them go out."

"I know. I crossed a border with no sky to guide by. Do you know what that costs one of my kind?" For the first time something moved across her face that might have been pain, quickly filed away. "The court sent me to find the seal-keeper's heir and bring him home. Home, Juniper. There is a place beyond the wardline where your blood means something other than debt."

*Home.* The word hit some bruised place in me I hadn't known was exposed. I had never once, in eleven years, used that word for the copying-house. I had never dared use it anywhere.

"I can't just leave," I said. "There are people—there's Old Marta in the infirmary, there are children in the debt-rows who—" I stopped, hearing myself. Defending the cage. Sylwen waited, patient as frost forming, and let me hear it too.

"The gates are locked," I said instead. "Corvane doubled the watch. Even if I believed you—even if any of this is true—"

"The guards are sleeping."

"The gate isn't."

"The catacombs run under it." She extended her hand. Her fingers were long and cool-looking, and the antlered shadow behind her spread wide against the wall like a door opening. "Flooded, half-collapsed, and utterly unknown to the temple that built on top of them without ever asking what was below. The valley keeps its secrets badly, scribe. It only keeps its people well."

I looked at her hand. I thought about my narrow cot, my ink-stained fingers, my ledger of prayers copied ten thousand times for a god who had never once answered. I thought about the stars going out one by one, and how the worst part hadn't been the darkness—it had been realizing that no one in power was surprised.

"If I go with you," I said slowly, "and this is a trap—if the fae want a weapon as much as the temple does—"

"Then you'll be a weapon that chose its wielder," Sylwen said. "It's more than you have now."

Somewhere above us, a bell began to ring. Not the prayer bell—wrong rhythm, urgent, the alarm they rang when something crossed the line. Boots thundered on the floors overhead. The sleeping guards stirred, groaning, coming up from wherever she'd put them.

Sylwen didn't lower her hand. "Decide faster, key-keeper. The temple is about to learn its lock is missing."

I took her hand. Her grip was cold and certain, and the mark on my wrist flared once—a brief, keyhole-shaped heat, like a word finally spoken after nine hundred years of silence.

"This way," she said, pulling me toward the black mouth of the catacombs. "And Juniper—when we're under, whatever you see the valley truly is, don't stop walking."

* * *

The last thing I saw of my old life was the ledger room, glimpsed sideways as we cut through the archive: ten thousand prayers in my own handwriting, row upon row, and not one of them answered.

Then the dark took us both, and the water rose to our knees, and we went down into the bones of Hollowmere.
