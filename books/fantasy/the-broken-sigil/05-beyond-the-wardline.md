## 5. Beyond the Wardline

*Juniper*

The wardline was a scar you could only see from the ridge.

Juniper had copied its description a hundred times in the temple ledgers—*the pale seam, running valley to valley, beyond which no debtor may pass*—but copying was not seeing. Up close it looked like frost that had forgotten how to melt, a band of white grass two paces wide, humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound is gone.

Sylwen crouched beside it, silver eyes narrowed. "It's still live. Thin, but live."

"You said it broke."

"The heart of it broke. The night your stars went out." She drew a knife with a blade like frozen smoke and scored a line across her own palm. Blood welled—dark, almost violet in the moonless dark. She pressed her hand flat to the frost-seam.

The hum changed pitch. The white grass shivered, and somewhere beneath them Juniper felt something vast turn over in its sleep, groaning, and then—a gap opened, narrow as a doorway, the frost thinning to translucence.

"Your blood would do this faster," Sylwen said. "Yours is half the lock."

"Then we'd be through already."

"Yes." She smiled without warmth. "But I promised Corvane's hounds a false trail, and bleeding you twice in one night seems wasteful. Come."

He hesitated at the edge. Behind him, Hollowmere lay black and shuttered, every lamp extinguished by order of the elders, the whole valley holding its breath around what it had lost. Somewhere back there, High Warden Corvane was assembling riders. Somewhere back there was everything Juniper had ever been: his cell, his desk, his ink-stained fingers, his debt that could never be paid because no one had ever told him what it purchased.

His whole life, he realized, had been a ledger entry. He was crossing out of the column.

He stepped through.

The sensation was like walking through a curtain of cold water that never touched him. Then—

Light.

Not starlight; there were no stars here either. But the land itself glowed faintly, the way old paper glows near a flame. Rolling hills the color of tarnished copper. Trees mid-turn, leaves caught between green and gold, neither falling nor greening. A river ran past them without moving, its surface a sheet of polished amber, fish suspended mid-leap like ornaments hung from invisible thread.

"It stopped," Juniper whispered. "The season."

"Mid-autumn," Sylwen said. "Three hundred years ago, more or less. The day the covenant broke." She walked ahead of him, boots silent on grass that did not bend. "Everything on this side has been holding its breath since. Waiting for someone to exhale."

"And if no one does?"

She glanced back at him. In the strange glow her face looked carved, ageless, beautiful the way a winter branch is beautiful. "Then the breath runs out. Winter comes for both realms, and it doesn't stop."

They walked until the sky lightened into a color Juniper had no name for—not dawn, not dusk, but the memory of both. His legs ached. His wrist throbbed where the sigil-mark sat beneath his sleeve, a slow pulse like a second heartbeat, and he noticed it had been pulsing in time with the land ever since they crossed.

"Sylwen." He kept his voice low. "Back in the cells. You said my bloodline sealed the covenant. Sealed it *and* broke it. Which was it?"

"Both." She didn't slow. "The Whitlocks were wardens once. The first line. Your ancestor's blood wrote the covenant into the keystone—mortal constancy binding fae wildness, each realm lending the other its missing season. Spring for us. Harvest for you. Balance."

"And then?"

"And then the temple lords of your valley wanted the magic without the price. They took the keystone. Buried it under their spire. The Whitlock line they didn't kill—they *kept*. Debtors, scribes, copyists. Generations of your family, filing the very prayers that fed the theft." Her voice went flat. "You weren't imprisoned, Juniper. You were archived."

The word landed like a stone in still water. He thought of his father, who had copied prayers until his eyes failed. Of his grandmother's ledger hand, which he'd been taught to imitate before he could write his own name well. Of the debt—always the debt—that passed down like eye color.

"I want to hate them," he said quietly. "I keep reaching for it and it won't catch."

"Hate is fuel. Save it for when you need to burn something." Sylwen stopped abruptly, one hand rising.

Juniper froze.

The amber river had gone silent. Not quiet—*silent*, the way a room goes when someone is listening behind the door. The suspended fish trembled. The copper hills dimmed as though a cloud had crossed a sun that wasn't there.

"What is it?" he breathed.

"Ambush," she said. "Don't run. Running marks you."

They came out of the ground.

That was the only way Juniper could describe it afterward—the hillside simply opened, and figures rose out of it like reeds out of floodwater. Tall, gaunt, crowned in antlers of black ice, their skin the gray of things left out in frost too long. Their eyes were hollows filled with drifting snow. There were six of them, then nine, fanning out across the dead grass with blades of jagged glass.

"Frostbound," Sylwen said, and for the first time since he'd known her, her voice held something like fear. "Court exiles. They felt the ward break too—and they know what walks the border now." Her smoke-knife slid free. "They can smell covenant blood."

One of them turned its snow-filled eyes toward Juniper.

"*Thief,*" it said. Its voice was wind through a broken window. "*The line that unmade the seasons.*"

"We've come to mend it—" Juniper started.

The creature moved. It shouldn't have been able to move that fast; nothing that gaunt should cross twenty paces in a blink. But it did, glass blade sweeping for his throat, and Sylwen was too far, and Juniper's body made a decision his mind hadn't approved.

He raised his marked wrist.

The sigil flared.

Heat lanced up his arm—white, blinding, *hungry*—and the air between him and the blade thickened into something visible, a lattice of burning lines, the ghost of an enormous sigil hanging in the air like a door seen edge-on. The glass blade struck it and shattered into a spray of glittering dust. The Frostbound staggered back, snow-eyes wide, and the other exiles went utterly still.

For one heartbeat, Juniper felt *vast*. Connected—to the land, to the stopped river, to the sleeping covenant beneath everything. He could feel the shape of the broken sigil the way he could feel the shape of his own hands. He could almost read it. Almost finish it.

Then the cold came.

It started in his fingertips and climbed. Not the cold of the borderland—this was colder, an interior cold, a cold with teeth. Warmth drained out of him like water through open fingers, and he watched, distantly, as the color left his own knuckles. Something in his chest—a small warm thing he hadn't known was there until now—simply *went out*, snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

The lattice faded. Juniper dropped to one knee in the unmoving grass, gasping, shivering in a world that suddenly seemed much further away than it had a moment before.

"Juniper!" Sylwen was beside him, gripping his shoulder. Her hand was warm—or his was so cold now that anything felt warm. "Breathe. Look at me. Breathe."

"That's the cost," he managed. His voice sounded strange to himself. Flatter. "Isn't it. Every time I use it."

She didn't answer fast enough, and the answer was in the slowness anyway.

"How much is left in me?" he asked.

"I don't know. No one knows. The last warden who fought with the mark burned out in a single night." She hauled him upright, arm braced across his shoulders. "Which is why you don't use it again unless you're dying. Understood?"

Around them, the Frostbound had not fled. They stood in a loose crescent, blades lowered but not sheathed, snow swirling in their hollow eyes. The one whose blade had shattered tilted its head—an oddly human gesture from something so far from human.

"*The mark wakes,*" it rasped. "*After three hundred years, the mark wakes.*" It raised one clawed hand, and the others stilled. "*Go then, little ember. Go to the Court of Standing Frost and tell them the thief-line returns. Tell them Vaelith's hunt begins at moonrise.*"

"Moonrise," Sylwen repeated grimly. "We have hours."

"Who is Vaelith?"

"The court's Hunt-Master." She pulled him toward the amber river, away from the watching exiles. "And the reason I told you not to come to the court expecting friends. Walk faster."

* * *

*Sylwen*

She had lied to him about the cost. Not entirely—but enough.

There were accounts, in the old records she'd memorized as an envoy, of what the covenant mark took. Not just warmth. *Warmth first*, yes, but warmth was only the outermost layer, the way bark is the outermost layer of a tree. Beneath it went other things. The records used careful words: *affect. Appetite. Attachment.* The wardens who burned brightest burned fastest, and what remained at the end was a person-shaped thing that could recite the covenant perfectly and feel nothing about it.

Sylwen had read those accounts as history. Now she walked beside the newest entry, feeling his weight against her shoulder, lighter than it should be, and understood she was reading a future.

He stumbled. She caught him.

"There's a waystation ahead," she said. "An old shrine, pre-covenant. We can rest until moonrise."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I learned from the best." A ghost of his old dry tone, there and gone. "You lied to me for three days in a cell."

"I lied to get you *out* of a cell. Different discipline entirely." She eased him down onto a fallen tree whose wood was petrified mid-fall, frozen in the act of dying. "Eat."

He ate the waybread she gave him mechanically, and that frightened her more than the Frostbound had. Three days ago, in the temple cells, this man had asked questions with his whole face—fear and fury and stubborn curiosity all fighting for space. Now the questions came slower. The face was quieter.

"How long have you been watching me?" he asked suddenly. "Before the wards broke. Before the arrest. How long?"

Sylwen weighed the truth and found it heavier than she expected.

"Eleven years," she said. "Since you were sixteen. I've been in Hollowmere eleven years, Juniper. Baker's apprentice. Chapel sweeper. Two winters as a tanner's clerk." She watched that land in him. "The court sent me the day the temple lords moved the keystone deeper. Someone had to watch the last Whitlock. Someone had to be ready."

"Eleven years." He stared at the motionless river. "So even the stranger who talked to me in the market was—"

"Me. Yes. You bought bad apples and complained about the price. I remember all of it." She paused. "Every word."

"Why?" His voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "Why watch? Why not just take me over the border years ago?"

"Because taking you wasn't the mission. *Keeping you* was. If the temple moved against you, I intervened. If the court moved to seize you, I intervened. You were the last living half of the covenant, and everyone wanted to spend you, and my orders were to make sure nobody spent you before the right moment." She met his eyes. "Including me. Especially me."

"And now?"

"Now the moment is here whether any of us are ready or not." She stood, listening. Far off, across the stopped hills, something was beginning to move—a pressure in the air, a wrongness, like the silence before a storm decides. "And now the Frostbound have told the court we're coming, and Vaelith's hunt rides at moonrise, and the temple riders will find our false trail by morning. We are caught between two armies and a deadline, and the only weapon either side wants is currently shivering on a log eating waybread."

Juniper looked down at his hands. Turned them over. The knuckles were still pale.

"When I blocked that blade," he said slowly, "for half a second I could *see* it. The whole sigil. Like a page I'd been copying my whole life, except I finally saw what the words meant." He flexed his fingers. "It's fading already. Like waking from a dream—you know you knew it, and then you don't."

"Then we get you to the keystone before it fades completely." Sylwen offered her hand. "Can you walk?"

He took her hand and rose. For a moment he swayed, and she steadied him, and he said, quietly, "Thank you. For eleven years. Even if I'm just... a mission."

Something twisted under Sylwen's ribs, in the place where fae keep the things they pretend not to have.

"You were never just—" she began.

The horn sounded.

It rolled across the motionless hills from the direction of the court—a sound like ice cracking in deep water, long and low and getting longer. The amber river shivered. The suspended fish scattered like startled birds. Above them, where stars should have been, the empty sky flickered once, twice, as though something enormous were blinking awake.

"Moonrise," Sylwen whispered. But the moon wasn't rising. The moon was *coming*—she could feel it now, a vast cold attention sliding over the horizon of the world, and riding beneath it, closing fast, the hunt.

Juniper's mark flared once, unprompted, answering the horn like a bell answers a hammer.

"They know exactly where we are now," he said.

"Yes." Sylwen drew her smoke-knife and turned to face the darkening hills. "Run anyway."
