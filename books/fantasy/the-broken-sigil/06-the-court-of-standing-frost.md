## 6. The Court of Standing Frost

The court had not moved in a hundred years.

Sylwen had told Juniper this on the road, and she watched him fail to understand it until they crested the last ridge and he saw it himself: the Court of Standing Frost, frozen mid-gesture. Courtiers caught between one step and the next. A fountain whose water hung in silver ropes. A queen's hand raised in welcome or warning—no one alive remembered which—and held there, glittering, while autumn refused to become winter and winter refused to end.

"It isn't dead," Sylwen said. "It's *waiting*. There's a difference."

Juniper said nothing. He had said very little since the wardline. He walked like a man carrying something breakable and was too polite to ask anyone else to hold it. His wrist, where the mark lived beneath his sleeve, had gone quiet since the ambush—the blade it had turned aside lay three miles back, split down its middle like a struck bell—but Sylwen could feel him anyway, a low hum through her teeth, the way you feel weather coming.

She hated how much she could feel him.

*You need him,* she reminded herself. *That is all this is.* The covenant needed its mortal half standing at the keystone when the reforging came. Everything else—the way he'd given his bread ration to a starving hedge-spirit without being asked, the way he flinched from praise but leaned toward kindness like a plant toward light—was irrelevant. Sentiment was a luxury of realms that still had seasons.

They passed under the gate, and the frost recognized them.

It always did. Frost was memory made mineral; it remembered every oath ever sworn in this court, and it parted for Sylwen with a sound like a long-held breath released. For Juniper it did something else entirely. It *bowed*. The ice along the gateposts dipped and shivered as he walked between them, and somewhere deep in the frozen hall, something ancient turned over in its sleep.

Juniper stopped. "Did you feel that?"

"Keep walking," Sylwen said. "They'll want you to feel it."

* * *

The throne room had been beautiful once. You could tell because the frost preserved beauty the way amber preserved flies. Chandeliers hung mid-fall, their crystals arrested like rain. Musicians stood with bows raised over strings that would never sound again. And at the far end, on a throne of black ice that alone in all the court remained unfrozen—alive, awake, *hungry*—sat Queen Vessandra of the Unmelting Hour.

Her eyes found Juniper before Sylwen finished announcing him. Her smile did not reach them.

"The scribe," she said. Her voice filled the room the way cold fills a valley. "The valley's little copyist. Do you know what you copied, child? All those prayers, all those ledgers. Did you ever wonder why Hollowmere's temple needed ten thousand pages of prayer a year?"

"I assumed faith," Juniper said.

Vessandra laughed. It was not a kind sound, but it was an honest one, and Sylwen found herself grateful for that much. "Faith. Yes. Your masters have been harvesting ours for three centuries, scribe. Every prayer your temple filed away was a thread pulled from our side of the loom. They did not build wards to keep magic out of Hollowmere." She leaned forward on her living throne. "They built a funnel. Your valley is a tap stuck into the vein of the world, and your temple lords have been drinking from it since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to write."

Sylwen watched the truth land on him. She had expected denial—mortals clung to their temples the way drowning men clung to driftwood, even when the driftwood was what dragged them under. But Juniper only went very still, the stillness of a man rereading a page he has copied a thousand times and finally seeing the error in it.

"The wards snapped," he said slowly. "When I crossed the border. Corvane said the debtors broke them."

"Corvane says what keeps him fed," Sylwen said quietly. "The wards didn't break, Juniper. They were *finished*. Three hundred years of siphoning, and the keystone that regulated the flow—they stole it, sealed it beneath their temple, and hoarded everything it gathered. Without it, the covenant can't balance. Magic pools on our side and starves on yours. Autumn froze mid-step because there was nothing left to turn it. Your stars went out because—" she hesitated, then gave him the whole wound, "—because the stars are the covenant's eyes, and someone sewed the lids shut."

"My blood," Juniper said. "Sylwen told me my line sealed the original covenant."

"And broke it," Vessandra said, "or so the story goes. Your ancestor was meant to be the lock's other half—a mortal hand on the mortal side, forever. Instead your line hid inside the valley, took temple ink, took temple debt, and let the lock rust shut." She rose. The frost did not bow to her; the frost *flinched*. "So. You will understand that we do not greet you as a guest."

"No," Juniper said. "I'm starting to understand that."

"You will stand at the keystone when we take it back. Your blood will open what your blood closed. And when the covenant is reforged—" her smile sharpened, "—you will be the hinge of a new age, scribe. The mortal realm will owe us a debt three centuries deep, and you will be the instrument by which we collect it."

And there it was. Sylwen had known it was coming; she had argued against this audience, argued for taking him to the keystone directly, but the Queen wanted her claim witnessed, and queens always got their witnessing. Still, hearing it spoken aloud in front of him was different from knowing it. Shame has a temperature. Sylwen felt hers climb her throat.

"A partner," Juniper said. "Or a weapon."

Vessandra spread her hands, gracious as a closing trap. "Is there a difference, in the end? Every tool believes itself beloved."

* * *

They gave him a chamber in the east wing, which was a courtesy, and posted guards outside it, which was the truth. Sylwen came to him after dark, past the frozen courtiers, past the fountain with its silver ropes of stopped water, and found him sitting on the floor with his back against the wall, copying something.

Of course he was copying. Give the man a candle and a scrap of charcoal and he would make a ledger of the apocalypse.

"What are you writing?" she asked.

"Everything she said. Word for word." He didn't look up. "Ink remembers better than people do. If I'm going to be somebody's instrument, I'd like the record kept straight about who played me."

Sylwen sat down across from him. The floor was cold enough to bite, and he noticed her wince and shifted sideways to block the draft from the window without comment, without even seeming to notice he'd done it. That was the problem with Juniper Whitlock. The kindness was structural. You couldn't argue with it any more than you could argue with load-bearing walls.

"You knew," he said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing—*checking*. "Before we crossed. You knew the court would want this."

"I knew the court would want *him*," she said. "The keystone-half. What form that wanting took depended on what you were when you arrived." She met his eyes. "I hoped you'd arrive angry. Angry men make demands. You arrived polite, and polite men get used."

Something moved behind his face—not hurt exactly. Recalculation. "And you? What do you want, Sylwen? The envoy who pulled me out of a cell. Was that mercy, or retrieval?"

"Retrieval," she said, because lying to him had stopped working somewhere around the second night, and because he deserved one person in either realm who didn't lie to him. "I need the covenant reforged or my people freeze. You were the key. I went and got the key."

"But?"

He always heard the *but*. She looked at the charcoal in his ink-stained fingers, at the careful small letters marching across the scrap, at the man the temple had spent thirty years teaching to be invisible, and who had turned a fae blade with his bare wrist rather than let harm come to the woman sent to fetch him.

"But I keep forgetting you're a key," she said. "You keep being a person. It's very inconvenient."

He almost smiled. Then the smile went out, and he said, quietly: "If I stand at that keystone and open it for her—if I give her the collection she wants—every debtor in Hollowmere pays it. Corvane's riders. The children in the warding-houses. The old women who taught me my letters. My people, Sylwen. They didn't steal anything. They prayed in a building they didn't know was a funnel."

"And if you don't," Sylwen said, "my people freeze. Mid-autumn. Forever. Children who will never see another spring because your temple lords got greedy before either of us was born." She leaned forward. "That's the shape of it, Juniper. That's the whole ugly shape. Two realms, one keystone, and everyone in both of them wanting you to pay a bill you never ran up."

He was silent a long time. Somewhere below, the frozen musicians held their note that would never sound.

"When I use the mark," he said at last, "something goes out of me. Warmth. A piece of it, every time, and it doesn't come back. I turned that blade and lost—I don't know. The taste of my mother's cooking. Maybe just the *wanting* of it." He looked at his wrist. "I've been thinking maybe that's what the reforging is. Not a ritual. An expenditure. Someone has to be the coin."

"*No.*" It came out of her harder than she meant. "The covenant takes blood and oath, not—"

"Not what? Not a person?" He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen yet, because it was gentle. "Sylwen. Everyone in this story has been spending someone. The temple spent the valley. The valley spent the debtors. Your queen means to spend me. The only question left is whether I get to choose what I'm spent *on*."

Boots in the corridor. Many boots, moving fast, in the disciplined cadence Sylwen had learned to dread on the border roads—fae guards did not march like that. Mortal soldiers did.

The door burst open, and the guard captain stumbled in, frost crackling off her armor, and the look on her face was the look of a soldier bringing news no one had paid her enough to carry.

"Envoy." She was breathing hard. "Riders at the wardline. Temple riders, armed for war, flying truce-banners—and their commander is asking for the scribe by name." She swallowed. "By his *full* name. High Warden Corvane says he's come to take Juniper Whitlock home."

Juniper rose slowly, charcoal still in his hand, and Sylwen saw the choice land on him like a yoke—one realm on each shoulder—and watched him square himself beneath it.

"Well," he said. "Let's not keep him waiting."
