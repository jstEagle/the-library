## 7. Corvane's Bargain

They took me at the ford.

I never heard the riders—Sylwen says the temple horses are shod in silence-iron, that Corvane paid a fortune for it back when he still believed in something—but I felt them, the way you feel a storm building behind hills. The mark on my wrist woke first, prickling like a limb gone to sleep, and then the trees along the water went quiet in the wrong way, birds lifting all at once, and Sylwen's hand closed hard around my arm.

"Down," she said.

I was already down. The net came out of the dark anyway, weighted with iron beads, and it hit me like a falling ceiling. I remember the taste of mud. I remember Sylwen screaming my name—not *Whitlock*, which no one had ever bothered to use, but *Juniper*, sharp and furious—and then a white flash of her power splitting the night, and then hands, so many hands, and the smell of temple incense masking horse sweat as they dragged me up onto a rider's saddle and tied my wrists with prayer-cord, the knots blessed so tight they burned.

The last thing I saw of the forest was frost blooming across every leaf in a widening circle, spreading from where she stood, and then the trees swallowed it.

* * *

They held me three days in a waystation chapel, chained to an altar rail that some pious mason had carved with warding sigils a hundred years dead. I knew they were dead because I'd copied their pattern into ledgers a hundred times. It was a strange comfort, sitting inside useless magic, knowing its whole grammar by heart.

On the third day, Corvane came.

He dismissed his guards, which surprised me. He sat on the front pew like a man visiting a grave he'd dug himself, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. Up close he looked worse than he had in the interrogation room—greyer, thinner, the high collar of his office sitting loose on a neck that had lost flesh. Whatever the temple masters wanted from him, it was eating him alive.

"You crossed the wardline," he said finally. "You, Juniper Whitlock. Debtor class, third generation, scribe's indenture, value assessed at—" he recited it from memory, my whole life reduced to figures, "—four hundred marks. You crossed the border of the only safe place left in the world, with a fae envoy, during a crisis of faith and state."

"It wasn't hard," I said. "There was a hole in the wall."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Corvane didn't smile; he permitted expressions.

"Do you know what the stars going out means?" he asked. "The elders tell the people it's a test. That the faithful will be rewarded." He turned a ring on his finger, over and over. "I've read the old records, scribe. The ones not kept in your scriptorium. Do you know what I found?"

"The valley is dying," I said. "Both valleys. Both worlds."

His eyes came up fast. For one heartbeat there was something almost like respect in them, and I understood—I'd been right about this man since the interrogation room—he was not a believer. He was a functionary who had begun, late and unwillingly, to see the shape of the machine he served.

"Then you understand the position," he said. "The court beyond the wardline has sent envoys before. Every one of them ended badly. This one—Sylven, Sylvain—"

"Sylwen."

"This one has done something different. She found *you*." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the silver threading his dark eyes—the same silver as hers, though his was fainter, diluted through generations, a river run thin. I'd never noticed it before. Or maybe it hadn't been there before. Maybe proximity to the broken covenant woke things in old blood. "Your family line, Whitlock. Your blood. Half the keystone's key has been sleeping in your veins since before the temple was built, and the other half is walking around wearing a face you've apparently grown attached to."

"I haven't—"

"Don't lie to me. Lying is my profession." He rose, paced to the altar, looked up at the empty niche where a god-statue had been sold off decades ago to pay temple debts. Even the gods were debtors here. "Here is what I can do. The masters want you brought back in chains and bled slowly for whatever's in you. I can offer you something better. Full discharge. Every mark of your family's debt, struck from the ledger in your own hand—you'd write the entry yourself, that's how binding it would be. Freedom papers. Passage anywhere in the valley. A life."

"And in exchange."

"You bring her in." He turned. "Not dead. The masters need her alive too, for now. Just—delivered. You know how she travels, how she thinks, where she'll go. You're the only bait that works on her, and the only hook she'll rise to. One meeting place, one signal, and you walk away free while the rest of us try to save two worlds with what's left."

It was a good offer. That was the horror of it. Four hundred marks, gone. My mother's debts, my grandfather's, the whole inherited weight of being Whitlock, struck away with one line of ink in my own careful hand. I had spent ten thousand hours copying prayers for other people's salvation, and here was mine, priced and offered, requiring only that I hand over the one person who had ever told me the truth about myself.

And it was a lie, all of it, or enough of it. Because I had seen beneath the temple. Well—she had seen, and shown me. The wards didn't protect anyone. They siphoned. The masters weren't failing to save the valley; they were the reason it was starving. Delivering Sylwen wouldn't save two worlds. It would feed the mouths that had eaten the first one.

But Corvane didn't know I knew. And a man offering you freedom in good faith is worth more than a man offering it in bad ones—if I could keep him believing, I could buy time. Time to reach the spire. Time to reach the keystone below the temple, which Sylwen swore lay under the sanctum, behind doors only debtor-blood could open. They had built their vault out of the very people they enslaved. There was a poetry in that I intended to weaponize.

So I did the hardest thing I have ever done with my mouth.

"All right," I said.

Corvane went very still.

"She trusts me," I continued, and heard my own voice go flat and careful, scribe's diction, the voice I used for copying words I didn't believe. "She thinks I'm the key to everything. She'll follow me anywhere. Give me three days and a meeting point near the old mill at Graywater. I'll signal with the mark—it flares when I want it to, now. She'll come to me alone."

"You'd sell her that easily?" He studied me, and there it was—the thing I feared most, worse than torture, worse than the bleeding the masters planned. Doubt. Corvane was looking at me the way he must have looked at every confession he'd ever extracted, sorting truth from performance, and I could not tell which side of the ledger he placed me on. "Three days ago you lied to me in an interrogation room to protect a stranger. Now you offer her up in four sentences."

"People change," I said. "I saw what's coming, Warden. Winter without end. I'd rather be free when it comes."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the chapel, wind worried at a loose shutter, patient as a creditor.

Corvane nodded once, slow. "Graywater mill. Three days. If this is a game, Whitlock, understand that I have played it longer than you've been alive, and the house always wins." He unlocked my chains himself, which I hadn't expected, and pressed a token into my palm—a small iron seal, the warden's crest, good for passage through any checkpoint in the valley. "Go. And Juniper—" my name in his mouth sounded strange, almost gentle, "—whatever you actually intend, decide if you can live with it. That's the only advice I have left in me."

He walked out. I sat on the chapel floor holding the iron seal and shaking, because the lie had worked, and working, it had taken something. I had said those words in Sylwen's voice's hearing distance of my own conscience, and my conscience had answered: *noted, filed, pending.*

* * *

She was waiting in the ruins of a shepherd's cot two miles south, exactly where our fallback plan said to wait, and I almost turned back at the door rather than walk in and say what I had to say. But time was bleeding out of the three days Corvane had given me, and winter was bleeding faster, so I went in.

She took one look at my face and knew.

"What did he offer?" she asked.

"Freedom. All of it. The whole debt." I set the iron seal on the stone sill between us, evidence of the bargain. "In exchange for you."

The frost in her eyes didn't change temperature. That was the terrible thing about Sylwen—her anger came out as stillness, as a kind of glacial attention that made you feel measured, weighed, found wanting.

"And you said—"

"I said yes."

The silence after that lasted long enough for me to count eleven heartbeats. When she spoke, her voice was quiet in the way deep water is quiet.

"You said yes."

"I said yes *to him*. Sylwen, listen—"

"No." She stood. The temperature of the room dropped, actual frost feathering up the walls, her control slipping for the first time since I'd known her. "I listened outside the chapel. Iron doesn't stop my ears, Juniper, only my hands. I heard every word. *She'll follow me anywhere.* You let him believe it. You let *me* stand there and hear you spend me like coin."

"To buy time!" The words tore out of me louder than I meant. "Three days, Sylwen. Three days where Corvane thinks I'm his, where the checkpoints open for this seal, where the masters think their bait is on the hook instead of swimming for the spire. What was I supposed to do—refuse nobly and get bled dry in a cellar while you tried to crack the sanctum alone? I lied because lying was the only move that kept us both alive!"

"Lied." She tasted the word like spoiled milk. "In my court, we have a word for saying one thing to a hunter's face while meaning another. We also have a word for what it costs. You mortals think lies are free because no one collects immediately." She picked up the iron seal, turned it over once, and set it down with a click that sounded final as a lock. "The debt-lords wrote your name in a ledger and you've hated it all your life. So the first chance you get, you start writing entries yourself. His entry. Mine."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, terribly calm again, the frost retreating, which was somehow worse than the rage. "It isn't. That's what makes it work. Come, then. We have a spire to reach and a keystone to steal, and two days before your warden learns whether his hook caught anything." She paused at the doorway, her back to me, silver hair catching the grey light. "I believe you, Juniper. That's the worst part. I believe you lied for strategy, and I believe you'd do it again, and I believe someday you'll lie to *me* with the same steady voice and mean it less."

Then she was gone into the grey morning, and I stood alone in a ruined shepherd's cot holding a warden's seal and a stranger's distrust, and understood the price of the bargain had never been Corvane's to charge.

Two days to the temple. Two days to prove that a liar's ink could still write something true.

Behind me, far to the north, the last star above Hollowmere flickered like a candle in a draft—and went out.
