## 8. The Keystone Below

**JUNIPER**

The temple's undercroft had never appeared on any map Juniper had copied. He'd spent eleven years transcribing the valley's records—tithes, debts, ward-maintenance schedules, the genealogies of every family that owed the temple its bread—and in all that ink there was not one line about what lay beneath the High Sanctum. That absence should have told him something. In Hollowmere, everything was written down. Everything except the things that mattered.

Now he stood at the top of a stair that spiraled down into blackness, and the ledger-keeper in him understood, with a cold and settling certainty, that he had been copying around a hole his whole life.

"You're stalling," Sylwen said behind him.

"I'm reading." He held up the wall. In the lantern light, faint as breath on glass, lines of script crawled across the stone—old covenant-script, the same hand that haunted the margins of the temple's oldest prayer-books. "It says *the debt is carried down*. Every time someone new descends, it rewrites itself."

Sylwen touched the letters and they brightened under her fingers like embers stirred. "Then we're expected."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." She took the lantern from him. Her silver eyes caught the light and gave it back wrong, the way moonlight gives light back to water. Three days since she'd overheard him bargain with Corvane. Three days of silence sharp enough to draw blood. But when the temple riders had turned on them both—when Corvane's masters had ordered *both* prisoners taken to the deep cells—she'd cut his ropes without a word, and that was as close to forgiveness as the dark allowed.

He followed her down.

The stairs went further than the temple was tall. Juniper counted out of habit—he counted everything, steps and candles and coins of debt—and lost the number somewhere past four hundred, in a place where the air stopped smelling of incense and started smelling of rain on old iron. Water dripped somewhere ahead, slow and patient, like a clock counting toward something.

"Sylwen." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "When we find it. The keystone. What does it ask for?"

She didn't turn. "Blood. It always asked for blood. Your line wrote that clause."

"And after?"

"After," she said, "it asks for more."

* * *

**SYLWEN**

The catacombs beneath Hollowmere were older than the temple above them, and Sylwen could feel the difference the way a musician feels a false note. Mortal work rang hollow. This stone *sang*—faintly, bitterly, the way a lute sings with a broken string. It was covenant-stone, fae-cut, hauled into this valley three thousand years ago by hands that had trusted the Whitlocks to keep their word.

And here, in the dark, she finally saw what the wards were for.

They came out of the stairwell into a vaulted hall where roots of pale light grew down through the ceiling like inverted trees. Ward-light. Siphoned magic, drawn up from the land itself and channeled downward, thread by golden thread, into—

Into a cage.

At the center of the hall, suspended in a lattice of iron and scripture, hung a shard of crystal the size of a grown man. Light moved inside it like weather. And all around it, feeding into it, the stolen threads of the valley's magic gathered and knotted and sank, swallowed year by year, season by season, while the fields thinned and the winters lengthened and the temple lords grew fat on prayers.

"They're not keeping magic *out*," Juniper said hoarsely beside her. "All those lessons. All the fear. They were farming us."

"Yes."

"Three thousand years."

"Longer. Your family sealed the original covenant to stop exactly this—the first lords who tried to hoard the land's blood. The keystone was the lock. They stole the lock and made it a tap." She looked at him. He had gone very still, the way he did when a ledger refused to balance. "This is why I needed you, Juniper. Not your mark. Not your blood alone. You're the last hand that knows how to write the covenant closed. A scribe. They kept your line illiterate for centuries, and then one of you learned letters anyway, copying prayers in the dark. Did you never wonder why a debtor was taught to read?"

He didn't answer. Above them, far away, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the temple bells began to ring—not the hour, but the alarm.

"They know we're here," Juniper said.

"They've always known. Now they're coming to stop us." Sylwen drew her knife and felt the old cold clarity settle over her, the envoy's calm. Except it wasn't calm anymore. Somewhere between the border and this drowned dark, the soft-spoken scribe had become necessary to her in a way she had no clean word for, and necessity, among her people, was the beginning of every dangerous thing. "Juniper. Whatever happens next—I need you to understand that I chose you before I knew I was choosing."

He looked at her, and for once the fear in his face had something else folded inside it, like a letter within a letter.

"Then let's not be late," he said.

* * *

**JUNIPER**

They almost reached the cage.

Juniper had the covenant-script open in his mind, the words he'd spent three days memorizing from Sylwen's recitation, and his blood was already singing in his wrist where the birthmark lived, and he was reaching for the first iron strut when the light changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed*—from gold to white, hard and total, and the shadows of the inverted ward-trees swung like scythes.

High Warden Corvane stood at the far end of the hall.

Juniper had seen him angry. He had seen him cruel, in the interrogation room, in the way he'd smiled at the stranger with silver eyes being dragged in chains. But he had never seen Corvane look like this: coat torn, one arm bound in a field dressing gone brown with old blood, face stripped of everything except a kind of terrible arithmetic.

"Don't touch it," Corvane said.

"You're too late," Sylwen said.

"I'm not talking to you, fae." He came forward, limping, and each step echoed off the vaulting. "I'm talking to the scribe. Do you know what I found tonight, boy? In the Archons' private vault, where they sent me to fetch the punishment ledgers?" He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "I found my own name. My father's name. Four generations of Wardens, written in the same book as the tithes. We aren't servants of the temple, Juniper. We're *line items*. They've been spending Warden blood to keep these wards fed for two hundred years. My brother didn't die at the border. He was *harvested*."

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well.

"So don't touch it," Corvane said again, closer now, and Juniper saw the knife in his good hand and understood, finally, that the man was not pointing it at them. "Because if you break the covenant wrong—if you seal it with the script half-written, the way they want it written—they'll take the surge and the valley dies and my brother's blood bought nothing. The Archons left me orders. *Kill the scribe before he reaches the stone.*"

Sylwen's blade cleared its sheath.

Corvane flipped the knife, caught it by the flat, and held it out to Juniper, handle first.

"I've spent my whole life believing the debt ran one direction," he said. "I'm done paying it. So here's my last order as High Warden, and you can take it or put this knife in me: *write it right*, scribe. All of it. Even the parts that cost."

Juniper took the knife. His hand shook only a little.

Above them the bells stopped ringing, which was worse than the ringing, because it meant the Archons had stopped warning and started descending.

"The stairs," Sylwen said quietly. "How many come?"

"All of them," Corvane said. "But there's another way up. The old processional stair—it lets out inside the spire. Where the keystone's crown-anchor sits." He met Juniper's eyes, and something passed between them that had no name in any ledger either man had ever copied. "You'll have minutes. Maybe less."

"Then we spend them well," Juniper said.

Sylwen snuffed the lantern, and in the sudden dark the keystone blazed like a captive star, and the three of them—scribe, envoy, warden; debtor, spy, traitor; the last hands of two broken peoples—turned together toward the light going up.

Behind them, far below, something in the cage began, very softly, to sing.
