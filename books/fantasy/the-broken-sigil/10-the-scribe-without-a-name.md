## 10. The Scribe Without a Name

Spring came to Hollowmere the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

Sylwen stood at the wardline where the frost had held for three hundred years and watched the last of it go. The ice did not melt so much as *remember* it was water. It slid off the standing stones in long bright ribbons, pooled in the ruts of the old cart road, and by midmorning the valley floor was a mirror holding a sky that had stars in it again, even at noon, if you knew how to look.

She knew how to look. She would always know how to look.

The gates opened on the first day of thaw. Not broken open, not torn down—opened, on hinges groaning with rust, swung wide by hands that had forgotten they were allowed. The temple elders had surrendered their keys to a council of debtors and dyers and orchard-keepers, and Corvane had stood on the gatehouse steps in his stripped-bare warden's coat and read out the terms of a covenant no one had understood until a scribe died to mend it.

*The valley keeps what it was given,* he had said, his voice cracking on the old formula. *And gives back what it took.*

Below him, someone laughed—a startled, disbelieving sound—and then the whole crowd was laughing and weeping at once, and Sylwen had turned away because fae do not weep in public, and she had already spent more of her dignity than she intended on mortal soil.

That had been forty days ago. Now she walked the road into the valley for the last time, or what she told herself was the last time, carrying nothing but her knife and a ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart.

* * *

The scriptorium still smelled of tallow and iron-gall ink. That was the thing about Hollowmere that had unsettled her from the first night, when she'd been dragged through its gates in chains: everything smelled of work. Of candle smoke and wet wool and the sour bite of copying-ink. A valley that had spent centuries grinding its people down to ledger entries, and still the air itself was thick with the labor of hands.

The young scribe was there, of course. She'd known he would be.

His name was Tam. He was perhaps sixteen, all elbows and ink-stains, with the particular hunched posture of someone who had spent his childhood being told he was worth exactly what he could copy. He'd been one of Juniper's—she searched for the word and found it, reluctantly—*friends*. One of the few. He had been the one who brought Juniper's meals when the wardens forgot, who covered for him during roll-calls, who now sat alone in the great drafty room where thirty desks stood empty, copying the same page over and over because his hands needed something to do and no one had told him yet that he was allowed to stop.

He looked up when she came in. He didn't flinch. She noted that with approval; most mortals still flinched, even now, even after the battle, even though the fae who fought beside them had bled the same red onto the same stones.

"You're leaving," Tam said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled fast in a valley that had spent three hundred years with nothing to talk about but weather and debt.

"At dusk."

"Back through the wardline." He said it carefully, testing each word. "Where the seasons are."

"The court is rebuilding. There's much to—" She stopped. Lied, almost, which was a strange sensation for one of the Standing Frost; her people lied beautifully but rarely bothered. "There is work," she finished.

Tam nodded and went back to his copying. Sylwen crossed the room slowly, running her fingers along the empty desks, and stopped behind his chair.

He was not copying scripture. He was not copying the covenant-formula, which the new council had ordered posted in every hall and copied into every hand. He was copying a story.

*In the year the stars went out,* it began, in a hand she recognized so violently that her breath caught—*a debtor named Juniper Whitlock sat copying prayers by candlelight, and did not know that the dark outside his window had been waiting for him all his life.*

"That's his writing," Sylwen said. Her voice came out wrong. Smaller.

"No." Tam didn't look up. "It's mine. I'm learning it." He turned the page so she could see the practice sheets beneath—the same sentence written twenty times, each attempt closer to the original's narrow, patient strokes. "I found his ledgers. The ones from before. He kept a private book inside the temple copies—little notes in the margins, jokes mostly, and once a drawing of a cat that wasn't very good. I've been practicing so I can write the way he wrote."

"Why?"

"Because everyone remembers what he *did*." Tam finally looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "They tell it in the taverns already. The scribe who mended the sky. They make it sound like a thunderbolt hit him. Like he was always going to be that person, like it was easy." His jaw tightened. "But I knew him. He was afraid of the dark stairwell to the archive. He gave half his bread ration to the widow Ostler every week and pretended he'd lost it. He bit his nails when he thought no one was looking. If we only remember the thunderbolt, then nobody gets to be him. But if we remember the rest—"

"Then anyone might," Sylwen said quietly.

"Yeah." Tam swallowed. "Anyone might."

* * *

She unwrapped the ledger on Juniper's own desk.

Tam went very still. "Is that—"

"It was in the spire chamber. After." She had climbed up there herself, three days after the stars reignited, when the fae dead were being carried home and the mortal dead were being carried down, and the keystone had blazed like a captive dawn above the ruined roof. The sigil was whole now, perfect, self-sustaining—a covenant sealed in blood and name and memory, turning slowly like a wheel of light. And at its base, fallen as if dropped rather than lost, lay this: the ledger Juniper had carried since boyhood. The record of his debts. Every prayer copied, every candle burned, every crust owed to the temple that owned him.

The final entry was in his hand, dated the night before the end:

*Paid in full.*

Below it, smaller, almost shy:

*I never learned what my name was for. I think maybe it was for this.*

"He wanted to be remembered," Tam whispered. "He used to say scribes are how the dead keep talking. And then he made himself one of the dead, and now he can't—"

"He can't," Sylwen agreed. "That was the price. Name and memory, bound into the stone. No one who loved him can hold his face in their mind anymore. You've noticed."

Tam nodded miserably. "My mother knew him better than anyone. She says trying to remember him is like trying to remember a dream—you get the shape of it, the feeling, and then it's gone. She cried for a week. Not because she's sad, exactly. Because she knows she *was* sad, and she can't find the reason anymore."

"I know." Sylwen touched the ledger's cover. Beneath her fingers, faintly, the paper seemed warm—or perhaps that was only the spring sun through the high windows, or perhaps it was neither, and she was a fool who had crossed two realms and lost a war and won a stranger one, and fools were permitted their warmth. "The covenant holds his memory the way the old wards held magic. It feeds on it. Keeps it. Somewhere in that light is everything he ever was, every joke and every bad drawing of a cat, and none of us can reach it."

"So he's gone."

"He's *kept*," she corrected. "There is a difference. My people have known it for a long time—we bind things into stone and starlight precisely because flesh forgets. He is not gone, little scribe. He is *elsewhere*, and elsewhere is not nowhere." She paused. "And there is this."

She slid the ledger across the desk to him.

Tam stared at it. "You're giving me—"

"A story needs a keeper. That was always the covenant's shape—one side writes, one side witnesses. The temple stole the witnessing and called it ownership. You're going to fix that." She tapped the cover. "His hand. His words. His debts, paid. Copy it true, and copy it often, and give it away freely—that part matters, give it *freely*, or you'll build another Hollowmere on top of the one we just buried. Let every child in this valley learn his letters from his own sentences. In fifty years no one will remember his face, because no one can. But they will know how he wrote. They will know he was afraid of the dark stairwell and generous with his bread and that he paid everything he owed and more than anyone asked."

Tam's hands hovered over the ledger without touching it, the way you hover over something holy, or hot. "Why are you giving it to me? You knew him longer."

"I knew him for one season," Sylwen said. "You knew him for a life. Besides." She smiled, and it cost her, and she let it. "Someone should carry the story who understands what it costs to make the letters straight."

* * *

She left at dusk, as she'd said she would.

At the gates, Corvane was waiting—not to stop her, she saw that immediately, but because some habits of vigilance die hard, and perhaps because he too had come to watch the road. The former High Warden looked older than the winter had made him and lighter, too, like a man setting down a weight he'd mistaken for his spine.

"They're saying the council wants to rename the valley," he said, without preamble. "Whitlockmere. The debtors voted on it."

"What did you vote?"

"Against." He shrugged at her raised eyebrow. "He'd have hated it. All those prayers he copied, and never once his own name in the margins where someone might see." Corvane looked out at the darkening road, the thaw-water shining in the ditches, the first green haze on the orchard rows. "Better a valley with an old name and an open gate than a monument with a locked one."

"Yes," Sylwen said. "That sounds like him."

Corvane glanced at her sideways. "Does it? You'd know better than any of us now. We're all working from stories."

"We are all *made* of stories, Warden. Some of us simply admit it." She pulled her cloak tight. Beyond the wardline, the standing stones glowed faintly gold in the last light—the covenant's new pulse, steady as a heartbeat, visible from both sides of the border for the first time in three hundred years. "Keep the gates open. Keep the ledgers honest. And when the young ones ask what happened here—"

"Tell them true," Corvane said. "Even the parts that don't flatter us." He almost smiled. "Especially those."

She walked out through the gates without looking back, because fae do not look back, and because she had already spent more of her dignity than she intended on mortal soil.

But at the wardline she stopped, just once, and laid her palm against the nearest standing stone. Warm. Alive. Humming with a name that no tongue could speak anymore, held safe in light and stone, tended now by a valley full of voices telling it forward.

*Paid in full,* she thought. *And given away freely.*

Then she stepped through, into the long gold evening of the other world, and behind her the stars came out over Hollowmere, one by one, like lamps lit in windows for someone expected home.
