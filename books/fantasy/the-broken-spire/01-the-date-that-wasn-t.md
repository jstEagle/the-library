## 1. The Date That Wasn't

The dead hum when they're content. I want that on the record, because everything else in this story is going to make me sound like a madwoman, and I'd like at least one sentence of it to be true.

Grandmother Pell's rib hummed while I carved it. Not a song I knew—something older than the valley's repertoire, a tune with a lilt in it that bent where our music doesn't bend. She'd been dead eleven years, and her granddaughter had brought me the rib wrapped in flannel with the solemnity of someone handing over a crown jewel, which, in the Valley of Vell, is more or less what it was.

"Make it a brooch," Tamsin Pell had said. "Something she'd have liked. Something *hers*."

I'd asked what the grandmother liked. Tamsin had thought about it for a long moment and then said, "I don't remember, actually," and laughed a little at herself, the way you do when forgetting something small, and neither of us found anything strange in it. That's the thing about this valley. We forget the way other places breathe. Constantly, effortlessly, without ever once wondering why the air keeps leaving.

But the bone remembered. Bone always does. It's the one honest material left in Vell—wood forgets, stone forgets, even the river forgets its own course every spring—but bone keeps its accounts. Which is why I carve it, and why the dead talk to me through it, and why I have spent thirty-one years being the valley's most useful secret and its least discussed oddity.

So there I sat, in my workshop above the chandlery, lamplight pooling on the bench, listening to Grandmother Pell hum her bent-lilted tune while my knife followed the grain of her. The commission was a wren. She'd been fond of wrens—I knew this because she told me, mid-hum, in that voice the dead use, which is less a sound than a pressure behind your ears, like memory pressing against glass.

*A wren for the windowsill,* she said. *She'll know why.*

"She won't," I said aloud, to the bone. "She doesn't remember you liked anything."

The humming faltered. It does that sometimes—the dead are not stupid, whatever the living assume—and I felt the particular silence of a ghost doing arithmetic. Then, softly, almost kindly:

*Neither do I.*

I set down my knife. That was wrong. Dead women always remember themselves; it's the living who misplace them. A ghost who can't recall her own tastes is like a clock that's lost its hands—it still exists, but it can't tell you anything about time.

"Madam Pell," I said carefully, "what year did you die?"

Silence. Long enough that I started counting the floorboard creaks from the chandlery below, where old Bram was closing up, dragging his bad leg across the boards in his usual pattern: drag-step, drag-step, pause at the third board, curse, continue. Eleven years I've heard that pattern. Some nights it's the only proof I need that the world still holds its shape.

*It was cold,* Grandmother Pell offered at last. *There were geese.*

Geese fly south in autumn. Helpful. I thanked her, told her the wren would be lovely, and got no more out of her that evening. But my hands weren't steady after that, and a carver with unsteady hands has two choices: stop working, or find something else to occupy them.

I chose wrong.

* * *

The book came home with me three days ago, though I hadn't opened it until tonight. Market day, the stall of the traveling peddler who comes up from God-knows-where twice a year with ribbon, tinware, and the literary ambitions of dead men. He'd spread a crate of books on a trestle table, water-stained and swollen, salvaged—so he claimed—from a cellar in some town whose name he couldn't quite recall. He never could recall the names. I've bought from him six years running and I've never once learned where he comes from or where he goes, and I've never once wondered about it either, which should have told me something all by itself.

Most of the books were sermons. One was a ledger full of nothing but the word *butter* in increasingly furious handwriting. And one was thin, cracked along its spine, its cover bloomed gray with damp, titled in faded gilt letters:

*PROPHECIES AND PORTENTS OF THE NORTHERN REACHES, Being a Faithful Account, 1823*

I bought it for a ha'penny as a joke on myself. A bone-carver who hears the dead, buying a book of prophecies—there's a wit in that somewhere, or so I told myself, tucking it under my arm between the tin snips and the ribbon.

Tonight, hands unsteady, I fetched it down from the shelf where it had been sulking.

Here is what you must understand about prophecy books: they are almost always frauds. This is not cynicism; this is professional courtesy. I traffic in the actual voices of the actual dead, and I can tell you that the dead rarely predict anything except weather and their relatives' bad behavior. A book claiming to see forward is selling the same snake oil as a peddler claiming to know where he's from.

I opened it expecting fraud. I got a ledger of small-town doom instead—comets, crop failures, a miller in some northern town who would hang himself over a debt, dated and dutiful and dreary. Water damage had eaten whole pages; others were stuck together with mold. I flipped through with the idle speed of a woman confirming her own skepticism.

Then the pages fell open—fell, I say, though I've since wondered whether *fell* is the honest verb—and there, in ink that had run but not vanished, in handwriting cramped and urgent, was an entry unlike the others.

*In the valley that keeps its mouth shut, a carver shall hear what was folded away. On the eve of the first household's forgetting, she will read these words. The date is written here so that she cannot doubt:*

And beneath it, in the same hand, a date.

I am not going to be delicate about this, because delicacy would be a lie. The date was today's. Not approximately. Not a coincidence of numbers. Today. The seventeenth of Frostmonth, in the year the valley currently calls 1924, though I have begun to suspect we call it that the way a prisoner calls his cell by a number.

I sat very still. Below me, Bram dragged across his third board and cursed.

There are rational explanations. There always are; I've spent a lifetime collecting them the way other people collect grievances. The book is a fraud planted by a prankster. The date is a printer's error. I misread it—I went back and read it four times, and I did not misread it. The handwriting changed, actually, on that final line. The rest of the entry was the neat, dry script of a man writing a catalogue. The date was written by someone pressing hard enough to tear the page, someone who wanted it *read*.

*On the eve of the first household's forgetting.*

What forgetting? Whose household? The valley forgot things constantly—we all did, cheerfully, the way you forget a dream by breakfast—but nobody called it *forgetting*. Nobody noticed it at all. That was the whole character of the place. The Valley of Vell kept its mouth shut, kept its ledgers short, kept its histories thin as onion skin, and none of us ever minded, because how do you miss what you never knew was missing?

Except now I had a dead woman upstairs in a box of shavings who couldn't remember her own tastes, and a book that said forgetting was coming like weather, and a date that was today.

I did what any sensible person does with dread. I made tea. I carried the book to the window, watched the snow start—first snow of the season, fat and unhurried, already filling the lane—and told myself that tomorrow I would laugh about this over breakfast.

That was when I noticed the rest of the entry. Underneath the date, smaller, squeezed against the gutter of the page as if the writer had nearly lost his nerve:

*If you are reading this, Marguerite Thornbury, then Edric's bones are closer than you think. Follow the humming.*

My tea went cold in my hands.

I don't know anyone named Edric. I've never known anyone named Edric. The name sits in my chest like a swallowed stone, cold and specific, and beneath it another feeling I like even less: the humming. Grandmother Pell's humming, the tune with the bend in it—I had assumed it was hers. Assumed, the way this valley assumes everything, without ever once checking.

The workshop was dark behind me. My lamp threw my shadow long across the floorboards, across the loose board near the hearth that I have stepped over ten thousand times without once asking myself why it was loose, or what was underneath it, or why I had never, in thirty-one years of living in this house, thought to look.

The snow thickened against the glass. Somewhere below, Bram locked the chandlery door, drag-step, drag-step, and the ordinary evening went on being ordinary all around me, patient as a held breath.

I looked at the loose board for a long time.

Then I put down the tea, picked up the crowbar I use for splitting femurs, and knelt on the floor of my own workshop like a grave-robber in training.

The board came up easier than it should have. As if it had been waiting.
