## 2. What the Bone Remembers

I did not sleep. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger the valley keeps of my failings, which I suspect is written in a hand very like my aunt's: *Marguerite Thornbury, who could not leave well enough alone, and see where it got her.*

The prophecy book sat on my workbench all night with its water-bled pages and its one legible line — *the bone-carver shall hear what was buried on the eve of the thaw* — and I sat across from it like a woman sharing a table with a debt collector. Outside, the last snow of the season ticked against the shutters. Inside, the grandmother's rib lay finished on its velvet, humming.

That was the thing. It was still humming.

Bones go quiet when the work is done. That is the whole architecture of my trade: the dead speak through the carving, say their piece, and then settle into silence like guests who have stayed exactly as long as politeness allows. This hum had no such manners. It had been going since sundown, low and tuneless, and it wasn't coming from the rib at all. I'd checked. I'd held the rib to my ear like a conch and gotten nothing but the faint smell of lavender and somebody's opinion about my posture.

The hum came from under the floor.

Now, a reasonable woman would have gone to bed. A reasonable woman would have decided that old houses make old noises, that a workshop built on river clay will groan and settle and mutter to itself the way old houses do, and that the correct response to a floor that sings is a strong cup of chamomile and an early night. But I have never once been accused of being a reasonable woman, except by Aunt Sable, who uses the word the way other people use *foolish*, and by my clients, who mean it as praise.

So I knelt on the cold boards with a lamp and put my palm flat against the wood, the way I do when I'm listening to a bone, and waited.

The hum moved. Under my hand. Deliberately. The way a cat headbutts your shin when it has decided you are its problem now.

"Well," I said to the empty workshop. "That's new."

* * *

The cache, when I found it, was insultingly well hidden for something I'd walked over for eleven years.

The loose board was beneath the drying rack, third from the wall, and it lifted only after I'd pried at it with a chisel, sworn at it in two languages, and finally asked it nicely — which, I note for future reference, works on floors as well as it works on bones. Beneath it: a cavity lined with oilcloth gone stiff as bark, and inside the oilcloth, wrapped in a second layer that crumbled at my touch, a bundle.

Not treasure. Not coins, not deeds, none of the things a sensible person hides under a floor. A bundle of papers fused into one gray brick, a tin of drawing instruments rusted into a single sculpture of itself, and a bone.

A femur. Human, or near enough. Long, dense, yellowed to the color of old candle wax, and carved — I saw this before I'd even finished unwrapping it, because carving announces itself to me the way music does — carved along its whole length in lines so fine they might have been spider silk. Not decoration. I know decoration. This was writing, or a map, or both, cut by someone with a steady hand and all the time in the world and, I suspected even then, not much else.

I picked it up.

The workshop went silent. Not quiet — silent, the way a room goes when everyone in it turns to look at you. The fire stopped popping. The snow stopped ticking. Even the hum stopped, which was the strangest part, because the hum had been the femur's, I realized, and it had stopped because —

"Finally," said a voice, from the bone, in my hands, six inches from my face.

I would like to report that I reacted with dignity. What actually happened is that I sat down very hard on the floorboards, the femur clutched to my chest like a rosary, and said, "No."

"Yes," said the voice. It was a man's voice, dry and precise, with the particular patience of someone who has rehearsed this conversation for a very long time. "Though I take your point. It *is* rather a lot."

"You're dead," I told him. This is, historically, my side of most conversations.

"I am," he agreed, without any apparent grief about it. "One hundred and four years dead, if the counting in here can be trusted, and the counting in here is all I've had, so I've made it trustworthy. My name is Edric Vale. I was a cartographer. And you are the bone-carver."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I wrote it down," he said, "in 1823, in a book that was supposed to be found tonight, and because I have spent a century listening to this floor while some fool above me carved bones and talked to herself and never once thought to look down." A pause, and when he spoke again there was something underneath the dryness, something that had been waiting a long time to be said. "You hear us. All these years, you've heard the dead. Did it never occur to you that one of us might be trying to be heard?"

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that didn't sound like an apology, and I wasn't ready to owe him one.

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, not even Sable, though she guesses at the shape of it: hearing the dead is like standing at a window, watching people in a lit room across the street. You see them. You learn their habits, their griefs, the songs they hummed. But the glass is thick, and they have never once looked up. I carve their memories out of their relics and send them downriver like lanterns, and if anyone received them, no one ever said so. Eleven years of talking to a room that couldn't talk back.

And now the room had talked back, and it turned out the room had opinions.

"The rib," Edric said, before I'd gathered myself. "The grandmother. She finished her song, by the way. You gave her that. Do you understand how rare — no. Later. We have less time than I'd hoped, and more than they think. Ask me why I'm under your floor."

"Why are you under my floor?"

"Because I was murdered," he said, "for drawing a map of a door."

* * *

He told it in pieces, the way the dead tell everything — not because they're coy but because memory in a bone isn't a story, it's weather; it comes in fronts and squalls. I sat on the floor with my back against the workbench legs and the femur across my knees like a sleeping animal, and let it rain on me.

He had been Osterhale's cartographer. Not a maker of pretty maps for merchants — a surveyor, the kind who walks the land and writes down what the land actually is. In 1823 the Spire-wardens had come to him with a commission: chart the valley's boundaries. All of them. Every edge.

"And I did," he said. "That was my mistake. I was good at edges. I found one that shouldn't exist."

"A door."

"A Door Out." The words came off him with weight, like stones set down one at a time. "The wardens didn't save Osterhale when the world turned on it. They folded it. Out of the world and into a pocket, and they paid for the folding with memory — theirs first, then ours, a tithe collected household by household ever since. The valley isn't a refuge. It's a prison, and every soul in it is either a witness or a jailer's descendant, and none of us knows which. I drew the Door. I drew the way to it. And the night I finished the map, men came to my house whose feet made no sound on the stairs."

"They killed you."

"They were very polite about it," he said, and something in his tone made my skin crawl, because I had heard that exact courtesy two nights ago — no. One night ago. The stranger at the market with the questions like fishhooks. "They took the map. They thought they took everything. They did not think to search the floor of a rented workshop, because I hid the map where only a carver would find it — inside the bone, along the marrow-canal, cut fine enough that no eye would ever catch it. I hid it and I died holding the location of this house in my mind like a coal, and I have burned on it for a hundred and four years waiting for someone with your gift to pick me up."

"Why me? Why wait for —"

"Because you hear the dead," he said simply, "and I needed to be heard. There are perhaps three people alive in this valley with the gift, and the other two are children, and I could hardly ask a child to pry up floorboards. You were always the one, Marguerite Thornbury. I have listened to you argue with chisels since you were nineteen. I know your swearing in two languages. I chose you."

I looked down at the bone in my lap and felt something I hadn't felt since I was a girl: watched back. It should have been comforting. Mostly it was terrifying, and underneath the terror, shamelessly, warmly, like a coal in the palm — welcome.

"The map," I said. "It's still in you."

"It is. And before you ask — no, I won't simply tell you where the Door is. Not because I enjoy mystery. Because the moment you know, you become what I was: a loose thread, and there are things in this valley that pull loose threads." His voice dropped, went urgent. "The book was found. Which means they know the thread exists. Which means you have days, Marguerite. Perhaps fewer. Read the map, yes. But first tell me — the book. What did it say? Exactly."

I told him. The date. Tonight's date, inked in 1823, naming the bone-carver and the finding of the buried thing.

The silence that followed was long enough that I checked whether he was still there. He was. When he spoke again, all the dryness had gone out of him, and what remained was older and colder.

"Then it's begun already," he said. "The erasure. They'll start small. A name here. A face there. They always do — you don't boil a valley that notices." A pause. "Carver. How many households are on your street?"

"Eleven," I said. "Twelve with the Weavers at the end."

"Remember them," Edric Vale said, from a century away, in a voice like a closing door. "Remember every one of them. Starting tomorrow, you are going to be the only one who can."

Outside, the snow had stopped. Somewhere down the valley, too far to see, something began — very softly, very patiently — to hum.
