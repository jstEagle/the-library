## 3. The Valley That Keeps Its Mouth Shut

I did not sleep. This will surprise no one who has spent an evening learning their own name from a book that predates their grandmother's grandmother, but I record it anyway, because the sleeplessness mattered. It gave me time to do what I do best with dread, which is to organize it.

By lamplight I made a list. Under the heading *Questions*, in my neatest carving-hand, I wrote:

1. What is Osterhale?
2. Why does no one say the word?
3. Who wrote this book, and how did they know tonight's date a hundred years early?

Three questions. A modest list. I have carved entire saints with fewer notes than that.

Under it, after some thought, I added a fourth: *Why does the humming of a dead woman sound like a warning?*

Then I blew out the lamp, because Aunt Sable was stirring upstairs, and there are things even a woman who talks to bones prefers not to explain by candlelight.

* * *

Morning came up grey and thin as scraped vellum. The valley does its best work in this light — the mist sits in the low fields like something poured, and the Spire-hill (we all call it that without knowing why; there is no spire on it, only a bare crown of rock and a windmill that has never once turned) stands above it all like a finger pressed to lips.

I dressed, ate bread that tasted of nothing much, and set out with the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth inside my work-bag, against my ribs, where a client's commission would normally ride. It felt heavier than any rib I've carried. Bones are honest weight. Books lie about theirs.

My first stop was the bakery, which is where one goes in Vell to learn anything, because Marta Kessel has sold bread to every household in the valley for thirty years and considers gossip a form of leavening.

"Marta," I said, arranging my face into idle curiosity, "a strange question. Has anyone ever mentioned a country called Osterhale?"

Marta was scoring loaves. Her knife paused — not long, a heartbeat, less — and then continued, and she said, cheerfully, "Osterhale? Never heard it. Sounds foreign."

"It's in an old book I bought at market."

"Old books say all sorts." She handed me a roll I hadn't asked for, warm, on the house. "You look peaky, Marguerite. Eat something. Did you hear about the Kettles' new pig?"

I had not heard about the Kettles' new pig. I took the roll. I said thank you. And I stood there in the warm yeast-smell of the only world I'd ever known while a perfectly kind woman looked straight through the word *Osterhale* as though it were a gap in a fence — something you can see through but never think to walk toward.

Here is the thing about Marta Kessel: she forgets nothing. She remembers the price of flour in the famine year. She remembers which of the Hedley twins bit her in 1841. If forgetting were natural, if Osterhale had simply slid out of common knowledge the way names slide out of fashion, Marta would have had some crumb of it. A song. A swear-word. An old coin.

She had nothing. Worse than nothing — she had *cheerfulness*. She changed the subject the way water flows around a stone, without effort, without noticing the stone at all.

* * *

The parish records live in a damp room behind St. Brannoc's-in-the-Vale, kept by Father Ellery, a soft man with ink-stained cuffs and the gentle vagueness of someone who has read too many sermons aloud. He owed me a favor — I had carved his late mother's knucklebones into a rosary, and he had wept over them in a way I pretend not to have witnessed.

"I need the oldest registers," I told him. "Everything from before 1830."

"For genealogy? Delightful. So many families tracing roots these days." He fetched them down himself, dusted them with a cloth he clearly loved more than most of his congregation, and left me alone with them.

The registers are exactly what you would expect: baptisms, marriages, burials, in a clerk's hand that starts confident and grows tired across the decades. I worked backward. 1850. 1840. 1835. The valley's story unspooled, ordinary and complete. Births. Weddings. A drowning. A good harvest noted with unusual feeling.

Then 1823.

The last entry before the gap is a burial, dated the ninth of October, 1823. The clerk — a different hand now, hurried, the ink blotted — begins the record properly. Name. Age. Cause. And then, mid-sentence, in the middle of a line, in the middle of a *word*, the entry stops:

*"Buried this day, Goodwife Aldous, aged seventy-one, taken by a fever of the —"*

Nothing. The rest of the page is blank. The next page is blank. The next forty pages are blank. Then, without comment, without explanation, a new hand resumes in 1824 with a baptism, as though the clerk had simply stepped out for a year and come back having forgotten how to finish words.

A fever of the *what*?

I sat in that damp room with my finger under the broken sentence and felt the hair rise on my arms. Sentences don't die mid-word. People die mid-sentence, yes — pens drop, hearts stop — but then there is a blot, a trailing stroke, evidence of a hand interrupted. There was none here. The line simply *ends*, cleanly, as if the word *fever* had been the last word the valley was permitted to write.

And beneath my fingertip, faint as breath on glass, I felt it — the paper remembered. Not the way bone remembers, nothing so articulate. But there was a hollowness in the record, a shape of absence, the way a missing tooth leaves a groove in the gum. Something had been written here and *taken out*, root and branch, and the paper had healed around the wound so smoothly that only a carver — only someone whose whole trade is reading what remains when flesh is gone — could feel the scar.

"Find what you needed?" Father Ellery asked, when I emerged blinking into the nave.

"Yes," I lied. "Thank you."

He smiled. "The valley keeps its records well. Nothing important is ever lost here."

I want that noted, whoever reads this: he said it warmly. He *meant* it kindly. And it was the single most frightening sentence anyone spoke to me that day, and Corvane hadn't even arrived yet.

* * *

I tried three more households before noon, working outward from the square the way Edric Vale's femur — no. Not yet. Working outward from the square the way a sensible person gathers testimony.

To old Tam Hedley I said, "Did your grandfather ever speak of a place called Osterhale?" Tam, who tells the same story about the great frost of '39 at every opportunity, whether asked or not, blinked at me and said, "Oster-what? Is that a cheese?"

To the Widow Prynne, who keeps her mother's letters bundled in ribbon and showed me them once, letters going back to the 1790s, I said, "May I see the correspondence again? I'm curious about the older postmarks." She fetched them gladly. Every letter, every one, bore the frank of the Valley of Vell. Not one mentioned a neighboring town, a capital, a king, a war, a border. The outside world might never have existed. When I remarked on it — gently, testing — she said, "Well, why would we write elsewhere? Everything's here."

Everything's here. The valley's whole creed, offered with tea and shortbread.

And here is what I noticed, walking home with the cold settling into me: nobody was lying. I know lies. Lies have a texture, a sweat on them, a flicker. These people were telling the truth as they knew it, wholeheartedly, and the truth as they knew it had been *tidied*. Somewhere between the world and their minds, someone had gone through the shelves and removed every book about Osterhale, and the shelves had closed up around the gaps so neatly that no one thought to miss what wasn't there.

Forgetting, I realized, standing in the lane with my breath steaming, is supposed to be ragged. Real forgetting frays at the edges. It leaves wrong dates and half-names and songs you can't place. This was not ragged. This was *curated*. This was forgetting with clean margins.

Which meant it was done to them. Which meant someone had the doing of it. Which meant—

"You've been asking questions."

* * *

Aunt Sable was waiting in the workshop doorway when I got home, arms folded, and I knew before she spoke that Marta or the Widow Prynne or the very walls had carried word ahead of me. News travels fast in a valley. Perhaps faster than it should.

Sable is seventy-one and built like the doorframe she was leaning in: narrow, hard, load-bearing. She raised me after my parents went into the ground — quietly, quickly, both of them, within a season, though I was young enough that grief wore the shape of confusion instead. She taught me the carving tools. She taught me which clients to refuse. She taught me never, ever to ask the dead about the living.

"Aunt," I began, "I found a book—"

"I know what book you found." Her voice was flat as a plane-shaved board. "The peddler's stall. Water-damaged. Red boards. You bought it yesterday evening, and you read it, and today you went out asking after Osterhale in front of Marta Kessel."

"How—"

"This is a small valley, Marguerite. Things echo in it." She came inside, shut the door, and shut the shutter over it, and drew the bolt, which she has done perhaps four times in my life, each time before a funeral. "Show me the book."

I unwrapped it from the oilcloth. She wouldn't touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a snake you've found in a child's bed — with pity, mostly, and a little respect, and a great deal of resolve.

"It names me," I said. "It names today's date. Sable, it knows things it cannot know, and the parish register breaks off mid-word in 1823, and Marta Kessel — Marta, who remembers everything — has never heard the name of a country that must have been *here*, and I think—"

"I know what you think." She sat down heavily on my work-stool, among the shavings, and for a moment she looked every one of her years and several borrowed ones besides. "Your mother looked like that. The same set of the jaw. Asking questions right up until—" She stopped herself. Closed her mouth on the sentence the way the clerk's hand had stopped on the page.

"Until what?"

"Marguerite." She leaned forward. "Burn the book."

"What?"

"Burn it. Tonight. Don't read another page. Don't ask another soul. Take it out to the yard, put it under the kindling, and stand there until it's ash, and then sweep the ash into the stream." She said it evenly, reasonably, the voice she used for teaching me to hold a chisel against the grain. "Do you understand me?"

"No," I said. "No, I don't. Because burning a book doesn't answer questions, it just proves someone doesn't want them asked. Who wrote it, Sable? What happened in 1823? What is Osterhale?"

"And there it is." She laughed, short and bitter, at nothing. "There's the jaw again. Listen to me, girl, because I'll say this once, and then I'll deny saying it if ever you quote me. Some questions are doors. And some doors are locked for the same reason cellars are locked — not to keep treasure in, but to keep children from falling down the stairs. The valley is safe. The valley is *quiet*. Every soul in it sleeps easy and wakes content, and the only thing threatening that — the only thing — is a bone-carver with a book in her bag and her mother's jaw."

"The dead hummed to me last night," I said quietly. "A dead woman's rib hummed a tune I'd never heard, and then I opened a book that named today, and I don't believe in that many coincidences. Do you?"

Something moved behind her eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or fear wearing recognition's coat.

"There are powers," she said slowly, "that keep accounts. And there are places they can't follow. This valley is one of those places. That is not an accident, Marguerite. That is *architecture*. And architecture has builders, and builders do not appreciate tenants who go poking at the foundations." She stood, steadied herself on the bench, and looked at me with an expression I had never seen on her face before and have never seen since: naked pleading. "Burn the book. Please. For once in your stubborn life, be ordinary."

I held her gaze. And I said the true thing, the thing I perhaps should have swallowed:

"I can't. Someone died writing that book, Sable. Someone died finishing a sentence the valley wasn't allowed to finish. And I'm the only person in this valley who can still hear the dead — which means I'm the only witness it has left."

For a long moment neither of us moved. The fire ticked. Outside, the mist lay on the fields like a held breath, and somewhere above it the windless mill on the Spire-hill stood utterly still, pointing at nothing.

Sable turned away first. At the door she paused, her back to me, and said, so low I nearly lost it under the fire's noise:

"When the stranger comes — and one will come, they always come — don't tell him about the book. Don't tell him about the humming. And Marguerite — whatever he offers you, however polite he is — remember that politeness is what power wears when it wants something."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and I was alone with the book, and the shavings, and the femur-shaped silence of the floor beneath my feet — a silence I had never noticed before, and would notice, God help me, every night thereafter.

Because here is what I did not yet know, what I learned only later, and what I set down now so the order of my heart is plain: I burned nothing that night. Instead I sat by the fire with the book open on my knee, and I listened — truly listened, the way I listen through bone — to the house itself.

And beneath the floorboards, beneath the clay, beneath a century of quiet, something answered.

Not a hum. Not a tune.

A voice. Dry as a map-fold, patient as ink, and unmistakably glad:

*"At last. I'd begun to think no one in this valley could hear me anymore."*
