## 4. A Visitor Without Footprints

The snow began at dusk and fell with the particular patience of snow that intends to stay the night. I watched it from the workshop window while I sanded a commission—a thimble-sized reliquary for the miller's late wife—and told myself, as I had been telling myself for three days, that there was nothing strange about a book.

Books had dates in them. That was their whole business. A book naming today's date was a book being thorough, not a book being *aware*.

The dead woman's tune was still lodged in my head, too. She'd hummed it while I carved her rib into a keepsake box, four notes rising and one falling, like a question asked twice and answered once. I caught myself humming it back while I worked, which is either a comfort or an infection, depending on how much you trust the dead.

I did not hear the knock so much as feel it: three raps, evenly spaced, delivered by someone who believed doors existed to be knocked upon and would be honored to do so.

I opened it on a stranger.

He stood in my yard like a portrait hung slightly crooked—everything about him correct, and yet something off by a degree no eye could name. Grey coat, grey gloves, hair the colour of ash that has thought better of being fire. He was perhaps fifty, perhaps five hundred; his face had the smoothness of something that had decided its age in advance and saw no reason to revise.

"Marguerite Thornbury," he said. Not a question. "Bone-carver. Niece of Sable Thornbury. Keeper of—" he paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before stepping over a puddle he does not wish to acknowledge, "—a very tidy workshop."

"And you are?"

"Corvane." He inclined his head a precise half-inch. "Warden Corvane, though the title is largely ceremonial these days. I appraise antiques. Provenance, condition, historical significance. I'm told the valley has a number of items worth cataloguing before—" another pause, longer this time, "—before the season turns."

It was already winter. The season had turned a fortnight ago. I filed this away in the drawer where I kept things I didn't want to look at directly.

"You've come a long way to catalogue thimbles," I said.

"One man's thimble is another man's century." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had clearly been practiced on people who mattered less than me.

Behind him, the snow lay unbroken from my gate to my door. I want to be clear about this, because I checked twice, and then a third time when he wasn't looking, and a fourth time after he left, holding a lantern out into the blue dark like a fool looking for a wound in white cloth. No footprints. Not pressed ones, not half-ones, not the ghost of a heel. Either he had fallen from the sky—which the valley's weather made unlikely but not impossible—or he had walked through my gate without troubling the ground to notice.

I let him in anyway. This is the part where you may call me an idiot. I have called myself worse since.

* * *

The trouble with a lifetime of hearing the dead is that you develop a terrible ear for the living. The dead are honest—they have nothing left to protect. The living hum and hedge and arrange themselves. And this man arranged himself the way a mortician arranges a face: competently, warmly, and with total commitment to a fiction.

He admired the hanging tools. He admired the shavings on the floor—"deer antler?" he asked, and I said elk, and he nodded as though elk were a small victory. He held up a finished piece, a brooch carved from a horse's coffin bone, and turned it in the lamplight with genuine appreciation.

"The grain work is extraordinary," he said. "You can almost see the animal in it."

"That's the point."

"Is it? Most carvers try to make the bone forget what it was." His eyes came up to mine, mild as milk. "You make it remember."

My hands, which have never shaken in front of a client, developed opinions on the matter.

"I make boxes," I said. "And brooches. And buttons, if the pay's decent."

"Mm." He set the brooch down with exaggerated care, aligning it precisely with the table's edge, and I understood that the precision was the message—that everything this man did was a message, and the message was *I am a creature of exactitude, and exactitude notices things.* "I'm told you recently acquired a book at market. Water-damaged. Leather boards, brass corner-fittings gone green. Sold by a peddler who claimed it came out of a house clearance in Lowmarsh."

The workshop went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"I buy books," I said. "I buy bones, books, buttons, and bad wine. It's a varied portfolio."

"This one would have been cheap. Unreadable, mostly. The ink's run." He drifted along my shelves, gloved fingers trailing an inch above the spines without touching them, which somehow was worse than touching. "Except for certain pages. There's a passage—it would be around two-thirds through, if the water damage follows the usual pattern—written in a hand that resisted the damp rather better than the rest. Names, dates. Prophecy, if you like. Or inventory, if you prefer the truth."

"And if I had such a book," I said, "what would you want with it?"

"What I want with all antiques." He turned. The lamplight caught his face and slid off it like rain off slate. "To see it properly catalogued. To ensure it reaches the right collection. Some objects are simply too significant to be left in private hands, Miss Thornbury. They distress the other objects."

I laughed. I'm rather proud of that—I laughed, short and sharp, because some animal part of me knew that fear was exactly the wrong currency with this man, and laughter at least confused the exchange rate.

"Distress the other objects," I repeated. "That's a new one. Usually buyers say they're doing me a favor."

"I am doing you a favor."

There it was. Underneath the courtesy, plain as a bone under skin. He said it the way you'd say *the tide is coming in*, and I felt the floor of the conversation tilt beneath us both.

"The book isn't here," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It isn't here *now*."

* * *

He stayed forty minutes. I know because I watched the clock the way drowning men watch the surface. In that time he managed to ask, with the light touch of a man dealing cards:

Whether Aunt Sable still kept her accounts in the old Osterhale numerals (she does; she thinks no one knows). Whether the parish records had ever been recopied after the fire of '23 (there was no fire of '23; I said so; he smiled as though I'd confirmed something). Whether I'd noticed anything unusual in the valley lately—fogginess, forgetfulness, households out of count. I thought of the Weavers, whose youngest boy had stopped coming round for scraps of my carving-wax, and whose mother had looked straight through me at the well yesterday when I asked after him, and I said, "Nothing unusual. It's a valley. Unusual doesn't survive here long."

"Wise," said Corvane. "Valleys that keep their mouths shut live long lives."

"And valleys that don't?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and behind the mildness of his eyes something moved—not menace exactly, but *attention*, vast and patient, the attention of a thing that had been watching this valley since before the valley had a name it remembered.

"Valleys that don't," he said softly, "get folded."

Then he thanked me for my hospitality, complimented the elk shavings again, and stepped out into the snow.

* * *

I gave him until the lantern-light faded past the gate before I moved. Then I did what any sensible woman does when a polite monster has just sat in her chair: I checked the obvious.

No footprints. Gate to door, door to gate, and out along the road as far as the lantern reached—the snow lay perfect, windless, untouched, a page no one had written on. My own tracks going out to check were the only marks in the whole white world, and I stood there among them feeling suddenly, absurdly guilty for existing so loudly.

Back inside, I did the less sensible thing. I pulled the loose stone behind the flour bin, took out the water-damaged book wrapped in oilcloth, and opened it to the page I'd been avoiding for three days.

*Marguerite Thornbury,* said the dry brown hand. *Bone-carver. On the night of the first heavy snow, she will be visited.*

Below it, in fresher ink—ink that had not been there when I bought the book, ink that had appeared sometime between the market and now, in handwriting I recognized with a lurch because it matched the humming, four notes rising and one falling:

*He counts footprints. Give him none.*

I sat down hard on the workshop stool. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the snow went on falling with that same patient intent, covering the road, covering the ridge, covering everything the valley had agreed not to look at.

A visitor without footprints had come to appraise my life, and I had let him hold my brooches and count my shelves and smile at my lies. He knew about the book. He knew about Sable's numerals. He knew there was no fire of '23, and he knew that I knew.

Power had noticed the valley's one loose thread, and it had been polite about it, and somehow the politeness was the worst part. You can fight a fist. What do you do with a handshake?

I wrapped the book back up, and my hand was steady until it wasn't, and I heard myself whisper into the empty workshop—to the ribs and femurs and quiet little skulls on their shelves, to the dead who had never once lied to me—

"If any of you know what a warden is, now would be the time."

And from beneath the floorboards—from the cache I hadn't yet dug up, from something I hadn't yet found—came a sound I had never heard in thirty years of carving the dead.

Something knocked back.
