## 5. Edric's Map

The femur sat on my bench for three days before I touched it, and I want it noted that this showed restraint. Most people, handed a talking bone by a dead man who claims he was murdered, would either throw it in the river or run screaming to the parish. I did neither. I made tea. I sat with it. I let it hum at me while I pretended to work on a commission—a christening spoon for the Hollis baby, from a lamb bone, which is about as far from a murdered cartographer as you can get while staying in the same material.

"You're stalling," Edric said.

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking for three days. I've been dead for a hundred years and even I moved faster than that."

I put down the spoon. "You were murdered. That tends to slow a person down."

"It slowed me down considerably," he agreed, "but not for three days."

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing the dead: they don't stop. They don't politely wait their turn or respect office hours. For thirty-one years I have had a river of voices running under everything—the rib of a grandmother humming, a wedding ring murmuring old vows, a coffin nail reciting the name of whoever held it last. I learned young to turn the volume down, the way you learn to sleep near a road. But Edric was different, and it took me those three days of tea and stalling to understand why: he wasn't just talking *at* me. He was listening back. When I set him down, he waited. When I picked him up, he said my name like he'd been keeping it warm.

No one had ever done that. The living certainly didn't. In the Valley of Vell, Marguerite Thornbury carves keepsakes and hears nothing worth mentioning, thank you kindly, and if you ask her about her gift she'll tell you it's a trick of suggestion and grief, and most people are happy to believe it because the alternative—that the valley sits on a century of unquiet dead—is bad for property values.

"Fine," I said on the third night, after Sable had gone to bed and the workshop lamp was the only light on Cold Row. "You said you drew a map. Of a door."

"I did."

"And they killed you for it."

"The Spire-wardens did, yes. Though 'killed' flatters them. Killing implies you stop existing afterward. They preferred to make sure you'd never existed at all." A pause, the way he paused when something cost him. "They missed a piece of me. The marrow-canal. Bone holds what flesh forgets—or rather, bone holds what they couldn't reach without breaking me open, and they were too proud to break me. Pride is the one crack in any prison, Marguerite. Remember that."

"Is that a metaphor or instructions?"

"Yes," said Edric Vale, dead cartographer, and laughed—a dry sound, like paper turning.

* * *

Opening a femur is not delicate work, whatever the ballads say about gentle hands. The marrow-canal runs through the center of the long bones like a wick through a candle, and in a century-old bone it's usually dry as chalk. To read it—to get at whatever Edric had hidden in there—I would have to split the shaft lengthwise, and splitting it meant that when I was done, there would be no more Edric to talk to. A voice needs a vessel. Crack the vessel and you get silence, permanent, the kind I usually only heard from things that had never been alive at all.

I want to be honest here, since honesty turns out to be the theme of my life whether I consented to it or not: I considered not doing it. I considered keeping him. A dead man who listens back is worth more than a map, surely. Surely.

"Don't," Edric said quietly, when I turned the bone over in my hands and didn't pick up the saw. "Don't you dare make me into another thing people keep on a shelf."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly. You've spent your whole life being the valley's little secret. The woman who hears. And now you've got a dead man all to yourself, someone who finally understands what it costs, and you'd rather keep the company than follow the map." He wasn't angry. That was the worst part. He sounded tired, the way only the very patient can. "I stayed behind on purpose. Do you think that was easy? A hundred years in the dark, waiting for someone with your ears? I did it so the map would get out. Not so I could have a friend."

"That's a horrible reason to stay dead."

"It's the only reason anyone stays anything," he said. "Now cut."

So I cut.

I won't dress it up. It was awful. The saw bit into the compact bone with a grinding whine, and Edric kept talking the whole time—not pleading, just talking, telling me about his mother's kitchen in a city called Carrowmere that no longer appeared on any map, about how he'd gotten lost in the fens at nine years old and been found by a shepherd who charged his father a hen for the trouble, about the smell of ink and the weight of a good ruling pen. He talked the way people talk on scaffolds and in lifeboats: fast, ordinary, human. And then the shaft cracked open along the line I'd scored, both halves falling onto the cloth with two soft clicks, and the workshop went quiet in a way I felt in my teeth.

For a moment there was nothing. Then, faint as breath on a winter window, Edric's voice came from somewhere deeper than the bone—from the map itself, maybe, or from wherever voices go when their houses fall down.

"There," he whispered. "Read it. Read it aloud. Maps want to be spoken."

* * *

The inside of the femur was not empty.

Etched into the inner wall of the canal—fine as frost-ferns, fine as the hairline cracks in old glaze—ran lines. Lines and more lines, dense and deliberate, worked into the bone with something harder than any burin I owned. I tilted the halves toward the lamp and the shadows in the grooves deepened, and suddenly it wasn't abstract geometry anymore. It was coastline. It was a river delta. It was roads—roads with mile-markings, roads with names too small to read until I fetched Sable's jeweler's loupe from the drawer where she kept it for judging gemstones she no longer trusted herself to appraise.

And in the lower third of the bone, where the canal widens toward the condyle, there was a building. Drawn in section, the way an architect draws, with walls and a floor and beneath the floor—

A door. An archway, rendered twice: once as it looked, once as it would look opened, drawn in dashed lines like a promise.

"Beneath the chapel," Edric said. His voice was thinner now, stretched over fewer inches of bone, but still unmistakably him. "The chapel of Saint Aldhelm-on-the-Water. Drowned in the year the wardens folded us out—they flooded the low country to bury it, drowned a church full of witnesses and called it weather. The Door Out is under its floor. Under the altar, if the altar's still standing. Which it isn't. Nothing there is still standing. That's rather the point."

My eyes were doing something traitorous, filling up, and I wiped them on my sleeve and kept reading. "There's writing. Around the archway. Small."

"Seven locks," Edric said. "Each one keyed to a memory, freely given. That was the wardens' own design, turned against them—they built the Door to be unopenable, and then they built it honest, because magic can't lie in this valley, only people can. Seven memories. Given freely. Not taken, not traded. Given." A pause. "It's the cruelest lock ever devised and the only fair one."

"Why cruel?"

"Because you'll see what it asks," he said, "and you'll pay it anyway."

I should have stopped there. I know that now. There was a moment—I can mark it precisely, the lamp guttering, the snow starting against the window, Sable's floorboard creaking upstairs as she turned over in her sleep—when I could have wrapped the two halves of Edric Vale in oilcloth, buried them under the floorboards next to the cache, and gone back to christening spoons. The valley would have gone on forgetting. The Weaver family would have gone on being whole, or whole enough. I would have gone on being the woman who heard the dead and told no one, which is a small life but a warm one.

Instead I asked, "Where's the ridge?"

Silence. The bad kind.

"Edric. Your map shows a ridge between the valley and the water. East-northeast, past the alder stands. Where is it?"

"Marguerite," he said carefully, "look at the map again. Look at what's *around* the ridge."

I looked. And this is the part I have turned over every night since, the part that made obsession take root in me like a seed in wet ground: the ridge on Edric's map wasn't drawn as land. Every hill and valley and road on the femur was etched in confident lines—but around the ridge, the lines *stopped*. There was a region perhaps three miles wide where the bone was smooth, untouched, deliberately blank—and at its edge, the etched lines simply ended, cut off clean, the way a sentence ends mid-word.

Except they didn't quite end. When I held the bone at the right angle to the lamp, in the blank space, I could see the ghost of continuation—grooves so shallow they might have been an illusion, lines that resumed on the far side as though the cartographer had drawn straight through a hole in the world and hoped someone would notice.

"He drew it anyway," I breathed. "Even though it wouldn't take."

"The wardens erased the ridge," Edric said. "Not flooded it, not buried it—*erased* it. Folded it out of existence along with the rest of Osterhale's borders. Your maps show nothing there because there is nothing there to show. No hill, no mist, no path. You could walk east-northeast until your boots wore through and you'd find nothing but the conviction that you'd already seen everything there was to see." His voice tightened. "But erasure leaves a seam. Everything folded leaves a fold-line. And a fold-line can be found—if you have a compass that remembers what was taken."

I looked down at the two halves of bone in my hands. At the map that remembered.

"You're the compass," I said.

"I'm the compass," Edric agreed. "Hold me toward the ridge and you'll feel it—where the lines end, I'll pull. Like water finding a drain. It's not subtle. I've been pulling for a hundred years; nobody had ears."

Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere down Cold Row a dog barked twice and thought better of it. I sat in the yellow circle of the lamp with a dead man's leg bone split open in my lap, reading a country that officially did not exist, and I felt the thing take root that I will spend the rest of this story paying for: the certainty. Not hope—certainty. Hope is a wish. Certainty is a debt, and I had just signed for it.

"The drowned chapel," I said. "Past the ridge that isn't there. Under the altar that isn't standing. Through seven locks that each cost a memory." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Edric, this is a suicide note with cartography."

"It's a map," he said. "Maps don't promise you'll survive the journey. They only promise the journey has an end." A pause, and then, gentler than he'd said anything yet: "Marguerite. The wardens are erasing households. You told me about the book, the date, the humming. They've noticed you. Whatever you choose, the quiet life is already gone—you're just deciding whether it goes loudly or softly."

I hated him for saying it. I loved him for saying it. Both at once, the way you can only love someone who has nothing left to lose by your reaction.

"One question," I said. "The blank space on the bone. The ridge. If I walk toward it, holding you—what happens at the edge?"

"That," said Edric Vale, "is the interesting part."

And far off, beyond the shuttered window, beyond the alder stands, in a direction where every map in the valley promised nothing at all—the lamp flame leaned, just slightly, as if something had exhaled.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I laid the two halves of the femur on the bench under glass—I keep a specimen case for particularly fine work, and it seemed obscene and correct at once—and I sat across from them drinking cold tea until the window went grey, watching the ghost-lines catch the first light. Around dawn I did the thing I'd been avoiding: I pulled out every map I owned. My father's survey of the valley, 1841. The parish boundary chart. The new ordnance sheet Sable ordered from a cartographer in Lowmark two winters back, the one that cost more than my tools.

East-northeast, past the alder stands: nothing. Clean paper. The ordnance sheet didn't even bother with the convention for unexplored terrain—it simply showed the valley ending in a soft fade, as though the engraver had fallen asleep, as though everyone had always fallen asleep, in exactly the same place, for a hundred years.

Every map lied the same way. That's what undid me. Not one error—one lie, repeated faithfully by every hand that ever drew this valley, so old and so consistent that no one thought to call it a lie. That's how you erase a country. Not with fire. With agreement.

"Well," I said to the bone, to the dead man inside it, to the grey morning. "I suppose I'd better learn to walk in a direction that doesn't exist."

From the case, faint as frost forming, Edric hummed the tune the grandmother's rib had been humming three nights ago. I hadn't told him about that. I never told him about that.

Some songs, apparently, travel on their own.
