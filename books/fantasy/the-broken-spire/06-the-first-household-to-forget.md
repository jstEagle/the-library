## 6. The First Household to Forget

The morning the Weavers forgot their son began, as catastrophes in the Valley of Vell tend to, with bread.

I was at my bench before dawn—habit older than sense—finishing the commission from old Marta's rib when I heard the knock. Three raps, polite, then a fourth that wasn't so much a knock as a fist giving up halfway through. That fourth rap told me everything before I opened the door. Grief knocks like that. So does fear wearing grief's coat.

Tam Weaver stood on my step with snow in his hair and his face the color of tallow.

"Marguerite," he said. "Something's happened."

"Come in before it happens again out here."

He came in. He stood in the middle of my workshop the way men do when they've been somewhere terrible and brought some of it back on their boots. His eyes went to the bones laid out on my bench—the ribs, the little carved finches I keep for children who can't afford anything else—and he flinched.

"Tamsin's gone," he said.

"Gone where?"

"That's the thing." He laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "That's the thing, isn't it? We don't know. We woke up and she—" He stopped. Started again, slower, like a man walking on ice he could hear cracking. "We woke up and there were two beds made up in the girls' room. Two. And we only have one daughter. Bess. But the second bed—" His voice did something I'd never heard it do. It bent. "The bed was warm, Marguerite. The sheets were rumpled. There's a tooth under the pillow. A milk tooth, with a hole drilled through it, on a cord. And nobody in this house knows whose it is."

I knew.

God help me, I knew before he finished the sentence, because I had drilled that hole myself, three years ago, with a bit finer than a sewing needle, while a gap-toothed boy of six sat on my stool swinging his legs and telling me—in the solemn way of six-year-olds negotiating their first contract—that when it came loose I wanted it, because everyone said I could make dead things sing and he wanted to know if teeth counted.

Tamsin. Tamsin Weaver, seven years old now, who liked his eggs burnt and his sister's hair pulled and who had once asked me, with devastating seriousness, whether the dead got bored. I still owed him an answer. I'd been saving a good one.

"Tell me about your son," I said.

"I don't have a—" Tam stopped. And here is the thing I will remember until whatever passes for my ending: he didn't argue. He didn't laugh or get angry or call me mad. He stood there in my workshop with his mouth open, and behind his eyes something moved like a fish under ice—a shape you can't look at directly, only the shadow of it passing. "Why would you say that?"

"Because you just said *she* first. You said 'we woke up and she.' Before you corrected yourself. Your mouth remembered even if your head doesn't."

He sat down slowly on my stool, the stool Tamsin used to swing his legs off of, and put his face in his hands. "There's a coat," he said into his palms. "In the hall. Small coat, blue wool, patched at the elbow. My wife swears she's never seen it. It fits no one in our house. But it hangs next to Bess's things like it grew there, and there's a name scratched inside the collar, and I can't read it. I look at it and my eyes slide off. Like trying to count raindrops."

*Tamsin*, I thought. The letters were probably right there. Probably legible as scripture. But the valley had reached into every head on Weaver's Row in the night and scooped one small boy out of them, and what remains when a name is taken isn't blankness—it's worse. It's a shape of not-knowing, worn smooth, like a stone in a stream. You can't grieve what you can't hold.

Except me. I could hold it. Because Tamsin had given me his tooth, and I had carved it, and the carving held him the way amber holds an insect—not perfectly, not alive, but *there*.

"Stay here," I said. "Don't go home yet. Don't let anyone talk you out of the warm bed or the blue coat. Write down what you know. Right now, while the edges are still rough."

"The edges of what?"

"Of the hole, Tam. Write down the shape of the hole."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I did next, because honesty is going to matter more than comfort in the pages ahead. I didn't rush to the Weavers' house to save anyone. I went to my shelf, and I took down the water-damaged prophecy book, and I checked the date.

*On the ninth night after the book is opened,* the faded ink said, in that cramped nineteenth-century hand I'd been telling myself was coincidence, *the Spire will take its first tithe. A child. The household will not mark the absence; only the bone-hearer will carry the weight alone.*

Ninth night. I had opened the book nine days ago at my kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold beside me, feeling clever, feeling like a woman who'd found a curiosity at market. Nine nights. And last night I had sat up late reading it by candlelight, tracing the dates with my finger, and somewhere across town the wardens—or whatever wore the wardens' courtesy—had walked into a house on Weaver's Row and taken a boy who liked his eggs burnt.

The guilt arrived on schedule and set up housekeeping. Because here is the arithmetic I couldn't escape: the book named me. The book named tonight's date weeks ago, and I'd read it, and I'd done nothing but worry privately, carve diligently, tell myself the valley's magic-dead silence would keep me safe the way it keeps everything else safe. I had treated a prophecy like a curiosity and a warning like weather. And a seven-year-old had paid the difference.

Edric's femur lay wrapped in linen in the drawer where I keep the pieces that talk too much. I unwrapped it.

"You knew," I said to it. To him. "The map, the Door Out, all of it—you knew they were still doing this."

The voice rose through the bone the way warmth rises through floorboards, thin and dry and terribly patient. *"I knew they would never stop. Erasure is not an event, carver. It is a maintenance schedule. A century ago they took a country. Now they take a child at a time, because a country is only ever the sum of what people refuse to forget."*

"Can I get him back?"

A pause. Edric Vale had been dead a hundred years and he still paused before answering hard questions, which told me something about how he'd been in life. *"Perhaps. The Door opens both ways, or it opens for nothing. But the longer a name goes unsaid, the thinner it wears. Say it. Say it everywhere. Wear a groove in the world with it."*

So I did what a bone-carver does, which is work with her hands while her heart catches up.

* * *

The Weavers' house smelled of porridge and panic. Mara Weaver met me at the door with Bess clinging to her skirts, and the tableau inside was worse than Tam had described, because worse in these cases always means *more ordinary*. The table was set for four. Nobody remarked on the fourth place. A pair of small boots sat by the fire, drying, though it hadn't snowed inside anyone's imagination enough to explain them. And on the mantel, between a chipped saint and a jar of buttons, stood a clay whistle—a bad, beloved, thumbprint-smeared clay whistle that no one in that house could account for.

Mara kept touching things. The doorframe, the counter, her own wrist. Grounding herself against a vertigo she couldn't name. "It's like a word on the tip of your tongue," she told me, low, ashamed. "All day. All day it's been like that. Except it's not a word, it's—it's a whole *person-shaped* word, and I can't—"

"Mara." I took her hands. "His name is Tamsin."

I watched the name hit her. Watched it land and slide off, land and slide off, like rain on oiled canvas. Her brow furrowed. "That's not—I don't—" And then, horribly, hopefully: "Is that a name? It sounds like a name. Why does it sound like a name?"

Bess, though. Bess was five, and five-year-olds haven't finished installing the world yet, and something in her heard the name and *caught* it. She let go of her mother's skirt. "Tamsin," she repeated. Then louder, delighted, the way children are delighted by forbidden words: "Tamsin Tamsin TAMSIN!"

"Bess, hush—"

"My brother," Bess announced, with the absolute authority of the very young, and then faltered, because the word *brother* had come out of her mouth and immediately meant nothing. Her face crumpled. "Mama, why did I say that? Mama, I had—I had a—" She looked around the room, at the fourth place setting, at the drying boots, and began to cry without knowing why, which is the most unbearable thing a child can do in front of you.

Mara gathered her in. Over Bess's head, Mara looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and lost. "What is happening in this valley?" she whispered. "What did you bring into it?"

Not *what's happening to us*. *What did you bring.* Word travels fast in a small town, especially the kind of word that says the Thornbury woman bought a cursed book at market. I couldn't blame her. I'd have said the same. I said worse, silently, to myself.

"I'm going to fix it," I said, and hated how it sounded—like a promise made by someone holding a map upside down.

* * *

Night came early, the way it does in February, and I sat in my workshop with the boy's tooth in my palm and the femur against my ear like a seashell, listening to the dead man think.

The tooth hummed. Not loudly. Teeth are small instruments; they hold a single note, the way a thimble holds a single swallow. But it hummed *him*—burnt eggs and pulled hair and the question about whether the dead get bored—and I sat in the lamplight and answered it at last, out loud, to a boy who couldn't hear me.

"No," I said. "They don't get bored. They get *patient*. There's a difference. Boredom wants out. Patience wants *through*."

*"Careful,"* Edric murmured from the drawer. *"That sounds like hope. Hope is expensive here."*

"Everything's expensive here. That's rather the point, isn't it?" I turned the tooth over. The drill-hole I'd made caught the light. "Nine days since the book. One child. If it's a tithe, it's a schedule. Which means there's a next time, and a time after that, and each one will be a household waking up to a warm bed and a name they can't read. How long before the whole valley is smooth stone, Edric? How long before it's me?"

*"Longer than most. You're anchored."* A dry rustle in the bone that might have been a laugh. *"You're anchored by a dead cartographer and a stolen page and a tooth. Hardly a fleet, carver. But an anchor."*

"And you? What anchors you? You gave up your name to the lock. You said so. What's left of Edric Vale to be anchored *with*?"

Silence, of the particular quality that means *you've cut too close and I'm deciding whether to bleed*. I let him have it. Dead men earn their silences.

When he spoke again, his voice had changed—lower, and for the first time since I'd pried open the marrow-canal, almost gentle. *"The map. I stayed because the map needed finishing. Someone had to draw the way out for whoever came after. A hundred years, carver, I assumed it would be a stranger. Some descendant, some scholar, some lucky fool with a spade."* The bone warmed against my palm. *"I never imagined she'd be a woman who talks back."*

"Get used to it."

*"God forbid."*

I should have laughed. Instead I looked at the tooth, and thought about the ridge past the drowned chapel that appears on no map, and about a warden with no footprints who had stood in my workshop being courteous at me, and about Aunt Sable—who had gone white as tallow when I'd mentioned the book, whiter than grief, whiter than fear, the white of *recognition*—and who had not spoken to me since except through the letter slot.

The erasure had begun the night we opened the book. But books don't open themselves into valleys that keep their mouths shut. Somebody had let the wardens in a long time ago. Somebody had built the walls of this silence brick by brick, and Aunt Sable, who had raised me, who had taught me to carve and to lie gracefully at parties and to never ask where she'd come from—

Somebody had known.

I wrapped the tooth in silk, tucked it over my heart, and put on my coat. Sable's window was lit. It was late, and it was snowing, and I had a boy's name to wear a groove into the world with, starting with the one person in this valley who flinched at the word *Osterhale*.

She opened the door before I knocked. She'd seen me coming, or felt me, the way you feel weather change. Behind her the fire was low and there was a box on her table—an old archive box, iron-cornered, the kind I'd only ever seen in engravings of the Spire.

"Marguerite," my aunt said, and her voice was sixty years tired. "You'd better sit down. And you'd better decide, right now, whether you love me enough to hate me."
