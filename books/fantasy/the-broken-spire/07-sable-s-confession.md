## 7. Sable's Confession

I have kept sixty years of secrets in a house with thin walls, which is either a testament to my discipline or an indictment of everyone else's hearing. I prefer to call it discipline.

The night Marguerite came home from the Weavers' with that look on their face—the one they get when the dead have said something the living ought not know—I put the kettle on and took the tin box down from behind the loose brick by the hearth. Sixty years I'd kept it there. The brick was loose because I loosened it myself, in 1823, with a chisel and shaking hands, in a house I had lived in for exactly four days.

Marguerite sat at the table and watched me without asking questions, which is the family talent. Thornburys don't ask; they wait until the truth gets uncomfortable enough to come out on its own, like a splinter.

"You've been to see the parish records," I said.

"And the sexton. And Mrs. Hollis at the market." They turned their cup a slow quarter-turn. "Everyone smiles like I've asked them about the weather. Aunt Sable, the records end mid-sentence. *'And it was agreed by all parties that the matter of Osterhale—'* Just stops. Ink still wet-looking after a hundred years."

"Vell keeps its mouth shut."

"The valley doesn't have a mouth. It has people, and people have mouths, and theirs are all full of nothing."

I poured the tea. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be. "Sit properly," I said, because when you cannot give someone what they need, you give them instructions instead. It's a very old trick. The Spire taught it to me.

* * *

You should understand something about archivists before you judge me. People imagine we are librarians with better posture. In truth we are the wardens' hands. The wardens decide what the world forgets; the archivists write down what was forgotten, so that if the forgetting ever needs to be *undone*—it never is, but the paperwork must exist—the paperwork exists. We kept the ledger of erasures. Names crossed out in a hand so fine it looked like frost.

I was good at it. That's the part that shames me most, even now. Not the theft. The competence.

The prophecy book came through our offices in the winter of 1823—a seized text, flagged for destruction, one of dozens that year. Some hedge-priest in Osterhale had written down things he had no business knowing: dates, names, the shape of the fold. I was assigned to catalog it before burning. And I read it. All of it. Including the page that named a bone-carver not yet born, and a date, and a door.

I want to tell you I stole it out of principle. Out of love for truth, or some such rot. No. I stole it because I read my own name in it—not *Sable*, but the description was unmistakable: *the archivist who flees east with fire in her satchel.* When a book tells you your future, you do not burn the book. You become its accomplice. That is the whole of human history, if you ask me, though nobody asks archivists anything. That's rather the point of us.

I ran east with the page sewn into my coat lining, past three checkpoints where my colleagues waved me through because I had the confident walk of a woman going somewhere sanctioned. And I came over the ridge into this valley—which did not exist on any map I'd ever filed—and I found a town of warm, dull, contented people who had never heard of Osterhale, and I thought: *here. Here is where the book can't follow me.*

I was wrong about that too. The book waited. Books are patient. It's why we burned so many of them.

* * *

"Say it plainly," Marguerite said. "Whatever it is. You've been circling the kitchen for ten minutes and the tea's gone cold, and you know how I feel about wasted tea."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Sixty years in this house and the child still ranked tea above apocalypse. "Your mother was the same," I said, and then stopped, because that was the wrong door to open, and Marguerite saw me stop, and their eyes went sharp as awls.

"Aunt Sable. Your hands are shaking."

"They're old hands. Old hands shake. It's practically their job."

"*Sable.*"

Nobody uses my name like that except when something is truly broken. I set down my cup. I took the tin box, and I opened it, and I laid the page on the table between us—the stolen page, water-stained at one corner where I'd been caught in rain in my first week here, creased into eighths so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth.

Marguerite didn't touch it. They just read. I watched them read, and I watched the color leave their face in the particular order it leaves: cheeks first, then lips, then that stubborn set of the jaw going gray around the edges.

"This is the same hand as the book," they said quietly. "The book from the market. This is—it's a piece of it. Where did you get a piece of a hundred-and-some-year-old book, Aunt Sable?"

"I told you. I stole it."

"From whom?"

"From the Spire-wardens of Osterhale." I heard myself say it out loud for the first time in sixty years, and the words came out surprisingly easily, like a bone that had been waiting years to be set. "I was their archivist. I catalogued what they erased. And then I ran, and I hid in the one place in the world their magic doesn't reach, and I built a life, and I raised my sister's child, and I told myself every day for sixty years that silence was the price of keeping you safe. Which was true. It simply wasn't the whole price."

Marguerite's voice went very careful. "What was the rest?"

* * *

Here is the thing about the valley, and I will say it once, plainly, because Marguerite deserved plainness and got far too little of it from me.

The Valley of Vell is not a refuge. It is a prison, and we are not the prisoners—we are the *evidence*. When the wardens folded Osterhale out of existence, they could not erase the people who had seen it done. Erasure eats memory, but witnesses were made of memory; wipe them clean and there was nothing left to hold the shape of the crime. So instead they gathered the witnesses up—all who would not swear silence, and some who would have, but were inconveniently located—and they folded them into a pocket of the world where magic cannot follow, and they locked the door behind them.

That's us. That's the cheerful blankness at the parish office. That's Mrs. Hollis smiling about the weather. Every soul in Vell is a witness who has been kept, like a document, in a sealed room. The wardens visit. They tidy. They take a household's memories now and again when the stock needs feeding—I didn't know that last part until this week, and I am ashamed of how long it took me to guess—but mostly they let us sit in the dark and be evidence.

And the price of my flight? The wardens do not chase. Chasing is undignified, and besides, the valley does their work for them. But they take payment. I crossed the threshold with their page in my satchel, and somewhere behind me, in the world I'd left, my sister woke up and found she had no sister at all. No gap where I'd been. No grief. Just a life with one person fewer in it, and no way to miss what was never there.

Her name was Wren. She raised Marguerite's mother alone, or rather, she raised her believing she'd always been alone, and she died believing it, and I stood at the back of the funeral in a borrowed coat while strangers buried the only person who had ever—

Well. There it is. Sixty years and it still catches in the throat like a fishbone.

"Do you understand now?" I said to Marguerite, and my voice cracked on the second word, damn it, after holding for six decades. "Every kindness I ever showed you was also a lie. Every year of this quiet life was bought with a page I should have burned and a sister I abandoned to be forgotten. And it held. Do you hear me? It *held*. For sixty years, nobody came, nobody asked, the valley slept, and you grew up safe and strange and loved. And now you've opened that wretched book, and the thread is loose, and the wardens will pull it, and everything I paid will have been for *nothing*—"

"Breathe, Aunt Sable."

"Don't you tell me to breathe in my own kitchen."

They reached across the table and put their hand over mine, over the page, over sixty years of cowardice wearing the costume of protection. Their hands are carver's hands. Steady, cool, patient. Hands that spend their days listening to the dead.

"Aunt Sable," they said, gently, the way you speak to something wild, "there was a boy. Tam Weaver. He's gone—not dead, *gone*—and his own mother stepped over his warm bed this morning and felt nothing. I'm the only person in this valley who remembers him, because he gave me a tooth once and I carved it, and the carving holds him. If the wardens can take a child out of his own house and out of his own mother's heart, then whatever peace you bought here isn't peace. It's just a slower way of disappearing."

* * *

I begged. I want that on the record, since I'm confessing anyway. I begged.

I told them about Corvane—I'd seen his kind before, the courteous ones, the ones whose boots never quite commit to the snow. I told them what the wardens did to archivists who turned, and worse, what they did to the families of archivists who turned, and I watched my own words land on Marguerite's face and slide off like rain off waxed canvas.

I told them the Door Out was a story. I half-believed it myself. Then I remembered Edric Vale—I *did* remember him, that's the ugly joke of it, I'd catalogued his file a century ago, cartographer, executed for sedition, his final map never recovered—and I saw in Marguerite's face that they had found something under the workshop floor, and I knew the story wasn't a story, and I begged anyway. Because when the truth is unbearable, begging is the last honest work left.

"Stop," I said. "Burn the book. Burn the page. Let me carve the memory out of you myself—I know the old techniques, I helped invent half of them, God forgive me—and in a week you won't remember being curious, and in a month you'll be happy, really happy, the way the valley is happy, and you'll live and grow old and die in a warm house with someone who loves you, and none of it will ever hurt. That's all I ever wanted for you. That's all the silence was *for*."

Marguerite was quiet a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, snow kept falling on a valley that had forgotten how to want anything.

"You loved Wren," they said at last. Not a question. They'd caught the name the way they catch everything, sideways, without seeming to listen.

"More than the world."

"And the wardens took her memory of you, and you stayed silent anyway. Sixty years." They shook their head slowly. "Aunt Sable, don't you see? Silence didn't save her. Silence didn't save anyone. It just meant she died thinking she'd always been alone, and you lived thinking you deserved it."

"That's precisely what I deserve."

"No." They stood, and tucked the page into their coat with the gentleness they use on old bone, and looked at me with those eyes that have heard too many dead voices to be frightened of living ones. "Deserving isn't the point. It never was. The point is that Tam Weaver's bed is warm right now, and by tomorrow it'll be cold, and by next week there won't even be a bed. I'm going over the ridge tonight."

"There is no ridge," I said. "There's mist and there's the edge of the maps and there's nothing."

"Yes," Marguerite said, pulling on their coat. "That's how I'll know I've found it."

And they went out into the dark with a dead man's bone in their pocket and my stolen page against their heart, and I stood in my kitchen of sixty years among the cups and the cold tea and the loose brick, and I understood, finally, with the terrible clarity that arrives only after every other option has expired:

I could let them go alone. I could sit here and keep the silence that had already cost me everything it was ever going to cost.

Or I could get my coat.

I got my coat.
