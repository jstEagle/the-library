## 8. Over the Ridge That Isn't There

There is a particular kind of lie that maps tell, and I have spent my whole life forgiving them for it. A map is a bone, after all—something dead that still holds a shape—and bones are honest in the way corpses are honest: they tell you exactly what happened to them and nothing about why. So when every map in the valley agreed that the land past the millstream simply stopped—the ink thinning out into blank paper like a river running into sand—I did what any sensible carver would do. I assumed the cartographers had been lazy.

Edric Vale disabused me of that notion on my third night of listening to him.

*They weren't lazy,* he said, his voice rising up through the femur like heat off a stone. *I drew the ridge myself. Forty years I surveyed this country, girl. There was a ridge. There were orchards beyond it, and a chapel with a bell that could be heard in three parishes, and a road that ran all the way to the coast. Then one morning there wasn't. Not destroyed. Not hidden. Wasn't. And every map in Osterhale quietly grew a blank space where it used to be, and every person who looked at that blank space felt a small, polite itch of nothing, and went back to their bread.*

*And you?* I'd asked.

*I got angry,* he said, with something like pride. *Anger's the only tool that works on a fold.*

So now I was going over the ridge that wasn't there, at night, in the cold season, carrying a dead man's leg bone in a satchel against my chest like the world's most morbid infant.

Aunt Sable had not spoken to me in four days. Not since the Weaver boy. She sat in her chair by the fire with her hands folded and her eyes fixed on the middle distance, and when I came down the stairs with my pack already buckled, she said only: "The mist lies low tonight."

It was not a blessing. It was not a warning either, or if it was, it was the kind of warning you give someone you've already given up arguing with—the verbal equivalent of closing a door softly behind them. I kissed the top of her head. She smelled of camphor and old paper, and she did not kiss me back, but her hand came up and gripped my wrist once, hard, harder than an old woman should be able to grip, and then let go.

That grip rode with me up the hill like a second passenger.

* * *

The valley at night is a bowl of held breath. I know its sounds the way I know my own pulse: the millstream worrying at its stones, the wind combing the pines on the eastern slope, the far-off complaint of some farmer's dog who has opinions about the moon. Tonight those sounds fell away behind me one by one as I climbed, and by the time I reached the shepherd's path above the tree line, the only noise left was my own breathing and the creak of frozen tussock under my boots.

The femur hummed against my sternum. Not Edric's voice—not yet—but that low bone-note I'd come to think of as his attention, the way a cat's purr means *I am here and I am watching*. It strengthened as I climbed northwest, weakened when I drifted east. A compass made of murder. I followed it.

The mist lay exactly where Sable said it would: pooled in the saddle between our hills and the next, a white lake of it, waist-deep even on the path. Every child in the valley knows that mist. We're raised on it the way other children are raised on scripture. *Don't go into the low mist, Marguerite, the mist will have you.* I'd always assumed it was the ordinary sort of superstition, the kind that exists to keep children from drowning in bogs.

But I had a book with today's date in it, and a murdered man's femur humming against my ribs, and a boy named Tam Weaver whose bed was still warm and whose name nobody else could say. Superstition and I were no longer on speaking terms.

*Stop here,* Edric said, when the mist was a hand's breadth from my chin. *Look at it properly. What do you see?*

"Mist," I said. My breath made a small ghost above it.

*Look again. Cartographer's eyes.*

I looked. And slowly, the way a face comes out of a crowd once someone says the name, I saw that it wasn't moving. Mist moves. Mist rolls and curls and thins and thickens; it has moods, tides, opinions. This mist was still. Perfectly, absolutely still, from bank to bank, like milk in a bowl, like something poured and set. And its surface—it had a surface, which mist does not—caught the starlight and gave it back slightly wrong, the way water gives back a reflection a half-inch above your actual face.

"It's not weather," I said.

*It's a seam,* Edric said. *Where they stitched the world shut. Walk straight in. Don't turn aside, don't stop, don't mind whatever it tells you. Folds hate a straight line.*

I took a breath that tasted of iron and old snow, gripped the satchel strap, and stepped into the mist.

It did not feel like walking into fog. It felt like walking into a held note—a sound too high to hear, pressed against every part of me at once. The cold went through my clothes and then through the skin beneath the clothes and then through whatever is beneath skin, and somewhere in the middle of it I heard—not with my ears, which had gone strangely silent, but with the sense I use for the dead—a voice like dry paper turning pages, saying words I couldn't parse, in a tone of tremendous bureaucratic patience. Asking me something. Asking me to state my business, perhaps. Or asking me to forget it.

*I don't hear anything,* I told Edric, which was a lie, because I heard everything and understood none of it.

*Good,* he said. *If you could understand it, you'd be the wrong sort of person. Keep walking.*

I counted my steps because counting is what I do when I'm frightened—one, two, three, the way I count chisel-taps when a piece fights me. At eleven steps the pressure lifted so suddenly I staggered, and my boot came down not on frozen tussock but on something hard and regular, and I looked down and saw, pale in the starlight, a paved road.

Not a track. Not a sheep-path. A road, wide enough for two carts abreast, its stones fitted close and worn concave by centuries of wheels and feet, running away ahead of me through the mist-thin air toward a horizon that had no business existing. Behind me, when I turned, the seam hung in the dark like a curtain of grey silk, and through it, very faintly, I could see the valley lights below—our lights, the ones I'd left, small and warm and few.

Between the road and the lights there was no ridge. There was nothing. The land simply declined away into the valley as though the ridge had never been—which, I reminded myself, gripping the satchel, was precisely the official position.

"Edric," I said. My voice came out flat and strange in all that open air. "How big?"

*How big what?*

"How much did they take?"

He was quiet for long enough that I thought the bone had gone cold. When he spoke again his voice had changed—the wry, fussy, lecturing cartographer stripped away, and underneath him something older and rawer, a man standing in front of a wound.

*Walk,* he said. *You'll want to see it in pieces. All at once would break you, and I need you unbroken.*

* * *

So I walked, and the country came at me in pieces, and each piece was worse than the last, because each piece was almost fine.

That was the horror of it, once I understood what I was looking at. Nothing out here was ruined the way fire ruins or flood ruins. There was no violence anywhere. Instead everything was *incomplete*—finished right up to a certain point and then abandoned mid-gesture, like a workshop where the carver stepped out for a moment and never came back.

The road ran true for half a mile and then began to fray—not crumble, *fray*, the stones losing their edges gradually until they were more suggestion than stone, until I was walking on a road-shaped absence that still held my weight out of what I can only assume was stubbornness. Milestones stood along it at intervals. I knelt at the first one and brushed away the rime with my thumb. There were letters cut into it, deep and well-cut, and my eye slid off them the way it slides off a word you've read ten thousand times. I knew they said something. I could feel the shape of the meaning sitting just behind my eyes, patient, waiting. It would not come.

"They took the *names* off things," I said aloud.

*They took the things,* Edric corrected grimly. *The names were just what refused to leave.*

Past the third milestone stood the first orchard.

I smelled it before I saw it—or rather, I smelled where it had been. Apples. Rotten-sweet apple ghost-smell, hanging in freezing air with no orchard in sight to explain it. Then I crested a low rise and there it was: rows of trees, dozens of them, silvered with frost, and every tree shaped like ash. Not burnt—ash-*shaped*, the way a log leaves an ash-shape in the grate, holding the form of what it was while being none of it. I walked down the row and touched one. My fingers passed through the trunk and came out the other side dusted with something finer than dust, colder than snow, and smelling overwhelmingly, unbearably, of cider pressed in somebody's barn a hundred years ago.

In the crook of one branch—an impossible gesture, a branch that shouldn't have been able to hold anything—sat a bird's nest. Whole. Woven. Real. The one solid thing in the entire orchard, and I stood there holding this nest of twigs and horsehair like a relic, because it was one. Everything else had been taken. Someone had decided the nest could stay.

Or—worse thought, and it arrived uninvited—someone had decided the nest wasn't worth taking.

*The chapel's close now,* Edric said softly. *Listen.*

I listened. Above the wind, above the creak of ash-orchards, there it was: water. A great slow shifting of water, vast and unhurried, the sound a lake makes when it turns over in its sleep. But there is no lake in the Valley of Vell. There is no body of water larger than the millpond. I had grown up in a valley whose every stream I could name, and now I stood on a ridge-that-wasn't and listened to water the size of a sea lapping at shores that appeared on no map, including—especially—the ones in my own head.

I came over the last rise and the drowned chapel showed itself, and I will be honest, because a woman who lies in her own head has nothing: I sat down. Right there in the frost, satchel and all, legs simply declining further participation.

The bell tower rose out of black water maybe three hundred yards from the shore, leaning slightly, its belfry arches stark against the stars. Below it the water spread in every direction, mile upon mile of it, flat and absolute, swallowing hills I could see the shoulders of—rounded green-black shapes offshore, hilltops standing in the flood like sleeping cattle. A whole country's worth of high ground, and everywhere else, water. Somewhere under all that stillness were streets. Doors. Kitchens with kettles still on hooks, probably, and beds with the shapes of sleepers pressed into them, preserved the way the ash-orchard was preserved: finished right up to a certain point, and then abandoned mid-gesture.

"That's not a chapel that flooded," I said. My voice sounded tiny. "That's a country that drowned."

*One morning,* Edric said. *I told you. I woke and the bell was underwater and I was the only man in Osterhale who remembered there had ever been a bell. Do you understand what that is, Marguerite? To stand in a world that has been edited, and be the only comma left in the sentence?*

I did understand. God help me, I understood it completely. I had spent thirty years hearing the dead in a valley where nobody else could, carrying voices around like contraband, wondering privately whether I was a gift or a symptom. And here was the answer, laid out in drowned miles: I was a loose thread. The valley was full of loose threads, thousands of us, all of us stitched in place by people who had learned—with tremendous power comes tremendous tidiness—that the safest witness is one who doesn't know she's testifying.

The wind came off the water and it was the coldest wind I have ever felt, because it had crossed a hundred years of silence to reach me.

I got up. I walked down to the shore. The water there was clear as glass over stones, and in it, lit by starlight, I could see the tops of a wall, a gatepost, the corner of something that might once have been a market cross—all of it wearing the same unfinished quality, real right up to the edge of itself and then dissolving into suggestion. I crouched and put my bare hand in the water, because apparently I had become the sort of person who does that.

The cold was total. And through the cold, faint as a hymn through a church wall, I heard them.

Not one voice. Hundreds. Thousands. Not speaking—there were no words left for most of them to speak with—but *present*, the way a room is present after the people have left it. A hum. A congregation-sized hum, coming up through the water, through the drowned walls, through the bones of a country that had been folded out of existence by its own protectors. It was the largest thing I had ever heard, and it was saying nothing, and it was saying it directly to me.

The femur against my chest was vibrating so hard the satchel buzzed.

*Marguerite,* Edric said, and his voice shook, and I realized that in all our nights together I had never once heard him shake. *The Door is under the chapel. Whatever you're hearing—don't answer it. Not yet. If you answer before you understand the price, they'll take the answer and give you nothing back. Come to the shore at dawn. Find the archway. And whatever happens—*

The hum swelled. Just slightly. Just enough. As if, somewhere beneath three hundred years of water, something enormous had turned over in its sleep and noticed, for the first time in a century, a listener.

"—what?" I whispered. "Edric. Whatever happens, *what*?"

The bone went quiet as a stone.

And out over the black water, in the belfry of the drowned chapel, the bell that had no rope and no ringers and had been silent since before my grandmother's grandmother was born—

began, very softly, to toll.
