## 9. The Drowned Chapel

The water was the color of old pewter, and it did not move.

That was the first wrong thing about it. Water should move—should shiver in wind, should carry leaves, should at minimum have the decency to ripple when a woman stands on its edge deciding whether to drown herself for a map drawn inside a dead man's leg. This water simply sat there beneath the broken bell tower like something poured and left to set. I dropped a pebble in. It sank without a sound.

"Well," I said to the femur wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, "that's not ominous at all."

"You're talking to a bone," Edric said, from inside my head where he had lived these past weeks, dry as chalk dust. "I'd lower your standards for what counts as ominous."

Edric Vale, cartographer, murdered circa 1823, current residence: his own right femur, which I had carried over the ridge that wasn't there, through orchards turned to ash-shapes, past roads that ended mid-stride as though their walkers had been lifted off them like figures from an unfinished embroidery. He had been quiet on the climb. Now, with the chapel bell tower leaning out of the water ahead of us—black stone furred with pale weed, its bell long gone, its windows gaping like the eye sockets of something that had died face-up—he would not stop talking.

"North transept," he said, for the fourth time. "The door is under the north transept. The water came later. The wardens flooded the valley floor when they folded everything—it was their way of burying the paperwork."

"Their paperwork being a chapel."

"Their paperwork being *people*, Miss Thornbury. The chapel is just where they filed them."

I looked at the water again. It was deep—I could feel that from here, the way you can feel a stair missing in the dark—and it was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Cold like a locked room. Cold like a held breath.

"I can't breathe underwater," I pointed out.

"No," Edric agreed. "But you can hold your breath, and you're too stubborn to die before you finish a commission. You spent three weeks carving a chess set for a man who beat his dog. You'll manage one dive."

He had me there. It is a terrible thing, being known by the dead. They have nothing to do but pay attention.

* * *

I stripped to my shift, bundled my clothes under a stone with the femur—no. No, I didn't. I brought the femur. That was the whole argument of the last hour, and I had lost it, so down we went together, bone against my breastbone like a second sternum, oilcloth knotted tight.

The cold took my breath before the water did. I had imagined cold all my life; I grew up in the Valley of Vell, where winter is less a season than a permanent administrative condition. But this was different. This cold went through me and kept going, as though I were the interruption and it the substance. And the silence—

I hear the dead. Always have. Every relic I carve hums or sighs or mutters; every rib and knuckle carries its leftover person the way a shell carries the sound of a sea it never saw. I have never once in my life been anywhere truly silent. Even asleep, there is the murmur of the workshop, the little congregation of finished pieces on their shelves, whispering their small eternities.

Under that water, there was nothing. Not quiet—nothing. A silence with walls.

Except him.

"I'm here," Edric said, and his voice was the only warm thing in the world. "Breathe out slow. Follow the tower down. The transept roof collapsed inward, not outward—you'll find the gap on the shadow side. Mind the rood screen. It's iron, and iron hates what we're carrying."

We went down. The light went from pewter to slate to a green-black dark that my eyes surrendered to almost immediately, and I navigated by touch and by the pressure of Edric's voice, counting strokes, correcting my angle. My fingers found the tower wall, followed it along carvings worn to suggestions—saints, maybe, or kings, or just names, the valley's habit of writing things down being older than its habit of forgetting. The gap in the roof was exactly where he said. Of course it was. He'd drawn this place. He had probably stood in this nave when it was dry, when it rang, when people filled it the way valleys fill with snowfall—slowly, inevitably, all of them certain they'd melt before spring.

I swam through the broken roof into the drowned church.

Even blind, even breathless, I felt it change. The nothing-silence thinned. There was something down here that made a noise—not a sound exactly, more a kind of pressure, a note held below hearing, the way you can feel an organ's lowest pipe in your teeth. My hands, moving through the black water, began to ache in a familiar way: the ache of standing too close to a relic. Except the ache was everywhere. The whole chapel was a relic. The whole chapel was remembering.

"There," Edric said softly. "Down the aisle. Follow the floor slope. And Marguerite—" a pause, the first hesitation he'd allowed himself since the ridge, "—whatever you see, it isn't a trap. But it isn't a gift either. Doors never are."

My feet touched tile. I stood in a drowned church in the dark, lungs burning, and walked.

* * *

I broke the surface gasping into air that tasted of stone and centuries—the great vaulted pocket trapped beneath the transept roof, an upside-down cathedral of air above the flood. My eyes adjusted slowly. There was light. That was the second impossible thing: a pale, sourceless glow, like moonlight that had gotten lost and decided to stay, falling through the water itself as though the flood were a window rather than a lid.

And in that light stood the Door Out.

I have carved bone since I was nine years old. I know every shade it comes in—ivory, cream, honey, the grey of old teeth, the brown of things buried badly. I had never seen bone-white like this. The archway rose from the flooded floor, thirty feet if it was one, smooth as the inside of a skull, and it did not look built. It looked *grown*. Grown up out of the drowned stone like a rib from a spine, curving overhead, and set into its face were seven shapes.

Locks. Seven locks, each different, each recessed into the arch like knots into wood. As I waded closer—they were spaced up the arch's height, the lowest at my waist, the highest well above any reach of mine—I saw that each was carved in the shape of something. A cradle. An open hand. A mouth, singing. A house. A name—I couldn't read it, the letters sliding away when I looked directly, like fish in shallow water. A voice. And at the very top, half-lost in the sourceless glow, a shape I couldn't make sense of at all: not an object but an arrow, pointing forward. Pointing at tomorrow.

"Seven locks," Edric said quietly. "Each keyed to a memory freely given. Not taken. Never taken—that was the whole design. The wardens could take memory by the sackful, but a lock like that won't turn for stolen goods. It has to be *given*. Willingly. Which is why they sealed the door instead of using it." A dry pause. "Power doesn't give. Power only takes. It's practically a definition."

I stood in the water, which came to my ribs now, and looked up at the topmost lock, the arrow, and felt the shape of the price begin to assemble itself in my chest the way a storm assembles over a mountain—slowly, then all at once.

"Freely given," I repeated. "Memories. Whose?"

"Whoever opens it." His voice had gone very gentle, the way voices go around sickbeds. "The door doesn't care who. It cares that the giving is real. A memory you'd fight to keep, handed over anyway. That's the coin. That's the only coin."

I thought about my mother's lullaby, four notes of it surviving, the rest gone with her. I thought about the smell of the workshop—linseed and bone dust and the tallow candle I've burned down to a stub every working night for twenty years because replacing it feels like a betrayal of routine. I thought about the first time I heard the dead, aged nine, a hare's foot in my palm saying *run, run, run* in a voice like wind through wheat, and how I hadn't told anyone for a year because I knew—knew the way children know—that the world would take it from me if it could.

"How many do I have?" I asked. My voice echoed strangely under the vault. "In me. How many memories does a person—"

"More than you can count," Edric said. "Fewer than you think. You lose most of them without any Spire's help. That's just being alive. But the ones the locks want—" he stopped.

"The locks want the load-bearing ones." I heard myself say it and hated how steady it sounded. "Not the embarrassments. Not the wasted afternoons. The ones holding the roof up."

"Yes."

I put my hand flat against the archway. It was warm. After that water, after that nothing-cold, it was warm as a sleeping animal, and it pulsed faintly under my palm, slow, patient. Somewhere far above us, somewhere folded out of the world, the Spire that no one in the valley had ever quite seen was doing whatever Spires do, and this door had been waiting under the flood for a hundred years for someone stubborn enough to come knock.

"Why seven?" I asked, because it was easier than asking the other question. "Why that number?"

"Because there were seven wardens," Edric said. "And each of them gave one lock its key. Their own memories. That was the founding bargain—they folded Osterhale out of existence, and to seal the escape route behind them, each surrendered one true thing. Freely. Because the magic demanded symmetry." His voice changed. Went flatter. Went, I realized with a lurch, somewhere it had not gone in all our weeks together. "I know because I watched four of them do it. I drew this chapel, Marguerite. I drew the door. I was the cartographer for the project. I told myself I was making maps, not graves."

The water lapped. The glow held steady. I waited, because I have spent my life waiting for the dead to finish their sentences, and they always do, eventually, if you don't rush them.

"My lock," Edric said at last, "is the fourth one. The name."

I looked at the fifth shape up—the letters that slid away like fish. Or was it the fourth? The count blurred when I tried; the door did not wish to be inventoried.

"You gave your name."

"I gave my name to the lock, and then I ran, because giving the key and turning it are different acts, and I meant to come back with witnesses, with proof, with the prophecy page, with *something*. They caught me on the ridge road." Flat as a ledger line. "They didn't kill me for drawing a map of a door, whatever I told you. They killed me for taking my key back out. You can't, you see. Once given, freely given, it stays given. Unless you die trying to un-give it. So I stayed dead. On purpose. In the leg bone, where the marrow keeps what the mind can't, and I waited a century for a bone-carver who hears the dead to dig me up and be pigheaded enough to swim into a drowned church."

"That's not a plan," I said. "That's a prayer."

"I'm aware," said Edric Vale, who had been dead for a hundred years and had had time to become honest. "It's the only kind of plan that survives a century. Everything else rots."

I leaned my forehead against the warm bone of the arch and let myself, for exactly ten heartbeats, feel the full weight of it. Seven locks. Each wanting a memory freely given—real ones, load-bearing ones, the beams and joists of who I am. And beyond the door, presumably, the machinery of undoing a century of theft, with two wardens still living that I knew of, one of them polite and footprint-less and already asking questions in my parlor. Against all that: me, a shopkeeper with wet wool clinging to her legs, and a dead man's opinions.

Ten heartbeats. Then I straightened, because self-pity underwater is just drowning with extra steps.

"Which memories?" I asked. "Do I get to choose?"

"They choose you," Edric said. "You'll know when you touch a lock. It'll... ask. And you'll know what it's asking for, and you'll know whether you can live without it, and that knowledge will be wrong, by the way. Everyone thinks they can live without it. That's what *freely given* means—giving it while still believing you can't."

"And if I empty myself out? If I give and give until there's nothing left holding the roof up? What walks out of that door?"

Silence. The bad kind. The kind with walls.

"Edric. What walks out?"

"A woman who opened a door," he said finally, "and can't remember why she wanted it open. That's the despair of the design, Marguerite. That's why it's never been done. The price isn't death. Death is cheap. The price is becoming someone to whom the whole errand was a stranger's errand." A pause, and then, quieter, almost tender: "Unless she brings people with her. People who remember *her*. Memory shared is memory doubled. That's not poetry, that's mechanics. The door takes what one person gives. It cannot take what a whole valley refuses to forget."

I thought of Aunt Sable, burning-letters-in-the-hearth Sable, whose warnings had lately begun to sound less like madness and more like a woman reading the same book I was, several chapters ahead. I thought of the Weaver boy's tooth in my pocket, carved with a running hare, warm as if recently bitten.

"Then I'll need to not do this alone," I said.

"You'll need considerably more than that," said Edric, "but yes."

I reached up and laid my fingers on the lowest lock—the cradle-shape, small and smooth as a child's toy—and felt it wake under my skin like something turning over in sleep. Felt it begin, gently, terribly, to ask.

And that was when the water behind me moved. Not rippled—*moved*, all of it at once, a single shudder through the flooded nave, as though the valley outside had flinched. The sourceless glow guttered. Far above, muffled by a hundred feet of still pewter water, came a sound I had hoped never to hear in this place: footsteps. Crossing the chapel floor. Unhurried. Courteous. Dry.

No one makes dry footsteps underwater.

"Marguerite," Edric said, and for the first time since I'd dug him out of my workshop floor, the dead cartographer sounded afraid.
