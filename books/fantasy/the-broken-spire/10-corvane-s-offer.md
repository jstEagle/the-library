## 10. Corvane's Offer

I came up out of the drowned chapel gasping like a landed fish, which is not the dignified exit I'd have chosen for myself, and found Warden Corvane sitting on a flat stone at the water's edge as though he'd been waiting for the tide to bring me in.

He had, I realized with a lurch that had nothing to do with the cold, probably been waiting exactly that long.

"Marguerite Thornbury," he said, in the tone of a man confirming a delivery address. "Bone-carver. Niece of Sable. The only person in this valley who can hear what's left of the dead." He gestured at the rock beside him without looking at it. "You're dripping on my view."

"My condolences," I said, and did not sit.

The water behind me was black and patient. Somewhere beneath its surface lay an archway sealed with seven locks, each one hungry for a memory I could never get back, and somewhere above us both—though no map had ever admitted it—a ridge that didn't exist, and beyond that a valley full of people who were being erased one warm bed at a time. I was soaked to the ribs, my lungs still burning chapel-water, and the last thing I wanted was a conversation.

Corvane, naturally, wanted nothing else.

"You've seen the Door," he said. It wasn't a question. Wardens, I was learning, didn't traffic in questions; questions implied doubt, and doubt implied that reality might be negotiable, which for them was rather like a fish admitting water might be optional.

"I've seen seven locks," I said. "And a dead man's name on the first of them."

"Edric Vale." He said the name gently, the way you'd handle something fragile you intended to throw away. "A cartographer. A talented one. A *meddlesome* one." He turned then, and looked at me properly, and I understood why no footprints followed him: some things don't press down on the world because the world has agreed not to hold their weight. "You've been reading his bone."

"It reads itself. That's rather the point of my gift."

"Your gift," Corvane said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him, because it was kind. Genuinely kind. I have been threatened before—I live alone with bones, threats are practically small talk—but I had never been threatened by someone who seemed to mean me well, and it undid something in my chest that I would have preferred stayed fastened. "Yes. Let's talk about your gift."

* * *

He stood, unhurried, and walked a little way along the shore, and I noticed—the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn—that the reeds bent away from him as he passed. Not wind. Not weight. Just a polite, vegetative refusal to touch him.

"Do you know what the Spire is?" he asked.

"A black needle nobody's ever quite seen."

"That's the shape of it. Do you know what it *is*?"

I wrung out my sleeve. It was either that or shake, and I refuse to shake in front of men who bend reeds. "A prison. Edric says the wardens built the valley to hold the witnesses. Folded Osterhale out of existence and kept everyone who might remember it penned up here like sheep who've been told the hills are the whole world."

"Sheep," Corvane repeated, tasting the word. "Charming. Your cartographer had a gift for grievance along with his gift for maps." He turned back to face me, hands folded behind him like a vicar about to deliver difficult news. "Here is what Edric Vale never drew, because Edric Vale died angry, and angry men draw borders, not pictures. The valley isn't a prison, Miss Thornbury. It's a *mercy*. And I've come to offer you the choice he never got."

"The choice," I said flatly, "being what? To forget I ever heard of any of this?"

"To keep everything you love." He said it simply. No thunder, no smoke. The lake lapped at the stones behind me, indifferent. "Walk away. Tonight. Go home to your workshop, dry off, light your lamp, and take the commission from Mrs. Aldous that's waiting on your bench—you know the one, her grandmother's rib, the humming tune. Finish it. Carve beautiful things for people who will weep over them. Marry or don't, grow old or don't, and when you die, the valley will go on forgetting Osterhale exactly as gently as it always has, and no one will ever be hurt again."

"No one," I said, "except every soul that's already been erased."

"They aren't hurt," Corvane said. "That's the part your dead man never understood. They're *finished*. There is no suffering in erasure, Miss Thornbury. Suffering requires someone home to feel it. What we do is closer to mercy than anything your chapel-god ever managed—we simply let the wound close all the way through."

"And the Weaver boy?"

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt—I don't think the machinery for it survived whatever made him a warden—but recognition, the flicker of a clerk finding a file he'd hoped was mislaid.

"An administrative necessity," he said.

"He had a name."

"He had a bed that needed warming less than the valley needed quiet." He spread his hands, and I hated how reasonable the gesture looked against the grey water. "You want monsters. I understand. Monsters are easier. Monsters can be fought. But I am offering you the truth, which is that there are no monsters here—only arithmetic. One hundred and two years ago, Osterhale stood at the edge of a war that would have burned three kingdoms, and the wardens of the Spire were given a choice: let the world burn, or fold one country out of existence and carry the cost ourselves. We carried it. Every day since. Do you know what it costs to hold a country shut? Do you know what we *feed* it?"

"Memory," I said quietly. "One household at a time."

"One memory at a time," he corrected. "Small ones, mostly. The name of a street. A grandmother's second husband. Things the valley won't miss." He paused, and the pause was honest, and honesty from him was more frightening than any threat. "Lately, larger ones. Because the Door has been disturbed, and the Spire wakes hungry, and I confess we are running low on things the valley won't miss."

* * *

I sat down after all. Not beside him—across from him, on a wet stone, because my legs had decided the matter without consulting my pride.

"Say the rest of it," I said.

"The rest of it." He nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some examination I hadn't known I was taking. "Open the Door. Give your seven memories—or six, or five, whatever you can stomach—and turn the lock, and yes. Every erased soul comes back. The Weaver boy warms his bed again. Edric Vale gets his name shouted from the rooftops. Osterhale unfolds like a fist opening, and the maps redraw themselves, and the bells ring in chapels that aren't drowned anymore." He let it hang there, gorgeous and terrible, letting me fill in the picture myself, which was crueler than describing it. "And with them come the crimes. Every crime."

"What crimes?"

"The ones the folding was meant to bury." His voice dropped, and for the first time the courtesy thinned enough to show the machinery underneath. "Do you imagine Osterhale was innocent? A hundred years ago it was a country of Spire-wardens and bone-magics and men who traded other men's memories for power—the same trade we make now, except they made it *badly*, greedily, and the reckoning was coming for them like winter. The wardens who built this valley weren't saving themselves, girl. They were quarantining a plague. Open the Door and you don't just restore the orchards and the roads. You restore the ledgers. Every family in your valley descends from someone who sold a neighbor's memory, or bought one, or looked away. And they will *remember*. All of it. At once. With nowhere to put it."

"So we're too wicked to be saved," I said. "How convenient for the wicked."

"How *human*," Corvane said. "Which is worse." He crouched, slowly, bringing himself level with me, and this close I could see that his eyes were the exact grey of the lake, the grey of water that has swallowed things and keeps its mouth shut. "Marguerite. I have watched this valley for a century. I have watched your aunt bake bread and curse her knees and love you like her own blood. I have watched the Weavers lose a son and grieve a grief they couldn't name, which is the cruelest grief there is, and I did not enjoy it. Understand that. I am not your villain. I am the man holding the dam, and you are the woman with a hammer asking me why the water can't just be allowed to be free."

"Because the water is people."

"Because the flood will drown the people too," he said. "Their happinesses are small, but they are real. Bread and lamplight and a tune hummed through a rib. Walk away, and those survive. Open the Door, and you trade every small happiness in this valley for one enormous truth—and truths, Miss Thornbury, do not keep anyone warm at night."

* * *

I thought about my workshop. I want that on the record, wherever records go when countries are folded out of them: I sat on a wet stone in front of a drowned chapel, being offered the whole world, and what I thought about was my workshop. The smell of the marrow-dust. The lamp with the crooked stem. The rib on my bench, humming.

Because that was the trap in his mercy, and I think he knew I'd find it, and I think he respected me for looking anyway. He was right about the small happinesses. They're real. I live inside them. I have built my entire life out of bread and lamplight and the voices of the dead, and he was offering to let me keep all of it, forever, at the price of a silence I could learn to call peace.

And then I thought about the boy.

Not the Weaver boy as Corvane saw him—an administrative necessity, a bed that needed warming less than the valley needed quiet. I thought about him as I'd carved him: eight years old, gap-toothed, solemn as a magistrate, pressing his own baby tooth into my palm because he'd heard I could make things sing and he wanted to know what *he* sounded like. I'd carved it into a little whistle-bird. He'd laughed so hard he fell off my stool.

His bed is still warm. Somebody irons his shirts without knowing whose they are. His mother sets out four plates and feels, every evening, a grief with no edges, like a bruise on an organ she doesn't have.

"There's no version," I said, "where you give him back and keep the quiet."

"No," Corvane said. "There isn't. That's the arithmetic."

"Then your offer isn't mercy. It's just the dam asking the flood to stay polite."

He straightened. For a moment—for one unguarded moment—he looked tired. Old in a way his face refused to be. A hundred years of holding a dam, and here was a soaked bone-carver telling him his kindness was arithmetic, and some buried remainder of the man he'd been before the Spire looked out through the grey of his eyes and, I swear it, *thanked* me.

"You'll open it," he said. Not a question. Wardens don't ask questions.

"I'm going to try."

"Then hear the terms plainly, since you'll accuse me later of hiding them." He stepped back, and the reeds bent away again, and the light went strange around him the way it does before weather turns. "Every lock costs a memory you chose to give—that's the old law, and even I can't bend it. But the Spire is awake now, and awake, it takes. While you fumble with your locks, I will be harvesting. Streets first. Then names. Then the word for bread. By the time your seventh lock turns—if it turns—you may not remember why you turned it. That is not a threat. It is a schedule."

"Then I'd best be quick."

"Yes," he said softly. "You'd best."

He turned to go, and stopped, and spoke without turning around.

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—Edric Vale liked you. He told me once, a hundred years ago, that if the Spire ever killed him, someday a carver would find his bone and hear him, and that she'd be stubborn enough to finish what he started." A pause. "He said it like a prophecy. I told him prophecies are just wishes with good posture." Now he did turn, and the smile was back, kind and terrible. "I hope you prove him right, Miss Thornbury. I hope it costs you everything. Some prices are the only proof that a thing was worth doing."

And then the reeds stood straight again, and the stone where he'd sat was dry, and there were no footprints in the mud, and I was alone at the edge of a lake that kept its mouth shut, shivering, with six locks left and a schedule I hadn't agreed to already running.

I put my hand in my pocket, because my fingers needed to hold something that was true.

The tooth-whistle was there, small and smooth, the bird I'd carved for a boy whose name the valley had eaten. It had gone quiet since the night the book was opened—dead bone, dead wood, no voice at all.

But as I closed my hand around it, deep in the marrow of the thing, so faint I might have imagined it, something pushed back against my palm.

Once. Twice.

Like knocking.

Like a small voice, from very far away, saying: *I'm still here. Say my name.*
