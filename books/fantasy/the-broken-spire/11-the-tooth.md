## 11. The Tooth

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being forgotten: it doesn't happen all at once. It happens the way frost happens on a window—creeping in from the corners, pretty, almost, if you didn't know it was eating the view.

My name is Tam. Tam Weaver. I'm nine years old—no. Ten. I was ten in the autumn, when I found the fox jaw in the mill stream and carried it to Marguerite Thornbury in my cupped hands like it was a bird with a broken wing. I'm ten. I'm nearly eleven. I have to hold on to that the way you hold on to a rope over a drop, because the forgetting doesn't take your body first. It takes your arithmetic. It takes your edges.

I live in the space behind my own house. Not the cellar. Not the attic. The space behind it, the way a thought lives behind your eyes. I can see the kitchen through the window if I press close, and this morning my mother walked past the glass three times with bread in her hands, and three times she did not see me, and on the fourth pass she stopped—stopped dead, flour to her elbows—and looked at the window the way you look at a word you've read a thousand times that has suddenly gone strange. *Is that how you spell it?* her face said. *Was that always the spelling?*

Then she blinked, and the strangeness went back into its hole, and she carried her bread on.

That's what I am now. A spelling that looks wrong.

* * *

But I have the tooth.

That's the important part. That's the whole of the important part, so listen.

Marguerite carved it for me from the fox jaw—the backmost molar, the one shaped like a tiny fist. I asked her to make it into a wolf, because I was nine—*ten*—and wolves were the best thing I knew. She said a tooth couldn't be a wolf, it could only be a tooth, but a tooth that had been loved by a fox and then loved by a boy was already two lives, and two lives was more than most things got. I didn't understand her then. I understand her now, from in here, where everything is understood too late and too well.

She carved the tooth and drilled a hole through its root and gave me a leather cord, and I wore it every day for a season. I wore it in the bath, which my mother said was unhygienic. I wore it to church, which the minister said was irreverent. I wore it to sleep, which nobody said anything about, because nobody watched me sleep except the dark, and the dark and I had an arrangement.

And here is the trick of it, the trick I found by accident, the trick that is the only reason I am still anything at all:

The forgetting took my mother's memory of me. It took my father's. It took the school roll, the church register, the notch on the doorframe where my height was marked in pencil every Michaelmas. It took my name out of every mouth in the valley. But it did not take the tooth. The forgetting is very thorough, but it works on *minds*, and the tooth is not a mind. The tooth is bone, and bone keeps what it's given.

Marguerite says—said—the dead speak through what she carves. Well, I am not dead. I checked. I pinched myself for an hour the first night, and I am *definitely* not dead, which is somehow worse. But I am in the tooth the way a voice is in a shell. I am the hum the bone remembers. And when the forgetting comes for me—when it comes in waves, cold and gray and patient, like water rising in a lock—I press the tooth to the place where my heart used to make noise, and I say my own name into it.

Tam. Weaver. Tam Weaver. Ten years old. Found the fox jaw in the mill stream. Made Marguerite Thornbury laugh twice, which I'm told is difficult, and made her laugh a third time on purpose, which I'm told is impossible.

The tooth holds it. The tooth holds *me*. It's the last shelf in the pantry and I am the last jar, and every day the shelf gets smaller, and every day I say the name again, louder, in case anyone is listening.

* * *

Somebody is listening.

I found this out on the fourth night, or the fourteenth—the nights here don't stack properly, they lean against each other like bad firewood. The forgetting had come through my house and taken my bed. Not the bed itself; the fact of it. My mother made up the spare room with lavender and didn't know why, and my father stood in the doorway of my room—my *room*, with my fox jaw on the sill and my boots by the door and my height notched on the frame—and he said, to nobody, to the air, "Whose boots are those?"

*Whose boots are those.* My father. Who laced those boots every frost-morning for six years because I could never get the second eyelet right. Who taught me to skip stones on the mill stream. Who called me *Tam-o'-the-brook* when I came home soaked past the knees.

Whose boots are those.

I wanted to scream. I did scream, probably—I don't know what screaming sounds like from in here, whether it comes out as wind or as nothing at all. But then I did the thing. The only thing. I pressed the tooth to my chest and I said my name into it, and I said it the way Marguerite had said *two lives* that day at her bench, slow, like it was worth saying.

And the tooth got *warm*.

Not warm like a stone in the sun. Warm like a hand. And through the warmth, faint as a voice through a floor, I heard—felt—*knew*—that somewhere out beyond the behind-of-my-house, someone was holding the other end of the name. Someone who had not forgotten. Someone the forgetting couldn't reach, or hadn't reached yet, or had reached and failed, because some people are carved out of the same stuff as the tooth, and bone keeps what it's given.

Marguerite. It had to be Marguerite. She was the only one who'd ever heard the fox jaw hum.

I pushed. I don't know how else to say it. Being forgotten is a door closing, and I put my shoulder against it, the way I'd put my shoulder against the mill gate in flood season when Father said *leave it, Tam, it's too heavy* and I said *nothing's too heavy* and he said *everything's too heavy, that's the first lesson of the world* and I said *watch me.*

I pushed the name through the tooth. *Tam. Weaver. Ten years old. Fox jaw. Mill stream. Two lives.*

And far away—farther than the ridge, farther than the places maps have stopped admitting to—something answered. Not a voice. A *pull*. The way a fish answers the line: not with words, but with the whole fact of itself, sudden and alive and other. Someone had felt me. Someone was reeling.

I have never been so tired in my life, and I have never been so alive, and I did not know those two things could live in the same body, or the same whatever-I-am-now. But there it is. Everything's too heavy. Watch me anyway.

* * *

The forgetting came back for the tooth on the sixth night. Or the sixtieth. Time in here is a spilled bag of marbles; you pick up the ones you can.

It came as a grayness at the edges of the behind-of-my-house, and it did not walk, because it has no feet, and it did not speak, because it has no mouth, and it was polite the way winter is polite—no malice in it at all, just an absolute patience, a certainty that everything green ends up gray eventually and what's the hurry. I understood, lying there with my tooth in my fist, that this was the same thing that had taken my mother's memory of me. The same thing that had taken my boots' owner. It wasn't a monster. It was a *tide*. And tides don't hate the sandcastle. Tides don't even see it.

But sandcastles have architects, and I had mine.

I held the tooth up—held it up the way you'd hold a lamp, the way you'd hold a flag, the way Father held me up on his shoulders at the fair so I could see over the crowd—and I said the name into it again, and this time I didn't just say the name. I said everything. I said the mill stream in March with ice on its elbows. I said the smell of Marguerite's workshop, which was dust and oil and something underneath like a church that had been very well loved. I said my mother's bread, which she burned on the bottom and pretended she hadn't, and how I always ate the burned end anyway so she wouldn't feel bad, and how she thought I didn't notice, and how I never told her, because that's what love is, partly—it's eating the burned end and keeping quiet about it.

The grayness came on. It took the corners of the house. It took the notches on the doorframe—no, it had already taken those; it took my *remembering* of the notches, which is a meaner theft, because now I only know they were there the way you know a dream was there.

And the tooth burned in my fist like a coal, like a star, like the smallest, stubbornest lamp in the world, and the grayness came right up to the edge of it and stopped.

Stopped. The way water stops at a door. The way winter stops at a lit window.

Because the tooth was given. That's the whole secret, and I figured it out at ten years old in the dark behind my own house, which I think entitles me to at least a small monument someday: the forgetting can take anything that was *kept*, but it cannot take what was *given*. My mother's memory of me was kept, and keeping is a kind of holding, and holding can be loosened. But Marguerite *gave* me the tooth. Freely. With a joke and a hole drilled through the root. And a gift, once given, is out in the world doing its work, and no tide has ever yet un-given a gift.

I am a gift. I am somebody's given thing. And I am still here.

* * *

I want to tell you about the reaching, because the reaching is the best part, and I don't know how many more best parts I get.

Every night now—every leaning, firewood night—I press the tooth to my chest and I reach. Not with my arms. With the name. I say *Tam Weaver* into the bone the way you'd knock on a door in a house where you're not sure anyone's home, and then I say it again, and again, patient as the grayness, patient as winter, because I learned patience from the best of them.

And I've started to feel the shape of the one who answers. It's Marguerite—I'd know her anywhere, I'd know her anywhere the way you'd know your own front door in the dark—and she is *moving*. She is moving away from the valley, up and out, toward the ridge that isn't there, and she is carrying the tooth's twin, or the tooth's mother, or the tooth's—no. She's carrying a bone. A long one. A femur, though I didn't know the word until it arrived in my head with the feeling of a map attached, and I am nine—*ten*—and even I can do that arithmetic: the carver who hears the dead is walking into the dark with a dead man's leg for a compass, and she is carrying my name with her, or I am carrying hers, and I honestly don't know which way the rope runs anymore, and it doesn't matter. That's the thing I understand from in here that I never understood from out there. It doesn't matter which end holds the other. What matters is that the rope doesn't break.

Last night the grayness took the word for bread.

Not my mother's bread—I keep that one in the tooth's warmest room, behind the burned end, where nothing gets in. But the *word*. I reached for it, the way you reach for a word in conversation, and it wasn't there. Just a hole, shaped like a word, and around the hole a terrible ordinary morning-feeling, and somewhere in the valley a hundred mothers pulling loaves from ovens and not having the word for what they held. That's how it works, I think. That's how it ends, if it ends. Not with a battle. With a valley full of people holding warm bread and no word for it, smiling, and not knowing what they've lost, because that's the cruelest trick of the tide: it takes the loss along with the thing.

But I have the word. I have it in the tooth, next to the name. *Bread.* And *Tam.* And *Weaver.* And *Marguerite Thornbury*, which is a longer name and takes more breath, but I've got nothing but breath in here, or whatever breath is called when you don't technically have lungs.

So here is my plan, and it is a nine-year-old's plan—*ten*—and it is therefore the best kind of plan, which is to say: simple, stubborn, and slightly impossible.

I will say the name every night. I will say it louder. I will say it into the tooth until the tooth knows it better than the grayness knows forgetting, until the name is worn into the bone the way water wears stone, and then I will push, and push, and push, and somewhere on a ridge that isn't there, a woman with dust in her hair and a dead man's leg under her arm will feel the pull on the line and know: *the boy is still here. The boy held. The gift held.*

And she will turn toward me. I know she will. Because I made her laugh three times, and the third one was on purpose, and if you can make Marguerite Thornbury laugh on purpose, you can make her do anything.

The grayness is at the door of the behind-of-my-house now. It's patient. It's winter. It's sure of itself.

It should have taken the tooth when it had the chance.

*Tam,* I say into the bone. *Weaver. Ten years old. Fox jaw. Mill stream. Two lives. Bread. Marguerite.*

*Watch me.*

And far off, past the ridge that isn't there, past the drowned chapel with its bell tower breaking the black water like a held breath—something answers. Not the pull this time. A *light*. Small, and bone-white, and shaped like an archway, and it opens the way a gift opens: all at once, and forever, and toward me.

I put the cord over my head. I hold the tooth in my fist like a lamp.

And I start to walk.
