## 12. Locks One Through Three

The mist over the drowned chapel does not behave like mist. Mist, in my experience, is a coward—it thins when you look at it hard, it retreats from lamplight, it has the good manners to be merely weather. This mist stands. It parts around me in a slow, deliberate way, like a congregation making room for someone they'd hoped wouldn't come.

I climb down through the ruin of the chapel yard with Edric's femur tucked against my ribs and Tam's tooth knotted on its cord at my throat, and I think, absurdly, of how Aunt Sable would scold me for the state of my boots. It helps to think about boots. Boots are small problems, and I have recently learned that small problems are a kind of food.

The water is black and still as poured ink. The bell tower rises out of it maybe forty feet from shore, leaning like an old man listening at a door he shouldn't. And beneath the water—Edric's voice says beneath, patient as ever, the way he has been patient for a hundred years—is the Door.

*You'll have to go under,* he tells me. Not unkindly. He never says anything unkindly, which is remarkable in a murdered man. *I'm sorry. I would have mentioned it sooner, but you were already committed by then, and I find commitment goes better uninformed.*

"You're a terrible cartographer," I say out loud, because talking to him out loud is the only thing keeping me from being a woman who talks to bones alone in the dark, which is a different thing entirely, apparently.

*I am an excellent cartographer,* he says. *I simply mapped honestly. Honesty is rarely convenient.*

The cold of the water is not a temperature. It is an opinion. It goes through my clothes and my skin and finds the marrow of me and settles there, and I swim anyway, one-armed, because the other arm is holding the femur above the surface like a fool carrying a candle through a flood. The tower grows. The doorway beneath it grows. And there—

There it is.

The Door Out is bone-white and taller than any door has a right to be, an archway of a single seamless piece, standing upright on the flooded chapel floor as though water were a rumor it declined to believe. Its surface is carved all over—not ornament, I understand immediately, but writing, or rather the memory of writing, worn to suggestion. Seven rings are set into the arch, seven circles of paler bone, and six of them are dark.

One is faintly warm-colored, like a coal remembered rather than seen.

*That one was mine,* Edric says quietly. *Lock four. I gave it my name before they caught me. You cannot open what I sealed—but you can pass what others open. That is the design. No lock opens by force. No lock opens by theft. They only open to a memory given freely, by someone who could keep it and chooses not to.*

"Freely," I repeat. My voice comes out strange underwater, even though I'm speaking above it now, treading, teeth chattering. "Define freely."

*Ah,* says Edric Vale, dead cartographer, voice of a femur, and I can hear him wince across a century. *There is the question.*

I pull myself up onto the archway's base where it breaks the surface—a ledge of white bone wide enough to kneel on, absurdly dry, as if the Door has drawn a line and the water respects it. From here I can see all seven rings at eye level. From here, shivering, soaked, holding a dead man's leg bone like a lantern, I wait for him to define freely.

*A memory given away does not leave you the way a coin leaves your hand,* he says at last. *It leaves the way a tooth leaves your mouth. The shape of it stays. The ache of it stays. But the thing itself—the sound, the warmth, the truth of it—you will know that you had it, and you will not be able to reach it. Ever. And you will give it anyway, or the ring will know, and stay shut.*

"That's not a lock," I say. "That's a confession booth."

*Most doors are,* he says. *People just don't usually notice.*

I laugh. It comes out cracked and half-drowned and it echoes off the bell tower, and somewhere in that echo I hear the boy—Tam, ten years old, fox jaw, mill stream, two lives, bread—Tam, who is currently a warm bed in an empty house in a valley pretending he never existed. I hear him in the echo the way you hear a name called from another room: not the words, just the fact of being called.

He pushed back against the forgetting. A child, half-erased, pushing. The least I can do is lose some souvenirs.

"All right," I tell the Door. "Which one first?"

*They will choose,* Edric says. *Or rather—they will ask, and you will answer, and you will not be able to lie. That is the whole mechanism. That is why the wardens built it this way and why they hate it. Power takes. This asks. They never learned the difference.*

The first ring warms.

Not glows—warms, the way a stone warms that's been sitting in sun, and a sensation moves through it into my hands where they rest on the arch, a sensation like being asked a question by something very old and very polite.

And I know what it's asking before it finishes asking, the way you know a tune from its first three notes.

My mother's lullaby.

Oh, that's cruel. That's beautifully, precisely cruel, and I want to write whoever designed this Door a strongly worded letter. Because of course it starts there. Of course it starts with the oldest thing I own.

Here is what I have: my mother died when I was nine, in this valley, of a fever the valley's doctors insisted had no name, and all she left me was her hands' habits—which is to say, carving—and a lullaby in a language I don't speak, four lines long, hummed more than sung, the melody going somewhere minor at the end like a path bending around a hill. I have hummed it to myself every night of my life. I hummed it the night Sable told me to burn the book. I hummed it walking over the ridge that isn't there. It is the smallest room in the house of me and I have lived in it more than anywhere else.

And the Door wants it.

"Not the words," I say. "I don't even know the words."

*It doesn't want the words,* Edric says gently. *It wants the melody. Freely.*

I sit back on my heels on the dry white ledge, dripping, and I do the arithmetic every giver eventually does. What is a lullaby, kept? It is a comfort. It is four bars of minor-key shelter. If I keep it, I keep humming it into my pillow until I die, and no one else ever hears it, because no one else alive knows it, and when I die it dies, and the valley forgets a little more, one lullaby at a time, exactly the way Corvane works—except mine would be voluntary, which somehow makes it worse.

What is a lullaby, given?

Given, it becomes part of a lock. Part of an opening. Part of a door that leads to a world where maybe—maybe—some other mother hums it to some other child, and neither of them knows it passed through a drowned chapel inside a bone-carver who can no longer remember why the tune makes her chest hurt.

"That's a trade," I say aloud, to the Door, to Edric, to the black water. "You're asking me to trade the last of her for everyone else's everything."

*Yes,* says Edric. Simply. He doesn't dress it up. He's never once lied to me, and I suspect he considers lying to the dying or the drowning a professional discourtesy.

I close my eyes. I hum it once, all the way through, deliberately, slowly, the way you look at a face before a long journey—minor turn at the end, path bending round the hill, sleep now, little bone, sleep now, little light.

Then I open my eyes, and I give it.

I don't know how to describe giving except that it feels like exhaling something that isn't air. The melody lifts out of me the way heat lifts off a stone, and I feel it go—feel the exact moment it stops being mine and starts being the Door's—and the first ring flares bone-white and turns, and somewhere deep under the water a sound like a great breath being released rolls up through the arch.

And then it's gone.

I test it immediately, the way you probe a missing tooth with your tongue. I try to hum. My throat makes the shape of humming. Nothing comes. There's a hollow where the tune was, smooth-walled, and I know—I know with total certainty—that my mother sang to me, that I loved it, that it mattered. I know the fact the way I know the year of my birth. But the thing itself is behind glass now, on the far side of a door I opened myself.

I sit there and shake, and it isn't the cold.

"Well," I manage, after a while. "That's one."

*You don't have to do the next one tonight,* Edric offers. *Rest. Warm yourself. The Door is patient; it's the only thing in this story that is.*

"No," I say. "If I stop, I'll start negotiating. And I've met me. I'll lose."

The second ring warms before I've finished saying it. Eager. Or perhaps just efficient. There's a difference between those two things and I choose not to examine which one this is.

This time the question arrives as a smell.

Wood shavings and linseed oil. Bone dust, fine as flour. The tallow-and-iron tang of my tools. The particular sweetness of marrow when a piece is freshly cut, which civilians find horrifying and I find honest. The workshop smell—the smell of the room where I have spent every working day of my adult life, the room where the dead speak to me through the pieces I carve, the room that smells like my entire self.

The Door wants the smell of my workshop.

I almost laugh. Of all the treasures to demand. Not my sight, not my hands, not my memories of Sable or of Tam or of the book itself—just the smell. It seems so small. It seems like a bargain, after the lullaby.

But I've been a carver too long to underestimate small things. A smell is a key that fits a thousand doors at once. Walk into any room in the world smelling of shavings and oil, and you are in your workshop; grief, terror, joy, all of it happens in that smell. Take it away and every day of my life gets subtly, permanently estranged from me. I will carve and the carving will happen in a place I visit rather than a place I live.

*It knows what it's doing,* Edric murmurs, and there's something raw in his voice now, something that sounds like a man watching someone walk a road he walked. *Mine took the smell of ink. I drew maps for thirty years and I have never since known what ink smells like. People tell me. I nod. It means nothing.*

"Why would it take that?" I ask. "What does the Door need with woodsmoke and bone dust?"

*Nothing,* he says. *That's the point. It doesn't need. It asks for what you'll miss most and least expect to miss, because that's how it knows the gift is real. Anyone would surrender a bad memory. The Door doesn't want your bad memories. It wants proof you're paying.*

I breathe in, slow and deliberate. Shavings. Oil. Bone dust. Marrow-sweetness. Tallow and iron. The smell of the room where I heard my first dead woman humming through a rib, the room where I dug up a murdered man's leg and got answered for the first time in my life, the room where I sat up nights with a prophecy book learning that my whole world was a prison built by polite monsters.

"I give it," I say. "All of it. Freely. Whatever that word costs."

It lifts out of me like steam off a fresh-cut bone, and the second ring blazes and turns, and the deep breath-sound comes again, closer now, and the water around the arch trembles.

And the world goes flat.

Not empty—flat. I'm still kneeling on white bone in black water under a leaning tower, and none of it smells like anything. Snow should smell of something. Water should smell of something. My own soaked clothes, my own skin: nothing. I am a woman in a painting of herself. I know these things are here. I cannot reach them. Somewhere in me a workshop exists, full of tools and voices and light, and I stand outside it forever, reading the sign on the door.

I put my hand flat on the arch to steady myself, and the third ring warms under my palm.

And this one, this one I knew was coming. I'd known since the first ring, if I'm honest. Doors like this go in order. Lullaby, workshop, and then—

The first time I heard the dead.

I was eleven. It was in this valley, in winter, in the churchyard, and I'd gone there to sulk, because sulking requires privacy and my aunt's house offered none. And a slate headstone—unremarkable, lichen-eaten, some farmer's wife dead sixty years—hummed at me. Not the woman humming. The stone. Grief so old it had become mineral, and it reached up through my palms where I'd braced my hands on it, and I heard her, and I wasn't frightened. That's the detail I've treasured all these years. Everyone assumes it must have been terrifying. It wasn't. It was like a window opening in a wall I'd thought was solid, and on the other side: people. All the people, all the time, patient, waiting to be heard.

It is my gift. It is the first moment of my gift. It is the reason I am a bone-carver instead of a shopkeeper, the reason I dug under my floorboards, the reason I'm kneeling on this archway at all. Every single thing that has brought me here passes through that afternoon in the churchyard.

And the Door asks for it.

*Marguerite.* Edric's voice, and for the first time since I pulled him out of the ground, it shakes. *Listen to me. This one is different. The other two were possessions. This one is the origin of you. If you give it, you may still be able to hear us—your gift may survive, it's yours, born in you—but you will not remember hearing for the first time. You will not remember the window opening. You will not remember why you trusted me, that first night, when a stranger's voice came out of a bone. Everything we've built rests on that trust, and you'd be giving away the memory it grew from.*

"I know," I say.

*Do you? Because I watched the wardens make trades like this for fifty years, and the ones who survived were the ones who understood exactly what they were trading.*

"Edric." I look down at the femur in my lap, at the bone that talked back, the first voice in twenty years of voices that ever said anything to me instead of through me. "Why did you stay dead?"

The question surprises him. I can tell. A hundred years of patience and I finally found the crack.

*Because,* he says slowly, *someone had to trust that the right person would come along and listen. And trust is worthless unless the person trusting has nothing to gain. I stayed dead so that someday a carver with your gift would dig me up and be believed—by me. That was the whole plan. That was the map.*

"Then let me pay it forward," I say. "Someone trusted me without proof. I'll trust whatever comes after without proof. That's not losing the gift. That's spending it."

The ring pulses. Waiting. Polite. Old.

I think of Tam Weaver, warm bed, empty house. I think of a valley that forgot the word for bread. I think of my mother's lullaby, already living in the first lock, doing work she never could have imagined, and I think: she'd have hated this, and she'd have done it, and she'd have made a joke about my boots.

"The first time I heard the dead," I say to the Door, clearly, so there can be no mistake, "I give to you. Freely. Keep it. Use it. Open."

It goes out of me like a lamp going out—no, like a window closing, quietly, in a wall I will always know is a wall now, because I gave away the memory of it ever having been a door.

The third ring blazes white. Turns. The breath-sound beneath the water comes again, vast, and this time the arch itself shivers, and one by one the dark rings begin to glow faintly at their edges, waking, hungry, waiting for the rest.

Three down. Four to go. And one of them belongs to a dead man who gave his name, and one of them, Edric told me once, in a voice he tried to keep casual, needs something no dead person has ever had.

I kneel on the white bone, hollowed out like one of my own carvings, unable to smell the water or hum or remember the beginning of myself, and I hold Tam's tooth in my fist, and I say:

"What's next?"

And the fourth ring—the dead man's ring—flickers, and refuses, and holds.

And behind me, across the black water, on the shore, someone has just stepped out of the mist.

She is small, and gray-shawled, and sixty years of silence are written all over the way she stands, and she has left no footprints in anything, because there is nothing left in her heavy enough to press them down.

"Sable," I whisper.

*Ah,* says Edric Vale, very softly. *Now it begins.*
