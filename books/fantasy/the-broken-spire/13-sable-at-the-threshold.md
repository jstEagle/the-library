## 13. Sable at the Threshold

I have spent sixty years being very good at not going places.

It is a discipline, like any other. You practice on small things—the road past the mill, the eastern shore of the lake, the ridge that every map in this valley agrees does not exist and which I have never once contradicted them about. You learn to arrange your life so that the forbidden directions are simply the ones you don't happen to need. You tell yourself it isn't fear. You call it contentment. Contentment is a wonderful word; it can be stretched over almost anything, like a tarp over a body.

But tonight I am walking east, in the dark, in weather that has no business being walked in, toward the drowned chapel past the ridge, because my niece is down there trying to unmake the only peace this valley has ever been allowed to keep.

Or so I would have told you yesterday. Tonight I am no longer sure who is doing the unmaking.

* * *

Let me be honest, since honesty appears to be the currency of the hour and I am badly out of practice with it.

I was not born here.

I know how that sounds. In the Valley of Vell, saying you were born elsewhere is like saying you were born on the moon—except the moon is at least visible from here, and Osterhale is not. Osterhale is not anywhere. That was rather the point of it. A century ago—or ninety-eight years, if we are counting precisely, and I have had nothing to do for sixty years but count precisely—the country of Osterhale was folded out of existence by its own Spire-wardens. Not conquered. Not burned. Folded, the way you'd fold a letter closed, and then tucked into a pocket of the world where magic could not follow, and forgotten on purpose, by design, with paperwork.

I know all this because I was one of the people who did the paperwork.

Archivist, third class, Spire of Vell. I catalogued memories as they came in. Names, mostly. Faces. The sound of particular voices saying particular things. The wardens called it harvest, which is a farmer's word, warm and honest, and they used it deliberately, because farmers are trusted and what we were doing could not have survived an honest name. People gave their memories freely—that was the rule, the sacred rule, the one law even the wardens pretended to obey—and we filed them, and the Spire grew fat on them, and Osterhale grew thin.

I fled with a single page. Page forty-one of the prophecy book, written in 1823 by a woman named Ilseva Corrigan who had the misfortune of being right. The page named a bone-carver. It named a date. It said, in essence: *one will hear what was buried, and the buried will speak back, and on that night the valley's long silence ends.*

I stole the page and ran, and the price of running was exacted before I cleared the border of the fold. They took my sister's memory of me. Not my memory of her—they were precise about such things, the wardens; cruelty was never their failing, only their method. Merrin woke the next morning in our childhood house with a sister-shaped hole in her heart and no idea what shape had made it. She grieved something she couldn't name. I have been told that is worse than grieving something you can. I believe it. I have watched her do it, these sixty years, from across a valley she doesn't know I crossed to reach.

Because here is the joke, the beautiful, obscene joke: I fled *into* the prison. The Valley of Vell is not a refuge. It is the holding cell the wardens built for everyone who might someday remember too much. I knew that when I came. I came anyway, because a cell with Marguerite Thornbury somewhere in its future seemed preferable to a tower without one.

And then I spent six decades keeping my mouth shut, and calling it love.

* * *

The mist over the ridge parts the way a curtain parts when someone behind it wants you to come through. I felt that, the wanting, and it frightened me more than the cold did. This land should not want anything. This land was built to be inert—a pocket, a dead pocket, magic-proof, memory-proof, proof against everything except the slow rot of ordinary time. But tonight the ground under my boots hums like a struck bell that hasn't finished ringing, and the mist folds aside for me as though I am expected, and I think: *she's already opened three locks.* Three freely given memories. I know what that costs. I took thousands of them, once, and never once paid.

The ruined road on the far side of the ridge is exactly as I remember it from the archives, which is to say it is exactly as I remember it from the ledgers, because I never saw Osterhale with my own eyes. Ash-shapes where orchards stood. Fence posts standing at attention over fields that forgot how to grow. And ahead, breaking the black water of the flooded vale, the chapel's bell tower, leaning like a drunk against the sky.

I walk faster. Somewhere below, my niece—who is not my niece, who is no relation of mine at all, whom I found as an infant left at the workshop door and claimed with the ferocity of a woman replacing what was taken—is diving into a drowned chapel to open a door that will end the world as I have carefully, cowardly preserved it.

I raised her, you understand. Whatever else I am, whatever the ledgers say, I raised her. I taught her to carve. I taught her to hide the hearing—*never let them know, child, never let the wrong person see you listen to a bone*—and I told myself I taught her that to protect her, and maybe I did, but I also taught it to protect the secret, and the secret and the girl are not the same thing, and I have known that for years and done nothing, because doing nothing is my discipline, my craft, my art.

Well. Tonight I am doing something. Whether it is the right thing remains to be seen, but it is certainly something, and after sixty years of practiced stillness the sheer novelty of motion nearly carries me down the chapel hill.

* * *

She is on the shore when I arrive, soaked to the ribs, hair plastered to her face, holding a femur the way other people hold a lantern. The Door stands behind her in the shallows—an archway of bone-white stone rising out of the water, impossibly tall, impossibly quiet—and I can see, even from the bank, that three of its seven locks hang open, and that opening them has cost her. There is a hollowness around her eyes that I recognize. I used to see it in the donors' faces, back when we called them donors.

"Sable." Her voice comes out scraped. "You followed me."

"I ran, actually. Following implies dignity." I pick my way down the shingle toward her, and every step I take I am composing the speech, the one that will stop this, the one about the valley's small happinesses, the bread and the bells and the children who sleep soundly because they don't know they're prisoners. I have been composing versions of this speech for sixty years. It is very good. It has never once convinced anyone, including me.

"You shouldn't be here," she says.

"No," I agree. "I've spent sixty years not being here. I thought I'd try the alternative."

The femur in her hands hums. I hear it—I hear it the way you hear a fly in a quiet room, a thin persistent note under everything—and Marguerite tilts her head, listening, and then looks at me with an expression I cannot read.

"Edric says you're lying," she says.

"I expect he does."

"He says you're not here to stop me."

I open my mouth to deliver the speech. The speech is right there. Sixty years of rehearsal, polished smooth as river stone, and it dies in my throat, because the archway is humming now too, a low chord, and the water around it is glowing faintly, and I look at my hands and they are shaking, and I realize that I did not run down that hill to save the valley.

I ran down that hill because I could not bear for her to cross this line alone.

* * *

"The fourth lock," Edric's voice says, from the bone, and it is a strange voice, dry and patient, a man who has been dead a century and has made his peace with the wait. "It takes a memory freely given. Any memory. But the Door knows the difference between a coin tossed and a coin surrendered."

"What does that mean?" Marguerite asks.

"It means," I say, and my voice surprises me by being steady, "that it won't accept a sacrifice you're trying to make. Only one you actually mean."

She turns to look at me fully then, and there is water on her face that may or may not be from the dive, and she says, quietly: "You knew. All along. You knew what the valley was, what the Spire was, what happened to Osterhale. You knew and you let me grow up inside the lie."

"Yes."

"You told me to burn the book."

"Yes."

"You told me forgetting was natural. Grief. Time."

"I told you what kept you alive." And that is true, and it is also the smallest possible version of the truth, the truth shaved down until it fits through the keyhole of my conscience. "Marguerite, I was an archivist of the Spire. Third class. I catalogued what they took. I know the weight of those locks better than anyone living, because I weighed them when they came in. Names. Lullabies. The smell of a workshop." Her flinch tells me she has already paid two of those. I press on, because if I stop pressing I will stop entirely. "You have given away your mother's voice and your own home and you are standing in freezing water telling yourself you can afford the rest. You can't. No one can. That is the entire architecture of the thing—it is built out of what people can't afford, given gladly."

"And your answer was sixty years of silence?"

"My answer was survival. It's a modest answer. It keeps a person warm." I laugh, and it comes out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what they took from me, when I ran? Not my freedom—I kept that, such as it was. Not my memories—I kept every one, that was the punishment, that was the *point*. They took Merrin's memory of me. My sister woke up whole and happy and missing a person she couldn't name, and I have lived down the road from her for sixty years, Marguerite. I buy eggs from her. She calls me 'the carver woman' and is kind to me the way you're kind to strangers, and every kindness is a knife, and I have borne it, I bore it because bearing it was the penance and the penance was the price and the price bought *you*, bought the page staying hidden, bought the prophecy sleeping another decade, another century—"

"Bought the Weavers' son staying erased," she says.

And there it is. The boy. Tam. I remember him—of course I remember him, I am one of perhaps four souls in this valley who do—and I remember the tooth he gave Marguerite to carve, and I remember thinking, when the Weaver household went blank, *this is the cost of my caution,* and I remember deciding, in the same breath, that the cost was acceptable, and I have been choking on that decision ever since.

"No," I say. "No. It didn't buy that. Nothing buys that. That was the bill coming due, and I have been hiding from the collector since 1823."

* * *

The water glows brighter. The archway hums a half-tone higher, and Edric's voice comes again, gentle as a man laying out cards: "Fourth lock. Whenever you're ready. Or never. The Door is patient. It was built by patient monsters."

I look at my niece. My not-niece. The child I stole from the margins of a prophecy and raised in a lie, who grew up hearing the dead because some inheritance older than either of us decided she should, who walked into the dark carrying a murdered man's leg and did not turn back at any point where a sensible person would have turned back, and who is looking at me now not with anger—which I could survive—but with an awful, patient hope.

"You don't have to," she says. "I'll find another way."

"There is no other way. There is only the way, and the ways around it, and I have lived in the ways around it for sixty years and they are all the same road." I wade toward the arch. The water is colder than sin and rises past my knees, my waist, and I do not stop, because stopping is my discipline and I am retiring from it tonight, effective immediately, no notice given.

The lock hangs at the height of my heart. Bone-white, seamless, with a hollow at its center shaped like nothing and waiting like a mouth.

"What do I do?" I ask.

"Think of it," Marguerite says softly. "Whatever you give. Think of it, and mean the giving, and put your hand in the hollow."

I think of Merrin.

Not the grief—I have worn the grief threadbare, it has no more texture than an old coat. I think of her *face*. That is the memory they left me, cruelly, the one side of the ledger: I kept hers, she lost mine. I think of her at seven, gap-toothed, furious about a doll. At fifteen, laughing behind her hand in chapel. At nineteen, the morning I left, standing in the doorway in her nightgown, saying *back by supper?*—and me saying yes, and meaning it, and meaning to keep it, and the supper I never came home to stretching out across a century.

Her face. I hold it up in my mind like a lamp.

And I understand, finally, why the lock must be given freely, because what I feel, offering it, is not loss. It is release. Sixty years I have carried her face as a wound, a relic, a reason for silence. To give it to the Door is to say: *I was wrong to hoard it. Love is not a thing you keep. Love is a thing you spend.*

"I'm sorry," I say—to Merrin, to Marguerite, to the boy whose name I helped bury, to the whole folded country, to myself last of all. "I stayed silent to protect you, and it was cowardice wearing protection's clothes. I choose the truth. Take it. Take her face. Let the truth have it, since I only ever kept it for myself."

I put my hand into the hollow.

* * *

It does not hurt. That is the terrible part. It feels like setting down a load you'd forgotten you were carrying—you stagger, you straighten, you reach for the weight out of habit and find air. The lock opens with a sound like a held breath released, and light runs around the archway's rim, and somewhere in the depths of the drowned chapel something vast shifts in its sleep.

And Merrin's face is gone.

I search for it the way you run your tongue over a missing tooth. I remember that I had a sister. I remember that I loved her. I remember that her face was the dearest thing I owned and that I gave it away an hour ago, or a moment ago, on purpose, freely, and the remembering is intact and the face itself is a white space, a silhouette cut from paper, a doorway with no one in it. I gave the Door the picture and kept the frame. It is exactly as bad as I always feared and completely different from how I feared it, and I am crying, and I am lighter, and both things are true.

"Sable." Marguerite is beside me in the water, gripping my arm, holding me up, though I hadn't noticed falling. "Sable, look at me. Are you—"

"I'm fine," I say, which is a lie, but a generous one, the kind you give away. "I'm lighter. Ask me her name."

"Merrin."

"Merrin." The name works. The name is still mine. "Good. Keep saying it. Apparently names survive the crossing."

She laughs—wet, cracked, real—and then stops, because the archway is changing. The remaining three locks have begun to glow, all at once, pulsing in rhythm, and the rhythm is wrong, it is quickening, it is the rhythm of a heart that has noticed it is being robbed.

Edric's voice comes low and urgent from the femur: "It felt that. The Spire felt that. Four locks open—the wardens will know now, they'll know *where*—"

Across the water, beyond the ridge, beyond the fold, a light kindles on the horizon. Thin. Black-edged. Rising.

Neither of us has ever seen the Spire before. No one in the valley ever has. That was the design—we built it invisible, we archivists, we filed its existence away with everything else.

Tonight it stands on the horizon like a needle threaded with lightning, and it is looking at us.

"Oh," I say, with the calm of a woman who has just discovered she has nothing left to lose. "That's not good."
