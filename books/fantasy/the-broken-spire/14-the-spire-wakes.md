## 14. The Spire Wakes

The light on the horizon does not flicker. That's the first thing I notice, and the first thing I hate about it. Fires flicker. Stars shimmer. Even lightning, for all its violence, has the decency to be brief. This is a column of steady black-edged radiance, thin as a needle threaded through the world's fabric, and it does not waver because it does not need to. It has been standing there for a century. It has simply never bothered to be seen.

"It's looking at us," I say again, because saying it twice makes it no more real and therefore easier to carry.

"Stop narrating," Edric says from the femur in my hands, "and start moving."

"I'm a carver. Narrating is half the trade."

"Marguerite."

The way he says my name—not *carver*, not *girl*, but *Marguerite*, with the weight of a man who has waited a hundred years to have someone to say it to—closes my mouth better than any argument. I crouch at the water's edge of the drowned chapel, the Door Out rising behind me in its arch of bone-white stone, four of its seven locks now dark and open, three still burning with a low amber light that pulses like something breathing.

The Spire pulses back.

I feel it in my teeth. In the bones I'm not currently holding. In the marrow of the world.

*It felt that,* Edric had said. Four locks open. Four memories freely given—my mother's lullaby, the smell of tallow and bone-dust, the first time I heard the dead speak through a sparrow's wing-bone, and Sable's sister's face, given by a woman who crossed sixty years of silence in one night to stand beside me. The Door knows we're coming. And now the thing that built the prison knows too.

"We need the fifth lock," I say. "Edric—the sound of your voice. You said each lock takes a memory freely given. If I give you up—"

"You'll give me up," he agrees, and there's something almost gentle in it. "And then I can't guide you anymore. Think, carver. There may be another order."

"There isn't another order. There's never an order. There's just what hurts least at the time, and you find out afterward how wrong you were." I stand, water dripping off my skirts, and look at the needle of light on the horizon. "But not tonight. Tonight we do it in whatever order keeps us alive longest."

* * *

We don't make it back over the ridge before the valley starts screaming.

Not with voices. With absences.

I feel it the way I feel everything now—through bone, through the femur clutched against my chest like a child clutches a doll against the dark. A tremor runs through Edric's old murder-bone, and his voice cracks mid-sentence: "—the eastern road, something's happening on the eastern—oh. Oh, no. Marguerite, the Harrow lane. The Harrow lane is *gone*."

"Gone?"

"Not destroyed. *Unwritten.*"

I crest the ridge and look down into the Valley of Vell, the valley that has been my whole world for thirty-one years, and I watch it forget itself.

It begins as a smudge. The Harrow lane—the crooked street where Widow Marsh sells candles and the Pell boys race their dog cart every market day—goes soft at its edges, like ink touched by a wet thumb. Then the smudge spreads. Houses don't fall; they simply stop insisting they were ever built. Roofs fade to suggestions. Chimneys become rumors of smoke. And the people—

The people keep walking. That's the worst of it. They keep walking their errands with the patient blankness of sleepwalkers, because a body remembers its habits even when the mind has been scooped out like a gourd. Widow Marsh stands in her doorway holding a candle she can no longer name the purpose of. One of the Pell boys turns in circles in the road, looking for his brother, looking for his dog, looking for the reason he came outside at all.

And behind it all, at the far end of the valley where the mist folds around nothing, a figure moves. Small at this distance. Unhurried. Courteous even in this, the way he was courteous in my workshop with snow melting nowhere near his boots.

Corvane is walking the streets of Vell like a tax collector, and he is collecting.

"No," I breathe. "No, no—he can't just *take* them. The locks needed memory *freely given*. That was the whole design, that was the—"

"The locks are the Door's terms," Edric says grimly. "The Spire has no such scruples. Why should it? It was built by men who'd already decided consent was a formality." A pause, and when he speaks again his voice has gone quiet in the way of a man reading his own death warrant. "This is what they did to Osterhale, Marguerite. Not all at once. Street by street. Household by household. They called it *tithing*. I drew maps of the districts as they went dark. I thought I was documenting a plague."

"What happened to your maps?"

"They became evidence. Evidence becomes motive. Motive becomes a femur in a cache under a floor." He laughs, and it's a terrible sound, dry as the marrow-canal I carved open. "I was very thorough about hiding them. It bought me perhaps a week."

* * *

I run. There's no dignity in it—a bone-carver of thirty-one years, hauling skirts through bracken with a dead man's leg bone pressed to her heart—but dignity is a currency for people whose valley isn't being spent. I come down off the ridge onto the mill road and the first thing I meet is Tam Osgood, the farrier, walking toward me with his hands in his pockets and his face smooth as an unscribed slate.

"Tam," I say, grabbing his sleeve. "Tam, the forge—is your forge lit? Is your wife—"

He looks at me with polite incomprehension. "Beg pardon, miss. Were you wanting something?"

His eyes are fine. Clear brown, unclouded. That's the horror of it—it isn't like madness, where the lights burn wrong. Everything in him works. There's just nothing *in* the rooms anymore. He's a house with the furniture carried out, and he doesn't know enough to miss it.

"Your wife," I try. "Her name. Tell me her name."

A flicker. Something behind his eyes strains toward a shape and finds only a wall where a door used to be. "I..." His brow furrows, and for one moment—one terrible, hopeful moment—I think some part of him is fighting. Then the furrow smooths. "I live alone," he says pleasantly, and walks on.

I stand in the middle of the mill road shaking. Because I know his wife. Bess Osgood, who trades me mutton fat for carving wax, who once told me her husband hums when he shoes horses, tuneless as a dropped bucket. She exists. Somewhere in this valley she exists, waking or sleeping, and her own husband would walk past her in the road without a flicker.

Behind me, further up the mill road, the smudge is spreading. I can see it now—not with my eyes, exactly, but with the sense Edric calls the long sight, the cartographer's instinct lodged in the bone against my chest. Corvane walks, and behind him the valley goes soft. The forge. The well. The schoolhouse where children learned letters this morning and will learn nothing ever again if he reaches it.

"He's heading for the center," Edric says. "Market square. Smart. Memory pools in gathering places—a century of markets, weddings, funerals, arguments over the price of eels. He'll harvest faster there. Feed the Spire in bulk."

"Can he be stopped?"

"By you? With what, carver? Your chisels?" But there's no mockery in it. Only exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion, something that might be shame. "I helped build the tithing protocols. I keep telling you that, and you keep not running, and I don't know whether to admire you or mourn you."

"Do both," I say. "Everyone else does."

* * *

The market square is worse than the lanes, because the market square is where everyone is.

They've gathered the way people gather when something enormous happens—drawn out of their houses by the light on the horizon, which they can all see now, which none of them can name. A crowd stands in the square staring at the black needle of the Spire with expressions I recognize from funerals: the particular slack bewilderment of people confronting something their vocabulary wasn't built for.

And in their midst, moving through them like a shepherd through sheep, walks Warden Corvane.

Up close he hasn't changed. Same grey coat, same courteous half-smile, same terrible tidiness. Snow doesn't touch him. Neither, apparently, does guilt. He pauses before one person and then another, and each time he pauses, he *inclines his head*—a small, gracious bow, the gesture of a man asking permission—and each time, the person before him shivers and goes quiet, and something visible leaves them. Not visibly. That's the trick of it. To the eye, nothing happens at all. But I have spent my life learning to see what bones remember, and I can see the threads: pale filaments streaming out of open mouths and open eyes, streaming across the square, streaming up, streaming toward the needle on the horizon, which drinks them the way a root drinks rain.

The crowd is forgetting in real time, and the Spire is getting *stronger*, and the light on the horizon has begun to pulse in rhythm with the three remaining locks on the Door behind me.

Because that's the other thing. I can feel them. Locks five, six, seven, burning away in the drowned chapel, and every memory Corvane rips loose makes them flare brighter—as if the Door and the Spire are two ends of the same instrument, and he is tuning one string while I try to pluck another.

"Miss Thornbury."

I don't turn. Of course he found me. Of course he's polite about it.

"You're making a spectacle of yourself," I say.

"I'm making a *harvest*." He comes around to face me, and up close his eyes are the color of weathered slate, and in them, for the first time, I see something that isn't courtesy. Urgency. "Four locks, Miss Thornbury. Do you understand what you've done? Each one weakens the seal by a seventh, and the seal is not merely a lock on a door. It is the membrane between the valley and everything we buried. Open it fully and Osterhale returns—yes. Along with the famine years. The purges. The wardens' ledgers, in full, in every surviving memory. Every neighbor who informed. Every family that looked away." His voice drops, and here it is at last, the thing beneath the courtesy: fear. "You would give these people back their dead and their shame in the same breath."

"You say that like it's a price," I say. "It's just Tuesday, where I come from. I hear the dead, Warden. I've heard what people bury. Shame keeps. It's memory that rots when you cage it."

Something crosses his face—fast, quickly mastered, but I catch it. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own younger self argue.

"You were a witness once," I say quietly. "Before you were a warden."

"Give me the book," he says, as if I hadn't spoken. "Give me the femur. Go home, Miss Thornbury. Carve your commissions. In a month you won't remember any of this, and neither will anyone else, and you'll die comfortable in a valley full of small happinesses, and honestly—" his gaze drifts across the blank-faced crowd, the sleepwalking widow, the boy turning in circles, "—honestly, most of them would thank you."

"Most of them can't," I say. "That's rather my point."

Behind him, the smudge reaches the square's edge. The fountain goes. The clock tower's face goes blank—not broken, just *unnumbered*, a circle with nothing to say. And a woman near the fountain—a baker, I think, flour still on her forearms—suddenly sits down on the cobbles and begins to cry, though she cannot possibly know why, because whatever she lost was taken so smoothly she'll never feel the shape of the hole.

I feel it, though. Through the femur, through the gift that has been both my curse and my whole life, I feel every thread Corvane pulls. Names going out of mouths. Faces sliding off the walls of memory. The word for *bread* flickering like a guttering lamp in a hundred minds at once.

And somewhere in that flood, faint as a heartbeat, something small pushes back.

A tooth. A carved milk-tooth on a leather cord, worn by a boy who no longer exists, held by a mother who can no longer hold him. I felt it in chapter after chapter of this nightmare—no. I felt it in the night the Weaver family woke without their youngest son. I feel it now, a pinprick of resistance in the tide, love outlasting erasure by sheer stubbornness.

The boy is still fighting. Half-erased, unwritten from his own bed, and he is still *fighting*.

"Locks five and six," Edric murmurs against my ribs. "Marguerite. While there's still a valley left to save."

Corvane watches me decide. He must see it happen—I feel my own face change, grief refining itself down to something harder and cleaner. He sighs, and steps aside, actually *gesturing* me toward the ridge like a host showing a guest to the door.

"For what it's worth," he says, "I hope you fail kindly."

"That's the only way I know how to fail," I tell him, and run.

* * *

The climb back over the ridge-that-isn't-there has never taken this long. Behind me the valley dims street by street, and above me the Spire's light thickens, fed on breakfasts and birthdays and the word for bread, and ahead of me the drowned chapel waits with its three burning locks and its cold water and its arithmetic of loss.

Five, six, seven. Two memories I still have to choose, and one I don't yet understand.

"Edric," I say, breath ragged, boots slipping on ash-shaped ground, "when I give the fifth lock—the sound of your voice—you'll be silent to me. Forever?"

"Yes."

"And the sixth—the feeling of being believed. I barely know what that means."

"You will," he says softly. "You've known it since the night you dug me out of your floor. No one had heard me in a hundred years, carver. And you didn't just hear me. You *listened*." A pause, and when he continues, his voice has changed—lower, careful, the tone of a man approaching a door he's avoided for a very long time. "There's something you should know about the seventh lock. Before we get there. Before it costs you anything you haven't agreed to."

The chapel bell tower breaks the water ahead of us, black against the Spire-lit sky. Three locks burning. A valley going dark behind me. A dead man in my arms, about to confess the last of his secrets.

"Tell me on the way," I say, and dive.
