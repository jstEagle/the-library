## 15. Edric's Last Secret

The Spire stood on the horizon like a splinter the world had swallowed and only now thought to cough up.

I say *stood*, though I don't know that standing is the right word for a thing that had always been there and never once been seen. It was as if my whole life someone had been holding their hand in front of my eyes, and tonight they'd finally grown tired. The black needle of it caught no moonlight. It drank what light came near. And across the valley, behind me, I could hear — even from here, even through water and stone — the sound of Corvane working. A sound like pages being torn out of a very large book, one household at a time.

Edric said, "Don't look at it. Looking is how it gets your attention, and you can't afford to be interesting to it just yet."

"I'm already interesting to it," I said. "I opened a book."

"You opened a book a hundred years after anyone sensible would have burned it. You're not interesting, Marguerite Thornbury. You're *late*." There was no malice in it. That was the strange thing about Edric Vale's voice, lodged in his own femur in my two hands: he sounded like a man who had made peace with being dead and found the accommodations surprisingly roomy. "Now sit down before you fall down. The water's cold and you've been diving since midnight."

I sat on the chapel steps, which rose out of the flood like the ribs of some drowned animal, and held the bone against my chest where it was warmest. The Door Out glowed faintly behind me under the water — six locks open now, six memories lighter in me, and I will tell you honestly that I could no longer remember my mother's face when she sang, or why the smell of beeswax and bone dust had ever mattered to me, or what it had felt like, that first time, when a dead woman's grief had poured into my hands like water into a cup. I knew these things had happened. Knowing is not the same as having. I was becoming a house with the furniture carried out.

"Ask me," Edric said.

"Ask you what?"

"The question you've been chewing since the ridge. You think I can't hear you chewing? A century in the dark, girl, and I have excellent hearing." A pause, and then, gentler: "Ask me why I stayed dead."

The water lapped at the steps. Somewhere behind us, across the valley, another street went quiet — I felt it the way you feel a tooth come loose, a small wrongness in the general architecture of things.

"You were murdered," I said. "Corvane told me enough of it, in his polite way. You drew a map of the Door. They killed you for it. So why—" I turned the femur over in my hands. It was smooth as a river stone, warm as a living thing, and it had been waiting under my workshop floor for a hundred years, directly beneath the spot where I did my carving, directly beneath the feet of every bone-carver who ever worked in this valley, and none of them had heard him. None of them but me. "Why didn't you go on? Why stay? Dead men are supposed to move along. Every relic I've ever carved, they linger a season, maybe two, and then they thin out and go wherever the dead go. You've been here a hundred years, Edric. That's not lingering. That's *choosing*."

He was quiet long enough that I thought perhaps I'd offended him, or worse, that I'd guessed wrong and there was nothing underneath my guess but an ugly accident — a soul too stubborn or too frightened to leave its own bone.

Then he laughed. It was a small laugh, dry as chalk dust, and it hurt more than any wail would have.

"There it is," he said. "There's the carver. Everyone else who ever touched this bone felt a sad ghost and pitied me. You looked at a hundred years of patience and called it *suspicious*. Marguerite, I could weep, if I still had the equipment."

"Answer the question."

"I'm getting there. Dead men are slow; we take the scenic route." He settled, the way a voice settles, if such a thing can be imagined — a long exhale without lungs. "You know what the wardens took, when they folded Osterhale away. Memory. They traded ours for power, and the valley is the box they built to keep the witnesses in, because witnesses who forget aren't witnesses at all, they're just neighbors. But here's the part Corvane won't have told you, because Corvane has spent a century telling himself a prettier version: the folding wasn't clean. It never is. Magic leaves seams. And a seam, if you're patient, can be unpicked."

"The Door."

"The Door. Seven locks, each keyed to a memory freely given — because the wardens knew that stolen memory opens nothing. Power can take all day long, but it can't *give*, and so it can't unlock what was locked by giving. Only a free gift turns the key. I learned that from the wardens themselves, back when I was their cartographer and fool enough to be flattered by the company." His voice tightened, just once, like a knot pulled snug. "I mapped the Door. I gave them the design of their own prison, thinking I was mapping a vault. When I understood what I'd drawn, I hid the map where they'd never look — inside the one material in Osterhale that remembers louder than people do. Bone. Then I ran, and they caught me at the ford, and I died badly and quickly, and none of that is the secret. The secret is what I did next."

The Spire pulsed on the horizon. Or I dreamed it pulsed. With six memories gone I no longer trusted myself to know the difference between seeing and remembering seeing, which is, I suspect, exactly how the wardens prefer people.

"They expected me to move on," Edric said. "That's the natural order. The dead grieve, the dead drift, the dead go. And I'll be honest with you, Marguerite — I wanted to. Dying hurts, and what comes after dying hurts differently, and there was a whole country of the gone over there, all the folk the wardens had erased before the valley was ever built, and I might have been among them. Rest. Silence. An end to being afraid." A breath he didn't need. "But I stayed. Because I am a cartographer, and a cartographer knows something the wardens forgot: a map is useless until someone reads it. I could hide the door. I couldn't open it. Opening it needed the living — needed someone who could hear the dead, which is rarer than you'd think, because the wardens bred that gift out of the valley's bloodlines the way you'd breed a color out of pigeons. One in a generation, if the valley was lucky. Usually none."

"And you waited a hundred years for one."

"I waited a hundred years for *you*."

I want to set that sentence down carefully, the way you'd set down something breakable, because I have turned it over every night since and it has not gotten lighter. A hundred years. He lay under my floorboards through plagues and weddings and hard frosts and the slow turning-over of the bone-carver's trade from master to apprentice, through my grandmother and her grandmother before her, and he heard every one of them walk overhead, and none of them heard him back, and he stayed anyway. Not because he was sure. Because he hoped.

"Do you understand what patience costs?" he said, and his voice had gone soft in a way that made my throat close. "Every year, a little of me thinned. Ghosts are candles, girl, and I was burning at both ends on purpose — spending myself to stay loud enough, staying loud enough to spend myself. There were decades I couldn't manage more than a hum. Your grandmother's grandmother held me once, I think, some old bone she'd dug up for stock, and I screamed at her with everything I had left, and she felt a draft and put me back in the ground. Fair enough. A draft." The laugh again, smaller. "And then you came down the stairs one evening with a client's rib in your hand, humming while you worked, and you stopped mid-note because you heard me humming back, and I thought: *there*. A hundred years, and there she is. Late. But there."

I pressed the femur to my mouth. I don't know why. It seemed like the thing to do with a truth that size — hold it somewhere it could be felt rather than heard.

"Why tell me this now?" I managed. "We've six locks open. We've almost no time. What does your confession buy us?"

"Everything. It buys you the seventh lock." He let that sit. "Six memories given, six keys turned. But the seventh lock isn't like the others, Marguerite. The wardens designed it that way on purpose, because they feared it above all the rest. The first six locks take memory of the past — things done, things loved, things lost. Any witness can pay those. But the seventh lock asks for a memory of the future. A thing that hasn't happened yet, held so firmly and loved so completely that it weighs like a thing remembered. And the dead can't give it." His voice cracked along its length, chalk on slate. "Do you see? That's why I stayed. Not for the map — the map was half the work and the easy half. I stayed because I am dead, and the dead have no future, and the last key to Osterhale could only ever be carried by someone alive. Someone who could imagine tomorrow and mean it. I could wait a century. I could not wait *for* them. That part was always going to be yours."

The water moved against the steps. Behind me, under the flood, the Door waited with its single dark lock, patient as everything else in this drowned place.

"A memory of the future," I said. "That's not a memory. That's a hope."

"That's exactly what it is. Hope, given freely, with the full knowledge of what it costs — which is why the wardens feared it. Hope is the one thing power cannot counterfeit and cannot steal, because it only works if you hand it over yourself, eyes open. Corvane could harvest this valley down to the word for bread and it would buy him nothing at that last lock. He knows it. It's the only reason he's still talking instead of simply taking." A pause. "It's also why he'll get desperate soon. Desperate men with infinite power are the most dangerous weather there is."

I looked out across the black water toward the valley, toward the lights of Vell strung along its floor like embers in a grate, and I thought about how many of those lights had already gone out behind their windows — not the flames, the *people*, the names and faces and the small warm histories that make a house different from a shed. The Weaver boy. Sable, God help her, walking empty beside the man who emptied her. I thought about a future in which none of that had happened, and I found — this surprised me — that I could hold it. Whole. Heavy. Like a stone warmed in the hand.

"Edric," I said. "When I give the seventh memory — when I stand at that lock and hand over a tomorrow — what happens to the tomorrow I actually get? If I spend the future on the Door, what's left for me on the other side of it?"

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, it was the gentlest I had ever heard him, gentler than the hum that had started all of this, and it was worse than cruelty would have been.

"I don't know," he said. "I'm a dead man, Marguerite. Futures are outside my jurisdiction. I only know the shape of the choice, because I've had a hundred years to study its outline: the wardens traded memory for power and got both wrong. You can trade memory for the world. It's a better exchange rate. But it's still a trade, and nobody walks away from a trade with both hands full." A beat. "I won't pretend that's kind. I'll only say that I chose to stay dead a hundred years on the strength of a stranger I hadn't met yet, and I have never once regretted it, and if that's not a recommendation for betting on the unseen, I don't know what is."

I laughed. It came out wet and crooked, but it was a laugh, out here at the end of the world with the Spire drinking the sky.

"You realize," I said, "that if this works — if the Door opens and Osterhale comes back — you'll be restored too. All the erased souls return, Corvane said so. You'll get to be alive again. Or at least remembered, which might be better."

"Ah," said Edric Vale, and there was something in the single syllable that stopped my heart harder than any horror of the night had managed.

"*Ah* what?"

"Marguerite. Sweet, stubborn, late Marguerite." The warmth in his voice was unbearable. "What do you think happens to a ghost whose unfinished business finishes?"

The femur went cool in my hands. Not cold — bones don't lie about temperature — but cooling, the way a fire cools, banked and settling, a thing concluding.

"No," I said. "No, you don't get to— you waited a hundred years, you don't get to spend it all on my account and just—"

"Just what? Go quietly?" He was laughing again, and this time the laugh didn't hurt at all; it was the laugh of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. "Girl, I have been *waiting* to go quietly since 1823. Do you know what I'll do first, when the Door opens and the country comes home? I'm going to finish my map. All of it, every road, every orchard, every ford where fools get shot. And then I'm going to sleep, and it is going to be *magnificent*." The coolness deepened, gentle as snowfall. "So here is your last instruction from your cartographer, and you will follow it, because you're the only reader I've got: when the seventh lock opens, don't waste a moment grieving me. Grief is for things taken. Nothing has been taken from me tonight. Everything I kept — the map, the waiting, the faith — I'm spending it all, freely given, exactly as intended. Turn the key, Marguerite. Walk through. And if you ever pass the ford where I died, plant something. Anything. I always liked this valley's apples."

"Edric—"

"Listen." Sharp now, sudden, urgent — and beneath the urgency, faint and rising from across the valley, I heard what he heard: a sound like a great page tearing, closer than before, and the particular silence that follows a name going out of the world. "He's coming to the water. Corvane. He can feel the sixth lock's warmth; he knows what you're about to try; and he is out of patience and out of mercy, and he is bringing something with him that he thinks will stop you where no threat ever could." The bone shivered in my hands. "Whatever you see on that shore in the next few minutes — whatever he sets down in front of you, whatever he asks you to look at — remember what I told you. Hope is the one thing he cannot counterfeit. Hold your tomorrow. Don't let him hand you his."

The mist at the far edge of the flood began to move against the wind.

And it was moving in the shape of a man carrying something small and light, the way you carry a sleeping child.
