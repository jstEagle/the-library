## 16. The Emptying

*Marguerite*

I have spent my life listening to the dead, so you would think I'd know what forgetting sounds like. I didn't. It sounds like nothing at all — which is to say it sounds like a workshop on a winter evening, the fire ticking, the snow settling, everything perfectly, horribly ordinary.

The first thing to go was my mother's lullaby.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. I did not lose the memory of her singing it. I lost the tune itself. I could still see her hands folding linen, still smell the tallow of the candles she never trimmed properly, but when I reached for the melody — the little descending phrase she hummed while she worked, the one I had carried in my chest for thirty years like a stone in a boot — there was a hole where it had been. A lullaby-shaped hole. I sat down on my stool and put both hands flat on the workbench and felt the bones of the house through the wood, and even the dead in their relics were quiet, as if they too were holding their breath.

Then the fire went out of my memory of the fire. Then the word for the iron tool I use for fine work — I stood there holding it and could not tell you what it was called, only what it was *for*, which is a strange way to hold an object, like greeting a friend whose name has been sanded off your tongue.

Outside, someone was screaming. That was almost comforting. At least they remembered how.

* * *

*Tamsin Weaver, who has three children. Or two. Or—*

I woke before dawn because the house was wrong. You know how a house sounds with three children in it? Like weather that argues with itself. This house sounded like two children, and the silence where the third should be was louder than either of them.

I went into the little room at the end of the hall. There was a bed there. The blankets were thrown back. The mattress held the shape of a body, still faintly warm, the way a cup holds the shape of tea after the tea is gone.

I stood in that room for a long time and I could not tell you — I cannot tell you now — whose bed it was. But my body knew something my head refused. My arms ached. They ached the specific ache of arms that have carried a child up these stairs, night after night, a child heavy with sleep, a child who—

There's a tooth. On the shelf above the bed, wrapped in a scrap of blue cloth. A milk tooth, carved with a wren in flight, so small and fine it must have taken the bone-carver days. Why would I keep a tooth? Whose tooth? My girls are past that age. My son is—

My son is eleven and sleeps down the hall and snores like his father, God rot him.

So whose bed is warm?

I asked Bram. Bram looked at me the way you look at someone speaking a language you almost know. "Tamsin," he said gently, "we have two children." And then he looked at the warm bed, and I watched him look at it, and I watched him decide not to see it, and that was the worst thing I have ever watched anyone do, watching my husband choose the comfortable lie over the warm blanket.

Except he wasn't choosing. That's what I understand now, standing here writing this on the back of a seed invoice because paper is the only thing that holds still anymore. He wasn't choosing. Something was choosing *for* him, and it had already chosen me out of most of myself, and whatever's left of me that remembers a third child — remembers a boy, remembers a name starting with a sound like a bell — that part is going to write it all down before it goes too.

His name starts with a sound like a bell.

Hold on to that, Tamsin. Hold on like the gate latch in winter, the one you have to lift with your sleeve so your fingers don't stick.

The gate latch is unlatched. I remember latching it. I remember latching it every night of my life.

I don't remember why.

* * *

*Corvane*

People imagine harvest as violence. It isn't. Violence is crude; violence leaves bruises, and bruises are evidence, and we have never been permitted evidence. What I do is closer to threshing. You walk down the street and you let the Spire breathe through you, and the loose chaff of the valley — the names of distant cousins, the taste of last summer's plums, the particular grief of a widow on a Tuesday — lifts away from them so gently that most of them smile as it goes.

They smile. That is the part the histories will never record, if there are histories. Forgetting feels like relief. Memory is a weight, and I am, in the most literal sense available to me, unburdening them.

The baker has forgotten the word for bread. He stands in his shop surrounded by loaves and calls them "the morning things" and his customers nod, because they've forgotten too, and there is a terrible harmony in it, a valley-wide agreement that costs nothing because no one knows they're signing.

The schoolmistress has forgotten the alphabet's back half. She teaches the children letters A through M with tremendous conviction and gets a headache whenever she looks at the wall chart.

Old Hollis at the mill has forgotten his wife's face but kept her anger, which means he flinches at empty chairs. I confess I paused outside his window longer than efficiency required. Even now, even doing this, some residue in me objects. I have learned to treat that residue as weather. It passes.

What I do not say to the Spire, what I would not say aloud under torture, is this: the chaff comes to me. All of it. Every forgotten breakfast, every unlatched gate, every name like a bell, pours through me on its way to the black needle on the horizon that none of them can quite see, and I am meant to be a channel, a pipe, a thing without interior.

But I remember being a witness. That is my punishment for reading this far into my own confession: the harvest does not take from me, because I gave everything away long ago, freely, and what you give freely cannot be taken. So I stand in the street of a dying valley holding ten thousand stolen mornings, and the only person in the world who might understand is the bone-carver, whom I have been sent to stop.

Understand. Not forgive. I'm not a monster. I'm worse. I'm reasonable.

* * *

*Marguerite*

Sable came to the door at moonrise and I knew before I opened it, because the dead in my relics went silent in a ring around the house, like guests making room for someone important.

She stood on my step with snow settling on her shoulders and not melting, which should have told me everything, and she said, "Marguerite, love, I've come to help you pack."

"Pack."

"For a long journey. South, perhaps. Over the mountains. There are valleys south of here, I'm told, though I couldn't swear to any of them." She smiled, and it was her smile, sixty years of it, the smile that had raised me after my parents went into the ground — and behind the smile her eyes were doing arithmetic, counting what was left of me, deciding what could be spared.

"You're one of them," I said. "Or you were."

"I was an archivist." She said it the way other people say *I was young once*. "I copied pages in a tower you've never seen, in a country that doesn't exist, and I stole one page because a girl I loved was on the list to be forgotten, and the price of the theft was—" Her hand rose toward her own face and stopped halfway, hovering, the way yours does when you reach for a word. "Someone paid it. Someone always pays it. That's the whole architecture of the thing, Marguerite. Everything the Spire is built on was given by someone who loved too much to refuse."

"Sable—"

"Don't." The word cracked down the middle. "Don't say my name like that. Names are — oh, God, names are *expensive* right now." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. When she took it away, I swear her eyes had gone paler, like bread left out in rain. "Go home, child. Let me forget you cleanly. It's a mercy. I've had sixty years to learn how much of a mercy it is."

And because I am who I am, because I have spent thirty years taking dictation from the dead, I heard it — under her voice, under the warden's leash in her blood, the last unharvested room of her, the one even the Spire couldn't enter because she'd locked it herself. It was humming. My mother's lullaby. The tune the Spire had taken out of me was sitting in my aunt's chest like a bird in a covered cage.

They hadn't taken it from her. She'd *kept* it. All these years, the song I thought was mine alone, she'd been carrying it too, silently, the way you carry a sister's face you're not allowed to grieve.

"Sable," I said, and stepped forward, and put my hand flat on her sternum, the way I do with relics, the way I do with the dying. "When this is over — if there's an over — sing it to me. Promise me. Whatever's left of either of us."

She looked at my hand. She looked at me. And for one moment the archivist and the aunt and the thief and the collaborator were all one woman standing in the snow, and she said, "You stupid, brave girl. It's under the chapel. Go. Go *now*, before I'm made to mean what I came to say."

Then her face closed like a ledger, and she said, in a voice with frost on it: "Marguerite Thornbury. Come inside. Sit down. Forget with me. It doesn't hurt."

I ran.

* * *

*Edric Vale, cartographer, dead one hundred years, currently resident in his own femur*

She's running. Good. Bad. Both. Running is what I did, at the end, and look where it got me: a hole in the back, a shallow grave, and a century of talking to myself in the dark until a bone-carver finally picked me up and — miracle of miracles — picked me *up*. Do you know what that's like? To be heard after a hundred years? I wept marrow. I'm not ashamed.

Now she's running through the snow toward the ridge that isn't there, and the valley is coming apart behind her in waves, and I can feel each wave hit her through the bone. That's the cruelty of my position. I'm her compass and her passenger and I feel everything she loses as a kind of static, a radio station dying mid-song.

Her mother's face just went. Not the lullaby — that's gone already, poor girl — but the face. She stumbled on the frozen road and I felt her grab for her mother and find a shape with no features, a portrait with the paint scraped off, and she made a sound I hope never to hear again, except I will, because we're not done losing things.

Listen to me, girl. Listen, because soon you won't be able to afford to listen and I'll only be able to talk.

The Door takes memories freely given. Freely. That word is doing more work than a cartographer's mule. Corvane's people think it means *willingly*. It doesn't. It means *without being made to*. There's a difference the width of a life.

And the seventh lock — I'll tell her when she can still hear me, I swear it, I've waited a hundred years to tell someone — the seventh lock wants a memory of the future. Only the living have those. Only someone who believes in tomorrow enough to spend it.

I stayed dead on purpose. Write that on my stone, if stones survive. I stayed dead because a ghost can't give tomorrow away, and I wanted whoever found me to be someone who could.

Hurry, Marguerite. The waves are getting taller.

* * *

*The boy*

It's dark and it's not dark. It's like being a coat on a hook in a house where everyone's gone to market.

I have the tooth. The wren-tooth. Miss Thornbury carved it for me when my tooth came out and I cried about the blood and she said, "Everything that leaves a body leaves a door behind it," which I didn't understand then and understand completely now, because I am on the wrong side of a door, pushing on it with my whole self, and the tooth is the handle.

I can't remember my name but I remember that it starts like a bell. I can't remember my mother's face but I remember her hands, flour to the wrists, and the way she says my na— the way she says *it*, the bell-sound, across the yard at supper time.

Somebody's coming up the hill. Somebody crawling, mostly, on hands and knees through the snow, and the snow is full of holes where things used to be — I can see them, the absences, they look like burnt paper — and she's holding me. Holding the tooth. Holding the door-handle that is me.

Push, she's saying, or thinking, or the bone is saying it for her. Push, boy.

I push.

Somewhere below us a bell begins to ring, and it rings my name.

* * *

*Marguerite*

Here is the apocalypse, since you were wondering. It is not fire. It is not a beast. It is Mrs. Pellowe standing in her garden at midnight calling a cow that died years ago, because the living animals have slipped out of her mind and only the dead one remains, anchored by grief, too heavy to lift. It is the church clock striking hours nobody counts. It is a child crying because she can't remember the word for *mother* and has started calling hers "the warm one," and the woman scooping her up anyway, because arms remember what minds are made to lose.

It is me, on my knees on the ridge road, holding a femur in one hand and a carved milk tooth in the other, trying to remember my own name and finding it — finding it, barely, Marguerite, *Thornbury*, carver, hearer, stubborn — because I have written it so many times on commission slips that the ink of a thousand receipts is holding the shape of me the way a riverbed holds the shape of a dried river.

Half of me is gone. I can feel the edges of the missing parts, smooth as carved bone. But the tooth is warm in my fist, warmer than flesh has any right to be, and somewhere ahead, past the fold in the mist, water is moving around a drowned bell tower, and beneath that water stands an archway with four locks open and three to go.

Corvane is coming up the valley behind me, unhurried, harvesting, and beside him walks a straight-backed figure with snow-white eyes and my aunt's coat.

Get up, Marguerite.

The mist folds. I step through.

The Door is waiting, and it has already begun, very softly, to count what I have left.
