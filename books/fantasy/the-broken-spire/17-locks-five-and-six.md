## 17. Locks Five and Six

The world had gone soft at the edges, like a drawing left out in the rain.

I knelt before the Door Out in the drowned chapel's flooded nave, water to my waist, and I could no longer remember what my workshop smelled like. That was the third lock's doing. The first had taken my mother's lullaby—I still knew there had been one, the way you know there was a room behind a bricked-up door—and the second had taken the smell of bone dust and beeswax and lamp oil that had been the whole atmosphere of my life. The third had taken the first time I heard the dead, which meant it had also taken the moment I became myself, and I was carrying that absence around inside me like a stone where my heart used to sit.

And still I could see. Mostly. The erasure came in from the periphery, eating the world the way frost eats a window. My hands were clear. The Door was clear. Everything else was negotiable.

"Fourth lock," I said aloud, because saying things aloud made them realer, and realness was becoming a scarce commodity. "Sable."

She had come to stop me. She had arrived at the threshold with sixty years of silence in her mouth and her sister's face folded up inside her like a letter she'd never dared open, and instead of stopping me she had pressed that face against the fourth lock and let it go. I had watched it leave her—the way her expression didn't change so much as *empty*, the way a house empties when the family leaves for good and takes even the curtains. She had smiled at me afterward. It had been the bravest ugly smile I'd ever seen.

Then Corvane had come, and the mist had moved, and when it settled again Sable was gone from beside me.

I told myself she had walked back across the ridge. I told myself this the way you tell yourself the dog went to live on a farm.

"Five," said Edric's voice from the femur in my hands, gentle as ever, as if we were discussing weather rather than the dismantling of my soul. "Whenever you're ready. No rush. Well. Some rush."

"You're very calm for a dead man."

"I've had a century to practice." A pause, the particular quality of pause he did when he was choosing his words with a cartographer's precision. "Marguerite. The fifth lock wants a sound. Not any sound—a memory of one. And I know what it will ask for, because I designed the ruddy thing, and I'm sorry in advance."

"What does it want?"

"It wants me."

I looked down at the femur. Water dripped off my chin. Somewhere above us, through the chapel's broken roof, the Spire stood on the horizon like a needle someone had pushed into the world's eye, and every few minutes I felt it *pull*—a sensation like a fishhook behind my sternum, hauling another name out of the valley. The Weaver boy's tooth hung at my throat on its cord, warm as a held hand. He was still pushing back. I could feel him faintly, the way you feel sun through a wall: not there, but not nothing.

"If I give up your voice," I said, "I won't be able to hear you anymore. Will I?"

"No."

"And after the sixth—"

"The sixth is worse," he said simply. "But we'll get there."

*We.* A dead man and a half-erased carver, standing in a flooded chapel at the bottom of a country that officially did not exist, and he said *we* like it was the most natural word in the world. I had spent my whole life hearing the dead and being heard by no one. Now I was about to lose the hearing, and the being-heard both, in exchange for a door.

Some bargains only look terrible until you hold them next to the alternative.

I raised the femur to my lips. It was absurd, kissing a bone, and I did it anyway, because if the last thing I kept of him was going to be a gesture, I wanted it to be an honest one.

"Goodbye, Edric Vale," I said. "Cartographer. Murdered man. Worst houseguest in history, living in a leg for a hundred years."

"Best landlady I never paid," he said, and his voice cracked on it—not broke, *cracked*, like ice over deep water—and then the fifth lock flared white and took him.

Not the femur. The femur stayed in my hands, solid, inert, just bone now, the way all bones are to everyone else. What it took was the sound of him. I opened my mouth to say something and discovered I no longer knew what his voice sounded like. I knew he had spoken. I knew I had relied on it the way you rely on a north star. But the voice itself was gone, scooped out, and in its place was a silence shaped exactly like a man.

I stood in the flooded nave and shook, and the shaking was not grief exactly. Grief would come later, presumably, if there was a later with room in it. This was more structural than that. This was a load-bearing wall coming out of me and the ceiling holding anyway, barely, out of spite.

One lock left. One.

I put the silent femur in my coat, against my chest, where the tooth already hung. Two relics of two men who had given everything they had to a stranger. I was becoming a reliquary of other people's courage, and I hadn't yet found any of my own.

* * *

The sixth lock was different from the others. The others had been specific—a lullaby, a smell, a face. The sixth was a feeling, and when I laid my palm against the cold bone-white arch, it showed me what it wanted, and I laughed.

It wanted the feeling of being believed.

Of course it did. Of course. The wardens had built their whole empire on the opposite—the enforced disbelief of an entire valley, a hundred years of neighbors cheerfully insisting Osterhale had never been, records ending mid-sentence, footprints refusing to form in snow. The Door demanded its exact inverse. It wanted the memory of the one time in my life someone had heard me say something impossible and simply—believed it.

That had happened exactly once.

It had been Aunt Sable, three winters ago, when I'd told her—carefully, casually, testing the ice—that the rib-bone commission had sung to me, an actual song, and wasn't that strange. She had set down her tea. She had looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that were older than they should have been, and she had said, "No, it isn't strange. It's the truest thing in this valley," and she had poured me a cup too, and we had drunk our tea like two women who had merely discussed the price of wool.

That was the memory. That was the lock. And giving it up meant giving up the knowledge—not the fact, but the *felt knowledge*—that belief was possible. That someone, once, had looked at the strangest thing about me and called it true.

"I notice," I said to the empty chapel, to the silent femur, to whatever remained of Edric that could still listen without ears, "that this door is designed to take away every reason a person has to keep going. Lullaby. Home. Purpose. Friendship. Voice. Faith in others. Whoever built this either hated people or understood them completely."

Silence, shaped like a man, said nothing.

"And I notice," I went on, wading closer to the arch, "that I'm going to do it anyway, and that the version of me who does it will be someone I wouldn't recognize, standing in a place I can't quite picture, for reasons I'll have to take entirely on faith." I laughed again, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "Which is fitting. Faith is the last thing I'll have left. Might as well spend it here."

I thought of the Weaver boy. Tam—that was his name, Tam Weaver, eight years old, gap-toothed, the kind of child who gave away parts of himself to near-strangers because he liked them and saw no reason to complicate the matter. His bed had been warm. His mother had set four plates and wept without knowing why. And Marguerite Thornbury alone remembered him, because a tooth carved by my own hands was a harder thing to erase than a memory.

If a boy could push back against oblivion with a single carved tooth, then I could push back with six empty sockets where my treasures used to be.

I gave the sixth lock the feeling of being believed.

It went out of me like warmth leaving a bath. I felt the shape of it go—the tea, the winter light, Sable's steady eyes, the words *it's the truest thing in this valley*—and then I felt only that something had been there, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. I knew I had once trusted someone completely. I could no longer locate the trust itself. It was like being handed a map of a country that had since burned, the paper intact, the territory gone.

The sixth seal split down its length with a sound like a sigh of relief.

Six of seven. The archway glowed now along its whole height, hairline cracks of light running through the bone-white stone, and beyond the seals I could sense—faintly, the way you sense weather coming—the heart of it. The heart-bone. The Spire's stolen centuries, beating behind the last lock like a caged thing.

One lock. One memory of the future, Edric had said, before his voice was taken from me. A thing only the living can give.

I turned to say something to him—to ask him, to thank him, to complain, anything—and remembered, freshly, horribly, that I couldn't. The femur lay against my chest, mute as any other bone. I had carried a companion across a ridge that didn't exist and into a drowned chapel, and somewhere between the fifth lock and the sixth I had lost the ability to know what his company had felt like.

I was so tired. That was the strange mercy of it. Underneath all the loss there was a plain animal exhaustion, and it kept the screaming part of me quiet.

That was when the water started moving against me.

* * *

He came across the nave walking on the flooded floor as though it were flagstones, and I suppose for him it might as well have been. Warden Corvane. Courteous as ever. His boots touched the water and the water declined to ripple, which was somehow worse than if it had caught fire.

Behind him, floating—no. Not floating. Being *carried*, in the way a puppet is carried, by strings I couldn't see, was Aunt Sable.

She walked when he walked. She stopped when he stopped. Her eyes were open and they were looking at me and there was nothing in them looking back. Sixty years of stubborn love, of burnt books and warnings and tea in front of the fire, and what remained was a woman-shaped vessel with Sable's face on it, moving when the wind of Corvane's will moved.

"There," Corvane said, and his voice was as pleasant as it had been in my workshop, appraising antiques, asking precise questions. "You see? No harm has come to her. I don't harm witnesses, Miss Thornbury. I *empty* them. It's cleaner. She feels nothing. In a sense, I've done her a kindness—she spent six decades afraid, and now she isn't anything at all."

Every plan I had—every scrap of strategy, every careful sequence of sacrifices Edric and I had mapped out in the dark—went out of me at once, and what was left underneath was simpler and much older.

Rage.

But it was a strange rage, refined, clarified, like metal heated past all impurity. I had expected to feel despair. Instead I felt *lucid*. Perhaps that was the erasure's gift: with the lullaby gone, the workshop gone, the friendship gone, the voice gone, the faith gone, there was very little left in me to confuse the issue. What remained was the fact of Sable walking on invisible strings, and the fact of a boy named Tam erased from his own warm bed, and the fact of a hundred years of theft dressed up as mercy, and the fact of the seventh lock waiting behind me, hungry for a future.

Grief without illusion. That was what I had. Grief with the fog burned off it, standing in waist-deep water, holding nothing but a tooth and a silent bone.

"Here is my offer," Corvane said, spreading his hands, and Sable's hands spread too, a beat behind his, and something in me went very quiet and very hard. "Walk away. Now, while six locks stand open and the Door is still sealed. The valley keeps its small happinesses. Your aunt keeps—well, she keeps nothing, that's spent, I regret it, but the rest of them keep theirs. The Weavers keep their three children and their inexplicable fourth plate. You keep your life, such as it is. Open the Door, and yes, every erased soul returns—but so do the crimes. Every complicity. Every neighbor who chose not to ask. The valley will remember what it agreed to forget, Miss Thornbury, and it will hate you for making it remember. Mercy is letting sleeping countries lie."

I looked at Sable's empty eyes.

And here is the thing I understood, standing there, that I could not have understood a week ago: he thought he was describing a cost. He thought the return of pain was the argument-ender. But I had watched a woman give her sister's face to a lock and call it the first honest thing she'd done in sixty years. I had listened to a murdered man stay dead on purpose for a century because he believed a stranger would hear him. I had held a tooth that a forgotten boy was using, right now, to hold the line against nothingness.

These people had already told me what they valued. They had paid for it in advance. Who was I to decide their suffering was too dear?

"You're right," I said.

Corvane blinked. It was the first unpolished thing I'd ever seen him do.

"The valley will remember its complicity," I said. "It will remember the neighbors who asked nothing, the records that ended mid-sentence, the households that slept through the thefts. It will hurt. Some of them will hate me." I waded forward, one step, water dragging at my coat, the tooth warm at my throat, the silent bone against my heart. "And some of them will be like your aunt was—" I stopped. Corrected myself. Like *my* aunt was. Names were getting slippery; the Spire's pull was reaching even in here. "Like she was. Glad. Glad to hurt, if hurting meant being real."

"She is nothing now," Corvane said softly. "And she will stay nothing unless you walk away. That is the arithmetic, Miss Thornbury. Her emptiness is on your ledger."

"No," I said. "Her emptiness is on yours. I'm just the one keeping count."

Something moved behind his courtesy then—something old and tired and, God help me, *familiar*. For half a heartbeat he looked less like a warden and more like what Edric had sworn the wardens all were: witnesses, once, who had chosen power instead of truth, and had spent centuries pretending the choice had been free.

Then it closed over, smooth as still water, and he gestured, and Sable turned her emptied face fully toward me, and he said:

"Last chance. Shall I show you what she looks like when I finish the job?"

Behind me, the seventh lock began, very quietly, to hum.

It was not Edric's voice. Nothing was Edric's voice anymore. But it was a sound, rising out of the final seal, thin and patient and waiting—and I realized, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that the lock wasn't humming at me.

It was listening.
