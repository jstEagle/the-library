## 18. The Seventh Lock

Corvane came the way winter comes—not all at once, but with a pressure you feel in your teeth before you understand why.

I was kneeling at the Door Out with Sable's emptied body cradled against my shoulder, and I want to be honest about what that felt like: it felt like holding a coat that had once been a person. Warm at the edges. Nothing behind the warmth. Sixty years of sharp tongue and burnt toast and secrets kept like coins under a mattress, and now she weighed nothing more than wool.

"You've made rather a mess of things," Corvane said.

He stood at the water's edge where the drowned chapel's steps descended into black glass. The mist did not touch him. I noticed that the way you notice a man walking through rain without getting wet—first with confusion, then with a cold slide down the spine. Behind him, across the valley, the Spire stood on the horizon like a needle someone had threaded through the world's eye, and streets were going blank behind him as he spoke. I could feel it happening. Little tugs. Somewhere a woman was forgetting her own wedding. Somewhere a gate would stand unlatched tonight because no one remembered why gates mattered.

"Give me her," he said, nodding at Sable. "And walk away. That's not a threat, Miss Thornbury. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I said. My voice came out scraped. I'd given five locks my best memories by then—the lullaby, the workshop smell, the first time I heard the dead, Edric's voice, the feeling of being believed—and what remained of me felt like a house after thieves: walls still standing, rooms echoing wrong. "You harvest people's grandmothers and call it arithmetic."

"I call it mercy." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse. Heat would have meant he thought he needed to convince me. "Look at what they had before us. Plague in the low towns. A war every generation, because Osterhale sat on every map and every map is an invitation. We gave them a valley where nothing follows them. No magic. No history heavy enough to crush. They are *happy*, Marguerite. Small and happy and safe. You're proposing to hand them back their griefs along with their names."

"And you're proposing to take the rest of them one household at a time until there's nothing left to protect." I shifted Sable's weight. The boy's tooth was in my pocket—I'd carried it since the Weaver boy vanished from his family's memory, a little crescent of bone I'd carved for him when he was six and losing his milk teeth and terrified the tooth-fairy wouldn't find a valley that wasn't on maps. *She'll find me,* he'd said. *You'll make sure.* Children decide things like that about you, and then you spend years trying to deserve it.

"The seventh lock," Corvane said, and his composure flickered—just at the corners, just for a moment. "It doesn't open on memories of the past. Edric never understood that. He spent his last decade trying to remember something worth surrendering, and everything he had was already given or already taken. The seventh lock wants a memory of the future. A thing that hasn't happened. Only the living can give it, and only—" his eyes moved over me, cataloguing, the way he'd catalogued my workshop that first polite evening, "—only someone who still believes in one."

"So you can't open it."

"No one can. That was the design. The wardens built a door even we couldn't walk through twice." He smiled, thin as paper. "Which is why I'm offering instead of taking. Walk away. Keep your gift—you've noticed, haven't you, that the erasure slides off you? It always has. Your hearing is cut from the same cloth we are. You could sit in your workshop another forty years, carving dead women's ribs, listening to their songs. The valley keeps its small happinesses. The boy stays... wherever he is, which is nowhere, which is painless. Everyone keeps exactly as much as they can bear to lose."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I finish the harvest tonight, and the Door seals itself for another century, and some other carver finds some other femur in a hundred years and starts this all again." He spread his hands. "I am very patient, Miss Thornbury. Patience is the first thing power teaches you."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of the world: it leaves you time to think.

The lock waited above me, the seventh of seven, set into the archway's crown like a pupil in a bone-white eye. The other six hung open around it, dark little doorways where my mother's lullaby used to live, where the smell of beeswax and bone dust used to live, where being believed used to live. I could feel the absences the way you feel a missing tooth—tongue going to the gap again and again, finding nothing, flinching.

Edric's femur lay across my knees. He'd been quiet since the water. Not gone—I could tell the difference now; silence-with-him-in-it has a texture, like a room where someone is sleeping—but quiet, letting me think, which was its own kind of kindness. Or its own kind of fear. Dead men who stay dead on purpose for a hundred years don't do it because they're certain.

"Edric," I said, under my breath. "If I give it a future and I'm wrong—if the valley remembers and hates what it was—"

*Then it will hate honestly,* he said, and his voice in the marrow-canal was tired and warm and closer than it had ever been. *Marguerite. Girl. Carver. I drew maps for a country that trusted them with its life. Do you know what I learned? People can survive almost any truth. What kills them is the shape of a lie worn smooth by handling.*

"That's very poetic for a man who died face-down in a ditch."

*I died face-down in a ditch because I was running toward a door with a warning in my teeth. Poetry was all I had time for.* A pause. *You're stalling.*

"I'm composing," I said. "There's a difference. Composing has dignity."

Corvane watched me murmur to a dead man's leg bone and made no move to stop me. That told me something too. He wanted me to try. Some part of him—a part buried so deep even he'd stopped visiting it—wanted the seventh lock to open, because if it opened for me, then maybe it hadn't been madness when he'd stood where I stood, a century and more ago, a witness with a choice, and chosen wrong. If no one could ever open it, his choice was just weather. If I opened it, it became a decision. And decisions can be regretted.

I stood up. My legs shook. Five memories gone will do that; I was learning what I was made of by subtracting from it, and apparently the answer was: less than I hoped, more than I feared.

"Corvane," I said. "Before I give this—answer me one thing. Honestly. You were a witness once. Before the folding. Before the Spire."

Something moved behind his face. Not softening. More like ice shifting on a pond—the sound of a depth becoming briefly visible.

"Yes," he said.

"And you chose power. To keep the crimes from catching up with everyone. To keep the peace. To keep them *safe*."

"They were dying, Marguerite. All of them. Slowly, stupidly, in ditches and birthing beds and border skirmishes. We were offered a way to fold the suffering out of the world, and we took it, and yes—we folded ourselves out with it, because you cannot hold a door shut from inside the room." His voice stayed level, but level the way a blade is level. "Every warden began as someone who could not bear what the world was doing to the people they loved. Remember that when you judge us."

"Oh, I intend to," I said. "Because here's my memory of the future. And it includes you."

* * *

I put my hand on the seventh lock.

It was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature—the cold of a held breath, of a question waiting. The bone-white arch hummed against my palm, and through the humming I felt the six absences I'd paid, circling me like fish that had learned my shape.

A memory of the future. A thing that hasn't happened, believed hard enough to weigh.

So I closed my eyes, and I built it.

Not a vision. I'm a carver, not a prophet. I built it the way I build anything: piece by piece, grain by grain, trusting the material.

I built a morning. Ordinary. Frost on the workshop window, because valleys have frost whether or not they're prisons. I built the sound of the Weavers' door opening and a boy's voice calling out—his name, his actual name, Tam, Tam Weaver, eight years old and furious about porridge—and his mother dropping whatever she was holding because her arms knew him before her mind caught up, and both of them crying into each other's shoulders over nothing, over everything, over a bed that had been warm and a name that had been gone.

I built Sable at the border of the restored country, older than old, standing in a road that had grown back overnight like grass through rubble, and a woman coming down that road with Sable's sister's face, sixty years unaged, saying *there you are, I've been looking everywhere for you*—because that's what sisters say, that's the first thing sisters say, and somewhere in the folding-back of the world that sentence was going to be true again.

I built maps redrawing themselves in ink shops and schoolrooms across three kingdoms, cartographers swearing and reaching for fresh parchment, and children learning a new-old name: Osterhale. Saying it clumsily at first. Getting it right.

I built a valley that remembered. That was the heart of it, the load-bearing wall: a valley that remembered its complicity and its courage, the neighbors who looked away and the ones who didn't, the wardens' crimes and the wardens' humanity, all of it, held together in one pair of hands like a bone that's been broken and set crooked but *set*, walking weight again. I built grief with a floor under it. I built truth without anesthesia.

And I built this: Corvane, at the water's edge, in the moment after the Door opens, when the stolen centuries come home to roost and the Spire's heart lies bare before him—he looks at it, and he falters. Because he remembers being a witness. Because the future I'm giving the lock includes a version of him that gets to choose again.

"There," I said, opening my eyes. My face was wet. I hadn't authorized that. "That's the memory. Take it. It's freely given. Every word of it I believe."

The lock drank it.

I felt the future leave me the way I'd felt the lullaby go—not torn, but *poured*, willingly, and the strangest grief of all was that I could still see the morning I'd built, could still hear Tam shouting about porridge, but it no longer belonged to me. It belonged to the door now. A prophecy, I supposed, was just a memory someone else was keeping for you.

The seventh lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking in a cathedral.

Light came through the archway—not bright, not blinding, just *true*, the light of a place that had been waiting a hundred years to be entered—and beyond the Door I saw stone steps descending, and at the bottom of the steps, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something vast and slow and alive was pulsing.

The heart-bone.

But I didn't move yet. Because Corvane—

Corvane had not moved either. He stood at the water's edge with his courteous face finally, finally broken open, and what was underneath wasn't rage. It was worse. It was recognition. He was looking at the open lock the way a starving man looks through a bakery window at bread he once knew how to bake.

"You included me," he said. His voice had lost its arithmetic. "In the future. You gave the door a future with *me in it*, choosing differently."

"You said every warden began as someone who couldn't bear the world hurting the people they loved." I picked up Edric's femur and held it against my chest like a shield, like a child, like a compass. "I'm choosing to believe that person still exists in there somewhere. That's the whole memory. That's the entire bet."

For a long moment the only sound was the water lapping the drowned chapel's steps and, far off across the valley, the Spire singing its thin hungry note as it pulled the word for *bread* out of someone's mouth.

Then Corvane took one step forward—and it was not a step toward stopping me. It was the step of a man who has been standing still for so long that movement itself is a kind of falling.

"The heart-bone," he said hoarsely, "will offer you a trade. It always does. It knows what you are. Go carefully, carver. It lies the way we lie—with perfect sincerity and excellent reasons."

"Noted," I said.

And I turned toward the Door Out, toward the steps, toward the pulsing dark where a century of stolen memory beat inside a single bone—while behind me, for the first time in longer than he could probably count, Warden Corvane followed a witness instead of hunting one.
