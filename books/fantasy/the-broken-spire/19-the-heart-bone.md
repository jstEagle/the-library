## 19. The Heart-Bone

The Door Out was a lie of a name. It didn't lead out. It led *in*—down, through the archway's bone-white frame, into a stair that spiraled beneath the drowned chapel like the inside of an ear. Which felt appropriate. My whole life had been spent listening to things other people couldn't hear. I suppose it was only fitting that the world's greatest secret lived in a place shaped like listening.

I went down with Edric's femur cradled against my chest and six memories lighter than I'd been the night before.

That's not poetry. I mean it literally. There were holes in me now, clean-edged as drill-work, where my mother's lullaby used to live, where the smell of my own workshop used to live, where the sound of Edric's voice—the voice that had carried me across a ridge that wasn't there—used to live. I kept reaching for them the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. The workshop one was worst. I knew I had a workshop. I knew I loved it. But when I tried to picture the light coming through the window at dusk, or the particular sweet-dust smell of boiling bone, there was nothing. Just a shape where a feeling should be, like a glove still holding the memory of a hand.

"You're doing it again," I said to the femur, because talking kept me sane and there was no one left to judge me for it. "You're being quiet on purpose."

Edric couldn't answer anymore. Lock five had taken his voice out of my keeping—I'd given up the sound of him, the dry cartographer's rasp of him, the way he said *Marguerite* like it was a place he'd mapped and found worth the trouble. What remained of him now was weight and warmth and the faint pressure of presence, the way you can feel someone standing behind you without hearing them breathe.

I missed him. That was the joke of it. I'd known the man for nine days and mourned his voice in four hours.

The stair ended.

I want to describe what I saw at the bottom, and I find I can't do it honestly without first admitting what I expected: some vault, some treasury, something grand. Wardens who traded memory for power would keep their power somewhere impressive, surely. Somewhere with columns.

It was a room the size of a chapel crypt, low-ceilinged, dry as a pocket, and lit by nothing—which is to say it was lit by everything, faintly, the way snow glows at dusk. And in the center, floating a hand's width above a plinth of black stone, hung a bone.

Not a dramatic bone. Not a femur or a skull or anything with pretensions. It was small—a knuckle-bone, maybe, or a child's finger-bone—no longer than my thumb, pale as milk, turning slowly end over end in the air. And around it, so dense they blurred together, were threads. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Fine as spider silk and bright as the moment before tears, they ran from the little bone outward in every direction, up through the ceiling, through the water above, through the earth, fanning out across the valley like roots seen from underneath.

Every thread ended in a person. I understood that without knowing how I understood it. Every thread was a memory. A stolen century of them, harvested household by household, name by name, breakfast by breakfast, all of it wound around one small white bone like thread around a spool.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small in the dry dark. "Oh, you *bastards.*"

Because here was the thing—the thing that made my hands shake and my eyes sting and my throat close up with something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite rage but was some bastard hybrid of the two, bred in captivity:

I recognized the magic.

I had grown up with it humming under my skin. When I carved a dead woman's rib and heard her hum a tune, that was this. When the tooth of a forgotten boy stayed warm in my pocket while the whole world forgot him, that was this. The gift that made me *me*—the hearing, the carrying, the terrible hospitality of letting the dead lodge in your work—it wasn't a separate thing from the Spire. It was the same current. The wardens hadn't invented their theft; they'd industrialized it. They'd taken what people like me did naturally, gently, one relic at a time—and built a machine to do it wholesale.

My gift was born of the same magic as the heart-bone. I had known that, in the way you know a thing you refuse to look at directly. Now, standing in its presence, I could not refuse to look.

And if the heart-bone shattered—

If Osterhale unfolded back into the world, if every stolen century came home, if the valley remembered and the boy got his name back and Sable's sister waited at the border—

Then the magic that fed my hearing would shatter too. You cannot break the well and keep drinking. You cannot unmake the loom and expect the cloth to hold.

The dead would fall silent in my hands. Forever. Every commission, every keepsake, every grieving widow who brought me her husband's knuckle and asked *is he at peace*—I would carve, and the bone would say nothing back, and I would have to hand it over and say *I don't know, I'll never know again,* and watch their faces do the arithmetic of disappointment.

I sat down on the cold floor. It seemed the thing to do.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about a choice like this: it doesn't arrive like thunder. It arrives like bookkeeping.

I sat against the wall with the femur in my lap and did sums. On one side: my gift. Fifty-odd years of it. The first time I ever heard the dead—I'd given that memory to lock three, so I only knew the fact of it now, the way you know your own birthday without remembering being born—but I knew what it had made of me. It had made me useful. It had made me strange. It had made me the person the valley came to when the living ran out of answers, and I will not pretend I didn't love that. I loved being needed in a way no one else could be needed. I loved the dead. God help me, I loved the dead better than the living most days, because the dead never lied to me and never left and always, always said thank you.

On the other side: Tam.

The Weaver boy. The one whose bed was still warm, whose mother had walked past his empty chair for three weeks without seeing it, whose name I was the last creature on earth to carry. He existed now in the space between memory and erasure, holding onto a carved tooth I'd made him, pushing against the forgetting with everything a seven-year-old has, which is more than you'd think.

If I kept my gift, Tam stayed gone. Not dead—gone. Unremembered even by his own warm bed. And not just Tam. Everyone the wardens had eaten, going back a hundred years. Whole generations folded flat. Sable's sister. Edric's Osterhale. All of it, held hostage by my attachment to my own reflection.

"Well," I said to the empty room. "When you put it like that."

The room said nothing. Rooms are honest that way.

But then—because I am who I am, because I have never once in my life let a silence go unnegotiated—I held the femur up to the glow of the threads and spoke to the man inside it.

"Edric. If you can hear me—and I know you can't answer, and I know that's my own fault, I gave away the sound of you like a fool gives away her coat in December—but if any scrap of you is still in there listening: tell me if I'm wrong. Tell me there's another way. Tell me the gift survives, tell me there's a version of this where I get to keep the hearing and the world gets to keep its dead. Because I have looked, old man. I have turned it over every way it turns. And there isn't. Is there."

The femur lay warm and mute in my palms.

And in the warmth and the muteness I heard—not his voice, I'd spent that, but the shape of what he'd say, the shape a century of patience leaves in a bone—he'd already answered. He'd answered a hundred years ago, staying dead on purpose, lodging himself in the one place a listener would eventually dig. He had given up everything—his name, his life, his voice, his map hidden in marrow—to make sure someone like me would find the Door. He hadn't done that so I could stand at the bottom of it and haggle.

He'd done it because some doors are only opened by people willing to walk through changed.

"You absolute menace," I told him, and my voice cracked on it, and I laughed, which cracked worse, until I was sitting on the floor of the world's worst crypt doing both at once like a woman possessed, which, fair.

* * *

I climbed the plinth. The threads parted around me as I reached for the little bone—they were warm, I noted distantly; a hundred years of stolen breakfasts and lullabies and first kisses, and the whole harvest was *warm*—and I closed my hand around the heart-bone of the Spire.

It fit in my palm like it had been waiting for exactly that hand. Maybe it had. Prophecy books have opinions about these things.

Up close I could feel it thinking. That's the only word. It hummed against my skin the way every relic I'd ever carved had hummed, except scaled up past sense—a cathedral chord where my relics played single notes. And threaded through the chord, faint but unmistakable, I caught voices. Thousands. The stored dead. The stored *lived*. A woman laughing at something I'd never hear. A man saying *pass the bread* in a language the valley had already forgotten the word for. Children. So many children.

They weren't suffering, precisely. The wardens hadn't been cruel in their storage; cruelty wastes product. They were just—held. Paused mid-note, mid-laugh, mid-breakfast, for a hundred years, so that a handful of men could stay powerful and a valley could stay quiet and I could sit comfortable in my workshop hearing ghosts and calling it a gift.

Calling it a gift.

I thought about Corvane, standing at the water's edge with his courteous questions and his footprintless feet, offering me mercy like it was a discount. *Walk away, and the valley keeps its small happinesses.* He'd meant it kindly, insofar as his kind could mean anything kindly. He'd genuinely believed a cage full of pleasant lies beat a world full of hard truths. And maybe for him it did. Maybe that was the whole disease, right there—the moment you decide other people are better off not knowing, you've already picked up the knife.

I had spent my whole life on the other side of that knife. The dead trusted me with their truths because I never once decided for them what they could bear.

So.

I stood in the dry dark with the heart-bone in my fist and the threads singing around me, and I gave the choice its final, honest shape:

Keep the hearing. Keep being the woman the bones spoke to, the strange necessary Marguerite Thornbury, keeper of the valley's ghosts—and leave Tam erased, and Osterhale folded, and a hundred years of stolen lives spinning on a spool in a cellar forever.

Or break it. Break the well. Lose the dead, lose the listening, lose the one thing that had ever made me feel like my strangeness was worth the space it took up—and give every stolen soul back to the world that forgot them.

There was no third option. I checked. Believe me, I checked.

"I'm going to need both hands," I said to the femur, and set him gently on the plinth, face-up, so that whatever was left of Edric Vale could watch.

The heart-bone pulsed in my grip, warm as a sleeping bird, and somewhere far above—through the water, through the ridge that wasn't there, across the valley where streets were going blank behind a walking man—I felt the Spire notice me. Felt it gather itself. Felt Corvane turn from his harvesting and *look*, the way you feel a storm change its mind about you.

He was coming. I had minutes, maybe less, and Sable's emptied body beside him for leverage, and six locks' worth of hollows in my chest where my best things used to live.

I raised the heart-bone over my head.

And stopped—one breath, just one—because the last thing I wanted to hear before the silence of the rest of my life was anything at all.

The threads sang. The stored dead murmured. The bone in my fist beat like a second heart learning my rhythm.

"Right," I said, to Edric, to Tam, to the boy's tooth in my pocket, to every ghost I'd ever hosted and every ghost I never would again. "Thank you for everything. Now hold on to something."

I brought it down.
