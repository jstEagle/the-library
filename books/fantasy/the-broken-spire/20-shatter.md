## 20. Shatter

The heart-bone was smaller than I expected.

That's the thing nobody tells you about the objects that hold up worlds: they're never grand. The Spire itself was a black needle a mile high, visible now from every window in the valley, humming like a struck tuning fork that refused to finish its note. But the bone at its center—the relic that held a century of stolen memory, the engine of all that polite erasure—fit in my two hands. It was the size of a knucklebone. It was warm. It was *listening*.

I had spent my whole life listening to bones. I suppose I shouldn't have been surprised that this one listened back.

"Put it down," Corvane said.

He stood at the threshold of the Door Out, and behind him the drowned chapel's water was rising—not flooding, exactly, but *climbing*, as if the valley's grief had gotten into it. Sable lay on the stones where he'd set her, emptied out, her face smooth as an unscribed page. I looked at her once. Just once. If I looked longer I would put the bone down, and then everything she'd given—her sister's face, sixty years of silence, the last of herself poured into lock four—would have been for nothing.

"No," I said.

"Marguerite." He said my name gently, the way you'd speak to a spooked horse. "You don't understand what you're holding. That isn't a weapon. It's a *ledger*. Every memory we took is written in it. Shatter it and the ledger doesn't burn—it *pays out*. All of it. Every crime, every complicity, every neighbor who looked away while their street went blank. You'll give this valley back its dead, yes. And its shame. Its guilt. A hundred years of knowing what they did to keep their small happinesses." He spread his hands. "I'm offering mercy. Not for me. For *them*."

"You keep using that word," I said. "Mercy. You've been using it since your footprints didn't show up in the snow."

"And you've been using *truth* the same way." His composure flickered—just a hairline crack, but I'd spent my life reading fine lines in hard material, and I saw it. "Truth is a luxury of people who weren't there."

"Were you?" I asked. "There? At the folding?"

Something moved behind his eyes. Something old, and tired, and terribly human.

"I was a witness," he said quietly. "Once. Like Edric Vale. Like your aunt. We were all witnesses, Marguerite. The difference is what we did when they offered us the choice." He lifted his chin. "I chose to survive. They chose to forget. And you—" his gaze dropped to the heart-bone in my hands, glowing faintly now, pulsing like something with a pulse, "—you're choosing to break the only thing keeping this valley from drowning in what it did."

"Good," I said.

And I raised the heart-bone over my head.

He lunged. Of course he lunged. A century of power doesn't stand politely by while a bone-carver commits arson against it. But the Door was open six locks deep, and the chapel was full of everything we'd fed it—my mother's lullaby, the smell of shavings and linseed oil, the first time I heard the dead, Sable's sister's face, Edric's voice, the feeling of being believed—and all of it rose up between us like water finding its level. Corvane hit that wall of given memory and staggered, because you cannot fight freely surrendered things. Power has no grip on them. It never did. That was always the joke at the bottom of the wardens' magic: they traded memory for strength, and strength bought them nothing that mattered.

The heart-bone sang against my palms.

Not a hum this time. Not Edric's tune, not the grandmother's lullaby. A chord—a hundred years of voices compressed into one held note, all the stolen breakfasts and unlatched gates and forgotten boys, pressing outward, wanting *out*, wanting *back*. It hurt to hold. It felt like holding a bird that had been caged so long it had forgotten wings, except it hadn't, not really, and it knew my hands were the cage door.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, you *do* remember."

Everything remembers. That was the secret the wardens had built their prison on and never understood. Bone remembers. Water remembers. Warm beds remember the boys who slept in them. Forgetting was never natural here—it was enforced, and enforced forgetting leaves scars, and scars are just memories wearing armor.

Corvane was screaming something. My name, probably. Or *stop*. Or perhaps, though I doubted it even then, *thank you*.

I brought the heart-bone down against the Door's threshold stone.

* * *

I want to tell you it shattered like glass. It didn't. It broke the way a promise breaks—one clean instant of refusal, and then everything that had been held back came through at once.

Light went through the valley like a bell-stroke goes through bronze.

I was on my knees—I didn't remember falling—and the light was coming up out of the ground, out of the water, out of the ridge that wasn't there until suddenly it was, mist peeling off it like skin off fruit. The drowned chapel drained around me, water sliding away down channels that hadn't existed a moment before, revealing roads, walls, orchards in ash-shapes beginning to remember how to be trees. Somewhere beyond the ridge, I knew without knowing how, streets were unblanking. Names were returning to mouths mid-sentence. A word for bread was settling back into a thousand vocabularies like a coin into a slot.

And the Spire—

The Spire screamed.

It was a sound I felt in my teeth, in the marrow-canal of my own bones: a black needle a mile high discovering gravity after a century of defiance. It didn't topple. It *unwove*. From the top down, thread by black thread, the way a sweater unravels when you find the right end and pull, and the sky closed over the place where it had been the way water closes over a stone, and I thought, absurdly, *so that's why no one ever quite saw it—we were all looking away, all the time, and now we can look.*

Corvane was on his knees across the chapel floor.

He was aging. Not dying—aging, the way a tree ages, seasons stacking on him visibly, his face furrowing, his spine bowing, a century of deferred time arriving all at once to collect. He wasn't fighting it. That was the thing I couldn't look away from. He had his hands open at his sides, palms up, receiving it, and his mouth was moving.

I crawled to him. My body felt borrowed, half-erased still, held together mostly by stubbornness.

"What did you say?"

He looked up at me with eyes that had gone the color of old paper, and he smiled, and it was the most terrible smile I have ever seen because it was *grateful*.

"Thank you," he whispered. "We couldn't. None of us could. Do you understand? Once you take the first memory, you can't—you can't put anything *down*. You just carry it. All of it. Forever." His voice was fraying at the edges, going soft as ash. "A hundred years, and I couldn't stop carrying. And you—" a cough, dry as chalk, "—you picked it up and *broke* it. Little carver. Little listener." His hand found mine. It weighed nothing. "Thank you."

Then he was dust, and the wind off the restored ridge took him, and I was alone in a chapel that was remembering how to be a chapel, with Sable's empty body beside me and silence coming down like snow.

* * *

Here is what I had not let myself think about, all those nights with Edric's femur in my lap, all those locks, all that giving:

When the heart-bone shattered, the gift shattered with it.

I knew. Edric told me, or the book did, or some part of me that had been listening all along finally spoke up. Born of the same magic, cut from the same cloth—the hearing and the hiding were two ends of one thread, and you cannot cut one end and keep the other. I had done the arithmetic in the dark hours and pretended I hadn't.

But knowing a thing and standing inside it are different countries.

The dead began to leave.

They didn't go all at once. They went the way guests leave a party—gradually, then all together. The grandmother whose rib I'd carved, who hummed while I worked: her tune thinned, wavered, went quiet mid-phrase, and I caught myself waiting for the next bar that never came. The parish dead, the field dead, the nameless dead under the chapel floor whose bones I'd brushed aside diving for the Door—all of them, all their murmuring, their grievances, their small eternally repeated sorrows—that great gray tide I had swum in since childhood was draining out of the world, and I could feel it going, feel each voice thinning from speech to whisper to the memory of a whisper to nothing.

"No," I said, to no one. "Wait. Wait, I'm sorry, I didn't—"

Edric was still there. He saved himself for last; I'm certain of it. He was courteous that way. Even dead, even dissolving, the cartographer in the femur kept his manners.

"Marguerite."

"Don't." My voice cracked straight down the middle. "Don't you dare be gracious about this. You stayed dead a *century*. You waited a hundred years in the dark under a workshop floor for someone to hear you, and I heard you, and now you're—"

"Now Osterhale is coming back." His voice was already distant, a map fading in rain. "Every road I drew. Every door. The girl who was kind to me. The hills I never finished surveying." A pause, and in it I heard the smile. "You were the best commission I ever took. And I never even paid you."

"You paid me with a map and a murder mystery and the worst several weeks of my life."

"The finest gifts are burdens," he said. "Listen—no. Don't listen. That's rather the point, isn't it? *Remember* instead. It's harder work. Better pay." The femur grew warm in my hands, warmer than bone should be, warm as a living thing saying goodbye. "One last thing, carver. When the silence comes—and it will come tonight, all at once, like a door closing—don't mistake it for emptiness. Silence is just the world holding its breath before it says something new."

"Edric—"

"Thank you," he said, and it was the second time that hour I'd heard the words from a dying man, and it undid me completely. "For hearing me. No one else ever did."

And the femur went cold in my hands, and the last voice I would ever hear went out of the world, and the silence arrived.

* * *

It was the loudest silence of my life.

You have to understand what my ears had been. Since I was four years old—since the first time, the memory I gave to lock three—they had been full. Full of murmur and grievance and song, full of the dead complaining about weather and inheritance and unfinished love affairs, full of a gray constant chorus that I had complained about my whole life and would have fought God himself to keep. And now: nothing. Snow-quiet. The particular nothing of a room after the clock stops.

I sat in the drained chapel with a cold femur in my lap and Sable's hand in mine—for she was breathing again, shallow but true, the light having poured back into her whatever Corvane had poured out, though whether she'd wake as herself or as some newer draft of herself, I couldn't say—and I listened as hard as I had ever listened in my life.

Nothing.

Nothing, and beneath the nothing, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow: the valley. Living sounds. Wind in orchards that were un-ashing themselves branch by branch. Water finding its old channels. Far off, past the ridge, a dog barking at a world that had grown a country overnight. And closer—much closer—running footsteps on the chapel road, light ones, quick ones, the footsteps of a boy who had a bed that remembered him and a family waking up to say his name.

I didn't hear him coming. For the first time in my life, I didn't hear anyone coming.

I just heard the door bang open, and turned, and saw him—solid, sunlit, impossible—standing in the ruined archway with the carved tooth clutched in his fist, looking at me with the fierce offended stare of a child who has been made to wait.

"You *forgot* me," he announced.

"For a little while," I said. My voice was wrecked. I didn't care. "Not anymore."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then crossed the chapel floor and climbed into my lap without asking, the way children do, the way only the living can, warm and heavy and utterly uninterested in the apocalypse I'd just survived. Behind him, through the archway, the light was still rolling across the valley in slow golden waves, and the ridge stood clear against the sky, and somewhere down in the world maps were redrawing themselves in ink that had waited a hundred years for the job.

Sable stirred beside me. Her fingers tightened on mine.

And in my lap, under the boy's weight, the femur lay cold and silent and finished—but as I shifted to rise, my thumb passed over the old inscription one more time, and I could have sworn, I will always swear, that the bone was warm.

Just for a moment. Just enough.

Like a door left ajar.
