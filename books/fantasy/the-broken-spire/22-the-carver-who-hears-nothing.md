## 22. The Carver Who Hears Nothing

The bone says nothing.

It has said nothing for seven months, three weeks, and four days — not that I'm counting, except that I am, the way you count anything you've lost: not to grieve it, exactly, but to prove it existed. The dead used to hum through my hands. Now they are quiet as furniture, and I have learned, slowly and without much dignity, to be grateful for the silence the way you're grateful for a scar that stopped aching — with a small, stubborn resentment underneath.

This morning I am carving a button. A button! Once I carved reliquaries and mourning brooches that whispered the names of grandmothers into the ears of grieving daughters. Now a cooper named Pell wants six buttons of sheep bone for his best coat, because he is getting married in spring and wants to look, in his words, "like a man from a country that matters."

Well. He'll get his wish. Osterhale matters now. It sits on every map like a fat inked thumbprint, right where the blank space used to be, and cartographers have been arriving all autumn with their instruments and their polite astonishment, measuring roads that were ash-shapes and finding them solid, finding them *old*, finding them older than anything else in the valley by a comfortable century. The drowned chapel has been drained — mostly; the bell tower still leans out of the shallows like a drunk leaning on a fence — and there is talk of making it a monument. There is talk of making everything a monument. The valley has discovered nostalgia the way a man discovers drink, and it is going about it with the same embarrassing enthusiasm.

I don't mind. Let them have their plaques. Let them put up a brass one outside my workshop if they like, though I hope they spell my name correctly, since apparently that's the only name anyone remembers anymore.

That's the joke, you see. That's the joke I keep telling myself at night when the quiet gets loud. Everyone in Osterhale knows Marguerite Thornbury now — the carver who broke the Spire, the woman who gave back the country. My name is in the new histories. Children are taught it. There's even a ballad, and it's terrible, and I've heard it sung twice in the Green Man with a straight face.

And I cannot remember myself.

Not entirely. Not dramatically, either — I haven't woken screaming, clutching at a face I can't find. It's smaller than that, and stranger. When I look in the mirror, I see a woman I recognize the way you recognize a house you lived in as a child: correctly, but from the outside. I know her history the way I know the town's history, because everyone tells it to me. *You did this. You chose this. You gave away your gift so we could remember.* And I nod, and I say yes, and I mean yes, and somewhere beneath the yes there is a hollow shaped exactly like the thing they're describing, and nothing fits inside it.

The gift itself I remember perfectly. That's the cruelest part. I remember what it was like to hold a rib and hear a dead woman humming. I remember Edric Vale's voice threading up through marrow like water through chalk. I remember being *heard* — one voice, one impossible, murdered, patient cartographer who waited a hundred years in the dark under my floorboards for someone who would listen back. I remember all of it vividly, the way you remember a country you emigrated from. I just can't do it anymore. The bone is bone. The silence is silence. And the woman in the mirror is a story other people tell me about myself.

Pell's buttons, then. Six of them. I carve them badly at first — my hands have been unreliable since the Spire fell, which no doctor can explain and which I suspect is simply the body's version of grief — and then well, and then beautifully, because some things survive everything, and my hands are one of them.

Outside, the snow is doing its honest work. Winter came early this year and stayed late, and the valley wears it like an apology. Through the window I can see the ridge — *the* ridge, the one that wasn't there, the one I climbed on the worst night of my life — and it is just a ridge now, ordinary and white-capped, with a proper footpath up it and a waymarker stone that some committee erected in October. The waymarker reads: **OSTERHALE ROAD — RESTORED 1823–PRESENT**. Someone chiseled the dates wrong on purpose, or maybe on purpose-by-committee, and I find I love it. History should be a little wrong. It keeps people looking.

The kettle mutters on the stove. I have kept the kettle habit from before — from the night I sat up waiting for a package I'd somehow already dreamed — and I keep it partly out of superstition and partly because a kettle gives a silence something to be quiet *against*.

Because here is the thing I have not told anyone, not Sable, not the historians, not the earnest young archivist from the capital who keeps writing to me asking for my account of events:

Some mornings, very faintly, from nowhere — from the bone on my bench, from the floorboards, from the cold air over the doorstep — I hear a single note.

Hummed.

One note. Never two. Never a tune. Just one low, warm note, like the first breath of a song someone is deciding whether to finish. It lasts less than a heartbeat, and it never comes when I'm listening for it, and every time it comes I stop breathing until my chest hurts, and then I tell myself: resonance. Draft. Memory of memory. The mind of a woman who spent thirty years hearing the dead does not simply stop hearing them; it hallucinates the absence, the way an amputated hand itches.

I have said this to myself so many times that the words have worn smooth as river stones, and I no longer believe them any more than I believe the mirror-woman's story. But I keep saying them, because the alternative is hope, and hope, in my experience, is just grief wearing its good coat.

* * *

Sable comes on Thursdays. She has come every Thursday since midsummer, and neither of us has ever once mentioned that we come out of guilt, both of us, each carrying our own particular sack of it, and that the tea is how we pass the sacks back and forth without setting them down.

Today she arrives with snow in her grey hair and a loaf under her arm and the expression she wears when she has news she's decided is too important to keep and too dangerous to share.

"Marguerite," she says, before her coat is off. "They've found another one."

"Another what?"

"Cache." She sets the loaf down like it might go off. "Under the old tithe barn, past the Weavers'. Bones. Dozens. Sealed in lead." She sits, heavily, the way she sits now, which is to say like a woman whose bones have begun filing complaints. "The wardens' bones, most likely. Or their victims'. The archivists are beside themselves."

"Are they going to open it?"

"They're going to *study* it." She pours her own tea, which is against our religion and therefore means she's rattled. "Sealed containers, gloves, the whole liturgy. And Marguerite—" She stops. Looks at me across the steam with those pale eyes that saw sixty years of silence and chose it, and unchose it, and paid for both. "There's a carving on the lid. They sent me a rubbing, because I'm the only one alive who reads old Osterhale script. And I'm the only one alive who'd know what it means."

She slides a paper across the table. A charcoal rubbing, all edges and shadow, and across the bottom of it, in a hand I would know anywhere — in the same hand that addressed a package I once waited for all night on a cold doorstep —

*She will know where the rest are buried.*

I set down my cup very carefully, because my hands have started their trembling again, and I have a reputation to maintain.

"That's not possible," I say. "Edric's femur was buried with honors. In the chapel yard. I attended. There was a plaque."

"I know," says Sable.

"The plaque had his *name* on it. His full name. Edric Merle Vale, cartographer, who died so that a country might live. I read it aloud at the ceremony. Half the valley was there."

"I know," says Sable again, gently, the way you speak to someone standing on ice you've already tested.

"And he's dead," I add, because apparently I've become the sort of person who states the obvious at the recently bereaved. "Thoroughly. Spectacularly. He's been dead for a hundred and one years, and the last year of that he spent being properly, officially, ceremonially dead, with a plaque."

"Yes," says Sable. "And yet."

* * *

I don't sleep that night, which is not unusual, but I sleep differently, which is. I lie in the loft above the workshop listening to the house settle and the wind worry the eaves, and I do the arithmetic I've been refusing to do since summer.

A note. One note, some mornings. Faint. Impossible. I told myself it was an itch in an amputated limb.

But Edric Vale stayed dead on purpose. That was his masterpiece — a century-long act of faith, a murder arranged like a letter, a map folded into his own marrow and posted to the future. He was a man who understood that the most reliable hiding place in the world is inside the thing everyone agrees is finished. Dead men deliver. Dead men are never suspected. Dead men can wait.

And if a man could wait a hundred years in the dark under my floorboards, holding one note of a song like a held breath—

What else might he have cached? What else might a cartographer — a man whose entire profession was knowing where things *were* — have hidden, in lead-sealed boxes, in places only a bone-carver who hears the dead would ever think to look?

*She will know where the rest are buried.*

I sit up in the dark. The house creaks. And from below — from the workshop, from the bench, from somewhere in the grain of the silent world — comes a note.

One note. Low. Warm. Unhurried.

And this time — I swear this on the plaque, on the terrible ballad, on Pell's six buttons drying on their string — this time it is almost, almost the first two notes of a tune. As if somewhere a dead man is practicing. As if patience, given a hundred years, has learned to be patient with patience.

* * *

Morning. Snow. Kettle on.

I am on my doorstep before the light is properly up, wrapped in two coats, with my breath hanging around me like a curtain I keep forgetting to walk through. Down the lane, the Weavers' youngest — Tam, eight years old, loud as weather — is breaking the crust on the pond with a stick, and when he sees me he shouts my name across the frozen field, because children here still shout my name like it's a good thing, and I lift a hand, and my chest does the thing it does.

The package comes at half past nine.

Not by post. By nobody. It is simply *there*, between one blink and the next, sitting on the snowy step as if it grew there overnight along with the frost-flowers: a bundle wrapped in sacking, tied with cord, addressed in a script that hasn't been written in a hundred years, in a hand I'd know anywhere.

I stand there longer than I should. Long enough for Tam to stop smashing ice and stare. Then I kneel, and the cold bites through both coats, and I untie the cord with fingers that have forgotten how to tremble on command.

Inside the sacking: bone.

Edric's femur. I know it the way you know your own front door in the dark — I carved it open, I read it, I carried it over a ridge that wasn't there and back again. But it has been polished smooth as riverglass, every score and gouge healed over into a soft, pale gleam, and along the shaft, cut fine and deep and filled with something dark that is not ink:

*THANK YOU.*

Two words. A hundred years of waiting, a murder, a map, a door, a broken heart-bone, a country unfolded back into the world — and the sum total of the invoice is *thank you*, which is either the smallest bill ever presented or the largest, depending on the day.

I laugh. Out loud, alone, on a frozen doorstep, at half past nine in the morning, I laugh until the snow on the eaves shifts, and then I sit down on the step with the bone across my knees like a sleeping cat, and I wait.

Because I know what I'm waiting for. I've known since the rubbing. Since the note. Since the night I sat up with the kettle and refused to chase.

The bone lies still. The morning lies still. Somewhere behind me the kettle begins, very softly, to sing.

And then — faintly, impossibly, unmistakably — from the polished length of a dead man's leg, in a voice like water finding its old channel after a century underground, comes a hummed note.

One note.

Then, for the first time in seven months, three weeks, and five days — a second.

And under the second note, so quiet I have to bow my head to the bone to catch it, a voice, dry and warm and unbearably patient, saying the only thing a cartographer would say to the woman who opened the door:

"Now then. About these maps. There are more doors than one, Marguerite Thornbury — and I should very much like to show you where the others are buried."

The kettle screams behind me. The snow goes on falling, honest and unhurried, on a valley that remembers.

And I — bone-carver, hearer-of-nothing, keeper of a gift that is supposed to be dust — pick up the femur, and carry it inside, and put the kettle back on.

Because some doors, once opened, stay ajar.

And this one has just asked me to walk through it again.
