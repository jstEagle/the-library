## 4. Thirteen Turns

Elder Maren's house smelled of dried yarrow and lamp oil, the way it had smelled for as long as Ulyana could remember. He had knocked on her door at first light, before the valley was fully awake, before he could talk himself out of it, and she had opened it as though she had been standing on the other side all night, waiting.

"You read it," she said. Not a question.

"I read it." His voice came out smaller than he intended. "The ink was fresh."

She stepped aside to let him in. The single room was crowded with the furniture of a long life—a loom pushed against one wall, jars of herbs ranked three deep on every shelf, a kettle already singing over the fire. She poured two cups of willow-bark tea without asking if he wanted any and set his down on the table between them like something being offered across a border.

"Sit," she said. "You look like a man who hasn't slept."

"I haven't."

"Good. Sleep would have made this easier, and it shouldn't be easy." She lowered herself into the chair opposite him, and for the first time Ulyana noticed how old she looked—not the settled, weathered age of the valley's elders but something thinner underneath it, a weariness that had been waiting years for this morning. "Tell me what you understand."

"That tonight is named. In a book written before my parents were born. That my name is beside it." He wrapped his hands around the cup because they wanted to shake. "That the wards flickered. Everyone felt it. They're saying it's a bad omen, a dying of the light-ferns, nothing more."

"And you know better."

"I know better."

Maren nodded slowly, and then she did something Ulyana had never seen her do: she closed her eyes and pressed the heels of both hands against them, briefly, as though holding something in. When she lowered them, she was composed again, but the composure cost her visibly.

"Then I'll tell you what I can, and you'll hate me for the parts I keep back, and that's right and proper." She reached into the collar of her dress and drew out a cord. On it hung a charm no bigger than a thumbnail—carved bone, or something like bone, ringed with thirteen notches, each notch filled with a different pale stone. "Do you know what this is?"

Ulyana shook his head.

"A moon-calendar. Older than me. Older than the archive, I'd wager." She turned it in the lamplight so the stones caught fire one by one. "Thirteen turns. Each time the moon wanes to nothing, one of these goes dark. When the thirteenth goes dark—" she stopped, started again. "The prophecy gives you thirteen turns of the moon. Thirteen chances to be somewhere else when the date comes around again next year, or to have done whatever must be done. But hear me, boy, because this is the part that matters."

She leaned forward, and her grip on the table was white-knuckled.

"If the thirteenth turn wanes with your soul still claimed—if you come home having failed, or having never gone at all—the contract doesn't end with you. Contracts of that kind are written broad. The court beneath the mountain doesn't take a debt from one person. It takes it from everything the debtor loved. Everything that sheltered him. Everything that hid him." Her voice dropped. "It takes the valley, Ulyana. Every soul behind those wards. Your silence bought them twenty-six years. Your failure would unbuy it in a night."

The tea had gone cold in his hands. He set it down carefully, because he was suddenly afraid of what his hands might do—they had always been quick to storm, even buried, even bound.

"My parents," he said. "Did they know? When they carried me through the wardline as an infant. Did they know what they'd signed?"

Something moved behind Maren's eyes—guilt, or grief, or the specific shape a lie takes when it has been held too long and grown heavy.

"They knew they were saving your life," she said. "Whether they knew the full price—" She rose abruptly and went to the window, her back to him. "I've said enough. More than enough. Take the charm. Count the turns. And be gone by morning, because the thing on the other side of the wardline felt you yesterday, and things that feel once do not forget."

* * *

He spent the last day of his life in Thorn Valley doing ordinary things, and it was the ordinariness that broke him.

He walked the terraced fields where he had worked every harvest since he was seven, pulling weeds nobody needed pulled, letting his fingers learn the texture of the soil one final time. He sat by the millrace where Sef Idris had found him weeping at fourteen, after the year his mother died, and said nothing about prophecies, only handed him half a honeycake and stayed until the crying stopped. He climbed the ridge above his father's cottage and looked down at the smoke rising from its chimney and memorized it—the angle of the roof, the crooked door, the way the wind coming off the eastern crags bent the smoke toward the pines.

No one knew. That was the cruelty of it. He had told no one, and Maren had told no one, and so the valley went about its evening the way it had gone about ten thousand evenings before: lamps lit in windows, bread pulled from ovens, children called in from the creeks by mothers who did not know their sanctuary was a debt still being collected. A woman pressed a loaf into his hands at the market square because he looked thin. An old man asked him to settle an argument about whether the rains would come early this year.

"The rains," Ulyana said, "will come when I call them."

The old man laughed. Everyone laughed. Storm-callers were a story in Thorn Valley, a rumor, a thing the wards had eaten. Ulyana laughed too, and it tasted like ash.

At dusk he stood at the edge of the wardline.

Everyone in the valley knew where it was, though few could see it—a seam in the air along the northern ravine, marked only by the light-ferns that grew thick there, glowing faintly blue-green even in daylight. Beyond it the world began: the wild lands, the roads, the mountains, everything his parents had died—or worse than died—to keep him from.

He raised his hand toward the seam, the way he had a thousand times as a child, pressing his palm flat against nothing, feeling the faint resistance like the skin on cooled milk. It was a game. It had always been a game. The wards kept magic out, and they kept his magic in, muzzled and sleeping, and he had hated them and loved them in equal measure for twenty-six years because they were the only reason he was alive and the only reason he had never once been himself.

Behind him, footsteps on the path. He didn't turn.

"You're really going," Sef Idris said.

"At dawn."

"I'm coming with you."

Ulyana turned then. Sef stood a few paces off with his satchel already packed, his gray-streaked hair tied back, his face wearing the expression of a man who had argued with himself all day and lost. "Maren told you."

"Maren told me enough. And I owe you a debt I've never liked carrying." Sef's mouth twisted. "A decade ago you kept silent about certain irregularities in the flood-year records. Irregularities that would have ended with me outside the wards, with nothing but the clothes I wore. You could have ruined me. You didn't. So." He lifted his chin. "Wherever you're going, I know the roads better than you do, and I know where that cursed book came from, which is more than Maren will say. Mount Vhalen. The under-court. If answers exist, they're there."

"Sef—"

"Don't thank me. I'll only regret it." But his eyes were kind, and frightened, and Ulyana understood that fear shared was a kind of company.

They stood together at the seam as the light failed, two men looking out at the darkening world, and neither spoke again until full dark, when the light-ferns brightened around them like drowned stars.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold.

Maren met them at the ravine, alone. She had dressed formally, in the indigo robes of her office, and she carried a walking staff she plainly did not need, and Ulyana realized with a jolt that this was ceremony—that she had come to mark the crossing the way one marks a funeral or a coronation, because it might be either.

She pressed the moon-charm into his palm and folded his fingers over it. The bone was warm, warmer than it should have been, as if it had been held near a fire all night.

"Thirteen turns," she said. "Count them honestly. Don't let hope round the numbers down."

"What happens when they're gone?"

"What I told you." Her voice was steady, but her hand trembled against his. "And Ulyana—when you stand before whoever holds your soul, ask them who paid for the wards. Ask them what the price was, and who agreed to it. Promise me you'll ask."

"Why does it matter who—"

"Promise me."

The urgency in her voice startled him. He promised. It seemed a small thing to promise a dying woman, and she looked like a dying woman suddenly, there in the gray light with the mist coming off the ravine.

She embraced him then, brief and fierce, her mouth close to his ear. "Your mother made me swear to keep you small," she whispered. "Forgive us both for how well we obeyed."

Then she stepped back, became Elder Maren again, remote and carved, and gestured at the seam in the air.

Ulyana faced the wardline. His heart was going hard, and his palms had begun, faintly, to itch—the old itch, the childhood itch, the one the healers had called nerves and he had privately known was something else straining at its leash.

He thought of the prophecy book with its fresh ink. Of the cold, patient attention he had felt answer him yesterday at this very spot, waiting on the far side like a creditor at a door. Of the loaf of bread in his pack, given by a woman who would never know why he left.

He stepped through.

* * *

The world tore open.

There was no gentler word for it. The wards had lain over his magic for twenty-six years like snowfall on a field, and crossing the line was spring—all of it at once, every buried season detonating in his chest. He heard himself shout, or maybe the sky shouted; there was no difference. Power came up through the ground and down through the clouds and sideways out of his own skin, and every hair on his body stood erect, and the light-ferns behind him flared so bright they threw shadows.

His knees hit the wet grass of the wild lands. He was laughing. He couldn't stop laughing, and tears ran down his face, and he didn't know which was real or if it mattered. The storm-sense unfurled from him like a fist opening after decades clenched—he could feel the weather for fifty miles, pressure ridges and moisture rivers and a cold front massing over the western hills like an army at dawn, and all of it *aware* of him, all of it leaning toward him the way plants lean toward light. He was not a man standing in the world. He was a node in it, a live wire, a place where the sky happened.

"*Ulyana.*" Sef's voice, thin and alarmed, from very far away. "Ulyana, the clouds—"

He looked up. Above the ravine, clouds were boiling out of a clear dawn sky, spiraling inward around the point where he knelt, stacking themselves into a tower that flickered internally with heat-lightning. The wind had risen from nothing to a gale. Somewhere a tree cracked.

*Stop*, he told it. *Stop, stop—*

But it was like telling blood to stop beating. Twenty-six years of dammed power had become a flood, and the flood had opinions. Lightning crawled down the spinning cloud-wall and grounded itself in the earth around him in a ring, close enough that he could smell the ozone burning, and part of him—that buried, starving part—sang at the nearness of it.

Then he thought of the valley behind him. The light-ferns. The children called in from the creeks.

He grabbed hold of the terror instead of the joy and used it, hauling the storm inward the way you haul a sled uphill, hand over hand, gasping, until the tower of cloud shuddered, unraveled, and collapsed into harmless rain that fell across the ravine in a soft gray curtain.

Silence. Dripping grass. His own ragged breathing.

Sef stood six feet away, soaked to the skin, staring at him with an expression Ulyana had never seen on anyone's face before—awe and horror in equal measure, braided tight.

"So," Sef managed. "The stories were true."

"The stories," Ulyana said, still on his knees, still shaking, "were *small*."

He got up slowly. Behind them, through the thinning rain, the wardline glowed among the light-ferns, and beyond it Thorn Valley lay green and oblivious in the early light, its chimney smoke bending toward the pines. He looked at it for a long moment—at the only home he had ever known, now on the other side of a line he could not cross again without bringing ruin down on everyone he loved.

Home was behind him. Everything else was ahead.

He closed his hand around the moon-charm. Through his new-opened senses he could feel it humming, faint and cold, counting something down—and far to the north, beneath a mountain he had never seen, he felt attention turn toward him like a head lifting. Patient. Certain. Already smiling.

The thirteenth turn had begun.
