## 6. The Thief Who Owed Him

Corvane smelled of tallow and secrets.

We came down off the ridge road at dusk, when the city's lamps were being lit one by one by boys on ladders, and I stood at the gate a long moment just breathing it in. Twenty years in Thorn Valley had taught me pine and rain and cold stone. This was different—smoke from ten thousand hearths, fish from the river market, horse dung, hot iron, and under all of it something sweeter and more rotten, like fruit going over. A city does not hide what it is. That was the first thing I noticed about Corvane, and the last thing I would come to love about it.

"You're staring," Sef said.

"I'm allowed to stare. I've never seen a city."

"You've seen Corvane before." He adjusted the strap of his satchel—the archive satchel he refused to replace even now, with its cracked leather and its ink-stains like old bruises. "You were nine."

"I was nine," I said, "and being carried through it in a covered cart with my mother's hand pressed over my eyes so I wouldn't look at anything and nothing would look at me." I turned toward the gate. "So as far as my memory is concerned, this is the first city in the world."

Sef said nothing to that. He had been saying very little since the drowned fields, and I had learned not to fill his silences for him. Some men need their guilt to have room to stand up in.

The gate guards barely looked at us. That was the second thing I noticed about Corvane: nobody looked at anybody. In the valley, every face was known, every stranger an event, every glance a small act of accounting. Here, ten thousand people moved through the evening streets and each of them was a closed door. I had spent my whole life trying to be a closed door. It was strange to walk into a place where everyone had succeeded.

"Where do we find her?" I asked.

"The Gullwing Stair," Sef said. "If she still works the same trade."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then we ask louder questions in worse places, and I would very much prefer she does."

* * *

The Gullwing Stair was not a stair. It was a tavern built into the bones of some older building—a cellar hall where the ceiling arched up into ribs of blackened timber, and the walls were papered, floor to rafters, with notices. Bills of sale. Missing-persons broadsheets. Reward postings with the rewards scratched out and new ones written above them in different hands. A whole wall of other people's business, layered like sediment, and men and women standing before it in the lamplight reading, copying, memorizing, trading.

Information brokers, Sef had called them. Standing there, I understood the term differently than I had from books. These were not scholars. They were gulls, all of them, screaming over the same fish.

"She'll be at the back," Sef murmured. "She always liked a wall behind her."

I saw Ryn Osk before he pointed her out, though I don't know how I knew. She sat at a corner table with her boots up on a stool, dealing cards to two men who clearly thought they were winning. Small woman, sharp-faced, hair cropped close and dyed a red that did not occur in nature. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow and her forearms were a ledger of scars and faded ink. When she laughed at something one of the card players said, it was a bright, easy laugh, and I watched her take three coins off the table while laughing, so smoothly that the man never saw his own money leave.

The last time I had seen her, she had been fourteen, bleeding from a split lip, backed against a wall in a valley market town while a mob shouted thief at her—and I, seventeen and terrified of my own shadow, had put myself between her and them and called down a storm small enough to frighten and large enough to scatter. I had never done anything braver before or since. I had also never told anyone. Storm-calling in the open, even a little storm, even for mercy—that was a death sentence in the lowlands, and we had both run separate directions into the rain and I had assumed, for eleven years, that she was dead or gone or had forgotten.

She looked up from her cards as we approached, and I watched recognition move across her face like weather.

"Well," she said, and set down the deck. "The storm itself. Sitting down, are you? Sit down. You're making my customers nervous, standing there looking like a funeral that learned to walk."

The card players took the hint and their losses elsewhere. We sat. A serving girl appeared; Ryn ordered three cups of something without asking us, which told me who would be paying.

"Eleven years," she said, when the girl was gone. "You get taller. You get—" she waved a hand at all of me, "—graver. Valley suits you. All that solemnity. What happened, did someone finally read your fortune and it said *doom*?"

"It said tonight's date beside my name in a book written a hundred years ago," I said. "So. Nearly."

Her grin died by degrees. Not all at once—she was too good a player for that—but I watched the light go out of it the way you watch a lamp gutter. "That's a joke."

"Yes," Sef said quietly. "And no."

Ryn looked between us. Then she leaned forward, elbows on the table, and all the tavern noise seemed to step back a pace, as if the room itself wanted to hear. "Tell me the whole of it. And Ulyana Quintana, if you lie to me or soften it, I will know, because I make my living knowing, and then I will be insulted, and you do not want me insulted."

So I told her. The archive, the book, the fresh ink on a century-old page. The wardline and the cold patient attention on the far side of it. Maren's charm against my throat, ticking down its thirteen turns—eleven dark already, if you counted the nights since we left, and I counted everything now. The hunters' country we had crossed to get here. I kept back only the drowned village, and I am not sure why, except that some shames want to stay private a little longer.

When I finished, Ryn sat back and was quiet for a long moment, turning her cup slowly on the table.

"A soul sold before birth," she said at last. "To the court under Mount Vhalen." She glanced at Sef. "And you're the archivist who can read whatever trail led there."

"I was an archivist," Sef said. "Before the disgrace."

"Everyone in this story has been disgraced or exiled or sold," Ryn observed. "It's practically a family reunion." But there was no humor in it anymore. She drummed her fingers once, twice, and then stopped herself, the way a gambler stops a tell.

"What do you know about Vhalen?" I asked.

"What everyone knows: the court buys things. Souls, mostly, but also names, debts, firstborn griefs, last words. They pay in favors, and their favors are good, which is the terrifying part. Nobody knows where the coin goes after. Nobody knows what they *do* with what they buy." Her eyes flicked to my throat, to the charm. "Except now maybe we guess."

"So you've heard of sold souls."

"I've heard of the *ledger*," she said, and here her voice dropped so low I had to lean in. "Every broker in Corvane has heard rumors of the Ledger of Names—the court's master record of every purchase ever made. Half my profession thinks it's a myth invented to scare apprentices. The other half would cut throats for a look at it." She smiled thinly. "I'm in the second half, obviously."

"And could you get us to the mountain?" Sef asked. "Roads, passes, safe houses, the approach. You've moved goods north before—I've seen the manifests."

"Manifests are forged, Archivist, but flattering. Yes. I can get you to Vhalen. I know the passes, I know which waystations sell water and which sell their guests, I know how to move three people through hunter country without leaving a shape on the road." She spread her hands. "For a price."

I had been waiting for it. There was no version of Ryn Osk that worked for gratitude alone; gratitude spoiled, and she struck me as a woman who kept only what kept. "Name it."

She looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—something quick and hungry and, I thought, a little hurt, though she'd have denied it under torture.

"The name," she said. "Whoever sold you. I find out who signed you away, and I take you to the mountain myself, and I don't leave your side until the contract's broken or you're dead. Full service. My best work." She tapped the table once, hard. "But I want the name, Ulyana. Written, spoken, however you keep it. Because a sale needs a seller, and a seller has a reason, and reasons are the most valuable thing in this city. Whoever gave you away got something for it. A favor from the court under the mountain is worth kingdoms. I want to know what kingdom changed hands when you were born."

The tavern noise came back all at once, like a tide returning. I became aware of my own heartbeat, and of the charm against my throat, cold as a coin left out overnight.

"I don't know the name," I said.

"No," Ryn agreed pleasantly. "But you'll learn it. On the road, in the court, somewhere between here and Vhalen—it'll come to you, because these things always do, because the truth is a cat and it hates closed doors. So here's my offer, properly stated: I help you reach the mountain. Somewhere along the way, the name surfaces. When it does, it's mine." She extended her hand across the table, scarred forearm, ink-stained fingers. "Do we deal?"

I looked at her hand and did not take it yet.

Because here is what I was thinking, and thinking hard: *the name*. I had been carrying the question of it for days now like a stone in my boot. Someone had sold me. Someone with the power to bargain with the court beneath the mountain, someone whose favor was worth a newborn's soul—which meant someone important, someone inside the valley, someone who might still be alive, might still be *there*, might be one of the faces I had grown up trusting, eating beside, bowing to. Elder Maren's voice came back to me unbidden: *if the thirteenth turn wanes with your soul still claimed, the court takes everything you love besides.* Everything I loved. Everything I loved lived behind wards that someone had bought with pieces of me.

And Ryn Osk, whom I had once saved with a storm, was asking me to hand her the weapon that could burn my whole world down.

"Loyalty," I said slowly, "is an interesting thing to price."

"Don't flatter yourself," she said, but gently, which ruined it. "This isn't betrayal. This is curiosity, and curiosity is the only honest sin. I saved your life's direction, you saved my actual life, and now we're square on the old account and opening a new one. New accounts have terms." Her hand stayed out. "Besides—think it through, storm-caller. If the name belongs to someone you love, wouldn't you rather know it *before* you stand in front of the thing that bought you? Wouldn't you rather walk into that cathedral knowing exactly who put you there?" Her voice softened further, and that was the most dangerous thing she'd done all night. "Or would you rather find out in front of Lady Whoever-She-Is, with her people watching, when you can't do anything about it but bleed?"

I looked at Sef. Sef was studying the papered wall with tremendous attention, and I remembered—suddenly, unpleasantly—that he had traced the provenance of the prophecy book, that he knew where it came from, that he had been strangely quiet about the whole chain of custody, and that the ending of our last night's conversation had felt less like a conclusion than a door held shut.

Everyone at this table was keeping something. Perhaps that was what trust was: choosing whose locked rooms you could live with.

I took Ryn's hand. Her grip was dry and strong and brief.

"We deal," I said. "But hear the counter-term. The name is yours when we're past the pass—at the mountain's foot, not before. If it surfaces early and you try to pry it out of me on the road, the deal's dead and you can explain to your brokers why you lost the only guide to Vhalen who's actually invited there."

Something kindled in her face—not anger. Delight. "Oh, you've gotten *spicy* in the valley. Done. At the mountain's foot." She stood, swept the coins from the table into her palm in one motion, and jerked her chin toward the back of the tavern. "Come on, then. You can't sleep at mine—too many people owe me money and visit at bad hours. But there's a flophouse two streets over where the beds don't bite and the landlady owes me a favor I haven't spent. We leave tomorrow, an hour past dawn, north gate, and Ulyana—" she paused, half-turned, lamplight catching the edge of her smile, "—wear a hood. Your face is fine but your *weather* follows you around like a dog, and there are eyes in this city that know what storm-sign looks like."

* * *

The flophouse room had one window, a candle, and two mattresses that had known better centuries. Sef took the one by the wall and was asleep within minutes, or performing sleep convincingly enough that I let him keep the fiction. I sat by the window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width, watching Corvane's lamps burn down one district at a time, and turned the day over in my hands like a card I wasn't sure how to play.

*A sale needs a seller,* she had said. *A seller has a reason.*

I touched the charm. Ten turns left, after tonight. Somewhere north, under a mountain, my soul—or half of it, or whatever portion of a person can be weighed and shelved—waited in the dark among other people's voices. And somewhere much closer than that, perhaps closer than the north gate, perhaps sleeping in this very city or sitting by a fire in Thorn Valley right now, was the person who had signed the paper.

Ryn thought she was buying a name. She didn't understand yet what she'd bargained for. A name was a key, and keys don't care which door you use them on, and once she had it she would turn it, because that was what she was—what I had apparently always known she was, even at fourteen, bleeding against a wall: someone who opens things.

Below, in the street, a lantern passed. Two figures walking northward through the wet cobbles, unhurried, and for just a moment—one heartbeat, no more—the flame in the nearer one's hand burned perfectly still, upright and unmoving, the way flames do not burn in wind, the way flames do not burn when the air around them has gone cold and attentive and *patient.*

They walked on. The lantern swayed again, ordinary, human.

I did not wake Sef. I sat with my back to the wall until dawn, listening to the city breathe, and rehearsed, over and over, the sentence I would have to say to Ryn Osk in the morning:

*I think the court already knows we're coming—and I think it sent something to watch us sleep.*
