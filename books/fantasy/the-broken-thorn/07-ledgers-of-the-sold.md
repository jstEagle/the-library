## 7. Ledgers of the Sold

Ryn Osk had broken into forty-one vaults in her life, and she could tell you that every one of them had a personality.

Some were arrogant—gilded doors, fat locks, guards who polished their boots more often than they checked their posts. Some were paranoid, layered with wards and counter-wards until even the air inside tasted like copper and suspicion. The vault beneath the Gilded Quill, Corvane's most discreet house of information, was neither.

It was *polite*.

That was what made it dangerous. The door was oak, unremarkable, set into a corridor lined with shelved ledgers like any clerk's back room. The lock was good but not remarkable. There were no wards she could feel, no tripwires, no bells. Just a small brass plaque screwed neatly beside the frame: *By appointment only.*

The Quill didn't guard its secrets with iron. It guarded them with manners. Nobody broke into a place that trusted you to knock. And so nobody ever had—which meant nobody had ever bothered to learn that the third shelf from the floor in the corridor outside was hollow, and that behind it ran a service channel barely wide enough for a woman who'd spent her childhood learning to fold herself into spaces no one else wanted.

Ryn folded herself into it now, breath shallow, dust sifting down her collar, and waited for the night clerk to finish his rounds.

Above her, through a hairline gap between shelf and wall, she could see his boots pass. Pause. Pass again. He hummed while he worked—an old harvest song, off-key—and Ryn found herself almost fond of him. She'd watched him for six nights. He watered a dying fern on his desk. He kept a photograph of two children tucked under the blotter's corner where he could see it when he looked up. He was, by all evidence, a decent man employed by an indecent business, and tomorrow morning he would discover a hollow shelf and a missing ledger and he would be terrified, because the Quill did not forgive its clerks the sins of its thieves.

*Sorry,* she thought at his boots, without much sincerity. She'd been sorry before. It hadn't slowed her down yet.

The boots receded. A door clicked. His lamp-glow shrank to a coin and vanished around the corner toward the front office, where he'd sit out the last hour scratching entries into the day-book.

Ryn counted to three hundred, slid from the channel, and went to work.

* * *

The lock took four minutes, which offended her professionally. Inside, the vault was cold and dry and smelled of vellum and lamp oil, shelves rising into darkness on all sides, each spine wrapped in plain brown paper with a number stenciled on it. No titles. The Quill sold certainty, not information; you told them what you needed to be true, and they sold you the document that made it true. Somewhere in this room was proof that half the merchant houses of Corvane owed their fortunes to paper that had never recorded anything except a price.

Sef Idris had given her the number.

He'd done it in the flat, careful voice he used when he was ashamed of knowing something, which—as far as Ryn could tell—was his only voice. *"Ledger eleven-fourteen,"* he'd said, not looking at her. *"Sales of intangible assets, southern provinces, century span. If a soul changed hands, it will be there. Souls are always filed as intangibles. The courts find it tidier."*

*"You've seen this ledger?"* she'd asked.

*"I catalogued its twin."* A pause, long enough to have weight. *"In another life."*

She hadn't pressed. Pressing Sef was like pressing on a bruise—he'd give you the truth eventually, but you'd both regret it. Besides, she had her own reasons for being here, reasons she'd fed to Ulyana in pieces: a debt, a promise, the memory of a boy in a rain-slick alley in Corvane seven years ago who'd stepped between her and a mob with nothing but his hands raised and storm-light crawling under his skin like lightning trapped in glass. They'd have killed him. Instead they'd killed her reputation, because when the Watch finally came, the only witness worth believing swore the thief had been alone.

Ulyana Quintana had lied to armed men to save a stranger, and then vanished before she could thank him, and Ryn Osk did not forget debts. She also did not do anything as simple as repay them. She compounded them. That was why she'd named her price when he came looking for her: *the name of whoever sold you.* Not money. Not passage. The name.

Because Ryn knew how sales worked. And somewhere in a brown-paper ledger in this polite little vault was a line that would tell her whether the boy who'd saved her life had been sold by strangers—or by someone who'd smiled at him across a valley table for twenty years.

Eleven-fourteen sat on the fourth shelf, third row, exactly where Sef said it would be. It was heavier than it looked. She carried it to the vault's single desk, lit the shuttered lamp she'd cached there the night before, and opened it.

And stopped breathing.

* * *

The ledger was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it—the thing she'd remember later, turning it over in her mind the way you probe a sore tooth. Whoever had kept these books had taken pride in them. The hand was fine and even, copper-brown ink gone soft with age, columns ruled true as plumb lines. Dates. Assets. Sellers, coded. Buyers, coded. Prices paid not always in coin—in land grants, in water rights, in *favors*, that word appearing again and again with the serene confidence of a currency everyone agreed to pretend was solid.

Ryn read fast. She'd been reading other people's secrets since she was nine years old, and she knew the rhythm of a ledger the way sailors know weather. Find the year. Find the province. Thorn Valley kept no trade records—it barely admitted to existing—but souls sold *out* of a place still had to be sold *from* somewhere, and the Quill's brokers tagged provenance like butchers tagged meat.

The year of Ulyana's birth fell near the middle of the book. She ran her finger down the column, past a soul bought by a shipping magnate to bless his hulls, past one purchased by a widow who'd wanted to hear her dead husband's voice again (the margin note said *fulfilled; asset consumed*), past entries so casual in their cruelty that Ryn had to pause once, press her knuckles to her mouth, and breathe.

Then she found it.

*Asset:* One-half soul, undivided interest, male infant, storm-affinity confirmed in utero.
*Seller:* [coded]
*Buyer:* VESSARINE, LADY, UNDER-COURT OF MOUNT VHALEN.
*Consideration:* One favor, unlimited in scope, redeemable upon demand, granted to [coded], of THORN VALE.
*Term:* Claimable upon the bearer's twenty-fifth year, or upon the waning of the thirteenth moon thereafter named.
*Condition of sale:* Asset to remain unaware until claim. Sanctuary wards of Thorn Vale to be maintained in perpetuity as consideration-in-kind.

Ryn read it twice. Three times. Her lips moved over the words *in perpetuity* like they were a curse in a language she almost spoke.

Half a soul. Not the whole of him—half. Sold before he drew breath, priced against the safety of everyone he'd ever known, and the ledger recorded it all in the same tidy hand that had recorded the widow's dead husband and the magnate's lucky ship. Somewhere a clerk had ruled those columns straight and gone home to supper.

But it was the seller's code that made her blood go strange and slow.

Every broker house used ciphers, and most ciphers were vanity—a substitution wheel, a key phrase, something a clever child cracked in an afternoon. This one wasn't. The seller's mark wasn't letters at all. It was a sigil, stamped rather than written, pressed into the page in wax gone amber with a century of dark: a circle quartered by a vertical line, with a small thorn-hook curling off the lower left quadrant.

Ryn knew that mark.

Not from ledgers. From *charms*. Every exile who crossed into Thorn Valley carried a token struck with the valley's seal—the same quartered circle, the same thorn-hook—blessed at the wardline by whichever elder held the keys that season. She'd seen it on Ulyana's moon-calendar charm, on Maren's staff-head, on the doorposts of half the hovels in the valley. It was everywhere. It meant *home.*

It also meant the cipher told her nothing about *who*. A valley-wide seal could belong to any official. Any elder. Any clerk with access to the stamping press.

Except.

Ryn leaned closer, lamp tilted, and saw what a hundred years of shadow had hidden: beneath the wax sigil, faint as a watermark, someone had initialed the entry. Two letters in that fine copper hand, small enough to miss, deliberate enough to mean something. *E.M.*

She sat back. The vault was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, the Quill's night clerk hummed his off-key harvest song, and the fern on his desk drank its water, and the world went on being ordinary while Ryn Osk stared at two initials and felt the ground tilt under everything she'd believed about the last week of her life.

*E.M.* Elder Maren. Maren, who had pressed the moon-charm into Ulyana's hands with tears standing in her eyes. Maren, who had read the prophecy page before he did and said nothing. Maren, whose signature Ryn had seen a dozen times on valley writs, and whose *M* curled exactly like that—no, stop, she told herself, *you want it to match, wanting isn't seeing*—but it did match, it matched the way a key matches a lock, and Ryn had built her whole life on trusting locks.

It could be forged. Of course it could be forged. That was the first law of her trade: the document is not the truth, the document is a *claim* about the truth, and claims can be manufactured by anyone with a steady hand and a reason. Someone had wanted the valley blamed. Someone had wanted Ulyana to walk out of his sanctuary angry and aimed, and the surest way to aim a man was to tell him his family had sold him.

Or.

Or it was real. Or a woman who'd helped raise him had stood at a wardline a century's worth of famine ago—no, not a century, the ledger was old but the entry was dated within living elders' memory, within *Maren's* memory—and traded half a child for the lives of everyone else, and had then spent twenty-five years watching that child grow up kind and quiet and invisible, carrying the secret like a stone behind her ribs, loving him anyway, or perhaps *because* she couldn't afford to love him, or perhaps precisely so that the betrayal, when it came, would come from someone who'd at least had the decency to weep about it.

Ryn closed the ledger. Opened it again. Copied the entry into her memory the way she copied everything—twice, once forward and once backward, so it couldn't slip—and noted the consideration clause one final time.

*A favor, unlimited in scope, redeemable upon demand.*

Unlimited. The court hadn't just bought a soul. It had bought an open door into Thorn Valley, held by whoever signed, good forever. Which meant that whatever else was true, the sale wasn't finished. Sales with open clauses never were. Lady Vessarine hadn't purchased a boy; she'd purchased a *lever*, and levers stayed loaded until someone pulled them.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the ledger's presence felt physical, like a body laid on the desk between her and the door.

* * *

Getting out was easier than getting in; it always was, once your hands stopped shaking. She wrapped eleven-fourteen in oilcloth, slid it into the channel behind the hollow shelf—she'd retrieve it tomorrow through the front door, appointment booked under a merchant's name she'd invented that morning, because the boldest theft was the one conducted across a clerk's own desk—and folded herself back into the dark.

She was three streets away, walking at the pace of a woman with nowhere particular to be, before she let herself think about what she'd do with it.

Tell Ulyana? Tell him what—that the mark on his sale was the mark of his own home, the initials possibly his own elder's, the sanctuary that had sheltered him purchased with the piece of himself he was now crossing a kingdom to reclaim? That every ward that had kept him safe had been paid for out of his own hide, and that the people who'd raised him had known, or might have known, or might merely have *signed*?

He'd break. Or worse—he wouldn't. Worse, he'd go quiet in that particular way of his, the way he'd gone quiet in the alley seven years ago with his hands raised and the storm crawling under his skin, absorbing the blow so no one else had to. Ryn had watched him do it once already. She knew how it ended: with the hurt going somewhere deep and staying there, growing roots.

And yet. She'd named her price. *The name of whoever sold you.* He'd agreed. A debt unpaid was a chain, and Ryn Osk did not wear chains—not even the ones she forged herself out of kindness.

Besides, there was a second thing, the thing she kept circling back to as Corvane's lamps burned low around her and the river-smell rose off the docks. The consideration clause. *A favor, unlimited in scope, redeemable upon demand.* If the sigil was genuine—if someone in Thorn Valley truly held an open debt to the under-court—then the sale of Ulyana Quintana wasn't history. It was a live contract with a signature still attached, and signatures could be *used*. Whoever held that pen a generation ago could be made to hold it again. The court didn't need to hunt Ulyana across the wildlands if it could simply call in a favor and have the valley deliver him.

Which meant the question wasn't only *who sold him.*

The question was *who could still sell him*—and whether that person was, right now, sitting by a fire in Thorn Valley, watching the moons turn, waiting to see if the boy would come home.

Ryn turned toward the safehouse, and quickened her step.
