## 8. Hunters of the Hollow Moon

The river came out of nowhere, or so it seemed to Ulyana, who had spent twenty years in a valley where every stream was catalogued, named, and archived. Out here water simply *was*—braided silver across a gravel plain, loud and indifferent, running from mountains he couldn't see toward swamps he didn't want to imagine.

"Kessrin Ford," Sef said, squinting at his battered map. "The only crossing for thirty miles. Which means if anyone is looking for us, they'll be looking here."

"Then we don't cross here," Ryn said.

"We cross here," Sef said, "because the alternative is the marshes, and I have read four accounts of the marshes, and none of the authors survived to write a fifth."

Ryn looked at Ulyana. It was becoming her habit—deferring to him without ever admitting she was doing it. He hated it. He had spent two decades being no one's decision-maker, and now every choice he made seemed to leave fingerprints on other people's lives.

"The ford," Ulyana said finally. "At dusk. Low light, long shadows. We'll be hard to see."

"And easy to hear," Ryn muttered. But she checked her knives anyway, which was how she said yes.

They waited out the afternoon in a stand of birches above the ford, and Ulyana tried to sleep and could not. The storm-magic sat under his skin like a second heartbeat, restless since the day he'd crossed the wardline. In Thorn Valley it had been a rumor of a thing, a pressure behind his eyes on bad nights. Out here it was a caged animal that had learned the cage was open, and it threw itself against his ribs at every gust of wind, every change in pressure, every distant grumble of weather over the plains.

He was learning its moods the way you learn a stranger's. It woke when he was angry. It surged when he was afraid.

That last part frightened him most of all.

* * *

Dusk came down violet and slow. They crossed in single file, Ryn first, testing each stone with her boot before committing her weight, Sef behind her clutching his satchel of maps and notes above the waterline like a man carrying an infant through fire. Ulyana went last, as agreed—if anything came out of the dark, it would come for him first.

The river was colder than he expected, knee-deep and quick, tugging at his legs with small insistent hands. Halfway across, the birds stopped.

All of them. At once.

Ulyana froze mid-step, one foot on a slick stone, and felt the evening change texture. The air went thin and tasted faintly of copper, of old coins, of blood that had been left too long in an iron cup. The magic under his skin flattened itself against him, pressing away from something, the way a dog presses against the back of a kennel.

"Ryn," he breathed.

She'd already stopped. She stood mid-river, head tilted, listening to the same silence. Then, very quietly: "Sef. Get behind Ulyana."

"Why? What—" Sef's voice cracked on the last word.

The shadows on the far bank unfolded.

There were three of them, though Ulyana's eyes kept sliding off the count, insisting on two, then four, then two again. They were shaped like people the way a glove is shaped like a hand—correct in outline, empty in substance. Tall, narrow figures in travel-stained cloaks, faces pale as paper in the dying light. And their eyes—

Their eyes were wrong. Not blind, not glowing, but *hollow*, deep sockets that held darkness instead of sight, darkness that moved, that leaned toward him like a plant toward sun.

"Hollow-eyed," Sef whispered from the shallows. "Gods preserve us. That's what the old records called them. Hunters of the hollow moon. I thought—I assumed it was metaphor—"

One of them spoke. Its voice was dry leaves and well-oiled hinges, and it used words the way a butcher uses knives, precisely and without feeling.

"*Ulyana Quintana.*"

His name in its mouth sounded like a signature on a contract.

"You are named," the hunter said. "You are owed. Come, and the others go free. Remain, and we take our due where we find it." The hollow eyes drifted—not to Ulyana, but past him, to Sef, to Ryn, weighing them the way a merchant weighs coin against coin. "Souls are portable. Ours is not particular about packaging."

Ulyana's heart slammed against his ribs, and the magic answered it, rising like floodwater. He felt lightning gathering in the clouds above the far ridge—he hadn't called it, hadn't asked; his fear had reached up and pulled the sky down like a blanket.

"No," he managed.

"No?" The hunter tilted its head, a gesture almost human, almost curious. "The valley taught you to say no. The valley will not be there when we collect."

It stepped into the river.

* * *

Later, Ulyana would try to remember the fight as a sequence of decisions, and fail. There were no decisions. There was only fear, and fear made the storm.

Ryn moved first—a knife flashing, closing distance with the second hunter before it finished wading into the current. Steel met whatever passed for its flesh and sank in without effect; the thing didn't bleed, didn't stagger, simply caught her wrist with fingers cold enough to burn. She hissed and twisted free, losing skin.

The third hunter went for Sef.

Ulyana saw it happen the way you see disaster in dreams—clearly, completely, too slowly to stop. Sef scrambling backward through knee-deep water, satchel abandoned, arms raised as if papers could shield him. The hunter's hollow face bending toward him, and Sef screaming—not in pain yet, but in that raw terror of a man who feels himself being *looked into*, whose name is being read off the inside of his skull.

Something in Ulyana tore loose.

He had buried his magic for twenty years. He had never once, not in the worst nights of his childhood, not in the loneliest years of his exile, let it fully off the chain. Now it came up through him like a geyser, and the sky answered with a crack that split the dusk in half.

Lightning struck the river.

He felt it leave him and he felt it return, doubled—the bolt leaping from cloud to hunter to water to hunter, and the river carried it, and the river did not care who it touched. The first hunter came apart in a bloom of white light and a smell like scorched hair and rotting flowers. The second released Ryn and turned toward Ulyana, and he hit it with everything, all of it, the whole buried decade-and-more of him, thunder rolling down the valley flat enough to flatten grass for a mile.

And the third hunter—Sef's hunter—was standing in the splash-zone of a god's temper.

Ulyana saw the arc of the discharge reach for it and understood, in one horrible flash of clarity, exactly what was going to happen. He tried to pull the strike aside. He tried to bend it the way he'd bent breezes in the valley, small and careful things.

But he was afraid. He was afraid for Sef, afraid of the hunters, afraid of himself, and the magic fed on the fear and grew past his grip the way a river grows past a levee. The lightning went where the storm wanted. The storm wanted everything.

The world went white.

* * *

When his vision cleared, Ulyana was on his knees in the shallows, and the river steamed around him, and dead fish floated belly-up in a widening ring. Two hunters were ash and twisted cloak, dissolving into the current like ink. The third lay crumpled on the gravel bar, smoking faintly, its hollow eyes fixed on the sky.

On Sef.

Ulyana crawled to him through water gone warm as bathwater. "Sef. Sef, look at me—"

Sef was alive. That was the first mercy. His chest moved; his eyes, when they found Ulyana's, were still human, still terrified, still *there*. But his left side was burned from shoulder to hip, the flesh angry red and weeping where the discharge had kissed him on its way past, and his right arm hung wrong, dislocated at the shoulder from where he'd thrown himself sideways into the shallows.

"I'm fine," Sef said, and then laughed, a high unhinged sound. "I'm—gods, I'm not fine. That hurt. Why does it smell like flowers? Why does burning smell like *flowers*—"

"Shock," Ryn said. She was beside them suddenly, soaked to the bone, a raw red stripe across her wrist where the hunter had gripped her. Her hands were steady as she stripped off Sef's pack and cut away his scorched shirt. "Don't talk. Breathe."

Ulyana knelt in the steaming river and shook.

Not from cold. From the understanding settling over him like sediment. He had killed before tonight—in the sense that everyone kills something, eventually, insects, unintended things. But he had ended two thinking creatures with a thought and a scream, and worse, he had nearly ended a third, a friend, because his power answered fear the way a bell answers a hammer.

*When I am afraid, I am dangerous,* he thought. *And I am always afraid.*

"It knew my name," Sef was saying through gritted teeth as Ryn reset his shoulder; his yelp echoed off the banks. "The hunter. It looked at me and it knew—Ulyana, it knew things. It said souls are portable. What did it mean, packaging—"

"It meant it would have eaten yours to get to mine." Ulyana's voice came out flat and strange. "Named souls. It feeds on names. On what people are."

Ryn looked up sharply. "Then why did it die so easily?"

"Because it wasn't hunting me properly." Ulyana stared at the dissolving remains of the nearest hunter, at the ash threading downstream. "It was an ambush. A test. Three of them, at the one crossing we had to use—they knew where we'd be because someone told them where we'd be." He heard his own voice harden. "The court isn't sending soldiers. Soldiers want victory. These things want *me*, specifically, and they're willing to spend themselves cheap to learn how I fight."

Ryn was quiet a moment, wringing river water from her braid. "And what did they learn?"

Ulyana looked at his hands. They were steady now, which felt like a betrayal of everything they'd just done.

"That I'm strongest when I'm terrified," he said. "Which means every hunter they send from here on is going to make sure I'm terrified first."

* * *

They made camp upstream, in the birch stand, because moving Sef in the dark was impossible and staying near the ford was unthinkable. Ryn worked by knife-light, cleaning burns with the last of their clean spirits while Sef bit down on a leather strap and made small disciplined sounds. Ulyana sat apart, at the edge of the light, and watched the clouds he'd summoned break apart over the ridge, and felt the absence of them like a missing tooth.

"You should sleep," Ryn said, appearing beside him with two cups of willow-bark tea. She handed him one. "You killed two monsters and half-killed our archivist. That's a full day."

"I nearly killed him."

"You *saved* him." She said it simply, without softness, which was the only way she offered comfort. "Those things weren't going to chat him to death, Ulyana. You put lightning between Sef and something that eats souls. Bad aim beats no aim."

"He's burned because my aim *is* bad. Because I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "In the valley, I practiced controlling it. Little things. Static. Warm winds. I told myself I was keeping it leashed for everyone's safety. But tonight I learned the leash was never attached to me. It's attached to my fear. The calmer I am, the more I hold. The more afraid I get, the less of it is mine." He turned the cup in his hands. "And we're walking toward a mountain full of creatures whose entire profession is making people afraid."

Ryn considered this with the practical expression she wore for problems that could be stolen, bribed, or stabbed.

"Then we keep you calm," she said. "Or we keep you angry. Anger's cleaner than fear—you aimed better when you were furious than when you were scared. I've seen fighters work that way. Some people are best cold, some hot. Maybe you're a hot-weather creature."

"And if I burn Sef again? Or you?"

"Then you burn us," Ryn said, "and we survive it, because that's what we do, and afterward you get better. That's the whole art of survival, storm-caller. Doing it badly until you do it less badly." She drained her tea and stood. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch. And Ulyana—"

She paused at the edge of the firelight.

"When we get to Vhalen, and you find whoever sold you—and we *will* find them—try to be angry, not afraid. For all our sakes."

She left him alone with the cooling cup and the birches whispering overhead.

Across the camp, Sef slept fitfully, muttering fragments of archive-speech, catalog numbers and accession dates, his burned side wrapped in Ryn's spare shirt. Watching him, Ulyana felt the guilt settle deeper. Sef had come on this road to repay a debt. Instead he'd paid in skin.

Near midnight, Sef surfaced from a nightmare, gasping, and Ulyana crossed to sit beside him until the shaking stopped.

"They knew the ford," Sef whispered, once he could speak. "Ulyana, listen to me. I've been turning it over. Hollow-eyed hunters haven't been seen outside court territory in a century—the records are clear, they're bound to the mountain's shadow unless *released*. Someone released them along our route. Someone who knows the roads, knows the crossings, knows—" He stopped, and Ulyana watched the thought arrive in his face like a slow dawn.

"Knows how an exile thinks," Ulyana finished quietly. "Someone from home."

Sef said nothing. But his eyes went, briefly, helplessly, toward the southwest—toward Thorn Valley, toward the archive, toward everything they'd left behind—and Ulyana saw in that glance a door opening onto a room Sef very much did not want him to enter.

"Sef," Ulyana said. "What aren't you telling me?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere downriver, a night bird called once and fell silent, as if listening.

Sef opened his mouth. Closed it. And when he finally spoke, his voice was the voice of a man reading from a record he wished had been lost in a fire:

"There's something about the prophecy book you need to know. About where it came from. And who brought it to the valley." He swallowed. "I didn't lie to you, Ulyana. I just... didn't tell you all of it. And after tonight, I don't think I can afford the difference anymore."
