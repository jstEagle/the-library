## 10. The City That Remembers Him

The rain found them three days out of Corvane, and for once it wasn't his.

Ulyana stood at the edge of the road and let it fall on him like a stranger's hand—cool, indifferent, belonging to no one. He had almost forgotten what that felt like. In Thorn Valley the weather had never touched him without also *knowing* him; here, past the wardline, rain was just rain, and he was just a man getting wet on a road that smelled of mud and woodsmoke.

"You're smiling," Sef said, hunched under an oilcloth he'd bought with coin he pretended not to be embarrassed about. "I haven't seen you smile since the river."

"I'm not smiling."

"You are. It's very unsettling. Stop it."

Ryn walked ahead of them both, hood up, reading the road the way she read everything—sideways, suspiciously, looking for the trick in it. She hadn't spoken much since the ambush at the crossing. Since Sef's wound. The bandage beneath his coat was changed twice a day now, and each time Ryn did it without being asked, her mouth a flat line, as if she were punishing the injury personally.

Ulyana let the rain keep falling. Somewhere behind them, in the dark of the last week, there was a dead man's face he still saw when he closed his eyes—the hollow-eyed hunter coming apart in a bloom of white fire and thunder, the smell of ozone and something worse underneath. He didn't tell them about the dreams. He told no one anything anymore, except when it was necessary, and even then he measured the words like a miser.

But the rain was not his, and he was smiling, and for half a morning that was enough.

* * *

They smelled the camp before they saw it: woodsmoke and wet wool and something cooking—barley, maybe, or turnips gone soft with too much water. Then they crested the ridge above the river bend and Ulyana stopped walking so abruptly that Sef nearly collided with his back.

Tents. Dozens of them. Hundreds. A ragged city of canvas and patched hide spilling down the slope toward the water, ringed with cookfires whose smoke rose straight and gray into the low sky. Carts with broken axles leaned against wagons with missing wheels. Children ran between the fires, shrieking some game whose rules existed only for them. And over everything, stitched onto banners and painted on cart-sides and sewn into shoulder-cloths in thread that had once been green—

A thorn branch, flowering.

"That's—" Sef's voice caught. "Those are valley colors."

"I can see that," Ulyana said.

He could see more than that. He could see the way the tents were pitched in the old pattern, hearth-fires to the east, latrines downwind, the sick kept apart near the water where the air moved cleanest. He could see the way elders sat in a crescent near the largest fire, the way the young men watched the road with the particular vigilance of people who had learned, recently and painfully, that roads bring soldiers. This was not a camp. This was Thorn Valley, folded small enough to carry, unfolded again on a riverbank two hundred miles from home.

"Why are they here?" Ryn asked quietly. "The wards—"

"The wards flickered," Ulyana said. "Before I left. Maren said they'd held for a century." He looked at the camp, at the thorn-flower banners, and felt something cold slide down his spine and pool in his stomach. "Maybe they're not holding anymore."

They might have walked around. There was a ford downstream, a path through the trees that would have taken them past the camp without a single greeting exchanged. Ryn pointed it out. Sef, practical to the bone, agreed.

Ulyana heard himself say, "We need supplies," and knew, even as he said it, that it was only half a lie. The other half was older than the prophecy, older than the sale, older than his own name: *I want to go home, even if home is only a copy.*

Ryn looked at him for a long moment. "Fine," she said. "But we don't tell anyone what you are."

* * *

It lasted less than an hour.

They came in through the eastern edge, heads down, asking nothing but directions to the quartermaster's tent. Ulyana kept his hood up and his hands in his pockets and his magic folded down inside him so tight it ached, like holding his breath underwater. For twenty years in the valley he had done the opposite—held the storm *out*, kept it from touching him—and now, outside, he had to hold himself in, and neither discipline fit him properly. He was a man wearing someone else's posture.

The quartermaster was a broad woman with burn scars up one arm who traded them dried meat and a new bandage-roll for two of Ryn's coins and didn't ask questions, which should have been the end of it.

Then the sky broke.

It was nothing—a spring squall rolling off the hills, the kind of weather that happened everywhere and belonged to no one. But the wind shifted, and the first fat drops came down hard, and somewhere deep in Ulyana's chest the storm answered before he could stop it. Not called. *Answered.* His skin prickled. The hair on his arms lifted. The pressure dropped so fast his ears popped, and the nearest cookfire guttered sideways, and every drop of rain within thirty paces bent, just slightly, toward him—toward the thing inside him that had been starved for twenty years and had never learned manners.

An old man by the fire looked up. Looked at the bending rain. Looked at Ulyana.

"Storm-caller," the old man said. Not a question. A recognition, the way you'd name a bird by its song.

The word went through the camp like a spark through dry grass. Ulyana felt it move—felt the attention of hundreds of people swing toward him all at once, a physical weight, a tide. He pulled his hood lower. He turned to leave. And a woman stepped into his path with a child on her hip and tears already running down her face, and she said:

"Please. Please, we saw the storms on the pass, three nights ago—we thought, we *hoped*—you're from the valley. You're one of ours. The wards are failing, do you understand? They flicker like a dying lamp. The things that used to stay outside—" Her voice cracked. "My sister stayed behind. My sister is *still there*. Please. Stay. Protect us. Protect *them*."

Behind her, the camp had gone quiet. Hundreds of faces. The thorn-flower banners dripping in the rain. An elder rising slowly from the crescent by the great fire, one hand pressed to her heart.

Ulyana opened his mouth to say *no*, and found that the word had somehow been stolen from him somewhere along the road, sometime between the river crossing and this moment, and he could not find it anywhere in his mouth.

"They said there were no storm-callers left," someone murmured. "They said the valley's luck died with the last one."

"He's not the last," the old man by the fire said softly. "Look at him. He's the *first*."

* * *

They gave him a tent at the center of the camp, which was the worst possible place, because it meant everyone could find him.

They brought him food he hadn't tasted in twenty years—black bread with caraway, honey from valley hives carried in jars across two hundred miles of hostile country, tea brewed with pine needles the way his mother had brewed it, the way everyone in Thorn Valley brewed it because proper leaves cost more than a goat. They brought him their names. That was the part that undid him. One by one, all evening, they came to the tent flap and gave him their names and the names of the people they'd left behind, pressing them into his hands like coins, like he could carry them, like carrying them was the same as saving them.

Danya, whose brother was a warden on the north line. Old Pell, who had taught half the camp's children their letters before the flickering started. The scarred quartermaster, Bren, who said only, "You knew my mother. Marisol. She mended your coats." Ulyana did remember Marisol. He remembered her laughing at how fast he wore through elbows.

Sitting in the lamplight among the gifts and the names, Ulyana understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had spent twenty years believing he had no home—that exile had burned it out of him—but home had never been a place. Home was a list of names, and he was holding it, and it was heavier than any storm.

"You have to eat," Ryn said from the corner, where she'd installed herself hours ago with a knife she was cleaning and re-cleaning. "And you have to stop making that face."

"What face?"

"The face of a man about to promise something stupid."

"I haven't promised anything."

"You will." She nodded at the tent flap, where shadows of waiting people shifted in the firelight. "They're not asking you for supplies, Ulyana. They're asking you to be a wall. And walls get broken, and whoever's standing under them when they come down gets buried first." Her knife paused. "I've seen it. In Corvane, when the flood took the low quarter—the man they'd begged to hold the levee. You know what they built him? A statue. You know what else? He drowned."

"I'm not going to drown."

"No," Ryn said. "You're going to do something worse. You're going to *want* this." She sheathed the knife and looked at him, and for once there was no irony anywhere in her face. "One night. Let them have one night. Tomorrow we walk, and you hate yourself for a month, and you live. Deal?"

Outside, the rain had stopped. Someone had begun to sing—one of the old valley songs, the harvest-round that children sang at the turning of the year, the melody worn smooth by generations of mouths. More voices joined it. The whole camp, singing in the wet dark, and the sound rose off the riverbank and into the hills like smoke with nowhere to go.

"One night," Ulyana said.

* * *

He went out among the fires, and it was the best night of his life, which is why he should have known.

He sang the harvest-round until his voice gave out. He danced the clumsy figure-dance with Bren's daughters and got the steps wrong and was corrected loudly by children a third his age. He sat with the elders and listened to news of the valley—the flickering wards, the cold spots where the hedge-magic had gone thin, the way animals had begun leaving the deep woods as if something on the far side of the border had started breathing differently. He healed a fevered child without meaning to, almost without noticing: the storm in him reaching out gentle as a nurse and shaking the sickness loose like water from a coat, and the mother weeping over him, and the elders exchanging looks over his head that he was too happy to read correctly.

For one night he was wanted. Not tolerated, not hidden, not *managed* the way Elder Maren had managed him for twenty years with her careful rules and her careful silences. Wanted. Needed. The storm-caller had come back, and the storm-caller would stand at the wardline and the sky would listen, and everything would be as it was in the stories.

He lay in his borrowed tent near midnight, warm and full and surrounded by the sounds of sleeping people, and thought: *this is what it would have been like. If I'd never been sold. If the prophecy had never found me. If I were just a man from a valley, with a valley's worth of people around me.*

He fell asleep smiling, and the smile was still on his face when the screaming started.

* * *

It was the dogs that woke the camp first—not barking but *whining*, a low collective moan of animal dread rolling through the tents like fog. Ulyana was on his feet before his mind caught up, and the second thing he registered was the smell: a sweetness under the woodsmoke, cloying and wrong, like flowers rotting in standing water.

Hollow Moon. He knew the scent now. He would know it in his grave.

"Hunters," Ryn breathed beside him. She was already dressed, already armed, already moving. "Three. Maybe four. They circled wide—they've been tracking us since the river, they must have picked up the trail when we—"

"When we walked into a camp of four hundred people and announced ourselves," Sef said grimly, fumbling with his boot. His wound had torn open; there was fresh blood on his shirt. "Ulyana. Listen to me. They're not here for the camp. They're here for *you*. If you run, they follow you and the camp is spared. If you fight—"

"If I fight, they die anyway," Ulyana said. "You saw what they feed on. Named souls. And every soul in this camp just spent the evening giving me their names."

The first scream came from the eastern fires.

He ran.

What he found at the edge of the camp he would spend the rest of his life trying not to see: the hunters moving through the firelight like holes cut out of the world, tall and wrong-jointed, their hollow eyes drinking the light instead of reflecting it. People fled around them and the hunters ignored them, mostly—ignored them the way a man ignores furniture while walking toward the door. Except where the fear was thickest. Fear had a flavor, and named souls had a scent, and the hunters drifted toward the densest clusters of terror with the patience of things that had never once needed to hurry.

Ulyana called the storm.

It came the way it always came now—too fast, too much, a lifetime of dammed power breaking loose at once—and the sky above the camp lit white from horizon to horizon. Thunder slammed down flat and enormous. Rain came sideways. And the hunters turned, all of them, as one creature, and looked at him with their empty eyes, and began to move toward him with something that was almost eagerness.

"YES!" he screamed at them, into the wind, arms spread, lightning crawling up his spine and out through his fingertips. "HERE! *ME!*"

They came. Of course they came. And the storm met them, and it was terrible and bright and it worked—the first hunter came apart in a detonation of white fire, the second staggered, burning—and Ulyana laughed, wild and afraid, because he was winning, he was protecting them, this was the thing the camp had begged him for—

And then a torch, knocked loose by the wind, landed in a pile of dry canvas.

That was all. That was the whole catastrophe. One torch, one gust of a storm-wind he had summoned, one tent going up like a struck match, and then the next, and the next, because the camp was made of cloth and desperation and the rain that should have saved it was falling too hard and too fast, hammering sparks into flying embers that scattered on the gale and seeded new fires faster than any bucket line could kill them.

"NO—" He grabbed at the storm, tried to gent it, tried to make the rain soft and the wind still, but the storm was a living thing and it was frightened, and frightened storms do not listen. "STOP, *stop*—"

People ran through the smoke, and the hunters ran among the people, and somewhere in the roaring orange chaos a child's voice cried out and Ulyana lunged toward it and a hunter's claw raked across his raised arm, opening him from elbow to wrist, and the pain hit his magic like a whip hits a horse. The sky *detonated*. Every drop of rain in the air flash-boiled. The shockwave flattened tents in a ring fifty paces wide and blew the remaining hunters backward into the dark like dolls.

Silence, after. Just fire, eating.

Ulyana knelt in the mud with his arm pouring blood and looked at what remained of the City That Remembered Him. The great fire was a black skeleton. The thorn-flower banners hung in charred ribbons. Bodies lay in rows where the survivors had begun laying them, and the survivors themselves stood in the rain with ash running down their faces, and they were not looking at the hunters' corpses.

They were looking at him.

Bren the quartermaster pushed through the crowd, her burned arm cradled against her chest, and stopped ten feet away, and Ulyana waited for her to curse him, to raise a hand against him, to give him something he could deserve.

She didn't. That was the worst of it. She just looked at him with an expression of such vast, exhausted grief and said, "Go. Please. Before they come back. Before—" Her voice failed. She gestured at everything. "*Before more of us learn your name.*"

He went.

Ryn found him a mile downstream, sitting in the shallows with his ruined arm in the current, watching the glow of the burning camp stain the clouds. She didn't say *I told you so*. She sat down beside him in the freezing water, which was its own kind of sentence, and after a long time she said:

"There's a village two days east. Burned out years ago—nobody will look for us there. We rest, we bind that arm, and then we go up the mountain road." She paused. "Ulyana. Look at me. Whatever they made you feel tonight—that was real. What they're using it for isn't. Vessarine sent those hunters knowing exactly what you'd do. You weren't the weapon tonight. You were the *ammunition*."

Ulyana stared at the burning sky and felt the truth of it settle into him like a stone into a well. The court hadn't just sent hunters. The court had sent hunters to a camp full of people who loved him, and let arithmetic do the rest.

"They'll write songs about tonight," he said hoarsely. "In the valley. About the storm-caller who came back and couldn't save them."

"Probably," Ryn said. "Songs are cheap. So are prophecies." She stood, and offered him her good hand, and pulled him up out of the water. "Come on. Thirteen turns, remember? We've lost one. We don't get to lose another."

Far behind them, the camp glowed like a wound in the night. And far ahead, barely visible through the rain, Mount Vhalen waited under the clouds—patient, cold, and, Ulyana now understood, already counting.
