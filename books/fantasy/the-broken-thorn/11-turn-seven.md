## 11. Turn Seven

The cipher broke on the seventh night because Ryn Osk had never once in her life done the honest thing first.

She'd been picking at it for three weeks, the way she picked at locks—patiently, sideways, with her tongue caught between her teeth and her fingers remembering shapes her mind hadn't learned yet. The ledger sat open on her knees in the firelight, its vellum gone soft as old skin, and she'd copied every symbol twice onto scraps of birchbark until the scraps covered the ground around her like fallen leaves. Sef slept sitting up, as he always did, his back against a deadfall, an open record-book sliding off his chest. Ulyana was out at the tree line doing whatever it was he did now that his storms had come back to him—talking to the sky, or arguing with it. The wind carried the smell of coming rain and something underneath it, ozone and iron, that Ryn had learned meant *he's thinking too hard again*.

The cipher wasn't a cipher. That was the joke of it, the ugly little joke she'd finally laughed at alone by the fire. It was a merchant's shorthand—the kind brokers used in Corvane when they didn't want their clerks reading over their shoulders. Prices compressed into knots. Dates folded into flourishes. She'd spent weeks hunting for hidden meaning in what was simply the private scrawl of people who sold souls the way farmers sold pigs.

The seller's mark was in the margin of the sale entry, pressed into the wax like a thumbprint: a thorn circled by a crescent, with three small notches cut into the crescent's inner curve.

Ryn knew that mark. Everyone from Thorn Valley knew that mark. It hung on the archive door. It was stamped into the ledgers children were taught to swear oaths on. Elder Maren wore it carved into the walking stick she'd used since before Ulyana Quintana was born.

She looked at it for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, thunder muttered like a dog dreaming.

"No," she said aloud, to no one. "No, that's wrong."

But her hands were already moving, copying the mark onto fresh bark, setting it beside the one burned into her memory from a hundred childhood oaths. Three notches. The valley's seal had three notches—she could see it behind her eyes, plain as her own name. The famine year. The year the wells went bitter and half the eastern terraces died, the year Maren had stood in the square and told them the wards would hold, *the wards will hold*, and the wards had held, and everyone had wept with relief and never once asked what holding cost.

Ryn sat very still while the pieces arranged themselves, the way they always did, the way stolen things fell into place in her hands whether she wanted them to or not.

Ulyana came back down the slope before she'd decided anything. He moved differently these days—looser, louder, taking up space in the world like a man who'd just discovered the world had room in it. Rain followed him down the hill in a thin silver curtain that stopped precisely at the edge of the firelight, because he was showing off, because he could, because twenty years of buried magic made even drizzle feel like a miracle.

"You're making that face," he said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've found something and you're deciding how much it'll hurt me."

Ryn closed the ledger. Then opened it again, because hiding it felt worse than showing it, and she was many kinds of thief but she'd never been good at being a coward. "Sit down."

He sat. He was getting better at reading her too; she hated that. His eyes went to the ledger, to the bark scraps, to the margin, and she watched him find the mark before she could say a word—watched his whole body go quiet the way a forest goes quiet before wind.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's the valley seal."

"Three notches. Crescent. Thorn." She turned the page toward him. "Pressed into the wax on the sale entry. Dated the famine year. Your soul, Ulyana. Sold from inside your own home."

The rain he'd brought down the hill kept falling past the firelight, patient as a debt. Ulyana didn't move for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, flattened, the voice of a man carrying something heavy across ice. "It's forged."

"Could be."

"It *is*. Someone wanted us to find this. Someone wanted me looking at Maren instead of—" He gestured vaguely uphill, toward the mountain they couldn't see yet but all of them felt, the way you feel a tooth that's gone bad. "Think about it. A broker's vault. A ledger left where a thief would steal it. It's bait, Ryn."

"I stole it out of a locked vault under a floor trap I nearly died disarming," Ryn said. "If it's bait, someone spent thirty years aging this vellum and learning Corvane merchant shorthand to season it." She held up the bark scrap with her copy of the seal. "And if it's forged, the forger knew the valley seal down to the notches. How many people outside Thorn Valley have ever seen those notches?"

"Someone who wanted to frame her."

"*Frame* her?" Ryn heard her own voice climb and dragged it back down. "Ulyana. Listen to what you're saying. You want it to be forged so badly you're not hearing it. The famine year. The year the wells went bad. The year Maren stood in the square swearing the wards would hold—and they did. Nobody asked why. Nobody ever asks why in that valley. We just thank the wards and keep our heads down and call it sanctuary."

"She raised me." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and he hated that too; she saw him hate it. "She taught me my letters. She's the one who told me to hide my magic, who kept me safe—"

"Or she's the one who knew exactly what needed hiding, and why, and what it had already cost you." Ryn gentled despite herself. This was the part she was worst at, the part where truth and mercy pulled opposite directions on the same rope. "I'm not saying she's a monster. I'm saying maybe she traded your soul-half to buy the wards through a famine, and then spent twenty years keeping you small and secret so the court would never come collect early. Maybe keeping you invisible *was* the payment plan."

Sef stirred against the deadfall. They both froze, guilty as children, but he only blinked at the fire, murmured something about catalog numbers, and slid back under.

"He knew," Ulyana said suddenly. "Sef knew. He's been dodging this ledger for weeks. Every time I ask about provenance he starts talking about binding glue."

"Sef knows everything and admits nothing; that's the whole man." But Ryn filed it away, another piece clicking into place. "Look. There's a third option nobody wants."

"Say it."

"Both things are true. She saved the valley *and* she sold you. People do that. People do it constantly. That's the trick of it—that's what makes it unforgivable and understandable in the same breath." Ryn poked the fire harder than it needed. "My mother sold my brother's boots to feed the rest of us. I hated her for years. Then I got hungry enough to understand, and then I hated myself instead. That's what this is. Except the boots were your soul."

Ulyana stared into the fire. The rain beyond the light had thickened without his noticing—he'd lost hold of it, which he never did anymore, which told her more than his face did. "Thirteen turns," he said finally. "We're at seven. If I go back and put this in front of her—"

"She'll deny it, or she'll explain it, and either way you'll learn something you can't unlearn."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you walk into that mountain not knowing whose hand signed you away, and Vessarine will tell you. Oh, she'll *tell* you. Ladies like that don't win with armies; they win with the version of the truth that breaks you best." Ryn leaned forward. "Better it comes from us. Better you choose which wound to carry."

Ulyana was silent long enough that the fire burned down a notch. When he looked up, there was something new in his face—not the boy from the valley anymore, not quite the storm-caller either. Something deciding itself.

"We wake Sef," he said. "Now. Tonight. He's going to tell us where this ledger really came from, and this time he doesn't get to talk about binding glue."

* * *

Sef Idris woke badly and lied immediately, which Ryn noted with something close to professional respect.

"Came from a broker's estate," he said, smoothing his rumpled coat. "Corvane. Standard acquisition, I told you—"

"You told me nothing," Ulyana said. "You've told me nothing for three weeks. You flinch every time I touch this book." He set the ledger down between them, open to the margin, open to the seal. "So look at it. Look at it and tell me the valley's seal is forged, Sef. Tell me you believe that."

Sef looked. Ryn watched him look—the long pause, the way his thumb found the edge of the page and worried it, the particular stillness of a man measuring the distance between two lies and finding it too far to jump.

"The notching is correct," he said at last, quietly. "Period-accurate. The wax compound matches valley apiaries—I'd know the scent anywhere; I sealed a thousand records with it." He lifted his eyes, and for once there was no archivist's deflection in them, only exhaustion. "I can't certify it forged. I've been trying for three weeks. I'm rather good at proving documents false. It's how I disgraced myself, as it happens—proving the wrong document false, for the wrong people."

"So it's real." Ulyana's voice was flat.

"Real isn't the same as true." Sef held up a hand before either of them could speak. "Marks can be transferred. Seals can be borrowed—a favor called in, a stamp applied by another hand entirely. What I can certify is that this entry was written in the famine year, by someone with access to the valley's seal, recording the sale of half a soul belonging to *Quintana, unborn*, purchased by Lady Vessarine of the under-court, payment rendered as—one moment—" he turned pages with an archivist's precision, though his fingers weren't steady— "'one favor, unspecified, redeemable at need, holder: the seller.' The seller's mark is the valley seal. The holder of the favor is unnamed." He closed the book gently, like closing a coffin. "Whoever sold you received something in return. Something they could call in later. Something perhaps called in during a famine, when the wells went bitter and the wards were failing."

The fire crackled. Above them, Ulyana's forgotten rain had become a proper storm, working itself down the far ridge in slow rolls of grey thunder, and none of them moved to stop it.

"Maren's favor," Ryn said. "Called in during the famine year. The wards held. Everyone lived." She looked at Ulyana. "Everyone except whoever paid."

"My parents." He said it like a word in a language he was still translating. "They sold me before I was born. And Maren—" His jaw worked. "Maren held the pen? Or Maren held the seal?"

"Does it matter?" Ryn asked, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Ulyana turned on her with something raw in his face.

"Does it *matter*? Of course it matters. If she held the pen, she chose. If she held the seal, she lent it—maybe under pressure, maybe deceived, maybe—" He stopped. Breathed. The storm overhead surged; loose branches came down the slope clattering. "Maybe she spent twenty years raising the child she helped sell because she couldn't undo it and wouldn't abandon him. Maybe that's worse. Maybe that's better. I don't know which."

"That," Sef said softly, "is the question that will follow you up the mountain. Not *was it done*—the court will show you the contract, they keep immaculate records, it's the one virtue of monsters—but *what it was worth*. Whether a valley full of lives balances against half of one soul that hadn't drawn breath yet." He shook his head slowly. "I've catalogued ten thousand cruelties in that archive, Ulyana. The ones that haunt me aren't the cruel ones. They're the reasonable ones."

Ryn watched Ulyana absorb that, watched the storm above them mirror it—building, fracturing, unable to commit to falling. And she understood, with the clarity that sometimes came to her in the middle of a job, right before everything went wrong, that this was the fracture point. Not hunters, not mountains, not courts. This. The group standing around a fire with a truth between them like a blade laid flat on a table, each of them deciding whether to pick it up.

Because here was what she didn't say, what she kept behind her teeth where she kept everything that mattered: she'd read further than the seal. Down at the bottom of the entry, in the compressed merchant-knots she'd decoded alone by firelight, was the redemption clause—the terms under which the sold soul-half could be reclaimed. And the terms required the *seller's consent*. The holder of the favor. Whoever bore that mark, or whoever had borrowed it, still held a key to Ulyana's own soul, and Ryn didn't know yet whether that key belonged to a traitor or a woman who'd spent two decades paying interest on a debt she'd never confess.

Either way, they couldn't finish this without going back. Or without going forward and letting Lady Vessarine open the door herself.

The storm broke at last—not over them, Ulyana pulling it apart with visible effort, scattering it into harmless rain across the ridgeline—and in the sudden quiet Sef gathered his books and said, "We should reach the pass within four turns. After that, questions become souvenirs; the court collects them."

Ulyana nodded and walked to the edge of the firelight, standing where the rain met the dark, a silhouette stitched out of lightning-flash. Ryn stayed by the coals, the ledger heavy in her lap, heavier than any lockbox she'd ever lifted.

Above the trees, through a tear in the clouds, the seventh moon rode thin and sharp as a paring knife.

Half gone. Half left. And somewhere ahead, in a cathedral of bone and lamplight beneath Mount Vhalen, something was singing in a voice that Ryn was beginning to suspect the whole world would soon recognize as Ulyana Quintana's.
