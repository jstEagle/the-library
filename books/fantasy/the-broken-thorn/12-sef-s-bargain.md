## 12. Sef's Bargain

The intermediary came to the market at Vell's Crossing on the last day of every month, and everyone pretended not to know it.

Sef had learned this the way he learned most things—by reading what other people left behind. A chalk mark on the third stall post, refreshed monthly. A butcher who suddenly stopped asking questions about the quality of his own meat. A beggar with too-clean hands who never begged, only watched the crowd from beneath the eaves of the tannery. The court beneath Mount Vhalen did not send heralds or riders into the lowlands. It sent clerks.

He told himself he was going for information. That was the archivist's lie, the one that had carried him through twenty years of quiet disgrace: *I am only observing. I am only cataloguing. I am not doing anything.* He had used it when he falsified the flood-year ledgers, and he was using it now as he walked through Vell's Crossing with a satchel of documents he had no right to carry and a speech rehearsed so many times in his head that the words had worn smooth as river stones.

Ulyana was asleep two miles back at the ruined mill, Ryn keeping watch whether she admitted she was keeping watch or not. The moon-charm Maren had given him hung against Ulyana's chest, six turns waned, seven remaining. Seven turns until the thirteenth, and the prophecy book back in Thorn Valley sat open on its shelf like a mouth waiting to be fed.

Sef could not stop seeing the page. He had read it first, after all—or rather, he had read it last, after a century of apprentices and juniors had shelved it wrong, mislabeled it, dusted around it. *The storm-child sold before breath.* His own handwriting was in the accession ledger beside it: *Received under seal, provenance withheld by order of the Elder Council.* He had been twenty-three years old and proud of the neatness of his letters.

He had helped bury the boy's whole life in a catalogue entry, and now the boy was walking toward the mountain to die on schedule.

So: information. And if information happened to lead somewhere else, well. Archivists were trained to follow a document wherever it went.

* * *

Vell's Crossing smelled of fish brine and wet wool, a river town that existed because the bridge existed and would drown within a generation when the bridge failed. Sef arrived an hour before dusk and bought nothing, touched nothing, simply stood at the edge of the market square with the posture of a man waiting for someone who was late.

The clerk found him, as Sef had calculated he would. They always noticed the ones who waited without buying. Waiting without purpose was the loudest thing a person could do in a market.

"You're standing in my light," said a voice at his shoulder.

Sef turned. The intermediary was smaller than he'd imagined—a narrow person in a grey coat the color of slate, face unremarkable in the way of official portraits, eyes the color of lamp-oil. Not young. Not old. The kind of face that appeared in no witness's description twice.

"I'm sorry," Sef said. "I'm looking for—"

"No, you're not." The clerk smiled thinly. "You've been rehearsing a speech since the bridge. I could see your lips moving. Come somewhere private before you say something expensive."

They walked to a room above the ferry house, a rented chamber with a table, two chairs, and a candle the clerk lit with a match struck on their thumbnail. Sef sat because his knees told him to. The clerk remained standing, adjusting the candle flame with one finger until it burned perfectly straight.

"Now," the intermediary said. "You have the look of Thorn Valley about you. Soft hands, ink stains, that particular way of flinching when someone says the word *mountain*. Let me save us both the hour of preamble." They folded their hands. "You want to make an offer. People who come to me always want to make an offer. Speak."

Sef's rehearsed speech dissolved. What came out instead was the truth, stripped of all its careful architecture:

"His name is Ulyana Quintana. He was sold before his birth. I know the contract exists—I've seen references in the accession records, the payment structure, the sealing formula. I'm offering myself in his place. Same terms, same price, same everything. Substitute the named party. I'm volunteering."

Silence. The candle flame leaned slightly, though there was no draft.

Then the intermediary laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh, which somehow made it worse. It was the laugh of a customs officer hearing a delightful joke from a traveler—warm, brief, entirely without mercy. They laughed with their whole narrow body, wiped the corner of one oil-colored eye, and then looked at Sef with something close to affection.

"Oh," they said. "Oh, you sweet, doomed little paper-man. You thought it was a name on a list. You thought we could cross one out and write another." They pulled out the chair across from Sef at last and sat, leaning forward, elbows on the table, the posture of a teacher addressing a promising but hopeless student. "Let me explain how a soul-contract works, since apparently no one in that valley of yours reads the foundational texts anymore."

Sef said nothing. His heart was hammering in a way he noted, distantly, as bad for the composition of one's thoughts.

"A soul is not property," the intermediary continued. "Property can be transferred. A soul is a *signature*. When Lady Vessarine purchased half of Ulyana Quintana's soul, she did not buy a thing—she bought a *clause in his existence*, written before he drew breath, sealed in blood-price, witnessed by powers whose names I am not paid enough to pronounce. The contract does not say *a storm-caller*. It does not say *the child of Quintana*. It says *him*. This soul, this exact fracture of this exact self, signed into being at the moment of his conception. There is no substitution clause. There has never been a substitution clause. Do you know why?"

"Why?" Sef managed.

"Because souls are specific." The intermediary spread their hands. "You cannot pay a debt in another man's coin. If you gave me your soul—freely, gladly, tonight—I would have to decline. Politely. With paperwork." They smiled again. "And believe me, the paperwork would be enormous."

The candle burned straight between them. Sef heard his own voice come out small and dry: "Then there's nothing. No mechanism. No appeal?"

"Appeals." The intermediary savored the word. "There are petitions. There are amendments. There is, in cases of demonstrable fraud in the original execution, a nullification process that takes roughly ninety years and requires the testimony of both original signatories, one of whom—" they consulted some inner ledger, "—is dead, and the other of whom is a valley. Valleys testify very slowly."

Sef put his hands flat on the table to keep them still. "Then I've wasted your time."

"Not at all." The intermediary's tone shifted, and Sef felt the change like a drop in air pressure before a storm. "Because you said something interesting. You said *same price*. Which means you've read far enough into the contract to know there *was* a price beyond the soul itself. Which means you know about the favor."

The room got colder. Or Sef did.

"The payment clause," he said carefully.

"Mm." The intermediary produced, from inside the grey coat, a slim folio bound in leather gone soft with age. They did not open it. They simply rested one hand upon it, the way a gambler rests a hand on a winning card. "Every contract has consideration. The soul-half was the *object* of sale. But the *payment*—what Lady Vessarine gave in exchange—that was a favor. One favor, freely given, redeemable at any time within a hundred years of the signing, by the bearer of the seller's mark." Their lamp-oil eyes fixed on Sef. "Ninety-one years remain. The favor has not been called. Do you understand what I'm telling you, archivist?"

"That the debt runs both directions," Sef whispered.

"That the debt is *still awake*." The intermediary tapped the folio once. "Your Elder Council sold a child's soul to buy something. Wards, I'd wager—the famine year, the hollow winter, whatever story makes it bearable. And in exchange, the valley holds a promissory note from the under-court itself. A favor. Any favor. To be named by whoever carries the seller's seal." They leaned back. "Someone in Thorn Valley could walk to that mountain tomorrow and ask for anything within the court's power. An end to the hunters. A pardon for every exile. The burning of the prophecy book. Even—" and here the intermediary's smile turned delicate, surgical, "—the release of a certain soul, perhaps, if the favor were framed correctly and the lawyers were feeling generous. It would take years. It might fail. But it exists. It is *live*. And here you sit, having offered me your entire self, unaware that the instrument of his rescue may be sitting in your Elder's writing desk."

Sef's mind was moving fast now, faster than it had moved in twenty years, pulling threads. Maren's seal. Maren, who had read the same page and said nothing for however long she'd known. Maren, who had pressed the moon-charm into Ulyana's hand with a warning shaped like a curse. Maren, who during the famine year had traveled alone to the lowlands for eleven days and returned with grain that should not have existed and a new lock on her strongbox.

Or it was forged. Ryn believed it was forged. Ryn, who trusted nothing that benefited anyone in power, would already be three arguments deep into why the seal was a trap.

But the intermediary had no reason to lie. Lying cost clerks their positions; Sef knew bureaucracies, and bureaucracies punished invention far more harshly than they punished cruelty.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

The intermediary considered the question honestly, which frightened Sef more than any evasion could have.

"Because I find the contract distasteful," they said at last. "Pre-birth sales are vulgar. Old money, older magic, no craft in it at all. And because in four hundred years of service I have watched perhaps sixty people walk into that mountain to honor a claim, and not one of them ever sent someone ahead to offer themselves first." They rose, smoothing the grey coat. "You are the first. It was badly reasoned, legally illiterate, and—" the pause was almost gentle, "—the finest thing anyone has said in my hearing this decade. So. Professional courtesy. The favor lives. The seal calls it. Ninety-one years. Tell your storm-child to hurry."

They walked to the door, then paused, hand on the latch.

"One more courtesy, free of charge. Your friends will ask where you went tonight. You'll tell them something—men like you always do, some half-truth worn smooth with practice. I'd advise you to choose it carefully." The lamp-oil eyes glinted. "Because the next time I see you, archivist, I suspect it will be across a negotiating table, and I would hate for our relationship to begin in dishonesty."

The door closed. The candle kept burning straight.

* * *

Sef did not go back to the mill.

He told himself he was being cautious—doubling back, watching the road, making sure no hunter had marked the town—but the truth was that he sat on the riverbank below the ferry house until the moon rose, turning the night over in his hands like a document with a damaged seal.

*Maren.*

He made himself build the case the way he'd been trained: evidence first, conclusion last. The famine year. Eleven days absent. Grain that outlasted the granaries of three neighboring provinces. The strongbox that no junior was ever permitted to shelve near. Her reaction to the prophecy book—not shock, not confusion, but a kind of grim *recognition*, the look of a woman rereading her own signature. She had given Ulyana the charm and the warning, yes. But she had also let him sleep through five turns of the moon believing his exile was accident rather than arithmetic.

And yet—and here Sef's training betrayed him, insisting on the counter-argument even as his chest tightened—if she had sold him, why arm him? Why warn him? Why let him leave at all? A woman protecting a purchase would have kept him in the valley, kept him ignorant, delivered him quietly when the thirteenth turn came. Unless the warning *was* the delivery. Unless sending him to the mountain was simply honoring the contract on schedule while wearing the face of mercy.

Both readings fit the evidence. That was the horror of archives; Sef had spent his life among documents that supported two opposite truths with equal elegance, and he had never once been permitted to forget that the difference between them was usually a person's character, and character left no accession record.

Ryn would say: *she's guilty, people with seals are always guilty, burn the seal and run.*

Ulyana would say—no. Sef didn't know what Ulyana would say. That was the worst of it. Twenty years of watching the boy from across the archive room, twenty years of silence purchased with Sef's own falsified pages, and he realized he had no idea what Ulyana Quintana would say when he learned that his whole life might have been a line item in someone else's survival.

The moon climbed. Six turns waned. Somewhere north, the mountain waited with its cathedral of lamplight and glass, and somewhere south, a valley full of exiles slept behind wards built—perhaps—out of a stolen child.

Sef stood, brushed the mud from his coat, and began the walk back to the mill. He would tell them. All of it—the offer, the laughter, the favor, the seal. He owed them that much, and more than that, he was tired. Tired of the smooth-worn half-truths, tired of the accession-ledger version of himself, tired of being the man who buried things neatly.

He was halfway up the cart-track when he saw the light.

Not lamplight. Stormlight—white and branching, silent, flickering above the ruined mill like a pulse. Ulyana's magic, loose again, the way it had been leaking out of him more and often as the moons waned, as though the closer he came to the mountain the less the world could hold him in.

But beneath the stormlight, at the treeline, something else. Shapes. Four of them, maybe five, tall and wrong, moving with the patient economy of things that fed on names.

Hollow-eyed. Hunters.

They had found the mill. They were waiting for the storm to tire.

Sef dropped behind the hedgerow, heart slamming, and understood with terrible clarity that whatever he had learned tonight would be worth nothing at all unless all three of them lived long enough to use it.

He reached for the iron stylus in his pocket—an archivist's tool, useless against hunters—and began, for the first time in his life, to pray to a bureaucracy.
