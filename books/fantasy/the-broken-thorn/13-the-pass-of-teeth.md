## 13. The Pass of Teeth

The mountain had been growing in the window of the world for three days.

Ulyana felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his eyes, like the moment before a storm breaks, except the storm was not in him this time. It was ahead. Mount Vhalen did not rise so much as *wait*, its peak wrapped in cloud that never moved, never thinned, a gray shroud stitched to the rock by hands older than any kingdom he'd ever read about in Sef's stolen maps.

"It's called the Pass of Teeth," Ryn said, crouched at the edge of the ridge with her spyglass out, "because the rocks look like teeth. Also because things die there. Mostly the second reason."

Below them the pass cut through the mountain's lower shoulder like a wound that hadn't healed right—narrow walls of black stone, a road barely wide enough for two carts, and no cover worth the name. The wind came up from it carrying a smell Ulyana had learned to dread on the road: cold iron and old blood and something sweeter underneath, something that made the hair on his arms stand up.

"Hollow-eyes," he said quietly.

Ryn lowered the spyglass. "How many?"

"I don't know. More than last time." He closed his eyes and let himself feel it—the wrongness in the air, the way the wind stuttered around certain points in the pass as though bending around bodies that didn't quite exist. "They're waiting. Not hunting. Waiting."

"Because they know we're coming." Sef sat against a boulder, his wounded arm cradled against his chest, the bandage spotted brown where the river-crossing wound had seeped through again. He'd gone gray around the mouth since morning. "Of course they know. We've been walking toward their mistress's front door for eleven turns of the moon. They've had time to set a table."

"Then we don't walk in." Ulyana opened his eyes. "We go over."

"Over?" Ryn looked at him like he'd suggested they fly. "The scree slopes above the pass are death. One wrong step and you're a smear on the rocks below."

"One wrong step in the pass is worse. At least falling is honest." He turned to Sef. "Can you climb?"

Sef considered the question with the gravity of a man consulting an archive. "I can climb badly. Whether I can climb well enough depends entirely on how much you're willing to catch me."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the mountain watched them do it.

Ulyana had never been watched by stone before. He'd grown up under the valley's green shelter, where the land slept soft and dreamless behind its wards, and even on the road the earth had felt indifferent to him—present, but not attentive. Vhalen was different. Every handhold seemed chosen for him. Every loose stone shifted just enough to make him doubt. It wasn't malice. It was curiosity, vast and slow, the way a cat watches a moth work its way along a windowsill.

His magic knew it too. Twenty years buried in the valley, and now it rose in him constantly, restless as water finding cracks, whispering against his ribs: *here, here, use me here.* The storm-calling had come back to him whole when he crossed the wardline, roaring like a freed animal, and in the weeks since he'd learned what the old stories meant when they said a storm-caller's power was not his own. It belonged to the sky. The sky merely lent it, and the sky kept accounts.

By dusk they reached the shelf of rock above the pass, close enough now that Ulyana could see down into the throat of it—and what he saw stopped his breath.

Torches. Dozens of them, strung along both walls of the pass like a festival nobody had been invited to. And between them, standing perfectly still in the windless torchlight, figures. Hollow-eyed hunters, their faces smooth as river stones, their eyes black all the way through. They fed on named souls; Maren had told him that much before he left, in one of her rare moments of honesty. A hunter could stand in one place for a year if the soul it wanted was sweet enough.

There were more than thirty.

"They're not even hiding," Ryn breathed beside him. She'd gone very still, the way she did when she was memorizing a room. "That's not an ambush. That's a reception."

"They want us to try the pass," Sef said. His voice was thin. "Or they want us to see them and despair. Both serve."

"No." Ulyana studied the arrangement—the spacing, the stillness, the way none of them faced outward toward the approach but inward, toward the far end of the pass, toward the mountain. "They're not guarding the way in. They're guarding the way *through*. They expect us to come up the road, and they expect us to break and run when we see them, and they'll let us run all the way back down the mountain before they take us. Fear first. Then feeding."

"So we don't take the road," Ryn said slowly. "And we don't go back. Which means..."

"The scree fields." Ulyana pointed east, where the slope fell away into a chaos of broken stone running parallel to the pass, half a mile of shattered rock that would bring them out beyond the hunters' line—if it didn't bring them down the mountainside in pieces first. "The hollow-eyes are bound to the pass. I can feel it. They were placed there, anchored. They can't leave their posts any more than a nail can leave a wall."

"You hope," Sef said.

"I *feel*," Ulyana said, and was surprised by how steady his voice came out. "It's the one thing I'm good at now."

* * *

They crossed the scree fields in the dark, and it was almost beautiful, right up until it wasn't.

The moon was three nights from full—the twelfth turn, Ryn's charm said, its silver disc nearly complete—and its light turned the broken stone into a river of ghost-silver. Ulyana went first, testing each step, listening to the mountain through the soles of his feet the way Ryn listened to rooms. Behind him came Ryn, light as spilled water, and behind her Sef, whose bad arm forced him to lead with the wrong hand and whose breathing had become a thin, whistling thing that Ulyana tried not to count.

Halfway across, the mountain exhaled.

It wasn't wind. It was deeper than wind—a shudder through the stone itself, and then the scree began to move. Not all of it. Just beneath Sef. A sheet of loose rock the size of a barn door simply decided, with the patience of something that had waited a century, to slide.

"Sef!"

Ulyana threw out his hand and the sky answered.

He didn't call the lightning. He didn't have time to call anything. The power simply *left* him, a white-hot thread that struck the slope below Sef and fused the sliding stone into glass with a crack like the world's spine breaking. The shudder died. The scree settled. Sef stood frozen on the edge of a scar of melted rock, staring down at it, then up at Ulyana, his face bone-white in the moonlight.

"That," Ryn whispered, "was loud."

She was right. The sound rolled down the valley of the pass, echoed off the black walls, and came back changed. And in the pass below, thirty pairs of hollow eyes turned upward, all at once, like a field of flowers following the sun.

"They can't leave," Ulyana said—but even as he said it, he felt the truth of it curdle. The hunters couldn't leave their posts. But the mountain could send other things. The mountain had been curious about him for days, and now he had shouted his name into its bones in lightning.

"Move," Ryn said. "*Now.*"

* * *

They ran the rest of the scree fields with no care for silence, because silence was a currency already spent. The slope dropped away beneath them in long rushing slides that Ulyana rode with his arms out and his magic coiled tight around the three of them like a shell, ready to catch, ready to strike, ready for anything except what actually happened.

The pass ended. The road beyond it opened onto a plateau of dark pine and frozen streams, and Mount Vhalen stood above them at last, near enough to touch, its cloud-shroud glowing faintly from within like a lamp behind paper. Beyond the tree line, maybe two hours' climb, the gates of the under-court waited somewhere in the rock.

And between the trees, coming out of the darkness without hurry, without sound, walked the things the mountain had sent instead.

They were not hollow-eyed. They were worse. They wore shapes—rough ones, ill-fitting, like costumes made by someone who had only heard descriptions of people. Faces too symmetrical. Hands with too many joints. And their eyes were not empty but *full*, brimming with a pale light that Ulyana recognized with a lurch of sickness: it was the same glow he'd seen in the vision the ledger had shown him, the singing glass, the souls kept beneath the mountain.

They were lit from inside by what they carried.

"How many?" Ryn's voice was flat, professional, her knives already out.

"Nine." Ulyana counted the pale eyes moving through the pines. "Maybe ten."

"Can you do what you did on the slope? Nine times?"

"I don't know." Honesty was a luxury he could no longer afford to skip. "That one took something out of me I haven't gotten back yet."

"Then we don't fight nine." Ryn was already moving, angling them toward a narrow ravine he could barely see in the dark. "We run. There's a game trail through here—I scouted it from the ridge yesterday—we cut north, come back to the gate path from above. The things follow the ground. They're bound to it, same as the hunters. Get off the ground and—"

The nearest shape moved.

It didn't run. Running would have been better. It simply ceased to be where it was and became where they were going, materializing at the mouth of the ravine with that terrible unhurried grace, its borrowed face smiling with too many teeth. And Ulyana understood, in one cold drop of clarity, that these things were not bound to the ground at all.

They were bound to *him*. To the name in the prophecy book. To the soul with the claim on it. Wherever he went, they could go.

"New plan," Ryn said.

"There's no new plan," Sef said. He had a knife out too, held wrong, held bravely. "There was never a plan past getting here. Everything after this was always going to be this."

The shapes came out of the trees.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and order and moments to breathe. This was a storm given flesh meeting hunger given flesh, and the space between the trees became a place where the air itself tore. Ulyana stopped trying to aim. He opened everything—all twenty years of buried sky, every thunderhead he'd never called, every gale he'd swallowed behind his teeth in the valley—and let it out in a rolling wall of wind and rain and white fire that turned the pine grove into a screaming cathedral of light.

The shapes burned. They burned slowly, horribly, their inner lights guttering like candles in a gale, and their voices—not voices, *sounds*, the trapped souls inside them crying out through borrowed mouths—rose over the thunder in a chord that Ulyana would hear for the rest of his life.

One of them got through.

It came low, along the ground, through the mud and the rain, faster than anything that misshapen should move, and its many-jointed hand reached not for Ulyana's throat but for his chest—for the place behind his sternum where a soul keeps house. He saw it coming. He was mid-storm, empty-handed, every scrap of his attention holding the sky together, and he saw it coming and could do nothing at all.

Ryn got there first.

She didn't get there cleanly. She got there the way she did everything—late, reckless, and absolutely committed. Her knife took the thing through the base of its skull and its momentum took her through the air, and they hit the rocks together, and when the lightning-flash came again Ulyana saw the shape's claw had found her anyway, raking from her shoulder down across her ribs before it died.

"RYN!"

The storm died with his shout. All of it, everywhere—the rain stopped falling, the wind stopped tearing, and in the sudden ringing silence Ulyana dropped to his knees in the mud beside her.

She was laughing. Of course she was laughing, blood between her teeth, her hand pressed hard against her side. "You should see your face," she managed. "You look like Sef reading a late fine."

"Don't—" His hands hovered over the wound, useless, shaking. The rain started again, softer, because his control was gone and the sky was leaking through the cracks in him. "Don't do that. Don't—"

"It's shallow. Mostly." She caught his wrist with her free hand, grip iron despite everything. "Listen to me, storm-boy. Look up."

He looked up.

Mount Vhalen filled the sky above them, closer than ever now, and the cloud-shroud around its peak had *opened*. A hole in the gray, wide as a cathedral door, and through it—descending, slow and silent and utterly unhidden—came a light. Not the pale soul-light of the shapes. Something older. Something that felt along the mountainside like fingers feeling along a scar, searching, patient, *interested*.

The mountain had noticed him. Not the court. Not the hunters. The mountain itself.

"It knows your name now," Sef said quietly. He had come to stand over them both, rain streaming off his chin, and his voice held a strange, terrible calm. "A century ago, the archivists wrote that Vhalen was not built on the under-court. The under-court was built *into* something that was already here. Something that sleeps. And everything the court does—the souls, the glass, the contracts—it does to keep that thing dreaming."

"Then I've just woken it up," Ulyana said.

Above them, the light in the cloud-hole pulsed once, slow as a heartbeat, and every soul-lantern in the distant gates of the under-court flickered in answer—and somewhere deep beneath the rock, faint but unmistakable, Ulyana heard singing.

His own voice, singing back.

Ryn coughed blood and grinned. "Well," she said. "No turning back now."
