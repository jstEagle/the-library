## 14. Beneath the Mountain

The entrance was not a door.

Ryn had warned them of this—had traced it on her stolen maps with a fingertip stained black from copying—but knowing did nothing to soften the sight of it. The mountain's southern face split open in a seam of pale stone, and within that seam hung a curtain of roots, thick as ship cables, descending from some impossible height above. They swayed without wind. They were warm to the touch, Ulyana discovered when he pushed through them, and they smelled faintly of iron and old honey, like blood that had been left to become something else.

Beyond them, the dark began.

"Torches?" Sef whispered behind him. His voice had been getting smaller for three days, ever since the Pass of Teeth, ever since he'd watched Ryn bleed and said nothing about why his hands shook so badly. He carried the archive-satchel against his chest like a child.

"No fire," Ryn said. "Not down here. Fire draws attention." She unhooked a lamp from her belt—a squat thing of green glass—and shook it once. It woke with a light that wasn't quite light, more a suggestion of it, pooling around their feet like water finding its level. "Court-lamp. Stolen, obviously. It shows us what's there. Nothing sees us by it."

"And if something does see us anyway?"

"Then we're dead, Sef, and you can stop asking questions."

Ulyana said nothing. He had learned, over thirteen turns of moons and two hundred miles of road, that his voice carried strangely underground—that stone remembered him longer than air did. So he walked first, because walking first meant no one could see his face, and his face, he suspected, would have told them everything: that beneath the fear, beneath the grief for Ryn's wound and Sef's silence, some traitorous part of him was *glad* to be here. That after twenty years of burying the storm, of learning to be small and quiet and weatherless, he was walking toward the place where his soul had been sold, and his heart was beating like a drum at a festival.

The passage descended in long spirals, cut so smoothly it might have been poured rather than carved. The walls were veined with something luminous—a mineral, or perhaps not a mineral at all—that brightened as they went deeper, as though the mountain were an eye slowly opening. Ryn's map showed seven galleries between the seam and the court proper. She'd marked four of them with symbols whose meanings she refused to explain until they reached them.

The first gallery held water. A lake, black and perfectly still, stretching beyond the lamplight in every direction, its surface so smooth it reflected their faces back at them upside down, as though another version of them stood on a ceiling far below, waiting. Fish moved under the glass-dark skin of it, pale and eyeless, in slow processions.

"The Drowned Records," Ryn murmured. "Everything the court wants forgotten gets written down and sunk here. Ink lasts forever underwater. Paper doesn't." She smiled without humor. "They keep the forgetting itself, archived. I told you these people were thorough."

The second gallery held bones—not scattered, not heaped, but *arranged*. Thousands of ribs arched overhead in interlocking curves, and Ulyana realized with a lurch that they were walking through the skeleton of something vast enough to have swallowed a village whole. Vertebrae the size of doors lined the path like pillars. Whatever it had been, the court had not killed it so much as *kept* it, hollowed and hoisted, made into architecture.

"They build with what they own," Sef said quietly. He was reading the gallery the way he read everything—with an archivist's terrible hunger, cataloging even while afraid. "That's the whole of their law, if you want it in one sentence. Ownership is structure. What is owned holds up the world."

*What was owned*, Ulyana thought, *holds up a valley.* He kept the thought behind his teeth. There would be time for it later, or there wouldn't.

* * *

The third gallery was where the singing started.

It came first as a pressure in the ears, then as a feeling in the sternum—the way thunder announces itself through the ground before the sky opens. Ulyana stopped so abruptly that Ryn nearly walked into his back.

"You hear it too," she said. Not a question. Her hand found his sleeve. "Good. Means it's real. Means I'm not finally mad."

"It's voices," Sef breathed. "Dozens. Hundreds." His archivist's composure cracked clean down the middle. "It's a *choir*."

It was not a choir. Ulyana knew choirs; the valley sang at every solstice, human voices braided together into something warm and communal and forgettable. This was different. These voices did not harmonize. They *coexisted*—each one singing alone, endlessly, in its own key and its own language and its own grief, and yet all of them occupying the same space, the way rain occupies air. Some of the voices wept. Some laughed, high and terrible. One, somewhere far off in the dark, was counting. It had counted past nine thousand when Ulyana stopped listening to the number.

The fourth gallery explained the singing.

Ryn's lamp rose, and the light fell upon shelves.

That was the only word for them—shelves, ranks of them, rising into darkness on both sides of a corridor wide as a road, carved from the same seamless bone-pale stone. And upon the shelves, in ordered rows, each resting in its own cradle of black velvet gone grey with age, sat glass.

Not ornaments. Not vessels. Each piece was a shape unto itself—a teardrop, a knot, a spiral, a shard, a sphere with a flaw at its heart like a pupil—and each one *sang*. The sound did not come from the air around them. It came from inside the glass, the way a voice comes from inside a chest, and when Ulyana's storm-sense brushed against the nearest shelf, he felt what his eyes had refused to understand.

Souls. Sold souls. Kept as singing glass.

He had known. Gods help him, he had known since the prophecy book, since Maren's warning, since the ledger with his name in it—he had *known*—and still the knowing had been abstract, a word, a transaction, ink on vellum. This was not ink. This was a woman's voice coming out of a piece of glass the size of a fist, singing a lullaby in a language Ulyana didn't recognize, and she had been singing it, he understood suddenly and completely, for a very long time.

"How many," he heard himself say. His voice sounded borrowed.

Ryn's jaw was tight. "The counting one back there? It counts the inventory. It never stops. I've heard recordings of it in the broker houses—it got past ten thousand before anyone thought to write the number down."

Ten thousand souls. Ten thousand people—or pieces of people, halves and quarters and slivers, sold and traded and inherited like coin—hanging in the dark of a mountain, singing to no one.

Sef had gone very white. He was staring at the nearest shelf with an expression Ulyana had seen once before, years ago, in the archive at Thorn Valley, the day Ulyana had caught him altering a record and had chosen, silently, to look away. Guilt, yes. But underneath the guilt, worse: *recognition*. As though he had seen these shelves before. As though he had dreamed them.

"Sef," Ulyana said softly.

"I'm fine." It came out flat, automatic, a door closing. "Keep moving. We shouldn't linger."

But Ulyana lingered. He couldn't help it. Because among the ten thousand voices, threading through them like a single silver wire through rope, was one he knew.

His own.

* * *

It was on the fifth shelf of the seventh rank, according to the small brass plate bolted beneath its cradle. The plate bore a catalogue number in a script older than any kingdom Ulyana could name, and beneath the number, in newer ink, two words:

*QUINTANA. HALF.*

The glass was small. That was the worst of it, the detail his mind kept snagging on like a nail: it was *small*, barely larger than his closed fist, a rough teardrop of grey-white crystal shot through with veins of blue that flickered. Flickered the way lightning flickers. Flickered the way his mother's eyes had, the one time she'd spoken of the bargain, before she'd caught herself and never spoke of anything true again.

And it was singing. His voice. Not an imitation—not an echo, not a memory pressed into crystal. His voice, the one he heard in his own skull every day, pitched exactly as he pitched it, singing a song he had never learned and somehow knew every note of. A storm-song. An old one. The kind his grandmother's grandmother might have sung to call rain onto a dry field, back before storm-callers became things to be sold.

He reached out.

"Don't," Ryn said sharply, catching his wrist. Her grip was strong despite the wound in her side; she'd stopped favoring it two days ago out of pure stubbornness, and Ulyana had stopped arguing. "You don't touch court property. Especially not *this* property. The glass remembers hands. If yours touches it before the contract breaks, the court will know exactly where you are, down to the inch, forever."

"The contract hasn't broken in a hundred years," Ulyana said. "Why would it break now?"

"Because you're standing in front of it," Ryn said, "on the night the prophecy named, holding a forged soul-glass in your pack and a thief's plan in your head. Which is precisely why you don't touch it *yet*."

He hadn't touched it. But he had leaned close—close enough that his breath fogged the crystal, close enough to see that the blue veins inside it moved, slow as weather systems seen from a god's height. Close enough to listen properly. And listening properly was a mistake, because the song had words, and the words were ones he'd said himself, once, twenty years ago, on the night the wards took his magic:

*I don't want to be a storm. I just want to be left alone.*

His own child-voice, coming out of the glass, singing his own cowardice back at him in endless patient loop. The court hadn't just taken half his soul. They'd taken the moment he gave it up—the wish itself, the wanting-to-be-small—and bottled that too. Somewhere in this cathedral of bone and lamplight, the boy who had begged to be ordinary was still begging, and would beg until someone came to stop the song.

Ulyana stepped back. His legs felt like someone else's.

"That's me," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "That's—gods, Ryn, that's *me* in there. Singing."

"I know." For once there was no edge in her voice. She looked at the little teardrop of glass with an expression Ulyana had rarely seen on her: something old and sad and furious all at once, the face of a woman who had grown up in a city that sold everything and had never once seen the merchandise sing. "I know it is."

Sef had drifted further down the shelf. He was reading brass plates, lips moving, and Ulyana watched him stop at one, go rigid, and move on too quickly. Then another. Then a third, where he did not move on at all, but stood with his hand hovering an inch from the velvet cradle, trembling.

"Sef." Ulyana crossed to him. "Whose is it?"

"A girl." Sef's voice was ash. "Mira Voss. Sold in the famine year. Age six." He swallowed. "Her brother was in my apprentice cohort. He spent his whole life petitioning the valley council to ask the court for her back. They told him there was no record of the sale." His laugh was a horrible small sound. "There was a record. I *filed* it. I filed it and buried it and told myself the alternative was the valley starving, and I have told myself that story for forty years, and she has been *singing* down here the entire—"

He stopped. His hand was drifting toward the glass despite everything, the way a moth drifts toward a lamp, and Ulyana caught it—caught his wrist the way Ryn had caught his—and held it.

"Not yet," Ulyana said. "You hear me? Not yet. But soon. Soon we come back for her. For all of them, if we can. I swear it on the storm."

Sef looked at him with wet eyes and something breaking open behind them, and nodded once.

* * *

They should have kept moving. Ryn said so, twice, in the low urgent tone she used for plans going wrong. But the gallery stretched ahead of them into darkness, shelf after shelf after shelf, and the singing wrapped around them like deep water, and Ulyana found he could not make his feet obey.

Because he was hearing them now. Properly hearing them. Not a chorus, not a horror—*people*. A man singing a wedding song to a bride fifty years dead. A soldier reciting drill-cadence, over and over, as though order could hold a soul together. A child who was not singing at all but calling a name—*Mama, Mama, Mama*—in a loop that had worn the word smooth as river stone. Exiles' kin. Valley names on valley plates. Every sanctuary wall in Thorn Valley, every ward that had kept the hunted safe for a century, and here was the interest paid on it, shelved and singing and utterly alone.

Awe curdled in Ulyana's chest and came out the other side as something colder and harder. He had come down the mountain fearing the court. He descended the last gallery toward the court proper fearing something else entirely: himself, and what he would do if they refused to give them back.

The corridor ended at a pair of doors.

They were bone, like everything—vast, curved ribs crossed and lashed with silver, taller than three men—and set into them at eye height was a single lens of clear glass, and behind the lens, an eye.

It opened.

It did not blink. It did not move. It simply *regarded* them, amber and ancient and patient as the mountain itself, and Ulyana felt it take his measure the way a jeweler takes the measure of a stone—weight, clarity, flaw. Beside him, Ryn's hand slid toward the forged soul-glass in her coat. Behind him, Sef made a small sound and went silent.

The eye looked at Ulyana for a long moment.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls, from the shelves behind them, from the singing glass itself—came a voice like warm oil poured over cold stone:

*"The thirteenth moon wanes, storm-child. She has been expecting you."*

The doors began, without a sound, to open.

And in the cradle on the seventh shelf, Ulyana's own soul sang louder—one clear rising note, like lightning deciding where to strike.
