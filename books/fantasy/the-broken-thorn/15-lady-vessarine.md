## 15. Lady Vessarine

The under-court did not announce them.

There were no trumpets in the deep places of the world, no heralds with silver staves. Instead, the corridor simply changed—the rough-hewn passage they had followed for hours through Ryn's smuggler tunnels widened by degrees until Ulyana realized he was walking down a street. A street beneath a mountain, lined with lamplight that burned without flame, and buildings carved from the living rock with doorways tall enough for things that were not entirely human.

People—*people*, though some of them only wore the shape—stepped aside as they passed. Not with fear. With something worse: recognition. Whispers followed them like a tide.

*"The storm-caller."*

*"She said he'd come on the thirteenth turn."*

*"Look at his eyes. You can see where they cut him."*

Ulyana kept walking. Sef pressed close at his shoulder, clutching his satchel of records like a shield, and Ryn moved ahead of them both with her hood up and her hand never far from the knife at her hip. She had not spoken since the last gate. He understood why. There was a look on her face he had seen once before, in Corvane, when she'd found her own name in a broker's ledger—a look like someone reading their own death warrant and finding it already signed.

The street opened into the cathedral.

He had no other word for it. The cavern soared higher than any hall in Thorn Valley, higher than the sky felt sometimes, its ceiling lost in a haze of lamplight and drifting motes. Pillars of pale stone rose from a floor of polished black glass, and between the pillars—

Between the pillars hung the singing glass.

Hundreds of shards, each the size of a hand or smaller, suspended on threads of light so fine they seemed woven from silence itself. They turned slowly, catching the lamplight, and each one *sang*. Not aloud. In that place behind the ears where a voice lives when someone you love is speaking to you from another room. A thousand voices, a thousand fragments, murmuring in chords that made Ulyana's teeth ache and his chest tighten around something he could not name.

He stopped walking.

One of the voices was familiar.

It took him three heartbeats to understand why, and when he did, his knees nearly went. It was *him*. A younger him, a half-voice, singing a note he had been humming all his life without knowing it—the gap in every song he'd ever tried to sing, the reason storms felt like coming home and home felt like drowning. His stolen half hung there among hundreds, turning slowly in the lamplight, and it sounded *lonely*.

"Ulyana." Sef's hand on his arm, steadying. "Don't listen too long. They can hear you listening."

"Then let him hear."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as banked coals, and the whole cathedral seemed to lean toward it. At the far end of the hall, upon a dais of black glass, a woman rose from a chair carved like a blooming thorn.

Lady Vessarine was not what the stories promised. The tales Ryn had traded information for spoke of a hag, a spider-queen, a thing of shadow and teeth. What stood before them was elegant and gray-eyed, wearing a gown the color of deep water, with silver threaded through dark hair and a face that might have belonged to any noble mother in any court above. Only her eyes gave her away—they held the same patient coldness Ulyana had felt pressing against the valley's wards all his life, the attention that had waited outside the walls for twenty years.

She had been waiting for him specifically. He knew it the way he knew weather.

"You came early," she said, descending the dais. Her footsteps made no sound on the glass floor. "I expected you on the final turn, ragged and desperate, dragged here by hunters. Instead you arrive with—" her gaze touched Sef, then Ryn, lingering on Ryn with something like amusement "—an archivist who falsifies records and a thief who has robbed two of my brokers this year alone. How delightfully unconventional. Please." She gestured, and three chairs of pale stone slid smoothly from the floor to meet them. "Sit. This conversation deserves comfort."

Ryn didn't sit. Neither did Ulyana.

"I'd rather stand," he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt.

"Of course you would." Vessarine smiled, and it was a genuine smile, which was somehow more frightening than a false one. "You've spent your whole life standing just inside a boundary, haven't you? One foot on the wardline, feeling me feel you back. I imagine you're very tired of standing, Ulyana Quintana. But we'll do it your way."

She walked past them, unhurried, her skirts whispering over the glass. She stopped before a pillar near the center of the hall—one of the tallest—and laid her palm flat against it.

"Do you know what this place is?" she asked. "Not what the exiles' stories say. Not *soul-eater*, not *the hungry court*. Do you know what it actually is?"

"A vault," Ryn said flatly.

"A library," Vessarine corrected gently. "A library of selves. Every shard you see hanging here was sold willingly—or bought fairly, which amounts to the same thing in law older than your valley. We do not steal souls, storm-caller. We *keep* them. There is a difference, though I grant it is a difference most people find unbearable." She turned her head, regarding him sidelong. "You came here believing you would find a monster. Let me show you the bookkeeping instead. It will hurt more, but it will be true."

She drew her hand down the pillar, and the stone opened like a curtain.

Inside was a niche, and in the niche, a single pane of glass—not a soul-shard but a window, a scrying surface, clouded like breath on a winter morning. Vessarine breathed on it, an oddly human gesture, and wiped it with her sleeve.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "in the famine year, two people came to my court through ways I will not describe, carrying a child not yet born. They were desperate. The valley's wards were failing—the old bargain that sheltered Thorn Valley was expiring, and the thing that waits beyond the northern fens had already begun to test the borders. Your elders had nothing left to trade. No gold, no land, no leverage. Except one thing." She looked at him. "Except you."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Ulyana heard them, watched the ripples spread, and felt nothing yet. Shock, perhaps. The mind's mercy of delay.

"My parents," he said.

"Your parents," Vessarine agreed. "They offered the unborn child's soul—half of it, precisely measured, the portion that would have governed storm-calling—to renew the valley's wards for a hundred years. A century of sanctuary, purchased with half a self. It was, I will say plainly, the best bargain anyone has ever brought across my table. Most people offer their *own* souls. Far less useful." She tapped the glass. "Would you like to see?"

"No," said Sef, sharply.

"Yes," said Ulyana.

Vessarine touched the pane, and it cleared.

The scrying showed a chamber he almost recognized—the archive of Thorn Valley, but younger, the shelves fewer, the lamps cruder. And there, bent over a table, were two figures he knew only from a locket and a grave-marker: his mother, round-bellied and gray-faced with exhaustion; his father beside her, holding her hands, both of them facing a figure whose face was blurred by the glass's age but whose posture Ulyana knew intimately.

Elder Maren. Younger by decades, but unmistakable. Standing witness.

His parents were signing something. Their hands shook. When it was done, his mother pressed both palms to her belly and wept—not the weeping of regret, Ulyana thought wildly, watching, *not regret*—and his father kissed her hair and said something the glass did not carry.

"They loved you," Vessarine said quietly. "I want that understood before anything else. They did not sell you out of greed or cruelty. They sold half of you because the alternative was watching every exile in that valley die when the wards collapsed—including, eventually, the two of you. They chose the many over the one. People have made worse choices for less reason." She paused. "Though I notice they never told you. That, I confess, I find harder to defend."

The chamber swayed. Ulyana became aware that Sef had taken his elbow, that Ryn had drifted closer, that the thousand singing shards overhead had grown louder, as if the whole cathedral were leaning in to hear what he would say.

"The wards," he managed. "The valley's sanctuary. It runs on—"

"On you." Vessarine said it without cruelty, which made it infinitely crueler. "Every morning the sun rises safe over Thorn Valley because half of you is bound into the wardline. Every night the hollow things pass by the borders because your soul is the lock. You spent twenty years hiding inside a fortress built out of your own flesh, storm-caller. You called it exile." Her gray eyes held his, steady and pitiless and—was that pity? actual pity? "It was a prison. But you were also the bars."

* * *

Afterward, Ulyana could not have said how long they sat in the small chamber off the cathedral—Vessarine served tea, absurdly, in cups of spun glass, and Ryn actually drank hers, which told him her nerves were nearer the surface than she'd ever admit.

Sef had produced his records and was arguing. It was what Sef did, what he had done across a thousand miles of road: meet horror with citation. "The contract terms," he was saying, unrolling a copy he'd transcribed from the broker's ledger, "specify reclamation upon the named date. Tonight. But the payment clause—the favor granted to the seller—that clause remains active. If the sale is voided, does the consideration return? Is the valley's protection—"

"Sef." Ulyana's voice came out strange to him. Flat. Very calm. "Stop."

Sef stopped. Ryn set down her cup.

Ulyana looked at Vessarine. "If I take it back. My half. If the contract breaks tonight, one way or another—what happens to the wards?"

Vessarine wrapped her hands around her own cup, and for a long moment she looked almost tired—an ancient, patient tiredness, the weariness of someone who had watched this exact scene play out in a hundred variations across centuries.

"The wards fail," she said. "Not instantly. The binding would unravel over days, perhaps weeks. Long enough for warning. Not long enough for safety." She met his eyes. "Every exile in Thorn Valley—the lycanthrope families in the western hollows, the hedge-witches, the cursed children, the ones who fled to those borders because no other border would hold them—all of it ends when the lock dissolves. The thing beyond the fens has been waiting a very long time, Ulyana. It knows exactly what your soul sounds like. It has been listening to it hold the door for twenty years."

The tea went cold in his hands. He hadn't drunk any of it.

"There is an alternative," Vessarine said, and her voice gentled into something that was almost kind, and was therefore the most dangerous sound he had ever heard. "But not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and grieve, and let the truth settle into its bones. Truth is heavy at first. Give it time and it becomes something you can carry." She rose, smoothing her gown. "Guest chambers are prepared. You are safe beneath my mountain—no hunter will touch you here, whatever happens after. That much I promise freely."

She paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the lamplight of the great hall, and looked back at him.

"For what it is worth," she said, "your mother asked me, at the end, whether you would ever forgive her. I told her the truth: that forgiveness depends on what you learn, and when. She said—" Vessarine's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "She said, *then I hope he learns slowly.*"

The curtain of stone closed behind her.

In the silence, Ryn spoke first, quiet and rough. "She's playing us. All that courtesy, the tea, the tragic mother story—it's a sales pitch. Soften the mark before the offer."

"I know," Ulyana said.

"And?"

He looked down at his hands. They were shaking now, finally, the delay expired. Twenty years of invisibility, of the wardline humming against his skin, of being the safest prisoner in the world—and all of it, every hour of it, had been purchased with himself. He had hated the valley for caging him. He had never once imagined that he *was* the cage.

"She wasn't lying about my mother," he said at last. "That's the worst part. I know a lie when I hear one—I've had twenty years of practice listening through walls. She wasn't lying about any of it."

Sef gathered his papers with trembling fingers. "Then tomorrow, when she makes her offer—"

"Tomorrow," Ulyana said, and stood, and walked to the chamber's small window of raw stone, where through a seam in the mountain he could see a sliver of sky and the fat white face of the twelfth moon, one turn from full.

Overhead, faint through miles of rock, the singing glass sang on. His half-voice among them. And beneath it now, once he listened, he could hear the others—the exiles' kin, the sold and the surrendered, hundreds of small lonely notes woven into the lock that kept his valley safe.

Somewhere below the lamplight, in the dark of the deep galleries, something answered his listening. A cold, patient attention, turning toward him like a head.

It was not Vessarine.
