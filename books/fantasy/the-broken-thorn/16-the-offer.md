## 16. The Offer

Ryn Osk had stolen crowns, relics, a saint's fingerbone once, and the deed to a house that turned out to be haunted. She knew how wealth spoke. It never raised its voice.

Lady Vessarine did not raise her voice either.

They were led into the receiving hall by servants who did not blink—literally did not blink, Ryn counted, watching one carry a tray of wine across forty feet of polished bone-floor without a single contraction of eyelid—and seated on chairs carved from what looked suspiciously like vertebrae scaled up to furniture size. The hall itself was a cathedral of lamplight and pale arches, and along its walls, in niches cut with terrible precision, stood the singing glass: hundreds of vessels, each holding a curl of light that moved like breath under water. Each one humming, faintly, in a voice.

Ulyana's face when he heard them was something Ryn would carry to her grave. He stopped walking. His hand found the strap of his pack and gripped it like a rope over a cliff edge, and his lips moved without sound, and Ryn understood he was listening for one particular note among hundreds.

He didn't find it. Not yet. But the listening changed him, the way a man changes when he realizes the room is full of people who can see him.

Vessarine waited until they were seated before she spoke. She was not what Ryn expected. She was small, gray-eyed, dressed plainly in charcoal wool, with the kind of stillness that made motion around her seem rude. She could have been someone's grandmother. She could have been the thing grandmothers warned you about. Both at once, probably.

"You've come a long way," she said, "to collect what was always yours."

"Have I?" Ulyana's voice came out rough. "Or have I come to find out what was taken?"

"Those are often the same journey." She poured wine herself, no servant, and set the cup near his hand without pushing it closer. A courtesy performed so gently it became a trapdoor. "But you should hear the terms of your inheritance before you decide how much of it you want."

*Inheritance.* Ryn filed the word away. Not *purchase*. Not *property*. Inheritance. Vessarine was already reframing the ledger, and they'd been in the room less than a minute.

Sef sat rigid beside Ryn, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man cataloging exits. There were none worth cataloging. Ryn had checked. Three doors, all guarded by things that blinked too rarely, and the ceiling arched away into dark where something large shifted its weight now and then, patient as weather.

"I'll hear them," Ulyana said.

* * *

Here is what Ryn learned about Lady Vessarine in the next hour: she never lied. That was the horror of her. Every courtier Ryn had ever robbed or bribed lied the way other people breathed—automatically, constantly, with pleasure. Vessarine told the truth the way a surgeon cuts. Precisely. Only where needed. And always with an explanation of why the cutting was necessary.

She showed them the original contract first. An apprentice brought it out on a cushion of black velvet, and there it was: a century old, sealed twice, written in two hands. One hand belonged to the court. The other—

The other belonged to Thorn Valley.

Ryn watched Ulyana read it. Watched his eyes move down the lines that named him before he existed, that weighed and priced half a soul the way you'd weigh grain against a bad winter. Watched him arrive at the signature and stop breathing for three full seconds.

He didn't say the name aloud. He didn't have to. Ryn had decoded that cipher seven turns ago, and Sef had confessed his own piece of it around a dying fire, and now here it was in ink older than any of them: the seal of Elder Maren, pressed deep and clean, no tremor in it at all.

"She was young," Vessarine said quietly. "The valley was starving. The wards were failing—you know this. You grew up inside their hum and thought it was the sound of safety. It was the sound of something being spent." She turned the page with one finger. "Your parents agreed. Your mother wept; your father did not sign, which I have always found interesting. And the valley ate well that winter, and the wards held for a hundred years, and every exile who crawled through the thorns found shelter. Paid for, in part, by you."

"You're telling me this," Ulyana said slowly, "because you want me angry."

"I'm telling you this because you deserve the whole shape of it. Most of my clients never learn even this much." Something moved behind her gray eyes—not warmth, nothing so cheap, but perhaps respect. "You are not most of my clients, Ulyana Quintana. You walked here. Through hunters. Through the Pass of Teeth. With a wounded thief and a frightened archivist and no army and no leverage except your own stubbornness." She smiled, small and genuine, and Ryn hated it. "I have been waiting a century for a soul of your quality to come collect itself."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the contract stands. On the thirteenth turn, what is held comes home to me, and you—" she spread her hands, apologetic, "—become a hollow thing walking. I don't wish that. Truly. Hollow men make poor neighbors."

* * *

They were given rooms. Of course they were given rooms—warm ones, deep in the mountain, with beds soft enough to swallow grief. Ryn refused hers on principle and then took it anyway, because principle doesn't keep you warm at four thousand feet under stone, and because she wanted to search the walls, which she did, thoroughly, finding nothing but more bone and more lamplight and, scratched into the underside of the bedframe in a hand gone shaky with age: *it was fair, it was fair, it was f*

Someone had tried to make peace with this place and failed mid-word.

At the evening meal—there was an evening meal, absurdly good, roasted roots and dark bread and wine that tasted of cherries—Vessarine made her offer.

Ryn saw it coming from three sentences away, and it still landed like a hammer.

"The valley's wards fail within a year of your soul returning to you," Vessarine said. "This is not a threat; it is arithmetic. The wards run on what was taken from you. Return it, and the sanctuary goes dark. Every exile in Thorn Valley—the lame, the hunted, the marked, the ones whose names are on lists in a dozen capitals—wakes to find the thorns are only thorns." She let that sit. Then, gently: "Unless."

Ulyana set down his cup. "Unless what."

"Unless the wards have a new source." She rose, and crossed to the window—or what passed for a window, a tall slit opening onto the cavern's vast dark—and stood silhouetted against the lamplight. "A storm-warden. Bound to the court, yes. Serving it, yes. But serving it *here*, Ulyana, feeding the wards yourself, willingly, hour by hour, with power you have spent twenty years strangling. Do you know what you could do, unburied? What you did at the pass, in terror, half-trained? Now imagine it deliberate. Imagine it *chosen*."

"And in exchange?"

"Your soul. Whole. Returned tonight, freely, no glass, no ledger." She turned back to face him. "And the valley safe forever. Not for thirteen turns. Forever. Your mother's bargain honored better than she ever managed. Every exile sheltered. Every hunter turned back at the wardline by *you*."

Ryn's stomach dropped, because it was perfect. That was the trap of it—it wasn't a gilded cage, it was a *good* bargain, honestly struck, generously framed. Everything Ulyana had ever wanted, laid out on a plate: belonging, purpose, protection for the people who'd sheltered him. Priced at a hundred years beneath a mountain, serving the thing that kept souls in jars.

"A hundred years," Sef said hoarsely. "You'd be—"

"A hundred years," Vessarine confirmed. "At the end of which, release. Written into the contract. I keep my contracts, Archivist; ask anyone I have ruined." A dry pause. "Better: ask no one, and simply observe that you are all still alive."

"Why do you want me?" Ulyana asked. "Really. Souls in glass, that's storage. Why does a storm-warden matter to a court under a mountain?"

For the first time, Vessarine hesitated. It lasted half a breath, but Ryn caught it, the way she caught everything—tells, exits, the weight of other people's silences.

"Because the world above is changing," Vessarine said. "Old sanctuaries are failing everywhere. Old bargains are coming due. The courts that survive the next century will be the courts with weather." Her gray eyes settled on Ulyana, and there was nothing grandmotherly in them at all. "You are not a purchase to me, Ulyana Quintana. You are a *future*. I am offering to share it with you."

* * *

That night Ryn couldn't sleep, which was normal; what wasn't normal was *why*. Not fear. Fear she could work with. This was worse: she was afraid because she understood.

She lay in the bone-dark room and turned it over. If she were Ulyana—if she'd spent twenty years invisible, unwanted, exiled from her own name—and someone offered her a place, a purpose, and the safety of everyone she'd ever known, all at once, in writing, honestly—would she refuse?

She knew herself too well to lie about it. She'd take the deal. She'd hate herself and take the deal.

That was why Vessarine frightened her. Not the bone cathedral, not the unblinking servants, not the singing glass with its choir of the sold. Any monster could be robbed or stabbed. But a monster who offered you exactly what you wanted, at a price you could almost live with—that was a monster you *negotiated with*, and negotiations were where Ryn Osk lost things.

She got up. Checked on Sef—he slept badly, muttering catalog numbers—and then stood outside Ulyana's door a long moment, debating whether to go in and argue against the offer.

She decided against it. Arguing would only harden him. People defended positions they'd announced aloud.

Instead she went looking for the archive.

It wasn't hard to find. Courts were all the same underneath: treasure near the top, records near the bottom, guards concentrated around whatever the lord loved most. Vessarine loved her ledgers—Ryn had seen it in the way her fingers rested on the contract's cover, the way a miser touches coin. So Ryn walked the lower galleries with the unhurried stride of someone who belonged, past niches of singing glass, past clerks who did not blink, until she found the door Sef had described from his own apprenticeship in another life: iron-banded, unguarded, because no thief wants paper.

No ordinary thief.

Inside, the archive breathed dust and lamp oil, shelves climbing into dark, and somewhere in it—she was certain—sat the master ledger of the under-court. Sales. Purchases. Favors granted and favors owed. Including, if Sef's trembling confession at the fire had been true, a payment clause still marked *active*: Maren's favor, a century later, never yet collected.

A favor the court could call in at any time. A favor that might, with the right pressure applied, become a lever long enough to move anything—even a lady who never lied.

Ryn flexed her fingers, felt the old familiar hunger settle over her like a second skin, and smiled in the dark.

Tomorrow, Ulyana would decide whether to sell himself forever. Tonight, she intended to find out what else in this mountain was for sale—and what it would cost to steal the one thing Lady Vessarine could not afford to lose.
