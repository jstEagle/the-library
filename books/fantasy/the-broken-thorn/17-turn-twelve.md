## 17. Turn Twelve

The penultimate moon rose over Mount Vhalen like a coin held up to the light of something that wanted to be paid.

Ulyana stood at the window of the guest chamber Lady Vessarine had given them—*given*, as though generosity were a thing the under-court understood—and watched it climb above the black teeth of the pass they had bled through to get here. The Turn Twelve charm Maren had pressed into his hand a year ago hung against his chest, and its silver face was nearly empty now, one thin crescent remaining before the thirteenth turn waned and everything he loved was forfeit.

Everything he loved. The valley. The exiles huddled inside it. Sef, who was somewhere below in the archive levels, cataloguing by lamplight because cataloguing was the only prayer he knew. Ryn, who was sleeping with one knife under her pillow and two within reach of her hand, because she was Ryn.

And himself. He supposed he counted too, though some nights it was hard to remember why.

The chamber was beautiful in the way the whole court was beautiful: bone-white arches, lamps that burned without smoke, tapestries woven from thread that caught the light and held it hostage. Everything here was lovely and nothing here was warm. He had been in the mountain for six days, and his skin still crawled with the wrongness of being underground, of stone pressing down where sky should be. His magic felt it too. It moved beneath his skin like water looking for an outlet, restless, thunderous, homesick for weather.

*You could have weather,* said the thought that had been circling him all day like a hawk. *You could have purpose.*

He turned from the window.

On the table by the bed, Vessarine's offer sat where she had left it—not written, never written, the court did not commit such things to paper—but present all the same, the way a scar is present. Serve as the court's storm-warden. A hundred years. In exchange, his soul returned to him whole, unclaimed, free. And the valley: warded forever, sheltered forever, every exile safe behind walls bought with his service instead of his stolen self.

It was reasonable. That was the horror of it. No cruelty, no chains—at least none he could see. A hundred years was not eternity, not to someone who had already lived half of one in hiding. The work would be real: turning storms away from the mountain's roads, calling rain for the terraced farms in the foothills, standing watch. Standing watch. He had spent twenty years learning how to do nothing but stand watch over himself.

And he would be *wanted*. Not hunted, not named in ledgers, not a line item in a century-old sale. Wanted. Needed. Home—of a kind. The word snagged in him like a fishhook.

He pressed his palm flat against the table and made himself look at the truth underneath the beauty of it, the way Sef had taught him to read a document: past the fine hand, past the seal, down to the ink's actual meaning.

If he accepted, the valley stayed safe. If he accepted, the singing glass stayed where it was—all of it, every shelf of it, every soul in every pane.

His hand curled slowly into a fist.

He had heard the glass once already, on their first descent into the reliquary levels, when Ryn's maps had taken them through a corridor lined with it. A cathedral of lamplight and bone, shelves rising out of sight, and on the shelves—glass. Panes the size of palms, panes the size of doors, each one holding a faint, shifting luminescence, like weather trapped behind a window. Sold souls. The court's treasury, its power source, its music. They sang when you walked past. Not words. Not quite. More like the sound wind makes through a ruined house, if the house remembered who used to live there.

Vessarine had walked him through it herself, courteous as a hostess, and he had heard his own voice among them—a thread of sound that matched the shape of his throat—and had kept walking because keeping walking was the only way to survive the hearing.

Tonight, though. Tonight the moon was almost gone. Tonight the choice was almost due.

He took the lamp from the table and went down.

* * *

The corridors of the under-court were quiet at this hour in the way that tombs are quiet. Ulyana knew the guards' rotation now—Ryn had memorized it in a day and a half and recited it in her sleep, muttering counts between dreams—and he moved through the gaps she had mapped for him, lamp shuttered, boots silent on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet that had no right to be comfortable here and had learned to be anyway.

He told himself he was only going to look. That was the oldest lie he owned; he'd been telling versions of it since he was nine years old and first crouched at the wardline, telling himself he only wanted to see the storm, not touch it.

The reliquary corridor opened around him like a held breath.

Lamplight without flame pooled along the shelves. Glass upon glass upon glass, thousands of panes, ten thousand, each one breathing its small light, and the singing rose to meet him—the sound he had fled from once, the sound of a hundred years of theft arranged in rows and alphabetized by Sef's predecessors, God rot them all, God rot the tidy hands that had done this.

He stopped walking. He hadn't meant to stop.

The nearest pane was small, no bigger than a playing card, set at the height of a child's reach. Its light was pale gold, and it flickered—there was no other word for it—in a rhythm too quick and uneven to be anything but frightened. And through the glass, faint as breath on a winter window, came a voice.

Not singing. Begging.

*—cold, it's cold, please, I want my mother, please, I didn't mean to—*

Ulyana's lamp slipped in his grip. He caught it against his chest, heart slamming, and stood frozen in the aisle while the voice went on, looping, the way a dream loops when the dreamer cannot wake. A child's voice. A child's soul, sold decades ago, maybe a century, still asking for a mother who was surely dust.

*I didn't mean to.* What hadn't the child meant to do? Call a storm? Be born? Exist loudly enough that someone with a ledger decided they were worth more as glass than as flesh?

His legs carried him deeper into the stacks before he decided to move. He would tell himself later that he had meant to leave, and he would know he was lying.

A pane the size of his two hands together, blue-green, humming a lullaby in a language he almost recognized—an eastern dialect, valley-adjacent, the kind spoken in the shepherd holdings beyond the Thorn River. An old man's timbre buried in it, patient, repeating the same four notes over and over like a man pacing a cell.

A pane that flared white when he passed, crackling, and the sound it made was *thunder*, a soul that had been a storm-caller too, once, another name in another ledger, and it recognized what he was and it screamed against its walls of glass and the scream had harmonics in it that hurt his teeth.

And then—halfway down the third row—he found the ones from home.

He knew them the way you know your own front door in the dark. The accent in the begging. The names stitched on the leaded borders, because the court labeled everything, because even souls must be filed: *Halewyn. Ostreva. Brannoc of the Mill Row.* Names from the valley's memorial rolls, the lists Elder Maren read aloud every famine-day, the honored dead, the lost, the *taken by fever, taken by flood, taken by the road*. Lies, all of them, or half-lies. Not taken by fever. Sold. Their families had signed papers they couldn't read—or hadn't signed, had simply been signed for—and the valley's wards had drunk the payment, and the exiles had lived safe and warm inside a sanctuary built out of other people's children.

Brannoc of the Mill Row. Ulyana had grown up on Mill Row. There was a Brannoc family three doors from the archive, a grandmother who gave him honey-bread every solstice and asked after his health in a voice like gravel and kindness. This pane—this small, shivering, golden pane—sang with the same gravel. Her husband? Her brother? Someone who had gone *on the road* forty years ago, and the whole street had mourned, and the wards had brightened that winter, and everyone had said how well the valley was protected lately, how lucky they were.

*Lucky.* The word curdled in his mouth.

He put his hand against the glass.

He didn't decide to. His hand simply went, the way it goes to a wound, the way it goes to a crying child, and the moment his skin met the pane the singing changed. All of it, down every row, shelf after shelf, the whole cathedral of stolen light turning toward him like flowers toward a sun that had finally come back.

*—you're one of us—*

*—can you hear us, can you hear us, we can hear you—*

*—storm-bringer, storm-child, you're loud, you're so loud, we heard you coming for days—*

*—don't let them put you in here—*

*—please—*

*—please—*

*—it doesn't stop, that's the thing no one tells you, it doesn't stop, a hundred years and it doesn't stop—*

Ulyana sank to his knees in the aisle with his hand still pressed flat to Brannoc's pane, and the sound poured through his arm and into his chest and he let it, because someone should let it, because they had been singing into the dark for a hundred years and no one had ever knelt.

He thought about the offer.

That was the terrible part, later, when he made himself remember honestly: even kneeling there, even drowning in their voices, the offer was still *reasonable*. He could see it whole, see it clearly, weigh it like a merchant: one hundred years of service, freely given, against the valley's eternal safety. Against these souls staying exactly where they were, because his acceptance would change nothing for them—would ratify everything for them. His signature beside theirs in the great ledger of the court, the final line, the one that said *this is how the world works; this is acceptable; sing quietly.*

Vessarine had shown him the arithmetic herself, gentle as a nurse: the valley's wards needed feeding. His soul-half fed them now. If he took it back, the wards fell. Unless he served, and his service fed them instead, and everyone lived, and wasn't that the best of all available worlds?

Everyone lived. Except the ones in the glass. Except the children asking for mothers who were dust. Except every future soul the court would buy, because a court with a willing storm-warden was a court with an appetite confirmed, a court whose ledger would keep growing, row after row, pane after pane, forever, with his lightning standing guard over the shelves.

He had spent twenty years learning invisibility, and he had told himself it was survival, and it was. But kneeling there, he understood—with the clarity that comes only when you are already broken open—that he had also spent twenty years practicing complicity. Every year he stayed hidden, the wards drank deep. Every quiet season in the valley was purchased. He had been paying for his safety with coins he never saw leave his pocket, and the pocket belonged to other people.

No more. Not one more coin. Not even a reasonable one. Not even a kind one.

Especially not a kind one. Kindness was how the court worked. Nobody down here was cruel; that was the trap of it. Vessarine was courteous. The clerks were meticulous. The hunters were merely following contracts. Evil in the under-court wore reading glasses and balanced its books, and it would have accepted his yes with genuine warmth and thanked him for his service, and a hundred years from now some other exile would kneel in this aisle and press a hand to *his* pane and hear him begging.

His hand slid from the glass. The voices rose, desperate, reaching—

*Wait, wait, don't go, don't leave us—*

"I'm coming back," he whispered, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "I'm coming back for all of you. I don't know how yet. But I'm not signing anything. Do you hear me?" He laughed, wet and awful, at the absurdity of promising anything to glass. "I hear *you*. That has to count for something."

The singing didn't stop. But it changed key—softened, the way a room softens when someone finally says the true thing out loud. Down the rows, pane after pane dimmed from frantic flicker to something steadier, something almost like rest. As if a hundred years of screaming had only ever needed one person to answer.

He got up. His knees ached. His eyes burned. The Turn Twelve charm lay cold against his sternum, one crescent from empty.

Somewhere above, the mountain shifted its weight, and far off in the dark of the corridors, a door closed with the particular care of someone who had been listening for a long time.

Ulyana went still.

He knew that care. He had heard it in Sef's footsteps for twenty years, in the way archivists move among things they are not supposed to touch. But Sef was below, in the lower archives, and this sound came from the stairwell behind him—from the direction of Vessarine's halls.

Which meant either the court had known where he was all along and let him come, let him kneel, let him promise—

Or someone else had followed him down, someone who was not court, and was now standing in the dark deciding whether he had just witnessed a betrayal or a beginning.

The lamp in Ulyana's hand guttered. Out of the stairwell's black, unhurried, a shape resolved—and Ulyana's whole body went tight as wire, because he knew that silhouette too, and it was the last one he had expected to find in the under-court, and it was already raising one finger to its lips.

*Don't wake the mountain,* the gesture said. *We need to talk.*
