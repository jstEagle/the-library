## 18. Theft of a Soul

Ryn Osk had stolen a great many things in her life, and she had a private taxonomy for all of them.

There were easy thefts—coins from a drunk's purse, bread from a baker who'd stopped counting loaves. There were clever thefts, which she liked best: the signet ring lifted off a magistrate's finger while shaking his hand, the deed swapped for a forgery while its owner wept over his wife's coffin. And then there were thefts that weren't really thefts at all but something older and stranger—taking back what should never have been taken in the first place.

Tonight was the third kind, and it terrified her more than any of the others.

She crouched in the shadow of the archive's outer wall, three levels beneath the mountain, and watched lamplight crawl along the bone-white corridor like something living. The under-court did not use torches. It used lamps of singing glass, each one holding a fragment of some sold soul, and their light moved wrong—too slow, too warm, as though the light itself remembered being alive and grieved for it.

*Don't look at them,* she told herself, the way Sef had told her on the walk down. *Looking is how they get into you.*

Above her, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, Ulyana Quintana was tearing the sky open.

She'd felt it begin an hour ago—a pressure in her back teeth, a hum in the soles of her feet, the particular wrongness of weather where no weather should be. Then the mountain had shuddered, and dust had sifted down through the lamplight like gray snow, and somewhere far above, the court's bells had begun to scream.

Every guard in Vhalen would be running to the surface. Every hollow-eyed hunter would be clawing toward the storm that dared to break over their master's roof. That was the plan. Ulyana's part of it was simple and suicidal: be loud enough that the whole mountain forgot anything else existed.

Ryn's part was quiet, precise, and required her hands not to shake.

They were shaking anyway.

"You're breathing like a bellows," Sef murmured beside her. He was pressed flat against the wall, his archivist's coat dark with stone-dust, the forged soul-glass cradled against his chest in a sling of velvet like a sleeping infant. "In through the nose. Four counts. Out through the mouth."

"I know how to breathe, Sef."

"You breathe like someone being chased."

"I am someone being chased. Have been since Corvane." She flexed her fingers, watching the tremor fade. "It's different when you're stealing a person."

"It's a piece of one."

"It's *him.*" She said it low and fierce. "Twenty years of him. Everything he buried in that valley so he could stay invisible, everything they carved out before he was born. It's been sitting in this mountain, singing, while he walked around half-empty and called it safety." She looked at Sef, and the lamplight made his face look older than it was, hollower. "I've stolen rings. Deeds. A crown once, off a dead king, which nobody missed because everyone pretended he still wore it. This is the first time I've ever stolen something that was owed."

Sef was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness he used in the archives, cataloguing horrors. "The vault is past the second gate. The glass are racked by date of acquisition. His will be among the oldest—the sale predates his birth."

"I remember the ledger."

"The forgery matches the rack dimensions. I measured twice."

"You measured a *guess* twice," Ryn said. "You've never seen the actual glass up close."

"I've seen the catalogue plates." He hesitated. "And I heard one, once. As an apprentice. They asked me to carry a replacement lamp down the east stair." His throat worked. "It sang the whole way. A woman's voice. She'd been sold forty years and she was still asking someone to tell her daughter she hadn't meant to leave."

Ryn looked at him. In the ten days since the river crossing, since the hunters and Ulyana's first kill and the confession at the fire that Ryn had swallowed down like a stone, she had learned to read Sef Idris the way she read locks—not by what he showed her, but by what resisted. Right now everything about him resisted. His jaw. His hands. The set of his shoulders, squared toward the second gate like a man walking toward a debt he intended to pay in full.

"Sef," she said carefully. "What are you planning?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Too smooth. "I'm planning to hold the door, as agreed, and to leave when you leave, as agreed."

She didn't believe him. But there was no time to dig at it, because the mountain shuddered again—harder this time, a groan of stone that went on and on—and somewhere above them a section of ceiling cracked and let down a curtain of grit. The lamps along the corridor flared bright, all of them at once, dozens of small souls crying out in sympathy with the storm, and every remaining guard in earshot turned toward the sound with the blind hunger of moths.

"Now," Ryn breathed. "Now, now, now."

* * *

The second gate was a thing of black iron and worse magic. It stood open—Ulyana's storm had pulled the wardens away—but the threshold itself still shimmered faintly, a skin of pale light stretched across the archway like ice over a well.

"Hunters' ward," Sef whispered. "It reads names. If you cross carrying a named soul, it will know."

"Then I won't carry it across. You will." She glanced at him. "Your name isn't in the contract. Mine might as well be—I've been in enough broker ledgers to have a smell they can follow."

He nodded, and for a moment she thought that was all it was—tactics, division of labor, the plan working exactly as drawn. He crossed the threshold first, wincing as the light licked over him, and nothing happened. The ward found no stolen soul in his arms, only a disgraced archivist and a forged lump of glass, and let him pass.

Ryn followed. The ward's touch was cold, probing, briefly intimate—like a hand patting down her pockets—and then it released her, uninterested.

The vault beyond was smaller than she'd expected. That was the horror of it, she thought later; she had imagined a cathedral, something vast enough to hold the scale of the crime. Instead it was a long room with racks of shelving, orderly as a wine cellar, and hundreds upon hundreds of lamps of singing glass, each one labeled in a fine clerk's hand. Date of acquisition. Name of seller. Name of buyer. Some of the labels were so old the ink had gone brown.

And underneath it all, so soft she almost mistook it for the ringing in her own ears, the room was *singing.*

Not words. Not anymore. Just voices, worn smooth by decades until they were only tone—grief reduced to pitch, hope reduced to resonance, a thousand people humming in the dark because humming was all they had left.

Ryn stood in the doorway and felt her whole body go cold.

"They're not asleep," she whispered. "Sef. They're not *asleep.*"

"No," Sef said quietly. "I don't think they ever were."

He moved down the racks with the terrible competence of a man who had spent his life among catalogues, reading dates, sliding lamps aside. Ryn kept watch at the door, one eye on the corridor, one on him, and tried not to listen to the singing. It was worse than listening. It got inside. One lamp near her shoulder sang in a child's register, high and thin, and she had to close her eyes and think of Ulyana's face on the night he'd pulled her out of the mob in Corvane—his terror, and how he'd stood there anyway, lightning crawling up his arms because a stranger was about to die and he couldn't not.

*That's why we're here,* she told herself. *All of these. For all of these.*

"Here." Sef's voice cracked slightly. "Ryn. Here."

She crossed to him. On the third rack from the end, in a row of lamps dated nearly twenty-five years back, one glass burned differently than the rest—a deeper blue, shot through with veins of silver that flickered and shifted like cloud seen from very high up. Storm-glass. Even sitting still on its shelf, it moved.

And it was singing. Not the worn-smooth hum of the others. Something rawer. Younger. A voice that had been in here since before it finished becoming a voice, and had never stopped reaching for a sky it barely remembered.

Ryn reached out and touched the shelf beside it, not the glass itself—Sef had warned her, *never touch the glass directly, it will know your name*—and the song bent toward her warmth like a plant toward sun.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Swap now," Sef urged. "While the wardens are above. Lift it straight up, seat the forgery in its place, align the label. I checked the tolerances—"

"I know. I built the thing." Her hands were steady now. That was the strange mercy of her trade: when it finally mattered, the shaking always stopped. She lifted the storm-glass free—it was lighter than it should have been, warm, and the song rose sharply, hopefully, a voice recognizing *movement*, believing maybe that movement meant *out*—and set the forgery in its cradle. Gray glass, dead glass, cut to match. She aligned the label. Squared the rack.

From two feet away, in bad light, to a warden who had never listened closely to this particular voice—

It would hold. It would hold long enough.

"Got it," she breathed. "I've got him. Sef, we move, we move *now*—"

But Sef wasn't moving toward the door.

He was standing very still, looking down the long rows of singing glass, and his face had gone the color of the stone around them. "Ryn," he said. "How long does the storm hold them?"

"What? I don't know—an hour, maybe less, the pass will bleed it dry—"

"And when it ends, the first thing they'll do is audit the vault. Count the lamps. Listen for the false note." He turned to her, and his eyes behind the dust were terribly calm. "A forgery won't sing, Ryn. They'll know within minutes of coming down here. And they'll know who came, because the ward logged us both at the gate."

"So we run faster than they audit. That was always the plan."

"That was your plan." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen since the camp burned. "I have another one. Give me the sling."

"No." The word came out flat and instant. "No. Whatever you're—no."

"Someone has to hold the archive door. That was always true. You knew it and I knew it and we both pretended the *someone* would be temporary." He stepped closer, and she saw his hands were trembling now, all that composure finally cracking to show the man underneath—the apprentice who had carried a singing lamp down an east stair forty years after it mattered and had never forgiven himself for setting it gently on a shelf. "If the door stays shut, they can't reach the vault until the audit. If the audit finds the forgery, they'll tear the mountain apart looking for the real one, and they'll start with the girl carrying it. But if the door holds—if it holds long enough for you to get him above ground, into real weather, into *his* weather—they can't take it back. Storm-claimed glass doesn't re-enter a rack. Even Vessarine's law says so. Especially Vessarine's law."

"Sef—"

"I falsified records for eleven years before you caught me." His voice didn't waver. "One entry at a time. Small things. Dates, names, provenances. I told myself it was mercy, smoothing the edges of a cruel system. It wasn't. It was me learning to make cruelty legible." He held out his hands for the sling. "This is the first entry I can write honestly. Let me."

Above them, distant but unmistakable, the storm began to falter. The mountain's bones creaked as the pressure eased. Somewhere, bells changed their rhythm—from alarm to recall.

"They're calling the hunters home," Ryn whispered. "Sef, they're calling them *home.*"

"Then you have perhaps four hundred heartbeats of head start." He took the empty sling from her—she didn't remember letting go of it—and slung the velvet over his shoulder like a man shouldering a yoke. "East stair. Two turns past the archive door. Don't stop, don't look back, and if anyone asks, tell them the thief took the archivist hostage." His mouth twitched. "It's the most believable lie either of us has ever told."

Ryn clutched the storm-glass against her chest. It was singing louder now, urgent, feeling the motion and the running and the direction—up, up, toward air—and its hope hurt worse than any wound she'd taken in the pass.

"If I leave you," she said, "Ulyana will never forgive me. And worse—" her voice caught, "—*I'll* never forgive me."

"You're leaving him with the only thing in this mountain worth carrying." Sef backed toward the archive door, toward the sound of boots beginning to descend the far stairs, and gave her a small, precise archivist's bow. "Go, Ryn Osk. And when you map the world without borders—" he smiled, "—put a good road through Thorn Valley. Someone should be able to visit."

Then the archive door boomed shut between them, and bars she hadn't known were there slammed home, and Sef Idris began, in a clear carrying voice, to recite the full catalogue of the vault's holdings—every name, every date, every crime—in the flat, flawless diction of a man who had spent his life making records, buying with every word another heartbeat of the head start she needed.

Ryn ran.

* * *

She ran the way she had learned to run in Corvane's rooftops: low, silent, spending nothing on pride. Two turns past the archive door. Up the east stair, where the air grew warmer and began to taste of storm. The glass against her chest sang the whole way, climbing in pitch, wild with hope, and she found herself whispering to it between breaths—*almost, almost, hold on, almost*—like soothing a child, like promising.

Behind her, fainter and fainter, Sef's voice went on reciting names. She counted them without meaning to. At thirty-one, the voice stopped mid-name, and something in the mountain went quiet in a way that was worse than any noise.

She didn't stop. He'd told her not to. She hated him for it, and would honor it, and hated herself for both.

Then the last stair, and the night air hit her face like a blessing—cold, wet, alive—and she burst out onto the mountainside into the aftermath of Ulyana's storm. Clouds shredded overhead. Rain fell in warm fat drops. And fifty yards down the slope, lit by the last flickers of his own lightning, Ulyana Quintana stood with his arms raised and his eyes white with reflected thunder, waiting, watching the door she'd come out of.

His face, when he saw what she carried, broke open like dawn.

"Is that—"

"It's you," Ryn said, stumbling toward him, holding the singing glass out in both hands. "It's all of you. Take it—take it *now*, before they—"

High above, the mountain's peak split with light that had nothing to do with storms. And a voice like cold silk rolled down the rock:

"*Thief. You have taken what is purchased. Return it—or the archivist pays your interest.*"

In Ryn's hands, the storm-glass sang louder—and this time, for the first time, it formed a word.

*"Run."*
