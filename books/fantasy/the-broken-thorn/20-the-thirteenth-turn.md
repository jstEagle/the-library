## 20. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon rose over Thorn Valley like a coin held up to the light, and Ulyana stood at the wardline with the soul-glass cupped in both hands, feeling it hum against his palms like a trapped wasp.

Thirteen turns. Maren's charm had counted them faithfully, each little silver notch darkening as the moons waned and waxed, until only this one remained—the thirteenth, swollen full above the eastern ridge, so bright it threw his shadow behind him like a second man fleeing the other way.

Behind him, the valley lay quiet. Lamplight in the scattered windows of the exile houses. Smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys into windless air. The sanctuary he had been born inside, the prison he had been sold into, the home that had been purchased with half of everything he was—all of it sleeping under wards that sang tonight, faintly, in a register only he could hear. He had heard it all his life without knowing what it was. Now he knew. It was him. The missing piece of his own soul, spun out thin across the wardline, holding up the sky for everyone who had ever fled here.

Ryn stood at his left shoulder, her arm bound where the hunter's blade had opened it in the Pass of Teeth, her face still gray from blood loss but her eyes sharp as flint. Sef should have been beside them. Sef was beneath the mountain, traded for a door he'd chosen to hold, and every hour Ulyana did not think about it was an hour bought with effort, like holding a stone at arm's length.

"You don't have to do this," Ryn said quietly. "There's still the offer. Vessarine keeps her word—that much I believe. A hundred years as storm-warden. The valley safe forever. You'd be *somebody*. You'd be *theirs*."

"That's why I have to do it," Ulyana said. "Because I finally understand what being theirs costs."

He turned the glass in his hands. It was small—no larger than a heart, which was fitting—and inside it his stolen half-soul moved like weather seen through a window, silver-gray, restless, pressing against the curved walls. When he held it close to his ear he could hear himself: a voice like wind through a gap under a door, saying things he didn't remember saying, singing songs he didn't remember learning. For fourteen days he had carried it out of the under-court while hunters howled along their trail, and all that time the voice had grown louder, more familiar, more *him*, until last night he had dreamed in two places at once—in his own body on the road, and inside the glass, pressed against the curve, looking out.

The other souls had taught him what it meant. In Vessarine's cathedral of lamplight and bone, before Ryn's theft, he had touched the singing glass and heard them—the sold ones, centuries of them, exiles' mothers and fathers and children, each one a small captive weather, each one begging. Not screaming. Begging. That was worse. They begged to be returned, to be finished, to be whole somewhere, anywhere, even if wholeness meant dying. And he had understood, standing there with his hand flat against the shelf, that if he accepted Vessarine's offer—if he took his soul back and served the court for a hundred years—he would become the keeper of those shelves. The warden of the begging. He would spend eternity listening to voices like his own and telling himself the arrangement was necessary.

"Ulyana." Ryn touched his sleeve. "The wards. If you break it here, they fall. Every exile in that valley wakes up tomorrow with no sanctuary. No walls. The court can't follow magic in—but nothing stops it from following *out*. Maren said—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Maren has no right to say anything anymore."

"She was right about this part, though." He looked at the valley again, at the small lights. "I've done the arithmetic a hundred ways since we left the pass. There is no version where everyone keeps what they had. Either the court keeps my soul and the valley keeps its walls, or I keep my soul and the valley loses its shelter. Those were always the choices. The prophecy just never told me which side of the scale I'd be standing on."

"And you're choosing yourself."

"I'm choosing truth." His hands tightened around the glass. "Vessarine called it an economy. Souls as currency, sanctuaries as interest payments. She showed me the ledger myself—my parents' names, the famine year, the favor granted, the seal that matched Maren's ring. Half of me, weighed against a valley's worth of hiding. And everyone who ever signed, ever sold, ever *accepted*—they all told themselves the same story. That it was necessary. That someone had to pay so the rest could sleep." He laughed, short and bitter. "I spent twenty years believing I was invisible by nature. Turns out I was invisible by purchase. Everyone in that valley lives the same way. Their safety is bought. Someone paid. We just never see the receipt."

"So you burn the ledger."

"So I end the currency."

Above them the moon cleared the ridge entirely, and the wardline answered—a shimmer running along the ground like heat-haze made visible, the boundary between sanctuary and world glowing faintly silver. Ulyana had crossed it once, thirteen turns ago, when his magic roared back like a freed animal. He remembered the exhilaration. He remembered the terror. Both felt very far away now, worn smooth by everything between then and this.

He knelt. Set the glass on the bare earth at the exact line where the shimmer met the grass, where sanctuary ended and weather began. Then he stood, and raised his hands, and for the first time since he was a child hiding his gift from everyone including himself, he reached for a storm with intent—with *purpose*—and did not hold back.

The sky answered instantly. It had been waiting thirteen turns; perhaps it had been waiting twenty years. Clouds boiled up over the ridge from nowhere, swallowing the moon, and the first thunder rolled down the valley walls like a drum announcing something. Wind came with it, hard and clean, smelling of rain and ozone and distance. His hair lifted. His scars ached—the old ones, the ones he'd earned burying his gift—and then, strangely, they stopped aching, as if the storm recognized them and forgave them.

"Ulyana," Ryn said, and now there was fear in her voice, because she had seen what he could do when he was afraid, and this was not fear. This was something larger. "Whatever you're going to do—do it fast. The mountain felt you in the pass. It feels you now."

"I know," he said. "Let it feel me."

He looked down at the glass. Inside, his other half pressed against the wall nearest him, silver and urgent, and he realized with a strange calm that it wasn't trying to escape. It was reaching. The way a hand reaches. The way he had reached, all his life, toward a horizon he was never allowed to walk to.

"Half of me was sold before I was born," he said aloud, to the glass, to the night, to anyone listening—to Sef, wherever he was, behind whatever door he'd chosen to hold. "Half of me kept a valley hidden. Neither half agreed to any of it. So this is the only thing I've ever decided completely on my own."

He raised the storm higher. Lightning walked the cloud-tops in slow, branching steps, and the wardline shimmered brighter, straining, its song climbing to a note that made Ryn cover her ears. Somewhere below, lights were coming on in the exile houses. Figures moving in doorways. They could feel it too—the whole valley could feel it, the pressure change, the wrongness, the end of something.

"They'll hate you," Ryn said. "Some of them. Maybe most."

"Probably."

"They'll say you chose your pride over their safety."

"Some will." He crouched, took up the glass again, held it at arm's length over the wardline stone. "But in a year, or ten, when the court comes asking for new souls to replace the old currency—because it will, Ryn, that's what courts like Vessarine's *are*—there won't be a valley of sleepers to harvest. There'll be a valley of people who learned to stand in open weather. That's not pride. That's the only inheritance I have to give."

The moon broke through a tear in the clouds directly overhead, full and white and merciless, and the thirteenth turn completed itself. Maren's charm, hanging at his throat, went dark—not faded, not broken, simply *finished*, its work done, its warning expired.

"Now," Ulyana said, "it's mine."

He brought the glass down.

It struck the wardstone and shattered—and the sound was not the sound of breaking glass. It was the sound of a held breath released after twenty years. Light erupted upward, silver-white, and the storm came down to meet it, and for one impossible moment Ulyana stood inside his own soul returning—every buried summer squall, every suppressed winter gale, every hour of silence he had paid for with scar tissue, all of it flooding home through the wound in the world he had just made. He screamed, or laughed, or both; he couldn't tell. The lightning found him and passed through him and he passed through it, and he was the storm and the storm was him, whole, entire, *unclaimed*.

And the wards fell.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible part. They fell the way a candle goes out—one moment the shimmer ran silver along the valley's rim, and the next it simply wasn't there, and the air changed, and every exile in Thorn Valley woke at once to a silence they had never heard: the absence of the song that had sung them to sleep their whole lives. Ulyana felt it go like a tooth leaving the jaw. Felt the valley exhale. Felt, beneath the thunder, a hundred doors opening at once.

The storm raged for a time because storms must, and then, because he asked it to, it gentled—rain instead of ruin, washing the valley clean, putting out no fires because there were none, soaking the fields that would need to feed people who no longer had walls to hide behind. By the time the clouds tore apart, the moon was setting, and Ulyana stood in wet grass at the edge of a borderless land, shaking, empty, *full*, with glass dust glittering on the wardstone like frost.

Ryn was staring at him. At the sky. At the valley.

"It's gone," she said. "The wards are actually gone."

"Yes."

"They'll come. The court. The hunters. Everyone who ever wanted what this valley hid."

"Yes."

She looked at him a long moment, and then she did something she had never done in all the months he'd known her: she laughed, tired and ragged and real. "Good," she said. "Then they can finally meet us."

They came down together as the sun rose—not fleeing, not hiding, walking openly along the road into Thorn Valley for the first time in Ulyana's life without the wardline checking him at the gate. The exiles were already gathered in the commons, drawn out by the silence, by the storm, by the animal knowledge that the shape of their world had changed in the night. Elder Maren stood at their head, ancient and upright, her seal-ring glinting, her face unreadable. When she saw Ulyana coming, her chin lifted, and he read the look correctly: she had known this might come. Perhaps she had arranged for it to come. The famine year, the favor, the sale—she had spent a lifetime paying installments on a debt she hoped he'd never collect.

He stopped before the crowd. Rain dripped from his hair. Glass dust still clung to his sleeves.

"The sanctuary is ended," he said, and his voice carried without effort, because the storm was in it now, always, patient as weather. "The wards were built on a theft—half my soul, sold before I was born, and the souls of others besides. I've returned what was mine. I won't apologize for it. But I'm not leaving you defenseless, because you never were defenseless—you were *hidden*, and hiding was costing more than any of us knew."

Silence. Then an old woman near the front said, "Without the wards, the court can take us."

"The court could always take us," Ulyana said. "It already had. Ask your ledgers. Ask your elders." He looked at Maren, and Maren, to her credit, did not look away. "What the wards prevented was never the court. It was each other. We forgot how to stand together because the walls made standing together unnecessary. Well. The walls are down. The weather is open. And I am done being currency—mine, yours, anyone's."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then the old woman stepped forward—out of the crowd, onto the wet grass, past where the wardline used to run—and stood beside him. Then another. Then a family with two children. Then a dozen. Then most of them, hesitating, frightened, furious in some cases, but *choosing*, each one, to take one step into a world with no border.

Maren came last. She stopped in front of Ulyana, and from her sleeve she drew a folded paper—old, yellowed, sealed with the mark he had seen in Vessarine's vault. The original favor. The receipt for his soul.

"Burn it," she said, holding it out. "You've earned that much. And then—" her voice cracked, just slightly, the first crack he had ever heard in it— "then tell me what happens now. Because I built the walls, boy, and I no longer remember how to live without them."

Ulyana took the paper. But before he could answer, hoofbeats sounded on the north road—fast, many, and coming down out of the hills where no valley rider had traveled in a century. A rider burst from the tree line, lathered, wild-eyed, and Ulyana knew the lean gray face under the hood before the horse even stopped.

Sef Idris. Alive. Changed—his eyes held a stillness that hadn't been there before, and his hands, gripping the reins, bore marks like he'd gripped something burning—but alive, and carrying a leather satchel that radiated cold the way a hearth radiates heat.

"The court withdrew," Sef said, sliding from the saddle, legs buckling, caught by Ryn. "When the glass broke, the contract burned—Vessarine's whole ledger, gone to ash. But Ulyana—" he looked up, and his voice dropped to almost nothing— "they weren't the only ones watching the wards fall. There are others coming. Others who've waited a hundred years for this valley to be *findable*."

* * *

The paper in Ulyana's hand was dry despite the rain, as if it refused even now to be part of the weather. He looked at it—his name, his price, his parents' signatures fading like footprints in snow—and fed it to a small flame that leapt obediently from his fingertips, the easiest magic he had ever worked. It burned green, then gold, then was gone.

"Others," he said. "What others?"

Sef's gray eyes went to the northern horizon, where the morning light was just beginning to touch peaks no one in Thorn Valley had named in living memory.

"The ones who wrote the prophecy," he said.
