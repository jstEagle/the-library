## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The merchant from Aldermarch smelled of tallow and road dust, and he carried the sea inside his head.

Sable Amberdale watched him from the shadow of the provisioning stall, hands buried in the pockets of a coat two sizes too large, and waited for him to do what travelers always did in the Veilless Valley: stop, look around at the gray slate roofs and the brown river and the hills that folded in on themselves like a fist, and think, *there is nothing here worth remembering.*

That was the moment. That was always the moment. A traveler's memory was like a purse left open on a table — you didn't steal it so much as help yourself to what spilled out. And this man, a wool-factor by his ledger-case and his boots worn soft at the instep, had walked into the valley carrying the most extravagant thing Sable had tasted all season.

The sea.

He'd stood at the rail of a coastal packet once, years ago, and the memory had never dulled: green water rising in glassy hills, spray stinging his lips, the whole horizon breathing. Sable could feel it radiating off him like heat off a hearthstone, faint but unmistakable, the way you could feel a lit candle in a cold room without looking at it.

*Patience,* Sable told themselves, and picked up an apple from the stall as cover, turning it over with fingers that had learned stillness before they'd learned letters.

The valley made patience easy. It made everything easy, if you were built for it. The Veilless Valley earned its name honestly: no magic crossed its borders, none lived within them. Travelers said it felt like walking through deep water with your ears plugged — a muffled, flattened place where charms guttered and wards went blind and even the fae's glamours slid off like rain off waxed canvas. Ordinary folk loved it for exactly that reason. Nothing followed you in. Nothing *found* you here.

Which meant that whatever a traveler carried in their head arrived intact, undefended, unguarded — because who needed guards against a valley where magic couldn't exist?

Nobody, apparently, had ever considered that a person might be born here who could do what Sable did.

It wasn't magic. Sable had decided that long ago, the way you decide the sky is blue — not because it's true, necessarily, but because deciding otherwise would break something. Magic couldn't live in the valley. Therefore what Sable did wasn't magic. It was just... taking. A hand slipped into an open purse. A memory lifted clean out of a mind like a coin from a well, leaving the water no poorer than it could notice.

The trick was that nobody missed one memory. Not really. The mind smoothed over the gap the way a river smoothed over a stolen stone. The wool-factor would go home convinced he'd seen the sea more distantly, or in worse weather, or perhaps he'd simply remember being *on* the ship without the vividness, the salt-sting of it. He would not miss what he could not name.

Sable had learned early that hunger had a shape, and the shape was other people's best days.

The merchant haggled over rope and hardtack, pocketed his change, and turned toward the ferry landing with his collar up against the wind. As he passed the stall, he paused — glanced at Sable, at the apple turning idly in those thin fingers — and gave the small nod one stranger gives another.

There. That flicker of attention, that half-second of eye contact. That was the door.

Sable took it.

It was never violent. That was the part people would never believe, if people ever knew. It felt like leaning close to a window on a winter night and breathing on the glass — except the fog that bloomed belonged to someone else, and when Sable drew back, they carried the shape of it with them. The wool-factor's attention touched theirs, and Sable reached along that thread and found the memory where it sat near the surface, still warm from the road, and lifted it out with the care of a pickpocket lifting a watch.

The world went green.

Not the valley's green — the tired olive of the hillsides, the muddy brown-green of the river — but a green that had never known dirt. Green light came up from *below*, through water deep as a cathedral, and the deck rolled under Sable's borrowed feet, and the wind hit with a taste in it, salt and iodine and something almost sweet, and the horizon —

The horizon *moved*. It rose and fell, rose and fell, a wall of water standing between the ship and the edge of the world, and it breathed, and it was alive, and it went on forever.

Sable stopped walking. They were dimly aware of their body doing something embarrassing — standing dead still in the market square with an apple in one hand and their eyes wide open, tears starting in the corners from the wind that wasn't there. They didn't care. They let the memory play out to its end: the merchant leaning on the rail, a woman laughing beside him, gulls screaming white against the green, and the vast indifferent gorgeous *bigness* of a world that did not end at the valley's rim.

Then it settled into them, the way all the taken things settled, filed away in the dark archive behind their own small stock of memories — a childhood of gray mornings, a hundred thousand gray afternoons, the pawnshop below, the hovel above, and nothing, nothing, nothing else.

"Oi." The fruit-seller rapped the stall. "You buying that or communing with it?"

Sable blinked the wet from their eyes and smiled the smile that kept them fed. "Buying. Sorry. Long night."

They paid with a coin that had passed through their hands four times already, bit it out of habit, and walked home through streets that knew them and forgot them in equal measure.

* * *

Harrowgate's pawnshop sat where the river bent, squat and crooked, three stories of timber and slate that leaned east like an old man listening for something. The ground floor was Tam Voss's kingdom: a cluttered cave of pledged coats and cracked fiddles, wedding rings in velvet trays, a taxidermied pike glaring down from the wall with the eternal outrage of its kind. Sable lived above it, in the attic room under the roof, in a space barely tall enough to stand in, furnished with a cot, a stool, a candle stub, and forty-one small jars.

The jars were the problem. Sable knew they were the problem. You didn't keep forty-one jars of other people's memories lined up on a shelf unless something in you was broken in a way you'd stopped examining.

Each jar held a scrap of cloth or a bead or a button — anchors, Sable called them, though there was no real need for them. The memories lived in Sable's head regardless. But holding the anchor helped hold the *shape* of the thing, the way a pressed flower held the summer. A brass button for the wool-factor's sea. A scrap of red silk for a peddler's memory of his daughter's wedding, the bride's face shining through her veil. A chipped die for a gambler's single perfect night when every throw came up his way. A pine sprig, going brown now, for a forester's recollection of snow falling in a forest so quiet you could hear it land.

Forty-one windows into lives Sable would never live. They took only the beautiful ones. That was the rule, self-imposed, endlessly rationalized: never take grief, never take shame, never take anything a person needed to stay themselves. Take the surplus. Take the shine. Leave everyone whole enough.

It was theft dressed as mercy, and Sable knew that too, and took the memories anyway, because the alternative was the valley itself — the valley with its forgetting fog that rolled off the hills some mornings and ate the edges of everything, the valley where Sable had been found on a doorstep twenty-odd years ago and never claimed, the valley where a person could grow up surrounded by two hundred souls and belong to none of them.

"You're back late." Tam Voss didn't look up from his ledger when Sable came through the shop. He was a narrow man, gray as his own inventory, with spectacles thick enough to start fires and hands that could price anything in the valley in under a breath. He'd raised Sable the way he handled his goods: carefully, at arm's length, with an accountant's suspicion of attachment. He had never once asked where Sable went or what they did there. In twenty years, he had asked exactly one question about Sable's origins, gotten no answer, and never asked again.

"Market ran long," Sable said.

"Mm." His pen scratched. "Ferry brought a wool-factor up from Aldermarch. Big fellow. Bought rope."

Something in Sable's chest tightened, old and automatic. Twenty years of practice kept it off their face. "Did he."

"He'll be gone on the morning tide." Scratch, scratch. "They're all gone on the morning tide. That's the arrangement." He looked up then, over the spectacles, and his eyes behind the glass were shrewd and tired and — Sable had never had a word for it until now, watching him across the cluttered shop — *afraid*. Faintly, constantly, the way a man is afraid who has agreed to forget why. "Supper's on the shelf. Don't let the candle burn the house down. I've no insurance against arson, only against flood, and the river's been low all year."

It was the closest thing to affection he offered, and Sable took it as such, and climbed the stairs.

* * *

The attic room was cold. It was always cold; the chimney drew badly and the roof leaked philosophy as well as rain. Sable lit the candle stub, set the brass button beside it, and sat cross-legged on the cot with the sea rolling green and enormous inside them.

This was the ritual. This was the whole point. Anyone could steal a thing; the crime was nothing without the savoring.

They closed their eyes and walked the deck again. Salt on the lips. Gulls like torn paper against the sky. The woman laughing — Sable had begun, on the third pass, to wonder about her. The merchant's wife? Someone he'd wanted to be? The memory didn't say, and Sable had no way to ask, and that was the peculiar loneliness of the trade: you got the highlight reel of a stranger's life with all the context stripped away, like finding a single page of a letter and building the rest from longing.

*I'll see it someday,* Sable told the sea, the way they told it every time. *The actual sea. I'll walk out of this valley and stand on a shore and it won't be borrowed.*

The thought had less force every year. The valley's borders weren't walls, exactly, but they might as well have been. Everyone who left came back changed — not magically, the valley forbade that, but practically. There was nothing for a person with no family name, no trade beyond pawnshop clerking, no story. And there was the other thing, the thing Sable never said aloud even in the privacy of their own skull: the one time, at fourteen, they'd walked as far as the western pass, the fog had come down thick and personal, and somewhere in the white they'd heard — or dreamed they'd heard — a voice saying *not yet, little thief, not yet*, and they had run home and never spoken of it and never tried again.

Magic couldn't enter the valley. But maybe it could wait at the border. Maybe it had been waiting for Sable specifically, all these years, patient as a creditor.

Below, the shop bell chimed. Footsteps. Tam's voice, low and courteous, doing the evening's last trade. Sable ignored it, the way they ignored all of Tam's business, and reached for the jar with the red silk instead, needing something warm after the sea's cold enormity.

And that was when they noticed the frost.

A single fern of ice, feathering across the inside of the attic window, delicate as lace. Sable frowned at it. The evening was chilly but not *that* chilly, and the room's bad chimney usually kept it warmer than outside, not colder. They reached out and touched the glass. The frost melted under their fingertip — and bloomed again the moment they pulled away, spreading another inch, another whorl, like something on the far side of the pane drawing breath.

Sable sat very still.

The valley was dead to magic. That was the first fact of their life, older than their own name. Frost did not bloom indoors. Frost did not bloom *against the direction of the weather*. And it certainly did not bloom in patterns that looked, now that Sable made themselves actually look, less like ice and more like writing — thin, branching characters in no alphabet sold in any shop in Harrowgate.

Downstairs, the bell chimed again. The footsteps crossed the shop and stopped directly beneath the attic, and Tam's voice said something too quiet to catch, and then a second voice answered — a stranger's voice, level and unhurried, and underneath its calm Sable heard a sound like wind moving through a very empty room.

The frost on the window finished its final flourish and stopped, complete, a message in a language of ice.

Sable looked at the brass button on the shelf, at the sea sleeping inside it, and understood with sudden, total clarity that the morning tide would carry the wool-factor away, and that nothing in the valley would ever be arranged quite the same way again.

They blew out the candle, and went downstairs to see who had come calling.
