## 2. A Buyer With Empty Eyes

The bell above the pawnshop door had a cracked voice, a thin *tink* like a coin dropped down a well, and it announced the stranger at half past the hour when sensible folk were already abed.

Sable came down the back stairs with the candle held high and found Master Odilon already in the shop, nightshirt tucked hastily into his trousers, one hand resting on the counter in a way that was meant to look casual and looked instead like a man steadying himself against a wave. He was seventy-one years old and had faced down drunk wool-factors, a fire that ate half of Tanner's Row, and once, memorably, a goat loose in the back room. He had never looked like this.

"Customer," he said, which was unnecessary, because there was plainly a customer.

The stranger stood in the middle of the shop floor where the lamplight from the street did not quite reach, wrapped in a hooded traveling cloak the color of wet slate. Rain beaded on the shoulders of it. That was the first wrong thing, though Sable would only count it later: the rain outside had stopped before midnight, and the cobbles under the eaves had been dry as chalk when Sable had leaned out to watch the frost finish its writing on the glass.

"Good evening," the stranger said. The voice was neither man's nor woman's, or perhaps both, pitched somewhere pleasant and unplaceable, like a note hummed through a wall. "I'm told this establishment buys and sells."

"We buy," Odilon said carefully. "We don't sell what we haven't got."

"You have what I want." The hood tilted, and for a moment Sable thought they saw the edge of a face beneath it — pale, smooth, unfinished-looking, like wax left too near a flame. "A memory. A specific one."

Sable's trade was quiet work. Travelers came through the Veilless Valley because it was the one place in two worlds where nothing followed you — no debts, no curses, no magic — and some of them carried things they were desperate to put down. Grief mostly. Guilt sometimes. Once, a soldier's whole war, which Sable had taken in three installments and still woke some nights tasting gun-smoke that belonged to another man's lungs. They lifted memories the way a leech takes blood: painlessly, if you knew the places to press, and the taker always felt it afterward, a hollowness shaped exactly like what was gone.

Nobody had ever come asking for something specific. Nobody had ever come knowing what they wanted before they walked in.

"What memory?" Sable asked.

The hood turned toward them. Sable felt the attention settle on them like a hand on the back of the neck.

"A lullaby."

* * *

Odilon poured tea nobody drank, because that was what Odilon did when the world misbehaved — he made things warm and small until they agreed to behave. The four of them — three, rather; the stranger declined to sit, declined tea, declined the stool by the stove with a small shake of the head that suggested stools were a custom of some foreign tribe — arranged themselves around the shop's back table among the unclaimed pledges: a taxidermied owl missing one eye, a music box that played the same six notes forever, a wedding dress in a box yellowed with somebody else's hope.

"A lullaby," Odilon repeated. "You want us to sell you a lullaby."

"I want to buy one. From this young person." The stranger's gloved fingers unfolded on the table, long and precise. "I understand the arrangement. You take what people wish to lose. I am told that occasionally, in the taking, other things come along. Stowaways. Fragments. Things the thief didn't mean to keep." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "I believe you are holding something of mine."

"It's not yours if someone gave it up," Sable said. "That's the trade. Given freely or not at all."

"Given freely," the stranger agreed, and something in the way the words sat in the air made the candle flames lean, all three of them, toward the hooded figure. "Freely given. Long ago. By someone who no longer remembers giving."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. They were good at this — at reading travelers, at knowing which drawer of a person to open — and everything their craft knew told them the thing across the table was not a traveler. Was not, possibly, a person. But the valley was dead ground; nothing magical could stand here, everyone knew that, it was the whole point of the place, the reason Sable's whole strange life worked. Whatever this was, it must be a trick. Tricks could be handled. Tricks had seams.

"Describe it," Sable said. "If it's mine to sell, I'll know it when you say it."

The stranger was quiet a moment. When they spoke again, the pleasantness had thinned, and underneath it was something older and much less comfortable, the sound of a lock turning very slowly.

"*Sleep, my heart, the veil is drawn,*" they recited. "*The moon has climbed, the sun has gone. Two doors stand shut, one door stands true — sleep, my heart, I'll watch for you.*"

The words went into Sable like cold water into a crack.

They had never heard them before. They were certain they had never heard them before, in the way you are certain of your own name, and at the same time the melody arrived whole behind the words — the little falling third at *veil is drawn*, the lift at *one door stands true*, the way the last line should be sung almost spoken, almost a promise, the way a mother —

The way a mother sings it.

Sable's hands were flat on the table. They did not remember putting them there. The shop smelled of tea and lamp oil and dust and it was suddenly the farthest place from home they had ever stood, though they had never in their life been farther than the quarry ridge.

"No," Sable said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them, and cost them more than shouting would have. "Never heard it."

"Liar," said the stranger, gently, without heat, the way you'd note the weather. "But that's all right. Lying is just a memory you're holding closed. Shall I hold it open?"

"Don't," Odilon said, sharp, and then, catching himself, softer: "Don't threaten the boy in my shop."

"Not a boy," said the stranger, and not unkindly, and Sable felt that land somewhere old and unhealed too. "Very well. Name your price. Gold — I have gold. Or shall we say gold and a question answered? Everyone wants a question answered eventually."

"How do you know I even have it?" Sable asked. "Fragments. Stowaways. You said it yourself — maybe I took it off some pilgrim ten years ago without knowing. Maybe it's buried under forty other things. Digging costs. Digging hurts. For me, I mean." They leaned back. "You'd be paying me to hurt myself."

"Yes," the stranger said simply. "I know. I'm sorry for it. It must be done tonight, and it must be done here, and it must be done by you." The gloved hands folded again. "There are rules older than either of us, thief, and I am more bound by them than you will ever guess. If I could reach into your head myself, believe me —" a small, terrible laugh — "the price would be different."

The candle flames stood straight again. Outside, the wind worried at the shutter, and beneath it, so faint Sable might have invented it, something sang. Two doors stand shut, one door stands true.

"Gold first," Sable said. "Then I'll see what I can find."

* * *

The stranger counted out coins with a moneylender's precision: twenty crowns, then twenty more, then a third stack, each piece stamped with a face Sable didn't recognize — a crowned woman, stern, her hair braided with what might have been thorns. Aldermarch minting, but old. Older than the current king. Older, Odilon murmured later, than the current dynasty.

"Now," the stranger said, "if you please. Close your eyes. Go down into your cellar, thief, and bring me the song."

Taking a memory out was easier than explaining it. There was no cellar, of course; there was a kind of attention, a settling, like sinking through warm water, and the traveler's mind opened to Sable the way a house opens when you know where the loose board over the key is. Most minds were cluttered. Most minds were generous without meaning to be — grief spilling into guilt spilling into shame, drawers left open everywhere.

Sable sank.

And stopped.

Their own mind was not cluttered. Their own mind was a corridor, and every door along it was locked, and the locks were not padlocks or bolts but seals — round, smooth, deliberate, pressed into the wood like wax stamps, each one marked with a sigil Sable had never seen and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own reflection even upside-down. A circle crossed by a river. A circle crossed by a river, repeated down the whole corridor, dozens of doors, dozens of sealed rooms inside their own skull.

They had never noticed. In twenty-odd years of going down into themselves, they had never once noticed that the house of their own head had locked doors, because the locks did not feel like locks. They felt like walls. They felt like the shape of the world.

Except one.

At the corridor's end, one seal was cracked — hairline fracture running through the river-mark, thin as a frost-flower on glass — and behind it, faint as light under a door, a melody was playing. *Two doors stand shut, one door stands true.*

Sable did not touch it. Every instinct they owned, every hard-won rule of the trade, screamed the same word: *not yours, not yours, not yours.* You did not open a sealed room in someone else's house. And this — whatever this was, whatever they were — this was a house where the doors had been locked on purpose, by someone who loved them enough to weld them shut.

They surfaced.

"Well?" the stranger said.

"There's something there." Sable kept their voice level. "Sealed. Not by me. I couldn't open it if I wanted to, and I don't want to."

The hood was very still. Then the stranger reached up, unhurried, and pushed the hood back.

The face beneath was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and wrong the way winter is wrong. Skin like moonstone. Hair white as the frost on Sable's window. And the eyes —

The eyes held no reflections.

The lamp was right there, three feet away, flame steady, and the stranger's eyes showed nothing of it. No flame, no table, no Sable leaning forward with their heart hammering. Just depth, gray and fathomless, like looking down a well and seeing no water at the bottom, only more well.

In the pawnshop's dusty mirror behind the counter, Sable could see the whole scene laid out: Odilon frozen with the teapot half-raised, the candles burning, the stranger's face turned toward them.

The mirror showed the candles. The mirror showed Odilon. The mirror showed the empty chair where the stranger stood.

"Ah," the stranger said softly, watching Sable look. "You see. Yes. This is why it must be you. This is why I pay gold for what I could take." Those reflectionless eyes did not blink. Did they blink? Sable genuinely could not say. "I gave that song away, thief. I gave it, and it was taken from me in the same breath, and I have been empty in that one particular place for longer than your valley has had a name. I only want it back. One small song. One small stolen thing, returned to its owner. Is that so monstrous?"

"Who are you?" Odilon whispered. The teapot was trembling audibly against its lid.

"Someone whose name is a debt," the stranger said, "and therefore cannot be spoken here." They rose. The motion was fluid and slightly too slow, like something moving through water, or like something remembering how bodies worked. "Keep the gold. Consider it payment for the looking. But hear me, thief —" and now the pleasantness was entirely gone, and what remained was vast and patient and terribly sad — "the seal on that door is failing. Whoever set it spent themselves doing it, and spent things do not last. When it breaks — and it will break, soon, within a season, within a month — everything behind it comes out at once, into a world that is already hunting what you are. You have until the eclipse. Find the rest of the seals before the world finds you. Find whoever marked that cloth —"

The stranger stopped. Tilted their head, listening to something far away.

"— ah," they said. "It's time. It's earlier than I thought. Of course it is. It always is."

"What's time?" Sable demanded. "What cloth? What am I?"

But the stranger was already moving toward the door, and the candles were guttering though there was no draft, and the temperature of the room was falling in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

"One kindness, since you looked and did not grab," the stranger said at the threshold, not turning around. "The song. Listen to it while you still can. It isn't a warning and it isn't a spell. It's a promise someone made you before you could remember promises. People spend their whole lives hunting for less."

The door opened. Cold poured in like floodwater.

And frost bloomed indoors.

It raced across the back window in feathering white branches, spread over the teapot, the counter, the pledged wedding dress, silvering everything, and the valley — the dead valley, the magicless valley, the one place in two worlds where nothing of the other side could follow — the valley did nothing at all to stop it. Frost ferns crawled up the wall and around the doorframe, and where the stranger had stood there was nothing but a ring of unmelted ice on the floorboards, perfect as a coin, and the smell of something green and distant, like a forest after snow.

Then the cold snapped off like a snuffed candle, and the shop was only a shop, warm and shabby and smelling of tea, and Odilon set the teapot down with a clatter and sat heavily in the stranger's abandoned chair.

"Sable." His voice shook. He was looking at the ring of ice, at the frost already fading from the window, at the stacks of ancient gold. "Boy. We need to talk about the night I found you."

Upstairs, forgotten, the brass button on the shelf held its little sea, and the sea, for the first time since Sable had stolen it, was storming.
