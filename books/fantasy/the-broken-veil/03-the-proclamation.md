## 3. The Proclamation

The bell woke the valley before the sun did.

Sable heard it from the loft above the pawnshop, a cracked bronze note rolling up through the floorboards and into their chest, and for three heartbeats they lay still in the dark, cataloguing it. Not the chapel bell — that rang only on high holy days, and this was no holy day. Not the quarry bell that warned of rockfall. This was lower, slower, struck at intervals too deliberate to be anything but official.

Someone had brought the crown to Veilless Valley.

They dressed in the dark out of habit, fingers finding buckles without light, and came down the narrow stair into the shop's back room where Old Tam was already awake. He stood at the cold hearth with his back to the door, and he did not turn when Sable entered, which was itself an answer to a question Sable hadn't asked yet.

"You heard it," Tam said.

"I heard a bell."

"That's what I said." He still didn't turn. In the grey pre-dawn light his shoulders looked narrower than they had a month ago, as though something inside him were slowly being winched tighter. "Stay in today. Mind the counter if you like, but keep the shutters half-drawn. There'll be criers by midmorning."

"Criers." Sable leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. "For what? Nobody proclaims anything in the valley. The valley doesn't care about crowns. That's rather the point of it."

Tam's hands moved on the mantel, straightening a candlestick that was already straight. "Crowns change their minds about what they care about."

Sable knew better than to push before breakfast. They'd learned Tam's rhythms over seventeen years the way a sailor learns tides: when to press, when to wait, when to pretend not to notice the way he counted the coins in the strongbox twice each night now instead of once. So they made tea from the bitter valley herb that passed for it, set two chipped cups on the table, and ate bread that tasted of nothing much, because everything in the valley tasted of nothing much, and waited.

The criers came at midmorning, exactly as promised.

There were three of them, which was obscene — three grown men in crown livery of deep blue and silver, standing in the market square before a crowd that had doubled in size since the first bell. Sable watched from the mouth of Tanner's Row, hood up, one shoulder against the old grain-post, and felt the crowd's mood the way they felt everything else: as texture, as pressure, as a thousand small signals of fear and curiosity stacking against their skin.

The middle crier unrolled a scroll with theatrical slowness. When he spoke, his voice carried with the flat, trained projection of a man who had said these words in forty towns and would say them in forty more.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Sovereign of Aldermarch and its Territories: let it be known that a state of peril exists within the realm. The Veil between worlds grows thin. Unnatural phenomena multiply. Crops fail where they should flourish. Children are born wrong."

A murmur went through the crowd. Sable kept their breathing even.

"Therefore, by order of the Crown, a Purge of the Gifted is hereby declared. All persons possessing unnatural aptitude — hedge-craft, hex-work, foresight, charm-speaking, memory-craft, or any art deriving from the Otherworld — shall present themselves for examination by the Crown's appointed inquisitors. Examination is mandatory. Examination is merciful. Those who conceal their gifts commit treason against realm and king alike."

*Examination.* The word sat wrong in Sable's ears, like a coin that looked right but weighed nothing. They had spent their whole life around words used to dress up uglier things. *Examination* was the kind of word men used when they meant *taking.*

"Where do they take them?" someone called from the crowd — Marta Ostrow, the dyer, whose voice Sable knew from a hundred haggled bargains. "The ones who go for examination. Where?"

The crier smiled. It was a professional smile, sanded smooth. "Those who pass examination return to their homes, cleansed and grateful."

"And those who don't pass?"

"Examination is merciful," the crier repeated, and rolled up his scroll.

The crowd did not disperse so much as dissolve, people peeling away in twos and threes, heads bent together, voices low. Sable stayed a moment longer, watching the criers. And then the middle one turned, scanning the square with the idle thoroughness of a man taking inventory, and his gaze crossed Sable's face for half a heartbeat — just long enough for Sable to see that his eyes were not doing what eyes did. They moved. They saw. But there was nothing behind the seeing, no flicker of thought or judgment, only a flat patient attention like a hook waiting in water.

Sable walked home the long way, and did not let themselves hurry until the lane curved out of sight of the square.

* * *

Tam had the strongbox open when they got back.

That alone told Sable everything. In seventeen years, Sable had seen the strongbox opened perhaps a dozen times, always at night, always with the shutters barred. Now it sat open on the counter in full daylight, and Tam was feeding papers into the hearth one at a time — not burning them in handfuls but reading each one first, his lips moving, before consigning it to the flames.

"What are you doing?" Sable asked, though they could see perfectly well.

"Housekeeping."

"Tam."

He fed another page to the fire. It was a bill of sale; Sable caught the name of a client from two towns over, a woman who'd pawned a silver locket last spring. Harmless paper. Unless you were looking for patterns. Unless you were building a map of everyone who'd ever trusted Old Tam with something they wanted forgotten.

"The proclamation," Sable said. "You knew it was coming."

"I suspected."

"For how long?"

"Long enough." He glanced up at last, and his eyes were red-rimmed and very tired. "Word travels slow to the valley, but it travels. There've been arrests in the river towns since harvest. A hedgewitch in Millbrook taken in the night. Two brothers in Corren's Ford who could calm livestock — gone. No trial. No return." He fed another page to the fire. "The crown has decided magic is a disease, and diseases get quarantined. Or cut out."

"They can't touch anyone here." Sable heard how young their own voice sounded and hated it. "The valley's dead, Tam. You've told me that my whole life. Magic can't follow anyone in. That's why people come here to hide. That's why—"

"That's why *you're* here." Tam said it quietly, and the fire crackled into the silence. "Yes. I know why you're here. Sit down, Sable."

Sable did not sit down. "I'm not gifted."

"No," Tam agreed. "Not in any way an inquisitor would find in an afternoon's looking. Your talent is quiet. It needs a mind to open, a hand to hold, a moment of trust. No one's going to catch you lifting memories in a market square." He paused. "But the criers aren't stopping here. There are more towns past the valley, and the roads between them, and the roads are where you work. And there is another matter entirely, which I have been putting off telling you for seventeen years, and it appears the hour has arrived whether I like it or not."

Something cold slid down Sable's spine. "What matter?"

Tam wiped his hands on his apron — an old habit, a shopkeeper's ritual, buying himself a moment the way he'd taught Sable to buy moments with silence — and went to the back of the shop, to the cabinet beneath the stairs where he kept the things that were not for sale. He knelt, worked the hidden latch Sable had never been able to find despite years of trying, and drew out a small wooden box, plain and dark with age.

He set it on the counter between them.

"When you ask me about your parents," Tam said, "I tell you I don't know. That's been true, mostly. What I know fits in this box, and none of it is enough, and all of it is dangerous, and I had hoped to die before handing it to you." He pushed the box across the wood. "Open it."

Sable's hands were steady. They noticed that about themselves distantly, the way they noticed everything — the thief's habit of watching their own body from a step away. They lifted the lid.

Inside lay a fold of cloth, wrapped around something soft. The cloth was fine-woven, a grey so pale it was nearly white, and across its surface ran markings Sable had never seen anywhere in the valley or in any traveler's goods: lines that seemed to move when looked at directly, curling inward like smoke frozen mid-dance, arranged around a central sigil like spokes around a hub. The cloth did not feel like wool or silk or linen. It felt like holding a breath.

Unfolding it released a smell — green and strange, rain on stone in a country Sable had never visited — and inside lay a second item: a small wooden token, carved with the same moving sigil, worn smooth as a riverstone by years of handling.

"This was around you," Tam said, "when I found you on my doorstep. Winter night, seventeen years ago. Wrapped in this cloth, with this token tucked against your chest, and not a sound out of you. Quiet as a held breath. And frost on my door in July."

"Frost," Sable repeated. Their voice came out flat. "In summer."

"In the Veilless Valley, where nothing of that kind can exist." Tam's laugh was short and without humor. "I told myself it was a trick of the cold. I told myself a great many things. But I knew what I was looking at, Sable. I'm a pawnbroker. I know valuable things from worthless ones, and I know marked things from plain ones, and that cloth is marked. Someone wrapped you in it to hide you from something. Or to mark you *for* something. I've never learned which, and I've spent seventeen years being glad I didn't."

Sable lifted the token. It was warm. Not sun-warm — the hearth was cold, the morning chill still in the room — but warm the way a living thing is warm, faintly, stubbornly, from somewhere inside itself. The sigil-lines shifted under their thumb, and for one vertiginous instant Sable felt the valley around them the way they sometimes felt a sleeping merchant's mind: vast, layered, and pretending very hard to be empty.

Then it passed, and the token was just wood, and the valley was just dead air, and Tam was just an old man watching them with terror he wasn't bothering to hide anymore.

"Why now?" Sable asked. "Why tell me today?"

"Because of the criers." Tam closed the box lid gently, over Sable's hands. "Because the man leading the examinations — the one they call Wren Vael — does not stop at hedge-witches and charm-speakers. She takes *marked* things. Things wrapped in sigils. Things left on doorsteps with frost in July." He met Sable's eyes. "If she ever comes to this valley — and she will, because the crown's reach grows every year and the valley's invisibility shrinks with it — she will look for exactly what I've been hiding. And I am an old man, Sable. I cannot fight her. I can barely lie to a tax assessor. Everything I have done for seventeen years, I have done to keep you ordinary. To keep you *here*. And I am beginning to think that keeping you here is the most dangerous thing I could have done."

The fire had burned the papers down to ash. Sable became aware, slowly, of the room around them: the counter scarred by a century of haggling, the shelves of unclaimed pledges, the narrow stair, the loft above where they'd slept every night of their remembered life. Every inch of it built to be small. Every inch of it, they understood now, built to be a hiding place.

Shelter, they thought, is just a cage with kinder bars.

"I need to think," Sable said.

"Aye." Tam took the box back, and hesitated, and then pressed the token into Sable's palm instead, folding their fingers over it. "Keep that. If the day comes when you must run, take the cloth too. Don't ask me what it means — I don't know. But whoever left you chose it carefully, and careful things save lives."

Sable climbed the stairs to the loft and sat on the edge of their cot with the token in their fist, listening to the sounds of the town below: carts, voices, the bell tolling the hour. Ordinary sounds. Dead-magic sounds. The sounds of a world that had held them like cupped water for seventeen years.

Except the water had been draining the whole time, and they'd never thought to check the level.

They should sleep. They didn't. Somewhere past midnight, drifting on the border of a doze they refused to fall into, Sable became aware of a sound from the street below — footsteps, unhurried, stopping outside the shop. Then a voice, low and pleasant, speaking to no one Sable could see:

"Lovely little valley. Nothing here at all. Nothing at all."

And then laughter, soft and dry as frost forming, and the footsteps moving on — toward the inn where strangers lodged, in a town that had no strangers.

Sable sat up in the dark, the token warm against their skin, and did not sleep again.
