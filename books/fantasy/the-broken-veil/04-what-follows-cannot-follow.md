## 4. What Follows Cannot Follow

The frost was still on the inside of my window three days later.

I told myself it was a draft. The Veilless Valley did not do magic — that was the whole point of the place, the entire architecture of my small and survivable life. Magic could not follow a person here. It pooled at the valley's rim like rain against glass and slid away, and whatever had stood in my shop with its reflectionless eyes had paid in honest gold for an honest memory, and the cold on my pane was only weather finding its way through bad caulking.

I repeated this to myself while I sorted the week's intake behind the counter of Joubert's Pawn & Curio, because repetition is how you build a wall out of nothing.

Old Tam was up a ladder in the back, re-shelving his endless inventory of other people's discarded lives: a wedding ring with the marriage still warm in the band, a soldier's compass that pointed home instead of north, a child's wooden horse that remembered being loved so hard its paint wore off. He'd raised me among these things. He'd taught me which memories kept and which curdled, how to wrap a stolen hour in waxed cloth so it didn't leak, how to price grief by weight.

"Sable," he said, without turning around. "You've checked that window four times since dawn."

"I'm checking for customers."

"Customers come through the door." He climbed down, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked at me over his spectacles with the expression he reserved for counterfeit goods. "Whatever walked in here wearing a stranger's face, it left something behind, and you keep looking for it."

"It paid in gold," I said. "Real gold. I bit it."

"Gold isn't what worries me." He crossed to the front window and pressed one knuckle against the glass, where the frost had grown overnight into something almost like writing — long vertical strokes, like a script no one in the valley had used in a hundred years. "This isn't what worries me either. What worries me is that I've lived in this valley sixty years, girl, and I have never once seen cold *behave* like that."

*Girl.* He'd called me that since I was six and would answer to nothing else. It was as close as he ever came to saying *daughter*, and hearing it now made my chest hurt in a way I decided not to examine.

"Then we agree it's weather," I said, "and we open the shop."

He sighed the sigh of a man who has lost arguments to me for twenty years and went to unbolt the door.

The morning came in gray and thin. Mist sat low between the rooftops of Amberfall — the town had been named for my family, though I hadn't known that until recently, and knowing it now felt like a splinter working toward the surface of everything. The market square was filling anyway. Market days didn't wait on omens. Farmers with turnips, tinkers with kettles, a wool merchant from the far side of the valley who carried a memory of his late wife in a locket and let me take a thread of it every year in exchange for winter boots. The valley forgot things on purpose. That was its mercy. People came here to be free of what followed them, and I fed on the overflow, and everyone pretended not to know what I was, and it worked, it had always worked.

It worked until the drums started.

* * *

They came down the pass road at midmorning, and the whole square went quiet the way a field goes quiet when hawks circle.

Crown soldiers first — forty of them, maybe fifty, in the black-and-silver of Aldermarch, moving in step, their boots striking the cobblestones in unison so the sound rolled through the town like slow thunder. Behind them rode riders bearing the royal standard, and behind *them* came a carriage of plain dark wood with no crest at all, drawn by horses that did not toss their heads or blow or shy, that moved with the terrible smoothness of animals under someone else's will.

And above the soldiers, on poles, the proclamation boards. Fresh parchment. Red wax the size of my fist.

"By decree of the Crown of Aldermarch," the herald read, in a voice trained to carry across battlefields, "the realm enters the season of Purification. All persons possessed of the Gift, by blood or bargain or birth, are commanded to present themselves for Examination. Sheltering the Gifted is treason. Concealment is treason. Flight is confession." A pause, professionally measured. "Examination is mercy. The crown does not wish to harm you. The crown wishes only to *know* you."

Tam's hand closed on my shoulder from behind, hard enough to bruise.

"Back room," he said quietly. "Now."

"The proclamation—"

"Was written by people who think *examination* is a gentle word. Back room. I mean it."

I went, but not before I saw her.

She stepped down from the unmarked carriage the way water steps down a ledge — no hesitation, no announcement, just a change in level. A woman in traveling grays with a soldier's posture and a scholar's stillness, perhaps thirty-five, perhaps older; her face had the unfinished quality of a portrait someone had stopped painting halfway. She stood in the middle of our square and turned slowly, once, all the way around, and I watched every person she looked at flinch and look away, and I understood, with the cold certainty of my own gift, what she was doing.

She was reading them. Not faces. Memories. Standing in the open air of a valley where magic could not follow, and reading them like a woman skimming letters.

Her gaze swept the pawnshop's windows and stopped.

Even across the square, even through glass, even with a wall of dead magic between us that should have muffled everything — I felt it. Like a hook set gently behind my sternum. Like being *noticed* by something that had crossed a desert to notice me.

Then she smiled — a small, courteous, awful smile — and inclined her head to me the way one traveler acknowledges another, and mouthed two words I couldn't hear and didn't need to.

*I know.*

* * *

"They can't be here." Tam paced the back room, knocking things off shelves without noticing. "That's not rhetoric, Sable, that's physics. The valley eats magic. Nothing gifted can cross the rim. My grandmother watched a witch try it in '61 and the woman turned around at the boundary stones and *wept*. Whatever that woman is—"

"She read me." My voice came out flat and strange. "Through the window. Through the ward. Tam, I felt it like a hand."

"Then she's not crossing the rim, because she can't, because—" He stopped pacing. Stopped breathing, nearly. And I watched sixty years of cleverness arrive at the same conclusion I had already reached and been refusing.

"She's already inside," I said. "Isn't she. They're already inside. All of them."

The drums were still going. But they were wrong now — I heard it properly for the first time. They weren't coming from the pass road anymore. They were coming from everywhere. From the quarry side. From the river flats. From beneath the cobblestones, if I let myself believe my ears, which I was beginning to.

The valley's silence had always been a kind of armor. We had told ourselves for generations that the thing that made this place safe was the same thing that made it dull: no wonders, no wonders, no wonders. And armor, I understood now, only works until someone brings a weapon shaped exactly for the gap in it.

A knock at the front door. Three knocks, courteous, evenly spaced.

Tam went very still. Then he took my face in both his hands — his palms smelled of metal polish and beeswax, the smell of my entire childhood — and he said, fast and low:

"Quarry tunnels. You know them better than anyone; you used to play in them when you thought I didn't know. The east adit behind the slag heap, the one with the collapsed timber — squeeze past it, follow the dry channel, it comes out in the birches above Miller's Ford. Don't stop for anything. Don't look back. Don't—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, and pushed through. "Don't be clever. For once in your life, don't be clever."

"What are you going to do?"

"Answer the door." He smiled at me, and it was the smile he gave buyers who thought they'd found a bargain — the smile that meant *you have no idea what this is going to cost you, and I'm going to make sure you pay it anyway.* "I'm an old man with a legal business and no Gift whatsoever. There are laws about old men. Let's find out if the crown remembers them."

"Tam—"

"*Go.*"

Another knock. Closer somehow, though the door hadn't opened. Then a voice, pleasant and clear, a voice like a well-lit room:

"Master Joubert. We know she's there. We would prefer not to make her mother's mistake twice."

My blood went to ice water. *Mother's mistake.* The words hit some buried nerve I hadn't known I had, and Tam saw it land, and whatever he read in my face made his decision for him.

He kissed my forehead, shoved the sigil-wrapped bundle from under the floorboards into my arms — the cloth I'd slept in as an infant, the one marked with symbols I'd never dared ask about — and turned to the door.

I went out the back. I swear to every god that never answered in that valley, I went out the back like he told me.

But I stopped in the yard. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

* * *

The front door opened before Tam reached it. It simply swung inward, politely, as if invited.

Wren Vael stood on the threshold of the shop where I had grown up, and up close she was worse than she'd been across the square, because up close I could see that her eyes were wrong in the same way the buyer's had been — no reflections, no shine, surfaces that took light and returned nothing. Two hollow places where mirrors should have been. She had been emptied, I realized. Somewhere, sometime, someone had scooped her out and filled the space with purpose, and purpose was all that looked back at you now.

"Master Joubert," she said again. "Thank you for receiving us."

"We're closed," Tam said.

"So you are." She stepped inside anyway, and her soldiers flowed in behind her like ink poured into water, filling the shop, touching nothing, breaking nothing, which was somehow more frightening than smashing the place to splinters. One of them lifted the frost-scripted windowpane out of its frame with gloved hands and wrapped it in canvas, careful as a relic. "We're here for the girl. The records are quite specific. An infant, abandoned twenty-two years ago at this address, wrapped in marked cloth. The crown has been looking for her a very long time."

"There's no girl here," Tam said. "There's a young woman who works for me, and she stepped out to the market, and if you have a warrant I'll read it while she's gone, and when she comes back—"

"Master Joubert." Wren Vael's voice stayed gentle. That was the horror of it; it never rose, never sharpened. "I was examined too. Did you know that? When I was nine. The crown found my gift and examined me, and it was very thorough, and afterward I was much improved." She tilted her head, considering him with those empty eyes. "I tell people that because it's true, and because it saves time. So let me save us time. Where is she?"

And Old Tam — seventy-one years old, bad hip, hands that shook when he wasn't holding a jeweler's loupe — Old Tam straightened up to his full height, which had never been much, and planted himself between her and the doorway to the back room, and said:

"You'll have to examine me first."

Something flickered across Wren Vael's ruined face. It might have been respect. It might have been hunger.

"That," she said softly, "can be arranged."

I ran.

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the yard, the woodpile, the gap in the fence I'd squeezed through since I was seven. I remember the slag heap's black slope and the birch scrub scratching at my arms and the collapsed timber over the east adit, rotten enough now that panic did what caution never could. I remember the dark swallowing me, and the dry channel underfoot, and my own breath tearing in and out.

What I remember most — what I will remember until the day something stops me — is the sound.

The shop was half a mile behind me by then, underground, through solid rock, and it should have been silent. Instead I heard it the way I heard memories: directly, intimately, as if the sound were being laid into me by hand. Tam's voice, steady, reciting prices and dates and the legal description of his property, keeping them talking, keeping them *in the front of the shop*, away from the back room, away from the yard, away from the fence with the gap in it. And then a pause. And then Wren Vael's voice, still gentle, saying something I couldn't parse, and Tam laughing — actually laughing, short and contemptuous — and saying, clear as a bell across half a mile of stone:

"Not her. You want the girl? The girl's worth more than everything in this shop, and I'll tell you something for free, madam — whatever the crown broke in you, her mother broke *less* getting out than you did staying in—"

The sound cut off. Not faded. Cut, like a snipped thread.

I was underground in the dark with my mother's wrapping cloth crushed against my chest, and the man who had raised me had just spent his life buying me thirty seconds' head start, and I did what he told me. I didn't go back. I didn't look back. I ran through the dry channel with tears I refused to acknowledge streaming down my face, and I let the grief and the guilt fuse together into something with edges, something that moved, something that could only point forward, because backward was a corpse in a pawnshop and forward was the only direction he'd left me.

*Don't be clever,* he'd said. Fine. For once, I wouldn't be.

I would be fast instead.

* * *

The tunnels went on longer than I remembered, or fear stretched them. Twice I passed the chalk marks of my own childhood — a lopsided sun, an arrow, the word HOME spelled wrong — and each one landed like a knife, because home was a burning question mark behind me now and had been, really, since the night a stranger bought a lullaby I shouldn't have known.

The third time I stopped, it wasn't exhaustion that stopped me.

It was the quiet.

The drums had ceased. The soldiers' boots had ceased. Even the drip of water in the deep galleries had ceased, and a valley that had forgotten how to be magical was holding its breath, and in that held breath I heard — faint, patient, coming from everywhere at once — footsteps. Not following the tunnel. Not following anything. Just... approaching, the way an idea approaches.

Light bloomed behind me, cold and white, throwing my shadow huge against the tunnel wall.

"You run beautifully," Wren Vael's voice said, echoing from a throat that shouldn't have been in this tunnel, from everywhere in this tunnel. "He gave you a good start. It was generous of him. Most people die smaller than they lived. He died at full size."

I turned. She stood at the far end of the gallery, alone, unhurried, her empty eyes catching the light of her own making and giving none of it back. Between us lay fifty yards of flooded dark, and I understood with perfect clarity that it might as well have been fifty inches.

"But here's the thing about running," she said, taking one step, then another, her boots making no sound at all on the wet stone. "You can only run *from* something. And I am not something, Sable Amberdale. I'm the end of a very long search. You can't run from the end of a search. You can only arrive at it slightly out of breath."

My hand closed around the wrapped bundle at my chest. Under the sigil-marked cloth, something I had carried my whole life without knowing — a name, an inheritance, a debt — seemed to pulse once, like a second heartbeat waking up.

And somewhere far above us, beyond the valley's rim, beyond the world I'd been born in, something answered it.

The tunnel filled with a light that wasn't hers.
