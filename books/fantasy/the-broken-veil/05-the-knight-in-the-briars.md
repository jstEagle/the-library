## 5. The Knight in the Briars

Corvane had kept his vigil in the briars for nineteen years, and in all that time the valley had never once let anything out.

He had learned to read the place the way a sailor reads weather. The Veilless Valley sat in the world like a hole in a coat — a absence, a held breath. Magic pooled against its borders and slid away, unable to cross, the way water refuses to climb a wall of glass. Birds detoured around it. Clouds thinned over it. Even the wind, Corvane sometimes thought, forgot the valley existed the moment it passed beyond the ridgeline, and only remembered on its way back.

He had watched it from the eastern slope, from a hollow of hawthorn and blackthorn that had grown over him so completely he might have been a stone with a grudge. Nineteen years of watching. Nineteen years of failing at the only duty left to him.

The Amberdales were dead, or scattered, or — he did not let himself finish the thought. The covenant was broken. The Veil was fraying thread by thread across two worlds, and the last of the line that had forged it was, as far as Corvane knew, a child left on a mortal's doorstep twenty-odd years ago, wrapped in a cloth he would have known anywhere, in any light, at any distance.

He had found the cloth. That was the worst of it. He had tracked the sigil to the valley's edge, to the pawnshop in the town called Grayhollow, and then the valley itself had closed like a hand, and his oaths had screamed in his blood every day since — *guard them, guard them* — and he had not been able to take one step down that slope.

The valley did not want magic in it. And Corvane was, at his root, nothing else.

So he waited. He had gotten very good at waiting. It was, he had come to believe, the only penance the world would actually accept.

Then, on the morning the world ended — or began; with Corvane it had always been difficult to tell the difference — the valley let something out.

* * *

He felt it before he saw it. A wrongness in the air above the quarry road, like a note played on a string that shouldn't exist. He rose out of his briars in a rustle of thorns and stood with his head cocked, listening with the long ears of his blood, and what he heard was a heartbeat. Mortal. Fast. Stumbling down the old quarry track at a dead run, alone, and wrapped in —

Wrapped in *that*.

Corvane went still the way only the fae can go still, every part of him suddenly a held instrument.

The sigil. The Amberdale sigil, stitched in thread that had been dipped in both worlds, the interlocked rings with the seam of light through the middle. He had last seen it on the shoulder of a woman running through a burning orchard, and she had looked at him with tears freezing on her face and said, *Look away, Corvane. That is my last order to you, and you will hate me for it, and you will obey.*

He had obeyed. He had hated himself for it in a hundred languages, mortal and fae, and then he had invented a hundred-and-first just for the occasion.

And now the sigil was running down the quarry road in a pair of boots too big for its owner, and behind it, impossibly, inside the valley's borders, the air was tearing.

He saw the figure stumble at the ridge where the valley's dead air met the living world, and he saw what happened next, and even now, years later, he could not decide whether it was beautiful or monstrous. It was both. Most true things were both.

The border crossed them, and they crossed the border, and the world's magic hit them like a wave hitting a man who had never seen the sea.

* * *

Sable Amberdale — though Corvane did not yet know the name, and would not for another hour — made it eleven steps past the ridgeline before their legs stopped being their legs.

He watched them pitch forward onto the quarry road, catch themselves on their palms, and then simply *stop*, kneeling in the dust with their head thrown back and their mouth open, and Corvane, who had crossed that border a thousand times in his long life, knew exactly what was happening to them, and felt his chest tighten with something that was not quite pity and not quite fear.

The valley trained a person to nothing. That was its cruelty and its mercy both. A child raised in the Veilless Valley grew up in silence — the great, total, magical silence of a place where the world's blood did not flow. Their senses never learned the thousand quiet voices every other living soul learned to ignore in the cradle: the hum of leylines under the earth, the whisper of ambient glamour off every blooming thing, the pressure of the Veil itself, vast and near, like the weight of the sea on a diver's shoulders. A valley-raised child heard none of it. Felt none of it. Was, in the most literal sense, deaf to the music of everything.

And then they stepped out of the valley, and the music did not wait for them to learn the words.

Corvane had seen grown soldiers drop like felled trees at their first crossing. He had seen a hedge-witch weep for three days because the colors were too much, because the world outside was *loud with light*, and she had never known. He had once watched a man claw at his own ears, screaming that he could hear the grass growing, and he had — this was the part he did not tell people — held the man's hands until it passed, because it did pass, always, the way drowning passes when the sea finally decides to give you back.

The figure on the road was not screaming. That was the first thing that made Corvane move.

They were kneeling in the dust, shaking, and their eyes were open, and they were *looking* — wide, unblinking, drinking it in even as it drowned them. Corvane had seen terror at the crossing. He had seen agony. He had never seen someone look at the overwhelming of the world with an expression like *that* — like a starving man set down before a feast so enormous it frightened him, reaching for it anyway.

*Oh,* Corvane thought, with a certainty that settled into his bones like cold iron. *Oh, you are hers.*

He stepped out of the briars.

* * *

Later, Corvane would try to reconstruct the approach and fail. He remembered the thorns letting him go reluctantly, as they always did, as if they knew what he was and resented serving a fallen knight. He remembered the road, the dust, the light of the late afternoon lying long and gold across the quarry scars. He remembered stopping ten feet away, because ten feet was as close as he could come without making it worse — a fae's presence has weight, and the weight was the last thing this drowning creature needed.

"Hear me," he said, and pitched his voice low, in the old cadence, the one that carried calm the way a bell carries sound. "You are not dying. I know it feels as if you are. You are not."

The figure's head turned toward him. Slowly, the way a door turns in a house that should be empty.

Corvane got his first true look at the last of the Amberdales and felt the world tilt a degree off true.

They were young — twenty, perhaps, no more. Thin in the way of people who ate by cunning rather than income. Dark hair cut short and practical, dust on their cheeks, a traveler's cloak that had been mended more times than it had been bought. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary, he realized — the kind of ordinary that was a craft, a discipline, a person who had learned to be forgettable the way other people learned to play the fiddle.

But the eyes.

The eyes were not ordinary. The eyes were his lady's eyes, and her mother's before her, a grey like the sky before snow, and set in that forgettable face they burned like lamps in a shuttered house. And behind them — Corvane saw it and his heart, which had not moved in nineteen years, moved — behind them was a hunger he recognized, because it had been his own once, long ago, before exile had taught him to eat ash and call it bread.

The hunger to belong somewhere. Anywhere. The hunger that made people do magnificent and terrible things.

"You're—" The voice came out cracked, half-drowned. The figure swallowed, tried again. "You're not real. You're — the light is doing something. The air is *doing* something, everything is doing something, what is *happening*—"

"The world is happening," Corvane said. "For the first time. You've just left the valley, and the valley is the only place in creation where the world holds its breath. Out here, it sings. And you have never heard it before, so it is singing very close to your ear." He lowered himself — slowly, showing his empty hands, palms out, an old courtesy between strangers — until he crouched at the road's edge, ten feet away, no closer. "It will pass. The first hour is the worst. After that, you learn to hear it under your thoughts, the way you learn to ignore your own heartbeat."

"Pass." The word came out flat with disbelief. "You're telling me this is — this is *normal*?"

"For everyone born outside that valley? Yes." He tilted his head. "What is your name?"

The figure's mouth closed. Corvane watched the whole small war happen behind their eyes: the instinct to lie, honed by years he could only guess at, warring against the fact that they were kneeling in the dust of the world, drowning in wonder, and a stranger with amber-flecked eyes and ears too long for any mortal skull had just crouched down and spoken to them gently, as if they mattered, as if they were *known*.

"Sable," they said finally. Just the name. No surname. He noted the omission and let it lie.

"Sable." He tried the name the way he tried all names, carefully, as if it might have edges. "I am going to tell you three things, and then I am going to ask you one question, and you may refuse to answer it, though I will ask it anyway because I have waited nineteen years to ask it and I am, apparently, still capable of no better than my nature. First: breathe. Slowly. In through the nose. The drowning passes faster if you breathe as though you mean to live. Second: do not stand yet. Your legs have forgotten their trade. Third—" and here his voice did something he had not authorized it to do; it softened, "—you are not broken. What is happening to you is not a sickness. It is the opposite of a sickness. It is the world remembering you exist."

Sable stared at him. Their breathing had slowed a little, he saw; the cadence was doing its work. "You talk like a book," they said, and there was, buried in the cracked and drowning voice, the ghost of something dry. Something with teeth. Corvane found himself, absurdly, almost smiling.

"I was educated by people who believed brevity was a mortal vice. Now. My question." He rose to his feet — slowly, again, giving them the shape of his body in the light, the grey travel cloak, the sword wrapped in dull leather at his back, everything muted and unremarkable except the things he could not mute. "The cloth you were left in. The one with the stitched rings. Do you still have it?"

He watched the drowning stop mattering.

It was remarkable to see. One moment Sable Amberdale was a soul awash in the world's first song, barely holding to the shore of themselves; the next, at the word *cloth*, they went utterly, dangerously still, and their eyes fixed on him with the flat, calculating attention of a creature that had survived by noticing things.

"How do you know about the cloth," Sable said. Not a question. A drawn blade.

"I have been looking for it," Corvane said, "for nineteen years."

"Nobody knows about the cloth." Sable's hand had moved — subtly, he noted, with a professional's economy — toward the knife at their belt. "The man who raised me didn't know what it was. I didn't know what it was. It's a rag. It's a *baby blanket*. So either you've been talking to someone who shouldn't be talking, or you knew my mother, or you're something that shouldn't exist, and in the valley, we have a word for the third one, and the word is *run*."

"I knew your mother," Corvane said.

The knife came out. It was a good knife, well-kept, held with the unlovely competence of someone who had used it on people. Corvane did not move. He had stood in front of charging boars with more composure than he felt now, but he did not move, because the blade was trembling, and it was not trembling with fear.

"You knew—" Sable's voice failed and came back rougher. "Nobody knew my mother. Nobody knows *anything*. I was left on a doorstep. That's the whole story. That's all there is. A doorstep and a rag and a man who smelled of lamp oil and never told me anything until last night, when men in grey came through a valley where nothing can follow anyone, and they *followed*, and he—" The voice cracked clean through. Sable stood there with a knife and a broken sentence and eyes that were suddenly, terribly bright. "He told me to run. He stayed behind. He stayed *behind*, and I ran, because I always run, it's the one thing I'm good at—"

"Sable."

"—and I don't even know what I'm running *from*, or *to*, or why the sky out here is so *loud*—"

"*Sable.*"

They stopped. The whole trembling, knife-held, grief-flooded shape of them stopped, because his voice had changed — he had let the old command come into it, the knight's voice, the voice that had once held a shield wall steady for six hours against the Hollow Hunt, and he hated using it on a child, on *her* child, but some things required it.

"Your mother's name," Corvane said quietly, "was Elira Amberdale. She was the last Warden of the Veil. She sang to orchards to make them bear twice a year. She argued with queens and won. She had a scar on her left palm from a binding-gone-wrong, and she laughed at her own jokes before she finished them, and she was the finest person I ever failed." He let out a breath that had been nineteen years in the leaving. "And she left you that cloth because it was the only thing in two worlds that could keep you hidden from everything that would otherwise have found you. Including me. *Especially* me. She ordered me not to follow you. It is the only order I have ever regretted obeying, and I have obeyed it every single day since, and I am done."

The knife lowered an inch. Another inch. Sable's grey eyes — Elira's eyes, the snow-sky eyes — searched his face with the thoroughness of someone appraising a pawn, and Corvane stood for it, let himself be read, let them find whatever they were looking for. He did not know what a person like that looked for. Truth, perhaps. Or the absence of a lie, which was a different thing.

"You're fae," Sable said at last. Flat. Certain.

"Yes."

"Fully?"

"Fully enough. Why do you ask it that way?"

Something moved behind Sable's face — a hesitation, a door held almost shut. "No reason," they said, and Corvane, who had spent a lifetime among beings for whom a held door was the loudest sound in the room, marked it and said nothing.

The light was going gold to amber on the quarry scars. Somewhere below, in the valley they had fled, a thread of smoke stood up straight from Grayhollow, and Corvane did not look at it, and knew that Sable did, and knew what it was, and knew that neither of them would speak of it yet. Grief keeps its own schedule. It would have them both soon enough.

"We cannot stay on this road," he said instead. "The men in grey who crossed into a valley where nothing can cross — they will not have stopped at the border. Nothing that eats memory ever does. There is a hollow in the hills above the old quarry, warded, dry, defensible. I have kept it for nineteen years against a day I did not believe would come." He extended his hand, palm up, empty, an old gesture, a knight's gesture, the gesture of a man offering his arm to be taken or struck. "Come with me, Sable Amberdale, and I will tell you what you are, and what was broken, and what is coming, and why the sky is loud. And I will tell it all, all of it, holding nothing back — because your mother made me promise to look away, and I am finished with promises that require me to be less than what I swore to be."

Sable looked at the hand for a long time.

Then they looked past him — out, beyond the quarry, beyond the hills, at the open world rolling away in the amber light, the world that was still singing, still too much, still enormous and terrifying and *real* — and Corvane watched them look at it the way a prisoner looks at the sea from a ship's deck. Wonder and terror in the same breath. The hunger and the fear of the hunger.

"The sky's been loud since I crossed the ridge," Sable said quietly. "But it's not the loudness that frightens me." Their eyes came back to his. "It's that some part of me has been waiting for it my whole life. Like I always knew the valley was the wrong size." They sheathed the knife. They did not take his hand, but they stepped up beside him — beside, not behind; he noted that too — and said, "Start talking. And if I find out you knew my mother and let me grow up stealing memories in a ditch—"

"I will deserve whatever you do," Corvane said. "I already do."

They walked. Behind them, far below, the smoke over Grayhollow stood thin and straight in the windless air, and somewhere on the quarry road they had left, the first frost of the evening began to bloom in footprints that had no business being cold — footprints that followed, unhurried, at the exact pace of someone who knew precisely where the drowning girl and the fallen knight were going.

Corvane did not see them. But the wards on his hollow would, before dawn, begin to whisper — and what they whispered would change everything.
