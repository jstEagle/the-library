## 6. Heir of a Broken Oath

Corvane had carried many burdens across the long years of his exile — a broken sword, a broken oath, a name spoken only in whispers in the courts he could no longer enter. But he had never carried anything so fragile as the girl who walked beside him now, stumbling through the briar-hollow with her eyes wide and streaming, her hands pressed over her ears as though the world itself were shouting.

To be fair to her, it was.

He had felt it the moment they crossed the valley's rim: the Veil's ambient tide rolling over Sable Amberdale like a wave over a swimmer who had never learned water. Every leaf-song, every root-whisper, every thread of glamour that wove through the borderlands sang at once into senses that had been trained, from infancy, to silence. In the Veilless Valley there was nothing to hear. Here, everything was speaking.

"Close your eyes," Corvane said, catching her elbow as she swayed against a hawthorn. "Don't fight it. Let it pass through you like weather."

"It's *loud*," Sable gasped. "It's — why is it loud? It's just trees. It's just *grass*—"

"The grass remembers being sung to. Everything here does." He eased her down onto a fallen log carpeted with moss gone silver in the dusk. "You've spent your whole life deaf in a world that assumed you were simply quiet. Now you can hear, and you don't yet know how to listen. They're not the same thing."

She looked up at him then, and he saw what he had seen in the first moment he'd found her crumpled at the valley's edge — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The terrible recognition of someone meeting a relative they never knew they had: this world, this singing, hungry world, wore her face in its features.

"You knew," she said. Her voice had gone flat, the way voices go when feeling has grown too large for them. "Back in Grayhollow. You looked at me and you *knew*. You said my name like you'd been saying it your whole life." Her hands curled in her lap. "So say the rest of it. Whatever it is you've been walking me toward since the quarry. Say it now, or I start walking back."

Corvane had imagined this conversation a hundred times across two days of hard travel. He had rehearsed it by firelight, refined it on the road, pared it down to something gentle. Standing before her now, with her chin lifted and her stolen sea-memory still glinting somewhere behind her eyes, he found all his careful words had rotted away.

So he told her the truth plainly, the way oaths are told.

* * *

"There was a time when your world and mine were one world," he began. "Not joined — one. The Otherworld and the human realm lay against each other like two hands folded, and things passed between them freely. Too freely. Your ancestors burned our forests; my people took your children in payment. There was a war so old that neither side remembers starting it, only that it must not happen again."

Sable said nothing. A moth had come down out of the dark and was circling her shoulder as if it mistook her for a lantern.

"So both realms chose three of their wisest to make a covenant. Not a wall — a covenant. An agreement with teeth. The Veil was woven as its seal: thin enough to let pilgrims and traders and promises cross where they were permitted, strong enough to stop armies and hungers where they were not. And the ones who wove it — the ones whose blood was bound into the very knot of it — were human. One family. Sworn to keep the terms alive on the mortal side, generation after generation, because a covenant is not a thing you make. It's a thing you *keep*, or it dies."

The moth lifted from her shoulder. Corvane watched it go rather than watch her face.

"That family was called Amberdale."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"No," Sable said.

"Yes."

"No. I'm a memory-thief from a hovel above a pawnshop. I eat other people's Tuesdays to remember what bread tastes like. Whatever the Amberdales were—"

"They were oathkeepers," Corvane said, "until they weren't. Until, one generation ago, the last of them broke the covenant. Broke it deliberately, at the heart of the forging-place, with the full weight of their blood behind the breaking. And the Veil — which was never a wall but a living thing, a held breath — *tore*. Not everywhere. In places. Leaks. Wounds. Spots where the worlds bleed into each other and neither can heal, because the hands meant to bind the bandage belong to the one holding the knife."

Sable had gone very still. "Why would they do that? What could possibly—" She stopped. He watched the thought arrive in her like cold water arriving in a well. "My mother."

"I don't know," Corvane said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and he hated himself for the shape of it. "I know what the courts believe: that the last Amberdale betrayed both realms for reasons of their own, and that the heir — if an heir survived — owes both worlds a debt written in the original ink. I know that the crown of Aldermarch believes it too, which is why their criers are reading proclamations about gifted souls while their inquisitors sharpen instruments I will not describe to you. And I know that the covenant can be reforged. That much was built into it, mercy woven into the making. Three stones were set apart at the forging — covenant-stones, each holding a third of the binding-words. Scatter them, hide them, and even a broken oath can be mended, if the blood-heir gathers all three before the Veil fails past mending."

"When does it fail?"

"At the next eclipse. When the sun and moon agree to look away together, the Veil thins everywhere at once. If it is still torn when that happens, the tear becomes a door, and the door becomes a flood, and there will be no more *sides* at all. Just one ruined country where two worlds used to be."

Sable stood up. She stood the way people stand when sitting has become unbearable, and she turned to face him, and Corvane saw that her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear.

"No," she said again, and this time it was a sentence, not a word. "No. I didn't ask for any of this. I didn't ask to be left in a magic-dead valley like something buried. I didn't ask to spend twenty years eating strangers' memories so I wouldn't forget my own name. I didn't ask for your covenant or your stones or your eclipse. Some family I never met broke a promise, and now the sky falls unless *I* fix it? That's not an inheritance. That's a bill. And I don't pay bills for dead people."

"Sable—"

"They should have hidden the stones better," she said. "They should have hidden *me* better. Or better than either — they should have just let it all fall! Maybe two worlds that build their peace on the backs of one abandoned child deserve to fall!"

The words rang off the briars and came back to her, and he watched her hear them, and flinch, and refuse to take them back. Good, some cold honest part of him thought. Be angry. Anger is a kind of strength, and you will need every kind you can find.

"You're right," Corvane said quietly.

That stopped her. He saw it stop her — the fury needing a target and finding none.

"You were left in that valley without your consent. You were given a hunger instead of a history. No one asked you, and everyone decided for you, and that is an evil whether it was done to save you or to use you or both. I won't argue you out of it. I have no right to." He rose slowly, joints aching with the honesty of his years. "But I'll tell you what I told myself, the day I understood what had been broken and what it would cost. The debt isn't fair. Debts never are. The question is only ever: who is standing closest when the bill comes due? And Sable Amberdale — you are standing closest. You have always been standing closest. You were *put* there."

"By whom?" Her voice cracked on it. "By *whom*, Corvane? Who put me there?"

And because he had sworn, long ago, an oath within the broken oath — *guard them, guide them, but let them come to truth at their own pace* — he said, "I don't know," a second time, and let the lie sit between them like a stone neither would pick up.

Sable turned away. For a long moment she looked out at the darkening hills, at the far shimmer where the world's edges grew uncertain, and Corvane let her look. Grief needs somewhere to put its feet.

Then she went rigid.

"What," Corvane said, "is that."

"Not sound." Her head tilted. "It's — under the sound. Like a note played wrong. Like a hole in the song, moving." She pointed, southeast, toward the ridge they had crossed at dusk. "There. Something's coming up the ridge, and where it walks, the grass stops remembering."

Corvane followed her finger and felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

He could not hear what she heard — his own senses were fae-fine but ordinary in their kind. But he could see, now, on the ridgeline black against the last violet of the sky: figures. Six of them. Seven. Moving in the unhurried formation of hunters who have never once needed to hurry, and around them, impossibly, frost — frost blooming in midsummer along the ridge-top, silvering the bracken, breathing white into the warm dark.

Frost indoors. Frost in summer. He had heard the stories from the border towns and dismissed them as panic dressed in detail. *Inquisitors who walk cold. Inquisitors who leave no reflection. Inquisitors who eat what you remember until there is nothing left to interrogate.*

Wren Vael's hounds. Which meant Wren Vael was not far behind.

"They shouldn't be able to follow us here," Corvane breathed. "The valley, yes — the valley is dead ground, they can walk it. But past the rim, the ambient magic should blind them. Gifted humans can't track through open magic. It's like following a scent through a burning perfumery."

"Maybe they're not tracking us," Sable said. Her voice had gone small and terribly steady. "Maybe they're tracking *you*. Or maybe—" and he heard the thought finish itself inside her before she said it aloud, "—maybe whatever I am carries farther than anyone guessed."

The lead figure paused at the crest of the ridge. Even at this distance, even in the failing light, Corvane saw it turn its head — unhurried, precise — and look directly at them. Not searching. *Finding.*

"We run," Corvane said, all in one breath, decision arriving whole the way old soldiers' decisions do. "Not south — they'll expect south. East, down into the hollowlands, to the river road. There's a town called Marrowgate two days on where glamours run thick enough to drown in, and if your gift works there the way I think it might—"

"My gift." Sable laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "You mean the stealing. The thing that makes me a thief even among thieves."

"I mean," Corvane said, already moving, pulling her by the wrist into the dark beneath the hawthorns, "the thing that might be the only reason either of us sees tomorrow. Ask me how I know later. Run now."

Behind them, on the ridge, the frost spread like a hand opening.

And Sable ran — furious, unchosen, heir of a broken oath she had never signed — down into the hollowlands where the grass remembered, carrying her resentment like a lit torch into the dark ahead of them all.
