## 7. The Road of Borrowed Faces

### Sable

The valley had never let Sable feel anything but their own small weather. Crossing its boundary for the first time was like being born into a storm.

They made it perhaps half a mile past the old quarry mouth before the world reached in and took hold of them. Magic hung in the air out here — not the dead silence of home but a living pressure, a hum that pressed against the skin like standing too close to a waterfall. Every blade of grass whispered with it. Every stone remembered being part of a mountain. Sable's knees buckled on the road, and Corvane caught them under the arm with a grip like iron wrapped in velvet.

"Breathe through it," the knight said. "You've been deaf all your life. The world is loud."

*That* was an understatement. Sounds had colors here. Colors had weight. A crow crossing the sky dragged a ribbon of pale silver behind it that only Sable seemed able to see, and when they stumbled again, Corvane muttered something in a language that tasted like cold honey and threw his cloak over Sable's shoulders.

"Less comes through wool," he said. "For now."

They walked through the afternoon along a road that wound down from the highlands toward the border town of Gildenhollow, which Corvane described as "a boil on the skin between realms." By dusk its lights were visible below: not lantern-light, exactly, but a glow that shifted hues when you weren't looking directly at it, gold becoming green becoming the pink of an old scar.

"Glamours run rampant there," Corvane warned as they descended. "Half the town is fae wearing human shapes, and a quarter of the humans are wearing fae shapes they bought or stole. Nobody is what they appear. It's the best place in Aldermarch to hide and the worst place to be recognized."

"Which are we doing?"

"Both. Simultaneously. Welcome to my exile."

Gildenhollow smelled of wet copper and frying onions and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun. The streets were cobbled with stones that had clearly been *arranged* rather than laid, spiraling in patterns that pulled the eye toward doorways. Market stalls sold bottled laughter, secondhand shadows, luck by the spoonful. A woman with too many joints in her fingers weighed out dreams on brass scales.

Sable kept their head down and their hood up and felt, beneath the overwhelming noise of the place, a familiar hunger stir.

It had been three days since they'd eaten properly — not bread, but memory. In the valley, travelers had been scarce since the Purge proclamation, and Sable had been rationing what little they'd lifted before fleeing: a fisherman's morning calm, a child's delight at a fair. Thin fare. And now, surrounded by thousands of minds rich with lives Sable had never lived, the hunger woke like a dog hearing its name.

"Don't," Corvane said quietly, without looking at them.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your stomach did. Or whatever it is you do." He steered them down an alley where the glow of the main street dimmed. "Feeding here is different than feeding in your valley. In the Veilless lands, a stolen memory leaves a hole and nothing fills it. Here—" He hesitated, and Sable watched the lamplight move strangely across his face, catching on features that were almost, but not quite, arranged the way a human face should be. "Here, memories grow back. Like grass. But the cutting is still felt. And some of the things growing in fae minds will notice being cut."

"Fae minds," Sable repeated. "You think I could—"

"I think you shouldn't try."

But the town had other plans. They needed food, lodging, and above all faces — because Corvane's features, however carefully he composed them, drew stares from certain corners. Twice already Sable had seen figures melt away from doorways at the sight of him, murmuring to each other in voices like wind through reeds. *The Thorn Court has eyes everywhere,* he'd said grimly. *And I am a debt they remember.*

So when they found themselves in the shadowed end of a market square, watching a fat merchant count coins with rings on every finger, Sable did what they had always done. They reached out — gently, the way they'd learned, a thread of attention hooked behind the eyes — and took.

Not coin. Not this time. They took the merchant's face.

That was how it had always worked, though Sable had never had words for it until now: every memory carried its own sensory weight, and a face seen often enough by its owner was worn into the memory like a path into a hillside. Sable lifted the merchant's self-image — forty years of catching his own reflection in shop windows, in polished kettles, in his wife's admiring eyes — and pulled it over their own skull like a hood.

The change rippled through them. Their jaw broadened. Their nose thickened. When they caught their reflection in a stall's tin basin, a stranger looked back: middle-aged, jowled, unremarkable.

Corvane stared. "That's not glamour," he said slowly. "There's no magic in it at all. You're just... *wearing him.*"

"It's how I've always hidden," Sable said, surprised. "In the valley. When the wrong kind of traveler came through, I wore whoever they'd recently met. Nobody looks twice at a face they know."

"No." Corvane had gone very still, in the way that fae went still — utterly, unnaturally motionless, like a heron before the strike. "Sable. Listen to me. What you just described is not a trick any human gift can do. Glamour bends light and perception. What you did was take a man's *knowledge of himself* and make it flesh. That isn't theft. That's something else entirely."

Before Sable could ask what something else, the trouble started.

It came up the alley on quick feet: three figures in the grey livery of the crown, moving against the flow of the market, checking faces. Inquisitors' outriders. Vael's people — the sigil on their shoulders matched the frost-patterned brand Sable had glimpsed on the woman who'd killed Old Tammas Hetch, back in the valley, back when the world still had edges.

Corvane moved first, pulling Sable into a doorway, but the doorway was already occupied. A fae woman stood there in the guise of a flower-seller, her basket full of blooms that were quietly screaming. Her eyes found Corvane and widened with recognition, and her mouth began to shape a word —

Sable didn't think. They reached.

Into *her.*

The world went strange. Where human minds were warm rooms full of cluttered furniture, hers was a cathedral of living green, thoughts growing like branches, memories hanging from them like fruit that sang. Sable had braced for resistance, for the tearing Corvane had promised. Instead the fae woman's mind simply... opened, startled, like a bird watching an impossible thing happen.

And Sable took a face from her too — one of her own stored guises, a memory of herself as a stooped old ferryman with a white beard and river-water eyes — and pulled it over Corvane's head.

The knight staggered. His features flowed like wax, settled, firmed. Where the exiled knight had stood, there now leaned a weathered old boatman, blinking, one hand pressed to the wall.

"You—" The flower-seller's voice came out strangled. She clutched her basket to her chest, and the screaming flowers went silent. "You touched me. You *took* from me. No mortal can—" Her eyes, suddenly ancient and suddenly afraid, fixed on Sable. "What *are* you?"

"Sable!" Corvane — the ferryman's mouth shaping the knight's voice — grabbed their wrist. "Run."

They ran.

* * *

### Corvane

Corvane had lived four hundred years, and in that time he had catalogued the world's wonders with the weary competence of a clerk. He had seen mountains walk. He had drunk wine with the moon. He had loved a mortal woman and obeyed her final order and hated himself for both, in that order, ever since.

He had thought himself beyond astonishment.

Then a half-starved thief from a magic-dead valley had reached into the mind of a fae of the Thorn Court's blood and *taken a disguise from it*, the way a child takes an apple from a bowl, and Corvane's whole careful architecture of understanding collapsed into rubble.

Fae minds did not open. Fae minds were fortresses older than human kingdoms, warded by nature itself. Even other fae could not casually plunder one another's stored shapes; that was why glamours existed, why courts dueled with riddle and contract rather than theft. For a mortal to reach in and *borrow*—

It wasn't possible. Which meant, Corvane thought as he hurried his charge through Gildenhollow's twisting back lanes in the body of a dead ferryman whose memories still sat oddly on his shoulders like a borrowed coat, that everything he believed about what Sable Amberdale was had been too small.

They ducked into a tannery yard that stank enough to discourage pursuit, and Corvane finally let himself stop and look at the young man — no, the young *person*, Sable had corrected him once, with the flat pragmatism of someone who'd never been given the luxury of identity — who stood shaking in the shadow of the vats.

"The flower-seller will report it," Sable said. "Won't she."

"She already has, somewhere, in whatever way her kind report things." Corvane flexed his borrowed hands. The ferryman's knuckles ached with old rope-burns that weren't his. Unsettling. "By tomorrow night, the Thorn Court will know a mortal walked through their agent's mind. And the crown—" He grimaced. "Vael's outriders saw nothing clearly, but they'll have felt *something*. You left ripples, Sable. Ripples travel."

"Then we leave tonight."

"We leave tonight," Corvane agreed, "but not before you eat. Properly. Because if you attempt another crossing of another mind on an empty stomach, you'll burn out, and I cannot carry you and fight at the same time." He paused. "Also, I want to watch what happens when you feed here. Forgive me. The scholar in me is showing."

"There's a scholar in you?"

"There are many things in me. Most of them disappointed." He gestured toward the lane. "Come. There's a tavern near the old gate — the Drowned Bell. Neutral ground, or as close as this town gets to it. We'll find a traveler full of good strong memories, you'll take one gently, and then we'll talk about what you actually are. Because I begin to suspect," and here his voice dropped, roughened, "that even I have been telling you less than half the truth. Not from deceit. From ignorance."

### Sable

The Drowned Bell was built from the ribs of two ships and the bones of a church, and it hummed.

That was the only word Sable had for it. The whole building vibrated faintly with accumulated memory — decades of sailors' songs and merchants' bargains and griefs poured out over cheap ale — and when Sable crossed the threshold, the hum rose to meet them like a tide recognizing the moon.

Every table held stories. Sable could *see* them now, faintly, the way they'd never been able to see anything in the valley: a soldier's nightmares pooling under his stool like spilled ink; a bride's joy rising off her in golden threads; in the corner, an old woman knitting, and around her the air was thick with a century of winters, each one soft and white and hers.

"Choose carefully," Corvane murmured, guiding them to a bench. "Something bland. A meal, a road, a boring sermon. Nothing you'll want to keep, nothing they'll miss."

But Sable's eyes had already found the far table, where a figure sat alone beneath a hood, and everything Corvane had said about choosing carefully fell out of their head.

Because the figure had no reflections.

Sable had learned to read reflections the way other people read handwriting — in pewter plates, in window glass, in the dark of a pupil. Everyone's showed something. This person's showed nothing. The candle flame burned beside their hood and cast light everywhere except into the space where a face should have mirrored itself, and around their boots, on the tavern's filthy floor, the wood was rimed with a thin, delicate frost.

Frost. Indoors. In a town where magic ran rampant and wild.

*Sable,* said a voice inside their head, gentle as snowfall, and Sable's whole body went rigid, because the voice was the lullaby — the one the buyer in the valley had paid gold to hear, the melody Sable didn't recognize but somehow knew, the one that had followed them out of the valley like a debt.

*You wear borrowed faces so well,* the voice said. *But you cannot borrow what was always yours. Come north, little heir. Before the eclipse. Before they make you into what she died to prevent.*

Across the room, the hooded figure raised a cup Sable realized was empty, drank from it anyway, and set it down. Where it touched the table, frost bloomed outward in the shape of a sigil Sable had seen once before — stitched into the cloth they'd been swaddled in, the cloth Corvane kept folded in his pack like a relic.

Then the figure stood, left a coin that gleamed wrong, and walked out through the crowd, and every single person in the Drowned Bell forgot they had ever been there — Sable felt the forgetting pass over the room like a hand smoothing bedclothes — everyone except Sable, and, slowly turning beside them, Corvane, whose face had drained of all its borrowed color.

"That sigil," the knight said hoarsely. "Where did you see it?"

"On my swaddling clothes," Sable said. "And on the frost. Just now. It spoke to me, Corvane. Inside my head. It knew what I can do — it called me *heir.*"

Corvane was already on his feet, pressing coins into the barkeep's hand, gathering their packs. Outside, the night had turned sharply cold, and in the street beyond the tavern door, the frost was spreading along the cobbles in a line — a trail, leading north, deliberate as an invitation.

Or a leash.

"We're leaving Gildenhollow tonight," Corvane said, "and we're not alone on this road anymore. Someone knows exactly what you are — and they wanted us to know they know."

Behind them, unnoticed, a grey-cloaked figure slipped out of the tavern's shadows and followed the frost-line north at a careful distance, and if her hollow eyes held anything at all, it was recognition.
