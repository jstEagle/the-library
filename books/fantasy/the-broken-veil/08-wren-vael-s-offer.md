## 8. Wren Vael's Offer

The border town of Marrowgate smelled of wet wool and secrets, and Wren Vael hated it.

She stood in the shadow of the tannery's overhang, watching the market churn below her — humans and glamoured fae brushing shoulders like oil and water, neither quite mixing, both pretending. Rain came down in a fine grey sift that never quite became honest weather. Somewhere in this crush of borrowed faces, the Amberdale heir was moving, guided by an exiled knight whose name still made old men in the Court of Thorns spit into their wine.

*Corvane.* She'd read his file twice. Once for duty, once for pleasure. A guardian who failed his charge — there was a shape she recognized.

"Captain." Corporal Ashe materialized at her elbow, rain beading on his oiled cloak. He was young. They were all young now; the older ones had been hollowed too far to field. "We've tracked them to the ferry quarter. The thief bought passage north with a coin purse that wasn't theirs an hour ago."

"And?"

"And nothing else. It's strange, Captain. The ferrymen remember them clearly — too clearly. Faces like they've been *polished* into memory."

Vael smiled without warmth. "That's because they have. Our thief has been working. Note it in the log: the Amberdale gift extends beyond human minds."

Ashe hesitated. "The men are asking, Captain. The stories from the valley—"

"The stories are accurate," she said. "I walked through four wards that should not exist inside a magic-dead hollow, and I watched a pawnbroker die smiling because he thought he'd bought a child ten more minutes. Yes, Corporal. The stories are accurate." She turned from the market. "Set the net at the chapel road. And Ashe — no one touches the thief. No one *feeds* on them. If a single one of my people so much as brushes that mind without my leave, I will show them what examination means. Am I understood?"

He went pale in the way soldiers did when they realized their captain was the most frightening thing in the army. "Understood."

She let him go and stayed beneath the overhang a moment longer, letting the rain speak its small grey language against the leather of her gloves.

Below, a child ran past chasing a hoop, laughing, and Vael's chest did the thing it always did around children — a tightening, a closing, like a door drawn shut against weather. She had learned not to look at them directly. Looking invited comparison, and comparison invited the memory, and the memory was the one thing in all the realms she could not steal from herself.

*The white room. The chair. The kind voice saying, this will only hurt until it doesn't.*

She had been seven years old when the crown's examiners came to her village with their brass instruments and their gentle questions. She had been able to do such a small thing, then — just a whispering, just knowing what her mother dreamed at night, just smoothing a neighbor's grief by taking a sliver of it into herself. A hedge-gift. A nothing. Enough.

They had called it examination the way butchers called it processing. Nine months in the white room. They had taken everything that made her *her* — every fear, every love, every summer afternoon — and left the machinery running with nothing left to run on. What remained was efficient. Obedient. Perfectly empty, and therefore perfectly suited to eating other people's emptiness out of them.

Wren Vael, Inquisitor First Class, Hollow Blade of Aldermarch. The crown's finest instrument.

Instruments did not get offers. Instruments got orders. But tonight, alone in the rain, she permitted herself the heresy of wanting something — and what she wanted was standing in the ferry quarter wearing a dead woman's sigil cloth.

* * *

She found Sable Amberdale at dusk, alone.

It was almost insultingly easy. The thief and the knight had taken rooms above a chandler's shop near the water, and Corvane — diligent, haunted, predictable Corvane — had gone out at full dark to walk the perimeter, as guardians did, as men carrying thirty years of guilt did. That left the heir sitting alone in a rented room with a candle, mending a strap on a travel pack, unaware that death had climbed the stairs behind them.

Vael didn't knock. She simply opened the door and closed it behind her, and set a small glass vial on the table between them — a null-ward, dead-magic glass, the kind that turned a room into a little pocket of Veilless silence. Inside it, no gift could reach beyond the walls. No scream would carry either.

Sable came up off the chair fast, hand going to the knife at their belt, and Vael watched the recognition move across their face like frost across a window. Good. The valley survivors knew her. The pawnbroker's blood was still fresh enough in the town's gossip.

"Inquisitor," Sable said. Their voice was steadier than she expected. There was something in it — a flatness under the fear, a practiced control. A thief's control. She catalogued it. "You're alone."

"I am."

"That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's neither. It's arithmetic." She kept her hands visible, loose at her sides. Empty hands. She had learned long ago that empty hands frightened people less than any lie. "Corvane is three streets away circling your lodging like a moth that's forgotten what it's mourning. My soldiers are at the chapel road, which is where you'll be tomorrow if you live past tonight. We have perhaps twenty minutes before either of those facts becomes a problem. Sit down, Amberdale. I'm not here to take you."

"You took Old Tam." The knife hadn't moved. "You took the man who raised me."

"Yes." She said it plainly, because flinching was a luxury instruments didn't have, and because she had decided — in the grey hour before dawn, in the rain, in the privacy of her own skull — that this conversation would be conducted honestly or not at all. "He died buying you time. He knew what he was doing. He chose it. I don't offer comfort about it because I don't have any. But I didn't come here to discuss him either."

"Then why *are* you here?" Sable's eyes flicked to the vial, to the window, to the door — reading exits, weighing odds. A survivor's arithmetic. Vael almost admired it.

"I'm here to make you an offer. And before you refuse it — and you will refuse it, everyone refuses it the first time — I'm going to show you something." She reached up, slowly, and drew back the hood.

Sable had seen her face before, at the quarry, at a distance, wreathed in her hunters' torchlight. But they hadn't seen it in good light, up close, with time to look. Vael stood still and let them look.

Her face was unremarkable. That was the horror of it, and she knew it, and she let it do its work. Thirty years old and smooth as river stone, because nothing had ever carved itself there. No laugh lines. No grief lines. No sun-wrinkles from any summer she could recall, because she couldn't recall any. Her eyes were dark and clear and perfectly level, and behind them was —

Nothing. A held breath. A room after the furniture has been removed.

"What am I looking at?" Sable asked quietly.

"A mirror," Vael said. "Twenty years early. Sit down."

And Sable — to their credit, to their ruin — sat.

* * *

"I was born in a village called Fernhollow," Vael said, "sixty miles east of where you grew up. You won't find it on maps anymore. Not because it fell. Because the cartographers' guild was ordered to remove it, along with eleven other villages, in the year the Purge statutes were first drafted. Trial runs, we were called. Pilot programs. I was seven."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"I could do one thing," she went on, her voice level, reciting. "I could take a bad dream out of someone's head and hold it for them. My mother used to bring me to the widow Doss's house on bad nights, and I'd cup the widow's face in my hands and drink her nightmares down, and she'd sleep like a girl again. My mother called it my kindness. She thought it was a blessing." A pause, precise as a scalpel cut. "So did the examiners, in their way. They examined me for nine months. Do you know what examination means, Amberdale? It means they take the gift apart to see how it works. And since the gift lives in the person, they take the person apart too. Piece by piece. Memory by memory. Until all that's left is the mechanism."

Sable's knuckles had gone white around the strap they were mending. "You're lying."

"Why would I lie? What could I possibly gain?"

"To turn me against the crown. To make me pliable. You're an inquisitor — manipulation is your trade."

"It is," Vael agreed. "And if I wanted you pliable, I'd have brought soldiers and taken you while you slept, and Corvane would be picking your memories out of my hunters' teeth by morning. Instead I'm sitting in a dead-magic ward telling you the one story the crown has spent twenty years and considerable gold making sure no one tells." She leaned forward slightly. "Ask me anything. Test me. Ask me what the white room smells like."

Sable's jaw tightened. They didn't ask. They looked at her instead — really looked, the way thieves looked, hunting the seams in a person — and whatever they saw in her face made them look away first.

"My mother tried to get me out," Vael said, softer now. "Third month. She bribed a guard with the family silver. They let her get as far as the courtyard." Her hands stayed perfectly still in her lap. "I watched. I want you to understand that part. I watched, and I felt nothing, because feeling had been removed in the second month, ahead of schedule. They noted it in my file as *excellent progress*. I remember the file. I don't remember her face. I remember that there was a face, and that it was doing something people do when they're grieving, and that I stood at the window and categorized it as *distress response, non-compliant subject, maternal relation*. Then they took that memory too, eventually. Standardization. Can't have instruments remembering they had mothers."

The silence stretched. Outside, the rain thickened against the shutter.

"What do you want from me?" Sable said at last. Their voice had changed — thinner, careful, a man walking on ice and listening for cracks.

"Three things happened last month," Vael said. "The Veil tore open over the Veilless Valley — a place that should be impossible to tear, which means the covenant isn't fraying, it's failing. The crown declared a Purge, which is not panic, it's preparation — they are collecting gifted souls the way a siege collects powder. And the Amberdale heir surfaced, carrying a sigil cloth, traveling with a disgraced Thorn knight, heading for things the crown wants very badly." She ticked them off on gloved fingers. "I am not stupid, Amberdale. I am many terrible things, but I read files the way priests read scripture, and I have read the sealed ones. The Amberdales forged the covenant. The Amberdales broke it. And now the last Amberdale is walking toward three stones that can remake it — while the crown follows behind, harvesting every gifted soul it can find, and calling it protection."

"You think the crown wants the stones."

"I think the crown wants a weapon, and the stones are the largest weapon in either realm, and I think you already suspect it, because you're alive, which means you're not stupid either." She rose, finally, and moved to the window, keeping her profile to them — an old habit, giving subjects a face in profile, less to grip onto. "Here is my offer. Come with me. Help me find the stones — all three, before the eclipse. And when we have them, I will give you my word, sworn on the only thing I have left, that the Veilless Valley is struck from the Purge rolls forever. Every soul in it. Your neighbors. The memory of your pawnbroker's shop. All of it, untouched, in perpetuity."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, the stones go to me, and I decide what happens to the Veil."

Sable laughed — a short, disbelieving sound. "You. A hollowed-out crown killer. You'll decide the fate of both realms."

"Yes," Vael said. "Because the alternative is the man who signed my examination order deciding it. Chancellor Hesse. The architect of the Purge. The man who keeps a ledger of every gifted soul in Aldermarch, and who has recently begun asking his engineers very interesting questions about whether the Veil's energy can be *channeled* rather than merely sealed." She turned back. "You think I serve the crown? I am the crown's *property*, Amberdale. There's a difference, and you of all people should taste it. I have been a tool for twenty-three years. Tools don't get to choose what they're used for. But tools can choose which hand picks them up — and I am telling you, with the last free scrap of judgment they left me, that if Hesse reaches those stones before anyone else does, there will be no Veil at all. There will be a doorway, and it will point wherever he points it, and everything on the other side will come through it *on command*."

Sable stood. Slowly. The knife was still in their belt, but their hand had drifted away from it, which Vael noted, and filed, and did not trust.

"You're asking me to betray Corvane."

"I'm asking you to survive him. He's a good man and a failed guardian, and those two facts are the same fact. He will get you killed protecting an oath that died before you were born." She let that sit. "But no. That's not the heart of it. Here is the heart of it, and then I'll go, because Corvane's circuit is nearly done and I keep my appointments with honesty precisely."

She crossed the small room until she stood close enough that Sable could see what lived in her eyes when she let it live there — which was nothing, which was the whole argument.

"You felt it, in the valley. When I came through the wards. You felt what I am." Sable flinched; she pressed on, gently, terribly. "Yes. I know you felt it. A hollow thing, walking. And I need you to understand that I am not threatening you with my future. I am *showing* you. This is what examination makes. This is what the crown does with gifts — it doesn't destroy them, it *refines* them, strips everything soft away until the gift is all that's left, a blade with no hand holding it but theirs. You can steal memories, Amberdale. Do you truly believe, if they catch you, they will kill you? You are worth infinitely more to them awake. You are a thief who can walk through fae minds. They will put you in the white room and take everything except the theft, and in ten years you will be standing in some border town making this exact offer to some terrified child, and you will not even remember that you ever wanted anything else."

"Stop." Sable's voice cracked on it.

"Help me find the stones," Vael said. "Give me the valley's safety and the stones' custody, and I swear to you — on the mother I cannot remember, on the name they scraped out of me, on whatever ghost of Wren-that-was still rattles around in this machine — that no child in Aldermarch gets examined again. Not while I hold what the stones can do. That is my offer. That is the only version of mercy I am capable of carrying."

For a long moment, Sable said nothing. The candle burned low. And Vael watched the war move behind the thief's eyes — fear and pity and fury and, worst of all, *recognition*, the awful mirror-recognition she had come here to plant, taking root.

"You poor bastard," Sable whispered. "You actually think you're saving me."

"I think," Vael said, "that no one saved me. And I have wondered, every day for twenty-three years, what it would have cost someone to try." She stepped back, retrieved her hood, and pulled it up, becoming once more the grey shape the rumors described. "You don't have to answer tonight. You have until the chapel road. After that, my orders resume, and I follow them, because I am what they made me. But consider, Amberdale, before you choose: Corvane offers you a dead oath and a glorious grave. I offer you a living valley and a fighting chance. Only one of us is asking you to survive."

She was at the door when Sable spoke again, quiet enough that the rain nearly ate it.

"The lullaby. The stranger who bought the memory — the one with no reflections in their eyes. Was that yours?"

Vael went very still.

She had wondered, these past weeks, about that report — the frost indoors, the buyer with mirrorless eyes, the memory of a lullaby purchased out of a child's head in the heart of a magic-dead valley. She had told herself it was a fae agent. A Thorn spy. Anyone.

But she had also, in the smallest locked room of herself, wondered something else. Because there was one memory the examiners had never managed to take — one song lodged too deep, in whatever cellar of her they had never found — and sometimes, in the space between orders, it played itself, and she did not know where it came from, and it made her hands shake.

"No," she said. And then, because honesty had become a sickness in her tonight: "I don't know. Find out for both of us."

She opened the door, slipped into the dark, and was gone — leaving Sable Amberdale alone with a guttering candle, an impossible choice, and the fading certainty that they had just seen their own future standing in the doorway, offering them its hand.

Behind them, unnoticed until now, frost was blooming across the inside of the window glass — in the shape, delicate and unmistakable, of a music note.
