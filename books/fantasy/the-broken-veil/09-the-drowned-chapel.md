## 9. The Drowned Chapel

The fen did not want us.

That was the first thing I understood about it, wading knee-deep through water the color of old tea, each step sucking at my boots like a hand around the ankle. The marsh had opinions. It had patience. It had been drowning things for longer than either realm had kept records, and it regarded two travelers and their borrowed faces with the flat indifference of something that knew it would win eventually.

"Stay to the standing stones," Corvane said behind me. His voice was low, not from stealth — we hadn't seen another living soul in two days — but from reverence, or maybe dread. "They mark where the ground remembers being solid. Step off them and you'll meet whatever the fen has swallowed that hasn't finished dying."

"Cheerful," I said.

"I was going to say *instructive*."

I glanced back at him. Even glamoured — his antlers folded down to nothing, his too-bright eyes dimmed to a traveler's gray, his skin shifted from moonlit silver to a weathered brown — he moved wrong for a human. Too smooth. Like water deciding to be a man. Two weeks on the road together and I'd stopped flinching at it, mostly.

The fog came up around our waists before noon, thick enough to chew. Somewhere ahead, a bell rang.

Not a living bell. No rope, no ringer, no churchwarden with cold hands. It was the memory of a bell, a sound the fen had soaked up centuries ago and now leaked back into the air whenever the wind turned right. It tolled once, faded, tolled again, each peal slightly wrong, slightly drowned, as if heard through fathoms of black water.

"The chapel," Corvane said quietly.

"You can hear it?"

"I can hear everything here." He said it like an apology. "The Veil is thin in this place. That's why your mother's people hid a stone in it. Nothing guards a secret better than a wound."

We walked until the light went strange — not dusk, though the sun should have been high, but a greenish underwater gloom, as if we'd sunk without noticing and were only now admitting it. And then the stones rose out of the fog.

It had been a chapel once. You could see the bones of it: a gable end still standing, window arches like open mouths, a tower leaning at the angle of a drunk telling a secret. The rest had gone under generations ago, the fen drinking it down stone by stone, so that what remained looked less like a building than like a shipwreck that had decided to be architecture instead. Ivy and bog-myrtle strangled the walls. Water lapped at windows meant for sky.

On the tower's face, half-eaten by moss, a carving: two hands clasped, one human, one long-fingered and crowned in thorns. Beneath it, worn nearly smooth, words in Old Aldermarchi that I couldn't read — but my eyes snagged on them anyway, caught like cloth on a nail.

*I know that sigil,* I almost said. *I've seen it wrapped around something.* But I didn't know where, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"The ward is inside," Corvane said. He'd stopped at the edge of the flooded nave and made no move to go further. "Sable. Before we go in, there are things you should—"

"Later." I said it too fast, and he heard it. He always heard it. "Whatever speech you've got prepared about dangers and choices and how I can still turn back — later. Vael is three days behind us and gaining. The eclipse is—" I counted without wanting to. "Thirty-one days. We don't have time for the speech."

"It isn't a speech." Something crossed his face, quick as a fish under ice. "It's an apology. But you're right. Later."

He gestured me forward and stayed at the threshold, and I understood why a moment later when I waded through the broken doorway and felt the chapel notice me.

* * *

The water inside was waist-deep and warm as blood, which was worse than cold would have been. Fallen beams lay beneath the surface; I felt them brush my legs like the ribs of something enormous. Light came down through the collapsed roof in pale columns, and motes drifted in it — not dust. They moved with intention, slow spirals, like snow falling upward somewhere far away.

At the center of the nave, where an altar should have been, something glowed.

It sat on a submerged plinth, maybe four feet down, and its light didn't scatter in the water the way light should. It gathered. A stone the size of a heart, faceted like cut glass, burning a soft gold that made the whole drowned nave feel like the inside of a lamp. Even across the distance, even through the water, I could feel it against my skin — a pressure, a hum, a note held so long it had become silence.

The first covenant-stone. The thing we'd crossed half of Aldermarch to find. The thing the crown wanted, the fae courts wanted, the thing my mother had apparently bled a covenant to hide.

And between me and it, the water began to move.

It didn't ripple. It *rose*, silently, in a column at the center of the nave, and the column became a figure — tall, faceless, robed in dripping gray, its outline never quite settling. Where its face should have been there was only a smooth blankness, like a portrait someone had scrubbed away.

Corvane's voice reached me from the doorway, strained, as if through deep water: *"The ward! Sable, listen — it will show you your worst memory. It will make it real. You cannot fight it, you cannot run from it — you can only walk through it. Do you hear me? Walk THROUGH—"*

The figure raised one hand.

The world went out like a snuffed candle.

* * *

Rain.

I was nine years old again, and it was raining, and I was standing in the doorway of the pawnshop with a chipped mug of barley tea going cold in my hands, and Master Odilon was saying, "You cried all night, that first night. Wouldn't stop till near dawn. Then you just — stopped. Looked at me with those big dark eyes like you were deciding whether I was worth trusting." He laughed his dry paper laugh. "Took you a year to decide I was."

I knew this night. Not remembered it — I'd been too young — but Odilon had told it so many times it lived in me like a scar lives in skin. The night a stranger left a bundle on his doorstep. The night I arrived in the Veilless Valley.

Except in the story, I was the bundle. In the story, I wasn't standing in the doorway watching.

In the story, I couldn't see her face.

The street beyond the pawnshop was rain-silvered and empty, and then it wasn't empty. A woman came down it fast, hood up, cloak plastered to her body, a bundle clutched against her chest with both arms. She moved like someone being hunted — checking over her shoulder every few steps, pressed close to the walls, choosing shadows over lamplight. At the pawnshop's door she stopped. She stood there a long moment in the rain, shoulders heaving, and then she knelt and laid the bundle down, tucked it against the wood where the eave's overhang kept off the worst of the wet.

"No," I said. My voice came out a child's voice, small and useless. "No, don't—"

She pulled something from inside her cloak. A cloth, fine-woven despite the rain, marked with a sigil I knew the way you know your own name — two hands clasped, one human, one thorn-crowned. She wrapped it tight around the bundle, tucking the edges with fingers that shook, and then she bent low over it and spoke, and the rain went quiet to let her be heard.

"My darling. My heart. Listen to me, because you won't remember, and that is the cruelest gift I have ever given anyone." Her voice broke and reassembled itself. "I am taking your memories because they will kill you if I keep them. There are men who would unmake you to use what you are, and there are worse things than men. So I am giving your first years to the stone, and the stone to the Veil, and I am leaving you in the one place none of it can follow. Forgive me. Forgive me. Grow up angry with me if you must, but grow up *alive*—"

"Don't," I said, and I was crying, nine years old and crying in a doorway thirty feet away that she somehow couldn't see, that the memory wouldn't let her see, because I had never been in the doorway. I had been in the bundle. I had been the thing in the rain. "Don't leave, I'll be good, I'll hide better, Mother, please—"

She kissed the bundle's forehead. She pressed her palm flat over the wrapped sigil, and her lips moved in words too quiet for even this memory to keep, and then she stood, and she did not look back, because looking back was the one thing she couldn't afford —

— and the memory *held* me there.

That was the trap. I understood it all at once, the way you understand drowning. The ward didn't just show the worst memory. It showed it from the angle that hurt most, and then it held you at the moment of worst hurt, forever, looping, while your body stood waist-deep in a fen and your mind knelt in the rain begging a woman who couldn't hear you. The blank-faced figure didn't need claws. It needed only patience. Travelers had stood in this nave for days, weeks, living their worst night on repeat until the fen took their bodies and the chapel took the rest.

The rain fell. She walked away. The loop reset — she came down the street again, hunted and desperate, and knelt, and spoke, and left.

"No," I said the second time, and the third, and somewhere in the tenth or twentieth I stopped counting, and the grief didn't dull. That was the horror of it. Memory doesn't dull inside itself. Every pass was the first pass. Every time, the fresh knife.

But.

Between loops — in the heartbeat where the rain reset — I noticed something. Small. Stupid. The kind of detail a thief notices because noticing details is the only trade I've ever had.

Her boots. In the memory, her boots were fine leather, court-made, ruined by the road. But when she knelt, when her cloak swung open — beneath it, around her neck, on a cord — she wore a plain wooden whistle. A shepherd's whistle. Cheap. Common. The kind of thing sold in valley markets for a copper.

My mother had come to the Veilless Valley before. Long enough to buy a trinket. Long enough, maybe, to watch from a distance, some market day, some festival, some ordinary afternoon when I was hauling crates for Odilon and complaining about my hands smelling of brass polish. She had stood in a crowd and bought a cheap whistle and worn it against her skin, and I had never known, because knowing was the one thing she couldn't give me.

The loop reset. Rain. Her footsteps. The kneeling.

And this time, when she bent over the bundle, I didn't beg.

I watched. All the way through. I made myself look at every second of it — the shaking fingers, the broken voice, the kiss, the palm pressed flat over the sigil, the straightening shoulders, the walk away without looking back. I looked at it the way I'd learned to look at everything in twenty-two years of taking other people's memories: closely, completely, without flinching, because flinching is how you lose the truth inside the feeling.

She hadn't abandoned me.

She had *garrisoned* me. Left me in the one fortress magic couldn't breach, guarded by the one spell worth more than any wall: forgetting. Every year of my lonely childhood, every stolen memory I'd lived on, every night I'd lain above the pawnshop wondering why — it had all been the shape of her love, drawn in negative, visible only from the side where it looked like absence.

The rain kept falling. The loop tried to reset.

And I stepped forward — out of the doorway, out of the angle the ward had chosen for me, into the middle of the memory-street, into the rain itself — and put myself between the woman and the door.

The world shuddered.

"You gave me up to save me," I said, to her, to the ward, to whatever ancient grief had built this trap. "Fine. Then I'll take it back myself. Both halves. The life you gave me AND the life you took. I'm not your sacrifice and I'm not your secret. I'm your *heir*. Watch me walk out of your worst night carrying both."

The rain stopped mid-fall.

Every drop hung in the air like beads of glass. The woman froze, hood up, hand half-raised. And then — slowly, like frost climbing a window — the whole scene lost its color, its texture, its weight, draining away into gray, into white, into—

* * *

Water.

I came back to myself gasping, on my knees in the flooded nave, the greenish light pouring down through the broken roof, the blank-faced figure already dissolving back into the water it had risen from. My whole body shook. My face was soaked and I genuinely could not tell fen-water from tears, and I decided, kneeling there in a drowned church, that I would never tell anyone otherwise.

"Sable!" Corvane was beside me — he'd broken his own rule, crossed the threshold, glamour or no glamour, consequences be damned. His hands hovered over my shoulders, not touching, fae-careful. "How long — the ward held you for hours, I thought — I thought I'd lost—"

"I'm fine." My voice came out scraped raw. I cleared my throat. Tried again. "I'm fine. It showed me the night I was left at Odilon's door. From the outside." I looked up at him, and something in my chest had rearranged itself, some load-bearing wall taken down and rebuilt in a different place. "Corvane. She wrapped me in the covenant sigil. She *spoke* the words over me. I heard them. Whatever oath the Amberdales broke — she was trying to mend it the only way she could. With me."

He went very still. That particular stillness of his, the one I'd learned meant a lifetime of guilt pressing up behind his teeth. "There is much I owe you of that night," he said quietly. "More than you know. When we're clear of this place, I'll—"

"Later," I said again. But softer this time. Because I'd spent hours inside my worst memory learning that *later* was a promise, not a refusal, and promises were the whole business of my family, apparently. Kept ones and broken ones alike.

I got to my feet. Waded to the plinth.

Up close, the stone was more than light. Looking into its facets was like looking into the fen water if the fen water had been honest — depths, movement, shapes drifting past that might have been memories or might have been worlds. Warmth came off it like a sleeping animal's breath. When I reached down and lifted it from its resting place, it weighed almost nothing, and it fit my cupped palms exactly, as if it had been cut to the shape of my hands a generation before I was born.

The hum I'd felt from the doorway resolved, suddenly, into something I could almost understand — a chord, two notes braided together, one bright and one dark, refusing to resolve into either. It sounded, absurdly, like home.

Above us, through the broken roof, the fog was lifting. And through it, faint but unmistakable, carried on a wind that had no business reaching this deep into the fen, came a sound that turned my blood to ice water.

A hunting horn. Crown-worked, triple-noted, the call Vael's trackers used to fix a quarry's position.

Then a second horn answered it. Closer.

Corvane's head snapped up, glamour flickering off him entirely for one naked instant — antlers, silver skin, eyes like struck flint. "They found the chapel," he said. "Or they found *us* in it. Either way, we have minutes, not days." He turned to me, and for the first time since I'd met him, the disgraced knight looked afraid. "Sable — there's a road out of this fen that only opens for those who carry a stone. It will cost us. But staying costs more."

I closed my fingers around the covenant-stone, felt its warmth pulse against my palms like a second heartbeat, and said, "Show me."
