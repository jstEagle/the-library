## 10. Corvane's Shame

The fire was small on purpose.

Corvane had built it that way — a fist of flame in a ring of river stones, tucked into the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite where the light would not carry. Old habits. For three hundred years he had made his camps the way a thief makes his alibis: small, deniable, gone by morning. The habit had outlived its purpose. The exile's habits always did.

Above him, Sable slept badly against their pack, one hand curled near the covenant-stone they'd pulled from the drowned chapel two days past. Even asleep, even unconscious of it, they kept a finger touching the thing — as if some part of them already understood what it meant to hold an oath in your palm and feel it waiting.

The stone glowed faintly through the leather wrapping. Fenwater-green, like light caught under ice. It had not stopped humming since Sable took it from the chapel floor, and Corvane had not told them what the humming meant, because he did not yet have the courage to say the words that went with it.

*It knows you,* the stone might as well have been saying, all night, every night. *It knows your blood. And I knew your mother.*

He fed the fire another stick and watched the sparks climb toward a sky stitched with stars too bright for the mortal realm. They were camped on the far side of the fen now, in the borderlands where the grass grew silver at the tips and the wind carried smells from both worlds at once — woodsmoke and night-blooming thornflowers, loam and something sweeter, older, like honey left in the sun for a century.

Sable stirred. Not waking. Turning toward the warmth the way children turn toward a voice.

Corvane looked away.

*You loved her.* The thought arrived with its usual cruelty, patient as erosion. *And you let her walk into the dark alone, because she asked you to. That is not obedience. That is cowardice wearing obedience's coat.*

He had worn the coat so long he had forgotten there was anything underneath.

The fae did not sleep as mortals slept — they sank into something nearer to standing death, eyes open, mind folded away like a letter no one would read. But tonight Corvane sat vigil instead, sword across his knees, watching the fire eat itself down to embers, and when the moon crossed the granite shelf and laid a blade of white light across Sable's sleeping face, he found himself speaking before he had decided to.

"You have her chin."

His voice came out rougher than intended, rusted from disuse. Sable didn't wake. He hadn't expected them to. Some confessions are easier made to the sleeping; that was why the fae confessed to rivers.

"Your mother had that same stubborn line to her jaw," he said quietly. "Maren Amberdale. She could out-argue a Thorn Court magistrate and make him thank her for the lesson." A breath. "She used to say the Veil wasn't a wall. She said it was a promise, and promises are only as strong as the people who keep remembering them."

The embers ticked. Somewhere down the slope, a nightjar called in a language that predated both realms.

"I never told you her name," Corvane said. "I've been traveling beside you for weeks and I've watched you grieve a mother you can't remember, and I've said nothing, because saying her name felt like handing you a wound wrapped in silk." His hand closed around the hilt of his sword, knuckles going pale. "But you'll need the name soon. You'll need all of it. So."

He drew a long breath, and began to unmake himself.

* * *

"My name is Corvane il Vess, last knight-sworn of the Order of the Seamed Banner," he said. "You know that much. What you don't know is what the order was *for*."

"When the first covenant was forged — nine generations before your grandmother's grandmother — the Amberdales did not act alone. No mortal house could bind two worlds with its own hands. They needed a witness who could not die, a guardian who could not be bribed or broken, someone whose life was tied to the oath itself so that if the oath failed, the guardian would know it in their bones. Feel it break. Carry the fracture forever."

He turned his wrist over. In the firelight, the old scar there stood out silver against his skin — a thin seam running from palm to elbow, like a stitch in flesh. He had shown it to no one in three hundred years.

"This is the Oathmark. Every knight of the Seamed Banner bore one. When the covenant was whole, it was warm. A living thing under the skin, beating slow as a second heart." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "When it broke, we felt every thread snap. All forty-one of us, across both realms, at the same instant. Like being flayed by sound. Eleven died of it outright — their oaths were their lives, literally, and when the oath tore, so did they. The rest of us..." He flexed his fingers. "The rest of us learned to live with a cold scar and a silence where our purpose used to be. Exile is just the word for what we did afterward. Wandering. Drinking. Trying to forget the shape of a duty we'd failed."

"The breaking happened nineteen years ago. Midwinter. And I was there."

The fire had burned low enough that the shadows reached him now, pooling in the hollows of his face. He let them.

"Maren Amberdale came to me on the eve of the solstice. She was — " His voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through. "She was eight months pregnant. She should have been hidden away, guarded, surrounded by the whole strength of a house that had kept the covenant for centuries. Instead she walked alone through the winter woods to find me, because she trusted exactly one person not to ask questions until she was ready to answer them."

"She told me the crown had come to her. Not the old king — the old king honored the covenant, more or less, the way a man honors a fence he's too lazy to tear down. But his advisors. His new breed of counselors. They had discovered what a dual-blooded child could be. Half mortal, half fae, born inside the covenant's own weaving. A child who could touch the Veil without burning. Who could *carry* it. Move it. Bend it." His jaw tightened. "A child who could be made into a key."

"They wanted you, Sable. Before you were born, they wanted you. And Maren looked at me — this knight sworn to protect the covenant above all things, above houses, above kings, above her — and she asked me the only question that has ever mattered: *what would you do if they came for your child?*"

Corvane stared into the embers as though the answer were still written there.

"I told her I would burn the crown to its foundations. And she smiled — that terrible smile, the one you make when someone has just proven your point for you — and she said, *then you understand why I can't let them find mine.*"

"She had decided. She would break the covenant herself. Deliberately. Tear the Veil wide enough to drown every scrying spell, every divination, every thread of fate-reading the crown's sorcerers could throw at the world — chaos enough to hide one small life inside it. A storm to bury a single footprint. She would shatter nine generations of oath, exile herself, abandon everything, all so that the crown would spend nineteen years hunting leaks in the dark instead of one child in a valley where magic couldn't follow."

"And she ordered me to look away."

The words fell into the fire and burned black.

"I was the guardian of the covenant. My entire existence, my oath, my scar, my soul's architecture — built to prevent exactly what she was about to do. If I had raised my voice, if I had gone to the Court, if I had done *anything* but stand in those winter woods and watch her walk toward the ritual site with her hand on her belly — " His breath shuddered. "I could have stopped her. I want you to understand that. There is no version of this where I was helpless. I was the strongest thing in those woods, and I stood aside, because she asked, and because I — "

He stopped. Started again, quieter.

"Because I loved her. Not the way knights love their charges, which is a clean thing, a duty-shaped thing. I loved her the way the tide loves the shore — uselessly, endlessly, knowing the shape of the loss in advance. And she knew it. Gods forgive her, she knew it, and she spent it. She took the one coin I couldn't refuse and bought my silence with it, and I let her, and I have hated us both for it every day since."

"Nineteen years ago, at midwinter, the Veil tore. Forty-one knights fell screaming as their oaths shattered. The Otherworld began to leak into yours — glamours in border towns, hungry things in the fens, doors opening where doors should never be. And I rode to the Amberdale house to demand answers I already knew, and found it empty. Cold hearths. A sigil cloth missing from the birthing chamber. And on the table, a letter addressed to the Order, in Maren's hand, containing two sentences."

He recited them the way a man recites the inscription on his own grave.

"*If you are reading this, Corvane, then you looked away as I asked, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry. Keep them safe until they're strong enough to choose for themselves — and then let them choose.*"

"That was it. No location. No name for the child. Just an instruction, and the cruelest mercy in it: *let them choose*. As if choice were possible for a thing like me, bound by oath to a bloodline I'd just helped unmake." He shook his head slowly. "So I searched. Nineteen years. I followed rumors of a memory-thief in a magic-dead valley — a thief who could take what no thief should be able to take, whose gift worked where gift-work was impossible. I told myself it was coincidence. I told myself a great many things."

"And then the crown proclaimed its purge, and the inquisitors walked into the one place they should never have been able to walk, and I understood that Maren's storm had finally blown itself out. The footprint was showing. So I went to the valley, and I found you coming out of it, drowning in your first breath of real magic, and I saw her face on you like a signature, and I thought: *there you are. The reason for everything. The sin and the absolution, wearing one pair of boots.*"

The fire had nearly died. Corvane coaxed it back with trembling hands — his hands, which had never trembled in battle, trembling now over kindling.

* * *

Sable was awake.

He knew it the way he knew weather — a shift in the quality of the silence, the held-breath stillness of someone listening so hard their body forgot to breathe. He didn't turn. Letting them decide whether to speak was the least courtesy he had left to offer.

"How much did you hear?" he asked anyway, because cowardice and honesty could share a bench.

"From 'you have her chin,'" Sable said. Their voice was sleep-roughened and very quiet. In the ember-light their eyes were unreadable — the same unreadability he'd seen in Maren, that Amberdale talent for holding a whole argument behind the face and releasing only the verdict.

"Then you heard all of it."

"I heard that you loved my mother." A pause. "And that you let her do the worst thing anyone's ever done, because she asked you to."

"Yes."

"And you've spent nineteen years deciding whether that makes you a traitor or a friend."

Corvane finally turned. Sable sat up against their pack, the wrapped stone in their lap, its green glow leaking between their fingers like something alive trying to see out.

"Both," Corvane said. "That's the trap of it. Treason and loyalty wore the same face that night, and I've never been able to tell them apart since. Ask the Thorn Court and I'm an oathbreaker who stood aside while the covenant died. Ask your mother and I was the only soul in two worlds who gave her what she needed." His mouth twisted. "Ask me, and I still don't know. I rehearsed this confession for nineteen years, Sable, and I never once got to the part where I forgive myself. I don't think that part exists."

Sable was silent for a long moment. The nightjar called again, closer now. The stone hummed against their palms.

"My whole life," they said at last, "I thought being left in that valley was abandonment. Something done *to* me. And now you're telling me it was the biggest act of love anyone's ever committed — a mother tearing apart the fabric of two worlds to keep one child's name out of a king's mouth." Their laugh was brittle. "Do you know what that does to a person? I built my whole self on top of a wound, and you've just told me the wound was a door."

"I know," Corvane said. "I've lived nineteen years on top of mine."

"Then we're carrying different halves of the same night." Sable looked down at the glowing bundle in their lap. "You obeyed her and lost everything. I was the reason and never got to know it. Neither of us chose any of this." They lifted their head, and in the dark their eyes caught the ember-light and held it, and for one vertiginous moment Corvane saw not the thief from the valley but something older — the line of Maren's jaw set in the exact expression she'd worn in the winter woods, the terrible calm of someone who has already decided.

"But the eclipse is in eleven days," Sable said. "And there are two stones left. So here's my choice, Corvane, since everyone keeps insisting I get to make one: I'm not going to waste it on hating you. Hate is heavy, and I've got mountains to climb." A pause, and something almost gentle moved through their voice. "But I need you to promise me one thing. Next time someone asks you to look away — next time loving someone means standing silent while they break the world —"

"You want my word that I won't."

"I want your word that you'll *tell me*. Whatever she asked of you, whatever anyone asks — I'd rather hear an ugly truth than be protected by a beautiful silence. I've had enough beautiful silences to last a lifetime."

Corvane bowed his head. Three hundred years of discipline, and it was this — a boy-woman-thief with stolen memories and their mother's chin — who finally found the crack in him.

"You have my word," he said. "No more beautiful silences."

"Good." Sable settled back against the pack, the stone glowing steadily against their chest now, its hum deepening by degrees — and Corvane realized, with a lurch, that the hum had changed pitch the moment Sable spoke of choosing. As though it were listening. As though it approved.

"One more thing," Sable murmured, already sliding back toward sleep. "This stone's been humming louder all night. Right after you said her name, it — did you feel that? Like it turned over in its sleep."

Corvane went very still.

He had felt it. He had felt the covenant-stone respond to Maren Amberdale's name the way a hound responds to its master's step — and that meant the stones remembered her, which meant the third stone, the one the mountain sang of, the one hidden where no thief could follow —

— might remember her too. Might hold more than a message. Might hold her voice, her face, her reasons, everything she'd locked away when she broke the world.

"Sleep, Sable," Corvane said softly, feeding the last branch to the flames. "Tomorrow we climb."

And in the dark, against his will, hope — that old, treacherous, tide-shaped thing — began to rise in him again.
