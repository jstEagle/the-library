## 11. The Court of Thorns Refuses

The briars parted for Corvane the way a crowd parts for an executioner.

Sable had noticed it over the past days of travel — how the thorned hedges that guarded every approach to the Thorn Court seemed to lean away from him, how the black roses along the path turned their faces to follow his stride like courtiers tracking a king they resented. He walked through brambles that would have flayed Sable to ribbons, and the brambles *apologized*.

"They remember me," Corvane said, when Sable asked. His voice was flat in the way it always went flat when the subject was himself. "That's not the same as being welcome."

"Where I come from, remembering someone is the closest thing to loving them."

Corvane glanced back at them, and something moved behind his eyes — quick and pained, like a bird striking glass. "Then your valley was kinder than any court I've known."

The road climbed through country that made Sable's stolen memories feel like counterfeit coin. They had carried the sea once, borrowed from a merchant's mind — salt wind, gull-cries, the green-gray heave of water under a pale sky. But this land put the sea to shame. Hills rolled in colors Sable had no names for: a violet so deep it looked bruised, grass that shimmered silver where the light touched it, trees whose leaves chimed faintly against one another like distant bells. The sky itself was wrong — or right — a shade of gold-green that made everything beneath it look newly painted.

And everywhere, magic. It pressed against Sable's skin like warm water, filled their lungs with every breath, hummed in their teeth. After twenty years in the Veilless Valley, where the air had been as flat and flavorless as old bread, the abundance of it was nearly unbearable. Like a starving man set before a feast. Like drowning in honey.

They had learned to wear it, mostly. Learned to keep their gift sheathed behind Corvane's lessons — *breathe slow, think gray thoughts, be a stone, be a stone* — because here, in the fae realms, a human who drank ambient power the way Sable did was not merely strange. They were a delicacy. A curiosity. A threat.

The Court of Thorns rose out of a valley of white poppies at dusk.

It was not built; Sable understood that immediately. It had *grown*, over centuries, from living wood black as iron and dense as stone. Towers spiraled upward like frozen smoke, crowned with thorns longer than swords. Bridges of woven briar arched between spires, and lanterns hung from the branches — not fire, but captured fragments of twilight, glowing amber and dimming and brightening again in slow rhythm, as if the whole palace breathed.

Great gates of interlaced hawthorn stood open at the base of the causeway. Beyond them, torchlight and music, and the murmur of hundreds of voices.

"Rules," Corvane said quietly, stopping Sable just short of the gate. He turned to face them fully, and in the failing light his features seemed sharper than usual, more angular, less human — the exile's mask slipping toward whatever lay beneath. "You speak only when spoken to. You do not accept gifts — nothing, not a cup of wine, not a compliment, not a flower. Gifts create debts, and debts are chains. If anyone asks what you are, you say you're my sworn charge and nothing more."

"And if they ask why an exiled knight has a sworn charge?"

His jaw tightened. "Then we've already lost."

He started toward the gate. Sable caught his sleeve.

"The sigil cloth," they said. "You said the queen might know it. That she owed my family." They had rehearsed this argument across three days of hard riding through the borderlands, refining it the way one whets a blade. "But you also said the Court blames humans for the Veil. So which is it? Are we petitioners or prisoners?"

Corvane looked down at Sable's hand on his sleeve, then up at their face. "Yes," he said.

* * *

The throne room of the Thorn Court was a cathedral grown rather than raised, its vaulted ceiling lost in living shadow, its pillars the trunks of trees that had been young when the covenant was forged. The floor was moss, soft and springing underfoot, and it swallowed the sound of footsteps so completely that the assembled courtiers seemed to glide rather than walk.

There were hundreds of them. Sable had expected fae — had steeled themselves against the beautiful, terrible faces Corvane had warned them about — but nothing had prepared them for the sheer *variety*. Some were tall and slender as herons, with skin like birch bark and eyes like pools of standing water. Some were small and sharp-faced, clustered on the mossy sills of high windows like starlings. Some were barely recognizable as people at all: figures of woven shadow, of river-mist, of autumn leaves bound together by will. And scattered among them, wearing collars of cold iron and expressions of careful blankness, were humans — servants, hostages, or worse.

Every face turned toward them as they entered. Every eye weighed them.

Sable kept their gaze forward, on the dais at the far end, where the Queen of Thorns sat upon a throne of white hawthorn.

She was smaller than Sable had imagined. That was the first shock. She sat with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting patiently for tea, and she might have been forty or four hundred — her face was unlined but ancient, her hair the silver-white of birch in winter, her eyes the exact color of the sky above the valley of poppies. She wore no crown. She didn't need one. Power came off her the way heat comes off a forge, and every creature in the hall bent subtly toward her, the way flowers bend toward sun.

Corvane knelt. After a heartbeat's hesitation, Sable knelt too, knees sinking into cool moss.

"Your Majesty." Corvane's voice carried without effort, filling the vaulted dark. "I bring word of the Veil, and a request—"

"We know what you bring, Corvane." Her voice was quiet, and yet it reached every corner of the hall as though she'd whispered into each ear individually. "We have watched you cross our lands with a human child who drinks magic like wine and hides it badly. We have counted the leaks in the Veil ourselves. Our own seers have read the coming eclipse." She unfolded her hands. "Rise. Both of you. And tell Us, thief-of-the-valley — for that is what your gift makes you, is it not? — why We should hear anything from the bloodline that broke the world?"

The hall went silent. Even the leaves stopped chiming.

Sable rose slowly. Their heart hammered, but underneath the fear was something harder, something that had been tempered in the pawnshop and the quarry tunnels and the drowned chapel where they had walked through their worst memory and come out the other side. They thought of Wren Vael's hollow eyes. Of the keeper's body left cooling on the floorboards. Of the letter in their mother's hand: *I broke it to save you.*

"I'll tell you exactly why," Sable said. "Because the Veil doesn't care whose fault it was. Because the leaks don't discriminate — they're bleeding magic into your realm and nightmares into mine, and in nine days an eclipse will seal them shut forever, or blow them wide open. I didn't break the covenant. I wasn't even born. But I'm the only one who can mend it, and you know it, or you wouldn't have let us walk through your gate alive."

A ripple went through the court — not laughter, exactly, but something adjacent to it. Shocked delight. A human, addressing the Queen of Thorns without honorifics, without fear, without asking permission.

The queen did not smile. But something shifted in her stillness, some minute recalibration, the way a chess player regards an unexpected move.

"Bold," she said. "Foolish, but bold. Tell Us, then, heir-of-Amberdale: what would you ask of the Thorn Court?"

"Alliance. Aid. Safe passage and shelter while I gather the remaining stones. Nothing more."

"Nothing more." The queen's gaze drifted past Sable, to the assembled court. "You hear it, my lords and ladies? The oathbreaker's heir asks for *nothing more*. As if the Amberdales had ever given *nothing* when they took from Us."

She rose from the throne. She was taller standing than sitting — or perhaps the throne simply diminished everyone else. She descended the dais steps with unhurried grace, and the court bowed away from her like grass before wind, until she stood directly before Sable, close enough that Sable could see the faint tracery of veins beneath her translucent skin, could smell something like frost and crushed roses.

"Do you know what your grandmother swore to Us?" the queen asked softly. "Not your mother. Your grandmother, who forged the second binding with her own hands. She swore that the Amberdales would stand as wardens between the realms — that no door between worlds would open or close without their consent freely given, on both sides. And then your mother—" her voice cracked, not with grief but with fury, a sound like ice splitting on a winter pond "—your mother closed the doors. All of them. Without consent. Without warning. She sealed the Veil's anchors and shattered the stones, and half our people who were walking in your realm that night were *trapped there*, cut off from home, aging, dying, forgotten. Do you know what became of them, heir-of-Amberdale? We do. We have spent twenty years counting our dead."

The words landed like blows. Sable thought of the letter hidden in the mountain's memory, of their mother's terrified face among the witnesses, pregnant and desperate. *She broke it to save me.* And all around that truth, the wreckage of it — fae stranded in a hostile human realm, fading, hunted, dying for the sake of one hidden child.

"I know what she did," Sable said quietly. "I know better than you do, maybe. She did it to hide me. To keep the crown of Aldermarch from making me into a weapon. She traded your people's safety for my life, and I have to live inside that arithmetic every day of my life. I'm not here to defend her. I'm here to fix what her choice cost both our worlds. Can you say the same about yours — that you've done nothing these twenty years but count your dead? Or did you seal your borders and abandon the stranded ones too, because helping them would mean admitting the Veil needed mending rather than avenging?"

The silence that followed was absolute.

Somewhere in the rafters, a single leaf chimed, once, and fell.

The queen regarded Sable for a long moment, her expression unreadable as deep water. When she spoke again, her voice had gone very soft, which Corvane had told Sable — in one of his rare pieces of tactical advice — was far more dangerous than when she shouted.

"You dare audit Us. In Our own hall." She circled Sable slowly, and the court held its breath. "Do you know what the law says of you, thief-of-the-valley? The law written in the first days, before the covenant, when your kind and Ours warred across a thousand battlefields? An oathbreaker's blood forfeits protection. An oathbreaker's heir may be taken and judged. And the judgment of the Thorn Court for the breaking of the covenant is—" she stopped before Sable again, and smiled, and it was the most terrible thing Sable had seen since the memory-ward in the drowned chapel "—death. Death, and the planting of a white hawthorn over the grave, so that even your ghost serves Our realm."

Guards moved. Sable hadn't seen them arrive — tall figures of woven shadow detaching from the pillars, closing in with the smooth inevitability of tide. Corvane stepped forward, and a dozen weapons leveled at him in the same instant.

"Corvane," the queen said without looking at him. "You will stand down. You are already exiled. Do not make yourself a corpse as well."

"Your Majesty." Corvane's voice was steady, but Sable heard the strain beneath it, the sound of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "Kill the heir and the last stone dies with them. The third stone isn't a jewel in a vault. It's bound to their blood. Kill them, and the Veil tears open at the eclipse, and everything your people suffered these twenty years becomes merely the prologue."

Lies, Sable thought wildly. Half-lies, at least — they didn't actually know if the third stone would die with them. But Corvane delivered it with the flat certainty of a soldier stating terrain, and the court murmured, and the guards hesitated, because even the fae feared the eclipse.

The queen's eyes narrowed. For a long moment the only sound was the faint chiming of leaves and Sable's own pulse in their ears.

Then, from the edge of the dais, a voice like dry paper: "Majesty. There is the matter of the debt."

An ancient fae unfolded from the shadows beside the throne — small, stooped, with skin like crumpled parchment and eyes milky with age. Sable hadn't seen him arrive. Perhaps he'd been there all along, part of the throne's own shadow.

The queen went very still. "There is no debt."

"There is," the old one said placidly. "The Second Binding. Your Majesty was not yet crowned. The Amberdale matriarch pulled your predecessor's soul back from the Hollow — from beyond the Veil itself — at cost of her own youth. The terms were witnessed. One favor, to be claimed by any Amberdale in need, unto the last of the line." His milky eyes found Sable. "This one is the last of the line."

"That debt was discharged—"

"It was *deferred*, Majesty. I keep the ledgers. I would know."

The silence stretched until Sable thought they would scream. Then the queen laughed — a single, sharp note with no joy in it whatsoever, the sound of a blade being drawn.

"So. The last Amberdale comes to Our hall to beg alliance, and instead finds an unpaid bill." She turned to Sable, and her terrible smile widened by a fraction. "Very well, thief-of-the-valley. The ledger stands. Claim your favor. Spend it now, and We will grant you safe passage through our realms, shelter, and noninterference while you gather your stones. The full weight of the Thorn Court's patience, extended to your little quest."

Hope surged in Sable's chest — and died just as quickly, because the queen was still smiling, and fae smiles were contracts.

"But understand the price of the accounting," she continued. "One favor. Once. Spent, it is gone forever, and the Amberdale name owes the Thorn Court nothing more — nor can it ever call on Us again. Whatever comes after — eclipse, catastrophe, the tearing of the sky — you will stand alone. No aid. No sanctuary. No appeal. Is that clear enough for your human ears?"

Sable's mouth was dry. *One favor.* Their only leverage, their only shield, spent in the first hour of the first negotiation, on mere passage — when Vael was out there, when the crown was out there, when the Thorn Court's army might be the difference between the world ending and the world continuing.

But the alternative was a white hawthorn growing over their grave.

"I claim it," Sable said. "Safe passage. Shelter. Noninterference. That's the favor."

"Spoken and witnessed." The old fae's quill scratched against parchment that hadn't existed a moment before. The air itself seemed to shiver, sealing it.

The queen returned to her throne and settled onto it, folding her hands once more, serene as a portrait. "You will be housed in the east wing. You will dine alone. You will depart at dawn — the Court does not extend hospitality beyond necessity." Her gaze flicked to Corvane, and something colder passed across her face. "As for you, Knight of the Broken Oath. You will remain. There are questions regarding your exile that We find... renewed interest in."

Corvane went rigid. "My duty is to the heir."

"Your duty died with your honor, Corvane. What remains is sentiment, and sentiment can wait a night." She waved a hand, dismissive as brushing away a fly. "Take them below. Give the human a room with a window, at least. We are not savages."

Guards escorted them — not roughly, but with the impersonal firmness of tide or gravity — through corridors of living wood lit by captured twilight. Sable walked in stunned silence, replaying it all: the sentence of death, the old fae's ledger, the favor spent like a fool spending their last coin on bread when famine waited ahead.

At a branching corridor, the guards stopped. One gestured left — Sable's way. Two others took Corvane's arms, gently, firmly, right.

"Sable." Corvane's voice was low. His eyes, over the guards' shoulders, were grim. "Whatever happens tonight — whatever they ask you, whatever they show you — remember what you are. Not what they say you owe. What you *are*."

"And what's that?"

Something almost like a smile touched his mouth. "Mine to guard. That was never the Court's gift to take."

They took him away, down into the dark heart of the palace, and Sable stood alone in the corridor of black wood and captured twilight, listening to the leaves chime overhead like a thousand tiny bells tolling for something that hadn't died yet.

Their room, when they reached it, did have a window. Through it, far below, the valley of white poppies glowed faintly in the fae-dark, and beyond it, impossibly far, Sable could just make out a thin scar of darkness on the horizon — the direction of the Veil, the direction of home, the direction of everything they were trying to save.

Nine days to the eclipse.

And somewhere below, in the dungeons grown from the roots of the palace, Corvane sat answering questions about an exile twenty years old — questions whose answers, Sable suspected with a cold certainty settling into their bones, had nothing to do with justice, and everything to do with the queen's renewed interest in the last of the Amberdales.

Sleep did not come. But near midnight, footsteps paused outside their door — light, deliberate, unmistakably intentional — and a scrap of parchment slid beneath it.

Sable snatched it up. The handwriting was spidery, ancient, familiar somehow:

*The queen knows what the third stone truly is. She knew before you did. Ask her why your mother came to the Court the night before she broke the covenant — and ask her what was promised in return.*

*Burn this.*

*— The Keeper of Ledgers*

Outside, far off across the poppy fields, thunder rolled from a cloudless sky.
