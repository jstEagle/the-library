## 12. Stolen From the Stealer

The road out of the fen country climbed through birch woods gone silver with early frost, and Sable had learned, in the weeks since the drowned chapel, to love the way magic felt at the edges of things.

That was the strangest part of leaving the valley. In the Veilless Valley there was nothing to feel — a silence so total it had seemed like the natural state of the world. Out here, magic was weather. It pooled in hollows like cold air, ran along streams in bright threads, hung over old battlefields in gray shrouds that made Sable's teeth ache. They were learning to read it the way they'd once learned to read travelers' faces: which ones carried something worth taking, which ones would notice, which ones would fight.

"You're doing it again," Corvane said without turning around. He walked ahead on the narrow path, his cloak drawn up against the cold, and though he wore a human shape — tall, dark-haired, unremarkable except for eyes the color of deep water — Sable could see the glamour's seams now, faint silver stitching where the fae knight underneath showed through.

"Doing what?"

"Reaching. You stretch toward every bright thing we pass like a child at a market stall. One day you'll grab something that bites."

"Everything bites. That's how you know it's alive." Sable lengthened their stride to catch up. "Besides, I'm not taking anything. Just... looking."

"With your hands." Corvane glanced back, and there was something in his face that might have been amusement or might have been worry; after ten years of exile, his expressions had rusted into ambiguity. "In my court, children who reached like that were given bells to wear. So everyone could hear them coming."

"Did you wear one?"

"I was the bell." He said it lightly, but the lightness had a worn place in it, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many thumbs.

They crested a rise where the birches thinned and the road dropped toward a river valley — and stopped. Below, half-hidden by the trees, stood a coaching inn with a sagging roof and a sign depicting a boar in a crown. Smoke rose from its chimney. Horses stamped in the yard. Ordinary. Safe-looking.

Sable felt Corvane go still beside them, the particular stillness of a predator deciding whether the thing ahead was prey.

"Inn means travelers," Sable said. "Travelers mean news. We haven't heard word of Vael since Thornhold."

"We heard word of nothing since Thornhold," Corvane said. "Which is worse. Wren Vael does not go quiet. She goes *still*." His hand drifted to the sword at his hip — an ugly, practical blade, nothing like the thorn-forged elegance he'd described from his old life. "We take a room. We ask nothing, we steal nothing, we leave before dawn."

"You're no fun."

"I am alive. It has required sacrifices."

* * *

The inn's common room smelled of tallow and wet wool and roasting onions, and it was so aggressively ordinary that Sable almost laughed. A dozen travelers hunched over bowls. A girl fed sticks into the hearth. The innkeep drew ale with the weary economy of a man who had done nothing else for thirty years and expected to do nothing else for thirty more.

Sable sat with their back to the wall and let the room wash over them — not reaching, just listening. A peddler grumbling about tolls on the north road. Two drovers arguing amiably about a dog. A woman in traveling leathers who kept her hood up even indoors, sitting alone near the fire, eating slowly, methodically, like someone fueling a machine rather than enjoying a meal.

Sable looked away from her. Looked back.

There was something wrong with the space around her. Not magic — the valley had taught Sable what true absence felt like, and this wasn't absence. It was more like a hole cut in the world's fabric and badly darned. The firelight bent slightly as it passed her. The sounds of the room went quiet in a small radius around her table, as if the noise itself was afraid.

*Sable.* Corvane's voice arrived inside their skull, cool and precise — one of the small magics he permitted himself, and only ever for warnings. *Do not look at the woman by the fire.*

*Too late.*

*I know. I recognized her the moment we entered. That is why I chose the table farthest from her.* A pause. *It is her. Or it is wearing her.*

Sable kept their eyes on their bowl and breathed. Ten years of surviving on wits in a town where everyone knew your name — those instincts hadn't died on the road; they'd sharpened. Don't run. Running confirms. Finish your meal. Be boring.

"She hasn't moved," Sable murmured into their stew.

"She doesn't need to. If she wanted us dead, we'd be dead. She wants something else." Corvane tore bread with unnecessary violence. "Eat. Then upstairs. Now."

They made it four steps toward the stairs.

The woman rose. Crossed the room. And pulled back her hood, and it was Wren Vael — gray-eyed, hollow-cheeked, with the terrible calm of a person who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to her and found it survivable.

"Amberdale," she said. "A word. Upstairs. Alone. Or I do this here, in front of all these people, and we find out together what happens when I empty a room."

Corvane's hand was on his sword. Vael didn't even glance at him. "You I'll allow to follow. At a distance. Where I can see your hands."

*This is a mistake,* Corvane said inside Sable's head.

*Probably,* Sable said. *But she's offering me a choice, and I'd rather know what she wants than make her show me.*

They climbed the stairs. Vael followed, three steps behind, close enough that Sable could feel the wrongness of her like heat from a forge — that darned-hole feeling, that sense of a person with pieces missing and edges left raw.

The upstairs corridor was dim and empty. Vael stopped outside the last door, opened it, and gestured them through with a courtesy that was somehow worse than a threat.

Inside: a narrow bed, a window overlooking the dark yard, a candle burning low. Vael shut the door and leaned against it.

"You've been busy," she said. "The chapel stone. I felt it move. Like a tooth being pulled from the world's jaw." Her head tilted. "You took it through a memory-ward. Walked through your own worst night and came out holding the prize. Do you know how many of the crown's examiners couldn't do that? How many we lost trying to reach things behind wards like that?" Something flickered across her face — envy, maybe, or grief wearing envy's clothes. "You're wasted on covenant-restoring, Amberdale. You should be working for us."

"I told you my answer at Thornhold."

"You told me fear dressed as principle. People do that." She pushed off the door and stepped closer, and Sable forced themselves not to step back. "I offered you a place beside me. I offered to let the valley go. You said no. Fine. But understand what happens now. The crown knows what you are. The crown knows what the stones can do. And the crown does not negotiate."

"Then why are you here alone? Why not bring the whole hunt?"

"Because what I'm about to do, I'd rather do quietly." Vael raised her hand, and Sable saw — really saw, for the first time — what lived under her skin. Threads. Dozens of them, hundreds, fine as spider silk and pale as grave-mist, running out from her fingers into the air, each one anchored somewhere else, each one humming faintly. Anchored in memories. In stolen things.

Her gift, laid bare. The gift the crown had hollowed her into.

"What are you—" Sable started.

"Examination," Wren Vael said, almost gently. "Hold still. It's easier if you hold still."

The threads struck.

* * *

Pain first — pain like a hook behind the eyes — and then something worse than pain, which was *absence*. Sable felt the memories tear free in long strips, and each strip took a piece of the world with it.

The sea. The merchant's memory of the sea, the first thing they'd ever stolen, the taste of salt they'd kept for fifteen years — *gone*, and with it the knowledge of what gulls sounded like, because the gulls had lived in that memory too. The pawnshop keeper's face, teaching them to weigh coins by feel — gone, and with it the ability to remember the weight of a coin at all. A decade of travelers, a decade of borrowed sunsets and borrowed laughter and borrowed griefs, ripped out in handfuls, and each removal left not a wound but a *gap*, a smooth blank place where knowing used to be.

Sable tried to scream and found they'd forgotten how breathing worked for a moment — actually forgotten, the mechanics of it, the rhythm — before the body remembered on its own, animal and stubborn.

Vael stepped back, threads retracting into her sleeves, and studied them with clinical detachment. "A decade. That's what the crown ordered. Everything you stole before the age of eighteen — they consider it evidence." She paused at the door. "For what it's worth, Amberdale — and it isn't worth much — I hated that order less than most. At least this way you'll learn what I learned. That you can lose everything and keep walking."

Then she was gone, down the stairs, out into the night, and Sable was alone in a candlelit room full of holes.

Corvane burst through the door seconds later — he must have come up the moment Vael descended — and caught Sable as their knees buckled.

"Sable. Sable, look at me."

Sable looked at him and felt terror of a kind they'd never known. Not fear of death. Fear of dissolution. Because they were looking at a man they trusted with their life, and they could not remember why.

"I know you," Sable whispered. "I know I know you. I can't— Corvane, I can't remember *us*. There's a shape where it should be and the shape is empty."

"Yes," Corvane said. His voice was steady, but his hands, gripping Sable's shoulders, were not. "Yes. Listen to me. This is what she does. This is what was done to her. You are still here. Do you understand me? The gaps are not you. You are what remains when the gaps are made, and you remain."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm going to prove it." He lowered them both to sit on the narrow bed, facing each other, knee to knee, the way you'd steady a spooked horse. "I'm going to tell you everything. Every day since the briars. And you're going to listen until the shape fills in. That's what memory is, Sable. It isn't only what's inside you. It's what other people carry of you. She can steal what you hold. She cannot steal what I hold."

And so he began.

"The first day. You crawled out of the Veilless Valley on your hands and knees, sick as a dog, because your senses had never touched magic and then touched all of it at once. You called it 'the world screaming.' You tried to steal my memories within the hour — don't argue, you did, you thought I wouldn't notice, and I let you try because I wanted to see. Nothing happened. You were furious. You accused me of having no inner life." A ghost of a smile. "I have never been so insulted or so flattered."

Sable's breath was ragged. "I don't—"

"You will. Keep listening. The second day. You asked me why I stayed exiled instead of going home to the Court of Thorns, and I lied to you, and you knew I lied, and you let it lie there between us because you understood that some wounds aren't ready to be spoken. That was the day I started trusting you. Thieves understand locked doors better than knights do."

He talked. The road of borrowed faces, the border town, the glamours — "You stole a face off a fae lord, Sable. A *fae*. No human thief has ever done that. You did it casually, like picking a pocket, and then complained that his memories tasted of rotting flowers." The drowned chapel, the memory-ward, the worst night — "You walked through the night your mother left you, and you came out the other side carrying the stone, and you cried, and then you made a joke about it, and I understood then that you were the strongest person I had ever guarded, including every knight and queen of two realms."

Piece by piece. Hour by hour. The candle burned down; Corvane lit another from his kit without stopping the recitation. And slowly — not healed, never healed, but *anchored* — Sable felt the shapes fill. Not with the memories themselves; those were gone, torn out at the root. But with the *witness*. With the knowledge that the events had happened, that they had meant something, that someone else had stood beside them and seen.

"It's like..." Sable searched for words and found the search itself exhausting. "It's like being told about a country I visited. I believe you. I believe all of it. But I wasn't— I'm not sure I was *there*."

"You were there. I was there. Between the two of us, that makes it real." Corvane's voice roughened. "My mother told me once — before the Court, before any of it — that a fae is only as real as their name is witnessed. We bind ourselves by being seen. I thought it was sentiment. It isn't. It's the oldest law we have." He gripped Sable's hands. "So I am witnessing you, Sable Amberdale. All night if I have to. Every day after. She took what you held. She cannot take what I hold. As long as I draw breath, you have a past."

Sable broke then — quietly, the way people break when they're too exhausted for drama — and Corvane held on and kept talking, softer now, filling the gaps with his voice like a man pouring sand into a breached hull.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the window. Somewhere below, the inn was waking: pans clattering, the smell of bread, horses stamping in the yard. The ordinary world, indifferent and continuing.

Sable sat upright, hollowed but standing, and found that the strangest thing was the *competence gaps*. Their hands remembered lockpicking — muscle kept what the mind had lost — but when they reached for the memory of learning it, there was only smooth blankness. They knew the pawnshop keeper had loved them. They could not recall his voice.

"He's gone twice now," Sable said quietly. "Once when Vael killed him. Once last night. I don't get to lose him a third time, Corvane. Whatever's left of him in me — I want it back."

"Then we take it back." Corvane stood, rolled his shoulders, and Sable saw the knight underneath the exile for the first time in days — the thing the Court of Thorns had made and then discarded, all iron and oath. "Vael keeps what she steals. It's how her gift works — she holds it, threads and all. Which means everything she took from you is still *in her*. Reachable."

"Reachable how?"

Corvane smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "You stole a memory off a fae lord. Tell me, thief — what do you suppose you could take off a hollowed woman carrying a decade of stolen goods?"

Sable stared at him. And in the blank places where their childhood used to be, something new kindled — not a memory, but a purpose, sharp and clean as a blade.

"She's heading north," Sable said. "Toward the mountain. Toward the second stone. She thinks she emptied me. She doesn't know you spent all night making me *witnessed*."

"No," Corvane agreed. "She doesn't know that at all."

Sable picked up their pack, settled it on shoulders that felt lighter and emptier than any pack had a right to be, and looked out the window at the north road, where frost still clung to the birches like a scattering of bones.

"Then let's go introduce her to the new me," Sable said. "The one with nothing left to steal — and everything left to take."
