## 13. The Mountain That Remembers

The mountain was alive.

I had told Sable this three times on the road, and each time they had looked at me with that particular expression — the one that said *I believe you're telling the truth, and I resent it* — and said nothing. Now, standing at its foot as the last light of dusk drained from the sky, I watched them finally understand.

Kareth-Moru did not loom the way dead stone looms. It *breathed*. Its flanks rose and fell in a rhythm too slow to be called breathing and too deliberate to be anything else, and along its ridgelines veins of pale gold light pulsed in time — the same rhythm, I realized with an old ache, as a heartbeat. Not a human one. A heartbeat like the Veil's own, if the Veil had ever consented to have a body.

"It's warm," Sable said. They had pulled off a glove and pressed their bare palm against the rock, and now they stared at their own hand as though it had betrayed them. "Corvane. The stone is warm."

"Granite holds the day's heat," I said. "This holds something older."

They shot me a look. Ten days of travel had taught me the full vocabulary of that look: suspicion, exhaustion, hunger, and beneath all of it, a stubborn ember of wonder that no amount of grief or fear had managed to smother. It was the ember that frightened me. Wonder was how the Amberdales had always begun.

"The wardens' records say the mountain remembers," I said, dismounting. My knees protested; fae bodies heal fast but do not forgive long rides, and mine had carried two centuries of grudges already. "Not metaphorically. When the covenant was forged, the first Amberdale spoke the oath here, at the mountain's heart, so that the words would never be lost. Kareth-Moru took them into itself. It replays the forging for anyone who climbs to the Hollow and asks."

"And you've asked before." It wasn't a question. Sable had gotten good at those — at statements shaped like knives.

"I stood witness once," I said. "A hundred and sixty years ago. Before your mother was born."

Something moved behind their eyes. They were getting better at hiding things, my thief — better than any thief I'd known — but grief still leaked through the seams when the name came up. *Your mother.* Two words, and the whole architecture of their composure shivered.

"And it just... shows you?" they asked. "No price?"

"There is always a price." I began unsaddling the horses. "But the mountain is honest about it. You pay in memory. Not stolen — spent. You give it a recollection freely, and it keeps the recollection, and shows you what it keeps. The old wardens called it tithing."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. In the valley where they'd grown up, magic could not follow, and so they had learned every human trick of survival — theft, misdirection, the quick smile and quicker hands. But standing before a living mountain, all those tricks must have felt like bringing a candle to interrogate the sun.

"I don't have many memories worth keeping," they said at last, and the flatness of it hurt worse than any accusation.

"You have more than you know," I said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

We climbed through the night.

The mountain permitted it — I felt its attention settle over us like snowfall somewhere past the first switchback, vast and unhurried, weighing us. Sable felt it too. I saw them stumble once, not from the path but from the pressure of being *noticed*, and I caught their arm before pride could make them refuse.

"It's reading us," they whispered.

"It's remembering us. There's a difference. To be read is to be opened. To be remembered—" I searched for the words in the human tongue, which has never had enough of them. "To be remembered is to be kept. Even the parts we'd rather discard."

"That sounds less comforting than you think it does."

"It isn't meant to comfort. Nothing true is."

The gold veins brightened as we ascended, until by midnight we walked in a light with no source, warm as lamplight, and the cold of the high passes fell away below us. Frost flowers bloomed on the stone and melted as we passed, opening and closing like slow eyes. Once, deep in the rock, something sang — a single low note that lasted a quarter hour and was not a sound so much as a *fact*, vibrating up through our boots into our bones.

Sable stopped walking. "I know that note."

"No one knows that note. It predates knowing."

"I know it anyway." Their hand drifted to the sigil cloth they still carried, folded now into an inner pocket, worn soft as mothwing. "It's the lullaby. The one the buyer bought off me, back in the valley. The one I didn't recognize but somehow knew." They turned to me, and in the mountain's gold light their face looked younger and older at once. "Corvane. Why does a mountain sing my mother's song?"

I had no answer that wasn't a wound, so I gave none, and we climbed on.

* * *

The Hollow opened without warning.

One moment we were in a corridor of stone barely wide enough for two; the next, the walls simply agreed to be elsewhere, and we stood in a chamber the size of a cathedral, domed and ribbed with those golden veins, all of them converging on a single point at the center of the floor — a basin of dark glassy stone, wide as a millpond, utterly still.

The air tasted of ozone and honey. Old magic, covenant magic, the kind that had been spoken rather than found.

"This is the Hollow," I said, though Sable hadn't asked. "The stone sleeps there. At the bottom of the basin. But you can't take it by reaching. The mountain decides who leaves with it, and it decides by showing you why it matters." I hesitated. "It will show you the forging. All of it. And it will ask a tithe."

"A memory. Freely given." Sable circled the basin slowly, and I watched their reflection move across its surface — except the reflection lagged, half a heartbeat behind, and once it turned its head when Sable did not. "What did you give it? Last time."

I had hoped they wouldn't ask. "The day I was knighted," I said. "My whole memory of it. The oaths, the queen's voice, the pride. I know it happened. I know what it meant. But it's gone, and what remains is only the shape of it, like a footprint after the rain." I met their eyes. "That is the price. Now you understand why I warned you to choose carefully."

"What would you give now?" they asked softly. "If you had to climb again?"

"Nothing," I said. "I have nothing left I'm willing to spend. Which is perhaps the most honest thing about me."

Sable laughed — short, startled, real. Then they knelt at the basin's edge, laid both palms flat against the dark glass, and said, in the tone of someone stepping off a cliff:

"Show me."

* * *

The mountain took hold of us gently, the way sleep takes hold.

The Hollow dissolved. We stood instead on a hillside under a sky with two moons in it — one silver, one green — and I knew immediately that we were not in any place but in a *remembered* place, rendered by the mountain's perfect, merciless fidelity. The grass bent in a wind I could feel on my face. Somewhere below, a river ran with water the color of hammered tin.

And between the hillside and the river, the worlds touched.

There was no other way to describe it. The air simply parted, a seam of light hanging vertical above the meadow, and through it came figures out of the Otherworld — tall ones, crowned ones, things with antlers of living wood and eyes like eclipses. And facing them, a line of humans in grey and amber, led by a woman whose face—

Whose face I knew.

"Maren," I breathed, before I could stop myself. Maren Amberdale, the First, matriarch of the line, dead eleven centuries and rendered here in flesh and wind and the small impatient gesture she made when she pushed her hair back — a gesture I had seen a thousand times over, because her descendants all made it, because blood is a long echo.

"She looks like—" Sable started.

"You," I said. "She looks like you. Or you look like her. The mountain doesn't care about the order of things."

Below us, the forging began.

I had witnessed this once before, a hundred and sixty years ago, and I had thought myself prepared. I was wrong. Time had not dulled it; it had sharpened it, the way a whetstone sharpens grief. We watched Maren Amberdale step forward alone into the space between the hosts, and we heard her speak the Oath of the Door — not a wall, never a wall, the first words out of her mouth were a refusal of walls — *Let there be a door between us, and let the door have obligations on both sides* — and the seam of light blazed, and the two moons leaned toward each other across the sky like lovers, and the first covenant-stone condensed out of the air itself, molten and singing, into Maren's cupped hands.

Sable made a sound beside me. Small. Involuntary.

"They're not sealing the fae out," they whispered. "Everyone in the valley said the Veil was built to keep them away. Teachers. Priests. Everyone."

"It was never a wall," I said. "It was a treaty. Walls are what people build when treaties die." I did not look at Sable when I said it. Some truths are kinder delivered sideways.

We watched the second stone pass to a fae lord whose name the mountain spoke aloud in a voice like distant bells — Vaelorin, Warden of the Eastern Threshold — and the third stone, the binding-stone, the keystone, Maren raised over her head and gave to the Veil itself, and the light swallowed it, and the seam settled into its long luminous quiet, and the hosts bowed to one another, and it was done.

Eleven centuries of peace, forged in an afternoon, on a hillside with two moons.

And then the mountain, merciless keeper that it was, did not release us. It held the scene, and the scene *changed* — the peace rippling forward like stones dropped in water, generation after generation flickering past, Amberdales renewing the oath at each eclipse, each renewal briefer, more ritual, less belief, until—

Until the last one.

The hillside again. The same grass, the same river, but only one moon now, and the seam of light torn and guttering like a candle in a draft. And among the handful of witnesses gathered to attempt a renewal — a crowd of perhaps thirty, their faces tight with fear —

Her.

Lyra Amberdale. Eight months pregnant, one hand braced against the swell of her belly, the other clutching a fold of the sigil cloth. She stood at the edge of the witnesses, not among them, and her face—

Gods, her face.

I had loved that face. I had memorized it in a hundred stolen moments and then obeyed her final order to forget it, and I had failed at the forgetting for twenty years, and now the mountain handed it back to me in perfect detail and I could not breathe. She was terrified. Not of the tearing Veil, not of the failing oath, not of the crown's riders I knew were already three days behind her. She was terrified the way only a mother is terrified — wholly, specifically, with every part of her aimed at one small unseen thing.

She was terrified *for the child she carried.*

Beside me, Sable had gone very still. Too still. The stillness of prey that has just understood the shape of the trap it was born in.

"That's her," Sable said. Flat. Barely voiced. "That's my mother."

"Yes."

"She's *here*. At the renewal. She's one of the witnesses." Sable's voice cracked down the middle. "Why would she come to watch the covenant break? Why would she—"

And then the mountain answered, because the mountain answers everything, and showed us.

Lyra stepped forward. Out of the circle of witnesses, out of the safety of the crowd, forward into the open ground where the torn seam hissed and spat light. One of the elder Amberdales shouted at her to come back. She did not turn. She pressed both hands to her belly, and she looked up at the failing Veil, and she spoke — not the Oath of the Door, not the formal words, but something quieter, something meant for no ears but the mountain's and the Veil's and whatever power lived in the space between worlds.

The mountain gave us her voice. Of course it did. It kept everything.

*"I know what's coming,"* Lyra Amberdale said, to a sky that was coming apart. *"I know what they'll do to a child born of both doors. So I'm asking you — whatever is left of the covenant, whatever is left of the bargain my family kept for a thousand years — hide my child. Take what must be taken. Keep what must be kept. And when the time comes, give it back."*

The seam flickered. And in the flicker, I saw it — saw what I had not been present to see, what I had been ordered to look away from and had obediently, damnably, looked away from: the third stone, the keystone, the binding-stone given to the Veil eleven centuries ago, answering her.

Not refusing her. *Answering.*

Light poured down from the tear like rain falling upward, and it sank into Lyra, into the child she carried, and when it faded she was weeping and steadier than she had been, and the cloth in her hand glowed faintly with a sigil that had not been there before.

The vision held there. Held on her face. Held on the tears cutting clean lines through the dust of flight, on the terrible resolve, on the way her lips kept moving after the words were done — shaping, over and over, soundlessly, a word the mountain did not need to give us because we both read it plainly on her mouth.

*Sable. Sable. Sable.*

Then the mountain let go, and the Hollow rushed back around us — the dome, the gold veins, the dark glass basin — and Sable was on their knees at its edge, shaking, and I knelt beside them because there was nowhere else in either world I could have been.

"She traded," Sable said. Their voice came from somewhere very far away. "The third stone. The keystone. She didn't lose it, didn't hide it in some vault — she gave it to the Veil, and the Veil put it in *me*. My childhood. My memories. They're not missing, they're not stolen, they're—" Their breath hitched. "They're collateral. Held in trust. By the sky."

"Yes," I said, because there was nothing truer or crueler to say.

"And the eclipse." Sable lifted their head, and their eyes were wet and blazing and more awake than I had ever seen them. "When the eclipse comes and the Veil tries to seal forever — it won't just close the door. It will keep me. Whatever I am, whatever she hid inside me, it gets locked away with the rest of it. Forever." They laughed, and it was the worst sound I had ever heard. "I'm not hunting the stones, Corvane. The stones were always hunting *me*."

Before I could answer, the basin moved.

The dark glass stirred like a pond disturbed by a thrown stone, and up from its depths, slow as a rising moon, came the second covenant-stone — molten-gold, veined with silver, humming the lullaby note that Sable had known without knowing. It rose until it hung a hand's width above the surface, turning, waiting.

The mountain had decided.

And then, from far below us — down through the living rock, up through miles of stone that breathed — the mountain shuddered. The gold veins flickered. Frost bloomed across the Hollow's walls in patterns I recognized, patterns I had seen once before in a pawnshop in the Veilless Valley, spreading inward from the world outside.

Someone was climbing the mountain. Someone the mountain did not want, and could not stop.

Sable reached out and closed their hand around the stone, and it did not burn them, and outside, faint but unmistakable, drifting up through the warm dark rock, came the sound of horns.
