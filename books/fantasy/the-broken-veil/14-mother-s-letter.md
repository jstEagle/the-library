## 14. Mother's Letter

The mountain did not let us go gently.

When the echo of the forging released us, we were no longer standing in the great hollowed chamber where the ancestors had sworn their oath. The stone had carried us deeper — down a throat of smooth, warm rock that pulsed faintly under Corvane's palm like the flank of a sleeping animal — and set us down in a space so small it might have been a heart.

It was round, perhaps ten paces across, and lit from nowhere. The light simply *was*, amber-gold and sourceless, pooling on the floor like honey poured too thick. And in the center of that pool stood a figure made of memory.

She was slighter than I had imagined. I hadn't known I'd imagined her at all until I saw her — but there she was, rendered in shimmering half-light: a woman with dark hair braided back from a face that was already turning toward me as though she'd been waiting thirty years for this exact moment.

I could not move. I don't mean fear. I mean the mountain held me, gentle as a hand on my shoulder, and would not permit me to look away.

"Sable." Her voice was not an echo. It was *present*. Warm, low, threaded with an accent I didn't know and somehow recognized anyway — the rounded vowels of the Otherworld's eastern courts. "If you're hearing this, then you found the second stone, which means you survived the valley, which means you are more stubborn than I ever managed to make you in the short time I had you."

A sound came out of me. Not a word. Something rawer.

Corvane shifted at my side. He had gone very still, the way he went still before battles, and when I glanced at him his face was gray beneath its bronze sheen. His eyes were fixed on the woman with an expression I had never seen him wear — not guilt exactly, and not grief, but some terrible alloy of both, smelted together until you couldn't tell one from the other.

He knew her. Of course he knew her. He had told me as much by the fire, in his careful, halting confession — that he had loved my mother, that he had obeyed her final order to look away while the covenant broke. But knowing it and seeing it were different things. Seeing her now, I understood that every careful word he'd spoken around her name had been a man walking on a frozen lake, testing each step.

"Listen to me," she said, and the warmth in her voice cooled into something harder, something urgent. "You will have questions. You deserve answers, and I am sorry — I am so sorry — that they come to you this way instead of from my own mouth. But if you're hearing this, I'm long past speaking, and the choices I made can't be unmade. Only understood. So understand them."

* * *

"I broke the covenant."

She said it plainly, without flinching, and the words landed in the small chamber like stones dropped into still water.

"Not carelessly. Not in anger. Every story you've been told — by fae courts, by human kings, by whatever disgraced knight is standing beside you looking like he wants the floor to swallow him — every version leaves out the only part that matters. I broke it *for you*."

My breath caught. Beside me, Corvane whispered, "Elowen," — her name, her actual name, the first time I'd heard it — and the memory-figure did not turn to him. She had recorded this for one listener only.

"You were born under a conjunction that comes once in a thousand years," she continued. "Half of the blood that forged the covenant, half of the realm it bound. Do you understand what that makes you? Not a bridge. Bridges are walked over. You are a *keystone* — pull you out and both worlds come down; hold you up and both worlds stand. There has never been anyone like you, Sable. There may never be again."

"And the crown of Aldermarch learned what you were before you could walk."

Her image flickered — anger, real anger, bending the memory like heat over coals.

"They sent men. Polite men, with polite letters, offering to 'foster' you at court. I knew what fostering meant. I had seen what their mages did to gifted children — I had *seen it*, in the first years after the Veil began to fray, when the crown started collecting anyone who could touch magic and calling it protection. They wanted you because a child who belongs to both realms could open the Veil like a door, or close it like a tomb, or hold it hostage forever. You weren't a person to them. You were a weapon that hadn't been forged yet. The perfect weapon. And they would have hollowed you out and filled you with their purposes, the way they hollow out every gifted soul they take for 'examination.'"

The word *examination* curdled in the air between us. I thought of Wren Vael — of her empty, hungry eyes, of what she'd told me about what the crown had done to her as a child — and felt the ground tilt beneath everything I believed about who my enemy was.

"So I chose," my mother said. "The covenant required an Amberdale to maintain it — to stand at the seam each season and pour their own memory into the binding, year upon year, generation upon generation. That was the price our family paid, and paid, and paid, until the price was all we were. If I kept the covenant, I would have to bring you to the seam. I would have to show the crown exactly where you were, every season, like ringing a bell over your head. Or I would have to leave you with kin who would trade you within a decade for a pardon and a title. I knew my family, love. I loved them, and I knew them."

"But if the covenant *broke* — if the Veil frayed into leaks and whispers instead of standing as a wall — then the Amberdales stopped mattering. The hunt would turn to the Veil itself, to sealing and weaponizing it, and a child hidden in the one place magic cannot follow would be forgotten. Dead to both realms. Safe."

She laughed then, short and bitter, and the sound cracked something open in my chest.

"It worked. Gods forgive me, it worked. The crown forgot you. The courts forgot you. Everyone hunted the fracture and no one hunted the child. I bought your life with the sky itself — with every leak that lets a hungry thing slip through, with every village that paid for my choice in nightmares. I have never once regretted it and I have never once slept well. Both things are true. Hold onto that, when you hate me. Both things are true."

* * *

I was shaking. Corvane's hand found my shoulder — hesitated — and stayed, warm and steady, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the woman made of light wasn't already saying better.

"There is one more thing," she said, and her voice gentled into something that undid me far worse than the anger had. "The last gift. The hardest one."

"The crown's mages had a way of finding dual-blooded children — not through the parents, but through the child's own power. A keystone child's magic sings in both realms at once, Sable. It can't be hidden, only muted. And the only way to mute it..." She pressed her lips together. "Was to take away the part of you that knew how to use it. Your earliest memories. The forging-ritual you learned in the cradle, before you could talk — yes, you learned it in the cradle, our children always do, it's in the blood like birdsong. Your father's laugh, which was the anchor-note of your fae half. My voice singing the binding-verse, which anchored your human half. Take those away, and your power had no roots. It went dormant. It became... small. Strange. A thief's gift instead of a keystone's."

*A thief's gift.* I thought of every memory I had ever stolen — the sea, the lullabies, strangers' weddings and griefs and ordinary Tuesdays — and understood, all at once, that I had spent my whole life scavenging scraps at the edge of a feast that lived inside my own skull.

"I gave them to the third stone," she said. "Your infancy. Your first three years. Every moment of your father, of me, of the ritual, of what you are. I bargained with the Veil itself — it took them into its keeping at the central tear, sealed in covenant-stone, untouchable by crown or court or time. As long as those memories stayed given, you stayed hidden. You stayed *small*. You stayed alive."

Her image stepped closer, close enough that I could see the fine lines at her eyes, the exhaustion carved there by years of watching the horizon for riders.

"And here is the cruelty of it, my love, and I won't soften it: taking them back will hurt. It will hurt more than anything the world has yet done to you. You'll grieve years you don't remember losing. You'll meet yourself — the self I buried to save — and you may not like them. You may not like *me*. Fury and love live in the same house, Sable. In my experience they've never once been apart."

"But hear me. Hear the only instruction I will ever give you." She raised her hand, and pressed it flat against the air as though against glass, as though against my cheek. "The eclipse is coming. The crown means to seal the Veil — or worse, to weaponize it, and if they do, both realms drown. The covenant can be reforged, but not by an oathbreaker's daughter hiding behind stolen memories. It can only be reforged by *you*. Whole. Both-blooded. Rooted. The third stone isn't a treasure, Sable. It's a mirror. When you find it — and you will find it, because you are my child and stubbornness is the one inheritance I made sure you got —"

Her voice caught. The memory flickered, and for one terrible instant I saw her not as the composed woman delivering a message but as a mother recording a goodbye she knew might be heard by a child who hated her.

"— when you find it, choose. Choose to be both. Choose to belong nowhere fully and everywhere entirely. It will cost you every easy answer you've ever had. I know, because it cost me the same, and I never got to learn whether it was worth it."

"You will. Make it worth it for both of us."

The light dimmed. Her hand, still pressed to the invisible glass, began to fade from the fingertips inward.

"One last thing," she said quickly, urgently, the words tumbling as the memory unraveled. "Corvane — I know you're there. I know you, old friend; you'd sooner die than leave an Amberdale alone in a mountain. Look after them. And forgive yourself. I ordered you to look away because I needed someone I trusted to carry the guilt I couldn't afford to carry myself. That was cruel. It was also necessary. Put it down, Corvane. You've carried it long enough."

His breath left him in a broken rush, and I reached for his arm without thinking, holding on.

"Goodbye, my heart," my mother said. "Find the stone. Find yourself. Come home to both worlds."

And she was gone — not fading like smoke but folding inward, like a letter being closed, the amber light gathering itself up into a single point that sank into the floor and left us standing in the dark.

* * *

For a long time, neither of us spoke.

The mountain breathed around us, slow and vast. Somewhere above, water ticked through stone. I became aware, distantly, that my cheeks were wet, and that my hands had curled into fists so tight my nails had drawn blood from my palms, and that I couldn't remember starting either.

"She lied to us," I said finally. My voice came out scraped. "The Thorn Court. They said the Amberdales broke the oath out of pride. Out of treachery. They sentenced me to death for it."

"She let them believe it." Corvane's voice was hoarse. "Because a hunted traitor's child is forgotten, and a martyred heroine's child is *found*. Elowen wore the villainy like armor so it would keep hitting her instead of you." He exhaled slowly. "I watched her make that choice. I watched her walk into the Court of Thorns and confess to a breaking she engineered, and I watched the queen sentence her in absentia, and I said nothing, because she ordered me to say nothing, and I have spent fifteen years telling myself obedience was loyalty—"

"Stop." I turned to face him. In the dark, his eyes caught what little light remained, twin embers banked low. "She just told you to put it down. Are you going to disobey her twice?"

Something moved through his face — pain, and then, unbelievably, the ghost of a laugh. "You have her temper."

"I have *her*," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "That's the problem. I have all of her — the fury and the love and the terrible arithmetic, trading the sky for one child, and I don't—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "I don't know whether to curse her or thank her. I've been starving my whole life, Corvane. Stealing other people's memories because mine began at four years old with a doorstep and a stranger. And it turns out I did it to myself. She did it *for* me. The hunger, the gaps, the — the *smallness*. All of it was a door she locked from the outside to keep wolves away, and I've been banging on it from the inside for twenty-five years without knowing the lock was a kindness."

"And now?"

I lowered my hands. The dark felt different now — not empty but expectant, the way a held breath feels.

"Now I know where the door is." I looked up at him. "The third stone. The central tear. My own infancy, kept by the Veil itself. She said taking it back would hurt more than anything the world has done to me." I laughed, and it came out unsteady. "Vael ripped a decade out of me on a roadside and I survived it. What's three more years, if they're *mine*?"

Corvane studied me for a long moment. Then he inclined his head — the formal bow of a knight to his liege, the first time he had ever given it to me, and I understood that somewhere in the dark of that little chamber, something between us had changed rank forever.

"The eclipse is in two days," he said quietly. "The tear lies east, past the Thornwood, through country the crown holds. And Sable — the army that marched from Aldermarch will not be far behind us. If what your mother said is true, they don't mean to seal the Veil at all."

"Then we'd better get there first."

We climbed. The mountain let us go the way it had held us — gently, reluctantly, a parent releasing a child's hand at a schoolhouse door. And as we emerged into the grey morning light on its southern slope, the wind carrying the smell of pine and distant rain, I felt them for the first time: the memories waiting behind the locked door, pressing against it, patient as seeds.

Somewhere far to the east, thunder rolled — though the sky held no clouds.

Corvane went rigid beside me, head tilting, ears catching something on the wind that I could not.

"What is it?" I asked.

"That's not thunder," he said grimly, already turning east. "That's the Veil. Tearing faster. Sable — we have less time than your mother thought. Run."
