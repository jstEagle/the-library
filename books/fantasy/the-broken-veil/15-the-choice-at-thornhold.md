## 15. The Choice at Thornhold

*Corvane*

The mountain began to scream an hour before the inquisitors arrived.

Not with sound — with memory. The stone passages that had sung the covenant's forging to them now shuddered and went dark, one vein of light at a time, as though something were drinking the mountain from the outside in. Corvane felt it through his boots first, then through the old oath-scar on his palm, which had ached every day since the Veil broke and now burned like a brand.

"They're here," he said.

Sable stood at the heart-chamber's threshold, the second covenant-stone cradled against their chest. It was a shard of black glass shot through with veins of gold, warm enough that Sable's fingers had gone pink where they gripped it. They looked up at him with the expression Corvane had learned to dread — not fear, but calculation. The look of someone deciding what they could afford to lose.

"There's another way out," Corvane said. "The deep galleries. We could be in the lowlands by dawn."

"And Vael takes the stone anyway, and we've lost our only head start." Sable turned the shard over. "No. She wants to talk. She's been wanting to talk since the fen. Let her."

"She hollowed a decade out of your skull, Sable. That was not an overture."

"It was proof she could." Sable's jaw tightened. "She didn't take the journey memories. She left you. Think about what that means."

Corvane thought about it, and did not like the answer his mind offered: *she left me because she wanted you to have something left to lose.*

The mountain's last lights died. In the dark, torchlight bloomed at the far end of the great gallery — a dozen flames moving in perfect unison, too perfect, the march of people who no longer wasted motion on anything so human as rhythm. And ahead of them, small and straight-backed, walked Wren Vael.

Corvane drew his blade. It was fae-forged, older than the crown of Aldermarch, and it sang a thin, hungry note in the dead air. "Behind me," he said.

"No," said Sable, and stepped past him into the open floor of the chamber.

*Sable*

The heart-chamber of the remembering mountain was a dome of veined stone, and in its center the echo of the forging still played on a loop too faint to hear — the ghost of an oath, repeating itself to an empty room for a hundred years. Sable felt it against their skin like warmth off a hearth. Their mother's face was somewhere in that echo. They had seen her there, pregnant and terrified, and they were still carrying the weight of it like a stone behind the ribs.

Vael stopped ten paces away. Her inquisitors fanned out along the walls, and their torches threw shadows that moved wrong — lagging half a heartbeat behind their owners, as if even the shadows weren't sure they belonged to anything anymore.

"You're holding it wrong," Vael said.

Sable blinked. "What?"

"The stone. Cradling it like a baby. It's not a baby. It's a key." Her voice was flat as slate, but her eyes — those eyes with nothing reflected in them, not torchlight, not Sable's own face — tracked the shard with something that in anyone else might have been hunger, or grief. "You hold a key like you mean to use it. Otherwise everyone in the room knows you're bluffing."

"Is that what they taught you in the examination cells?"

Something flickered across Vael's face. Gone in an instant, but it had been there. "I taught myself," she said. "They only supplied the material." She extended one gloved hand. "Give it to me, and I'll let you walk. You have my word."

"Your word isn't worth the air it rides on."

"No," Vael agreed. "But my restraint is. I could have taken this from your corpse at the drowned chapel. I could have taken it while you slept three nights ago, when your knight was on watch and dreaming — yes, fae dream, whatever he told you. I didn't. Ask yourself why, Amberdale."

The name landed like a blow. Sable hadn't given it to her. No one living should have known it.

"You've been reading my stolen memories," Sable said quietly.

"I've been reading everything the crown ever collected on the Amberdales. Which is more than you know about yourself, I'd wager." Vael took a step closer. Behind her, the inquisitors did not move; they never moved unless she moved first, like puppets waiting on a string only she could feel. "Here is what happens if you refuse. I take the stone. I take whatever else of yours I need to make you tractable. And then I deliver both to the Lord Inquisitor, who will use them exactly as the crown intends — and you already know what the crown intends, because you saw the siege engines massing at Greywater Ford. Sealing the Veil was never the plan. Owning the tear is."

"I know," Sable said. "Why do you think we're running?"

"Running isn't the same as stopping them." Vael's hand was still out. "You can't outrun an army, heir. You can't out-thieve a state. Every road ahead of you ends at the eclipse with the Veil in the crown's fist and your name on a list. Unless—" and here her voice changed, dropped its slate flatness for something almost curious, "—unless you have something better to trade than your surrender."

Sable's heart was going hard. Beside them, Corvane had shifted his weight forward onto the balls of his feet, blade low, coiled. One word from Sable and he would spend himself against twelve inquisitors, and it would buy perhaps ninety seconds.

"What could I possibly have," Sable said, "that you want?"

"The favor of the Thorn Court."

The words came out of Sable before they had decided to say them — and yet some part of them, some part that had been turning the problem over all through the descent from the mountain, had clearly decided long ago. Vael went very still. Even her shadows stopped lagging.

"You know about that," Sable said.

"I know the queen of thorns owes the Amberdale line a debt of one favor, no more, redeemable by the blood-heir. I know because I was sent to find out." A pause. "I also know what a favor from the Thorn Court is worth. It is the only currency in either realm the crown cannot mint, cannot counterfeit, and cannot survive being spent against it."

"You want us to ask the fae queen to free you." Corvane's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip. "That's madness. That debt is ours. It's the only shield Sable has — the only reason they're not dead already—"

"It's a shield that stops one sword," Sable said, not looking away from Vael. "And there are ten thousand swords coming. Corvane. Look at her."

He looked. Sable watched him look — watched him see what Sable had seen in the fen, in the border town, in every encounter since: a woman built out of absence, wearing a crown's uniform like a wound that had been dressed so many times it had become the body itself.

"She was a child," Sable said. "They took everything she was and made it into a tool pointed wherever they point it. And she's standing here telling us she'd rather not be pointed anymore. That's not an offer we get twice."

"She hollowed you."

"Yes." Sable touched their own temple, where the gaps still lived — whole seasons gone soft-edged and blank, skills they'd had to relearn, a friend's face they knew they'd loved and could no longer summon. The anger was still there. It would always be there. But anger was a fuel, not a compass. "And I'm not going to become her by doing to her what the crown did. Somebody has to stop the ledger somewhere, or it just keeps writing itself."

The echo of the forging murmured overhead, that endless ghost-oath cycling through its hundredth year of repetition. *Bind and be bound. Bind and be bound.* Their ancestors' voices. Their mother among the witnesses. Sable thought: *this is what the oath actually meant. Not walls. Obligations. Even to the person who cut a decade out of you. Especially then.*

"Sable," Corvane said, low, urgent, using their name like a hand on their arm. "If you spend this and she betrays us—"

"Then I'll have been wrong," Sable said, "the way Mother was wrong about trusting no one. At least I'll have been wrong generously."

They turned back to Vael. "Here are the terms. Not surrender. Trade. We redeem the Thorn Court's favor, and we spend it on you — your freedom, your severance from the crown's control, whatever form the queen's binding takes. In exchange, you give us back the second stone, you give back what you took from me—" Vael's mouth opened, and Sable raised a hand, "—or as much of it as can be returned, I'm not a fool, I know some things don't come back. And you stand with us at the tear. Not for the crown. For yourself. For every kid like the one you used to be, who'll grow up under the new covenant if we live to write one."

Silence. The torches hissed. Somewhere far above, wind moved over the mountain's mouth and made a sound like a held breath.

"You understand what you're offering," Vael said at last, and her voice had lost its slate entirely; it was quiet, and careful, and almost young. "A favor from the Thorn Court, spent on me. Do you know what they do to humans who owe them? What they'd be entitled to demand of *you*, when you have nothing left to pay with? You'd be naked in front of two courts and a crown, with nothing but a stone and a disgraced knight."

"I've been poor my whole life," Sable said. "I know how to be naked. What I don't know how to do is build a future out of leverage I was too scared to spend."

Vael looked at them for a long moment. Then she did something none of her inquisitors did, something that made them shift for the first time, unsettled by their master's motion: she laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, like a hinge unused for years.

"When they examined me," she said, "they asked me questions for eleven days. Memory after memory, peeled and weighed and catalogued. And on the eleventh day the examiner leaned close and said, *what do you want, little wren?* And I couldn't answer. There was nothing left in me that wanted. They'd taken the wanting with everything else." She pulled off her glove. Her bare hand was pale as paper, and across the knuckles ran a thin scar in the shape of the crown's sigil — the brand they gave the ones who survived processing. "It took me twenty years to grow a want back. Do you know what it was? The first thing?"

Sable shook their head.

"To watch the Lord Inquisitor's face when the thing he built turns around and looks at him." She closed her fist. "Your offer is better than that. Barely. But better." She opened her hand again, palm up, and in it lay the second covenant-stone — she must have lifted it from Sable's grip without their feeling a thing, back when the conversation started, or perhaps she'd had it since before the torches entered the mountain, and this entire parley had been theater staged to give her a reason to hand it back. "Take it. We ride for the Thorn Court at first light. And heir — when you speak the redemption, choose your words like your life depends on them. With the thorns, it does."

Sable took the stone. It was warm, and heavier than it had been, or perhaps Sable was simply lighter.

Behind them, Corvane sheathed his blade with a sound like a door closing on an argument he intended to resume later. "This is either the wisest thing you've ever done," he muttered, "or the last foolish thing, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"That makes two of us," Sable said.

Above them, through the mountain's throat, the sky was beginning to bruise toward evening — and in the west, low on the horizon, the sun already carried a faint dark bite out of its edge. Three days to eclipse. Maybe less.

*Corvane*

They camped that night in the mountain's lee, and Corvane did not sleep. He sat with his back to the fire and watched Wren Vael sit apart from her inquisitors — who waited, motionless, for orders that did not come, abandoned mid-spell like instruments whose player had set them down. She was dismantling something in her hands. When the moon rose high enough, he saw what it was: the crown's sigil-badge, torn from her collar, its pin bent back and forth until it hung by a thread of metal.

She caught him watching. She did not put it away.

"You think I'll betray them," she said. Not a question.

"I think you'll do what you've always done," Corvane answered. "Survive by the shortest path. I've known knights like that. Some of them were the best I ever served beside. Some of them got everyone killed."

"And which am I?"

"Undecided," Corvane said. "Ask me at the eclipse."

Almost — almost — her mouth curved. Then it flattened, and she looked down at the broken badge, and Corvane saw her thumb move once across the crown's sigil the way a person touches a scar they've stopped hating and started merely reading.

"He'll come for me personally," she said. "The Lord Inquisitor. When he learns what I've done. He doesn't send others for things like this. He likes to look at you while it happens." She snapped the badge in two and dropped the halves into the fire, where the gilt flared green and was gone. "I need you to know that, knight. Whatever you've decided about me. I need you to know what's coming, so you can't say afterward that I hid it."

"We knew the army was coming anyway."

"Not the army." Vael looked up, and for the first time since the fen, Corvane saw reflections in her eyes — small ones, just the fire, but there, trembling, alive. "Him. He was the one who designed the examinations. He was the one who held my head while they poured. And he has spent thirty years making certain that nothing in either realm can surprise him." She fed another stick to the flames. "You're about to surprise him. I'd start deciding now how much of yourselves you're willing to burn to do it."

Across the fire, wrapped in a threadbare blanket with the black-gold stone glowing faintly against their sternum like a second heartbeat, Sable slept the dreamless sleep of the spent. Corvane watched them breathe, and counted the cost of the day's bargain, and found — to his considerable irritation — that he could not make the arithmetic come out against it.

Mercy, spent like coin. It was the most Amberdale thing he had ever witnessed. It was, he suspected, exactly what the covenant had been for, before his generation broke it.

Far to the north, beyond the mountains, beyond the Veil's fraying edge, something answered the dying fire with a whisper of its own — and every hair on Corvane's arms rose, because he recognized the voice.

The Otherworld was calling its stones home.
