## 16. Eclipse Eve

The tear in the world did not look like a wound. It looked like a held breath.

Wren Vael stood at the edge of the Hollowmere and felt it through the hollow places inside her — that vast, patient pressure, like a lung about to exhale after a hundred years of holding. The Veil's central tear spread across the fen before her, a seam of wrongness stitched through the air itself. Where it ran, the reeds grew silver instead of green. Where it pooled, the water hung motionless as glass, reflecting a sky that was not quite the sky above them — a sky with two moons where there should be one, a constellation shaped like a crown no astronomer had ever named.

And beneath it all, whispering.

The dead did not rest here. They drifted along the tear like leaves caught in an eddy, their voices thin as paper, saying things half-remembered: names, apologies, the last words of people who had died with something still caught between their teeth. Vael had heard the dead before. The crown's examination chambers were full of them, pressed into the walls by generations of stolen magic. But these whispers were different. These were *waiting*.

She counted the hours without meaning to. It was a habit the examinations had carved into her — she measured everything now, every kindness and cruelty, in units of time remaining. Four hours until the eclipse began. Perhaps five until totality, when the moon would slide across the sun like a lid closing over an eye, and whatever was done at the tear would be done forever.

Behind her, footsteps on the peat. Two sets. One light and human, one with the particular weighted grace of fae feet that never quite disturbed what they crossed.

"They're here," Corvane said, though of course Vael had already known. She always knew. It was one of the few gifts the examinations had left her — not sight, exactly, but a sensitivity to the shape of minds nearby, the way a blind man learns to feel weather on his skin.

Sable Amberdale stopped three paces away. Vael did not turn. She watched the tear instead, and let them come to the edge of her silence.

"You came," Sable said. There was wariness in it, and under the wariness, exhaustion. Good. Exhausted people were honest people.

"You asked." Vael finally turned. In the tear's strange light, the young heir looked older than they had in the border town, older than they had kneeling in the drowned chapel mud. The stolen decade Vael had ripped from them was growing back — not as memory, but as something else. Calluses over the gaps. "I said I would meet you at the Hollowmere at dusk. I keep my bargains. It is the only part of me the crown never managed to take."

Corvane's hand rested near his blade, the way it always did around her. She didn't blame him. A memory-eater who kept her word was more frightening than one who didn't; it meant the word was worth something, which meant taking it from her would cost.

"The second stone," Sable said. "You have it."

"I have it." Vael reached into her coat and drew it out — a shard of pale stone no larger than a knucklebone, humming faintly, its surface crawling with light like oil on water. She turned it in her fingers. Even this close to the tear, it pulled at her hollows, offering to fill them. That was the trap of covenant-stones. They gave you back everything you'd lost, and then they owned you. "And I am going to give it to you. But first, you are going to listen. Because I did not spend fifteen years as the crown's hound to hand the last Amberdale a lie."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a leaning menhir, one of the old boundary stones that predated even the covenant, its carvings worn to suggestions. Vael built no fire. Firelight near the tear attracted things — she had seen what came sliding out of the thin places when warmth called to them, and she had no wish to introduce Sable and Corvane to her nightmares before dawn.

Instead she sat across from them in the grey-green gloaming and told them the truth, because there was no longer time for anything else.

"When I was nine years old," she said, "the crown's examiners came to my village in the Marchlands. I could hear what people meant instead of what they said. My mother thought it was a blessing. She told the census-taker herself." Vael kept her voice level. It was easier than it used to be; the story had been sanded smooth by repetition, first as testimony, then as recruitment speech, and now, finally, as confession. "They took me to Thornhold. There is a chamber beneath it — you've seen the mountain that remembers, so you know the old magic. Well, Thornhold has the opposite. A mountain that *forgets*. They put me in it for six years. They fed my gift into their engines until there was nothing left in me but the shape of taking. And when I came out, I was very useful, and very obedient, and I believed — truly believed — that I had been saved. That the examination had cut the rot out of me and left a servant of the realm."

Sable's face had gone still. Vael recognized the expression. It was the face of someone looking into a mirror and seeing a possible self — the self that would have existed if the valley hadn't hidden them, if the pawnshop keeper hadn't burned the papers, if the crown's riders had reached them at nine years old instead of twenty-odd.

"You know what the Gifted Purge is for," Vael continued. "You've been running from it. You think it is fear. Panic. A king who heard the Veil was tearing and decided to blame the gifted for the tearing." She shook her head slowly. "I thought so too, once. I told myself that story for years, because the alternative was unbearable. But I have access to the sealed correspondence. I have stood in rooms where the Privy Council speaks plainly because they believe nothing in the room can be stolen from them." Her mouth curved, and it was not a kind curve. "They are not afraid of the Veil falling, Sable Amberdale. They are *counting on it*."

The whispering of the dead seemed to rise, just slightly, as if the tear itself were listening.

"The old covenant was a wall," Vael said. "A wall with obligations on both sides — that was its genius, and its flaw. Humans resented paying tribute to the fae. The fae chafed at being bound to honor human oaths. For three hundred years, both courts looked for a way out. And then your mother broke it, and the Veil began to leak, and the crown discovered something extraordinary: that a leaking Veil could be *harvested*. The magic that bleeds through doesn't vanish. It pools. It can be drawn, stored, refined. Thornhold has been sitting on reservoirs of it for a generation. Enough raw power to do anything. To win any war. To make Aldermarch the center of the world — if only the leaks could be controlled rather than sealed."

Corvane had gone very still, in the way of fae who have just understood the shape of a betrayal older than their exile. "The eclipse," he said quietly.

"The eclipse," Vael agreed. "Totality opens the tear wide. For a matter of minutes, the Veil will not be a wall or a leak but a *door*, standing open, unguarded. The crown's plan — the plan I helped build, thinking I was building a seal — is to drive the siege engine into the gap. They call it the Loom. It will catch the Veil's threads as the eclipse pulls them taut, and instead of letting the covenant reknit naturally, it will *spin* them. Anchor one end in Thornhold's reservoirs, run the other end through the tear, and hold both realms on a spindle. Every act of magic in either world, flowing through Aldermarch's hands. Taxed. Rationed. Owned."

Sable's voice came out low. "That would kill the Otherworld."

"It would kill both worlds, eventually," Vael said. "Magic isn't a river you can dam. It's blood. You can pump it through a machine for a while, and the machine grows strong, and everything else grows pale. I've seen the projections. In fifty years, the Otherworld is a desert with beautiful ruins. In a hundred, this side follows. But kings do not plan in hundreds of years, and the men who built the Loom will die rich and powerful long before the bill comes due." She set the second stone down on the flat top of the menhir, between them, and slid it across. "So. Now you know why I'm really here. Not to trade. Not to bargain. I came to tell you that the army behind me is not coming to stop you. It is coming to *use* you. You are the key that fits the lock, and Lord Chancellor Merrick does not care whether the key turns willingly."

* * *

Sable picked up the stone and held it a long moment, and Vael watched the play of feeling across their face — suspicion warring with hope, hope warring with the memory of what she had torn out of them on the road. They had not forgiven her. She had not asked them to. Forgiveness was a currency she had stopped carrying years ago; honesty was all she had left, and she spent it now like a woman spending her last coins.

"Why tell us?" Sable asked at last. "You could have delivered us to Merrick. Delivered the stones. Been his hero."

"Because I remember being nine," Vael said simply. "Because I remember what it is to be taken apart by people who call it salvation. And because—" she paused, and the pause cost her more than the whole confession had— "because when I took your memories on the road, I felt what was underneath them. Your gift isn't theft, Amberdale. I don't know what it is. But it touched my hollows, for half a heartbeat, and for half a heartbeat I remembered what it felt like to be *full*. I have chased that half-heartbeat across two kingdoms. I would burn the Loom myself if I could reach it. Since I cannot — since my orders chain me to it — I will do the next best thing. I will give you the stone, and stand beside you, and when the moment comes, I will turn my gift against the men who made me. Consider it a debt payment. Fifteen years' wages, rendered in betrayal."

Corvane laughed — short, disbelieving, edged. "You expect us to trust you."

"No," Vael said. "I expect you to be pragmatic. Trust is for people with time. We have four hours." She nodded toward the south, where the dark was gathering thicker than night had any right to gather. "And we have less than that. Listen."

They listened. At first there was only the wind off the fen and the dead whispering their paper-thin litany. Then, beneath it, another sound. Boots. Thousands of boots, still miles off, but coming on with the disciplined rhythm of an army that had marched all night and intended to march all the way into legend. Beneath the boots, a deeper vibration — the groan of the Loom's carriage wheels, forty oxen apiece, hauling a cathedral-sized engine of brass and bone up the pilgrim road toward the Hollowmere.

Torches were beginning to kindle along the southern ridgeline, one by one, like a constellation being nailed into place.

"They'll reach the tear by midnight," Vael said. "Merrick wants the engine positioned before totality. Which means we have until midnight to decide how we greet them." She rose, brushing peat from her coat, and looked at the two of them — the exiled knight with his hand still on his sword, the last Amberdale with a covenant-stone in each pocket and a third waiting somewhere in the heart of the tear itself. Enemies, a week ago. Something else tonight, though neither of them would name it yet.

"There's an old pilgrim causeway," she said, "that runs along the tear's northern edge. The crown's engineers dismissed it as unstable. It's unstable because it runs *through* the thin place — which means it's the one ground where the Loom cannot follow, and where a handful of people can fight a thousand, if they're willing to let the tear fight alongside them." She met Sable's eyes. "The dead here don't rest, Amberdale. But I've walked among them enough to know they aren't hostile. They're *waiting*. Waiting for someone to finish what was broken. I think they've been waiting for you."

Sable looked out at the tear — at the silver reeds and the doubled moons reflected in still water and the slow drift of voices that had once been people. Then down at the stones in their pockets. Then, briefly, at Corvane, who gave the smallest nod, the nod of a sworn guardian who had failed one oath and did not intend to fail another.

"All right," Sable said. "Shoulder to shoulder, then. Until midnight."

Vael almost smiled. It was the closest she had come in fifteen years. "Until midnight," she agreed.

And then the first horn sounded from the southern ridge — a long, brazen note that rolled across the fen and scattered the whispers of the dead like startled birds — and the army of Aldermarch came down out of the hills with torches ten thousand strong, and the eclipse began to eat the sun.
