## 17. The Battle of the Fraying

### Corvane

The tear in the world hung above the fen like a wound in wet silk, and Corvane had sworn an oath to it once — long ago, in a hall of woven light, with his knee on cold stone and the old queen's voice saying *guard it until your bones forget their names*. He had failed that oath. Tonight he meant to fail more slowly.

"Left flank," Sable shouted from the seam's edge, where they crouched with the two covenant-stones cradled against their chest like sleeping birds. "They're bringing ladders."

"They brought ladders to a hole in reality," Corvane said.

"They brought siege engines to a hole in reality," Sable corrected grimly. "The ladders are for the engineers."

He almost laughed. There was no time for it. The first wave of crown soldiers came down through the reeds in disciplined silence — no war cries, Aldermarch did not waste breath on theater — and behind them walked the inquisitors in their grey lacquered coats, faces smooth as eggs, hands empty and terrible.

Corvane drew his blade. It was not a fae blade anymore; the Court had unmade its enchantment when they cast him out, and what remained was honest steel, which was somehow fitting. He had been honest steel for fifteen years. Dull, heavy, reliable.

"Hold them at the waterline," Vael said beside him. She stood very straight, her hollow eyes fixed on the approaching greys. "If they reach the seam before midnight, everything we've bled for is kindling."

"I know how sieges work," Corvane said.

"You know how oaths break," she said. "I'm told you have experience."

He let that one pass. There would be time to hate each other properly later, if there was a later.

The armies met at the fen's drowned chapel path, where the water ran shin-deep and black as ink. The first soldier to reach him swung a billhook and Corvane took the man's wrist instead of his throat, turned him, used him as a shield against the second. Fae reflexes did not fade with exile. Only patience did.

*Hold the line,* he told himself. *Not win it. Hold it.* Winning was Sable's task, down at the tear's edge, where the air tasted like copper and childhood. Corvane's task was simpler and worse: to be the wall between ten thousand torches and one frightened heir, and to remain standing until the moon finished eating the sun.

A horn sounded — three short notes, the inquisitors' signal — and the greys parted like water around stones, letting something through.

Wardens. Crown wardens, six of them, walking in a hexagon with their linked hands raised, and between their palms the air curdled and thickened into a net of pale light. Corvane knew that working. Every fae child knew it. It was the snare they used on wild things, on things of the Otherworld, and it would wrap around his glamour and peel it back layer by layer until nothing remained but the raw creature underneath, screaming its true name to the sky.

"Vael," he said quietly. "Your people are being creative."

"They were never my people," Vael said. But she stepped forward, and Corvane saw her roll up her sleeves the way a surgeon does, unhurried, precise.

The first warden's net caught him across the shoulder. It burned like frostbite in reverse — heat that stole rather than seared — and Corvane felt the first layer of himself go translucent, his reflection in the black water flickering, thinning. He dropped to one knee. The second net was already forming.

Then Wren Vael walked into the middle of the hexagon, unarmed, and smiled at the six wardens with a face that had forgotten how.

"Gentlemen," she said. "Look at me."

They looked. They could not help it. That was the horror of her gift and the mercy of it too: memory-eaters do not take by force. They take because you open your mouth in the dark and they are standing there with a lamp, and you are so tired, so long tired, and they say *I can carry that for you.*

Corvane watched six trained instruments of the crown look into Wren Vael's empty eyes and begin, gently, to become empty themselves. One by one their hands fell. The nets dissolved like sugar in rain. The youngest warden sat down in the shallow water and began to cry without knowing why, and Vael knelt beside him and touched his forehead with something that might, in a different life, have been tenderness.

"That's ten years off each of them," she said, rising. "Skills, mostly. Drill formations. How to march. They'll wake up tomorrow unable to remember why they ever obeyed an order." She looked at Corvane, and her voice cracked along some old fault line. "It's what was done to me. I'm only returning the post."

"It doesn't make it clean," Corvane said.

"No," Vael agreed. "Nothing tonight is clean. Hold the line, knight."

And then the eclipse's shadow slid fully over the sun, and the world went dark at noon, and the dead in the fen began to sing.

### Vael

Wren Vael had spent fifteen years being a weapon and four hours being a person, and the person was proving inconveniently loud.

*You could stop,* the person said, as she cut through the press of soldiers toward the ridge where the crown's command banner flew. *You could walk away. You owe these people nothing.*

*I owe them exactly what they gave me,* she answered, and kept walking.

The plan had been Sable's, sketched in dirt beside a dying fire: let the army come, let them commit, and then take apart the thing that made them an army — not their bodies, never their bodies, but the architecture of obedience underneath. An army is just a memory of orders, repeated. Remove the memory and you have ten thousand confused people standing in a swamp holding weapons they suddenly don't remember how to use.

It was, Vael had thought, the most merciful battle plan she had ever heard. It was also monstrous. She suspected those were the same thing, here at the end of the world.

She reached the command rise as the eclipse reached totality. Above her, the Veil-tear blazed against the darkened sun like a second, truer star, and through it she could see — everyone could see now — the Otherworld leaking through in ribbons: towers of glass thorn, rivers running backward, a sky the color of a bruise healing. Ten thousand soldiers had stopped fighting to look up at it. Even the officers. Even the hard ones.

General Hesse Marrow did not look up. He stood at his map table with his back to the wonder, because Hesse Marrow had been hollowed so thoroughly and rebuilt so carefully that there was no room left in him for awe. Vael remembered him. She remembered being eight years old in the examination cells below Thornhold while he signed the intake papers, his pen scratching, her mother screaming three floors up.

"Lord Marshal," she said, stepping into the lantern light. "You should sit down."

Marrow turned. For a moment — one human moment — something moved behind his carved face. Recognition, maybe. Or the ghost of recognition, the way a house remembers a fire.

"The memory-eater," he said. "Vael. You were our best work."

"You flatterer." She walked closer. Around them, his honor guard shifted, and she felt their attention slide toward her like oil toward a flame, and she said, softly, "Don't. Please. I only want him."

"You want the stones," Marrow said. "The crown wants the tear. These goals are compatible. Walk away, Wren, and in the new order there will be a place—"

"There was a place for me in the old order," Vael said. "It was four feet wide and had straps."

She reached out and took his hand, the way a nurse does, the way a daughter might, and she began to give it all back.

That was the part no one understood about her gift, the part the crown had never bothered to learn because they only ever used it one direction. Memory did not have to be taken. It could be *returned*. Fifteen years of other people's stolen recollections sat inside her like swallowed stones — every gifted child's first word, every inquisitor's mother's face, every confession wrung out in the examination cells — and she opened herself now and poured them into Hesse Marrow until he staggered, until he gasped, until he gripped the map table with both hands and wept like a man drowning in his own history.

She gave him the children. All of them. Every small voice from the cells below Thornhold, every name, every plea, filed for fifteen years in the vaults of her hollowness, waiting for a lock worth opening.

"There," she whispered, as he sagged against his officers. "Now you know what you built. Carry it. I carried it long enough."

Behind her, the command structure of the army of Aldermarch folded like a tent with the pole pulled out. Officers looked at their orders and found they could not remember who wrote them or why. Some sat down. Some threw down their swords. A colonel began loudly reciting his daughter's birthday, over and over, like a prayer.

But far to the north, past the ridge, past the supply lines, something vast groaned against the earth — the sound of the siege engine called the Sundering Loom beginning its final wind, the machine the crown had truly marched all this way to fire. Not to seal the tear. To widen it. To aim it.

Vael turned south, toward the seam, toward the knight and the thief and the last stone, and started to run.

### Sable

The tear sang to Sable in their mother's voice.

That was the worst of it, or maybe the best — standing at the edge of the fraying with two covenant-stones burning cold against their chest, and the torn Veil whispering in Elara Amberdale's tones: *hurry, little one, hurry, the door won't hold.* The dead of both realms crowded the fen's edges now, drawn up out of the mud by the eclipse, and they watched with the patient hunger of an audience that has waited a very long time for the final act.

"Sable!" Corvane's voice, ragged, from the waterline. "The Loom! They're turning the Loom!"

Sable looked north. On the highest ridge, silhouetted against the eclipsed sun, the Sundering Loom rose like a gallows the size of a cathedral, its cables singing with gathered power, its great lens already swiveling toward the tear. They had lied about everything, the crown. Seal the Veil, protect the realm — lies stacked on lies. They meant to crack the door off its hinges and pour the Otherworld through it like wine, weaponized, bottled, sold.

"We need the third stone," Sable said aloud, to the tear, to the dead, to whoever was listening. "Where is it?"

The answer came not in words but in memory — the tear reaching into them the way Vael once had, gentle this time, apologetic — and showed them a night twenty-three years gone: a woman running through this same fen with a bundle in her arms, blood on her hem, a bargain half-shouted to the dark. *Take it. Take the years. Hide them where even I can't reach.* And the Veil itself answering, the way the hungry answer, *give, and be given.*

The third stone was not hidden in the fen. It was not hidden anywhere. It was the tear's own heart, the knot at the center of the fraying, and it was made of Sable's infancy — every stolen first, held in trust by a dying covenant for the child who would someday come home to claim it.

"Oh," Sable said. Very quietly. "Oh, it's me. It was always me."

The Loom fired.

There was no warning worthy of the word — a sound like the sky clearing its throat, a lance of white wrongness arcing from the ridge — and Sable had one heartbeat to throw up the two covenant-stones like a shield before it struck the tear's edge. The impact went through the stones, through Sable's arms, through their teeth and marrow. The Veil screamed. Somewhere in the Otherworld, something ancient and beloved fell. The tear ripped wider, a hand's width, an arm's width, enough for things to start coming through — not monsters, worse, *refugees*, fae streaming out of their collapsing world with their homes unraveling behind them, falling into the mud of a realm that had spent a generation learning to hate them.

And the crown's soldiers, seeing the enemy pour through, forgot their emptied orders and remembered only fear, and raised their weapons against drowning people.

"No," Sable breathed. "No, no — that's not — that isn't how any of this was supposed to—"

They ran for the tear's heart. There was no other move left. Behind them, Corvane was shouting something — their name, probably, or a warning, or simply *no*, the oldest word — and ahead the rippling wound in the world opened like a mouth, and inside it Sable could see it at last: a small stone, no bigger than a heart, glowing with a light they recognized the way you recognize your own reflection.

Their whole life was in there. Their mother's lullaby. A father's laugh with bells in it. The forging ritual learned before language, sung into a cradle. Everything given up to keep them safe, everything they'd spent a lifetime stealing from strangers because their own first years had been pawned to save them.

To take it back meant feeling all of it. Every joy, and under the joy, the abandonment, the fen-cold night, the mother's footsteps fading. Wholeness, with the pain still in it, the way wholeness always comes.

The Loom began to charge again. The eclipse had minutes left. The Veil was coming apart beneath their hands like wet cloth.

Sable Amberdale, thief of memories, last heir of a broken oath, reached into the heart of the tear —

— and the third stone reached back.
