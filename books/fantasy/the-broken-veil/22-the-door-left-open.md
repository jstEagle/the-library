## 22. The Door Left Open

The seam woke me before the birds did.

It always sang at dawn — a thin, silver thread of sound that ran through the floorboards of my cottage and up through the soles of my feet, like a harp string plucked somewhere beneath the world. A year ago I would have called it magic. Now I just called it morning.

I rose from bed in the half-dark and padded barefoot to the eastern wall, where the Veil-seam passed through my house the way a river passes through a mill. It glowed faintly there, a hairline fracture of light no wider than a finger, humming its low note of *here, here, here*. On one side of the wall hung drying herbs from the human woods — yarrow, feverfew, bog-myrtle. On the other side, where the wall simply stopped being a wall and became a trellis of living hawthorn, grew things with no human names at all, their blossoms opening backward, petal by petal, as the sun came up over Aldermarch.

My cottage straddled both realms. That was not a metaphor. The front door opened onto a lane in the Veilless Valley, two miles from Old Tam's pawnshop, now run by his niece with a new sign and the same crooked shutter. The back door opened onto a meadow in the Otherworld where the grass chimed when the wind moved it. I had built the cottage myself, stone by stone, on the exact spot where the reforged covenant crossed the old quarry road — because if I was going to be the thing that stood between two worlds, I decided I ought to live in the doorway.

I pressed my palm flat against the seam. It was warm. It was always warm, but this morning it hummed a half-tone higher than usual, and beneath the hum I felt something I had learned to read the way farmers read clouds: a weather coming. Not a storm. Not yet. More like the first pressure drop before one.

"Later," I told it, the way you tell a restless horse.

The seam pulsed once against my hand, unimpressed, and let me go make breakfast.

* * *

They arrived at midmorning, as they always did — the children.

There were six of them now. Wren, who was not named for the inquisitor but for the bird, and who could walk through reflections the way other children walked through doorways. The twins, Ash and Ember, born to a human mother and a river-husband out past Thornhold, who could only be seen clearly by moonlight and were therefore perpetually late to lessons held in daylight. Little Maren, whose gift was the gentlest and the heaviest: she heard the dead the way I had once heard the sea in a stolen memory, constantly, patiently, whether she wanted to or not. And Bram, oldest at fourteen, sullen and sharp-eyed, who had walked three hundred miles from the northern marches after the crown's new sanctuary edict reached his village, carrying nothing but a knife and the conviction that everyone would eventually turn him in.

He had been with us four months and still sat nearest the door.

"Today," I announced, setting down the basket of bread and the bowl of honeyed milk, "we're going to talk about hunger."

Bram snorted. "We do that every day. It's called lunch."

"Hush." I sat cross-legged on the wide hearthstone, the one I'd laid directly over the seam so that its warmth rose up through the floor like a living thing. "Not the kind your belly feels. The kind your *gift* feels. Wren, what happens if you go three days without stepping through a mirror?"

Wren considered this with the grave seriousness of an eight-year-old philosopher. "Everything gets loud. The reflections start shouting. Last time I got so hungry I nearly stepped into the well-water without asking it first."

"And why is that dangerous?"

"Because the well didn't invite me." She recited it like scripture, because it was. It was the first law I taught them, the law that had cost me more than any other lesson of my life: *a gift taken without invitation is theft; a gift offered freely is a covenant.* I had spent twenty years stealing memories from travelers to survive, and I could still taste the guilt of it, cold at the back of the throat, like well-water I hadn't been invited into.

"Maren, what does your gift ask of you?"

Maren's hands twisted in her lap. "That I listen. Even when it hurts."

"And what do you get to choose?"

Her chin lifted a little. "Whether I answer."

"That's right." I looked around the circle — at Ash and Ember flickering in and out of focus in the morning light, at Bram pretending not to listen while listening harder than anyone. "Every gift is a door. And every door opens both ways. The crown spent years telling people like us we were weapons. The old courts told us we were mistakes. Both of them were wrong. You are not what your gift can *do*. You are what you *choose* to do with it. That's the whole lesson. Everything else is practice."

Bram stared into the fire. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "What if what you choose is running away?"

"Then you choose it until you don't," I said. "And then you come somewhere like this, and someone feeds you, and you start choosing again."

He didn't answer. But that night, for the first time, he moved his bedroll away from the door.

* * *

Corvane came up from the Otherworld side at noon, which meant I heard him long before I saw him — the chime of grass parting, the particular silence birds make around a knight in full stride even a year out of armor.

He wore no armor now. He wore a teacher's plain coat, dark green, though he had never quite lost the bearing of a man who expected to be challenged at any gate. His antlers — grown back fuller since the reforging, since the Thorn Court had grudgingly restored what it had stripped from him in disgrace — caught the light like branches holding the last of winter.

"The seam's singing sharp today," he said, dropping onto the hearthstone beside me. Up close, his eyes were the color of deep water over slate, and they held reflections again. They had begun to, after the covenant. Whatever my mother's bargain had hollowed out of the world, putting it back had put *him* back too.

"You felt it too."

"I felt it at dawn. So did the Court. Queen Vess sent a raven made of thorns, which is her way of saying she's concerned but won't admit it." He accepted the bread I handed him and tore it in half. "She wants to know if the wardenship has noticed anything in the north."

"The north," I said. "Where the Veil was never torn. Where nothing has ever happened."

"Which is precisely why anything happening there would be interesting."

We ate in companionable silence. This was the shape our days had settled into: lessons in the morning, tending the seam at midday, arguments about fence posts and fae politics in the afternoon. Corvane had stayed. I had never asked him to, and he had never explained it beyond a single sentence spoken the first night the cottage stood: *I failed one Amberdale. I will not fail another.* But somewhere across the seasons, the sentence had changed meaning without either of us saying so. He was not guarding me anymore. He was simply *here*, the way the hearth was here, the way the seam was here — part of the architecture of my life.

"Do you ever miss it?" I asked him. "The Court. Being somebody's knight instead of nobody's schoolmaster."

"I was never nobody's knight." He brushed crumbs from his coat. "I am the warden of the seam, keeper of the stones, and tutor to six small catastrophes. It is the most responsibility anyone has ever trusted me with, and I include the fate of both worlds in that assessment." A pause. The corner of his mouth moved. "Also, the Court's wine was terrible."

"It really was."

* * *

The letter came at dusk, and it came the way Wren Vael did everything: precisely, without wasted motion, and slightly terrifying.

A hawk landed on my windowsill — a real hawk, which surprised me; Vael had learned to work within the covenant rather than around it, mostly. It carried a tube of oiled leather, and inside was a single sheet in her narrow, upright hand.

*Sable.*

*I write from Coldharbour, last town before the northern wastes. The crown's survey teams have found something under the ice — a structure older than the covenant, older than the Amberdales, older, the scholars say, than the division of the realms itself. It should not exist. Nothing should predate the sundering. And yet the ice around it is melting, and the men who dug too deep have stopped dreaming. All of them. At once. The same night.*

*You reforged the Veil as a door with obligations on both sides. I am writing to honor mine: whatever sleeps up here predates every promise we made, and I do not think it knows it has been made any.*

*The eclipse comes again in four months. The astronomers say it will pass directly over Coldharbour.*

*I would value your counsel. Or failing that, your company. Or failing that, your stubbornness, which I have found to be the most reliable force in either realm.*

*— W. V.*

I read it twice. Then I set it on the table and stood looking out the back window, where the Otherworld's meadow was going violet with evening, and the seam along my wall hummed its high, uneasy note, and I understood that the two things were saying the same thing.

Corvane read the letter in silence. When he finished, he did not say *we should go* or *this is terrible news*. He said, "Who will mind the children?"

"Wren can hold the mirror-lessons. Maren knows the ward-chants better than I do now, honestly. Bram—" I hesitated. "Bram is almost ready to sit with the seam himself. Someone should give him the chance to prove it."

"He'll be terrified."

"He'll be magnificent. Those aren't mutually exclusive. You taught me that."

Corvane folded the letter with great care, as if it might bite. "Four months to the eclipse. Coldharbour is nine weeks' ride, longer with the passes melting. We would arrive with weeks to spare, if we left soon."

"We."

He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I had seen only rarely in a year — the expression of a man watching a door open onto a country he thought he'd never see again. "I swore an oath to guard the Amberdales," he said quietly. "You've spent a year teaching children that oaths are doors, not walls. Did you imagine yours to me was any different?"

I hadn't. But hearing it was different from knowing it.

* * *

I packed that night, and packing took longer than it should have, because everything in the cottage asked to be taken.

The stones — all three of them, fused now into a single tri-lobed heart of light that I kept in a wooden box lined with my mother's sigil-cloth — hummed when I lifted them, eager or warning, I couldn't tell. My mother's letter, recovered whole from the mountain's echo, went into the inner pocket over my chest, where I carried it the way some people carry saints' bones. A jar of valley honey for the road, because the north would be bitter enough. And at the very bottom of the pack, wrapped in soft leather, the small tin box where I kept the memories I had chosen to keep rather than take: Old Tam's laugh, the sea from a merchant's mind, the lullaby from the hooded stranger that I finally knew was my mother's voice, sung to me before I could remember anything else.

Before I closed the pack, I went to the eastern wall and laid both hands on the seam.

It sang against my palms, high and urgent now, unmistakable. *Something is coming,* it said, in the wordless way it said everything. *Something older than the door.*

"I know," I whispered. "I'm going."

The seam pulsed — and this time I could have sworn the pulse traveled *outward*, away from me, northward, like a message sent ahead. Like a warning given to whatever waited under the ice: *the warden is coming, and she does not come alone.*

Behind me, the children slept. Bram's bedroll lay far from the door. Maren murmured in her sleep, answering someone gently, as she always did. In the morning I would wake them, and explain, and trust them with the seam the way my mother had once trusted the Veil with the pieces of me — imperfectly, desperately, out of love.

At the threshold I paused. The front door opened onto the valley lane, silvered with moonlight, ordinary and dear. The back door opened onto the chiming grass, wild and waiting. For twenty years I had believed belonging was a place you were locked out of. Then I had learned it was a place you were forged into. Now I knew the truth underneath both: belonging was not a place at all. It was a door, and you chose it, every morning, every crossing, every time you opened it and stepped through — knowing you would never be entirely one thing, and that this was not a wound.

It was the whole point.

I opened the door, and stepped toward the north.

* * *

Far away, under the ice, something that had slept since before the sundering felt the seam's warning ripple across the world — and smiled, in the dark, with a mouth that had never learned what doors were for.
