## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The rain came to Vell the way it always did: sideways, gray, and smelling of chimney smoke and river rot. Astrid Marchetti stood beneath the sagging awning of her stall in Tanners' Row and watched it hammer the cobbles, and she thought, as she did every morning, that a storm like this would have been so easy to call. One breath. One unclenching of fingers. She could pull the lightning down out of those low clouds like a fisherman hauling in a line.

She did not, of course. She had not in twenty years.

Instead she arranged her wares with the practiced care of a woman who had nothing to hide, which was itself a kind of hiding. Copper charms on leather cords — love-knots, luck-knots, knots against toothache and tax collectors. Dried lavender sewn into muslin squares. Little pressed-tin saints that weren't really saints at all, because Vell had no gods and no patience for anyone who suggested otherwise; they were "guardians," "watchers," "old friends." The words mattered here. Words were the only magic Vell permitted, and even then only the harmless kind.

"Morning, Marchetti." Old Tam Hesk, the cooper, shuffling past with his collar up. He never bought anything. He simply liked to say her name aloud, as if confirming the world still held the things he expected it to hold.

"Morning, Tam."

Everything about her stall was designed to be expected. The faded striped canvas. The hand-lettered sign — *MARCHETTI'S CHARMS & CURIOS, ALL ITEMS BLESSED BY NO ONE* — which had earned a laugh from the market inspector eleven years ago and bought her a tolerance she'd been careful never to spend. Even her accent was a performance: the flat, clipped vowels of the Vellish middle class, scrubbed clean of the sea-salt lilt she'd been born to. She had been Astrid Marchetti for so long that some mornings she woke and the name fit without pinching.

Some mornings. Not this one. This morning the air tasted of iron, though she couldn't have said why.

She hung the last charm, touched the little tin watcher at the corner of the awning — habit, nothing more, though her fingertips lingered a half-second longer than habit required — and opened for trade.

* * *

Vell did its business in the rain the way it did everything: grudgingly, and for money. By midmorning the Row had filled with fishwives and clerks, apprentices running errands with their hoods down despite the weather, two city guardsmen making their slow circuit with the bored menace of men who knew exactly how much power they had and enjoyed counting it.

Astrid sold three luck-knots, a love-charm to a boy who blushed so hard she feared for him, and a toothache knot to Tam's wife, who bit down on it theatrically and declared herself cured, which was nonsense but good advertising. Ordinary work. Ordinary day. The storm coiled in her bones stayed coiled, a faint pressure behind her sternum like weather waiting at the horizon, familiar enough now that she only noticed it when she made herself notice — a discipline, a penance, a prayer to nothing.

It was nearly noon when the stranger appeared at the edge of the crowd.

She saw him first as a wrongness in the pattern of the market: people parting around him without seeming to know why, the way water parts around a dead thing. He was tall, gray-cloaked, moving with the deliberate care of a man who has been wounded for a long time and has stopped pretending otherwise. Rain ran off his hood in sheets. Beneath it his face was bloodless, and there was blood elsewhere — she could smell it under the wet wool and river stink, sharp and bright, a smell her body recognized before her mind did.

He crossed the Row toward her stall. Toward her, specifically. His eyes found hers through the rain and something in them — recognition, or relief, or terror, all three braided together — made Astrid's hands go still over her charms.

"No," she said quietly, to him, to the day, to whatever god didn't exist. "No, you've got the wrong—"

He reached the stall. His hand shot out and closed around her wrist, and his grip was cold as a drowned man's, and he said, in a language she had not heard spoken aloud in twenty years, a single word:

*Caelwyn.*

The word went through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs. Around them the market went on — haggling, laughing, cursing the rain — oblivious, mercifully oblivious. Astrid stood frozen with a stranger's hand on her wrist and twenty years of careful silence cracking down the middle like river ice in spring.

"You're mistaken," she said in Vellish, loud enough for the neighbors. "I don't know you. Take your hand off me or I'll call the guard."

Something flickered across his face — understanding, maybe, or apology. Then his legs simply stopped being legs. He folded, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and Astrid caught him more by reflex than choice, staggering under his weight as he collapsed against her stall. Charms scattered. A luck-knot rolled into a puddle. Somewhere a woman shrieked and someone shouted for a physic, and the crowd began, with the awful appetite crowds have, to gather.

"Help me," Astrid snapped at the nearest bodies. "You — yes, you, get the watch surgeon, go!"

They propped him against her counter. Up close she could see the wound now: a long dark bloom spreading across his left side, soaked through cloak and shirt, still bleeding sluggishly. A knife wound, deep, angled upward. Someone had known what they were doing.

His eyes found hers again. Gray eyes, rain-wet lashes, and in them a desperate urgency that made her lean closer against every instinct screaming at her to run.

"The Gate," he whispered.

The word landed in her chest like a struck bell. She felt it hum in the place where the storm slept, felt the pressure behind her sternum shift and stir, and for one vertiginous heartbeat the rain falling off the awning seemed to hang suspended, each drop a small silver decision.

"I don't know what that is," she lied.

He smiled. It was a terrible smile, the smile of a man spending his last coin. His hand fumbled inside his cloak and came out holding a scroll — a map, rolled tight, sealed at the center with a blob of dark red wax stamped with a symbol she could not look at directly, the way one cannot look at the sun. He pressed it into her hands and folded her fingers over it with both of his, and his grip, for all his dying, was strong.

"They'll come for it," he breathed. "Don't let them read it. Don't let them—" A cough took him, wet and rattling. Blood at the corner of his mouth. "You sealed it once. You can—"

The crowd surged closer. Voices. Boots. And underneath everything, faint but unmistakable, the taste of ozone on the back of her tongue — her own power answering a summons it hadn't heard in two decades, rising like floodwater.

"Hush," she said. "Hush. You're safe. Just hold on."

But he wasn't listening anymore. His gaze had drifted past her shoulder to the gray sky, and his lips moved once more — shaping a name, perhaps, or a prayer, or just the word *home* — and then the light in his face went out the way a lamp goes out, all at once and completely.

Astrid knelt in the rain beside a dead man in a godless city, holding a sealed map against her breastbone, and realized that everyone in the crowd was looking at her.

* * *

The watch surgeon pronounced him dead of a knife wound and shrugged, which was Vell's official position on most mysteries. The guardsmen asked their questions with the flat efficiency of men filling out forms: Did she know him? No. Had he said anything? Something about a gate — the Tanner's Gate, she supposed, the old archway at the end of the Row? They wrote it down. They took the body. They told her she could reopen tomorrow, and one of them lingered a moment too long, looking at her with the particular attention of a man who would remember her face, and then the rain closed over the whole affair and the market went back to being a market.

Astrid packed her stall with shaking hands.

She should burn it. That was the first clear thought, arriving whole and reasonable while her deeper mind screamed underneath. Burn the map, unread. Melt the wax, feed the fire, scatter the ash in the river. Whatever the stranger had seen in her — whatever mistake had made him choose her out of all the faces in Vell — it died with him, and if she destroyed the thing he'd carried, then none of it had ever happened. Tomorrow she would sell luck-knots in the rain, and the storm would stay asleep in her bones, and the walls she'd built around her life would stand another twenty years.

She got as far as striking the flint.

She sat in the dark of her rooms above the dyer's shop, map in her lap, candle lit, flint and steel in hand, and she could not do it. Her hands wouldn't move. It wasn't sentiment — she owed this man nothing, knew nothing of him except that he'd bled out against her counter whispering her country's name. It was the seal. The wax seemed to drink the candlelight, and when her thumb passed near it she felt that pressure behind her sternum lean toward it like a plant toward sun, and beneath her skin, faint as a memory of fever, something turned over in its sleep.

*You sealed it once.*

She hadn't. She was a charm-seller. She was Astrid Marchetti of Tanners' Row, born in Vell, daughter of nobody, keeper of knots. She had never sealed anything in her life except letters and jars of preserves.

And yet her hands remembered the weight of the wax the way hands remember a rope they climbed as a child.

Outside, boots on the cobbles — more boots than the hour warranted. She blew out the candle and looked through the shutter's gap and saw them moving up the street in pairs, gray cloaks, silver badges shaped like a closed eye. Wardens. Not the city guard; the Wardens did not walk Vell's streets, had not needed to in years, because there was nothing left in Vell for them to find.

One of them paused beneath her window. Looked up. Moved on.

Astrid sat back from the shutter in the dark, the map clutched to her chest, her heart going like a drum in a procession. Twenty years of silence, and it had taken one dying stranger and six words to crack it open. *Caelwyn. The Gate. You sealed it once.*

She did not know yet what the seal would show her. She did not know that the dead man's face was already stirring something buried so deep she'd mistaken the grave for empty ground. She knew only this: the storm in her bones had woken, and it was pressing against her ribs like a bird against a cage, and somewhere below, in the rain, the Wardens were asking questions.

She looked down at the wax seal in the darkness, and the darkness, very faintly, looked back.
