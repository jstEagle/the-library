## 2. The Weight of Wax and Paper

The map would not burn.

Astrid had tried everything the night before. She had held it over her hearth until the wax began to weep and the paper's edges curled like dying leaves — and then the flame had simply *gone out*, snuffed as cleanly as a candle pinched between wet fingers. She had tried again with a taper, with an oil lamp, with the little iron brazier she used to heat the glue for her charm-work. Each time, the fire died at the exact moment it touched the seal, leaving the parchment untouched beneath, cool as riverstone.

She had tried the canal next, in the grey hour before dawn, when even the gulls were asleep. She had weighted the map with a cobblestone from her own courtyard and dropped it from the Saltbridge into water black as ink. She had watched it sink. She had walked home through the fog feeling lighter than she had in twenty years.

And when she unlocked her door, the map was lying on her table, dry, the cobblestone nowhere in evidence, the wax seal unbroken and gleaming faintly in the dark like something breathing.

That was when Astrid Marchetti stopped trying to destroy it and started trying to understand it.

* * *

Morning came to Vell the way it always did: bells first, then smoke, then the long grey light that made the city look like a corpse dressed in its best clothes. From her window above the tannery lane, Astrid could see the market square filling — the fishwives with their silver scales, the spice men shouting prices, the children running messages for coppers. A city of ten thousand people pretending very hard that magic had never existed anywhere in the world.

Vell did not hate magic the way a man hates a snake. It hated magic the way a scarred man hates fire: with memory, with law, with bonfires in the public square every quarter-year for those who forgot. The Wardens saw to that. Grey-cloaked, grey-eyed, patient as winter, they walked the streets with their iron rods and their ledgers of names, and everyone in Vell knew someone whose name had gone into one of those ledgers and never come out again.

Astrid had spent two decades being no one. Charm-seller, yes — but charms of the harmless kind, the kind the law permitted because they were nothing at all. Knotted red thread against bad luck. Dried lavender sewn into linen for sleep. Little glass beads blown with a prayer inside them, or what passed for a prayer in a godless city. She sold hope shaped like trinkets, and the Wardens' ledger had never once contained her name, because there was nothing in her stall worth writing down.

Except, of course, for the storm.

It still lived in her bones. She felt it now, as she felt it every waking hour — a low pressure behind her ribs, a taste of ozone at the back of her throat, the ghost-weight of lightning coiled along her spine like a sleeping serpent. Twenty years ago she had traded almost all of it away to survive. Almost all. What remained was small, a banked coal, enough to warm her palms on cold nights if she dared, which she never did. Enough to make the wax seal flicker.

*Flicker.* That was the word her mind kept returning to, and she hated it, because it meant the thing on her table had *recognized* her.

She sat down across from the map and looked at it properly for the first time.

It was drawn on calfskin, old and soft, the kind of vellum that cost more than her stall earned in a season. Inked lines crawled across it in a hand both careful and hurried — coastlines, mountain ranges, a river delta rendered in fine stippling. But none of it matched any geography Astrid knew, and she knew the maps of the Nine Kingdoms well; she'd memorized them as a child, back when knowing maps was safe. This was somewhere else. Somewhere that did not exist, if you believed the atlases. Somewhere erased.

Three marks stained the parchment in rust-brown ink: a drowned lowland in the south, a ruined tower in the middle country, and at the northern edge, half-hidden by a fold-wear crease, a symbol she could not read — a circle with a hollow center, drawn so that looking at it too long made her eyes ache.

And pressed into the center of it all, sealing a folded inner layer she had not yet been able to open, the wax.

It was not candle wax. It was too pale, almost opalescent, and it moved when she wasn't watching — not visibly, never visibly, but she would glance away and back and find the whorls of its surface arranged differently, like clouds rearranging between blinks. Last night, when she had pressed her thumb against it, desperate and furious, wanting only to crack the thing open and find inside it some explanation, some *absolution*, something other than the death of a stranger in her arms —

— it had flickered.

Not the wax. Her power. The buried storm had risen toward her skin like a dog hearing its name, and for one terrible half-second her fingertips had glowed blue-white, and the air in her kitchen had smelled of the sea before a squall, and the seal had *warmed under her touch*, gently, like a living thing leaning into a hand.

She had torn herself away and vomited into the courtyard gutter.

Now, in the morning light, she reached for it again. Slower this time. She told herself it was caution. She knew it was hunger.

Her fingertips brushed the wax.

The flicker came — softer this time, a shimmer beneath her skin, the old pressure in her ribs shifting like something turning over in sleep. And through the contact, faint as a voice through a wall, she felt the seal *acknowledge* her. Not open. Not yet. Just... recognize. The way a lock recognizes a key it has been waiting decades to hold.

Astrid snatched her hand back and sat very still, listening to her own heartbeat, to the market noise rising below, to the ordinary sounds of a world in which she was nobody.

The dead man's face floated up behind her eyes. She pushed it down. She would deal with him later. Or never.

* * *

She never got the chance to decide.

The Wardens came to the market district just past midmorning, and they came in numbers Astrid hadn't seen since the anniversary sweeps of her first year in Vell. They entered the square from three sides at once, grey cloaks moving through the crowd without hurry, without apology, the way weather moves. Their iron rods hung at their belts, dull and ugly, and where they passed, the market's noise thinned to a hush, the way a fire quiets when snow falls on it.

They were asking questions.

Astrid watched from her stall, hands busy with a knot of red thread she had no interest in, heart hammering a rhythm entirely at odds with her idle fingers. Two Wardens worked the far row of stalls, showing something to each merchant in turn. A scrap of paper? A sketch? She couldn't see. A third stood at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, questioning the fishwife Marta, who shook her head so vigorously her earrings swung.

*They know he came here,* Astrid thought. *They know he died here.*

Because of course they knew. A man bleeding out in a public square did not go unwitnessed in Vell. Someone had reported it — perhaps several someones, eager to be helpful, eager to be seen helping. The body would have been taken. The questions would follow the blood backward, stall by stall, face by face, until they arrived at hers.

She had planned for many things in twenty years of hiding. She had rehearsed answers about her name, her origin, her lack of talent. She had never once planned for a dying stranger to press a sealed map into her hands and say *the Gate* in front of forty witnesses.

One of the Wardens turned toward her row.

He was young, she noted distantly, with the terrible calm of the newly converted, and he was holding the sketch out to the potter two stalls down. The potter leaned in, squinted, shook his head. The Warden thanked him — *thanked* him, politely, which was somehow worse — and moved on.

Three stalls. Two. Astrid arranged her charms with exquisite care. Red thread. Lavender. Glass beads. Nothing here but a woman selling superstition to superstitious people, nothing here but —

"Goodwife."

She looked up. The Warden stood before her stall, and he was holding the sketch at arm's length, and the sketch was a face.

She knew the face. Of course she knew the face; she had held it while it died. But that was not why her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

She knew it from *before*. From twenty years ago, from a night of rain and screaming that she had buried so deep she'd told herself it was a dream, a story, a wound that had healed into nothing. A younger face, unlined, laughing — laughing in a doorway lit by lightning, in a house by the sea, in a country that officially did not exist. The dead man in her stall had been there. He had been there on the last night, the night the sky tore open and her parents knelt before her and told her what she had to do, and this man — older now, grey-streaked, hollow-cheeked, but unmistakable — had been standing in the rain outside, keeping watch, his coat streaming, shouting for them to hurry.

*Hurry, hurry, the Wardens are coming, hurry —*

"Goodwife." The Warden's voice didn't change, but his eyes did, sharpening by a degree. "Do you know this man?"

"No," said Astrid Marchetti, and her voice was steady, and she marveled at it from a great distance. "I've never seen him."

The Warden studied her for a moment longer. Then he nodded, thanked her, and moved on down the row, and Astrid stood among her harmless charms with her harmless smile nailed in place, and inside her chest the buried storm woke fully for the first time in twenty years and pressed against her ribs like a fist.

They would come back. Questions in Vell were never asked once. By nightfall, maybe sooner, someone would remember seeing the stranger stumble toward the charm-seller's stall. Someone always remembered. And when the Wardens returned, they would search her home, and they would find the map that would not burn, the map that had climbed out of a canal to lie waiting on her table like a patient animal.

She could run. The thought arrived whole, unbidden. Take the map, take her go-bag from under the floorboards — she had kept one for twenty years, updated, oiled, ready — and be out the Saltgate before the bells rang evening. Disappear south, change her name again, sell knots and lavender in some other godless town until she died small and safe.

But the seal had recognized her. The map had refused to drown. And the dead man's face belonged to the one night she had spent two decades refusing to remember, which meant the map was not a stranger's burden at all.

It was hers. It had always been hers. It had simply taken twenty years and a death to find its way back.

Astrid looked down at her hands — a charm-seller's hands, chapped and thread-marked — and watched, very briefly, deliberately, the faintest shimmer of blue-white light pass beneath the skin of her fingertips. Then she closed them into fists.

By the time the Warden reached the end of the row and glanced back, the stall was empty, the awning folded, and Astrid Marchetti was gone into the crowd with a map that would not burn pressed against her heart — and behind her, at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, a broad-shouldered stranger in a travel-stained coat was watching her go with eyes that had seen the inside of a Warden's cloak.
