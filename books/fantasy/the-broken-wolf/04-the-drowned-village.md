## 4. The Drowned Village

The lowlands began three days out of Vell, where the road gave up pretending and sank into the water.

Astrid had never seen country like it. The map called it the Meres — a sprawl of drowned fields stretching to a horizon that could not decide whether it was sky or flood. Dead orchards stood waist-deep in grey water, their branches bare as drowned men's fingers. Here and there a rooftop broke the surface like the back of some sleeping animal, thatched with reeds gone silver in the cold. The only sounds were the lap of water against their boots and the far-off crying of birds that had no business being this far north this early in the year.

"The map says west," Bren said for the third time that morning, holding the wax-sealed paper at arm's length as though it might bite him.

"The map says *through*," Astrid corrected. She took it from him before he could argue. The seal had grown warm against her palm over the past days — not hot, exactly, but alive, the way a banked fire is alive. It responded to her touch with faint pulses she was learning to read: a quickening when they strayed, a settling when they found the way again. Whatever her parents had bound into that wax twenty years ago, it knew her blood.

She pointed to a line of willows half-submerged ahead, their trunks leaning at angles no living tree would choose. "There's a causeway under there. See how the reeds grow in a row? Someone planted them."

Bren squinted, unconvinced. Behind him, Sefa rode on Orla's back, the child's small face buried in the healer's hood, watching the water with the particular attention she gave all weather. Snow-speech, Orla called it, though there was no snow here yet — only the promise of it, hanging in the air like a held breath. The winter had come early and wrong, and they all felt it pressing down from the north like a hand.

"You've been here before," Bren said quietly. Not a question.

"No." But her boots seemed to remember something her mind did not. Twice now she had turned left where the map showed nothing but open water, and twice the way had opened. It unsettled her more than she let show. There were rooms inside herself she had locked so long ago she'd forgotten the doors existed, and the map kept finding the keyholes.

They waded the causeway through water that rose to their thighs and then, mercifully, fell away. And then the village appeared — or rather, revealed itself, because it had been there all along, sunk into the landscape the way a stone sinks into moss.

It stood on a rise that became an island in wet seasons: forty or fifty houses built of black timber and pale stone, their lower stories stained dark by decades of floodwater, their upper windows glowing amber against the dusk. Smoke rose from chimneys. Laundry hung between buildings. A dog barked somewhere, deep-chested and unafraid, which was the detail that undid Astrid most — a dog that barked at strangers because it had never learned to fear them.

"There's no village here," Bren said flatly. He had his hand on his sword hilt, old habit. "I memorized every settlement in the borderlands during my service. This valley is listed as uninhabited marsh."

"Then I suppose we're nowhere at all." Astrid started forward. "Try to look harmless."

"I don't know how."

"Then try to look less armed."

* * *

The villagers watched them come with a stillness that was not hostility but something older — the wariness of people who had survived by never being seen. Doors opened along the main lane, faces appearing in gaps. Astrid counted children among them, which meant families, which meant permanence, which meant this place had endured the Wardens' censuses for years. Her chest tightened with something she refused to name yet.

An old woman came out to meet them, walking with a stick of river-wood worn smooth as glass. She was small and bent and wrapped in layers of dyed wool, and her eyes — pale as shallow water — went first to Bren's sword, then to Orla's healer's satchel, then to Sefa, and softened. Last of all they went to Astrid, and stopped.

The woman said nothing for a long moment. Then, in a language Astrid had not heard spoken aloud since she was seven years old — low, vowel-heavy, the tongue of Caelwyn's western coast — she said: *"Storm-daughter. Marchetti blood. I know that face. I carried your grandmother across the ford at Aldenmere the summer the floods took the mill."*

Astrid's breath left her. "You can't—"

"Maren Ostrova," the woman said, switching back to common speech. "Though the name's worth nothing now. Few names are." She looked at the map case at Astrid's hip, and her expression changed — not to fear, exactly, but to the gravity of someone setting down a heavy thing. "You're going to the Gate."

It wasn't a question either. Word traveled, even underwater, even underground. Astrid realized the whole village must have known what the map was the moment word of strangers reached them.

"We need shelter," she said. "One night. We won't bring trouble."

Maren Ostrova laughed, a dry sound like reeds breaking. "Child, you *are* trouble. You've been trouble since you were born, if you're who I think you are. Come in. Trouble gets fed here same as anyone."

* * *

The house Maren led them to sat at the village's heart, its ground floor surrendered to the flood long ago — they entered by a stair to the second story, crossing a little bridge of planks that creaked like a ship. Inside, the room was warm and close and crowded with life: herbs drying from rafters, nets and coils of rope, a stove of black iron radiating heat, and charms.

Charms everywhere.

Astrid stopped in the doorway and stared. Bundles of knotted thread hung from the ceiling beams, each knot a small working — she recognized warding knots, luck knots, a few cruder things meant to turn away sickness. Jars lined the shelves containing charms sealed in river-clay. A girl of perhaps twelve sat cross-legged by the stove, painting sigils onto slips of birch bark with a brush made of three hairs and a twig, her tongue between her teeth in concentration.

In Vell, any one of these objects would mean the pyre. Here they hung like laundry.

"You smuggle," Bren said. His voice had gone strange.

"We *trade*," Maren corrected, easing into a chair by the stove. "Charm-wrights in Vell make beautiful useless things and sell them to rich women who want love potions that smell nice. We make things that *work*. There's a market for that, even in a godless city. Especially in a godless city." She nodded at Astrid. "I'd wager our guest knows a bit about hiding a working in plain sight. That pendant you wear isn't just pretty."

Astrid's hand flew to her throat. The storm-charm — the one she'd worn for twenty years, a dull glass bead on a leather cord that everyone assumed was a keepsake. "How—"

"Because I made its twin," Maren said simply. "For a woman named Ilse Marchetti, the winter before the erasure. A suppression charm, the finest I ever worked. She said it was for her daughter." The old woman's eyes were kind and terrible at once. "She paid me in storm-glass. Said her girl had too much thunder in her for one small body, and the world had no patience for thunder anymore."

The room tilted. Astrid sat down hard on a bench without meaning to. Orla moved toward her, but Astrid raised a hand — *wait* — and the healer, to her credit, waited.

Her mother. Her mother had walked into this drowned village, this impossible place that did not exist, and bought the charm that had hollowed Astrid out. Had carried it home through the snow. Had fastened it around her daughter's neck with her own hands, perhaps while saying something gentle, something loving, something about keeping her safe.

And then the Gate. Then the sealing. Then the silence.

"Astrid." Bren's voice, careful. "What is it?"

"Nothing." The lie came automatically, smooth as river-water. But Sefa was looking at her from beside the stove, and the child's eyes were the grey of coming snow, and Astrid heard herself say, "Everything. Give me a moment."

She took the moment. Outside, the wind changed, and the first flakes of the unnatural winter came down through the window-light, fat and slow, falling upward slightly on a thermal off the warm stove. Sefa turned her face to the glass and murmured something too soft to catch, and the snow — Astrid would have sworn it — hesitated, as if listening.

* * *

Supper was fish stew and dark bread and a fermented berry drink that warmed the chest like a small sun. Other villagers came, drawn by the novelty of guests: a broad man who'd been a ferryman before the floods took his route; twin sisters who ran the charm-line south to Vell hidden in barrels of salted eel; a boy who showed Sefa how to fold paper boats and float them in the flooded street below the bridge, where they sailed between doorways that hadn't opened in years.

Astrid sat among them and felt something in her chest unclench, joint by joint, like a fist relaxing after decades.

This was what she had forgotten. Not the magic — she'd told herself for twenty years that what she missed was the power, the storm, the wild bright thing in her blood. But sitting in a smoky room full of strangers who spoke her grandmother's language and ate from the same pot and asked nothing of each other except presence, she understood the theft had been larger than that. They had taken her *people*. The ordinary, daily, unremarkable warmth of belonging somewhere. She had been so busy mourning the lightning she hadn't let herself mourn the hearth.

Orla caught her eye across the room and smiled — a real smile, the first since her family's name had come up in the waystation, since grief had closed her face like a book. "You're crying," the healer said gently.

"It's the smoke."

"It's not the smoke."

"No," Astrid admitted. "It's not the smoke."

Later, when the fire burned low and Sefa slept against Orla's side and Bren stood watch at the window out of a restlessness he probably didn't understand himself, Maren Ostrova beckoned Astrid to her chair by the stove.

The old woman held out her palm. On it lay a charm Astrid hadn't seen before: a ring of braided hair, human hair, silver-grey, wound around a sliver of something dark and glassy. Storm-glass. It hummed faintly against Astrid's senses, the way the map-seal did.

"My mother's?" she whispered.

"Your mother's." Maren closed the old woman's fingers — no. Closed *her own* fingers around it, then pressed it into Astrid's hand. "She left it with me for safekeeping, the night before she went north. She said—" The old woman paused, choosing words with the care of someone handling broken glass. "She said, *if my daughter ever comes this way, tell her the Gate opens both ways, and it always asks.*"

"Asks what?"

"That's all she said. *It always asks.*" Maren's grip tightened on Astrid's wrist, surprisingly strong. "Listen to me, girl. I've sent four parties toward that pass in twenty years. Four. I gave each of them supplies, blessings, and this same warning. None of them came back. Not one. Whatever waits behind that door doesn't want what you're willing to give. It wants what you can't spare."

Outside, the snow thickened against the windows, falling harder now, falling *wrong*, in great silent sheets that muffled the world. Sefa stirred in her sleep and murmured, and the wind outside answered her — bent, briefly, to the shape of her breathing, then resumed its howl.

Astrid closed her hand around her mother's charm and felt, beneath the glass, a heartbeat that was almost her own.

"They'll have followed us," Bren said quietly from the window, not turning. "Wardens don't lose a trail in open country. When the snow lets them see, they'll come."

Maren nodded, unsurprised, unsurprisable. "Then you'd best sleep fast, storm-daughter. Tomorrow I'll give you what my boats can carry and what my mouth can warn." She looked at the shuttered window, at the white dark beyond it, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing.

"And pray the price hasn't gone up since your mother asked."
