## 6. Three Names on a Map

They outran the singing by a night and a half.

Astrid would remember little of it afterward — the blur of white, Bren's hand hauling her up a scree slope when her legs gave out, the way the map's amber pulse slowed and dimmed as they put miles between themselves and the choir. By the time they reached the tree line on the far side of the pass, where black pines stood shoulder to shoulder like mourners, the seal had gone cold again. She told herself that was mercy. She was beginning to suspect it was patience.

The waystation behind them had been a trap waiting to close. They'd left its fire burning in the hearth as a false scent for the Wardens and slipped out through the wood-shed into wind that cut like broken glass. Now, two days later, with frost blooming in Bren's beard and her own fingers too stiff to feel the map's edges, Astrid saw smoke rising ahead — thin, gray, deliberate. Not a chimney's careless plume but a signal fire, banked low, the kind of smoke made by someone who wanted to be found by friends and no one else.

"Could be Wardens," Bren said. He said it every time now. It had become a ritual between them, like a prayer neither believed in.

"Could be," she agreed. "But Wardens don't build their fires small."

* * *

The smoke led them down into a hollow where the pines opened around a frozen pond, and there — absurdly, impossibly — stood a house.

It was less a house than the memory of one: stone walls furred with ice, a roof sagging under snow, windows glowing amber against the blue dusk. A garden lay buried beneath the drifts, but Astrid could see the shapes of raised beds beneath the white, and a trellis, and the bare canes of what might once have been roses. Someone had planted this place intending to stay. Someone had refused, season after season, to believe the winter would win.

The door opened before they knocked.

The woman who filled the doorway was broad as a doorframe herself, gray-haired, with forearms like a blacksmith's and eyes the color of strong tea. She looked at Astrid. Then at Bren. Then at the Warden-issue boots on Bren's feet, and her tea-colored eyes went flat as pond ice.

"He's with me," Astrid said quickly. "He's — it's complicated."

"It always is." The woman stepped aside. "In or out. The cold doesn't wait for explanations, and neither do I."

Inside, the house was warm in a way that felt almost violent after the pass — a peat fire roaring in a stone hearth, dried herbs hanging from every beam, the air thick with the smell of rosemary and woodsmoke and something medicinal, sharp and green. And sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the fire, so still Astrid nearly missed her, was a child.

A girl of perhaps eight or nine, wrapped in a coat three sizes too large, with dark hair cropped short and skin the deep brown of turned earth. She wasn't looking at the fire. She was looking at the window, where frost ferns crawled across the glass, and she was — Astrid realized with a jolt — talking to them. Her lips moved without sound, and where her breath touched the pane, the frost patterns changed. Spirals bloomed. Then, unmistakably, a small figure with stick arms raised in greeting.

"Don't mind Sefa," the woman said, following Astrid's gaze. "She talks to the snow. Snow talks back, far as anyone can tell." She shrugged, as if this were the most ordinary thing in the world, and perhaps in Caelwyn it had been. "I'm Orla. Orla Voss. And you'll be wanting food before talk, because you both look like death rehearsing."

Astrid's throat tightened. That cadence. That particular way of saying *you'll be wanting* — the vowels rounded soft, the r's swallowed. It was the accent of the river valleys. It was her mother's accent. It was an accent that officially did not exist.

"You're from Caelwyn," she said.

Orla's face did something complicated — grief and pride wrestling to a draw. "Born in Millbrook-on-the-Alden, which is now a lake bed if the maps are to be believed, which they aren't, because there are no maps." She ladled something from a pot over the fire — barley and root vegetables and salt meat, steam rising in fragrant clouds. "Sit. Eat. Then tell me why a storm-caller with a hidden brand on her wrist is walking toward the Hollow Gate in the dead of winter with a deserter at her back."

Astrid froze, her sleeve pulled instinctively over the faded mark. "How—"

"The snow told Sefa you were coming two days ago. Said a storm was walking in boots." Orla set the bowls down with a clack. "Snow sees more than people give it credit for. Now eat."

* * *

They ate. And then, because Orla Voss was not a woman who tolerated evasion, they talked.

Astrid gave her the shape of it — the stall in Vell, the dying stranger, the map, the seal that answered her touch. She left out the memories pressing at the edges of her mind like fingers against a curtain; she barely let herself look at those. Bren added what he knew of the Wardens' movements, the manufactured winter, the choir that sang the borderlands shut. Orla listened without interrupting, which Astrid came to understand was her way of honoring a story.

When they finished, Orla was quiet for a long moment, turning her bowl in her hands.

"I lost everyone in the erasure," she said at last, simply, the way one reports weather. "Husband. Two daughters. My whole village went into the winter and I was away gathering herbs when the Wardens came, and when I walked home there was nothing to walk home *to*. No ruins. No ash. Just a valley with a lake in it that nobody remembered digging." Her voice didn't break. It had clearly done all its breaking years ago and come out the other side smooth as river stone. "I spent four years trying to get *in*, if you can believe it. Trying to find the door from the outside. Everyone said the erased were dead. But dead people don't need a door kept shut, do they? You don't lock a grave against the living."

"You think they're alive in there," Astrid said.

"I think the Gate doesn't kill. I think it *keeps*." Orla looked at her, and her tea-colored eyes held twenty years of a grief that had never been allowed a funeral. "And I think whatever's on that map chose you for a reason, storm-caller. So. You'll rest here tonight. Tomorrow I'm coming with you."

Bren shifted. "It's dangerous—"

"Boy, I've been dangerous since before your mother was weaned." Orla's mouth twitched. "Besides. A healer's no use to a village that isn't there anymore. Maybe I'll be some use to one that might be again."

From the corner, Sefa spoke for the first time. Her voice was small and clear as a bell struck in an empty room.

"She comes too."

"Sefa—" Orla began.

"The snow says the storm-lady needs us." The girl turned from the window, and her eyes were old in her young face, dark and unblinking. "The snow says the door remembers her. The snow says—" she frowned, tilting her head as though listening to something very far away, "—the snow says *she put it there*."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled against the cold. Astrid felt the words go through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

"That's nonsense," she said, and heard how thin her own voice sounded. "Childish nonsense. I've never been near the Gate in my life."

Sefa just looked at her, pitying and certain, the way only children and the dying can look at a person. Then she turned back to the window and traced another spiral in the frost, and said nothing more.

* * *

That night Astrid slept badly, and woke before dawn to find the fire burned to embers and Orla awake across the room, mending a coat by candlelight with quick, sure stitches.

"Can't sleep either?" the older woman asked, not looking up.

Astrid sat down across from her. For a while neither spoke. The candle guttered. Outside, real snow fell — gentle, ordinary snow, the kind that meant nothing — and Sefa slept curled against it like a cat against a sunbeam.

"May I see the map?" Orla asked finally.

Astrid hesitated, then drew it out. The wax seal sat dull and cold in the candlelight, and the parchment beneath showed its strange geography: the drowned village, the waystation, and then three marks beyond, each annotated in the dead man's cramped hand. Three waypoints between here and the Hollow Gate.

Orla studied it. "Ruined watchtower here. Frozen shrine here. And the Gate itself." She tapped each mark in turn. "Whoever drew this knew the old pilgrim road. These are the stations my grandmother spoke of — the stops travelers made on the way to the Gate, back when there was a way *to* make."

Something cold slid down Astrid's spine. Slowly, as though her hand belonged to someone else, she took the map back and looked at the first waypoint. The ruined watchtower. *Tor Anselm*, the annotation read.

And memory moved behind the curtain.

Not a flood — not yet — but a pressure. A fragment surfacing like a body rising in a lake: grey stone against a grey sky. Her own small hand, chapped with cold, pressed flat against a wall. Her mother's voice saying *don't look back, Astrid, whatever happens, don't look back.* The taste of iron and snow. A tower. She had stood in a tower. She had been *afraid* in a tower, afraid in a way that lived in the bones rather than the mind—

"Astrid?" Orla's voice, distant. "You've gone white as the pass."

"The second one," Astrid whispered. Her finger moved down the parchment to the second mark. *The Shrine of the Ninefold Spring, frozen these twenty years.* And there — another fragment, breaking the surface: water singing under ice, nine voices braided into one, and herself kneeling at its edge while her father held her shoulders and said words she couldn't yet hear, words that were coming for her, climbing toward her through the dark like the Wardens' choir—

She shoved the map away. It skidded across the floorboards.

"What is it?" Orla had set down her mending. "What did you see?"

"Nothing. Nothing, I—" Astrid pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "These places. I know these places, Orla. Not from stories. From *inside*. Like I've stood in that tower. Like I've knelt at that spring. And I don't remember going, and that's worse than remembering, because things that are forgotten aren't usually forgotten *on purpose*—"

Her voice cracked. In the corner, she heard Sefa stir, and the frost on the window creaked and spread, responding to something in the room that Astrid could not name and dared not examine.

Orla was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was gentle as snowfall.

"My grandmother used to say the Gate was sealed by a child," she said. "A child with a storm in her chest, given up by her own blood to buy the rest of them time. Everyone thought it was just a story. A comfort story — proof that even the smallest among us had been asked to carry something enormous." She reached across and laid her broad, scarred hand over Astrid's trembling ones. "Three waypoints, storm-caller. And three memories knocking at a door inside you that someone locked from the other side."

Astrid looked down at the map, lying where she'd thrown it, its wax seal catching the candlelight. And in the amber depths of the wax — faintly, patiently, like a heartbeat waiting to be counted — something pulsed.

Once. Twice.

Like a knock. Like an answer. Like a door, far away and long shut, beginning — slowly, slowly — to open.
