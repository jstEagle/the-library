## 7. The Second Seal

The watchtower had been a Warden post once, Bren knew. He had stood in others like it, back when the black-and-silver of the Order still meant something to him — back when it had meant everything. This one had been abandoned for twenty years, left to rot at the edge of the frozen pass like a pulled tooth. Its upper floors had collapsed into themselves, and frost furred the broken stonework so that the whole ruin gleamed pale and bone-like against the grey sky.

He felt the wrongness of it before they reached the walls. A pressure behind his eyes, a taste like copper coins on his tongue. Magic, old and patient, waiting.

Astrid stopped at the threshold and would not go further.

She stood with her hand pressed flat against the doorframe, her breath coming fast and shallow, and Bren watched the color drain from her face by degrees, as though something were drinking it out of her. The storm that lived coiled beneath her skin — he had learned to see it now, a faint blue shimmer in her knuckles when she was afraid, a crackle in the air around her hair — that storm was moving. Rising. Answering something inside the tower.

"The seal," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as if she were speaking from very far away. "It knows this place."

"Then we don't go in," Orla said quickly. She had Sefa by the shoulder, pulling the girl close against her side. "Astrid. Listen to me. Whatever's in there, we can go around—"

"There is no around." Astrid's fingers curled against the stone. "The map ends here. The third waypoint is past the pass, and the pass opens only—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Only for the one who closed it."

The words dropped into silence like stones into a well.

Bren looked at her. Really looked. And saw that she was not looking at any of them; she was looking through them, through the wall, through twenty years of carefully constructed nothing, at something only she could see.

"Astrid," he said quietly. "What did you just say?"

"I don't know." Her voice cracked down the middle. "I don't know where that came from. I've never—" The shimmer in her hands flared bright enough that Orla hissed and shielded Sefa's eyes. "It's not mine. It can't be mine. I was six years old."

* * *

They made camp inside the tower's ground floor because the wind outside had teeth, and because none of them could bear to leave Astrid alone, though she sat apart from the fire with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped tight around herself, rocking slightly, like a ship at anchor in bad weather.

Sefa crept toward her first. The child always did — she seemed to know things about grief before grief itself arrived, the way animals know about storms. She settled beside Astrid without touching her and began, softly, to hum. The snow that had drifted in through the broken roof gathered near the girl in small obedient spirals, and Bren thought, not for the first time, that whatever the Wardens had done to Caelwyn, they had failed to understand what they were destroying. Children who spoke to snow. Healers who wept over strangers. Storm-callers selling charms in godless cities.

He was still thinking this when Astrid screamed.

It tore out of her without warning — a sound he had never heard from her, not in ambush, not in grief, not on the ice fields when Orla fell — and the fire leapt three feet high and turned white. The seal, wax-dark on the map she still carried, split open like an eye.

And the memory came home.

Bren saw it happen to her: her spine arching, her eyes rolling back until only white showed, frost blooming across her lips. He lunged for her but the air around her body burned cold, and his hands came away numb to the wrist. He could do nothing. None of them could do anything but watch as twenty years arrived all at once in a body too small to hold them.

Later, when she could speak, she told them. She told them in pieces, in a voice scraped raw, staring into the fire as if it might swallow her if she blinked.

She told them about the night the Wardens came to Caelwyn — not the borderlands, not the villages, but the heart of it, the Hollow Gate itself, the last door out of a country being unwritten. She told them how her parents had carried her up the mountain through the snow, how her mother had knelt in front of her and held her small face in her hands and said, *you are the only one who can, little storm, you are the only one strong enough.* She told them how they had pressed her palms against the Gate's cold surface while the Wardens closed in, how the power had poured out of her like blood from a wound, how she had sealed the door with everyone still inside — every soul who hadn't run fast enough or far enough, sealed behind the winter, alive, alive, alive, but lost.

"They didn't die," she whispered. Her hands were shaking so hard the firelight fractured in her eyes. "My parents didn't sacrifice me to save themselves. They sacrificed *everyone else*. They used their own daughter to lock the door behind us, and then they ran, and then they died anyway, and I forgot. They made me forget. Because they knew what I'd become if I remembered."

No one moved. Sefa had gone very still, her small hand resting on Astrid's knee.

"You were a child," Orla said at last, gently. "Whatever they made you do—"

"I closed the Gate." Astrid's voice rose, wild. "I am the reason my people have been freezing in the dark for twenty years. Every person in that drowned village, every exile on this road — I did that. *Me.* Not the Wardens. Me."

"That's not—" Bren started forward.

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and thunder answered it, rolling over the mountains though the sky held no clouds. Frost raced outward from where she stood, silvering the stones, and the fire guttered and died. In the sudden dark her eyes glowed faintly blue, and Bren understood, finally and completely, what she was. What she had always been. What Vell's charm-stalls and false names had been built to bury.

"You don't get to comfort me," she said to him, quieter now, and infinitely more terrible. "Not you. You held the lanterns that night, didn't you? You told me once you were there. You watched a child walk up that mountain and you *let her*."

He had no answer. There was no answer. He stood in the dark with his guilt rising in his throat like floodwater and let her hate him, because she needed somewhere to put it, and he was the only thing in the room that deserved it.

* * *

The Wardens came an hour before dawn.

Bren heard them first — the particular crunch of disciplined boots on snow, too many, moving in formation. He was on his feet before his mind fully woke, shaking Orla awake, pressing Sefa behind him into the shadow of the collapsed stairwell.

"How many?" Astrid asked. She was already standing. Something had changed in her since the memory — she moved differently, looser, as if some part of her had burned away along with the forgetting.

"Twelve. Maybe more." He peered through the arrow slit. Lantern-light bobbed among the pines below, and at its center, a figure in a commander's white cloak. Ilse Vane. Of course. She had been two steps behind them since the waystation, patient as winter itself. "They're fanning out. They know we're cornered."

"Then we go now, through them—"

"No." He caught her arm. "Listen. They're not here for all of us. Look at the formation — they're leaving the north side open. They want one of us to run." His stomach turned as he understood it. "They want the child."

Sefa.

Snow-speakers were rare. Snow-speakers who could be trained, leashed, turned into instruments of the Order — rarer still. Bren had seen what the Wardens did with children like Sefa. He had helped carry some of them away.

"They'll try to separate us," he said. "Vane will offer each of us a bargain — she always does. Amnesty, absolution, whatever cuts deepest. And when the bargains fail, they'll take what they came for."

The first crossbow bolt shattered against the wall above his head.

What followed happened fast and ugly. The tower erupted — Warden wardens chanting containment circles, blue light lancing through the broken windows, Orla screaming for Sefa to stay down. Bren fought the way deserters fight: dirty, desperate, all elbows and fury. He broke a Warden's nose with his forehead and took his blade and put himself between the north gap and everything he had come to love against his own will.

But they were twelve and he was one, and Astrid's storms were still learning their own shape again, wild and half-blind, striking as much at random as at aim. A containment net caught Orla and dragged her down. Another pinned Bren's arm to the wall, frost crawling up his sleeve—

—and through the chaos, two pairs of hands closed on Sefa's coat.

The girl screamed, and every snowdrift within a mile rose at once.

Bren tore free of the net with the frost eating into his flesh and threw himself across the gap. He got one arm around Sefa's waist as the Wardens hauled; for one horrible suspended moment she stretched between them like a wishbone, her small boots skidding on ice, her eyes finding Astrid's across the burning room—

*Astrid.*

She had gone utterly still. And in that stillness Bren saw her make her choice — saw her stop fighting the storm and *become* it. Lightning walked down her arms like living things. The temperature plummeted. Every lantern died, every ward-circle flickered, and the ruined tower filled with a light like the inside of a falling star.

"*Let. Her. Go.*"

The Wardens let her go. Most of them were thrown clear of the walls entirely. The rest fled into the trees after their commander's retreating white cloak, and the silence that followed rang like a struck bell.

Bren knelt in the wreckage with Sefa clutched against his chest, her heart hammering against his ribs, and looked up at Astrid Marchetti — storm-caller, gate-sealer, the woman who had spent twenty years pretending to be no one.

She was crying. The lightning still crawled along her arms, and she was crying, and she had never looked more powerful or more lost.

"The third waypoint," she said, when she could speak. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, leaving streaks of soot. "It's not a place on the map. It never was." She looked east, toward the pass, toward the mountain where a six-year-old girl had once locked a door on ten thousand souls. "It's me. It was always me. And Vane knows it — that's why she wants me alive."

Above them, dawn broke grey and cold over the pass, and somewhere in the high snowfields, the Hollow Gate waited for its keeper to come home.
