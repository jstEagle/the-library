## 8. The Long Climb

The pass began where the trees gave up.

Astrid felt it before she saw it — a thinning in the air, a hush that pressed against her ears like deep water. The pines of the borderlands had been dwindling for hours, shorter and more twisted, until at last there was only stone: a grey stairway of ridges rising into cloud, cut long ago by hands that had wanted a road and had not cared what it cost. The map called it the Serpent's Throat. The old woman in the drowned village had called it the last honest mile, because beyond it, nothing in Caelwyn had ever been honest again.

Orla walked in front of her, staff in hand, breath smoking in white ribbons. Sefa rode on Bren's shoulders with her mittened fists knotted in his collar, and every few minutes the child would tilt her head and say something quiet to the snow — *not yet, not yet* — as if negotiating with it. Bren said nothing at all. He had not said much since the waystation, since the confession Astrid still carried in her chest like a swallowed stone. He simply climbed, and carried the child, and watched the ridgeline behind them the way a man watches a wound he knows will open.

The cold was wrong. Astrid had known cold all her life — the honest cold of storms, of wet wool and woodsmoke, of breath freezing on a scarf. This was not that. This cold came from below and above at once, and it had *intent* in it. The snow did not drift; it marched. It lay in ridges like furrows, like something plowed, and where their boots broke the crust, the frost healed over behind them in seconds, smooth and seamless, as though the mountain were erasing them.

"The Wardens are pushing the winter up the pass," Bren said at the first rest, crouched with his hand flat against the stone. "They're herding it. Anyone trying for the Gate has to climb into the teeth of it."

"Can they do that?" Ola asked. "Hold a winter like a net?"

"They held one over a whole country for twenty years." Bren did not look at her when he said it. "This is nothing. This is a man flexing his fingers."

Astrid said nothing. She was listening to the thing in her bones.

It had been waking for days now — the storm, the one she had traded away as a child and never wholly lost, the ember of it that had made her palms itch in the market at Vell whenever thunder rolled in from the sea. But here, under the manufactured weight of the Warden winter, it was not an ember anymore. It was a fist, and it was opening finger by finger, and every step toward the Gate loosened another.

She was afraid of it. She was more afraid of what would happen if she never used it.

* * *

They saw the riders at dusk on the second day.

Black figures on the switchbacks below, small as ink-mites against the white, moving with the terrible patience of people who knew their quarry had nowhere to go. Bren counted them with a spyglass he'd taken from a dead man's pack and passed it to Astrid without comment.

"Six," she said. "And one on foot ahead of them. Walking like she owns the mountain."

"Vane," Bren said, and the name came out of him flat and heavy, like a coin dropped on a table.

Astrid had heard the name twice before — once from the exiles in the drowned village, spoken in the tone people used for plagues, and once from Bren, in the waystation, in a voice so carefully empty that she had understood it was the fullest voice he had. *Ilse Vane. The woman who wrote the order. The woman who read it aloud.* Twenty years ago, a country had been erased from every record, and someone had to hold the pen. Vane had held it, and then she had spent two decades making sure no one dug up what was buried.

"She's not sending soldiers up first," Orla said slowly. "She's *coming herself*. Why would she — "

"Because it's not a hunt," Astrid said. The understanding arrived cold and complete, the way the worst understandings do. "It's a negotiation. She thinks I can still open the Gate. She wants to be there when I decide."

They climbed through the dark after that, because stopping meant letting the winter-net close. Sefa slept against Bren's back and murmured to the snow in her sleep, and the snow, for a wonder, seemed to listen — their trail stayed broken, their sheltered hollows stayed a degree warmer than they had any right to be. Orla hummed an old Caelwynn lullaby under her breath, one Astrid hadn't heard since she was small enough to be carried, and the melody did more against the cold than any charm.

Near dawn, in a hollow beneath an overhang of blue ice, Vane's voice found them.

It came from below, flung up the pass on some Warden engine of sound, so clear and reasonable that it might have been a woman standing at Astrid's shoulder.

"Astrid Marchetti." The name, her true name, spoken without hatred. That was the worst of it. "I know you're awake. I know you can hear me. So hear this."

A pause, long enough for the wind to fill.

"You were seven years old. You didn't seal the Gate — your parents sealed it *through* you, and they are dead for it, and I have never blamed a child for what her parents made her carry. I don't blame you now." Another pause, softer. "The people inside are dead, Astrid. They have been dead for twenty years. What's in that winter is not your mother and father. It is not your neighbors. It is cold wearing their shapes, and if you open that door, it will pour out into a country of people who remember nothing, and it will not stop this time with one small girl and one small storm.

"Let it die. Walk away. I offer you amnesty — full amnesty, papers, a name, a house in Vell with a garden. No more running. No more hiding charms in a godless city. You can be *no one*, safely, for the rest of your life." The voice gentled to almost nothing. "Or you can climb to that door and spend everything you have to open a grave. I will not stop you. The mountain will."

The voice ended. The wind resumed. And in the hollow under the ice, four people sat in silence, each of them looking anywhere but at Astrid.

It was Sefa who spoke, small and certain, her breath fogging the dark. "She's wrong. The snow says they're *waiting*. Not dead. Waiting is different."

"Sefa — " Orla began.

"Waiting is different," the child said again, with the flat finality of the very young, and rolled back into Bren's cloak and slept.

Astrid lay awake beside the banked fire, her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, feeling the storm turn over like something dreaming. *Amnesty.* A garden. A life with the sharp edges sanded off. She thought of her stall in Vell, of the harmless little trinkets, of twenty years spent being so carefully no one — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Vane had not offered her mercy at all. She had offered her the same bargain her parents had accepted on her behalf when she was seven. *Be small. Be silent. Be safe. Let everyone else be the price.*

She had been seven. She was not seven now.

* * *

The ambush came at the third waypoint, where the map showed a cairn and the cairn had been toppled — deliberately, recently, its stones scattered like teeth.

It happened fast, the way the worst things do. One moment they were picking their way across a shelf of wind-scoured rock; the next, three Wardens in grey rose out of a drift that had no business hiding a man, and the world went to noise and white.

Bren moved first — she would give him that, always, in whatever account she kept of him. He put himself between the nearest Warden and Sefa and took a blade across the forearm doing it, and then the two of them went down together into the snow, and Astrid lost sight of them entirely.

She saw the fourth Warden. The one who had flanked wide, who came up the shelf from the right with his crossbow leveled — not at her, not at Bren.

At Sefa.

The child was standing in the open, snow whirling around her in a small obedient spiral, too startled even to scream. And Astrid watched the bolt leave the string, watched it begin its short flat journey across ten feet of freezing air, and everything in her went very quiet and very old.

Orla got there first.

The healer did not run so much as *arrive*, the way the dying say the dead arrive, impossibly, between one heartbeat and the next. She put her body between the bolt and the child the way she had put her hands between sickness and the sick for forty years, without ceremony, without hesitation, because it was where she stood and it was what she was.

The bolt took her high in the chest.

Astrid did not remember crossing the distance. She remembered Orla's weight coming into her arms, the staff clattering on stone, the smell of blood and rosemary — the healer had carried rosemary sprigs in her pocket since the drowned village, *for the memory*, she'd said, though her memory was the one thing that had never needed keeping. She remembered Orla's hand finding hers and gripping it with a strength that had no right to be there.

"The door," Orla said. Blood at the corner of her mouth, and her eyes — her eyes were not on the sky or the pain or the child she'd saved. They were fixed on the pass above, on the notch of black stone where the map ended and the Gate began, and they were *bright*. "Astrid. Listen to me. The door is real. I lost everyone to that winter and I never — I never once stopped believing the door was real. Don't you dare let them make you believe it isn't."

"Orla — "

"Open it." Her grip tightened. "Open it, and tell them — tell them Orla of the Low Fens kept the faith. Tell them — "

And then, in the manner of the dying, she was simply not there. The hand went slack. The bright eyes dimmed to something looking at a far wall. The wind took her last breath and carried it up the pass, toward the Gate, like a message sent ahead.

The remaining Wardens were already falling back — Bren had broken the ambush, or the ambush had done its work, or Vane below on her switchback had called them off with a raised hand, satisfied. It didn't matter. Nothing on the mountainside mattered.

Astrid knelt in the snow with the dead healer in her arms, and the grief did not come as tears. It came as *weather*.

It started in her chest, in the old sealed place, in the room her parents had locked when she was seven and told her never, never to open. The grief hit that door like a gale hits a shutter. Once. Twice. And on the third blow, the door — the seal, the wax and the word and the twenty years of being small — did not break.

It *melted*.

The storm came out of her the way light comes through a shattered window: all at once, from everywhere, blinding. Astrid threw back her head and the sky answered — clouds boiling up out of a clear dawn, purple-black, gravid, the first true storm Caelwyn's borderlands had seen in twenty years. Lightning walked the ridgeline. Thunder cracked the overhang of blue ice above the toppled cairn and brought it down in glittering ruin. The wind rose off the mountain in a spiral around her kneeling body, lifting her hair, lifting the snow, lifting Orla's cloak like a banner, and the Warden winter — the plowed, marching, intentional cold — met that wind and *broke against it*, furrow after furrow collapsing into ordinary, honest, mindless snow.

Sefa stood in the middle of it, laughing, snow whirling around her like a flock of delighted birds. Bren stared from the bloodied rocks with his ruined arm pressed to his chest, and on his face was not fear but something like awe, and something like grief, and something like the end of a long penance beginning at last.

Astrid rose. The storm rose with her.

Below, far down the switchbacks, a single figure in grey stood motionless in the wind, watching. Ilse Vane did not retreat. She simply looked up at the woman standing crowned in lightning at the top of her pass, and Astrid felt the look like a hand on her face, and heard — not with her ears — the commander's voice one last time:

*So it's to be the door, then. Come and see what's behind it.*

Astrid took Orla's staff from the snow. She pointed it up the pass, at the black notch where the map ended, and the storm leaned forward around her like a hound taking scent.

"Then we go open it," she said, and climbed.
