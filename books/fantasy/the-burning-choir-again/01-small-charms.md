## 1. Small Charms

The lanterns of the Night Market came up at dusk like a fever breaking across Vespergate's skin — amber along the Tanner's Row, green in the fishmongers' quarter where they said the light kept the flies honest, and one thin blue flame outside the tea house that nobody lit and nobody ever put out. Wendell Ishikawa arrived with the first of them, as they always did, carrying a lacquered tray that looked like it held sweets.

It did not hold sweets.

Each bottle was no longer than a thumb, blown glass the color of drowned things: river-green, dusk-violet, the pale gold of candlelight through honey. Inside each one curled something small and warm and stolen. A memory. Bottled, stoppered with wax, sealed with a sigil that read, to any warden who cared to look, *harmless charm — sentiment only*. The sigil was good work. Wendell had paid a forger three months' earnings for it, and had never once regretted the price.

Magic was a hanging crime in Vespergate. Everyone knew the shape of the gallows behind the Hall of Judgment; children played beneath it without looking up, the way you learn not to look at the sun. But charms were permitted. Charms were *comforts*, sold openly, taxed politely. A widow could buy a vial of her wedding day and press it to her breastbone and feel, for an hour, the weight of a hand on hers. That wasn't magic, the city said. That was grief management.

That it was also theft — that every vial on Wendell's tray had been lifted from some sleeping skull in some dark room over the past year — was a fact Wendell kept folded up very small and very far away, the way they kept everything.

"Evening, Ishikawa." Old Maren from the pickle stall, already three cups into his cider. "Anything new?"

"Something soft," Wendell said. "Rain on a tin roof. Someone humming while they cook."

Maren bought it without haggling, because he always did, because what he actually wanted was the feeling of a kitchen he'd never described to anyone, and Wendell had learned years ago which stalls belonged to people whose kitchens had gone quiet. He pressed the glass to his temple, sighed like a man setting down a heavy load, and shuffled back to his barrels wearing someone else's contentment like a borrowed coat.

Wendell counted the coin. Careful. Always careful. Never more than twelve bottles shown at once, never the same corner twice in a week, never a memory taken twice from the same head — a harvested mind went thin, went *gray*, and gray minds drew wardens the way open wounds drew flies.

*Careful* was the whole of the religion Wendell practiced. They had no other gods, no family name they could trace further back than the foundling ledger at Saint Verity's, no memories at all before the age of eight. The sisters there had called it a sickness of the blood, this blankness. Wendell had learned to call it nothing at all. You could not mourn a room you had never seen. You could not miss a door you had never walked through.

So: the tray, the wax, the sigil, the smile that showed exactly enough teeth. Twelve bottles. Eleven by the time the moon cleared the wall.

* * *

The trouble came at the hour when the market thinned and the lantern oil burned low, when the buyers left were the ones who came late on purpose, so no one would see them buying comfort.

She was maybe fifty, dressed in mourning grays gone soft with washing, hands knotted around a purse she didn't open for a long time. She stood at the edge of Wendell's blanket and looked at the bottles the way people look at graves.

"For remembering," she said finally. "Not — I don't want anything grand. Just something of a voice."

"I have voices," Wendell said. "A mother singing to a child. An old man telling a joke badly. Two sisters arguing about bread."

"The mother." Her breath caught on the word. "The mother singing."

Wendell lifted the little green bottle, and the woman paid without counting, and pressed it to her temple the way they all did, eyes closing, lips parting —

— and then she made a sound Wendell had never heard a customer make. Not the sigh of pleasure, not the small wet gasp of grief satisfied. This was a *recognition*. A sound like a key turning in a lock that hadn't been opened in twenty years.

"That's her," the woman whispered. Her eyes were still shut. Tears ran straight down through the creases. "That's my mother. That's her voice, that's the song she sang when the fever was in our street and we all slept in one bed — *that is her*." Her eyes opened, and they were terrible, and they were fixed on Wendell with a gratitude so vast it had teeth. "How did you — where did you find her? Is she alive? Do you know where —"

"She's dead," Wendell said. It came out flat before they could soften it. "I'm sorry. I don't know her. I take these from —"

From where? From dreams, they told customers. From old houses, old letters, the residue of lives. Lies worn smooth as river stones from handling.

"— from wherever they're left behind," Wendell finished.

But the woman was pressing the bottle to her heart now, both hands, bowing over it like it was a relic, and she said, "Then she isn't. Not all of her. You've given me back the part that didn't burn." And she thanked them. She *thanked* them, pressing coins into their hand far too many, and walked away into the blue-lantern dark clutching a dead woman's voice against her chest, singing under her breath a song she was learning again note by note.

Wendell stood alone among the folding blankets and the guttering lights and did not move for a long time.

Here was the arithmetic they had always refused to do: the woman's joy was real. The comfort was real. Somewhere a skull had been lighter by one song, and here was a daughter made whole by its weight. Perhaps — perhaps the theft was a kind of rescue. Perhaps Wendell was less a thief than a ferryman, carrying lost things across to the living.

But the memory had come from a house on Coopers' Lane, three weeks ago, third floor, a man asleep alone with the window open. Wendell remembered the taking perfectly — the stillness of the spell, the silver thread of it lifting out of the sleeper's temple and coiling into the bottle like smoke poured backward. The man had frowned in his sleep. He had murmured something. He had reached, in his dream, for whatever was being carried out of him, and his hand had closed on empty air.

He would wake tomorrow and feel a smooth place where the song used to be, and he would not know what was missing, only that something was, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

Comfort built on theft. Comfort *was* theft. There was no ferryman. There was only Wendell, and the tray, and eleven bottles, and the small cold knowledge that they had just watched their own crime love someone.

They packed up early. Their hands were steady. Their hands were always steady. That was the worst of it — that nothing in them shook.

* * *

They took the long way home, along the inner face of the Wall, because the Wall was honest. Forty feet of black Vespergate granite, older than the kingdom, older than the crown, and set into it every hundred paces a ward-stone the size of a coffin lid, carved with spirals that predated every alphabet taught in the city schools. The stones hummed. You couldn't hear it so much as feel it, a note below hearing, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar.

Everyone knew the wards kept the dark out. What lived beyond the Wall — whether wolves or worse, whether anything at all — was a question mothers used to frighten children with, and the crown had spent forty years and a fortune keeping the answer on the other side of the stone.

Wendell paused, as they sometimes did, at the ward-stone nearest the Tanner's gate. Put a palm flat against it. It was warm tonight. Warmer than usual, almost like skin, almost like a brow.

"Hungry?" they murmured to it, because talking to the Wall was harmless madness, the sort of thing no warden would arrest you for.

The stone hummed. And for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat they would spend the rest of their short life trying to explain away — the hum answered. It rose in pitch, quickened, *leaned toward them* the way a dog leans toward a familiar hand, and somewhere deep in the granite Wendell felt something turn over, vast and slow, like a sleeper recognizing a voice.

Wendell snatched their hand back. Stood in the empty street with their heart going like a trapped bird, telling themselves it was the cider Maren had pressed on them, the late hour, the woman and her dead mother's song still rattling loose inside their chest.

Above the Wall, the stars were out. Hundreds of them, the cold ordinary constellations that had hung over Vespergate since before it had a name. Wendell checked them out of habit — careful people checked exits and stars — and found them all where they should be.

All but one.

There was a gap in the northern sky, a hole the size of a coin, where a star had certainly been last week. Wendell stood a long time looking at it, telling themselves it was cloud, it was haze, it was their eye, it was anything.

Behind them, at the far end of the street, a bell began to ring. Not the watch-bell, not the temple bells. The great cracked bell in the Hall of Judgment tower, the one that rang only for one thing, and had not rung in forty years.

The wards are falling, said the bell, in the language of iron.

The starless patch of sky widened, very slightly, like an eye opening.

And Wendell Ishikawa ran.
