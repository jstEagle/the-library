## 2. The Night Without Stars

The last charm of the night was a lie about rain.

Wendell held it up to the lantern so the customer could see it properly: a thumb-sized vial of green glass, stoppered with wax, the memory inside swimming like a minnow caught in oil. It looked like nothing. That was the point. In Vespergate, magic was a hanging crime, and so everything magical had to look like trinkets — like something a grandmother might buy at a festival and forget in a drawer.

"Rain on a tin roof," Wendell said. "A whole afternoon of it. You'll feel it behind your eyes when you warm the glass. Good for sleeping. Good for forgetting."

"Forgetting," the woman repeated. She was a fishwife with cracked knuckles and a shawl that had been mended more times than Wendell could count, and she held the vial like it might bite her. "That's what they're for, then? Forgetting?"

"That's what they're for."

She paid in copper, three coins counted out one at a time, each one examined under the lantern's light as though Wendell might have shaved them. The night market of Saltrow Street went on around them — oyster smoke, fried dough, a fiddler playing badly two stalls down — and no one looked twice at the little table with its rows of colored glass. Trinkets. Charms. Harmless.

Wendell smiled the smile they'd practiced for eleven years, took the coins, and hated themselves a little more thoroughly than usual.

* * *

They had learned the trade young, though not, they sometimes thought, as young as they claimed. There was a hole in Wendell's history, smooth-edged and painless, like the place where a tooth used to be. They knew they'd come to Vespergate at seven years old. They knew the orphanage on Tanner's Lane, the matron with her ring of keys, the first stolen memory — a taste of honey cake lifted off a baker's boy for a crust of bread. Everything before the age of seven was a white room. Not painful. Just absent. Everyone had gaps, Wendell told themselves. Most people simply didn't build their livelihoods inside them.

The craft itself was simple enough to be obscene. Every strong feeling left a residue — grief, joy, terror, the smell of a particular kitchen — and a person with the knack could draw that residue out through the fingertips and seal it in glass. The donor forgot, mostly. Not the event, never the event, but the *heat* of it. A widow would remember her husband's funeral and feel only the shape of it, like reading someone else's letter. Wendell bottled the heat and sold it to strangers who wanted to feel something real without paying the price of having lived it.

It was theft. Wendell knew it was theft. They'd built an entire careful life on top of that knowledge the way you build a house over a sinkhole — quickly, quietly, and without looking down.

Eleven years in Vespergate. Eleven years of selling rain and first kisses and mothers' lullabies to people who needed them more than their original owners did. That was the arithmetic Wendell did every night, lying awake in the rented room above the chandler's shop. *They weren't using it. I gave it somewhere it mattered.* Some nights the arithmetic worked. Most nights it didn't.

* * *

The market was thinning when the woman came back.

Wendell noticed her because she wasn't browsing. She stood at the edge of the lantern light with the green vial in her fist, and she was crying — not loudly, not the way beggars cried for coin, but the terrible silent way, tears dropping straight off her chin onto the cobblestones.

"You said rain," she managed, when Wendell beckoned her closer. "You said it was rain."

"It is rain. Warm the glass and—"

"It's my mother." She thrust the vial at Wendell as if it were evidence, as if Wendell were a magistrate who could be made to see reason. "It's her voice. Singing. She used to sing while she gutted fish, she sang the same song every morning, and I haven't heard it since I was nine years old because she died, and this—" Her hand shook. The memory inside swam faster, agitated, like a bird against a window. "This is *her*. How do you have her? Who sold her to you? Did *she* sell herself? Was she so poor she—"

"I don't know," Wendell said, and it came out too fast, too true. "I buy from brokers. I never— I don't always know where they come from."

"Then find out." She pressed the vial back into Wendell's palm and closed their fingers over it, her grip fierce and fish-cold. "You keep it. You keep it until you know whose it is, and then you give it back. Do you hear me? Whatever you are, whatever this is—" her voice dropped to a whisper that was worse than shouting, "—you give people back what you took."

She walked away into the crowd, and Wendell sat very still with the vial in their hand, feeling the mother's song move faintly against their skin like a pulse.

Around them the market laughed and haggled and smelled of frying oil, and Wendell understood, with the slow vertigo of a floor giving way, that every vial on their table was a voice. A morning. A person. Comfort built on theft, stacked in neat rows, priced at three coppers.

They were still holding the vial when the sky broke.

* * *

There was no warning. Later, everyone in Vespergate would tell the story differently — some said there was a smell of hot metal, some said the dogs went mad at dusk — but Wendell remembered only the sound.

It began high above the harbor, a single clear note like a wineglass about to sing, and then every ward-stone along the city wall answered it at once. Wendell felt it in their teeth. Felt it in the charms — all forty vials on the table shivering, chiming, the memories inside pressing against their glass like drowning things. And then the sound *broke*, all together, a crack that ran around the entire circuit of the walls, and the night went wrong.

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — every one, in the same instant, as if a hand had passed over the sky and pinched them like candle flames. Where the constellations had been there was nothing, a black so total it seemed solid, and the moon — three days past full, fat and silver over the harbor not a breath before — was simply gone, leaving a circle of deeper dark where it had been.

For three heartbeats, Saltrow Street was silent.

Then Vespergate screamed.

Chairs overturned. Stalls collapsed as vendors fled. Somewhere a child wailed its lungs empty, and somewhere else a man began reciting the Litany of the Veil in a voice cracking with terror, because everyone in the city knew what the wards meant and what their failure meant: forty years of safety, gone between one breath and the next. The things beyond the border — the things the wards had been raised against before anyone living could remember — were no longer being kept out. Or down. Or asleep.

Wendell's hands were shaking so hard they couldn't close the stall. They swept the vials into their satchel by feel, glass clicking against glass, and the memories inside were *screaming* now, or seemed to be, a chorus of borrowed terrors rattling in their bottles.

"Magic," someone hissed nearby, and Wendell froze. "It's the witchery — the wards were witchery, and it's failed, it's turned on us—"

"They'll hang someone for this," another voice said. "They'll hang whoever did it."

Wendell pulled their hood low and moved with the fleeing crowd, one more body in the dark, one more frightened citizen with nothing in their pockets but trinkets. The streets were chaos — torches bobbing, wardens' whistles shrilling uselessly, a bell in the Temple district tolling the alarm with a cracked, frantic rhythm. Overhead the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

And that was when the stranger stepped out of an alley directly into Wendell's path.

* * *

Everyone else was running toward the Temple, toward the keep, toward anywhere with walls and light. The stranger was walking *against* the current, unhurried, hooded, and small — small enough that Wendell thought at first it was a child, until it spoke in a voice like dry leaves.

"Wendell Ishikawa."

Wendell's whole body went cold. No one used that name. No one *knew* that name — they'd invented the surname themselves at fourteen, lifted off a broken shipping crate, and only the chandler's shopkeeper had ever heard it, and he was deaf and seventy.

"I think you've mistaken me for—"

The stranger's hand shot out — quick, certain, terribly gentle — and pressed something into Wendell's palm. A coin. But warm. Blood-warm, fresh-from-the-body warm, and heavier than any coin Wendell had ever held, and through the skin of their fingers they felt it *humming* with the same note the wards had sung just before they broke.

"The wards are falling," the stranger said, "because what fed them has run dry. Forty years, and the well is empty, and the thing on the throne is drinking faster than the city can bleed." The hood tilted. Beneath it there was no face Wendell could see — only shadow, and two points of reflected torchlight where eyes should be. "You of all people should understand feeding a lie with other people's lives."

"I don't know what you're talking about." Wendell tried to close their fist around the coin, to push it back, and found their hand wouldn't obey. "I sell charms. Glass. Nothing. Please, I'm nobody—"

"You were somebody once." The leaf-voice softened, and somehow that was worse. "You were somebody, and they took it, and you've walked around the hole your whole life without daring to look down. Haven't you. Haven't you wondered why you can't remember anything before you were seven?"

The crowd streamed past them. The bells tolled. The sky pressed down, starless and blind, and Wendell stood in the middle of the ruined night unable to move or speak, because the stranger had found the exact shape of the wound Wendell had spent eleven years pretending wasn't there.

"Your name isn't Wendell," the stranger said. "That's the coat you put on over it. Your true name is—"

And here the strangest thing happened, the thing Wendell would spend the next day turning over and over like a stone with something alive beneath it: the stranger spoke a word, a short word, two syllables, plain as bread —

— and Wendell *knew* it. Knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, deep and instant and undeniable, knew it belonged to them, had always belonged to them, was the sound their mother had called across a room in a house they could not remember in a life they could not remember —

— and the moment it ended, it was gone. Slid out of the mind like water through fingers. Wendell opened their mouth to repeat it and produced nothing. A blank. A white room.

"There," the stranger said quietly. "Now you know what they took. Now you know how much."

"What—" Wendell's voice came out shredded. "What was it? Say it again. *Say it again.*"

"It won't stay. Names that are stolen can't be carried — only returned." The stranger stepped backward into the alley, into the dark, and the torchlight bent strangely around them, as if reluctant to follow. "The coin will show you the way. Under the city. Follow it down, Wendell Ishikawa, and come meet the people who remember who you are."

"Wait — who *are* you? What's happening to the sky? What's—"

But the alley was empty. Not recently-empty, not footsteps-fading empty — *empty*, the way a room is empty when no one has stood in it for years, and the dust on the cobbles lay undisturbed except for one set of prints that stopped mid-stride, as if their owner had simply ceased.

In Wendell's palm, the blood-warm coin pulsed once, twice, like a second heart.

And then it rolled — of its own accord, gently, insistently — out of their open hand, across their wrist, off their fingers, and onto the cobblestones, where it spun, settled, and began to roll south. Toward the river. Toward the old cistern gates below the Tanner's Lane bridge.

Toward *down*.

Behind Wendell, the city tore itself apart with fear. Ahead, the coin glinted once in the torchlight and slipped into the shadows, patient as a debt.

Wendell looked at the satchel of stolen voices on their shoulder. Looked at the starless wound in the sky. Thought of a fishwife's cold hands closing their fingers over a dead mother's song — *you give people back what you took* — and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the floor of their careful life open all the way down.

They followed the coin.
