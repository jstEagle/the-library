## 3. The Choir in the Cisterns

The coin was still warm when Wendell woke with it clenched in their fist.

They had slept — if it could be called sleep — fully dressed on the cot above their stall, shutters barred, the little glass bottles of their inventory wrapped in wool against the tremors that kept running through the floor. All night Vespergate had made noises Wendell had never heard it make: bells rung out of order, dogs howling in packs, and beneath it all a low grinding moan from the walls themselves, as if the city were a jaw trying to remember how to close.

The coin sat in their palm like a live coal gone cool. It was not any currency Wendell knew. No king's face, no mint-mark. Just a single word struck into the metal in letters worn nearly smooth:

*DOWN.*

And around the rim, so fine you had to tilt it toward the lamp to see it, a thread of script that hurt to read — not because it was small, but because it seemed to squirm away from the eye, the way a memory squirmed away when you tried to hold it too hard.

Wendell had spent eleven years being careful. Careful meant never selling the same flavor of charm twice to the same street. Careful meant bottling only borrowed feelings — a widow's contentment, a baker's pride in his bread — never anything that could be traced to a living, complaining source. Careful meant knowing every warden's route by heart and being somewhere else when the hanging drums sounded.

Careful did not mean following strange coins into holes in the ground.

But the stranger had said their name.

Not *Wendell*. The other one. The one that had landed in their ear like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known their chest possessed, and left something rattling loose inside ever since. Wendell had turned the sound over all night, tasting it, and found nothing behind it — no face, no mother's voice saying it across a kitchen, no teacher scolding it in a schoolyard. A name with no history attached was worse than no name at all. It was a door painted on a wall.

So, an hour before dawn, Wendell pulled their coat on, hid their most valuable charms in the false bottom of their satchel, and went down.

* * *

The way beneath Vespergate began where the lamplight ended.

There was a cistern-gate in the Foundry Quarter that every child knew and no adult admitted to: an iron door set flush into the canal wall, rusted shut for as long as anyone could say. Wendell had walked past it ten thousand times. Tonight the rust fell away at a touch, flakes crumbling like burnt paper, and the door swung inward on hinges freshly oiled.

The coin tugged. Not physically — Wendell checked twice, holding it up, watching it hang dead and ordinary in the dark. But something behind their sternum leaned toward the open gate the way a plant leans toward light, and Wendell had survived this long by trusting the body's knowledge over the mind's objections.

Stairs. Then more stairs. The air changed as they descended — canal-stink giving way to cold stone and clean water and, faintly, impossibly, the smell of beeswax and old song. Someone had been living down here a long time.

The last stair opened onto a gallery above a flooded cistern vast enough to swallow the Foundry whole. Pillars rose out of black water in ranks, their capitals lost in darkness overhead, and the surface of the water lay so still it held reflections like a second ceiling. Along the gallery edge, lamps burned behind colored glass — red, blue, amber — and by their light Wendell saw that the far shore of the underground lake was lined with scaffolding, benches, music stands built from broken cart-wheels and chair legs.

And people. Perhaps thirty of them, wrapped in mismatched coats, tuning instruments or flexing their hands or simply sitting in the particular silence of people waiting for something they dread and need in equal measure.

"Mind the third step," said a voice at Wendell's elbow. "It lies."

Wendell flinched, hand going to the knife under their coat. A woman stood there — sixty maybe, iron-gray hair cropped short, a long scar splitting one eyebrow. She wore a warden's greatcoat with the badges ripped off, the torn threads still visible, and she looked at Wendell the way wardens looked at suspects: patiently, as if everyone confessed eventually.

"You're the one Sefa sent the coin to," she said. "Ishikawa."

"That's not—" Wendell stopped. Swallowed. "How do you know my name?"

"Same way I know mine." She shrugged the ruined coat higher on her shoulders. "Brava. Formerly Warden-Captain Brava, formerly of the Third Gate watch, currently of nowhere in particular. You're late. She hates that." She turned and walked along the gallery without checking whether Wendell followed, which was either trust or a trap, and Wendell — cursing every careful instinct they owned — followed.

The gallery curved down to the waterline, where a flotilla of flat barges ferried them across the mirror-still lake. Wendell stared over the edge and saw, far below the surface, pale shapes like drowned statues — older cistern levels, older cities, Vespergate stacked on its own bones. The nearer they came to the far shore, the more Wendell felt the coin's pull become a pressure, a hand flat between their shoulder blades.

On the shore, the choir waited.

Up close they resolved into individuals: a hedge-priest with ink-stained fingers and a cassock patched with three different fabrics; a girl of perhaps nine sitting cross-legged on a crate, utterly silent, drawing in a slate book with fierce concentration; a fisherman missing two fingers; a dyer whose arms were stained blue to the elbow; a dozen others whose trades showed in their hands. Outcasts. The people Vespergate stepped around.

At the center, standing on a crate as if it were a pulpit, was a tall woman with silver threading her braids and eyes the color of deep water. When she saw Wendell, something moved through the crowd — not quite a murmur, more a settling, the way a congregation settles when the doors open.

"Wendell Ishikawa," the woman said. Her voice carried across the water without effort, the way good voices do in stone places. "Bottler. Charm-seller. Eleven years in the night market, and in all that time not one warden's report, not one complaint, not one mistake." She smiled, and it was not entirely kind. "You are very good at being small. I'm Sefa. I lead this choir. And I'm sorry to tell you that your talent for smallness is about to be wasted."

"I don't know what you think I am," Wendell said. "I sell trinkets. Feelings in glass. Nothing that—"

"Bottled memories are memories," Sefa said. "Thinned, sweetened, made harmless — but the substance doesn't change. Substance is what the wards ate, Wendell. For forty years, the border wards of this city have been fed on harvested memory, and whoever fed them took yours when you were a child. Last night the feeding stopped. Last night the stars went out." She let that sit in the cold air. "Your stolen years are the keystone of the fallen wards. We can feel it from here. Can't you?"

And the terrible thing — the thing Wendell would carry out of that cistern like a splinter — was that they could. Now that it was named, they could feel it: a hollow ache behind the breastbone shaped exactly like the coin's pull, a sense of being a hook upon which something enormous had once hung.

"I want my money back," Wendell said, because it was the only sentence their mouth could find. "Whatever this is. Whatever you think I owe. I didn't ask for—"

"No," Sefa agreed gently. "You didn't. None of us did." She gestured, and the crowd shifted, and Wendell saw what they'd been arranged around: a low stone basin at the water's edge, filled with clear water, ringed with candles. "But asking isn't how belonging works. Belonging is what's left after everything you chose has been taken away. Look in the basin. Anselm will show you. He'll show you what was taken, if you let him. Then you can decide whether to walk back up those stairs."

"And if I do walk?"

Sefa's eyes didn't waver. "Then we grieve you and sing without you. But you won't walk. Nobody who looks in the basin walks."

* * *

The hedge-priest — Anselm, ink-stained, soft-spoken — asked permission three times before he touched Wendell's temples, and Wendell, hating themselves, gave it.

What happened next was not like selling a charm. Selling was theft at arm's length: skim the feeling, decant it, seal it, done. This was an opening. Anselm's fingers pressed cool against Wendell's skin and the world folded sideways, and suddenly Wendell stood inside a memory that was not theirs and was theirs, both at once —

— a room with yellow walls. Rain on a window. A woman humming while she stirred something on a stove, and the humming was a melody Wendell's body knew, though their mind had never heard it, and a man's laugh from another room, and small hands — *their* hands, smaller — pressing a drawing against the yellow wall, and a voice saying a name, the true name, the stranger's name, saying it with love, saying it like a bell —

Then the memory tore. Not faded — *tore*, cut clean at both ends, and through the cut Wendell glimpsed, for half a heartbeat, the other side: a vault of gray light, endless shelves, and labels drifting past like snow. One label swung close enough to read.

**ISHIKAWA, W. — HARVESTED AGE 7 — QUALITY: EXCEPTIONAL — ALLOCATED: BORDER WARD, NORTH FACE.**

Wendell came back to themselves on their knees at the basin's edge, gasping, the taste of rainwater and someone else's kitchen in their mouth. The silent girl was beside them, holding out her slate book without a word. On the page was a drawing: a child with Wendell's face, seven years old, laughing, standing in front of a yellow wall.

"They cut it out of you whole," Anselm murmured, kneeling, his voice gentle as bandaging. "Forty years the crown has done this. Every ward-stone in the wall is a life somebody doesn't remember living. Yours went north, child. Your childhood is holding up the sky — or it was, until two nights ago."

Wendell looked at the drawing. At the choir, watching without pity, because pity would have been easier to refuse. At the black water holding the drowned city's reflection.

"Why me?" they whispered. "Why does it have to be me?"

"Because exceptional quality holds longest," Sefa said quietly, "and because you're the only keystone that can walk." She extended her hand, palm up, over the basin water. "We rehearse at moonrise, such as moonrise is now. Unbinding songs — songs that loosen what the crown has locked. We've been singing them in hiding for twenty years, and until two nights ago they were only comfort. Now they're a weapon, and weapons need a keystone." Her hand stayed open between them. "Stay. Sing with us tonight. If you hate it, leave at dawn, and no one here will follow you. That's the whole bargain."

Wendell should have stood. Should have climbed the stairs, locked the rusted gate behind them, gone back to the stall and the careful smallness and the bottled comforts of strangers.

Instead they heard themselves say: "One night."

The choir exhaled — thirty people breathing at once, a sound like a tide coming in — and somewhere in the dark above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the first bell of the starless morning began to toll.

It was ringing backwards. And every head in the cistern turned toward the sound, and Sefa's open hand closed slowly into a fist.

"They've found the gate," she said. "Change of plans. We sing *now*."
