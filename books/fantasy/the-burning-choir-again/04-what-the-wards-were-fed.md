## 4. What the Wards Were Fed

Anselm had performed the Rite of the Opened Hand four hundred times in his life, and every time it frightened him.

Not the ritual itself — the ritual was simple, a hedge-priest's bread and butter, the kind of thing you could do drunk at a funeral and half the time he had. Two people, palms pressed together, a thread of shared memory passing between them like water finding its level. What frightened him was the arithmetic of it. Every memory given was a memory less. He had married couples with this rite, so each would carry a piece of the other's childhood. He had comforted the dying with it, letting them pour their last bright hours into someone who would stay. And every single time, he watched the giver's face as they felt themselves grow smaller, and he thought: *this is holy, and this is theft, and I have never once been sure which.*

Tonight there was no question which it was. Tonight they were going to rob Wendell Ishikawa blind — with their own hands pressed willingly against his.

"Last chance to refuse," Anselm said, kneeling on the wet stone of the cistern floor. The candle stubs around them threw more shadow than light. Above, the drowned arches of the old reservoir rose into darkness where the stars should have been and were not. "Once we open the channel, what comes through comes through. I can't call it back."

Wendell sat across from him, cross-legged, wrists loose over their knees. They looked, Anselm thought, like a person holding very still because they suspected the floor was lying about being solid. "You're saying my memories are in the wards," Wendell said. "All right. Show me."

"You understand that if you're wrong—"

"I'm never wrong about bottles." A thin smile, no warmth in it. "I know what stolen goods feel like. I've been selling them for eleven years. If something of mine is inside those wards, priest, I want to see its face."

*Its face.* Anselm closed his eyes briefly. Hedge-priests learned early that the ones who joked hardest were the ones standing closest to the cliff edge.

Sefa stood at the rim of the cistern, arms folded, watching from above like a gargoyle with opinions. Brava leaned against a pillar with her warden's coat buttoned wrong — she'd been doing that all day, buttoning things wrong, and Anselm had decided not to mention it. Pip sat closest of all, close enough to touch, which the child almost never did with anyone but Sefa.

"Begin when ready," Sefa said.

Anselm took Wendell's hands.

The first touch was always the strangest — two skins deciding to be one membrane. He felt Wendell flinch, felt them master the flinch, felt the thief's reflexes come up like shutters: *catalogue the sensation, bottle it, sell it later.* Then the shutter cracked, because there was nothing in Anselm to steal worth having, only forty years of funerals and hymns and the smell of tallow, and Wendell relaxed by degrees into something almost like trust.

Anselm spoke the opening words. Old words. Pre-Vespergate words, from before the walls, back when magic was weather instead of crime.

*"What was taken, let it be seen. What was buried, let it sing."*

The channel opened.

He had meant to guide them gently. He had meant to start small — a taste of the ward-song, the low hum under the city stones, the way the border wards drank and drank without ever being seen drinking. But the moment the channel opened, the wards *recognized* Wendell. Anselm felt it happen, felt the great slow attention beneath the city turn toward them like a whale turning in dark water, and then the memories came up the channel in a flood, and all Anselm could do was hold on and witness.

* * *

They saw harvest.

That was the word for it. Not theft — the crown's own documents, surfacing through the ward-memory like bodies rising in a pond, used the word *harvest*, and used it proudly. Anselm had known, in the way a man knows his country does terrible things and arranges not to know it louder. Now he knew the way you know a wound.

Forty years ago, King Aldous — young then, already hollow-eyed, already listening to the crown more than to his counselors — had stood before the assembled wardens and announced the Crisis of the Borders. The old wards were failing. The old wards were hungry. The old wards needed feeding, and there was only one food in Vespergate rich enough.

Memory.

The first harvesters wore grey robes and carried brass instruments that looked like tuning forks crossed with syringes. They went door to door with royal writs. *By order of the Crown, in service of the Shielding.* They took from the poor first, because the poor could not refuse. A fishwife gave up her wedding day and couldn't remember why she loved her husband, only that she did. A lamplighter surrendered his mother's face and spent the rest of his life greeting strangers hopefully. The instruments left no scars. That was the genius of it, Anselm thought, sick to his soul. No scars, no graves, no grief anyone could point to. Just a city full of people with soft places in their minds, smooth as river stones, who couldn't say what was missing.

And the wards drank, and the borders held, and the stars stayed, and everyone agreed it was a fair price, the ones who remembered agreeing at all.

Then the harvesters got better. They stopped taking whole memories and started taking *kinds* — bottling joy here, grief there, sorting the city's inner life like grain. And eventually they stopped knocking on doors altogether, because they found they could buy. There were always thieves willing to sell bottled memories cheap. There was always a night market. The crown didn't need harvester robes anymore; it needed only to be the quietest customer at the market, buying in bulk through intermediaries, feeding the wards on the city's own smuggled soul.

Anselm heard Wendell make a sound beside him. Small. Like a hinge.

Because Wendell understood. Eleven years of charms, sold one copper apiece to smiling customers — and how many of those bottles had traveled, resold, traded upward, until they reached the grey-gloved hands that fed them to the border stones? Wendell hadn't just survived the system. Wendell had been *supplying* it.

*I didn't know,* Wendell's mind said along the channel, not words exactly, more the shape of words. *I swear before anything holy, I didn't know.*

*I believe you,* Anselm answered, and found he did. *But look. You have to keep looking. This is why Sefa brought you.*

* * *

The flood narrowed. Deepened. They sank past the decades — twenty years ago, thirty — down to the oldest strata of the ward's diet, the founding meals, and there, filed in the crown's own ledger-memory, sealed with the Aldous seal, was a shipping manifest written in a clerk's careful hand.

Anselm felt Wendell go rigid.

The entry was dated thirty-one years past. It listed a seizure — not a purchase, a *seizure*, one of the last forced collections before the crown went respectable — conducted at a tenement on Chandler's Row following the arrest of two unlicensed practitioners. Names redacted. One surviving dependent, aged seven, remanded to the Wardens' Foundling House.

The confiscated inventory ran three lines.

*Ambient childhood recollection, intact, approx. age 0–7. Quality: exceptional. Emotional density: high. Recommended use: keystone anchoring, northern quadrant.*

And beneath it, the label. The clerk had written it twice, once for the ledger and once for the brass tag that would be wired to the storage vessel, and both times appeared now, hovering in the shared dark between their joined hands:

**ISHIKAWA, W.**

The cistern was silent except for water dripping somewhere, keeping time like a patient heart.

Anselm held on. His old hands ached around Wendell's, and Wendell's grip had gone from stillness to something harder, a drowning person's grip, and he let them squeeze because there was nothing else to give. Through the channel he felt what Wendell felt, and it was worse than horror. Horror has edges. This had none. It was the vertigo of a person shown a photograph of a house and told, calmly, *you grew up here, you loved it here, it burned down, and you will never remember one room of it.*

A child of seven. Taken with everything else in the flat after the arrests. Filed. Tagged. Poured into the northern quadrant of the border ward, where it had sat for thirty-one years, humming, holding up the sky.

*Those are mine,* Wendell thought, and the thought had no thief's cleverness in it at all. It was naked. It was seven years old. *That's — I'm in the wall. I've been in the wall my whole life. Everything I am started after — after they—*

*After they took the first you,* Anselm finished, because someone had to say it. *Yes.*

*The woman in the market,* Wendell said suddenly. *Last week. She bought a charm and said it sounded like her dead mother singing. I told her it was a good vintage.* A pause, and then, quieter, the worst thing: *It might have been. It might have been her actual mother, harvested off some street, passed through six hands, and I sold it back to her daughter for two coppers and she thanked me.*

Anselm had no absolution to offer. He was a hedge-priest; absolution was technically his trade; and he found he could not perform it. "The rite shows what is," he said aloud, his voice cracking on the wet stone. "It doesn't show what must be done. That part's yours."

Above them, Sefa spoke, and her voice came down into the cistern like a stone dropped into a well. "Now you understand why the wards fell the night the stars vanished. Something stopped the feeding. Something at the top got greedy — or got afraid. The wards haven't been starved, Wendell. They've been *called home*. Everything the crown ever ate, it's pulling back into itself. Into the crown. Into whatever wears it."

"And if it finishes eating?" Brava asked from her pillar, buttons crooked, voice flat.

"Then there won't be a border," Sefa said. "There'll be a mouth."

Pip crept forward and touched Wendell's shoulder — a first, Anselm noted distantly, a first that mattered — and held out a scrap of paper. On it, in the child's quick charcoal strokes: a stick figure with Wendell's sharp chin and tired eyes, and beside it, a smaller figure, round-faced, laughing, holding something that might have been a kite.

Wendell stared at the smaller figure for a long time.

"I don't know who that is," they said finally. Their voice was steady. Their hands were not. "I don't know who that is, and it's the worst thing I've ever lost, and I didn't even know I'd lost it until tonight."

Anselm released their hands. The channel closed. The ward-song faded back beneath the stones, vast and digesting, and in the silence afterward Wendell Ishikawa sat very straight in the candlelight, wearing their own name like a garment someone else had chosen.

It was Sefa who broke the quiet, and what she said made the cold come up out of the water:

"The crown-thing knows we know. It tasted you tonight through the ward — I felt it turn. Which means, choir, we have days at most before it sends hunters. Wendell — it knows your memories are the keystone. It knows you could take them back and bring the whole border down for good." Her eyes were steady and pitiless. "So the question isn't whether it wants you. The question is whether you're going to the palace before it comes for you — or after."
