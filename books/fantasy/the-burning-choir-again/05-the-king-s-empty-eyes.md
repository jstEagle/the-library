## 5. The King's Empty Eyes

Sefa had sung at three coronations and one funeral that was supposed to be a coronation, and she knew the smell of the palace the way a fishwife knows the tide: by what it left on her skin. Cold stone and tallow. Old incense burned to cover older rot. And beneath it all, faint as a hairline crack in a bell, something sweet — the smell of memory going stale, like wine turned to vinegar in a sealed jar.

She felt it strongest as they passed under the Gate of Bells, where the crown's hunters checked faces against their ledgers.

"Keep your charm warm," she murmured, not turning her head. Beside her, Wendell walked in the body of a grain-factor's clerk, gray and forgettable, borrowed from one of their own bottled memories. Brava loomed on Sefa's other side in the shape of a palace guard she'd once arrested and never quite forgiven. Anselm had wanted to come; Sefa had left him in the cisterns with Pip, because a hedge-priest's hands shook too much for forgery of this kind, and because someone had to stay alive to sing them home.

The guard at the gate barely looked up. "Purpose?"

"Harvest tithe," Sefa said, and let her voice carry the flat boredom of forty years of bureaucracy. "Quarter-day accounts."

The word *harvest* did it. It always did. The guards had been trained not to think about what the tithe was, only that it flowed upward, and men trained not to think will wave through anything rather than start thinking.

Inside, the palace swallowed them.

* * *

The Hall of Audience was worse than Sefa remembered. She had last stood in it twenty years ago, in warden's black, presenting a prisoner, and she remembered a room that was merely grand — pillars of veined marble, banners of the Nine Houses, the throne on its dais like a ship's prow cutting through courtiers. Now the grandeur had gone strange. The banners hung motionless in air that should have stirred them. The marble pillars were veined still, but the veins had shifted since her day, darkened, arranged in patterns that hurt to follow with the eye, spirals that seemed to tighten if you looked too long.

And the courtiers — the courtiers stood too still. A hundred nobles in their quarter-day finery, and not one of them shifting weight, not one whispering behind a fan. Their eyes were open. Their eyes were *empty*, glazed as the surface of charms left too long in Wendell's kiln.

"They're not dead," Wendell breathed beside her, so quietly the words were more vibration than sound. "I can feel them. Memories, hundreds of them, all just... *open*. Like doors left swinging."

"Hold," Sefa said.

King Aldous sat on his throne, and Sefa's heart broke a little, because she had known him when he was only a prince, quick and laughing, before his father died and the crown came down on him like a lid. He was sixty now, or the body was. He sat straight as a corpse propped up, hands on the armrests, and his face wore the mild, pleasant blankness of a man listening to music no one else can hear.

The crown sat on his head.

Sefa had seen the Iron Crown of Vespergate in portraits all her life — a plain circlet, honest iron, made by the first king from a plowshare so no ruler would ever forget he was a servant of the soil. What sat on Aldous's head now had kept the shape and forgotten everything else. It was black where it should have been gray, and it moved. Not visibly. But Sefa's ears had been trained by forty years of song to hear what the eye misses, and she could hear the crown *breathing* — a slow, wet rhythm, in and out, in and out, like gills.

As they joined the line of petitioners — real ones, farmers and merchants come for judgments — Sefa watched the thing feed.

A woman stepped forward with a land dispute. The king's mouth opened. "Your grievance is heard," he said, and the voice was Aldous's voice, worn smooth as a river stone. And then the crown *leaned*. There was no other way to describe it. The whole head tilted a fraction toward the woman, and the breathing sound quickened, and the woman swayed on her feet, blinking, suddenly unsure why she had come, what she had lost, whether the field had ever been hers at all. She thanked the king warmly and walked away lighter, smiling, hollowed.

"Gods below," Brava whispered. Her borrowed face couldn't sweat, but her real body, somewhere under the charm, surely did. "It doesn't take the memory. It takes the *having* of it. She'll remember the field exists and never feel it was hers again."

"That's how you feed wards for forty years without emptying the city," Sefa said. "You don't drain the cup. You drink the wine and leave the cup standing." She had spent two decades telling herself the wards were a necessary cruelty, that the harvests were small, that the alternative was the border falling and the things beyond it walking in. She had told herself the crown served Vespergate. Looking up at that dais, she understood at last that there had never been any *crown* serving anything. There was only the thing wearing the crown, wearing the king, wearing the city like a coat it had grown into.

The line moved forward. Three petitioners. Two. One.

Then it was them.

* * *

Up close, the king smelled of the same sweetness Sefa had caught at the gate, concentrated, thick enough to taste. His eyes were the worst part. They were blue, human blue, and behind the blue was nothing at all — not darkness, not emptiness, but *absence*, the way a burned house is absent. Somewhere far back in there, Sefa thought, a man might still be tied to a chair watching thieves walk through his rooms. She hoped so. She prayed so. It would mean there was something left to save.

"State your business," said the mouth of King Aldous.

Sefa bowed, held out the ledger of false accounts, recited the tithe numbers Anselm had forged. The words came out steady. Inside, every instinct she owned was screaming, the old warden's instinct, the one that had kept her alive through the Purges: *something is wrong, run, run now.*

The crown leaned toward her.

The breathing sound filled Sefa's skull. She felt it touch her mind the way a customs officer turns out your pockets — impersonal, thorough, amused. It riffled through her: the cisterns, the choir, Anselm's shaking hands, twenty years of songs she'd sworn she'd never sing again. She braced to be torn apart.

Instead, it stopped. It went very still, the way a hound goes still when it catches a scent it hasn't smelled in a century.

And then it turned — the whole head, the whole feeding attention of it — onto Wendell.

Wendell stood frozen in their borrowed face, and Sefa saw the moment the disguise stopped mattering, the way a paper lantern stops mattering the instant fire touches it. The crown-thing didn't see the clerk. It saw through the clerk, down to the magic underneath, the bottled memories clicking softly against each other inside Wendell's coat like teeth chattering.

"*There you are,*" the king said, and the voice was still Aldous's voice, but something else spoke through it now, something vast and patient and delighted, the voice of a collector finding the one piece missing from a shelf. "*Little keystone. Little stolen stone. Forty years I built my walls, and the mason hid the cornerstone in a bottle-seller's pocket.*"

"Sire?" Sefa said loudly, playing confusion, playing offense. "This one is under my supervision—"

The crown-thing ignored her the way you ignore a fly. One of the king's hands rose from the armrest — slowly, joints cracking like green wood — and extended one finger toward Wendell's chest. It didn't touch. It hovered an inch above Wendell's sternum, over the heart, and where it hovered the air began to glow. Faintly. A sigil burning itself into being, a mark like a brand seen through skin, some glyph in a script older than the Nine Houses, older than Vespergate's walls.

Wendell made a small sound. Not pain, exactly. Recognition. Sefa would learn later that the mark felt, to Wendell, like a hand closing around something they hadn't known they were carrying.

"*Marked,*" the thing said, almost tenderly. "*Mine now. Run home to your little cistern, little stone. Sing your little songs. When I want you, I will know exactly where you are — and I will come and take back what the masons stole, and I will wear this city's dreams until the sun burns out.*"

The hand withdrew. The king's face smoothed back into its pleasant blankness. "Your accounts are accepted," said the mouth of Aldous, bored again already. "Next."

They walked out of the Hall of Audience on legs that belonged to someone else, past the hollow-eyed courtiers, past the guards who saw nothing, through the Gate of Bells, into streets where the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

No one spoke until they reached the tanner's quarter and Brava pulled them into the reek between two sheds, safe for the moment from ears.

"It marked them," Brava said. "It can find us. It can find the cisterns through them. We should—" She didn't finish. Sefa watched her decide, visibly, not to say *we should leave Wendell somewhere far away and hope it follows the wrong trail.* Warden's arithmetic. Sefa had done that arithmetic herself for twenty years.

"No," Sefa said.

"You don't know what I was going to—"

"I know exactly what you were going to say. I've said it myself, in that exact tone." She turned to Wendell, who stood pale and shaking in their own face again, the charm burned to ash in their pocket, the new mark glowing faintly through their shirt like a coal wrapped in cloth. "It thinks marking you was a leash. It thinks it just claimed its property."

Wendell looked up. Under the terror in their eyes, Sefa saw something else kindling — the same thing she'd seen in the cisterns the night they'd first refused to run.

"It made a mistake," Wendell said slowly. "Thieves know leashes. We cut them, or we use them." A thin, fierce smile. "It showed me its attention. For one moment, I felt where it lives. Sefa — I know which way the door faces."

Sefa felt the dread in her chest turn over, sharpen, and become something else entirely. Twenty years ago she'd been a warden who believed the walls were worth any price. Then she'd been a fugitive who believed nothing was worth anything. Standing in the tanner's stink under a blind sky, holding the gaze of a bottled-memory thief marked by a god, she felt a third thing take root, patient and terrible:

Resolve.

"Then we go home," she said, "and we plan. Because it knows where we are now — and that means, little stone, we have until it comes for you to learn how to make it wish it had never tasted your magic."

Behind them, across the city, the palace bells began to ring — though Sefa could swear no ringer had touched them, and the tolling sounded less like a call to prayer than like something ancient, licking its lips, counting.
