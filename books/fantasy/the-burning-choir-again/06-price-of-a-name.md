## 6. Price of a Name

The mark the crown-thing had left on me itched like a splinter of cold light under the skin of my wrist. I kept my sleeve pulled down over it and told myself that was enough.

It wasn't. In the cisterns, where the choir had carried me through three days of dripping dark and singing that made the water tremble in its channels, I could feel the mark listening. When Sefa's voice rose into the unbinding scales — those strange descending phrases that sounded less like music and more like a lock being persuaded open — the thing on my wrist warmed, faintly, like a coin held too long in a fist. It was reporting back to something. I was sure of it. Somewhere above us, behind the walls of the palace, a mouth wearing a crown was tasting my name on its tongue.

So we went deeper, and we went quiet.

"Rule one," Brava said, crouching at the edge of the flooded lower gallery where the choir now made its camp. She'd built a fire in an old brazier, green-flamed from some hedge-priest trick of Anselm's, so it gave light without smoke. "Nobody goes up. Nobody sells. Nobody charms." Her eyes found mine when she said that last part, and I looked away first. "I know what you are, Ishikawa. I've spent eleven years hunting your kind. But for as long as you're under this roof — this wet, miserable, leaking roof — you don't work."

"I'm not going to starve out of principle," I said.

"You're not going to starve because Pip will feed you whether you want it or not." She jerked her chin toward the far wall, where the mute child sat cross-legged with a tin plate balanced on their knees, dividing a loaf of black bread into portions with the grave precision of a surgeon. "And because Anselm says the wards still taste you even down here. You bottle anything, you light up like a festival lantern."

Anselm, who had been dozing against a pillar with his prayer-knots wound around his fingers, opened one eye. "A slightly inaccurate metaphor," he murmured, "but spiritually sound."

I hated all of them a little, which is how I knew I was starting to belong.

* * *

Brava left before dawn, dressed as a customs clerk, with her warden's insignia buried in a false seam of her coat. She didn't tell us where she was going. She told Sefa, quietly, by the water's edge, and I pretended not to hear: the Lamplighters' Rest, off Cooper's Row, where retired wardens drank themselves honest and dishonest in equal measure. Where old contacts could be bought with old debts.

She was gone two days.

On the first day, I learned the shape of waiting. It turns out I had never waited for anything in my life. I had *worked* — worked the night market, worked the alleys, worked the careful arithmetic of which memories to steal and which to leave intact so no one noticed the missing hours. Waiting was a country I'd never visited, and it was terrible. I counted drips. I memorized the cracks in the ceiling vaults. I watched Sefa tune her voice against the cistern walls until the echoes came back clean, and I asked her, finally, because the silence was worse than the question:

"Why me? Out of everyone the crown has robbed. Why is my childhood the keystone?"

Sefa didn't stop tuning. Her hum ran down a scale and came apart at the bottom, and she started over. "Because of when they took you," she said. "The wards were rebuilt forty years ago, after the first collapse. They needed a foundation memory — something whole, something young, something given before it could be refused. Most of what feeds the wards is scraps. Thousands of little thefts, thinned out across the city so nobody misses them. Yours was different. Yours was taken entire. A child of seven, everything, all at once." She glanced at me. "You're not a brick in that wall, Wendell. You're the cornerstone. Pull you out, and the whole thing has to be re-laid."

"And if I'm put *back*?"

"Then the sky comes home." She said it simply, without drama, the way she said most true things. "But only if it's willing. That's the whole doctrine of the unbinding, Ishikawa. Nothing taken can hold. Only what's given."

I thought about that for a long time afterward, lying on a pallet that smelled of mineral water and lamp oil, staring up at stone that hadn't seen stars in forty years either. *Nothing taken can hold.* My whole life was taken things. My trade was taken things. I had built a self out of other people's leftovers and called it survival.

Somewhere in the dark, Pip was watching me. I could feel their eyes the way I felt the mark on my wrist — a small warm pressure, patient, unjudging.

* * *

Pip did not speak. Anselm said the crown's harvesters had taken their words along with their mother's face, both in the same winter, when Pip was five. What remained was drawing — charcoal stubs and chalk nubs collected in a cracked cup, and every flat surface in the cistern slowly colonized by pictures. Warden helmets with the faces scratched out. The bell tower. A dog, drawn many times, always the same dog, always running.

On the second evening of Brava's absence, Pip came and sat beside me without asking, which was very much how Pip did everything, and pressed a piece of paper into my hands.

It was me.

Not me as I was — not the sharp-faced charm-seller with the sleeve pulled over the mark. It was a child, round-cheeked, maybe seven, standing in front of a shop with a striped awning. The child was laughing. Behind the counter of the shop stood a woman with flour on her hands, and the woman was laughing too, and between them, drawn in careful strokes, hung a string of paper birds.

My throat closed.

"How do you know this?" I managed. My voice came out wrong, scraped. "Pip. How could you possibly—"

Pip tapped the paper. Then tapped my chest. Then made a gesture I didn't understand until Anselm, drifting over with his knots, translated softly: "They say the songs remember. When Sefa sings the unbinding scales, the water carries echoes of everything the wards ever swallowed. Pip listens to the water. Pip has been listening to the water since before any of us met them." He lowered himself onto a crate with a groan. "The child has been hearing your childhood for weeks, Wendell. The bakery on Tanner's Lane. Your mother's name was Odile. You used to fold paper birds while the bread proved, and she'd hang them in the window, and customers would ask if the shop sold them."

I looked down at the picture again. At the flour on the woman's hands. At the laugh I couldn't remember but could suddenly, horribly, almost hear.

"I don't want it back," I said, and it came out flat and hard, a lie with armor on. "Whatever they took, they can keep it. I've done fine."

Pip looked at me with the terrible directness of children and the wordless, which is a combination no lie survives. Then they took the paper back, and turned it over, and drew — quickly, furiously, charcoal squeaking — a second picture. This one was the cistern. All five of us: Sefa with her chin lifted mid-song, Brava scowling with her arms crossed, Anselm tangled in his own prayer-knots, Pip holding a charcoal stub. And me, at the center, with the mark on my wrist drawn not as a brand but as a string — a string tied to each of them, like the paper birds strung across a bakery window.

They handed it back. They pointed at the first picture, then the second. Then they shrugged, eloquently, and walked away to feed the fire.

"They're telling you," Anselm said quietly, "that you get to choose which picture is true. That's more than most of us were ever offered." He settled his knots around his wrist, where, I noticed for the first time, he wore a faded scar in the shape of a brand. "For what it's worth, I chose the second picture thirty years ago. I have never once regretted the exchange rate."

I sat with both drawings until the green fire burned low. Then I folded the bakery into my coat, over my heart, the way I'd once folded charms for strangers, and I kept the cistern in my hand.

* * *

Brava came back on the third night, and she came back bleeding.

Not badly — a split lip, a gash over one eyebrow already glued shut with pitch — but fast, moving through the water with none of her usual parade-ground economy. Sefa caught her arm. "What happened?"

"Got the name." Brava spat pink into the channel. "Cost me a favor I can never repay and a promise I'm probably going to regret. The old thing under the crown — the real one, the one that's been eating Vespergate since before Aldous was weaned — it has a name from before names were dangerous. *Vessh-Karuun.* Say it right, say it whole, and it can't hold its shape. That's the doctrine, isn't it? Nothing taken can hold." She laughed, short and ugly. "But there's a catch, because there's always a catch."

"Say it," Sefa said.

"It knows we're coming. My contact says the hunters were paid yesterday — paid in *memories*, fresh ones, whole lives. Someone sold us out." Her eyes swept the gallery, counting us, and stopped on me, and softened by a degree I'd never seen in them before. "We move tomorrow night. The square outside the palace, where the old bell hangs. We sing the unbinding, Wendell speaks the name, and we pray the wards break before the hunters find us." She pressed a folded scrap of paper into Sefa's hands — the name, written in a dead alphabet. "Until then, nobody sleeps alone."

That night I lay on my pallet with Pip's drawing under my hand and the mark on my wrist burning colder than it ever had, and I understood, finally, what the burning meant.

It wasn't listening anymore.

It was answering. Somewhere above us, in a throne room lit by no stars, something ancient had just learned its own name was loose in the world — and it was calling every hunter in Vespergate home to roost.

In the morning, the bells began to ring wrong.
