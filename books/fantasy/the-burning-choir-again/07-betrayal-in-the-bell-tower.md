## 7. Betrayal in the Bell Tower

Brava had spent twenty years learning the weight of a door before she opened it. A door told you things — who stood behind it, how many, whether they were afraid. The bell tower of Saint Vesper's had a door that weighed wrong.

She stopped on the third step and put her hand flat against the cold iron band.

"Anselm," she said quietly. "Don't sing tonight."

Above her, in the dark, the old hedge-priest's voice drifted down through the stairwell like smoke. He was humming one of the unbinding songs, the slow one, the one he said was older than the crown itself. He hummed it the way other men prayed.

The door felt wrong because there were too many boots behind it. Not hunters' boots — hunters moved soft, wrapped their soles in wool the way she'd taught them, back when teaching them was her job. These boots shifted. Waited. The way men wait when they've been paid to wait.

Her hand went to the warden's knife at her hip out of habit, then past it to the second knife, the one without a maker's mark. Twenty years since she'd worn the gray coat. Some nights she still reached for the coat's hem that wasn't there.

She climbed anyway. You didn't survive Vespergate by walking away from doors that weighed wrong. You survived by knowing which side of them to stand on when they opened.

* * *

The man waiting in the belfry was not a hunter.

He was worse. He was family.

"Dov," Brava said.

Dov Marren stood by the great bronze bell with his hands folded in front of him like a man at a funeral, which in a way he was. He'd kept his hair short. He still wore the gray coat, though it fit him looser than it used to — grief does that, or guilt, or both. The lantern light caught the warden's badge at his throat: the tower-and-key of Vespergate, polished bright.

Behind him, half-hidden in the shadow of the bell's yoke, four hunters in plain clothes. She counted their hands. Two had knives out. One had a net weighted with lead shot — anti-magic work, the kind they dropped over hedge-witches in the market squares. The last held a lantern and nothing else, and that one frightened her most, because it meant someone up the chain thought this would be easy.

"You look well," Dov said.

"I look like a traitor. So do you." She kept her voice level. Below them, through the tower floor, she could hear the cistern choir's echo rising faintly through the stone — Pip's small footfalls, Sefa's low count of the song's rhythm. Anselm's humming had stopped. Good. He'd heard the boots too. Old men who lived outside the law learned to hear boots the way farmers hear rain.

"There's a writ," Dov said. "Sealed. Your name is on it, and the priest's, and the child's, and the woman Sefa's. And the charm-seller." His jaw worked. "Ishikawa."

"The charm-seller has a name."

"They all have names. That's what makes the work hard." He took a step toward her, and the hunters shifted with him, a choreography so practiced it was almost tender. "Brava. I argued for you. I told them you were misled, that the priest had you under some working—"

"You told them what kept your badge clean."

"I told them what kept you alive this long." Something cracked in his voice, just barely, the way ice cracks before it gives. "Forty years I've served the wards. Forty years my whole city has slept safe under them, and now you want me to believe they're built on — what? Stolen memories? That the sky fell because the king's crown eats children?" He shook his head slowly. "I know what you saw in that cistern. I know what Anselm showed you. But I've seen the border too, Brava. I've seen what comes through when the wards go thin. You don't want what's out there getting in. Whatever the price of the walls — we pay it, and we sleep."

"And the child pays it," Brava said. "Pip is nine years old. Pip can't speak, and the crown took whatever memories might have taught her why."

"The crown takes nothing that isn't given."

"Then you've never read a harvest ledger."

His face did something complicated. For one heartbeat she thought — hoped — she'd found the seam in him, the place where the man she'd trained beside still lived. Then the lantern-hunter cleared his throat, softly, and the seam closed.

"It doesn't have to be the tower," Dov said. "Give me the priest. Just Anselm. The songs die with him; the rest of you scatter, and I lose the writ in a drawer somewhere. That's the best I can buy you. It's more than you'd get from anyone else in this city."

Below, faint as breath, Anselm began to hum again.

Brava knew that song. They all did now. It wasn't the slow one. It was the loud one — the one Sefa called the *opening*, the one meant to be sung in a square, in daylight, with a crowd. Anselm was singing it alone in a bell tower at midnight, and he was singing it at full voice, and she understood in one cold flash what the old fool intended.

A song that loud, in a tower with a bronze bell — it would carry across half of Vespergate. It would bring every hunter in the district running. And it would give the others below time to run the opposite way.

"He's going to ring it," she said aloud, and Dov's eyes went wide, and then everything happened at once.

* * *

Anselm's voice rose through the floorboards like water through cracks, and the words of the opening-song were not words at all but something older, syllables that made the lantern flames lean sideways and the lead-shot net tremble in its holder's grip. Brava threw herself at Dov — not to hurt him, never to hurt him, just to be between him and the stairs — and the belfry came apart into noise.

The bell spoke first.

Anselm had both hands on the great wheel, an old man's hands, spotted and scarred from forty years of illegal blessings, and he hauled with everything left in his body. The bronze swung. The first note went through Brava's bones like a blade of pure sound, and beneath it she heard the hunters shouting, heard the net-man scream that his charms were burning, heard Dov yelling her name — *Brava, Brava, stop him* — and above it all, impossibly, Anselm laughing.

She got to the wheel. She put her hands over his, meaning to pull him free, meaning to drag him down the stairs ahead of the hunters, meaning all the sensible things — and he looked at her with eyes bright as a boy's and said, over the thunder of the second peal:

"Hold the rope, Warden. One last duty. Sing it with me."

And gods forgive her, she sang.

She didn't decide to. Her voice simply joined his, two notes braided together, the harmony Sefa had drilled into them in the dripping dark of the cistern until it lived in their spines. The bell drank the song. That was the thing about bronze, Anselm had told her once, drunk on cheap wine in the cisterns: bronze remembers. Every note ever rung in it lives in the metal forever, layered like rings in a tree. A bell is a memory that everyone in the city shares.

The hunters broke. She saw it happen — the net-man dropped his burden and fled down the stairs, and the knife-men followed, and even Dov staggered back against the parapet with his hands over his ears, though his ears weren't what was hurting. The song was doing what the songs did. Unbinding. Loosening. Somewhere in the tower's stones, forty years of harvested memory stirred like sediment in a flooded well.

They got three verses out before the crossbow bolt took Anselm through the chest.

* * *

There hadn't been a fifth hunter. There had been a sixth, on the roof of the scriptorium across the square, and Brava would spend the rest of her life not forgiving herself for not counting roofs.

Anselm didn't fall. That was the terrible, holy thing of it. The bolt hit high, below the collarbone, and his hands stayed on the wheel, and his voice — his voice never stopped. Blood ran down the bronze bell in long threads and the bell rang on, ringing his blood into its memory along with his song, and Brava held the rope and screamed the harmony through her tears and could not make herself let go, because letting go meant the song stopped, and the song stopping meant Anselm had died for a verse and a half.

"Go," he said, on a breath, between peals. "The others. Go *now*. I'll hold the bell."

"You're dying, you mad old—"

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly, and smiled at her with blood on his teeth. "So the bell and I have an understanding. Go, Brava. Tell Wendell—" The next peal swallowed his words. When the sound passed, he finished, softer: "Tell the charm-seller that what the crown offers freely is always the trap. Always. Make them remember."

The hunters were coming back up the stairs. She heard them. The song was still holding them at bay — she could feel it fraying at the edges, a rope parting strand by strand — but it wouldn't hold long, and Anselm's hands were already slipping on the wheel, slick and red.

She kissed his forehead, salt and copper, and went down the stairs three at a time while the bell rang above her like a heart that refused to stop.

* * *

The cistern was emptying when she reached it — Sefa moving with terrible calm, rolling the song-scrolls into oilcloth; Pip clutching her chalk and her slate and the little hoard of things children save; Wendell standing frozen in the middle of it all with a bottled charm in each hand, as if the bottles could be argued with.

"They're coming," Brava said. "All of them. Dov sold us — no." She corrected herself, because it mattered, because Anselm would have wanted it true. "Dov tried to buy us out. Someone above him didn't bargain. Anselm held the bell so we'd have the streets." Her voice failed on the last word. She let it fail for exactly one breath, then rebuilt herself, because she was still a warden somewhere underneath, and wardens delivered the news straight. "He's dead. Or he will be before we're out of the district. His song is in the bronze now. They'll hear it ringing over Saint Vesper's for a hundred years and never know why it sounds like defiance."

Sefa closed her eyes. Opened them. "The square," she said. "We do it in the square, sooner than planned, before they regroup. Tonight if we must."

"No," Wendell said.

Everyone turned. The charm-seller stood very still, and Brava watched their face do something she'd seen on a hundred suspects in interrogation rooms: the look of a person reading a letter they shouldn't have.

"It knows where we are," Wendell said. Their voice was strange, flattened. "It's been talking to me. Through the marks — since the throne room, since it tasted my magic. I didn't say anything because I thought I was imagining it." They lifted one hand, palm out, and in the lamplight Brava saw the sigil burned there, the crown-shaped brand that had appeared after the audience, and it was *glowing*, faintly, pulsing like a slow heartbeat. "It just spoke clearly for the first time. Whole sentences."

"What did it say?" Sefa asked.

Wendell's laugh came out broken. "It offered me a trade. My childhood. All of it — every memory in those ledgers, ISHIKAWA, W., returned intact. My mother's face. My real name. Everything I've been selling to strangers in bottles my whole life." The glow pulsed. "In exchange for the choir. Your lives, handed over, and I walk out of this with a self I can't even remember wanting back."

The cistern was silent except for the drip of water and, far above, faint through forty feet of stone, the bell of Saint Vesper's still ringing Anselm's song into the night.

"And what did you tell it?" Brava asked quietly.

Wendell looked at each of them in turn — Sefa, Pip, her — and Brava saw the exact moment the temptation lost, saw it happen like watching a scale tip, and hated the crown-thing for making them carry the weight of it either way.

"I told it I needed until dawn," Wendell said. "Because I'm a thief, and thieves don't say no to a mark. They say *maybe*." They closed their fist over the glowing brand. "It believes I'm coming to the throne room tonight. Alone. Which means for the next few hours, it thinks it's won — and it isn't watching any of you."

Sefa began, slowly, to smile. It was not a kind smile.

"Then dawn," she said, "we give it a choir."
