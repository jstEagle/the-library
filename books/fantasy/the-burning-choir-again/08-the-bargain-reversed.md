## 8. The Bargain Reversed

The summons came at dusk, carried by a child in palace livery who would not meet Wendell's eyes. No soldiers followed him. None were needed. The mark on Wendell's wrist — a thin ring of cold, like a bracelet of winter water — had been pulling them toward the palace for three days, gentle as a fisherman's line.

*Come alone,* the boy said, and fled before Wendell could answer.

Sefa argued until midnight. Brava argued longer, and louder, and with more profanity. Pip didn't argue at all; the mute child simply took Wendell's hand, pressed it flat against their own small chest, and held it there over the heartbeat until Wendell understood: *I will feel it when you stop.*

That was the worst part. Not the dying. Being felt.

"You don't have to do this," Sefa said finally, in the cistern dark, her voice worn smooth as river stone. "There are other ways."

"There aren't," Wendell said. "And you know it. Anselm is dead because we ran. The bell tower is ash. Every hour we hide, that thing eats another district's memories to keep its wards stitched together — did you see the Tanner's Quarter this morning? Half those people couldn't remember their own children's faces." They flexed the marked wrist. "It wants me. It offered me *me*. I'm going to go and find out why a god needs to bargain with a bottle-thief."

"Because it's afraid of you," Sefa said quietly. "Remember that, when you're standing in front of it. Gods only bargain with things that frighten them."

Wendell kissed her forehead — an impulse, sudden and strange, the kind of thing family did — and climbed out of the cistern into the starless night.

* * *

Vespergate had never been beautiful, but it had been *full* — full of noise and smoke and the particular music of ten thousand lives overlapping. Now the streets were quiet in a way that hurt. People walked as if through deep water. A woman stood at her own door turning a key over and over, unable to recall what it opened. A man sat on a curb weeping without sound, mouthing a name he'd lost.

The crown-thing was feeding faster now. Starving things always did.

The palace gates stood open. Of course they did. You don't lock the door when you've set the trap.

Inside, the halls were lit with cold white lamps that threw no warmth, and the silence had texture, like walking through cobwebs. Wendell counted doorways out of habit — a thief's habit, mapping exits — and found, with a chill that settled deep, that every corridor behind them ended in blank wall. The palace was rearranging itself around them. Or the thing wearing the crown simply didn't believe Wendell would need to leave by any path but its choosing.

The throne room doors were bronze, taller than three men, worked with scenes of King Aldous's coronation. Wendell looked closer and saw that the sculptor had given the young king no face. Just a smooth oval, waiting.

They pushed, and the doors swung without a sound.

* * *

The throne room was vast and empty and wrong. The banners on the walls hung motionless though the air moved. The great windows showed nothing outside — not night, not sky, just a blackness so complete it seemed less like absence than like something *swallowing*.

King Aldous sat on his throne, and the crown sat on King Aldous, and neither of them was exactly there.

Up close, in the white lamplight, Wendell could see what the audience hall's distance had hidden. The king's chest rose and fell, but slowly, like a man long asleep. His hands lay open on the armrests, palms up, the posture of surrender or of a table set for a meal. And above his slack face, the crown — old iron and black gold, ugly up close, pitted like bone left in weather — pulsed faintly. Not light. *Appetite.* Wendell's marked wrist ached in time with it.

"You came," the crown said.

It did not speak through the king's mouth. The voice came from everywhere, from the walls, from inside Wendell's own skull, resonant and patient and old beyond reckoning. It was not a cruel voice. That was somehow worse. It was the voice of something that had never once needed to hurry.

"I came," Wendell said. Their voice sounded small in all that space. "You said you'd give them back. My memories."

"I said I would *offer*." The king's head tilted, an awkward marionette movement, but the eyes stayed fixed on Wendell — and they were not empty anymore. They were hungry. "I keep my bargains, little thief. It is the one virtue I have cultivated across a very long life. Your childhood. Seven years of it. Every stolen afternoon, every lullaby, the smell of your mother's kitchen, the name you were born with. All of it, returned whole. In exchange—"

"The choir," Wendell said. "Their lives. Their names. You'll let them walk out of Vespergate and forget they ever sang."

"Forget they ever *were*," the crown agreed pleasantly. "A fair trade. One thief's past for six futures. You have spent your life selling other people's yesterdays, Ishikawa. Tonight you may buy back your own. There is a poetry in it I find satisfying."

Wendell walked forward. Each step made the mark on their wrist burn colder. Ten steps from the throne, they stopped.

"How do I know you won't take the choir anyway?"

The thing laughed, and the laughter made the banners shiver. "You don't. That is what trust is. But consider: I have fed on this city for forty years, and in all that time, no one has ever stood where you are standing and asked me a question. They beg. They weep. They offer each other. You *negotiate*. I find I want to honor that, if only for novelty's sake."

*Because it's afraid of you,* Sefa had said. Wendell looked at the vast black windows, at the starving dark pressing against the glass, and understood suddenly what the fear was. The wards were falling. The thing's food supply was failing. It needed Wendell — needed the keystone memory, the child labeled ISHIKAWA, W., whose stolen years held some structural secret even the crown itself might not fully understand.

It wasn't offering a bargain. It was begging, in the only language gods knew.

"All right," Wendell said. "Give them back."

* * *

The crown-thing did not hand anything over. It poured.

The king's body sat up — the real Aldous somewhere far beneath, surfacing like a drowning man glimpsed through ice — and raised one trembling hand toward Wendell. And the memories came.

They came like floodwater. Wendell had braced for pain and got something stranger: *recognition*. A kitchen smelling of ginger and scorched rice. A woman humming off-key, her back turned, flour on her knuckles. Rain on a tin roof. A father's laugh, big and sudden, shaking the floorboards. A name spoken in the dark — *Wendell, Wendell, come inside, the stars are out* —

The stars. There had been stars. Wendell remembered lying in a rooftop gutter at seven years old, memorizing constellations because their mother said the sky was the only book that never lied—

And underneath the sweetness, the trap. Wendell felt it even through the joy: the returning memories were being laid down in layers, and between the layers ran threads of the crown-thing's own substance, fine as spider silk. Take the gift and be bound by it. Become a ward-anchor again, willingly this time, a living pillar in the palace of the thing that ate cities. The bargain had teeth after all. It always did.

But here was the truth about floods, and Wendell Ishikawa had built a career on it: water runs both ways.

The transfer was a channel. Two vessels, one pipe. And while the crown-thing poured seven years of a stolen childhood through that narrow bright passage, it was *open* — wide open, drunk on the pleasure of giving back what it had taken, savoring the reunion, glutted and slow and careless as any feeder at a feast.

Wendell reached down the channel.

Not with hands. With the oldest trick they knew — the trick that had kept them alive since they were fourteen and first learned to press a stranger's grief into glass. They had bottled ten thousand memories. They knew the shape of memory better than anyone alive, better perhaps than the ancient thing that merely *ate* them. Eating was appetite. Bottling was craft.

At the very bottom of the crown-thing's hoard, deeper than forty years of harvested citizens, deeper than the founding of Vespergate itself, there was a single memory older than all the rest. Wendell felt it the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall: a word. A name. The name the thing had borne before it was hunger, before it was crown, back when it had been small enough to be loved by something.

Wendell closed their fingers around it — metaphor, craft, instinct, whatever you called the place where a thief's skill lived — and *bottled*.

The crown screamed.

Every banner in the room snapped taut. The black windows cracked from top to bottom. The king's body convulsed on the throne, and the voice that tore out of it was no longer patient, no longer amused, no longer anything but vast and wounded fury:

***THIEF.***

"Yeah," Wendell gasped, staggering backward, both hands pressed to their temples where two streams of memory now fought for room — their childhood pouring in, the stolen name burning behind their eyes like a coal. "Yeah, I — get that — a lot—"

The last of the childhood memories crashed home. Wendell's mother's face. The rooftop. The stars. *Ishikawa, W.* — whole, entire, *theirs*, and threaded through it all the binding silk that should have made them the crown's puppet forever.

Except the silk needed an anchor at both ends. And Wendell had just ripped out the other end and put it in a bottle.

The mark on their wrist shattered — they felt it go, a ring of ice breaking — and the crown-thing rose off the throne in a shape that was not a shape, a darkness with an appetite at its center, and the doors of the throne room sealed themselves like a wound closing, and Wendell Ishikawa stood alone in the belly of a starving god holding the only thing it had ever truly feared losing.

Its name. Its true, original, unbinding name.

The darkness gathered itself to strike, and Wendell — laughing, weeping, seven years old and twenty-seven years old in the same breath — turned and ran for the cracking windows, shouting over their shoulder the only words that mattered:

"Sefa! Get everyone to the square! *I have it!*"

Behind them, the palace began to scream.
