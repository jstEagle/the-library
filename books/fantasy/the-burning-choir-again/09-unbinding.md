## 9. Unbinding

**Sefa**

The square had been called the Square of the Returning Light once, back when there was light to return to. Now it was a field of broken flagstones and old bloodstains the rain never quite lifted, and above it — nothing. No stars. No moon. A ceiling of black so complete that Sefa felt it pressing on her skull like a hand.

She stood at its center with four people she would have called strangers a season ago and could no longer call anything but hers.

Brava checked the rooftops one last time, her warden's coat swapped for a dyer's smock, her hands still moving like a woman who expected to be arrested for existing. Pip stood at Sefa's left, chalk in one pocket and Wendell's sleeve clutched in the other fist. And Wendell —

Wendell looked like someone standing at the edge of a high place in the dark.

"You can still walk away," Sefa said quietly. "We find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't," Wendell said. "You taught me that song yourself. The last verse only works if someone means it."

They were right. That was the cruelty of unbinding songs — they were honest whether you wanted them to be or not. The choir had spent three nights in the cisterns learning the harmonies Anselm had died to keep alive, and every verse of the final movement required a voice that was *giving* something, not just singing.

"Then we do it together," Sefa said. "No one carries their part alone."

Above them, at the far edge of the square, the palace doors opened, spilling torchlight across the stones. The crown-thing had felt them coming. Of course it had. It had marked Wendell in the throne room; it had been tasting their magic on the air ever since, patient as rot.

It wanted them here.

Sefa raised her arms, and the choir began to sing.

* * *

**Brava**

The first note left Brava's throat and did something notes should not do: it went *outward*, past the square, past the walls, into the starless dark like a hand reaching up to knock on heaven's locked door.

She had sung warrants before. She had sung the Warden's Litany at executions, flat and official, while people swung. This was nothing like that. This was Anselm's music — the hedge-priest's ragged, half-illegal harmonies he'd hummed while cooking, while mending nets, while dying with his arms around a bell so the hunters couldn't silence it. He was in every line of it. She could hear him in the intervals, off-key in exactly the way that made them true.

The wards answered.

Not the great border wards — those were dead, had been dead since the night the stars vanished. But the small ones, the ones stitched through Vespergate's stones by forty years of stolen memory, woke and shivered. Brava felt them the way you feel an old scar ache before rain. Every charm-bottle in the city, every bottled grief and borrowed joy, hummed in sympathy.

And the crown-thing screamed.

It came down the palace steps wearing King Aldous like a coat, and Brava's stomach turned over, because she had sworn oaths to that face. Aldous had been old when she was a recruit — stern, tired, human. What walked now moved wrong, joints bending a half-beat late, as though the body were being operated from above by strings made of hunger. The crown sat on his head not like metal but like a mouth, and the mouth was open.

"*Little thieves,*" it said, and its voice came from everywhere, from the stones, from the dead wards, from inside Brava's own teeth. "*You bring me my keystone. How kind.*"

"We bring you a name," Sefa said.

Brava kept singing. That was the discipline of it — the horror walked toward them and she held her note anyway, because Anselm had held his, because the song did not stop for monsters, had never stopped for monsters, that was the whole point of songs.

* * *

**Pip**

Pip knew the monster was scared.

Grown-ups thought Pip didn't notice things, on account of the not-talking, but Pip noticed everything. The monster's shadow was shaking. Shadows weren't supposed to shake unless the thing casting them was afraid of something, and Pip looked around the square to see what it could possibly be scared of, because it was the biggest scariest thing Pip had ever seen, bigger than the flood year, bigger than the dog with the broken chain.

Then Pip understood. It wasn't scared of the choir.

It was scared of Wendell.

Wendell stood in the middle of the singing with their eyes closed and their hands open at their sides, palms up, the way Pip drew people when they were giving something away. And around Wendell's wrists, faint as breath on cold glass, was light. Not ward-light. Not charm-light. Something older, the color of the sky was supposed to be.

Pip squeezed Wendell's sleeve once, hard, the way you hold someone's hand at the doctor's, and then let go, because this was the part Wendell had to do alone.

The monster roared and the torches all went out at once.

* * *

**Wendell**

In the dark, Wendell spoke the name.

It was a small name, when it came down to it. They had carried it out of the throne room folded inside a stolen memory, wrapped in layers of the crown-thing's own hoarded past — its first feeding, its first fear, the long eons when it had been nothing but a hunger drifting between stars looking for a world soft enough to eat. And at the very bottom of all that appetite, worn smooth like a pebble in a riverbed, was what it had been called before it learned to wear crowns.

*Vess.*

Just that. A sound like wind finding a crack in a wall.

When Wendell said it aloud, the square *inverted*. The darkness peeled back like wet paper, and for one impossible heartbeat Wendell saw the thing as it truly was — vast, gray, endless, a wound in the shape of a king — and then the choir's song caught it, caught *Vess*, and began to unravel it thread by thread.

King Aldous screamed. Not the parasite's many-voiced shriek but a single, cracked, human sound, and suddenly the man was *in* his own eyes again, weeping, his hands rising to claw at the crown as if waking from a forty-year nightmare into a body that had done terrible things without him.

"It sang to me," the king sobbed, tearing at the metal. "Every night, it sang me to sleep — I only wanted the city safe — I only ever wanted —"

"I know," Wendell said, and meant it, because they of all people knew what it was to have your memories taken and be told it was for your own good.

The crown came free.

It hit the flagstones with a sound like a bell falling, and where it landed the stones blackened and curled, and the thing inside it thrashed, shrinking, starving, its stolen decades burning off it like fog in sun. The choir sang louder. Brava's voice cracked and held. Pip's small clear note threaded through the harmony like a needle pulling shut a wound.

And then the song reached its last verse, and stopped.

Because the last verse needed a willing memory. The wards were empty vessels, and vessels did not fill themselves. Sefa turned. Brava turned. Even the ruined king turned, tears cutting tracks through the grime of his stolen reign.

Wendell stepped forward, already reaching into themselves, into the newly returned country of their own childhood — the mother's voice, the smell of ink and river water, the name on the coin, the seven-year-old Pip had drawn with such careful, hopeful lines.

"Wendell." Sefa's voice broke on the name. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," Wendell said again, gently this time. "But listen — I'm not losing everything. I'm choosing what I keep." Their hands glowed brighter. "I keep tonight. I keep all of you. The rest —" they smiled, and it was the saddest and most certain smile any of them had ever seen, "— the rest goes to the sky. Someone should get some use out of it."

They poured themselves out.

Light rose from Wendell like heat-shimmer, like a flock of white birds, carrying forty years of a stolen childhood home to the dark above the city — and the dark *remembered how to be a sky*. One star kindled. Then ten. Then a hundred, a thousand, spilling back into the heavens like coins from a cut purse, and the crowd that had crept into the square's edges — the whole waking city, drawn by the singing — gasped as one.

Wendell swayed. Sefa caught them before they fell.

"What's left?" Brava asked hoarsely, holding Wendell's other arm.

Wendell frowned, searching. Then, slowly, they looked at each of them in turn — Sefa, Brava, Pip, even the weeping king — with the bright, bewildered look of someone meeting dear friends for the first time.

"I don't know why," Wendell said, "but I feel like I've known you my whole life."

Pip laughed, wet and joyful, and hugged them around the waist, and above them the stars kept coming, filling the sky over Vespergate like a city lighting its lamps at dusk.

Only Pip, watching Wendell's face, saw the flicker of something else there — a shadow behind the new-made smile, a whisper of the self that had been given away — and wondered, with a child's terrible clarity, whether a memory poured into the sky was ever really gone.

Because far overhead, among the returning stars, one light burned a little warmer than the rest.

And it seemed, just sometimes, to twinkle in time with the choir's unfinished song.
