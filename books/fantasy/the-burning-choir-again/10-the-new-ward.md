## 10. The New Ward

Pip knows three things about the ruined square before anyone says them out loud.

One: it smells like rain that never fell. Two: the sky is still empty, and empty skies make Pip's teeth ache. Three: Wendell is going to do something that will hurt, and everyone in the choir already knows it except maybe Wendell, and maybe not even that.

Pip counts things when words get too big. It is a good trick. Words are heavy for Pip; they sit in the chest like wet stones. Numbers are light. Numbers go where you point them.

So Pip counts the choir standing in a broken circle around the old ward-stone: Sefa with her hands open like she is catching something invisible. Brava in her warden coat with the buttons torn off, because Brava tore them off herself and said good riddance. Anselm is not there. Anselm is a bell now, or inside a bell, or the bell remembers him so hard he might as well be there. Pip holds up four fingers anyway, then folds one down. Four minus one. The choir breathes together, and even missing one, it breathes.

Wendell kneels at the ward-stone. The stone is older than the walls of Vespergate, Sefa said once, and Pip believes it, because the stone looks tired the way only very old things look tired. Cracks run through it like the lines on Mama's palm, back when Pip had a mama, back before the crown-thing ate her Sundays one by one until there was no Sunday left to visit.

The crown-thing is gone now. Unraveled. Wendell spoke its name into the dark and the name was small — that surprised Pip most of all, how small the name was, smaller than *bread*, smaller than *yes* — and King Aldous woke up crying on his throne like a man coming out of a long fever, and the thing wearing him came apart like smoke remembering it used to be a fire.

But the wards are hungry. That is the rule nobody made and everybody has to keep. Wards eat memory. That is what they were always eating, all those years, all those stolen jars of other people's lives poured into the border stones while the city slept and called it safety.

Sefa said: *It must be given, not taken. A willing memory. That is the whole law.*

And Wendell looked at the jar in their hands — the little glass bottle with the wax seal, the one the crown-thing filled while pretending to bargain, the one holding seven years of a child named Ishikawa, W., who laughed at pigeons and was afraid of deep water and loved somebody enough to cry about it — and Wendell said, quiet as a coin dropped in a well:

"It was never mine to keep anyway."

Pip knew then. Pip always knows first. Knowing first is not a gift. But Pip takes Wendell's sleeve and pulls, pulls hard, and makes the sound that means *no*, the low buzz in the throat that Anselm taught them, the one that carries farther than shouting.

Wendell crouches down to Pip's height. Wendell's eyes are wet but steady, like a lamp in wind.

"Pip," Wendell says. "I got these memories back an hour ago. I don't know the person in them. I never got to know them. But I know you." Wendell taps Pip's chest, gentle, right over the counting place. "I know Sefa. I know Brava. I know a bell that hums. You're my family. The child in this bottle — I'd just be keeping them in the dark forever. This way they get to be the sky."

Pip does not have words for that. So Pip does the other thing Pip can do.

Pip draws.

* * *

Pip draws fast, on the back of a playbill fluttering against the rubble, charcoal stub clutched like a knife. Pip draws the child from the picture Pip gave Wendell last week — the seven-year-old with the crooked smile, standing under drawn stars. Then Pip draws Wendell beside the child, same person, two ages, holding hands across the paper. Then Pip draws the whole choir around them both, and above everything, stars, dozens of stars, scribbled so hard the paper shines.

Pip holds it up.

Wendell looks at it a long time. Longer than a look should take. Then Wendell laughs — a cracked, real laugh, the kind that comes out sideways — and says, "You're better at arguments than Brava," and Brava says, "That's true," and nobody argues.

Wendell presses the drawing flat against the ward-stone, just for a moment, like a promise. Then Wendell stands, and works the wax seal off the bottle with their thumbnail, and the smell that comes out is the smell of summer, somehow, here in the cold ruin — cut grass and river water and bread burning slightly in a good way.

"Will it hurt?" Sefa asks. Not stopping Wendell. Just asking, because Sefa asks everything, that is how she leads.

"Like setting down something heavy," Wendell says. "I think. I hope." A pause. "If I forget you —"

"You won't," Pip writes on the slate, because some things are worth the heavy words. "Family isn't memory. Family is choosing. You chose us. We choose you. That part stays."

Wendell reads it twice. Wendell mouths the last sentence like tasting it. Then Wendell tips the bottle over the stone.

* * *

The memory goes in like light goes through water.

Pip sees it happen and will spend years trying to draw it and never quite manage, but this is what happens: the childhood pours upward, silver and slow, a ribbon of small bright moments — a pigeon startled into flight, a hand held crossing a bridge, a mother's voice singing badly on purpose to make a child laugh — and the ward-stone drinks it, and the cracks in the stone begin to close like wounds remembering how to be skin.

Then the light runs along the ground. Out of the square. Down the streets. Along the borders of Vespergate, mile after mile, the old wards waking one by one, fed at last by something given instead of stolen.

And the sky —

The sky begins to come back.

Not all at once. One star, high over the north wall, small and stubborn as a nail. Then another. Then three. Then Pip loses count, which has never happened before, which is the best thing that has ever happened, because Pip's numbers finally ran out of room. Stars come back like a city lighting its windows at dusk, like a crowd standing up all at once, like every candle in every jar Wendell ever sold unstoppering themselves at the same time.

Brava falls to her knees. Sefa starts singing — not an unbinding song, nothing with edges, just a low sweet wandering tune with no name, and the choir picks it up, and the square fills with voices, and the stars fill the sky, and somewhere in the palace a king who is only a man now stands at his window and weeps at all that light.

Wendell sways. Wendell catches themself on the stone. And when Wendell turns around, Wendell's face is doing something complicated — grieving and glad at once, the way faces do when they mean it.

"Do I still know you?" Pip writes, holding the slate up, heart hammering.

Wendell crosses the square and takes the slate and reads it, and Pip watches — really watches, counting Wendell's blinks, counting breaths — and Wendell smiles the crooked smile. The child's smile. Both smiles at once, which should not be possible and is.

"I don't remember learning your name," Wendell says slowly. "But I know it. It's in my hands. Look —" Wendell signs *Pip* in the air, the shape they always use, the little flick and tap. "My hands remembered you even if the rest of me had to give the rest away. Hands are loyal like that."

Pip throws both arms around Wendell and holds on, and Wendell holds back, and the choir piles in around them, Sefa laughing, Brava pretending not to cry and failing completely, and overhead the stars keep arriving, patient and endless, like they were only ever waiting to be invited home.

* * *

They carry Wendell through the streets.

Nobody plans it. Brava just hoists Wendell onto her shoulders like a sack of very precious flour, and Sefa starts the walking song, the one with the beat like boots, and the choir moves, and the city moves with them. Doors open. People come out in their nightclothes, faces tipped up to a sky they had stopped believing in, and when they see the choir — the disgraced warden, the mute child on Brava's hip, the hedge-priest's bell tolling itself softly somewhere behind, the memory-thief riding high with starlight in their hair — the cheering starts.

It rolls down Copperlane and up Chandelier Row. It crosses the market where Wendell used to sell bottled lives to people who didn't ask questions. Someone presses a hot meat pie into Pip's hands. Someone else puts flowers in Sefa's hood. A woman grabs Wendell's boot and shouts, "Was it you? Was it you who brought them back?" and Wendell calls down, "It was all of us!" and the cheer that follows nearly knocks Brava over.

Pip sits on Brava's arm and draws the whole walk without stopping. The crowds. The lights. The bell humming Anselm's last song in time with the footsteps. Wendell above it all, arms out, laughing, lighter than Pip has ever seen anyone — because Wendell gave away the heaviest thing a person can carry, and what was left was just a person, a chosen person, a family person.

At the door of the cistern-house — their house now, officially, since Brava bullied the new council into granting it before breakfast — Wendell slides down off Brava's shoulders and stands a moment in the doorway, looking at the shabby room, the mismatched chairs, the slate covered in Pip's drawings, the bell hanging in the corner that hums when the wind finds it.

"Home," Wendell says, testing the word. It fits.

Inside, Pip does the last drawing of the night. Big paper, saved for big days. Five figures under a full sky of stars: Sefa tall with her hands open, Brava broad with her torn-off-button coat, the bell floating with little music-lines coming out of it, Wendell smiling crooked, and Pip in front with the charcoal, drawing the drawing, which Pip includes because it is true — Pip is always drawing them, and they are always worth drawing.

Underneath, Pip prints the title in careful letters, the hardest writing yet, taking as long as it needs to take:

**THE CHOIR.**

Wendell studies it for a long moment. Then Wendell gets the charcoal and adds one small figure Pip missed — no. Not missed. A figure Pip left space for, without knowing why. A sixth figure, kneeling, hands open, face turned up toward the returned stars.

Anselm.

"There," Wendell says softly. "Now it's finished."

Outside, Vespergate glows under more stars than Pip can count, and for once Pip doesn't try. Some things are bigger than numbers. Pip falls asleep that night in a room full of breathing family, under a sky that remembers how to shine, dreaming in pictures — and in the dream, far off past the walls, past the wards, something vast and old and grateful turns over in its sleep beneath the earth, and murmurs a small, small name, and it sounds almost like *thank you*.
