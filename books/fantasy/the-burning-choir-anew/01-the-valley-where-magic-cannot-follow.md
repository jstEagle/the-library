## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The rain in Hollowmere never fell properly. It came down in a thin grey drizzle that seemed to forget, halfway to the ground, what it was supposed to be doing, and it had done so for the eleven years August Kavanagh had lived there. He had learned not to mind. Rain that forgot itself could not be called, could not be shaped, could not answer him when he whispered to it in the old tongue his mother had bled into his bones before he was old enough to refuse.

He was mending the roof of the Widow Corren's smokehouse when he felt it — or rather, when he felt nothing, which was the whole point of Hollowmere and the only reason he had stayed past the first winter, past the second, past all the winters since.

Nothing. The valley's great gift. A silence so complete it pressed against the skin like wool.

He knelt on the wet shingles with a hammer in one hand and a fistful of wooden pegs between his teeth, and he let the silence hold him the way a mother holds a child she does not entirely trust. Below him, the village went about its small gray business: smoke from chimneys that burned peat instead of wood because wood was scarce and always had been; goats complaining in their pens; old Tammas Hale arguing with his son about fence posts in the flat, uninflected voices of people who had lived too long where nothing changed. Hollowmere sat at the bottom of its bowl of hills like water at the bottom of a cup, still and shallow and safe.

Magic could not follow him here. That was what the elders said, what everyone said, what he told himself on the bad nights when dreams of thunder woke him with his hands raised and sparks crawling along his knuckles that died before they could become anything at all. The valley was dead to the old powers. Whatever ran beneath the world — whatever had once answered his family's songs across nine generations — could not reach into this place. The stones drank it. The air smothered it. Even prayer, the villagers said, went nowhere in Hollowmere. You could whisper at the sky until you were hoarse and the sky would simply go on being the sky, indifferent as a wall.

August had come here at nineteen with blistered feet and a name he did not use, and he had learned to mend roofs.

It was honest work. It required nothing of him but his hands and his patience, and neither of those had ever called down lightning. He liked the arithmetic of it: a leaking roof needed pegs and pitch and a straight back, and when it was mended, it was mended, and no one wept, and nothing burned. There was no arithmetic like that for the things he used to do. He had once held back a flood with a word. He had once — and here his mind flinched away, as it always did, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth — he had once been asked to do something with a storm that he had refused, and then something else that he had not refused fast enough, and after that there had been ash, and after the ash there had been exile, and after the exile there was this: shingles, pegs, drizzle, silence.

He hammered a peg home and listened to the small solid sound of it. Good. Honest. Done.

"August!" Widow Corren's voice floated up from below, thin and cracked as her chimney smoke. "You'll fall and crack your skull and I'll have to bury you next to my husband, and he'll complain."

"I'm careful," he called back.

"You're *careless*," she said, with the particular satisfaction of a woman who had been right about everything for eighty years. "There's a difference. Come down when you've finished the north slope. There's soup."

There was always soup. Soup and silence and drizzle and roofs. Eleven years of it, and he had almost — *almost* — stopped listening for thunder.

* * *

He saw the pilgrim first as a dark seam in the grey of the road.

Hollowmere sat at the end of a track that no one maintained because no one traveled it; the valley had one road in and one road out, and both were more suggestion than path, winding up through the hills to a pass that was snow-choked eight months of the year. Merchants did not bother. Pilgrims had stopped bothering decades ago, when the shrines along the high road crumbled and the gods they honored were declared, by scholars far away who had never heard of Hollowmere, to be either dead or sleeping or — this was the theory Sera's order would later make fashionable — merely quiet, the way a held breath is quiet.

So when August looked up from the trough where he was washing the pitch off his hands and saw a figure crawling along the last mile of the valley road, his first thought was not wonder. His first thought was a dull, practiced dread, the kind that lives in the body rather than the mind: *nothing comes here. Nothing is supposed to come here.*

The figure was on hands and knees. As August crossed the muddy square toward it — walking quickly now, the other villagers pausing in doorways to watch, because a crawling stranger was the most interesting thing Hollowmere had produced in a season — he could see that the man had been walking for a long time before he'd started crawling. His boots were worn through at the soles, the leather gone the color of old blood. His cloak was travel-ruined, stiff with dried mud and older stains. He was young, maybe, though it was hard to tell; hunger and distance had sanded the years off his face until only the bones remained.

And he was talking. Muttering. The same phrase over and over, in a voice scraped down to a thread:

"She said — she said bring it to the silent valley — she said it can't follow me there —"

August crouched beside him. Up close the pilgrim smelled of sweat and road dust and something else underneath, something faintly wrong, like the smell of a struck match in a closed room. His eyes were unfocused, rolling, searching the grey sky as if he expected it to open.

"Here." August got an arm under the man's shoulders. He was light in the terrible way of someone who has eaten very little for a very long time. "You're in Hollowmere. You made it. Whatever you were running from—"

"It doesn't run," the pilgrim whispered. His hand came up — trembling, blackened at the fingertips with frostbite or worse — and clutched at August's collar with surprising strength. "It *waits*. She said — the silent valley — she said magic cannot follow —"

"And it can't," August said, and hated how steady his voice sounded, how easily the lie came after eleven years of practice. "Nothing follows anyone here. Let's get you warm."

The pilgrim's eyes found his then, focused all at once, sharp and terrified and lucid, and August felt the look of them like a hook going in under his ribs.

"You live here," the man breathed. "In the dead place. You're not — you don't hear it? All the way across the moors, every night, I could hear it. Singing. Like bells underwater. And when I crossed the pass it stopped, it *stopped*, oh thank every god that ever was, it stopped—" He laughed, a broken little sound. "Silence. Real silence. I'd forgotten what it was."

August had not forgotten. August thought about it every day, the way a man with a healed wound thinks about the wound: not often, but completely, whenever he thinks of it at all.

Inside the widow's kitchen, they fed the pilgrim soup he was too far gone to taste and tea he was too far gone to drink, and he slept by the fire without waking, breathing in a shallow, whistling way that August did not like at all. The frostbite had gone black to the wrist on his left hand. There was fever building behind his eyes like weather.

It was while they were settling him — August lifting the man's pack off his shoulders, meaning only to set it aside — that he felt the weight of it. Wrong weight. Heavy in a way that had nothing to do with pounds.

"You shouldn't—" the pilgrim murmured, half-asleep, his good hand twitching toward the pack. "Careful. She said. Careful with it. It's *listening*."

Every hair on August's arms stood up.

* * *

He should have left the pack alone. He knew that even as he knelt by the fire long after the widow had gone to bed, long after the pilgrim's breathing had gone ragged and quick, and opened it.

But eleven years of silence makes a man curious about sound. Eleven years of nothing makes a man reckless about *something*. And besides — and this was the truth he would not say aloud, not to the elders, not to himself — besides, the pack had hummed. Faintly, briefly, when his fingers first brushed the canvas. A note so low he might have imagined it. Might have. Except that August Kavanagh had spent his childhood learning the difference between a hum and a memory of a hum, and this was a hum.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with handling, was a stone the size of a heart.

It was not beautiful. It was river-smooth and grey-brown, the kind of stone any child might pocket from any streambed in the world. But it was warm — warm through the cloth, warm like a living thing — and when August peeled the wrapping back another fold, the warmth became a pulse, and the pulse became a sound, and the sound was singing.

Not loudly. Not even audibly, quite. It was the singing the pilgrim had described: bells underwater, a choir heard through a wall, a hymn remembered by someone who has forgotten all the words except the feeling of them. August had not heard anything sing in eleven years. The valley killed such things. The valley was *supposed* to kill such things.

His hands began to shake.

Prayer-stone. He knew the shape of the craft if not the making of it — every child of the Stormbound line had been taught the old lore whether they wanted it or not, his mother insistent, his mother desperate, his mother saying *you must know it even if you never use it, August, knowing is not calling, knowing is only a door you can choose not to walk through.* Prayer-stones were vessels. Ordinary rock, sometimes bone, sometimes glass, prepared and given to the faithful so that a prayer spoken over it would be caught and carried — carried to a shrine, to an oracle, to a god. They were common enough once, in the world outside. In the world outside they had been dead trinkets for a generation, cold as gravel, because the gods had stopped answering and everyone knew it.

This one was alive. This one was *full*. It pulsed against his palms like a second heartbeat, and the song in it was not empty, was not ambient, was not the general hum of the divine. It was carrying something. A message, folded tight, waiting to be delivered.

"No," August said aloud, to the stone, to the fire, to the dead air of the valley. "No. Not here. Not me."

The stone sang on, patient as water.

He should wake the pilgrim. He should carry the thing to the elders' hall tonight, tonight, before dawn, and let old Mother Ostrey decide, because that was the law of Hollowmere and he had obeyed the law of Hollowmere for eleven years without exception, because obedience was the shape his penance took. But the pilgrim's breath had gone worse — wet, rattling, each exhale a small drowning — and August's eyes kept coming back to the stone, and the stone's song kept threading into his ears, and underneath the fear, underneath the dread, underneath eleven years of careful silence, something in him that he had buried alive turned over in its grave and *listened*.

He told himself he was only checking it. Only holding it to the light to see the maker's mark, the sigil of whichever shrine had issued it, so the elders would know where it came from. He turned it over in his shaking hands.

There was no maker's mark. There was only a single word, scratched deep into the stone's surface by some desperate stylus, in a hand that had gouged the letters crooked and overlapping, written by someone in a hurry or in terror or both:

*ALDRIC.*

The king's name.

August's whisper escaped him before he could stop it — barely a breath, barely a sound, the involuntary syllable of a man reading aloud to himself in shock:

"Aldric?"

The stone answered.

Later — much later, standing in the widow's dark kitchen with the fire burned to embers and the pilgrim dead or nearly dead in the loft above — August would try to describe what happened, and fail, and try again, and fail better. The stone did not speak. The stone did not glow. What it did was *open*, the way a fist opens, the way a throat opens, and everything it had been carrying poured out through the room in a single instant that lasted, somehow, longer than an instant: a pressure, a presence, a vast attention swinging around like a lighthouse beam and fixing itself —

on him.

On *him*, in the valley where magic could not follow, in the dead place, in the silence he had bought with exile and ash and his mother's grave. Something out there in the wide awake world had heard him. One whisper, half a syllable, less than a prayer — and something had *answered*, swift and glad and hungry, the way a hound answers a whistle, and the answering filled the kitchen from floor to rafters with a sense of enormous eyes.

And through the roaring of his own blood, August heard it. Not words. Worse than words. An impression, pressed into him like a seal into wax, gentle as a lover's hand and total as a verdict:

*I know that voice. Stormborn. I have missed that voice. Soon.*

Then it was gone. The stone went cold in his hands — cold as gravel, dead as the gods were supposed to be — its burden delivered, its long burning finally spent. Somewhere above him the pilgrim's rattling breath hitched, stuttered, and did not resume, and August did not move, could not move, stood in the ember-light holding a dead stone and a dead man's errand while the drizzle ticked against the window like fingernails.

*Aldric,* the stone had carried. The king was dying. Or the king was already dead. Or — and this was the thought that made August sit down slowly on the cold flagstones, the stone still clutched in his fist like a coal that had burned through to the bone — or the king was neither dying nor dead, and something else was answering prayers in his voice, something patient and glad and hungry, something that had just crossed the impenetrable silence of Hollowmere as if the valley's famous deadness were a curtain and not a wall.

Something that knew his bloodline's name.

Outside, far off over the hills, beyond the pass, beyond the moors, in the world he had renounced — thunder rolled. Once. Soft and low and unmistakable, like a knock at a door.

Hollowmere's magic-dead air swallowed it, as it swallowed everything.

But August had heard it. And the worst part — the part he would carry into every chapter that followed — was that some buried, starved, exiled piece of him had heard it and *leaned toward it*, the way a drowning man leans toward light.

The past had found him. It had taken eleven years, and it had crawled the last mile on its knees, and it was lying dead in the loft above him with a king's name carved in its hand.

August put the cold stone in his pocket, because he could not make himself leave it on the table, and he did not sleep at all.
