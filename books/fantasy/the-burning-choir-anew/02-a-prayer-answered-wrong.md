## 2. A Prayer Answered Wrong

August did not sleep that night.

He sat on the floor of his small house with the prayer-stone cradled in a fold of wool, the way one might hold a sleeping bird or an unexploded shell of thunder-iron — both of which he had handled in another life, in another valley, under another sky. The stone was warm. That was the first wrongness. Hollowmere's nights went cold enough to frost the inside of windows by mid-autumn; nothing in this valley held heat after sundown except a living body and a banked fire. The stone held both and neither. It lay against his palms like something freshly dead and still cooling, and it hummed.

Not loudly. Not the way it had hummed when it answered him at the pilgrim's side, when his own voice had slipped out of him unbidden — *who carries you* — and the stone had flared like a struck match. Now it was quieter. A sound felt more than heard, up through the bones of his wrists, into the place behind his sternum where he kept everything he refused to think about.

Outside, Hollowmere slept the way it always slept: completely, gratefully, without dreams worth mentioning. The valley was magic-dead the way a drained marsh is water-dead. Whatever thin currents moved the wider world — prayers rising, gods listening, the slow respiration of power beneath the skin of things — none of it reached here. The elders said the valley had been scoured clean generations ago, though no one agreed on what had done the scouring. August had never cared. He had come here to be a man to whom nothing happened, and for eleven years nothing had, beautifully, mercifully, happened.

Then a dying man had crawled up the sheep-track with a stone in his fist, and August had whispered to it without meaning to, and it had answered.

He turned it over in the lamplight. It was smaller than he'd remembered from the road — a grey oval, river-smoothed, veined with hairline threads of silver that shifted when he wasn't looking directly at them. Prayer-stones were old craft. His mother had carried one, once, before carrying such things became a death sentence for her line. They were made to hold a prayer the way a bottle holds a letter: sealed, patient, delivered only to the one named inside. This one had been meant for someone else. He was almost certain of it now. The dying pilgrim — Vane, Sera Vane, the sister who would come looking — had babbled a name through cracked lips, and it hadn't been *Kavanagh*. But the stone had answered *him*, and stones did not make mistakes.

Stones were not supposed to burn themselves out delivering messages to the wrong man, either.

The silver veins pulsed. August's breath went shallow. He knew what was coming the way animals know weather, and he set the stone down on the hearthstone and stepped back anyway, as if distance would save him.

It spoke.

Not in sound — the walls of his house stayed still, the lamp flame didn't so much as lean — but in his head, in a voice like a voice remembered rather than heard, worn smooth by repetition:

*The king is dying. The king is dying. What answers in his name is not the king.*

Then the light in the veins guttered, browned, and went out, all along every thread at once, like a city losing its lamps street by street. The warmth died with it. The stone became what it appeared to be: a rock, a grey egg of nothing, cold as the floor of the world.

August stood in his kitchen for a long time, listening to his own heart.

*What answers in his name is not the king.*

He thought: I don't know any king. He thought: I am a roofer. I mend thatch and split shingles and I have not called lightning in eleven years and I will not call it tonight. He thought these things in the flat, stubborn tone of a man reciting a list of possessions while his house burns down around him.

Because he did know a king. Everyone knew King Aldric — the Merciful, the Beloved, the king whose face was stamped on coins and carved over courthouse doors in towns August would never see again. And everyone knew the stories that had started drifting even into Hollowmere's sealed quiet, brought by traders who didn't stay long: the king had grown strange. Too generous. Too smooth. Smiling at executions. Speaking blessings that arrived a half-second before his lips moved, as if the words were being played back.

August had filed those stories away with all the other news of the outer world, in the drawer marked *not my problem*, and had kept the drawer locked for eleven years.

The stone sat dead on his hearthstone and the drawer hung open.

* * *

In the morning he took it to the elders, because there was nowhere else to take it, and because some part of him — the part that still believed institutions could save a person, poor ruined fool — hoped they would tell him he'd imagined it.

They met in the meeting-house, which had been a shrine once, before the icons were pried off the walls and burned in the square. Nine elders on nine benches arranged in a horseshoe, and August standing in the open end of it like a defendant, which, he understood within moments, was exactly what he was.

Maren Ostrow spoke for them, as she usually did. She was seventy, built like a fence post, with hands that had pulled calves and buried husbands and feared very little. She looked at the dead stone where it lay on the table between them the way she'd look at a snake in a milk pail.

"Say it again," she said. "All of it."

So he said it again. The pilgrim on the track, the wound in his belly gone black at the edges, the stone that hummed. His whisper. The flare of light. The message in the night. *What answers in his name is not the king.* He kept his voice level. He had gotten good, over eleven years, at saying terrible things in a level voice; it was one of the three skills exile taught, along with patience and how to sleep anywhere.

When he finished, the silence had a texture to it, dense and grainy, like flour in the air of a mill.

"You touched it," said Old Hollis, who was mostly deaf and mostly blind and missed nothing. "You put your hands on a live prayer-stone."

"It was in a dying man's hand. Someone had to take it."

"Someone had to bury it," Maren said. "With him. In the ground, deep, where the valley could do its work." She leaned forward. "Do you understand what you're holding, Kavanagh? Do you understand what it means that it woke?"

"I know what it said."

"I know what it *means*." Her voice dropped, and the drop was worse than any shout. "It means the valley isn't holding. Eleven years we've told ourselves the border was a wall. It's a sieve, boy. Things leak through. Small things. A humming stone. A whisper from a man who—" She stopped herself. She didn't finish the sentence, and her not finishing it was louder than if she had: *a man who used to call storms.*

"The stone is dead," August said. "Look at it. Burnt out. It gave its message and it's done."

"And the message is done with *you*?" Hollis asked mildly, and August had no answer, and everyone saw that he had no answer.

Maren stood. She came around the table and stood close enough that he could smell the juniper salve she rubbed into her knuckles, and she looked up at him — she barely reached his shoulder — with an expression he had last seen on his mother's face the day she pushed him onto the southbound cart and told him never to write.

"Destroy it," she said. "Today. In the lime kiln, where nothing survives. Then you'll stand in the square and swear before the whole valley that you touched nothing else, called nothing, kept nothing. And then, August—" and here her voice gentled, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet— "then you pack a bag. Because whether you mean to or not, you've opened a door, and doors don't care whose house they're in."

* * *

He walked up to the lime kiln that afternoon with the stone in his pocket and did not throw it in.

He told himself he was going to. He climbed the track above the village with the kiln's white smoke smearing the sky ahead of him, and he rehearsed the oath in his head, and his hand found the stone in his pocket — cold now, truly cold, dead weight — and he stopped at the lip of the burning pit and stood there while the heat rolled up and bent the air.

Below him the valley spread out gold and green in the late light. His roof. His neighbors' roofs, forty of them, each shingle laid by his own hands over the years, each one a small argument that a man could be defined by what he built instead of what he'd broken. Smoke rising straight from chimneys because storms never came here, never, the valley wouldn't allow them, and he had spent eleven years learning to love a sky that never argued back.

And somewhere beyond the mountains, past the passes he'd crossed half-dead and hollow-eyed, a king was dying, and something else was answering prayers in his voice, and a stone had crossed a hundred miles of magic-dead earth to say so specifically, particularly, *personally* to August Kavanagh.

Stones didn't make mistakes. His mother had told him that too, kneeling beside him in a cellar while soldiers searched overhead, her hand pressed flat over his mouth, her other hand holding a stone just like this one. *They find their way, my love. That's the whole horror of them. You can't send a prayer wrong. You can only refuse to answer it.*

She had refused. It had killed her. He had run, and it had saved him, and neither fact had ever balanced the other.

August took the stone out of his pocket and held it over the kiln.

The heat came up in waves. One good throw. One arc of his arm and it would be slag and fume, and Maren would nod, and he would swear his oath, and the drawer marked *not my problem* would close again, and maybe — maybe — the door would swing shut behind him and the valley would go back to holding.

His arm trembled. Not from the weight. The stone weighed nothing.

*What answers in his name is not the king.*

If it was true — if even half of it was true — then somewhere a country full of people was praying to a thing that wore their king like a coat, and every prayer was being received, and answered, and the answers were coming out *wrong*, smooth and smiling and a half-second early. And the only person in the world who knew it had happened was standing on a hillside above a village of roofers and shepherds, deciding whether to be comfortable or honest.

He thought about the pilgrim's face at the end. Not the pain — he'd seen plenty of that, had caused some of it, in the life before. It was the confusion. The man had died trying to understand why his sister's stone had lit up for a stranger mending a fence, and August had lied to him, gently, uselessly: *it's just heat shimmer, friend, rest now.* The man had known he was lying. Dying men always know. He had gripped August's wrist anyway, hard, with the last strength he had, and pressed the stone into his palm, and his eyes had said what his throat could no longer shape: *take it, take it, someone has to.*

August closed his hand around the stone and stepped back from the kiln.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to Maren, to the valley, to the version of himself he'd spent eleven years building out of thatch and silence. "I'm sorry. I can't."

* * *

He hid it badly, at first, the way people hide things they already plan to retrieve. Under a floorboard. Behind a loose brick. Each spot lasted a day before his skin crawled and he moved it, because hiding it near himself felt like sleeping next to an open grave, and hiding it far away felt worse, felt like abandonment, and he could not have explained the difference to anyone, least of all to himself.

On the third night he wrapped it in oilcloth, waxed and tied, and carried it up past the terraces to the old cairn on the shoulder of the mountain — a pile of stones raised by hands centuries dead, before the valley was scoured, before anyone decided holiness was a thing to be drained out of the ground like poison gas. He wedged the bundle deep in the cairn's heart, among rocks that had been sitting in the same places since before his grandmother's grandmother, and he told himself it was temporary. Until he decided. Until he understood. Until—

Until the dogs began to bark in the village below, all of them at once, and did not stop.

August stood in the dark with his hands still in the cairn and listened. Dogs in Hollowmere barked at foxes, at drunks, at thunder that never came. They did not bark like this — sustained, frantic, a chorus with a single note of fear running through it — unless something was coming up the valley road.

He descended faster than wisdom allowed, taking the switchbacks two at a time, and came out onto the last terrace above the village in time to see lanterns converging on the gate, and beyond the gate, on the moonlit road, riders.

Four of them. Sitting their horses with the stillness of men who had ridden a long time and were in no hurry now, because whatever they had come for was not going anywhere. Even at this distance August could see the quality of their gear — not bandit leather, not trader's patchwork, but matched dark harness, and on the lead rider's shoulder, catching the lantern-light, a badge of office he had hoped never to see again in his life.

A crown, worked in silver, over a coiled serpent.

Crown-hunters. The king's own hounds, sent out after relics and rogues and runaway bloodlines, and they had come to Hollowmere, to the one valley in the world where nothing was supposed to be able to follow.

The wind shifted and brought their voices up the slope, thin and clear across the cold air. The lead rider was speaking to the gate-wardens in a pleasant, reasonable tone, the tone of a man reading from a document he had read many times.

"—a pilgrim, deceased, bearing stolen property of the Crown. We are authorized to recover it and to question any persons who may have handled it." A pause, and then, lightly, almost kindly: "We are told one of your people has been having interesting dreams lately."

August's hand went to his chest, to the empty place where the stone's warmth had lived for three days, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Maren had been right about everything. The valley was a sieve. Exile was not safety. It had never been safety. It had been delay — a debt deferred, gathering interest in the dark — and the collectors had finally found his door.

Behind him, up on the mountain, the cairn stood silent over its secret. Below him, the lanterns were already moving through the gate.

There was, he realized, a woman coming down the same road behind those riders, walking, alone, with a soldier's stride and a pilgrim's staff — the dead man's sister, arriving late to a conversation that had already begun without her — and August Kavanagh, roofer, liar, heir of the Stormbound line, turned and ran for his house to pack.
