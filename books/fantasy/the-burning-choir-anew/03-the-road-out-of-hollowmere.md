## 3. The Road Out of Hollowmere

The dogs came first.

August heard them before dawn, three days after he buried the pilgrim under the rowan tree behind his cottage — a thin, ragged baying threading through the fog that pooled in Hollowmere like milk poured over stone. He lay still beneath his wool blanket and listened the way he had learned to listen in another life: not with his ears but with the small hairs on his arms, the ache behind his teeth, the old animal part of him that remembered what it meant when men brought dogs into a place like this.

Hollowmere was a valley without magic. That was its whole purpose, its whole holiness, if you wanted to call it holy. The elders said the gods could not see into it, that prayers died at the ridgeline like birds striking glass, that a man who came here carrying power would set it down at the edge the way a traveler sets down a heavy pack. For eleven years August had believed it — or told himself he believed it, which amounted to nearly the same thing on most mornings.

But the prayer-stone was under his floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, humming.

He felt it now the way you feel a second heartbeat, faint and wrong, down where your own pulse should be alone. The dogs were coming up the shepherd's track from the south pass, and they were not valley dogs. Valley dogs did not wear harnesses that clinked. Valley dogs did not move in formation.

August rose, dressed in the dark, and lifted the loose stone from beside his hearth.

The prayer-stone lay in its nest of rags like an egg laid by something that should never have nested. It had been white once, the pilgrim's sister had said — no. He hadn't met the sister yet. It had been white once, the dying man had gasped, white as a chapel tooth, and now veins of amber crawled across its surface, pulsing slow as a sleeping throat. When August's fingers closed around it, warmth flooded up his wrist, past his elbow, into the hollow of his shoulder where the storm-song used to live, and for one terrible half-second the fog outside lit blue-white with silent lightning.

He dropped it back into the rags. Outside, the dogs went quiet all at once, the way animals do when they've found what they're looking for.

*Eleven years*, he thought, standing barefoot on his cold floor with a stolen god's-message against his chest. *Eleven years of mending roofs and hauling water and learning to sleep through thunder without flinching.* And it had taken one dying stranger crawling out of the mist to undo all of it.

A knock at his door. Not the dogs' handler — too polite for that. Three knocks, spaced evenly, the knuckles of someone who had knocked on many doors in many weather and expected them all to open.

"Master Kavanagh." A woman's voice, low and roughened, pitched to carry no farther than the doorframe. "I know you're awake. I watched your chimney smoke die twenty minutes ago, and no one banks a fire that fast unless they're leaving or hiding. I'm betting on hiding."

August said nothing. His hand drifted toward the iron poker by the hearth.

"I'm Sera Vane," the voice went on. "Brother Aldous Vane was my brother. The man you buried under the rowan tree — don't look surprised, I've been following his trail for two months and the crows marked every campsite. I want the stone. And then I want to be gone from this valley before the men with the dogs find either of us."

"And if I give it to you," August said quietly, "you'll take it away and I'll go back to mending roofs."

A pause. Then, dry as autumn grass: "You say that like it's a life. Mending roofs." Another pause, shorter. "Open the door, Master Kavanagh. The hunters reached the south shrine at moonset. You have perhaps half an hour, and I'd rather spend it walking than talking."

He opened the door.

She stood in the fog with her hood back, a woman maybe ten years his junior, hard-used and hard-kept, with a sword slung across her back in a plain farmer's scabbard that fooled no one. Her hair was cropped short and unevenly — sheared, not cut, the way soldiers shear it after fever or disgrace. Around her neck hung a broken chain, and on the chain, threaded like a bead, a small wooden symbol August recognized from roadside shrines all across the lowlands: the sunburst of the Order of the Vigilant Flame.

Except hers had been split down the middle with a knife.

"You're wearing heresy around your neck," he said.

"You're wearing exile around yours," she said, nodding at nothing, at everything — the shabby cottage, the careful garden, the whole gray valley pressing in around them like a held breath. "At least mine's visible. Can we walk?"

* * *

They went north, because the south pass was lost and the east road was watched, and because Sera Vane walked like a woman who had already decided where she was going and merely permitted the world to come along. August carried the stone inside his coat, against his heart, and hated that he could feel it warming there, patient as a coal.

"The men hunting it," he said as they climbed the terraces above the village, the fog peeling away below them, "who are they?"

"King's men in name. In practice—" She shrugged. "Something else wears the king's colors these days. They call themselves the Quiet Chorus. They collect things that pray. Or things that get prayed *to*." She glanced sideways at him. "Your stone got prayed to. Loudly. By a lot of people, all at once, right before my brother stole it and ran."

"He wasn't a thief."

"No," she agreed, and something moved behind her eyes, quick and swallowed. "He was a coward who finally stopped being one. There's a difference, and it matters to me, and it won't matter to the Chorus at all." She lengthened her stride. "Keep up, Kavanagh. You've lived soft."

"I've lived *quiet*. There's a difference," he echoed, "and it kept me alive eleven years."

"It delayed you eleven years," she said. "There's a difference."

The morning sharpened around them as they climbed. Hollowmere spread out below in its grays and greens — the slate roofs, the terraced barley, the millpond lying flat as hammered tin. From this height August could see the whole valley at once, the bowl of it, the cradle of it, and for the first time in years it looked less like home and more like what it had always secretly been: a cell with good lighting. He had chosen it. He had needed it. The magic-dead earth had been the only silence deep enough to drown the thing inside him.

And now the silence had a hole in it, shaped exactly like a humming stone, and through the hole he could hear the whole loud world calling him back.

Behind them, far down the south pass, a horn sounded. One note, clean and cold, bouncing off the ridgeline.

Sera didn't break stride. "Faster," she said.

* * *

They cut across the high pasture where the sheep grazed stupid and calm, and August allowed himself one glance back at the village — at Elder Maren's crooked chimney, at the roof he'd rethatched in spring, at the rowan tree with its fresh-turned earth. No one had followed them out of the village. No one had raised the alarm. But he knew how it would go: the elders would find the disturbed floorboard, or the Chorus would tell them what their guest had been hiding, and Hollowmere would decide, in its slow gray way, that August Kavanagh had never quite belonged here after all. Exile within exile. He almost laughed.

"They'll burn the stone if they find it," he said. "Maren will insist. She'll call it mercy."

"Maren is right," Sera said. "That's the worst part. If I could kneel in that dead little valley for the rest of my life, I'd do it. I'd burn the stone myself." Her jaw tightened. "But the message wasn't finished. Did it speak to you? Before it burned itself quiet?"

August's hand pressed flat against his coat, against the warm weight. "One sentence. Over and over. *The king is dying, and something answers in his voice.*"

"Then it finished," Sera said grimly, "and it finished addressed to someone. Prayer-stones don't hum for anyone. They hum for the bloodline they're bound to." She stopped at the crest of the pasture and turned to face him fully, and for the first time he saw that under the soldier's hardness she was afraid — afraid in the controlled, rationed way of people who have spent fear carefully for years and cannot afford to spend much now. "It hums for you, doesn't it. It's been humming since you touched it. Don't lie to me; I watched you flinch at every heartbeat of it the whole climb."

August said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Stormbound," she breathed. Not a curse. Almost a prayer, and she seemed to hate herself for the shape of it in her mouth. "My order spent two hundred years saying your family was a fable. Then they spent thirty years saying you were heretics. Then they spent one very bad night discovering you were neither." She started walking again, faster. "Wonderful. My brother died carrying a letter to a fable."

* * *

The valley's edge announced itself long before they reached it.

First the barley thinned, giving way to bracken, then to bare stone. Then the birdsong changed — August noticed it the way he noticed everything about this border, having crossed it only twice in eleven years, both times to stand just outside and hurry back in like a man testing bathwater. Inside the valley the birds sang the scattered, tuneless songs of ordinary birds. At the edge, where the ridgeline rose in gray shoulders against the sky, the songs grew strange: echoes arriving before their causes, notes hanging in the air a half-second too long, as though the air itself were unsure what rules applied.

The elders called it the Hush. Pilgrims called it the Mercy. August had always called it the wall, and he had loved it the way prisoners love walls, which is to say completely and dishonestly.

Sera stopped at the last terrace of stone and looked back down the valley one final time. "Say goodbye properly," she said. "Places know when you sneak off. They hold it against you."

"It's a valley."

"It's a valley that ate eleven years of your life and asked for more. Say goodbye."

So August stood at the edge of Hollowmere and looked back at the only home he had left — the smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the millwheel turning its slow silver circle, the small gray lives arranged in their small gray peace — and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was not fleeing *toward* anything. He was fleeing, full stop. The stone against his chest pulsed once, twice, like a fist knocking gently on a door from the inside.

"Goodbye," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on it, and he hated that Sera heard.

Then he stepped across the line.

* * *

The world broke open.

There was no other way to describe it, though he would spend years trying. One stride he was in the Hush, in the dead air, in the blessed numbness — and the next stride the magic of the living world hit him like diving into a winter river. It wasn't sight or sound. It was *pressure*, everywhere, the whole waking world humming with currents and weights and intentions: the wind carrying messages between hilltops, the groundwater threading silver through the rock, the clouds above the ridgeline dragging their bellies full of rain like pregnant animals. Eleven years he had lived in silence and told himself the silence was the natural state of things.

It wasn't. The silence was the wound. This — this roaring, crowded, breathing abundance — this was the world.

August staggered. Sera caught his arm without comment, and he was distantly grateful that she pretended not to notice the tears freezing on his cheeks, because they weren't grief-tears or fear-tears, or not only, and explaining them was beyond him.

Above the ridgeline, the clouds had begun to turn.

He felt it before he saw it — felt the pressure shift overhead, felt something in the deep cellar of himself rise up and *answer*, the way a struck bell answers, the way a dog answers its name after years apart. The dormant thing in his chest, the storm-song his mother had bled to teach him and bled again to hide, uncurled from its long sleep and stretched, and where it stretched the air went electric. Static crawled along his arms. His hair lifted from his brow. The temperature dropped so fast his breath bloomed white in the middle of the afternoon.

"Kavanagh." Sera's voice, sharp, careful. "What's happening."

"I don't—" He did know. That was the horror of it. He knew exactly what was happening, the way you know exactly what's happening when an old injury wakes aching before a storm. "It's me. It's — the storm wants to follow me. It remembers me."

The first cloud rolled over the sun and the pasture went dim as dusk. Somewhere above, thunder cleared its throat — not near, not yet, but *oriented*, the way a hound orients, and August realized with a lurch that the thunder was listening for him. That it had been listening for eleven years. That the valley's great mercy had never been protecting the world from him.

It had been protecting him from being *wanted*.

"Make it stop," Sera said, and her hand was on her sword-hilt now, not threatening him, just holding onto something solid, and he loved her a little for the honesty of it.

"I don't know how to want it to stop," August whispered. "Wanting it was the problem. Wanting it is why they exiled me."

But he pulled it in anyway — dragged the rising song back down into its cellar, wrestled it like a drowning man wrestling water, and slowly, grudgingly, the clouds loosened, the pressure eased, the static sank back into the ground. The sun returned, thin and pale. The thunder withdrew, sullen, keeping its distance the way wolves keep a distance from fire they intend to circle back to.

August stood shaking in the wet grass at the edge of everything, terror and exhilaration running through him in the same hot wire, indistinguishable, inseparable. He had forgotten. Gods, he had actually forgotten — the sheer unbearable *aliveness* of it, the way his own power made the world sit up and pay attention. Eleven years of mending roofs, and his body had been quietly starving the entire time, and now it had tasted one mouthful and would never, ever forget again.

"That," Sera said quietly, "is why the Chorus is hunting the stone instead of destroying it. They don't want the message. They want what the message calls." She looked at him the way you look at a lit fuse. "How far to the capital from here, Stormbound?"

"Six weeks' walk. Faster if the roads are kind."

"The roads won't be kind." She settled her pack and started down the far slope, into the green loud living world, and after a moment August followed, because there was nowhere else left to go, and because the stone against his heart had begun, very softly, to hum again — a new note this time, lower, patient, almost pleased.

Far behind them, at the lip of the valley, a second horn sounded.

Closer than the first.
