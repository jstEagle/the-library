## 5. The Choir of Little Saints

They heard the children before they saw them.

It began as a sound August could not name — not birdsong, not wind through the wheat, but something layered and bright that rose over the hedgerows like smoke from a chimney fire. Sera stopped mid-stride on the rutted road, her hand going to the broken mace-head she carried at her belt the way other priests carried relics.

"Do you hear that?"

"I hear singing," August said. "Badly."

"Everyone sings badly," Sera said. "Not everyone sings *together*. Listen — they're harmonizing. Orphans don't harmonize unless someone taught them, and no one teaches orphans anything for free."

The road bent around a fallow field gone gold with late summer, and there they were: a dozen small figures strung along a merchant's wagon like laundry on a line. Some walked, some rode the rails, one small boy sat atop the canvas cover beating a rhythm on an upturned bucket with two spoons. Their clothes were patched in six colors apiece, their boots worn through at the toes, and their voices — thin, cracked, untrained — wove together into something that made the hair on August's arms stand up despite himself.

A hymn. An old one. He knew it from before, from the years when temples still had congregations and congregations still believed the words were heard.

*Light the lamp though the dark be long,*
*sing the song though the singer be gone—*

"That's a harvest hymn," Sera said quietly. "Pre-Silence. My order retired it forty years ago because—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Because we decided the gods weren't listening anymore, so why sing as if they were."

"And now?"

"Now I'd very much like to know who taught these children to sing it."

As if summoned by the question, a figure peeled away from the wagon's shadow and came toward them down the road. He was perhaps seventeen, narrow as a fence rail, with a face full of angles and a grin that arrived a half-second before his eyes decided whether to mean it. His coat had once been fine — merchant's cut, good wool — and was now a patchwork museum of every year since. He walked with his thumbs hooked in his belt like a man surveying land he owned.

"Well met, travelers," he said. "You've got the look of people who are lost, hungry, or running. We can help with all three, though the third costs extra."

"We're not running," August said.

Tam — for that was the name he offered, hand extended, *just Tam, like the sound a door makes* — looked at August the way a jeweler looks at a stone he suspects is worth more than its seller knows. "No," he agreed pleasantly. "You're not. Running people walk faster." He turned to Sera and his grin sharpened into something almost respectful. "And you're Vane. The heretic. We heard about you in Corrow's Ford — the battle-priestess who told her whole chapter house the gods went quiet years before anyone admitted it. They burned your name off the rolls, didn't they? That's a rare honor. Most people just get forgotten."

Sera's expression did not change, which August had learned meant she was either unbothered or deeply bothered. "Your information travels fast for a wagon full of children."

"Our information travels faster than most things." Tam spread his hands. "We're the Choir of Little Saints. Orphans, castoffs, one reformed liar, and a girl who bites. We sing for our supper, mend what we can mend, and mind our own business — mostly. You're welcome to travel with us as far as the river towns. Roads are bad this season. Bad roads are worse alone."

"Why?" August asked.

Tam tilted his head. "Why what?"

"Why help strangers. Nobody does it for free. You said so yourself."

For a moment the grin flickered, and underneath it August glimpsed something older and wearier, a boy who had learned to count exits before he'd learned his letters. "Because somebody helped me once," Tam said, "and I never got the chance to pay it back, so I spend it forward instead. It compounds." The grin returned, banked and bright. "Also, you look like you can fight, priestess, and your tall friend here looks like he can carry things. A choir needs strong backs."

August opened his mouth to refuse — refusal had become reflexive over the years, a door he kept barred out of habit — but Sera spoke first.

"We'll travel with you," she said. "One condition. No prayers. None of the children prays where I can't see it, and nobody teaches them new ones."

Tam's eyebrows rose. "That's a strange rule for a priestess."

"It's the only rule I have left."

He considered her for a long moment, and whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him. "Done," he said. "Though I'll warn you — rules about praying are like rules about rain. You can forbid it all you like. It falls anyway."

* * *

The wagon belonged to a mule named Duchess who tolerated the children the way a mountain tolerates weather. By evening August had learned eleven names: Pip, who bit; Marisol, who could make any lock click open with a touch and swore she didn't know how she did it; brothers Odo and Fen, who finished each other's sentences and each other's thefts; little Wren-of-the-Choir (not to be confused with any soldier August had yet to meet), who was six and missing two teeth and followed August around the campfire asking questions with the relentless precision of a small inquisitor.

Where were you going? Why were you going there? Was the priestess really a heretic? What was a heretic? Had he ever seen a dead man? Did storms really follow him? Because Marisol said storms follow him, and Marisol lies, but she lies *well*, so it might be true—

"I don't call storms," August said, for perhaps the fourth time.

"But they come anyway?"

"They come anyway."

Wren-of-the-Choir nodded gravely, as if this confirmed some private theory. "That's how it works with us too. We don't ask for things. Things just... notice us." He held up his hands, palms out, as if presenting evidence. "Marisol touches locks and they open. Odo dreams things three days early. Tam hears—" He stopped abruptly, glancing across the fire, and lowered his voice to a whisper that carried perfectly in the still air. "Don't tell him I said. He doesn't like people knowing."

"Knowing what?"

But the boy had already darted away to escape bedtime, and August was left sitting with the question turning over in his chest like a stone in a streambed.

The camp settled by degrees. Sera took first watch without being asked, sitting apart with her back against a wheel, sharpening a knife that needed no sharpening. The children sang again — softer now, a lullaby version of the harvest hymn, the words worn smooth as river pebbles from generations of mouths. August sat at the fire's edge and let the sound wash over him, and found, to his quiet alarm, that his shoulders had come down from around his ears for the first time in days.

It was dangerous, this feeling. He knew its shape. It was the shape of Hollowmere's kitchen at dusk, of Maren laughing over the mending basket, of every place he had ever let himself believe he was safe. Safety was a debt that always came due.

"You're doing it wrong."

Tam dropped onto the log beside him, producing from somewhere in his coat a cloth bundle that unfolded to reveal bread, hard cheese, and a jar of pickled onions that smelled aggressive enough to defend itself. He split the bread three ways without asking.

"Doing what wrong?"

"Sitting." Tam gestured at August's posture with a wedge of cheese. "You sit like a man at a funeral watching the door. Relax. Nobody's hunting you tonight."

"You don't know that."

"I know more than you'd think." Tam ate with quick, economical bites, eyes on the fire. "I know you're from the valley — the magic-dead one, the one where people go to stop being what they were. I know your friend the priestess is taking you somewhere urgent, and I know it's not a market. And I know—" he paused, and for the first time since they'd met, he looked genuinely uncertain, "—I know the gods are awake again. Before you ask how: I hear them."

August went very still. "Hear them."

"Prayers." Tam said it simply, the way another man might say *rain* or *debt*. "Always could, far as I remember. Little threads, all over. Most of them fray out unanswered — that's how it was my whole life. You pray, and the prayer goes up like smoke, and nothing catches it. But three weeks ago—" he snapped his fingers, and the sound cracked oddly loud in the night, "—things started catching. Prayers getting answered. Not all of them. Enough."

"That's good," August said slowly. "Isn't it? People prayed for years into silence."

Tam looked at him then, fully, and the grin was entirely gone. In the firelight he looked much younger and much older at once. "Mostly it's good. Bread appearing when a mother prays for it. Fevers breaking. One old fellow in Ashford prayed for rain and got exactly enough, right on his fields, none on his neighbor's — that one kept me up nights, actually, the *precision* of it. But some answers..." He turned the pickle jar in his hands. "Some answers come back wrong-shaped. Like someone heard the words but not the meaning. A child prays for her father to come home, and a stranger comes home instead, and moves in, and smiles too much. Small things. Wrong things. I keep telling myself it's just the gods being rusty after all those silent years." He laughed, short and humorless. "Gods being rusty. Listen to me."

"What do the children pray for?" August asked quietly.

"Nothing," Tam said. "That's the rule, isn't it? Your priestess's rule. No prayers." He smiled faintly. "Good rule. I enforce it harder than she knows. These kids have spent their whole lives being noticed by things they can't see. Last thing they need is to start talking to them."

Across the fire, Wren-of-the-Choir had fallen asleep against Marisol's shoulder, mid-sentence, his questions finally run dry. Marisol caught August looking and gave him a gap-toothed, conspiratorial smile, and something in August's chest ached with a warmth he did not trust and could not refuse.

* * *

It happened on the third morning, at the edge of a village called Thistledown, where the road crossed a stream so clear the children begged to stop and fill the water skins.

Pip — the biter, a ferocious scrap of a girl maybe five years old — crouched at the bank while the others splashed, and August, filling skins upstream, heard her voice rise small and solemn over the water's chatter.

"Please," Pip said, to no one, to the water, to the space above the water. Her hands were cupped around something August couldn't see. "Please. I want my mama's blue scarf back. The one the bad men took. Please."

The prayer of a child. The oldest kind of prayer, the kind that predates theology, liturgy, and shame. August felt his stomach drop even as the words ended — because he had heard Tam's warning, had absorbed Sera's fear, and he knew what came next the way you know the last step of a staircase in the dark.

Nothing should happen.

Something happened.

The light changed. There was no better word for it. The morning sun, which had been slanting gold through the willows, thickened — deepened — as if a hand had adjusted a lens. The stream's babble faltered, held, resumed. And downstream, caught on a snag of root not four feet from where Pip knelt, a scrap of blue fabric stirred in the current and washed free, drifting to rest against the mud at the girl's feet.

A scarf. Blue. Faded, torn at one corner, unmistakably loved.

Pip shrieked with joy — pure, uncomplicated, terrible joy — and snatched it from the water and pressed it to her face, and the other children crowded around exclaiming, and Tam stood frozen at the wagon with an expression August would carry to his grave: not wonder. Horror. The horror of a boy who had spent his life hearing prayers die unanswered and knew, better than any of them, exactly how impossible this moment was.

Sera moved fast. She reached Pip first, gently pried the story loose — bad men, a raid last winter, the scarf torn from her mother's neck — and then she stood and scanned the banks, the willows, the road, the sky, with the flat professional sweep of a soldier expecting ambush.

"Current," she said at last, quietly, to August alone. "It could have been in the stream. It could have washed down from upstream. It could be coincidence."

"Could it?"

"No." She said it without drama, which made it worse. "I watched the light do that. Light doesn't do that." Her hand found her broken mace-head and gripped it. "August. Three weeks ago the wells in the river towns started tasting of ash — I've had letters saying so. Two nights ago I dreamed in a language I don't speak. And now a child prays for a rag and the world rearranges itself to give it to her." She turned to face him, and behind her the children laughed and sang and waved a stolen-lost-stolen-again scarf in the sunshine, and the picture of it was so warm and so wrong that August's skin crawled. "The gods aren't waking up. They're *waking up everywhere*. All at once. Listening to everything."

"Maybe that's mercy," August said, but his voice betrayed him. "After all those silent years. Maybe they're making up for lost time."

"Mercy has a face," Sera said. "This has a *speed*. Did you feel it? The answer came before the prayer finished. Nothing that loves you listens that fast. Only things that are *hungry* listen that fast."

Downstream, Tam had gathered the children back toward the wagon with a briskness that fooled none of them, his voice bright, his hands steady, his eyes finding August's across the water with a look that said plainly: *now you understand why I don't sleep.*

They rolled out within the hour, the choir's songs ringing down the road ahead of them like bells rung by no hand. August walked at the rear, beside the wagon wheels, and tried to tell himself the warmth in his chest was only the morning, only the children, only the ordinary grace of company on a long road.

But as the sun climbed, he became aware of a sensation he hadn't felt since the valley's edge — faint, distant, at the threshold of sense. A pressure behind his sternum. A gathering, like the first stillness before thunder.

Somewhere ahead, or above, or *within everything*, something vast had turned its attention toward the road.

Toward the singing.

Toward, he realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the wind, *him*.
