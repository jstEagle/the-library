## 6. What the Wells Remember

The river town was called Marrowford, and it smelled wrong before they ever reached its gates.

Sera noticed it first — she always noticed things first, a habit the Order had beaten into her and then punished her for keeping. The others smelled woodsmoke, river mud, baking bread. Sera smelled something underneath all of it, thin and bitter, like a candle snuffed in a sealed room. Like ash that had never been allowed to fall.

"Something's burned here," she said.

August drew his cloak tighter. "The town's not burned."

"No," Sera agreed. "That's what worries me."

Marrowford sat where the river bent lazy around a hill, all slate roofs and leaning chimneys, a bridge old enough to have saints' names worn off its stones. It should have been loud. Market towns were loud — hawkers and cart-wheels and children shrieking underfoot. But the noise here had a strange texture to it, too smooth, like a song hummed by someone who didn't know the words. Voices rose and fell at the right moments and carried nothing inside them.

Tam felt it too. He walked with his shoulders hunched and his hands jammed in his pockets, and when one of the little ones asked why everyone was smiling, Tam said, "Because they're paid to," in a voice that meant *shut up and don't ask again*.

They found lodging above a cooper's shop, two rooms for six coppers, and Sera went out alone before dusk to do what she still could not stop doing, even disgraced, even branded: she went to pray.

Or rather — she went to listen.

* * *

The wells of Marrowford numbered eleven, or so said a boy selling eels on the bridge, who told her this for no reason at all except that she'd made eye contact. Eleven wells for a river town, which was itself strange. Who dug eleven wells beside clean running water?

Sera stood at the first well in the market square and watched a woman draw water.

The bucket came up dark. Not filthy — dark, as though the water remembered being shadow somewhere deep down. The woman drank from her cupped hand without hesitation, without a face, and Sera's stomach turned.

"Excuse me," Sera said. "Your water. Does it taste—"

"Sweet as prayer," the woman said, and smiled, and walked away.

Sera waited until she was gone. Then she leaned over the well lip, cupped her own hand, and drank.

Ash.

Not dirt, not mineral, not rot. Ash — the taste of burnt offerings, of hymnals fed to fire, of everything her order had ever destroyed in the name of purity. She knew that taste intimately. She had tasted it for three years after they took her vows, her robes, and her name, and gave her back a brand and a road.

She spat over the well lip and knelt, and because she was weak, because she had never learned any other way to be, she prayed.

*O Hidden Ones, who wait behind the veil of the world —*

The reply came before she finished the sentence.

It did not come as it once had, in the long slow silence she had spent her whole life learning to read, the silence she'd been branded for describing honestly. It came as an answer. Immediate. Warm. Close as breath against the back of her neck.

***Soon.***

Sera jerked upright so fast she cracked her head on the well's crossbeam. The square went on around her — vendors folding their stalls, children chasing a hoop, the woman with the dark water laughing at something — and none of them had heard it. None of them needed to. It hadn't come through her ears.

It had come through the water.

She went to the second well. Then the third. By the fifth she had stopped kneeling; by the eighth she had stopped praying words and simply pressed her palm flat to the cold stone lip and thought, deliberately, loudly: *Who are you?*

***Soon.***

*What is coming?*

***Soon.***

*What did you do to this town?*

***Soon,*** said the well, gentle as a lullaby, patient as a grave, ***soon, soon, soon.***

Eleven wells. Eleven mouths, all saying the same word, all tasting of ash, and every soul in Marrowford drinking it down three times a day and calling it sweet.

Sera walked back to the cooper's shop with her hands shaking and her mind doing what it always did when terror came: organizing it into doctrine, into categories, into the neat architecture of heresy. The gods had gone silent — that was her crime, her truth, the thing she'd said aloud at nineteen years old while the Prior's face went grey. *The gods stopped answering decades ago. Everything since has been echo.* They had stripped her rank for it. They had burned her writings.

But the gods weren't silent anymore. Something was answering now, everywhere, instantly, and it knew only one word, and the word was a promise, and promises like that were never kind.

* * *

August was sitting up when she returned, awake in the dark the way he often was, mending a strap on Tam's satchel by feel because he refused to waste candles. He looked up when she came in, and whatever he saw in her face made him set the strap down.

"The wells," he said.

"You've drunk from them."

"We all have. The kids are fine." A pause. "Aren't they?"

"They're fine tonight." Sera sat across from him, close enough that the moonlight through the shutters striped them both. "August. What do you remember about your mother's people? Your line. What were you called?"

He went very still. She had learned his stillnesses by now — the stillness of a man listening for weather far away.

"That's not a name we used."

"I know. I'm asking anyway."

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then, quietly, in the tone of someone setting down something heavy he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight: "Stormbound."

"And you're the last of them."

"My mother was the last of anything," August said. "After her there was just me, and I made sure there wasn't much of that either." He turned the strap over in his hands. "Why are you asking? What did the wells tell you?"

"They only say one word. *Soon.* Every well in this town. The water tastes of ash, August. Burnt-offering ash. And everyone drinks it and smiles." She leaned forward. "I prayed at eight different wells tonight and got eight identical answers, instant, eager — and I want you to understand what that means, because I don't think you do. For thirty years the gods did not answer anyone. I staked my entire life on that truth. Whatever is answering now, it isn't the gods I was trained to serve. It's something wearing their voice. And it's listening *everywhere*, and it answers *fast*, and fast answers are never given freely."

August was silent. Outside, the river murmured, and beneath the river's murmur — faint, rhythmic, wrong — Sera could almost hear the wells breathing.

"There's an old priest," August said finally. "At the chapel by the bridge. Tam heard him talking to himself today, real talking, not the smiling kind. He's the only person in this town whose prayers sound like prayers."

"Then tomorrow," Sera said, "we talk to the only man in Marrowford who hasn't swallowed the answer whole."

* * *

Father Aldous Merren was ninety if he was a day, and he was sweeping the chapel steps at dawn as though the sweeping were the only thing holding the world together. He was a small man, dried-out and bent, in vestments washed to the color of old bone, and when he saw Sera's brand — she'd stopped hiding it; hiding it never helped — he did not flinch. He simply nodded, the way one soldier nods at another across a ruined field.

"Heresy," he said pleasantly. "Good morning. You'll be wanting the truth, then. Everyone else around here only wants breakfast."

Inside, the chapel was cold and immaculate and utterly empty of the presence Sera had spent her childhood feeling in such places. The hollow gods had left even this. Candles burned before bare alcoves where statues had once stood — not removed, she noted, but *melted*, sagging like wax left too near a flame, though no fire had ever touched the chapel.

While the others waited outside — Tam watching the door, the children marveling at the melted saints — Father Merren poured tea that tasted only of itself, blessed water from a skin he kept locked in a chest, and studied August with eyes gone pale as river fog.

"You'll be Kavanagh's boy," he said.

August's teacup stopped halfway to his mouth. "You knew my mother?"

"Knew *of* her. Knew of the line, which is more than most living priests can say, because most living priests were taught the line was a story." The old man set down his cup. "The Stormbound. Before your Order, priestess, before mine, before the bells and the books and the pretty hierarchy — there were people who carried the storm in their blood. Not magic the way your colleges teach it. Inheritance. A song, passed mother to child, that could bind or unbind the oldest things. Your order called them heretics too, eventually. We were thorough about that."

"We?" Sera said.

"I'm older than my heresy, girl. I was thorough once myself." He waved a knuckled hand. "The Stormbound made the great bindings. Chained the hungry ones beneath mountains and cities and rivers so the world could sleep safe. And the last of the Stormbound lines died out, or hid, or was hunted — depending which archive you believe — until there was one woman left, and she had a son, and she taught him nothing, because teaching him would have been painting a target on his back."

August's voice came out flat. "She taught me lullabies."

"Lullabies." Merren smiled with genuine warmth, and it was the saddest thing Sera had seen in weeks. "Boy, do you think the great bindings were set with incantations? With circles and candles? They were set with songs a mother could sing a child to sleep with, so the child would carry them forever without knowing what he carried. That's how you keep a key safe. You make it a part of the lock."

The room was very quiet. Outside, distantly, someone laughed — one of the children, bright and unafraid — and the sound seemed to come from another country.

"So the wells," Sera said. "The ash water. The *Soon.*"

"The wells are the smallest of it. Ears, not mouths. Something old is waking under the capital, and it's drinking the country's faith through a thousand straws, and this town —" Merren gestured vaguely at the walls, at the melted saints — "this town already belongs to it. Has for a generation. We smile because it's easier. We drink because we're thirsty. And every prayer anyone offers gets answered by the same thing, the same word, because it wants us ready. It wants us *waiting*. Nothing obeys like the hopeful."

August had not moved. His hands lay flat on his knees, and Sera saw the tremor in them, fine as a plucked string.

"If it's waking," August said slowly, "then something bound it. Once."

"The Stormbound bound it." Merren looked at him with terrible gentleness. "And the binding is a song, and the song lives in the blood, and you, heir of the Stormbound, are the last verse left in the world."

"I don't know any binding," August whispered. "I don't know anything. My mother died keeping it that way. She *died* for it—"

"Yes," the old priest said. "And I'd wager she died praying you'd never need to learn why."

* * *

They left the chapel as the morning bell rang — unstruck, Sera noticed now, ringing from nothing, soft and sweet, and nobody in the street remarked upon it.

August walked ahead of the group and said nothing for a mile. The children babbled about the melted saints; Tam kept his hand near his knife and his eyes on the rooftops; Wren whistled tunelessly and pretended not to watch August's back. Sera fell into step beside August and let the silence stretch until it thinned enough to speak through.

"Inheritance is a burden," she said. "I know something about that. My order gave me one too — the truth, whether I wanted it or not. You can put down a sword. You can't put down knowing."

"I could keep walking," August said. "West. Over the mountains. There's ocean past them. No wells out there but salt."

"Yes."

"I could take the children somewhere it can't reach. Take all of you. Start over."

"Yes," Sera said again. "You could. And every night for the rest of your life you'd hear that word in the water. *Soon.* And you'd know it was coming anyway, and that you were the only soul alive who might have stopped it, and that you chose the ocean instead." She looked at him. "I'm not telling you your duty, August. I was branded for refusing to tell other people theirs. I'm just telling you what the burden weighs."

Ahead, one of the little ones — Pip, the smallest, the one who still held Tam's sleeve in crowds — stumbled on the rutted road, and Tam caught her without looking, lifted her onto his shoulders without breaking stride, and kept walking. August watched them the way a starving man watches bread.

"They answered her prayer," August said quietly. "Back on the road. She wished for apples, and there were apples. Instantly. I told myself it was chance." His jaw tightened. "It was never chance, was it. It was feeding. It's feeding all of us, little wishes, little comforts, getting us fat and tame and *waiting*."

"Now you understand the wells."

"I wish I didn't."

Behind them, Marrowford shrank along its river, eleven wells drinking deep, a thousand throats swallowing ash and calling it sweet. And as the road bent away through the hills, Sera heard it one last time — not in her ears, not in the water, but in the marrow of her, patient and warm and terribly close:

***Soon.***

That night, camped in open hills beyond the town's reach, Sera woke to find August standing at the edge of the firelight, facing east, toward the capital, toward the mountain, toward whatever wore a king's crown and waited behind the word *soon*. The wind had come up, and it moved through the grass in long low waves, and it sounded — she would swear this later, to herself, in the dark — it sounded almost like a hymn.

"August," she said softly.

He didn't turn. "There's a shrine," he said. "My mother talked about it once, when she thought I was asleep. A ruin in the eastern hills where the first binding was made. Where our line's records were kept, if any survived." His fists clenched at his sides. "If I'm the last verse, Sera, I want to know what song I'm standing in."

East, then. Toward the shrine. Toward the archives of a broken order and the truth of a binding written in blood — and behind them, in every well of every town they passed, the water waiting patiently to taste of ash, and whisper:

***Soon.***
