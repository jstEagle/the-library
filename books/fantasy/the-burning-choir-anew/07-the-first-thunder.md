## 7. The First Thunder

The road out of the river town climbed through country that had forgotten how to be beautiful. August felt it in his teeth before he saw it — a thinness to the air, the way the pines stood too straight, like soldiers holding formation long after the war had ended. The land here was old grief given geography: gray stone, black water, and a sky the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

He walked at the rear of their strange procession, as he always did, counting heads the way he'd once counted heartbeats between storms.

Sera Vane led, her broken order's sigil scraped from her pauldron but its ghost still visible in the scratched metal — a sun with seven rays, six of them defaced. Behind her came Tam, all elbows and watchfulness, herding the children the way a sheepdog herds something it loves and does not entirely trust itself. There were five of them now; they'd picked up a sixth in the river town, a girl called Moss who could make candles gutter with her moods. The children walked in a knot, the way small creatures do when they've learned that the world is large and hungry, and they sang under their breath as they went — little snatches of hymn, half-remembered, off-key, alive.

August hated how much he loved the sound.

*You are a door,* his mother had told him once, kneeling in the rain with his small face held in her calloused hands. *And doors don't get to choose what walks through them.*

He had spent eleven years in Hollowmere learning to be a wall instead. Walls were safe. Walls kept things out, and more importantly kept things in. But walls, he was remembering, mile by mile, could also be prisons, and the thing locked inside him had begun to press against the mortar.

The prayer-stone rode against his sternum in a leather pouch, cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket. He told himself he carried it because destroying it would have been wasteful. He told himself a great many things.

"Storm-caller." Sera had dropped back without him noticing, which was its own small humiliation. She fell into step beside him, her stride eating ground. "The birds have gone quiet."

"Birds go quiet all the time."

"Not like this." Her eyes moved along the ridgeline above them, reading it the way he read cloud-shapes. "Not all at once, not for three miles. Something up there is listening hard enough that everything else is holding its breath."

August listened. She was right. The silence had a texture to it, a held-note quality, like the moment after a conductor raises his baton. And beneath it — faint, rhythmic, wrong — the sound of hooves on stone.

"How many?" he asked.

"If they're good? Eight. If they're crown-hunters, four is enough." She unslung her mace from her back with the weary economy of a woman who had done this arithmetic before and never liked the answer. "Get the children behind the rockfall ahead. Don't argue. For once in your stubborn life, don't argue."

For once, he didn't.

* * *

The rockfall was an old one, tumbled granite furred with lichen, and it made a rough pocket against the hillside. August herded the children into it with hands that wanted to shake and didn't dare. Tam crouched at the mouth of it, two knives already in her fists, looking younger and older than her seventeen years at the same time.

"What's happening?" whispered Pip, the smallest, a boy who heard prayers the way other children heard music.

"Nothing," said Tam. "Bandits, maybe. Sera'll chase them off."

"They're not bandits." Pip's voice was flat and certain, the terrible certainty of children who knew things. "I can hear them praying. They're praying for our deaths. They think it's holy."

August's blood went cold. Crown-hunters didn't pray. But men who believed themselves instruments of a god's will — men whose god answered —

"Tam." He kept his voice low and even, a voice for mending roofs, a Hollowmere voice. "If I tell you to run with them down the gully, can you do it?"

"You're coming with us."

"I'm the reason they're here."

"And you think I'm going to lead six god-touched children away from the only person here who can—" She stopped. Looked at him. Really looked, the way she did when she was picking a lock or a pocket, finding the mechanism. "You haven't got anything left, have you. Whatever you were in the valley. You left it there."

"I left it there because it kills people, Tam."

"Good," she said, with a ferocity that almost made him laugh. "Maybe it's about to kill the right ones."

Then the first arrow came down out of the trees, and there was no more time for talking.

* * *

They came off the ridge like water finding a crack — four figures in gray-and-gold, faces masked, moving with the drilled grace of the royal guard gone feral. Sera met the first one at the bottom of the slope and the sound of mace on shield cracked across the valley like the world clearing its throat.

August put himself between the fight and the children and discovered, with a lurch, that he had no idea what he was doing. Eleven years of mending roofs. His body remembered forms his mind had buried — the stance, the breathing — but memory was not power, and power was what the moment demanded.

The second hunter ignored Sera entirely. That was the discipline of them, he realized distantly; they'd been briefed. They came around the rockfall's flank with a crossbow leveled past August's shoulder, at the pocket of huddled children, and August moved without deciding to move.

The bolt meant for Pip took him high in the arm instead, a white-hot punch that spun him half around. He heard Tam scream his name. He heard the hunter working the windlass, calm as a man drawing water.

And inside him, behind eleven years of mortar, something turned over in its sleep and opened one eye.

It started in his scarred palm. A hum. Then his teeth. Then the air itself, every hair on his arms lifting, the pouch with the prayer-stone buzzing against his chest like a trapped wasp. The clouds overhead — that bruised, undecided sky — *decided*.

"No," August said aloud, to himself, to the sky. "No, no—"

The hunter fired again. Tam threw herself into the bolt's path with a knife that shouldn't have been able to deflect it and nearly managed to die proving it; the shaft grazed her temple and she went down among the stones, and the sight of her — bright, sharp, *alive* Tam, folded small and bleeding among the rocks —

The wall came down.

* * *

Later, August would try to remember it as a decision. It was not a decision. It was a dam failing, and he was both the flood and the man standing in the valley floor watching it come for him.

The thunder did not follow the lightning. In Hollowmere, in the old stories, in every honest account of storm-calling ever written: the thunder came *first*, because the storm arrived the way grief arrives, before you understand what has been lost. The crack of it split the sky open directly overhead and every pine on the ridge bowed at once, and then the light came down.

He had meant, in whatever fraction of a heartbeat counts as meaning, to strike the crossbow. To break the weapon. To frighten.

What he hit was everything.

Lightning walked the hillside in great stuttering strides, hunting, hungry, indiscriminate as fire. It took the crossbow and the arm holding it and the man behind the arm, and it did not stop there. It ran down through the wet granite, through the lichen-furred stone where Tam lay bleeding, through the gully where the children crouched, and August felt all of it — felt the current find Tam's body and leap, felt her heart stutter like a bird hitting a window, felt the small bright candle of her flicker—

He tore the storm back. That much was choice, that much was eleven years of discipline screaming through the ruin: he seized the lightning the way you seize a dog by the collar mid-lunge, and dragged it up, and hurled it into the empty sky where it detonated against nothing, a flower of white fire that scorched the clouds.

Then the rain came down, warm and smelling of ozone, and August Kavanagh stood in the middle of it with smoke rising off his own skin and understood exactly what he was.

Two hunters fled, dragging their burned companion. Sera let them go; he heard her shout something after them and couldn't process the words. The world had gone narrow and ringing. He turned, slowly, joints creaking like a ship's rigging, and looked at the rockfall.

Tam was sitting up.

Blood sheeted down the side of her face from the graze, and her hair stood out from her head in a dark corona, and she was staring at him with an expression he had never seen on her face and hoped never to see again: awe. Not fear. Awe.

"You," she said, and her voice came out sanded rough. "Your eyes were *storms*. August, your whole eyes—"

"Don't." The word came out of him like something coughed up. "Don't look at me like that."

Pip crept out from the rocks, then Moss, then the others, wide-eyed, rain streaming down their small faces. They weren't crying. That was the horror of it. They were *marveling*, gathering toward him the way people gather toward a hearth, and Pip said, reverent, "The sky answered you. The sky knows your name," and one of the girls began, softly, to sing — the same half-remembered hymn they'd been murmuring on the road, except now it sounded less like a children's rhyme and more like an invocation, and August's scarred palm throbbed in time with it.

"Stop singing." His voice cracked across the hollow. The hymn died. Six children flinched, and he had done that, he had taught them fear in the space of a single breath, and it was better this way, he told himself, it was safer, and the telling rang false as a cracked bell. "Get your things. We move. Now."

"August." Sera stood over the dead hunter, breathing hard, her mace dripping. She was looking at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of someone cataloguing damage. "The man Tam saved you from—"

"I know what I did."

"Do you?" She crossed to him, boots squelching, and lowered her voice below the children's hearing. "That lightning found her. You know that. Another half-second and we'd be burying her. So no, I don't think you know what you did. I think you know what you *almost* did, and you're going to carry it like a stone, and meanwhile that thing inside you just learned that it can get out. That it *wants* to get out. You don't bind a lesson like that by sulking at children."

"I said we move."

"August—"

"*We move.*"

She held his gaze a moment longer, and something passed between them that wasn't agreement but wasn't far from pity, and then she turned to rouse the children, and August Kavanagh stood alone in the warm rain and made his vow.

He made it the way his mother had taught him vows were made — not with words, which the gods could overhear, but with the will, pressed down into the marrow like a seal into wax. *Never again.* He would build the wall higher. He would pour the mortar thicker. He would walk into the capital, do whatever grim thing the inheritance demanded, and he would do it as a man, a mortal, a mender of roofs — and the storm would stay behind the stone if he had to stand against the door of himself for the rest of his life.

Behind him, he heard Tam getting to her feet, refusing Pip's help, laughing shakily — *laughing* — and saying, "Did you see the sky? Did you see what he *did*?"

They thought they had seen a miracle.

August closed his eyes and felt, deep beneath the vow, beneath the mortar, patient as groundwater, the storm agree to wait.

* * *

They walked until dusk, putting miles between themselves and the ambush, and camped in the shell of a roadside shrine whose god-statue had been decapitated so long ago that moss had accepted the neck as a garden. Sera set watches. The children ate hard bread and cheese and fell asleep in a pile like puppies, and Tam sat apart, pressing a wet cloth to her temple, watching August across the dead firepit with those new stars in her eyes.

He went to her because avoiding her would have been worse.

"It should have been me checking the flank," she said, before he could speak. "My fault. Not yours."

"That's not—" He crouched beside her, forearms on his knees. The rain had stopped; the air smelled washed and new, and he hated that even that beauty belonged to the thing inside him. "Tam. What you saw today. What you felt when you looked at me. I need you to bury it. Whatever story you're telling yourself about the sky knowing my name — bury it next to the knives."

"Why?"

"Because the last person who looked at me like that died protecting what I am. Because the storm doesn't care who it loves. Because—" His voice failed, and he mastered it. "Because I nearly killed you. You know that. You *felt* it."

Tam was quiet a long moment. Rain dripped from the shrine's broken lintel, keeping time.

"I felt it," she admitted. "For about half a heartbeat, I was somewhere very cold and very far away, and then I was back, and you were standing there with smoke coming off you like a struck match." She looked at him sideways, and her voice gentled into something dangerously perceptive. "You think being afraid of yourself is the same thing as being strong. It isn't. It's just another way of letting the storm decide."

"The storm doesn't get to decide anything. That's the whole point of binding it."

"Mm." She touched her bandaged temple, winced, and smiled — the crooked, unsurrendering smile that had gotten six orphans across half a kingdom. "Well. When you're done deciding for it, come sing us something. Pip's been asking. Says the sky-song is still in your voice."

She rose and went to the sleeping children, and August stayed by the dead firepit a while longer, alone with the moss-garden god and the vow and the waiting.

It was Sera who broke the silence, dropping onto the stone beside him without invitation. She handed him a strip of dried meat he had no appetite for and nodded east, down the long dark of the road.

"There's a courier road joins ours tomorrow, near the Bellwater ford. Royal post runs it twice a week." She chewed, swallowed, watched the dark. "Crown-hunters don't operate alone, August. Four in the field means a relay. Means orders. Means someone in the capital knows a Stormbound is walking toward them, and wants him stopped before he arrives." She glanced at him, and in the starlight her scarred face was unreadable. "Which means someone in the capital is afraid of what you might do. And I find myself wondering — tonight of all nights — whether they're afraid of the right thing."

August followed her gaze east, toward the distant smudge of the capital's glow against the underside of the clouds, where a king wore a crown that answered prayers, and bells rang unstruck, and something waited with a kind face and an open door.

The storm inside him rolled over in its sleep, and waited too.
