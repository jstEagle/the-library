## 10. Tam's Terrible Bargain

Tam had learned to hear prayers the way other children learned to hear their own names.

It started in the foundling house at Marrow's Cross, back when he was small enough that the sisters still bothered to lie to him about his future. The prayers came through the walls like water through old mortar — thin, constant, seeping. *Let her fever break. Let the tithe be small this year. Let me die before winter.* He'd told Sister Ondile once, when he was six and stupid with honesty, that he could hear what people whispered into their cupped hands at night.

She had looked at him for a long moment with an expression he would spend years learning to name. Not wonder. Not tenderness. *Appraisal.* The look a fishmonger gives a fish.

So Tam stopped telling people things. That was the first lesson of his life, learned early and kept close: what you are is only as safe as the number of people who know it.

The second lesson came later, on the road, with a troupe of god-touched orphans who had somehow become his. That one was simpler and much worse: what you love can be taken from you by anything strong enough, and there is always something stronger.

He lay awake in the loft of the wayhouse barn thinking about both lessons, listening to the rain come down on the shingles like a crowd that wouldn't stop applauding, and below him eleven children slept in a heap of straw and shared blankets, breathing in the ragged unison of the very young. Pip had a fever. It wasn't a bad one — not yet — but Tam had heard the prayers of mothers over fevers all his life, and he knew how quickly *not yet* could become *too late*.

And he knew something else. Something he hadn't told anyone, not even Sera with her priestess's careful questions, not even August with his storm-gray silences.

The gods were awake again. And they were *listening*.

* * *

He felt it first three nights ago, outside the river town where every well tasted of ash. The children had been singing one of Sera's hymns around the cookfire — the little round one about the lantern-keeper, the one even Wren hummed when he thought no one noticed — and somewhere in the second verse, Tam had felt the song *land*. Not fade out into the dark the way songs do. Land. Arrive somewhere. Be received.

Like throwing a stone into a well and hearing, instead of the splash, a door open.

Since then he'd been careful. Careful the way he was always careful, keeping his own prayers small and dry and unsaid, folding them up and putting them away like coins he couldn't afford to spend. But careful wasn't the same as safe, because the children prayed too. They were god-touched; praying was what they were. Little Fern prayed over her shoes so they wouldn't wear through. The twins prayed against nightmares. Pip, burning and shivering in Sera's arms that evening, had prayed the smallest prayer Tam had ever heard, a thread of a thing, barely a breath:

*Don't let me be sick tomorrow. I want to see the bells.*

The bells. Word had come down the road with the couriers and the refugees: in the capital, the great bells rang at dawn with no hand upon them, and the king smiled, and the people smiled, and everything was gentle and wrong. Pip wanted to see them. Children always wanted the beautiful terrible things.

And Tam, lying in the hay with the rain drumming and the fever-warm ghost of Pip's prayer still caught in his ears like a burr, felt something answer it.

Not fully. Not words. Just — attention. A turning, vast and slow, like something enormous shifting its weight in the dark beneath the world. The sensation crawled up through the floorboards, through the hay, through the soles of his feet where they pressed against the loft's edge, and it *noticed* him. The boy who heard prayers. The little antenna of want and fear sleeping above eleven children.

*There you are,* said the attention, without saying anything at all.

Tam sat up in the straw, heart slamming, and did the only thing he knew how to do with dangerous knowledge.

He started planning.

* * *

In the morning Pip's fever broke. Sera called it the medicine. Wren called it luck. The children called it a miracle and sang the lantern-song twice, and Tam stood at the edge of the yard with his arms crossed and his teeth set, watching the sky for the shape of the thing that had turned over in its sleep.

August found him there. August always found him; the man had a hunter's instinct for whoever in the group was carrying the heaviest thing.

"You didn't sleep," August said. Not a question. He leaned on the fence rail beside Tam, and for a while neither of them spoke, which was one of the reasons Tam tolerated him. August Kavanagh understood the value of an unsaid thing better than anyone alive.

"Did you feel it?" Tam asked finally. "Last night. After we sang."

August went very still, the way he did when a memory put its hand on his shoulder. "Feel what?"

"The listening." Tam kept his eyes on the road, on the wet ruts full of silver rainwater. "Something heard us. Heard Pip." He hesitated, hating himself for the honesty and doing it anyway, because lying to August had begun to feel like lying to a wall that could bleed. "I think it's been hearing us for a while."

August was quiet long enough that a crow crossed the whole gray field and vanished into the hedgerow.

"We leave within the hour," he said at last. "Sera wants to make the shrine ruins by nightfall. There are archives there — records from her order. Answers." He pushed off the rail, then paused. "Tam. If you hear anything else — anything that feels like it's aimed at you — you tell me. Not Sera first. Me."

"Why you?"

"Because I know what it sounds like," August said, "when something old decides you're interesting." And he walked away across the yard with the rain-smell coming off his coat, leaving Tam alone with the crowless sky and the cold spreading through his chest like water finding the cracks in a foundation.

* * *

They made the shrine by dusk, as promised — a gutted hulk of pale stone crouched on a hill above the road, its bell tower broken off halfway like a snapped finger. Sera disappeared into the undercrofts with her order's seal-ring and a lamp, hunting archives. Wren took the children down to the sheltered cloister to get a fire going. August stood watch at the gate with his shoulders squared against the wind, and Tam —

Tam climbed.

He told himself it was to keep watch from height, which was even true, mostly. But his feet knew the real reason, taking the ruined stair two steps at a time, up and up through the broken tower until he stood on the open platform where the bell used to hang, with the whole dark country spread out below him and the sky enormous and star-scattered and *open*, like a mouth.

Here, high up, away from everyone he loved, he let himself do the thing he'd been not-doing for three days.

He prayed.

Not the folded-up kind. Not the small dry coin-shaped prayers he kept hidden. He opened the whole purse. He knelt on the cold stone, put his palms flat against it, and let everything pour out of him at once, all of it, the hoarded flood of it:

*I need strength. I'm just a thief, I'm nothing, I pick pockets and I steal bread and I keep children alive with luck and lies, and it isn't enough, do you understand? It has never been enough. There are eleven of them and they pray like lamps burning and every hungry thing in the dark can see them, and I am the only wall between, and I am made of paper. So give me something. Give me stone instead of paper. Give me iron. Give me anything, and take whatever you want for payment, because I have nothing left worth keeping except them, and if you take them there's no me left to rob.*

The silence after his own voice was the loudest silence Tam had ever heard.

Then the stars went out.

Not all of them. Just the ones directly overhead, a patch of black opening in the sky like a pupil dilating, and through it came the attention again — but no longer drowsy, no longer distant. *Focused.* It poured down through the broken tower like light through a lens, and Tam's whole body lit up with it, every nerve singing, and he heard — really heard, for the first time, with words —

*Little listener.*

The voice was everywhere and nowhere, patient as erosion, warm as a fever.

*You offer yourself. How rare. How lovely. They usually offer others.*

"I—" Tam's voice cracked. He knelt lower, forehead to the stone. "I mean it. Whatever it costs."

*A bargain, then. A true one, sealed in the old way.* The warmth gathered, coiling around him like smoke with intent. *I will give you strength. Stone where you are paper. Iron where you are flesh. You will stand between your lambs and every wolf, and no wolf will pass you. In return—*

The pause stretched. Tam's heart beat once, twice, three times in the ringing dark.

*—you will be my door. Small now. A crack. But doors widen, little listener, and I have been shut in the dark a very long time, and I am so very hungry, and your ears are already mine.*

Tam should have run. Some animal part of him, some deep-buried instinct older than the foundling house and the streets and all his careful lessons, screamed at him to run. But another part — the part that had watched Pip burn with fever, that had counted the children every night for two years like a miser counting coins, that had buried two of them already under cairns beside the road — that part knelt where it was.

"How do I seal it?" he whispered.

*Blood,* said the dark fondly. *A drop will do. From a listening tongue. Speak the words after me—*

Tam bit his tongue. Tasted copper. Opened his mouth—

"—and *that's quite enough of that.*"

Light split the tower.

Not lightning — though it looked like lightning, white and branching, crawling up the stones from below like frost growing in fast-forward. Tam was thrown sideways off his knees, skidding across the platform, and the pupil in the sky *snapped shut*, and the voice cut off mid-word with a sound like a door slammed on a scream, and August Kavanagh stood in the tower doorway with his arm outstretched and storm-light dying along his fingers and an expression on his face that Tam had never seen before and would never forget.

Terror. Pure, naked, animal terror — but not for himself.

"TAM." August crossed the platform in three strides and hauled him up by the collar, shaking him. "What did you say? What did you *agree* to? Tell me exactly — word for word —"

"Let go of me!" Tam wrenched free, stumbling back against the parapet, blood still welling on his tongue. His ears rang. His skin felt scoured, electrified, hollowed. "I wasn't — I wasn't doing anything wrong, I was *praying*, that's all, people are allowed to—"

"You were sealing a covenant!" August's voice cracked like his lightning. "On a bell tower, with your blood, with *it* — do you have any idea what you were opening? Do you have any idea what uses a door?" He grabbed Tam's chin, tilted his face to the starlight, and his eyes went wide. "Your eyes. Gods below, your eyes —"

"What?" Tam's stomach dropped. "What about my eyes?"

But August wasn't answering. He was already moving, tearing a strip from his own sleeve, muttering words under his breath — old words, binding words, words with weather in them — and he pressed the cloth to Tam's bitten tongue and the storm-light sank into Tam's skin like water into parched earth, cold and clean and *severing*, and Tam felt something rip.

He screamed. He couldn't help it. It felt like a tooth being pulled from the root of him — the warmth, the coiled promise, the iron-and-stone future he'd been offered, dragged out through the wound in his tongue and burned away in a flash of white. The last thing the departing presence managed, in the half-second before August's binding closed around it, was not anger.

It was *patience*.

*Another time, little listener. Doors remember.*

Then it was gone, and Tam was on his knees on the cold stone, sobbing without knowing why, and August knelt in front of him with his hands hovering an inch away, not touching, looking like a man standing in the wreckage of something he'd failed to prevent.

"It's done," August said quietly. "It's cut. You're clean. As clean as I can make you."

"Clean." Tam laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You talk about me like I'm a wound."

"No." August's voice gentled in a way that was almost worse. "I talk about you like someone who almost became a wound. There's a difference, and right now it's the only difference in the world."

* * *

They didn't tell the children. That was the first fight.

"They'll find out," August said, pacing the cloister where the fire burned low and the eleven slept in their heap of blankets, oblivious, sacred. "Whatever touched you left a mark, Tam. Things like that don't forget an address. When it comes back — and it will come back — they deserve to know why."

"And what do they do with knowing?" Tam hissed. "Huh? Fern stops sleeping. The twins start praying *harder*, because that's what they do, they're god-touched, praying is their *nature* — and every prayer is a knock on a door, and you just taught me that something answers. You want to put that in their heads? They're *children*, August."

"They're god-touched children walking toward a city where a god wears the king," August shot back. "Ignorance isn't armor. Believe me. I spent twelve years in a valley built out of ignorance, and it didn't keep the past out. It just meant I met it alone."

That landed like a blow and Tam flinched from it visibly, and August immediately looked like he wished he could take it back, and for a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind moving through the broken tower above them.

"That's not fair," Tam said finally, quietly.

"No," August admitted. "It's not. I'm sorry." He sat down heavily on the bench across from Tam, elbows on knees, and looked at him across the fire with those storm-colored eyes, and what was in them wasn't anger anymore. It was the terror again, banked but not out. "Tam. I exiled myself from my own magic for twelve years because I was afraid of what it would cost the people near me. And you know what I learned on this road? The fear doesn't protect anyone. It just means you love them badly — from far away, with your hands in your pockets, ready to run." He shook his head slowly. "I caught you tonight because I was *listening*. Because Sera taught me your tells. Because we've become the kind of people who notice when one of us goes quiet. That's the only armor any of us has ever actually had. Each other."

Tam stared into the fire. His tongue throbbed. His eyes, when he caught his reflection in the rain barrel, still looked faintly wrong — pupils a shade too deep, like wells with no bottom — though August swore the binding would fade it in a day or two.

"You broke it," Tam said. "The bargain. Can it — can it be remade? Could I—"

"*No.*" August said it fast and hard, then made himself slow down. "No. Listen to me. It offered you strength, yes? Stone instead of paper?" Tam nodded. "Then understand what I understood at nineteen, standing over a man I'd killed with weather: power that arrives when you're desperate doesn't make you strong. It makes you *available*. It wasn't offering to make you a wall, Tam. It was offering to make you a *gate*. And gates don't get to choose what walks through them." He leaned forward. "You want to protect them? Then stay paper. Paper bends. Paper *warns*. And paper has us — all of us — folded around it."

Tam laughed despite himself, wet and exhausted. "That's a terrible metaphor."

"I'm a storm-caller, not a poet."

"You're not a storm-caller anymore either, from what Sera says."

"I'm whatever this family needs me to be," August said simply, and the word *family* went through Tam like a hand through a window, gentle and devastating, and he had to look away at the sleeping heap of blankets and breathe for a while.

When he trusted his voice again, he said: "If it comes back. If it finds another way in. You'll tell me? Not manage me. Not decide for me. *Tell* me."

"Every word," August promised. "That's the bargain I'll keep."

* * *

Near dawn, Tam climbed the broken tower one more time. Not to pray. Never to pray again, maybe, or at least never alone, never with his blood available, never with the sky open above him like a mouth. He climbed because he needed to look at the place where he had almost ended, and to spit — carefully, painfully, with his wounded tongue — over the edge into the dark.

Below, the camp stirred. Sera emerged from the undercroft with an armful of ancient ledgers and an expression like a woman carrying live coals, and August went to meet her, and even from the tower Tam could read the shape of it in their bodies: she had found something. Something old and heavy. Something that made August go rigid and Sera grip his arm.

She looked up — straight up, directly at Tam in the broken belfry, as if she'd known exactly where he'd be — and called his name across the waking camp.

"Tam! Get down here. We found the archives." Her face was pale in the gray dawn light. "And Tam — there's a page missing from the binding records. Torn out. Recently."

Tam's hand rose unconsciously to his mouth, to the bandage on his tongue, and the wind moved through the empty tower, and far away to the east, over the direction of the capital, he heard — faint, patient, unmistakable — the sound of bells beginning to ring with no one touching them.
