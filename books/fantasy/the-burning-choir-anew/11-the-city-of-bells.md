## 11. The City of Bells

They saw the city before they heard it.

August had expected the opposite. Every story he'd ever been told about Corvale — the capital, the Crown of the Nine Rivers, the City of a Thousand Bells — began with sound: the harbor horns, the market roar, and above it all the bells, great bronze throats that rang the hours so loudly farmers three valleys away set their clocks by them. Wren had talked about those bells for two days straight as the road climbed out of the riverlands, his deserter's guilt loosening into something almost like homesickness. *You'll hear them before you see them,* he kept saying. *The Dawn Bell especially. Old Abbot Merren used to say you could feel it in your teeth.*

But on the last morning of their journey, cresting the ridge where the pilgrim road bent toward the sea of rooftops below, August heard nothing at all.

The city lay in the bowl of its hills like a coin at the bottom of a well. Morning mist clung to the districts in gray ribbons, thinning where the first light touched the palace spire — a needle of white stone rising from the city's heart, crowned, even at this distance, with gold. The harbor was a smear of masts. The walls were old and vast and ivied. And every bell tower stood dark and silent against the brightening sky.

"Maybe we're too far," Tam said, but his voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was lying to himself. "Sound carries funny over water."

"It's not the distance," Sera said.

She stood at the front of the group with her hood back, her brand — the broken circle of the Order of the Listening Hand — catching the early sun on her cheekbone. She hadn't slept. None of them had, not really, not since the letters. August carried them still, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest: eleven scraps of parchment in a dead man's boot, the king's lucid handwriting crawling between the lines of decrees no lucid man would sign. *It writes with my hand while I watch. It smiles with my mouth. I can hear it thinking, and it thinks in bells.*

"The bells stopped," Sera said now, flat and certain, "the day the king stopped being the king."

They stood on the ridge a moment longer, seven travelers and one child asleep against Tam's shoulder, looking down at a capital that did not greet them. Then the mist shifted, the wind turned, and the sound came after all.

Not from the towers. From everywhere.

It rose out of the city itself, out of the stones and the streets and the bones beneath them — a single low tone, deep as a struck mountain, swelling without any visible cause until August felt it in his molars, in the soles of his feet, in the cage of his ribs. The sleeping child woke and didn't cry, which was somehow worse; she only went still in Tam's arms, eyes wide, listening the way animals listen before an earthquake.

"There's no bell ringing," Wren whispered. His face had drained to the color of the mist. "I know every bell in that city. That's not — there's no rope being pulled. There's no *bell*."

August knew what it was. He'd known since the wells, since the ash-tasting water and the one-word answer that had come up from every depth in the river town: *Soon.* This was the same voice, scaled up. A god clearing its throat through ten thousand tons of bronze and stone.

And beside him, faint but unmistakable, the prayer-stone fragment in his pocket began to hum.

He pressed his hand flat over it, hard, as if holding down a lid. The humming didn't stop. It matched the bell-tone, note for note, like a tuning fork laid against a table.

"August." Sera was watching him. She always watched him when the stone woke; he'd stopped pretending she didn't notice. "How bad?"

"It's singing along," he said.

The tone held for the length of nine breaths. Then it faded, sinking back into the city's foundations, and the silence it left behind was worse than the sound — a silence with weight, a silence like a held note waiting to resume.

Below them, the gates of Corvale opened for the morning traffic.

* * *

Inside the walls, the wrongness changed flavor.

August had expected fear. He had built the whole architecture of their plan — pilgrims' robes, forged waybills, Wren's knowledge of which gate guards took bribes and which took oaths seriously — on the assumption that a city under a dying king, under a *something*, would be afraid. Fear made gaps. Fear let people look away from seven dusty strangers with too many children among them.

There was no fear.

The streets of the lower city were full, clean, and calm. Bakers handed loaves across counters with unhurried smiles. Fishwives argued prices in voices so mild the arguments sounded rehearsed. Two porters who collided under a heavy crate laughed, apologized in unison, and parted without a flicker of anger. Children played a ring-game around a fountain, singing a skipping rhyme August half-recognized, except the words had changed — he caught fragments, *ring the bell, ring it well, soon, soon, all shall be well* — and every adult who passed smiled at the children, and the children smiled back, and none of the smiles ever quite reached anything behind the eyes.

"They're not unhappy," Sera murmured, walking close at his shoulder. Her hands were hidden in her sleeves; August knew she was gripping her prayer-beads hard enough to bite her palms. "That's what I can't — look at them, August. They're *happy*. Or they think they are. It's like watching someone dream they're awake."

"It's the doubts," said a small voice.

August looked down. Pip — the youngest of Tam's choir, six years old, gap-toothed, god-touched in the small way of knowing when bread was about to burn — walked between them with her thumb in her mouth, which she only did when something frightened her badly enough that she'd forgotten to be embarrassed.

"What doubts, little fish?" Tam asked, crouching to her height without breaking stride. He'd gotten good at that, moving with the children woven into him like threads in cloth.

"When Mama was sad," Pip said, "I could hear it. Underneath. Like a mouse under the floor. Everybody has a mouse." She frowned at the smiling street, at the laughing porters, at the baker. "Nobody here has a mouse. The floor is empty."

Tam straightened slowly. He met August's eyes over her head, and August saw in him the same thing he felt in himself: the urge to pick the child up and run, all the way back to Hollowmere, all the way back to anywhere the floor wasn't empty.

They couldn't run. The letters had made sure of that. Somewhere above these clean streets, in the white spire at the city's heart, a man who remembered his name but not his son's face was trapped inside his own smiling body, writing decrees in someone else's hand, begging strangers in secret ink to end it.

August touched the oilcloth packet through his shirt. *Hold on,* he thought, uselessly, the way you think at a grave. *We're coming.*

A woman selling honey-cakes pressed a free one into Pip's hands as they passed. "For the little pilgrim," she said warmly. "Blessed is the road, and blessed the arrival. Soon, little one."

"Soon," Pip echoed automatically, and then bit the cake, and then stopped chewing, and looked up at August with her whole small face gone pale. "Mister August," she whispered, cake crumbs on her lip, "why did my mouth say that? I didn't tell it to."

No one answered her. No one had an answer that wouldn't make it worse.

* * *

Wren's contact was a garrison quartermaster named Doss Halloway, and Wren had promised them he was a good man, which turned out to mean he was a frightened man who'd gotten very skilled at hiding it.

They found him at dusk in the cellar of a chandler's shop near the soldiers' quarter, a broad, soft-bellied man counting candle-stubs by lamplight with the door triple-barred behind him. When Wren knocked the old rhythm — two, pause, three — Halloway opened the hatch a crack, saw the deserter's face, and pulled him through it fast enough to bruise.

"You mad bastard," Halloway breathed, hugging him, then shoving him back to arm's length. "You *mad* bastard, you came back. Do you know what happens to deserters now? Nothing. That's what happens. *Nothing.* They found Corporal Ellery — ran in the spring, made it forty miles. They brought him back and he thanked them. Stood at attention, smiled, thanked them for bringing him home, and asked to be flogged for his ingratitude. And they *didn't flog him*. They just smiled and put him back on the wall." Halloway's hands shook as he relit the lamp. "You want to know why I live in a cellar, Ashfall? Because down here I can't hear it. Up top, some nights, the whole city hums, and if you let yourself hum along it feels *good*, and that's — " his voice cracked — "that's the part I can't say out loud twice."

"We need to get into the palace district," Wren said quietly. "All of us. Before the consecration."

Halloway laughed, one dry bark with no humor in it anywhere. "The consecration. Of course. Everyone's talking about it. First grand rite in the cathedral in a year — the king himself will preside, they're saying. They're saying it'll be the holiest day in a century." He wiped his mouth. "My wife's already sewn the family into new robes. She stayed up three nights doing it, happy as a girl. She says she can't remember why she ever dreaded church." He looked around the lamplit cellar at all of them — the branded priestess, the storm-eyed exile, the pickpocket with orphans hanging off him like burrs — and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "You're going to do something. Against it. Against *him*."

"Yes," Sera said.

"Then I'll get you in." Halloway said it fast, before courage could fail him, the way men volunteer for drowning. "The laundry barges come up the canal to the palace kitchens every third morning. Pilgrim delegations for the consecration start arriving tomorrow — hundreds of them, nobody checks faces anymore, nobody checks *anything* anymore, that's the horror of it, they just smile and wave you through. I can get your papers stamped. After that—" He spread his soft hands. "After that you're inside the lullaby with the rest of us. Gods keep you. If gods still do that sort of thing uninvited."

"They do," August said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

They spent the night in the cellar, and August did not sleep.

He sat apart, near the coal chute where a knife-slit of evening sky showed overhead, and worked the prayer-stone fragment out of its wrapping. It was smaller now than when the dying pilgrim had crawled into Hollowmere with it cradled against his heart — each message it delivered burned a little more of it away, and it had delivered several since: warnings, coordinates, once a scrap of hymn in Sera's order's oldest notation. What remained was a shard the size of a thumbnail, milky-white, veined with faint gold, warm as a held hand.

Up in the city, faint through the chute, the evening bell-tone began. Not struck. Never struck. Rising out of the ground itself, out of the walls, patient as tide.

The shard hummed in harmony.

"I keep wondering," Tam said, settling beside him without asking, the way he always did, "what it's *for*. The ringing. A bell tells time, right? Calls people to pray, warns of fire. But this one doesn't do anything. It just... rings. Like it's keeping something else on time."

"Or keeping something else *down*," August said. "Or keeping everyone asleep. A lullaby doesn't have to mean anything, Tam. It just has to be repeated until the listener stops fighting it."

Tam was quiet a while. Below them, the children had finally gone down, piled together like puppies, Pip clutching the honey-cake she'd refused to finish. Sera sat against the far wall with her beads, lips moving in a hymn she sang silently, guarding her own mind the way she'd taught them all to.

"My ma used to sing to me," Tam said at last. "Before the plague year. I don't remember the song. I remember that it meant sleep was safe." He turned the coin he always carried — a stolen temple token, worn blank — across his knuckles. "This city's got a song like that now. Only it isn't anyone's ma."

"No," August agreed. "It's something wearing the crown."

"And we're going to walk right up its throat tomorrow."

"That's generally how these things go."

Tam huffed — almost a laugh — and knocked his shoulder against August's, and for a moment the cellar was just a cellar, and the fear was just fear, ordinary and survivable, shared between two people who'd chosen each other somewhere on a long road. August held onto that moment deliberately, the way you cup a match in wind. Whatever came next, he wanted to have carried this with him into it.

Then the evening tone swelled again — longer this time, deeper, and every child in the cellar sighed in their sleep at once, a single soft exhalation, seven small mouths breathing as one — and the moment curdled, and August sat rigid in the dark with the humming shard against his palm, and understood that they were already inside the throat.

* * *

The laundry barge left at grey dawn.

They went as pilgrims, seven of them plus four children old enough to walk quietly; the two smallest stayed behind in Halloway's cellar with a bribe, a promise, and Tam's blankest, most dangerous smile. Their papers bore the seal of a shrine-order three hundred miles south that Sera swore no longer existed to complain. The canal gate guard glanced at the documents for less time than it takes to yawn, stamped them, and beamed at the children.

"Blessed road," he said. "Soon now."

"Soon," said everyone, because everyone said it, because it was easier, because the word came out of the mouth like water finding a downhill grade, and August felt each repetition land on his tongue like a small surrender. He caught Sera's eye across the barge. She gave the tiniest shake of her head: *don't fight it here. Not yet. Blend.* So he smiled too, and hated the smile, and kept it on his face like a mask strapped tight.

The canal wound upward through the city's layers, past markets opening in perfect cheerfulness, past a wedding party dancing in the street to music no musicians were playing, past a funeral procession where the mourners wept gently, serenely, like rain on glass — grief present but sanded smooth, all its jagged edges planed away. On the palace hill above, the white spire caught the first true sunlight and threw it back down over the roofs, and for one vertiginous moment the whole city seemed lit from within, golden and gentle and utterly hollow, a lantern with nothing alive inside it.

The barge passed under the last arch, and the palace district opened before them: wide pale avenues, gardens pruned into obedience, guards at post with relaxed shoulders and easy eyes. And rising over everything, filling the morning the way it filled every morning, the bell-tone began — and this close to its source August could finally hear what he'd been refusing to hear since the ridge.

It wasn't one note.

It was thousands. A chord of such vast patience that human ears resolved it into a single hum: the sound of every citizen's smoothed-over contentment, every swallowed doubt, every empty floor where a mouse should be, all of it sounding together, all of it feeding upward, funneling toward the spire like smoke drawn up a flue.

The god wasn't ringing the bells.

The god *was* the bell, and the city was the strike, and every smiling soul within the walls was the bronze.

Pip's small hand found August's and gripped it. He looked down. She was staring up at the spire with her thumb halfway to her mouth, and she said, in a whisper pitched only for him:

"Mister August. There's a man in the tower. He's screaming. Nobody else can hear it but me."

The barge bumped the quay. Ahead, the kitchen doors stood open, and servants in palace livery waved the pilgrims in with wide, warm, welcoming smiles.

August stepped off onto the stone of the palace island, holding a child's hand, carrying a dead man's letters and a dying stone, and walked into the lullaby's heart.
