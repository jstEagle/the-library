## 12. Audience With the Smiling Court

The pilgrim's robes itched. That was the first thing Sera noticed about the capital — that even her disguise felt wrong against her skin, coarse gray wool dyed the color of penitence, and beneath it the brand on her collarbone seemed to warm as they passed through the outer gates, as if the city itself recognized what she had once been and found her amusing.

*Heresy*, the brand said, in the old script of the Order of the Quiet Flame. *She asked too many questions.*

Well. She was still asking them.

"Keep your eyes down," Wren murmured at her shoulder. He wore his old garrison cloak with the insignia torn away, and he moved through the gate crowd like a man walking through a memory — every turn anticipated, every guard's rhythm known to him. "Dawn court is open to petitioners. Pilgrims from the western shrines are common enough this time of year. But the guards count faces now. They've started counting faces since spring."

"Since the king changed," August said quietly.

Wren didn't answer. He didn't have to.

They came through the Gate of Bells into a city that rang without hands.

Sera had heard the stories on the road, and she had not believed them — or rather, she had believed them the way one believes a fever dream, knowing it meant something terrible but unable to give it weight. Now she stood in the great square before the cathedral and heard it for herself: every bell in Vael Corin ringing at once, unstruck, unswung, a sound that came not from bronze but from everywhere and nowhere, rising out of the stones themselves, out of the cobbles, out of the air in her lungs. It was not loud. That was the horror of it. It was gentle. It was the sound a mother makes over a cradle, scaled up to hold an entire city.

Around her, the citizens smiled.

They smiled as they hauled carts. They smiled as they argued prices. A fishmonger scolded her son for stealing a candied plum, and she did it smiling, her voice pitched sweetly under the bells, and the boy smiled back while tears ran down his face, and neither of them seemed to notice anything wrong.

"They can't hear it," Tam whispered. The pickpocket stood close to Sera, closer than he'd stood to anyone in weeks, and his face had gone the color of tallow. "That's the thing. They can't hear the bells. I can hear prayers, right? I've always heard them, little ones, big ones, all the noise people make inside. And this city—" His throat worked. "This city isn't praying. It's being prayed *for*. Somebody's saying grace over all of them, all day, every day, and they just... hum along."

"The lullaby," August said. His hand had drifted to his chest, where the prayer-stone's last fragment hung wrapped in oilcloth against his skin. Sera saw his fingers flex, the way they did when the storm in him stirred, and saw him force them still. In the city of bells, a storm-caller could not afford to twitch.

"Smile," Wren said. "All of you. Smile like you mean it."

Sera smiled. She had been a battle-priestess for eleven years; she knew how to make her face lie. But as they crossed the square toward the palace steps, joining the river of petitioners, she let herself look — really look — at the people around her, and what she saw made her want to weep and scream in equal measure.

It wasn't only the smiling. It was the *evenness*. Grief smoothed flat. Joy smoothed flat. A woman carried a small coffin-sized bundle wrapped in linen toward the cathedral, and her face held the same mild contentment as the man selling roasted chestnuts beside her. Whatever the bells took from these people, they took the edges first. The sharp places. The places where doubt lived.

*This is what Vessel offers*, she thought. *Not peace. The absence of struggle.* She remembered her own order's teachings, the verses they'd made her recite before they branded her: *Blessed are the yielded, for they shall be held.* She had thought it metaphor. She had been young.

It had never been metaphor. Somewhere under this city, something old had been listening to that verse for centuries, learning how it tasted.

* * *

The Hall of Petition had been built to awe, and it still managed it, even hollowed. Sera walked beneath columns of white stone veined with gold, past banners of the Aldric dynasty gone soft with incense smoke, and felt the old architecture pressing down like a hand on the crown of her head. The Order had trained her to read sacred spaces — where power pooled, where it flowed, where it had been *bled*. This hall had been bled dry and refilled with something else. The air tasted of ash and honey.

They filed into the petitioner's queue among farmers and merchants and one trembling widow, and Sera kept her hood up and her eyes lowered and listened.

The bells rang softer here. Inside the hall, they were almost tender.

"He'll grant your suit," the widow ahead of them whispered to no one, swaying slightly. "He grants everything now. He's so *kind*."

"His Grace is mercy itself," the man behind agreed, smiling at the ceiling.

"Mercy itself," someone else echoed, two rows over.

"Mercy itself." A child's voice. Six years old, maybe seven.

The words rippled outward through the crowd like a stone dropped in still water, each voice taking it up a half-breath after the last, until half the hall murmured it in ragged unison — *mercy itself, mercy itself* — and then, as if a conductor's hand had come down, they all fell silent together. At exactly the same instant. Two hundred throats closing on the same syllable.

Sera's skin crawled so hard she nearly broke cover.

Beside her, she felt August go rigid. She couldn't look at him. Looking at him would undo them both. She fixed her gaze on the mosaic floor — saints ascending, their gilded faces worn to smudges by centuries of knees — and breathed through the brand on her collarbone, which had begun, impossibly, to ache.

Trumpets. Not the bells — real trumpets, real lungs, heralds in silver who looked, Sera noted, like the only frightened people in the building.

And the king came out onto the dais.

* * *

Aldric of House Merren had been crowned at nineteen, and the portraits in the shrine-towns showed what that boy had become: a broad-shouldered man with a scholar's stoop, a beard going gray early, laugh lines carved deep by a face that had learned to be amused by the world. Sera had never seen him. But Wren had described him a hundred times on the road, always in the present tense, always with that deserter's guilt cracking his voice: *He used to read petitions himself, my lady. All night sometimes. He'd write notes in the margins. He got my brother's pardon signed because the man couldn't afford a scribe.*

The figure on the dais wore Aldric's body the way a marionette wears its strings.

He was seated — no, he was *arranged* — on the Sunthrone, and every part of him was correct. Robes of state, immaculate. Crown sitting true. Hands folded with princely ease. His posture was perfect, his breathing measured, and when he lifted his head to regard the first petitioner, his face arranged itself into an expression of such warmth, such benediction, that several people in the crowd sighed aloud.

But the movement was wrong. Sera had watched enough men die and enough men pretend to be well to know the difference between a body that moves itself and a body that is moved. Aldric's gestures arrived a half-beat late, as though traveling a longer distance than the space between intention and flesh. When he turned his head, it turned like something on a hinge, smooth and total, no micro-adjustments, no human hesitation. And when he smiled, the smile reached every muscle at once, evenly, perfectly —

— except the eyes.

The eyes begged.

Sera had seen begging eyes before, on battlefields, in plague camps, in the moment before an execution when the condemned finally understands no one is coming. It was a specific look: wide but not afraid, fixed but not seeing, screaming without sound. The king's eyes did that. Behind the serene mask of his face, behind the generous curve of his mouth, two brown eyes — a father's eyes, a tired man's eyes — looked out at the hall full of his kneeling people and *pleaded*.

*See me*, the eyes said. *Someone see me.*

Sera dug her nails into her palm hard enough to bleed. Revulsion and pity rose in her together, twined like serpents, indistinguishable. She wanted to run at the dais. She wanted to be sick. She wanted, more than anything, to look away, and she refused to allow herself to, because looking away was what the whole city did every day, and that was how the thing on the throne stayed fed.

"Rise, daughter, and speak," the king said.

The widow shuffled forward. Her petition was small and terrible: her son had been taken by the crown-guard three months ago for shouting in the square — shouting, the widow said, in a voice gone thin with disbelief, *shouting doubts*, just words, just questions, nothing more — and she had not seen him since.

The hall held its breath. Even the bells seemed to lean in.

And the king smiled wider, and said, in a voice like warmed honey poured over iron: "Your son doubted. Doubt is a wound, good mother, and wounds must be cleaned. He has been cleaned. You will find him at the Cathedral of the Choir, singing with the dawn chorus. Go and hear him. He is happier now."

The widow's face flooded with relief so complete it looked like ecstasy. "Praise the Kindly Throne," she breathed. "Praise the mercy of the crown."

"Praise the mercy of the crown," the hall answered, in unison, instantly, every voice at once, and this time Sera felt the words pass through her own chest like a physical pressure, like a hand testing a door.

She had not spoken. She was certain she had not spoken. But her lips had *shaped* it, she realized, cold flooding her gut. Her mouth had begun to move on its own, following the crowd, following the bells, and only eleven years of discipline had caught it in time.

Beside her, Tam had both hands clamped over his own mouth.

* * *

The audience went on. Petition after petition, and the king granted every one — generously, lavishly, with a largesse that should have been beautiful and was instead the most obscene thing Sera had ever witnessed. A farmer's blighted fields: healed, the king promised, by morning. A merchant's debt: forgiven. A young soldier, weeping, confessed to desertion — actual desertion, the crime that had put Wren Ashfall on the road with a price on his head — and the king rose from the throne, descended the dais steps, and laid both hands on the boy's head, and said:

"There is no shame in weariness. There is no shame in anything. Be held."

And the soldier stopped weeping. Mid-sob, mid-shudder, he simply stopped, and his face smoothed over like water settling, and he smiled, and walked back to his place, and sat down, content.

No one else reacted. No one else found it strange that a man's grief had been switched off like a lamp.

August's hand found Sera's wrist beneath her sleeve. His grip was shaking. She turned her head a fraction and saw his face beneath the pilgrim's hood — gray, sweat-slick, jaw clenched so hard the muscles stood out like cables. The storm-caller, she thought, who had sworn off storms, who feared his own power more than any hunter — he was watching a god do with gentleness what he had always feared doing with violence, and she could see which one horrified him more.

*It doesn't take cities by force*, she thought, watching the king return to his throne, watching the strings draw him smoothly upward. *It takes them by kindness. By rest. By the promise that no one ever has to struggle again.* And gods forgive her, some exhausted animal part of her own soul leaned toward that promise like a cold hand toward a fire. She understood the citizens. That was the worst of it. Standing in that hall, bone-tired from the road, branded and exiled and hunted, she *understood* why they smiled.

The bells swelled, gently, approvingly.

And the king spoke with them.

It happened during the next petition — a land dispute, trivial — and Sera nearly missed it because it was so seamless. The king began a sentence, and the bells came in underneath it, carrying the same cadence, the same pitch, the same rhythm, so that his voice and the bronze voices were one instrument played two ways. *Let the boundary stone stand where love has placed it* — and the bells rang those exact words, wordless and yet unmistakable, meaning pouring out of pure sound.

Then again. Then again. Every sentence now, the bells echoing him, or he echoing the bells; there was no telling which led. The king's body spoke in perfect unison with a thousand bells across a city of eighty thousand souls, and the crowd glowed, and the widow wept happily, and somewhere in the rafters the trumpeters stood very still with faces like men at a funeral.

Only the eyes kept begging.

As the audience wound toward its close, the king's gaze swept the petitioner ranks — a slow, ceremonial blessing of the crowd, his head turning on its hinge — and for one instant, one impossible instant, the sweeping gaze *stopped*.

On August.

The eyes widened. The smile stayed exactly where it was, serene, beneficent, unmoved — but the eyes stretched, strained, and Sera saw in them something new, something beyond pleading: recognition. Hope. A drowning man sighting a rope.

The king's lips parted. The bells throughout the city faltered — a single skipped beat, a stutter in the lullaby, felt in Sera's teeth.

"*Help*—"

One word. Ripped out of him like a splinter from deep flesh, in a voice that cracked halfway through, in a voice that was *only a man's*, hoarse and terrified and human. Half the word, really. *Hel-* — and then the smile reasserted itself, the jaw eased, the voice resumed its honeyed register mid-breath as though nothing had occurred: "—*me* understand your devotion, children. It sustains the realm."

The bells resumed. Smooth. Gentle. As if they had never stumbled.

But the guards had heard. Sera saw it — two of them, near the western pillars, exchanging a glance that contained, for the first time all day, genuine human confusion. And a third guard, older, gray-templed, whose hand had drifted to his sword hilt and whose eyes were scanning the petitioner ranks with slow, deliberate care — not the glazed patrol-sweep of the smiling city, but the sharp, frightened search of a man who had just heard his king cry for help and needed to know if anyone else had heard it too.

Wren made a small sound, low in his throat. Sera felt him shift his weight, angling his body between the searching guard and August.

The king raised both hands in benediction. "Go in peace," he said, and the bells sang it with him, and the crowd surged forward to touch the hem of his passing robe as he withdrew behind the golden screen, and the audience was over.

* * *

They did not speak until they were three streets away, in the shadow of a shuttered dye-house, where the bells rang softer and the few passersby smiled at nothing. Even then, Wren spoke first, and his voice was wrecked.

"You heard it. Tell me you heard it. Tell me I didn't—"

"We heard it," Sera said.

"That was him. That was *him*, that was—" Wren pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Three years I served him. Three years. He used to sneak extra bread to the stable boys. He remembered my mother's name. And that *thing* up there is wearing him like a coat, and everyone in that hall knelt to it, and *smiled*—"

"It knows we're here." August's voice was flat and quiet, the voice he used when the other voice — the storm voice — was too close to the surface. He stared at the middle distance, at nothing. "It looked right at me. Out of all of them. It found me in a crowd of hundreds, and it lost control for half a second trying to reach me." His hand rose unconsciously to the fragment at his chest. "It's not just wearing him. It's *using* him to call for help. Or—" his jaw tightened, "—it's showing me what it holds. What it will keep holding unless I come to it."

"Or it's bait," Tam said. He had his arms wrapped around himself, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You walk in there thinking you're saving him, and it closes its hand. That's what I'd do. That's what anything smart enough to sing a whole city to sleep would do."

"Maybe," Sera said slowly. "But bait implies it knows what we are. That look wasn't calculation, Tam. I know the difference between a trap and a scream. I've heard both." She pulled her hood back and rubbed the ache between her brows, and the brand pulled tight against her collar. "There's a sealed mind under that smile. Wren's letters said as much — lucid pleas hidden in official decrees, a man writing from inside his own prison. If Aldric can push one word through during open court, when the bells are loudest, when the god is busiest performing mercy..."

"Then he can push more," August finished.

"With help," Sera said. "From the inside. Something that can reach into the sealed place and speak to him directly." She looked at August, and hated what she was about to say, and said it anyway, because someone had to. "The prayer-stone fragment. It opened a channel once already — it burned itself out delivering a message to you across half a kingdom. A message *meant for another man*. Which means it can carry words to a man who cannot send them."

August's face went still in a way she had learned to fear. Stillness, in August Kavanagh, was not calm. It was the eye of something.

"You're asking me to use the last piece of the thing that dragged me out of Hollowmere," he said, "to knock on the door of a god wearing a king."

"I'm asking you to answer a man who begged a stranger for help in front of five hundred witnesses," Sera said, "and got away with it by half a second. We don't get a second invitation, August. The gray-haired guard will report what he heard. By tomorrow the palace will know someone in the petitioner ranks made the king stumble. We have tonight."

The bells above the dye-house rang the noon hour, soft and sweet and everywhere, and beneath their beauty Sera fancied she could hear — faint, far off, threaded through the bronze like a hair through milk — a single hoarse voice, repeating the same unfinished word.

*Hel-. Hel-. Hel-.*

August closed his hand around the fragment through his shirt, and nodded once.

"Tonight, then," he said. "Take me to the king."
