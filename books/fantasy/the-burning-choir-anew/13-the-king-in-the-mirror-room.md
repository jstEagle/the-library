## 13. The King in the Mirror Room

The prayer-stone had been dying for weeks.

August kept it wrapped in wool at the bottom of his pack, and every night he took it out and held it to his ear like a seashell, listening for the sea of it — that faint, stubborn hum that had crawled into Hollowmere on a dead man's back and refused, ever since, to be quiet. Each night the hum was thinner. Each morning the stone was lighter, as though something inside it were being spent, coin by coin, on nothing he could see.

Tonight, in the bell-tower's shadow where Wren had hidden them among dusty vestments and roosting pigeons, August unwrapped it and found it glowing.

Not humming. Glowing — a pale gold light bleeding through the cracks in its surface like dawn through shuttered windows, and along those cracks ran words too small and too old for him to read, moving like fish under ice.

"Sera," he said, and his voice came out wrong, thin as a boy's.

She was there before he finished her name, still half-dressed, her broken order's medallion swinging against her collarbone. She looked at the stone for a long moment, and then at him, and he watched her arrive at the same conclusion he had been circling all night: *it is going out, and when it goes out, whatever chance it carries goes with it.*

"It's reaching," she said quietly. "All this time I thought it was only a message. It isn't. It's a hand, stretched out across the whole distance between here and wherever it was made." She crouched beside him, close enough that he could smell the road on her, woodsmoke and iron. "And someone on the other end has finally found the strength to grip it."

"The king."

"The king." Sera turned the stone over in her palm with a priestess's careful irreverence. "Vessel keeps Aldric sealed behind glass, August. We've seen what that looks like from the outside — the smiling, the bells, the eyes. But glass has two sides." Her thumb traced one of the crawling lines of script. "This is the sound of a man pressing his mouth against his side of it."

August's hands had gone cold. He thought of the letters Wren had carried out of the palace — lucid pleas stitched inside official decrees like smuggled letters sewn into a coat lining, *I remember my name, whoever finds this, I remember my name* — and how the whole company had wept or raged or gone silent around that table, and how August alone had felt something worse than any of it. He had felt *recognized*. Because he knew what it was to be sealed away from the world and made into a warning.

"What do I do?" he asked, which was not the question he meant.

Sera heard the real one anyway. She always did. "You hold it," she said. "You let it spend itself. And you go where it takes you, and you come back, because—" her voice caught on something and she pushed past it, "—because I have lost enough people to gods who answer prayers wrong. You will not be one more."

He almost told her then. Almost said: *if it comes down to me or him, I've already decided.* But the stone flared in his hands, gold flooding through every crack at once, and there was no more time for honesty or cowardice, whichever it was.

The last thing he saw was Sera's face, lit from below, saying his name.

Then there were no things to see.

* * *

He fell upward.

That was the only way he could ever describe it afterward — a falling that went the wrong direction, through dark that pressed on him like deep water, past shapes that might have been memories or might have been bells, each one tolling a note he felt in his teeth rather than heard. The storm-song stirred in his chest without his permission, rising like water answering the moon, and he clamped down on it, terrified that in this place — wherever this place was, whatever it was made of — his magic would be a shout in a room full of sleeping people.

When he stopped falling, he was standing in a corridor.

It was a corridor he knew. Not from his own life — from everyone's life. It was the corridor every mind builds behind its own eyes: tall doors receding into distance, some open, some shut, some bricked over so long ago the mortar had gone the color of bone. Dust hung in the air without settling. There was no floor, exactly, only the idea of one, and no ceiling, only a darkness above that breathed.

Most of the doors were shut. Most of them were *wrong* — swollen in their frames, painted over, nailed through with nails of light. And down the whole length of the corridor, spaced evenly as fence posts, stood mirrors.

They were not ordinary mirrors. They were tall and narrow and framed in gilt gone green with age, and they did not show the corridor. They showed a city. August recognized it from Wren's descriptions, from the children's songs, from the bells that rang unstruck at dawn: the capital, seen from the height of the cathedral, its streets laid out below like circuitry, its citizens moving through their days with that terrible ease he had witnessed from a rooftop three days ago. In every mirror the city was the same. In every mirror the sun stood at the same angle, noon forever, and the people smiled, and the bells rang, and nothing anywhere changed.

"Ah," said a voice behind him, cracked and human and astonished. "Ah. You're real."

August turned.

The man standing at the end of the corridor was not what the coins showed. The coins showed King Aldric IV in his prime, broad and golden-bearded, a sword-arm and a smile. This man was gaunt as a winter branch, dressed in a nightshirt gone grey with years of wearing, barefoot on a floor that did not exist. His hair had gone white not with age but with the particular white of things bleached — left too long in light that was not sunlight. His wrists bore marks like old rope burns, except the rope had been made of light too.

But his eyes. His eyes were awake. After the smiling court, after the perfect unison of voice and bell, after the mercy granted generously by a mouth that belonged to something else — these eyes were the most beautiful thing August had ever seen, because they were afraid, and only living things are afraid.

"You're real," Aldric said again, and took a step forward, and staggered, and August caught him, and the king of the realm gripped the front of August's shirt with both hands and shook him like a beggar shaking a bread loaf. "You're real, you're not one of his, you *hurt*, I can feel your hands, oh — oh, forgive me, forgive me, I haven't touched anything true in six years—"

"Your Majesty—"

"Don't." Aldric laughed, and it broke in the middle. "Don't call me that. That word belongs to him now. Call me Aldric. Call me nothing. Call me the madman in the mirror room, it's what I am." He pulled back, holding August at arm's length, studying his face with desperate attention. "The stone worked. I didn't think — I prayed into it every night for a year, just words, just *is anyone there, is anyone left who isn't his* — I never thought anyone would actually—"

"I'm August," August said. "August Kavanagh."

Something moved across the king's face — recognition, and beneath the recognition, grief, arriving late like thunder after lightning.

"Kavanagh," Aldric repeated. "Stormbound. Your mother sang at my coronation, did you know that? Before everything. Before I put the crown on and learned what else was on it. Maerin Kavanagh, with a voice like weather coming." His gaze drifted past August's shoulder, to the mirrors, to the frozen city in them. "She tried to warn me, near the end. Sent word that the binding was failing, that Vessel was waking hungry. My advisors called it superstition. My advisors were already his by then, I understand that now. I understood nothing then. I was thirty-one years old and I wanted to be a good king, and wanting to be good is the most open door there is."

"Aldric." August kept his voice level the way you keep a lamp steady in wind. "We've come to free you. All of us — a battle-priestess, a deserter, a thief, seven god-touched children who sing better than any choir in your cathedral. We know what's wearing you. We know how the binding was made. My family helped make it, a thousand years ago, and my mother died carrying the song that can undo it. We're going to sing it. We're going to tear him out of you."

For a moment — one bright, unbearable moment — hope transformed the king's face into the face from the coins. August saw the man he must have been: the young king laughing at his own coronation, crown askew, certain the world could be governed kindly.

Then the hope went out, deliberately, like someone pinching a candle rather than letting it burn them.

"No," Aldric said.

August's stomach dropped. "No?"

"No." The king stepped back, wrapping his arms around himself, and August saw that he was trembling. "Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I have rehearsed this speech for six years with no one to say it to, and I will only get to say it once. You cannot tear him out."

"He can be bound again—"

"There is no *again*." Aldric's voice rose, cracked, fell. "Boy — August — listen. When your ancestors bound Vessel beneath this city, they needed a vessel. Do you know why? Because a god cannot be chained to stone. Stone doesn't *want* anything. A chain needs an anchor that wants. So they gave him a person — some poor soul from the Stormbound line, someone who agreed, someone who was told it would be sleep, rest, a small dark room at the bottom of the world. And it was, for a thousand years, until the binding wore through, and Vessel woke up starving, and the nearest door was a young king who wanted so badly to be good."

He turned to the nearest mirror. In it, the city glittered under its permanent noon, and somewhere in it, faintly, bells.

"Do you know what he does?" Aldric asked softly. "Everyone thinks he devours us. He doesn't devour. Devouring ends. He *smooths*. He takes the sharp edges off. Doubt, grief, hunger, love that costs anything — he files it all down until what's left smiles easily and rings in unison and calls it peace. Six years, and I have watched him do it to my court, my city, my—" his voice failed, gathered itself, failed again. "My son."

August went very still.

"Tamir," Aldric said. "He'll be eleven now. Eleven. I have held him maybe four times since the crowning, because Vessel dislikes... interruptions, and I learned to stay away, to keep the glass between us thick. And now I stand in this corridor and I try to remember his face and I *can't*." He said it without drama, flatly, the way you report a wound that has stopped bleeding because everything that could bleed has bled. "I remember his name. I remember he had a scar on his chin from falling off the garden wall, I remember he was afraid of geese, I remember facts. But his face — Vessel took it. Filed it smooth. And every day I forget one more thing about him, and every day I think: soon there will be nothing left of me but the shape of a king, and he won't even need to wear me anymore. I'll just be a coat on a hook."

"Aldric—"

"So hear my rehearsal." The king faced him fully, and there was nothing royal left in his posture, only a father in a ruined nightshirt, and it was the most kingly thing August had ever seen. "Your song can unbind him. Fine. Wonderful. But unbinding needs a vessel, and if you tear him loose with nowhere to put him, he will take the nearest wanting thing. Not me — I'm worn through, I'm mostly glass myself. He'll take one of your children. Or your priestess. Or *you*, storm-caller, you with all that weather in your blood — you'd be a feast. So either you bind him into someone, which makes you your ancestors, which makes you the thing that did this to me—" his voice shook, "—or you starve him, and starving him means feeding him yourself until there's nothing left, and I have watched what that costs, I have spent six years watching it from inside, and I am telling you as the only man alive who knows: *do not pay it.*"

"Then what do we do?" August's own voice was rough now. "Walk away? Leave you ringing bells forever?"

"Yes," Aldric said simply. "Leave me. Go home. Take the children somewhere the bells don't reach and let me be the price that was already paid. Because there is one other way, August, and I have saved it for last, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide you're too kind to do it."

The corridor seemed to lean in. The dust hung. In the mirrors, the city smiled its noon smile.

"When you sing your song," Aldric said, "and he tears loose, and he reaches for the nearest soul — offer him mine. Not my body. My *self*. What's left of me in here." He tapped his own breastbone, once, gently, like knocking on a door he hoped was empty. "There's barely enough of me left to matter. A name. A few facts. A face I can't even find anymore. Let him eat that, let him choke on the dregs of me, and while he's swallowing, pour the storm into the hollow where I was and seal it cold. No vessel. No anchor that wants. Just a dead room with a dead god in it, forever."

"And you?" August whispered, though he already knew.

"Gone." Aldric smiled, and it was the first honest smile August had seen in the capital, and it was devastating. "Gone, and glad of it. August Kavanagh — heir of the Stormbound, son of Maerin — I am a king with nothing left to govern but his own ending, and I am *begging* you for it. Every night I press my mouth against the glass and pray, and do you know what I pray for? Not rescue. Rescue is just a longer version of this. I pray that someone strong enough will come and *end it*, even if ending it ends me. Especially then." He stepped closer, and took August's hands, and his grip was astonishingly strong for a ghost. "You want to save me. I can see it. You're kind, and kindness always wants to save the person in front of it. But the person in front of you is a coat on a hook, and the people behind you are seven children and a woman who loves you, and if you waste your one song on mercy for me, he will wear *them* next, and he will be so gentle about it, and they will smile, and they will ring."

August could not speak. The storm-song was rising in him again, unbidden, grieving, and far away — impossibly, through layers of dark and glass and god — he thought he heard Sera's voice calling his name, calling him home across the distance, and he clung to the sound of it like a rail in floodwater.

"I can't promise," he managed at last. "I can't promise you what you're asking. But I promise you this: I will not leave you ringing. Whatever it costs, and whoever pays it — it ends, Aldric. Soon."

The king closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and grateful, and terribly tired.

"Soon," he echoed. "He says that too. To the city, through the wells, through the bells. *Soon.*" A broken laugh. "Let's hope you both keep your word."

The corridor began to dissolve — walls going soft as breath on glass, mirrors dimming one by one, the frozen city flickering out street by street. Aldric was fading with it, growing translucent from the feet up, and he did not seem frightened. He seemed relieved, the way a sentry feels at the sound of boots coming up the stair.

"Wait," August said. "Your son — Tamir — his face. Try. For me. Try to—"

And for one instant, one grace note before the dark took it, the king's face cleared, and August *saw*: a round-cheeked boy mid-laugh, gap-toothed, a scab on his chin, geese honking outrage behind him in a sunlit garden that existed nowhere anymore.

"Tell him," Aldric said, already mostly gone, his voice coming from everywhere and nowhere, from behind the glass, from inside the bells, "that I remembered. Tell him I fought hardest for that."

Then August was falling again, downward this time, toward warmth and lamplight and a woman shouting his name, and the last thing he carried with him out of the drowning dark was not the king's plea, nor the plan forming cold and terrible in his mind —

— but the boy's laugh, ringing sweet and mortal and doomed, like a small bell in a city of great ones.

He woke with Sera's hands on his face and the prayer-stone crumbling to grey sand between his fingers, and the first thing he said, before his eyes had even opened, was:

"We have a problem. And I think I'm the answer."
