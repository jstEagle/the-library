## 14. Stormsong, Recovered

The vaults beneath the palace smelled of cold stone and older secrets.

Sera had expected gold. She had expected reliquaries and crowns, the gaudy hoard of a dynasty grown fat on its own legend. What she found instead was silence — a silence so complete it pressed against her ears like deep water, broken only by the drip of moisture somewhere in the dark and the soft shuffle of Wren's boots behind her.

"Are you sure this is the way?" the deserter whispered. His lantern threw their shadows long across walls carved with processions of figures — kings, priests, kneeling crowds — all rendered with their faces worn smooth by time or deliberately effaced. Sera did not want to think about which.

"The archivist's ledger said the Deep Repository was below the third cistern level." She ran her fingers along the wall until she found the seam she'd memorized from the stolen plans: a vertical line disguised as a drainage channel. "The Stormbound materials were catalogued here after the Purge of Kellin's Reach."

"After they killed his mother, you mean."

Sera didn't answer. She pressed her palm flat against the stone and pushed, and the hidden door gave with a groan that seemed to come from the mountain's bones rather than the hinges.

* * *

Inside, the repository was smaller than she'd imagined. A single chamber, octagonal, its ceiling lost in darkness above the reach of their light. Shelves lined the walls, but most were empty — cleared out, she supposed, when Vessel woke and began tidying history to suit itself. What remained had been overlooked, or deemed harmless, or simply forgotten in the god's long slow digestion of the kingdom's memory.

On a pedestal in the center, under a dome of glass gone cloudy with age, lay a tuning fork.

It was bone-white. Not ivory — Sera had seen enough ivory to know the difference. This was something else, something that caught the lantern light and held it the way snow holds the last glow of sunset. It was longer than her hand, forked at one end, and it hummed.

Not audibly. But she felt it, the way you feel a struck bell through the soles of your feet, a vibration too low for ears and too insistent for skin to ignore. The moment she lifted the glass dome, the hum sharpened, and somewhere far above them — floors and floors up, in the cathedral where the bells rang unstruck at dawn — something *noticed*.

"We need to be quick," Wren said. His voice had gone thin.

"It noticed us the moment we entered the city," Sera said. "Quick won't save us. Only quiet will." She wrapped the fork in the wool cloth she'd brought for exactly this purpose, muffling its song, and tucked it inside her coat against her ribs. Even muffled, she could feel it beating there like a second heart. "There should be documents. Paper. Anything relating to the binding rites."

Wren swept his lantern along the shelves. "Here. Ledgers. Correspondence." He pulled a bundle free and coughed at the dust. "This one's sealed with the storm sigil."

Sera took it from him. Her hands were steady — her hands were always steady; the Order had trained the tremor out of her before she was old enough to understand what trembling meant — but something behind her sternum was not steady at all.

The seal broke easily. Inside were letters, dozens of them, bound with cord gone brittle as straw. And on top, folded separately, a single sheet of heavy paper bearing a water stain shaped like nothing at all and yet, to Sera's eyes, like a woman with her arms flung wide.

She read the first letter. Then the second. By the third, she had sat down on the cold floor without deciding to, the way your legs decide things without consulting you.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

"It's her." Sera's voice came out strange. "August's mother. Maerin Kavanagh. These are her letters — to the Order, to the crown archivists, to anyone who would listen. She knew what was coming. Decades ago, she *knew*, and she wrote and wrote and wrote, and no one—" Sera stopped. Swallowed. "No one answered. The same way no one answered me."

She thought of her own heresy trial. Nine years old at the center of it, no — twenty-three, but it had made her feel nine — standing before the Consistory while men who had never prayed honestly in their lives pronounced anathema on her for saying the gods had gone quiet. *You claim the heavens are empty. You claim our prayers rise into nothing.* They had made her kneel on stone for six hours while they decided whether to brand her or merely break her.

And here was Maerin Kavanagh, thirty years earlier, writing the same truth in a steadier hand, and being ignored so thoroughly that her letters ended up filed in a basement instead of burned.

"They didn't ignore her because she was wrong," Sera said slowly. "They ignored her because she was right, and being right was dangerous. If the Stormbound line still existed — if the binding could still be renewed — then the crown couldn't pretend the old arrangements were eternal. They wanted the question buried. So they buried her."

* * *

They carried everything back up through the cistern levels, past the dripping dark and the faceless carvings, to the safehouse Wren had secured above a disused chandlery in the Tanners' Quarter. The bells had rung at dawn that morning — they always rang at dawn now — and the sound had followed Sera down into her sleep afterward, smoothing her dreams into featureless white.

August was waiting when they returned. He was always waiting; he slept badly and rose early and spent the hours before dawn sitting by the window with his hands open on his knees, as if waiting for weather that never came. When he saw the bundle in Sera's arms, he went very still.

"You found something."

"I found her."

He crossed the room in three strides. Then stopped, as if the distance between them had become suddenly real, suddenly wide. "Her letters?"

"And more." Sera unwrapped the tuning fork first, because she was a coward about the harder thing, and set it on the table between them. The hum filled the room immediately, that subterranean vibration, and August flinched as though struck.

"That's—" He didn't finish. He reached toward it and pulled his hand back twice before he finally touched it, two fingers resting on one tine, and Sera watched his whole body change — watched the exile fall away for a moment, the mender of roofs, the man who had spent years teaching himself not to listen to the sky. His eyes closed. "I know this sound. I've known it my whole life. I thought it was the wind in Hollowmere." A breath. "It was never the wind."

"The fork is the key," Sera said. "Or part of it. The letters describe the rite — the original binding. Maerin reconstructed it from fragments. But the actual song, the words, the melody—" She drew out the final document, the one with the water stain. "This is it. Her final hymn. The one she died carrying out of Kellin's Reach."

August looked at the paper the way a man looks at a snake he knows is venomous.

"No."

"August—"

"No." He stepped back from the table, and his voice cracked on the second word, and Sera realized with a lurch that he wasn't angry. He was afraid. Genuinely, deeply afraid, in a way she had never seen him — not in the ambush, not facing the hunters, not even the night Tam's bargain had nearly consumed him. "You don't understand what that song is. You can't. I heard pieces of it when I was a child, humming through the walls of our house, and even the *pieces* — even half-remembered, even sung wrong — they *demand* things. The song doesn't just bind Vessel. That's the trap. That's what nobody understands." His hands were shaking now, openly, and he stared at them as if they belonged to a stranger. "The binding needs a vessel. It has always needed a vessel. My mother didn't die smuggling a weapon out of Kellin's Reach. She died smuggling a *door*, and every door opens both ways."

"Then we read it and we find another way through," Sera said. "We're not children fumbling in the dark anymore, August. We have the fork, the rite, the letters. We have Wren's garrison contacts, the choir, Tam. We have each other. Whatever the song demands, we'll meet it together, and we'll—"

"*Read it.*" His voice came out raw. "You want me to read my dead mother's handwriting. Her last words. The thing she bled for." He laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "Do you know what I remember about her? Nothing. Almost nothing. I was six. I remember her hands. I remember she sang off-key when she thought no one was listening. I remember the night the hunters came, and my father putting me in the root cellar, and the sound—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "The sound the sky made. For thirty years I have built a life on top of that sound like building a house over a grave, and you want me to dig it up. You want me to hold her voice in my hands and *read* it, knowing that the reading is the beginning of the end. Because once I know the song, Sera, once it's in me, it will start making demands. Songs like that don't wait politely to be sung. They *insist*."

The room was silent except for the fork's buried hum and, beneath it, faintly, the morning bells.

Sera looked at him — this man she had come to love in the fierce, unsentimental way the road teaches, love measured in shared watches and split bread and the wordless language of people who have each saved the other's life — and she understood that she was asking him to do the one thing exile had been designed to prevent.

"Yes," she said quietly. "I am asking you to dig it up. Because Aldric is up in that cathedral right now, wearing his own body like a borrowed coat, begging with his eyes, and the only shovel we have is your grief." She gestured at the letters. "Your mother spent her whole life trying to put this song in someone's hands. Not because she wanted a sacrifice. Because she wanted a *choice*. Read her letters, August. All she ever asked was that someone get to choose."

August stood motionless for a long moment. Rain had begun outside — real rain, ordinary rain, the first they'd heard since entering the city, drumming on the chandlery roof — and his head turned toward it involuntarily, the way a compass needle turns, the way it always turned.

"I can't," he said at last. "Not today. Maybe not—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Leave it on the table. Leave it where I can see it. That's all I can do today."

"That's enough," Sera said. "For today, that's enough."

* * *

He did not touch the hymn that day, nor the next.

But Sera saw him looking. She saw him circle it the way you circle a fire, drawn and repelled in equal measure. On the second evening she found him holding the tuning fork, turning it over in his fingers, and when she sat beside him he spoke without preamble, in the low voice men use for confessions.

"My father told me once — the only time he ever spoke of her — that she sang to the storm the night they came. Not against it. *To* it. Like it was a frightened animal and she was the only one who spoke its language." He turned the fork in the lamplight. "What if the song demands me, Sera? What if that's why it survived? What if she wrote it knowing it would need a Kavanagh throat, and she hated herself for writing it, and she sent it out anyway because the alternative was letting Vessel eat the world?"

"Then she also wrote thirty letters begging whoever found it to look for another way," Sera said. "Grief makes liars of us all, August. It tells us we deserve the worst interpretation. Don't let it write your mother for you. Let *her* do that."

He looked at her then, and something in his face shifted — not resolution, not yet, but the ground where resolution might take root.

That night, long past midnight, Sera woke to the sound of paper unfolding.

She did not get up. She lay in her narrow bed listening to the rain, and to the silence beyond it, and to the small terrible sound of a man reading his mother's voice for the first time in thirty years. Once, she heard him make a noise — half breath, half wound — and she clenched her fists against the blanket and did not go to him, because some griefs must be walked alone or not at all.

At dawn, when the false bells began their smooth, smiling peal, August appeared in her doorway. His eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear.

"She left me a note," he said. "At the end of the hymn. Just four words." His voice shook. "*Sing it as yourself.*"

Before Sera could answer, the door burst open and Tam stumbled in, gray-faced, rain streaming off him, the children huddled behind him in the stairwell like a flock driven before a storm.

"The bell tower," Tam gasped. "One of ours talked. They know where we are. There are guards on every street, Sera — they're coming *now*."
