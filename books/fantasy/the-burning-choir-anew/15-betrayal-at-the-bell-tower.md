## 15. Betrayal at the Bell Tower

Tam had always known which silences were dangerous.

Growing up in the gutter-choirs of Marrowgate, he'd learned to read a room the way other children learned their letters: the hush before a watchman's boots turned down your alley, the held breath of a mark deciding whether to call for the guard, the particular stillness of a house where someone was crying behind a closed door and pretending not to. Silence wasn't empty. Silence was full of things people didn't want said.

So when Lark went quiet at supper, Tam heard it like a scream.

She sat at the edge of the tallow-lit circle with her bowl untouched, small hands wrapped around it as if for warmth, staring at nothing. Nine years old, gap-toothed, god-touched in the small way most of the choir children were — she could make candle flames lean toward her when she hummed, could find lost coins by asking them politely where they'd rolled. A gentle gift. A useless one, in a city that had started smiling too easily.

The others ate. Wren argued softly with Sera about garrison rotations. August sat apart, as he always did now, turning the bone-white tuning fork over in his fingers like a man worrying a wound.

And Lark watched him. Not with awe, the way she'd watched him in the first weeks. With something hungrier. Something that looked, to Tam's prayer-hearing ear, exactly like longing.

He knew that look. He'd worn it himself, not so long ago, kneeling in the dark with his palms pressed together and something vast and patient listening on the other side.

*No,* Tam told himself. *She wouldn't. She knows better. We all know better.*

But knowing better had never once stopped a hungry child from reaching.

* * *

They were hiding in the bell-ringer's loft above the chapel of Saint Vessa-the-Drowned, three streets from the cathedral square, because Wren's old garrison contact swore no crown-guard came here — the bells rang unstruck these days, and ringers were out of work, and out-of-work ringers made poor informants. The loft smelled of dust and old rope and pigeon droppings, and through the slatted shutters they could see the great bronze throat of the cathedral bell across the rooftops, gleaming dully in the perpetual amber dusk the consecration preparations had cast over everything.

Three days until the rite. Three days until Vessel bound the whole city's faith into itself forever, if August couldn't stop it.

Lark hadn't spoken since yesterday morning.

Tam found her that night on the loft stairs, knees drawn up, whispering. His blood went cold before he even caught the words, because he recognized the posture — the bowed head, the cupped hands, the little altar of a child's folded fingers. He'd knelt exactly so in a flooded cellar while something ancient offered him strength in exchange for everything.

"—and I want to go home," Lark was whispering, "I want my mum, I want *anyone*, please, please, I'll be good, I just want—"

"Lark." He crouched beside her, kept his voice gentle, the voice he used on the little ones after nightmares. "Hey. Look at me."

Her eyes were wet and shining. Too shining. There was a faint glow deep in them, like coals seen through a keyhole, and Tam's stomach dropped through the floorboards.

"Something answered you," he said.

"I didn't—" She flinched. "It was just a prayer. Prayers are allowed. Sera says prayers are *allowed*—"

"Sera says prayers are allowed when nobody's listening." He took her hands. They were warm. Children's hands should never be that warm. "What did it say back?"

Lark's chin trembled. And then, horribly, she smiled — a smile that wasn't hers, too smooth, too certain, borrowed from the faces in the streets below. "It said it could take me home. It said home is wherever the bells are. It said—" her voice cracked, split into two registers, the child's and something underneath it that resonated like struck bronze, "—*it said the storm-caller will break everything, and you can stop him, and everyone will love you forever.*"

Tam's mouth went dry. "Lark. Listen to me. Whatever it promised you, it lies. That's what it does. It wore a king like a coat. It will wear you like a sock puppet and throw you away when—"

"You don't know!" She wrenched free, and the candle stubs along the stair wall all leaned toward her at once, flames stretching like necks. "You have August! You have Sera! You have *people*! I had nobody before the choir and I'll have nobody after, because after we do whatever we're going to do, everyone goes home except me, because I don't *have* one!" The glow in her eyes flared. "It said it would give me one."

The words landed in Tam's chest like a knife finding the gap between ribs. Because she was right. That was the worst of it. In the plan they'd built — Wren turning his garrison, Sera's hymns, August's terrible song — there had been no place written for nine-year-old girls who wanted their mothers. They'd carried the children this far because leaving them behind was unthinkable, and now carrying them had brought them to the edge of a war, and every plan has casualties, and none of them had said aloud who those casualties might be.

"It already gave me a way," Lark whispered. "It's already done, Tam. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"What did you do?" He grabbed her shoulders. "Lark. *What did you do?*"

Below them, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to toll.

Not one of the unstruck bells that rang at dawn. This one was small, cracked, half-forgotten — the chapel bell directly beneath their feet, which had not rung in forty years. Its voice rose through the floorboards, through the rope-loft, through Tam's bones, and it was ringing the old alarm pattern, the one every gutter-child knew: *soldiers in the street, soldiers in the street, soldiers in the street.*

* * *

"Wake up! Everyone up, NOW!"

Tam hit the loft at a run. Sera was already moving — battle-priestess reflexes dying hard — snatching her mace from beneath her bedroll, her face going from sleep to grim clarity in the space of a breath. Wren rolled to his feet with a hand on his sword. The younger children woke crying, and Pip, the oldest girl after Lark, gathered them against the wall with her arms spread wide, her little weather-gift crackling static through her hair.

August stood slowly. He didn't reach for any weapon. He looked at Lark, standing at the top of the stairs with her arms wrapped around herself, and Tam watched him understand — watched it move across his face like weather.

"Oh," August said quietly. "Oh, child. What did you give them?"

"I'm sorry." Lark's voice was tiny again, all the borrowed bronze gone, just a little girl in an oversized coat. "They said they wouldn't hurt anyone. They said they just wanted to talk to him. They said the king forgives everybody, the king forgives *everybody*, he's been so merciful lately, everyone says—"

"That's the point," Sera said, low and furious, pulling the shutter open a finger's width. Below, torchlight was blooming in the street like a row of terrible flowers. Crown-guard, a dozen at least, in the new polished armor that gleamed too bright, moving with that smooth unhurried coordination that made Tam's skin crawl. No shouting. No battering ram. Just men arranging themselves around the chapel doors with the patience of people who knew exactly how this ended. "That's always been the point. Mercy is the leash. Come see the king, little pilgrim. He only wants to help."

"The back stair," Wren said. "Rope loft connects to the robing room, robing room has a crypt door. Old priests didn't like walking past the poor to get to their own graves." He was already kicking open the hatch. "Children first. Tam, take the little ones. Sera, rear guard. August—"

"I know." August pocketed the tuning fork. His face was pale and set. "I'm the thing they want. I go last."

"No," Tam said.

Everyone looked at him.

"You go second-to-last," Tam said. "Because last is where blades come from, and I've been watching doorways since before any of you could walk." He forced a grin, the quick crooked one that had charmed purses out of pockets in six cities. "Professional habit. Somebody's got to be the ugly surprise at the bottom of the stairs."

"Tam—" August started.

"Move," Tam said. "*Move.*"

* * *

The crypt was cold and smelled of stone and centuries, and the children's footsteps echoed wrong, too loud, each small footfall a drumbeat announcing them. Tam counted heads twice, then a third time — Pip, Mossy, the twins, little Ansel with his broken arm healing crooked, and Lark, trailing at the back with her head down, and Tam couldn't decide whether to put her in the middle where she was safe or the front where he could watch her, and chose the middle, and hated himself for choosing anything at all.

Behind them, above, the chapel doors gave way. Not smashed — opened. That was worse. The guard had a key, or the chapel had simply decided to let them in, and either way it meant the whole city was the god's body now, every lock a joint, every hinge a muscle.

"They're in the nave," Sera breathed from the rear. "Moving slow. They're herding us toward the crypt door. There'll be more outside it."

"There's always more outside it," Tam muttered. "Story of my life."

The crypt door opened onto a bone-dry ossuary passage, and the ossuary onto a drainage culvert, and the culvert ran black and knee-deep toward the river — and there, exactly as Sera had predicted, torchlight waited at the far arch. Four guards. Halberds. Unhurried.

Sera stepped forward, mace rising, hymn already building in her throat — and that was when the fifth man dropped from the ceiling vaults where he'd been waiting, silent as spilled ink, blade already falling.

Toward August.

Later, Tam would try to reconstruct the moment and fail. There was no decision in it. There was no thought at all. There was only the geometry of it — the arc of the blade, the angle of August's turned shoulder, the half-second in which no one else had even seen — and Tam's body solving the problem the way bodies do when they've spent ten years learning that the fastest way between two points is yourself.

He hit August's chest with both hands and shoved.

The blade came down anyway.

It took him across the back, below the left shoulder blade, a line of white-hot cold that he felt before he felt anything, before the pain, before the sound — and the sound was Lark screaming, high and raw, a child's scream with nothing borrowed in it at all, and that was somehow the loudest thing in the world.

Then the world broke open.

August screamed too — but his scream had thunder in it. The tuning fork sang a note that wasn't a note, and every drop of water in the culvert leapt off the stones at once, and lightning — actual lightning, indoors, underground, impossible — tore down the passage in a blinding violet flood. The four guards at the arch went down like cut strings. The fifth, the assassin, was hurled backward into the wall hard enough to leave a body-shaped shadow in the slime.

And Tam was on his knees in the shallows, watching his own blood bloom in the black water like ink in milk, thinking, distantly, *huh. So that's what that feels like.*

"Tam!" August caught him before his face met the water. "Tam, stay with me, stay—"

"'M fine," Tam said, and laughed, and the laugh came out wet and wrong. "Told you. Ugly surprise. Works every time."

"Shut up." August's hands were pressing hard against his back, and they were shaking, and Tam realized with distant wonder that the storm-caller, the heir of the Stormbound line, the man who'd thrown lightning through a catacomb, was crying. "Shut up, Tam."

"Is he—?" Sera was there, tearing cloth, packing the wound with hands that knew exactly what they were doing and hated every second of it. "Deep but not mortal. Missed the lung by a finger. He's lucky. We're all lucky. MOVE."

The children were running. Pip had Lark by the wrist, hauling her along, and Lark wasn't resisting, but she was looking back — back at Tam, back at the blood, back at what she'd done — and her face was coming apart, piece by piece, the way faces do when the promise finally shows its teeth.

"I didn't know," she was saying, over and over, to no one, to everyone, to the listening dark that had promised her a home. "I didn't know, I didn't know, I didn't—"

*You knew,* Tam thought, as the darkness came up warm and soft around him like a blanket drawn by kind hands. *That's the whole tragedy, little bird. You knew, and you hoped anyway. Same as me.*

*Same as all of us.*

* * *

He woke to dripping water and Sera's hymn, low and steady, knitting him back together the slow way, the mortal way, thread by thread. He was lying on someone's bedroll in a place of wet stone and green-slicked brick, and the air tasted of the river, and somewhere nearby August sat with his head in his hands, and the tuning fork lay silent beside him like a dead thing.

Around them, pressed close for warmth, the children slept in a pile — all except one. Lark sat apart, at the very edge of the firelight, knees up, face buried, shoulders shaking without sound. Nobody spoke to her. Nobody had spoken to her since the culvert. Pip had moved her bedroll away from Lark's with a small deliberate cruelty that children perfect early, and the twins whispered when she passed, and Ansel just watched her with big flat eyes.

Found family, Tam thought. Found family, bleeding in the dark, with a hole in its youngest member shaped exactly like a mother's voice.

He tried to sit up. Failed. Tried again, managed it, gasping.

"August."

The storm-caller looked over. His eyes were hollowed out, rimmed red.

"She's nine," Tam rasped. Every word cost him; he paid anyway. "Whatever you're deciding right now, whatever the plan is becoming — she's nine years old, and something older than kingdoms lied to her, and it was *good* at it. I know, because it lied to me too, and I'm supposed to be the clever one." He gripped August's sleeve. "Don't you dare leave her behind in some crypt. Don't you dare make her the price. If we're what we say we are — if any of this found-family rubbish is true and not just a song we sing ourselves — then she comes with us. All the way. Even now. *Especially* now."

August was quiet a long moment. Water dripped. Sera's hymn wound through it, patient as roots.

"And if she does it again?" August asked quietly. "If the listening dark comes for her again, and next time we're not lucky?"

Tam lay back against the wet brick, and above them, muffled by fathoms of stone, he could just barely hear it: the cathedral bell, ringing unstruck in the dark, counting down the hours to the coronation of ash.

"Then we hold on tighter," he said. "That's the whole job, innit? That's all any of us ever figured out."

Far off, beneath the bell, something else began to answer.
