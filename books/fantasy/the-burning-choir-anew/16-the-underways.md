## 16. The Underways

The water in the underways was black as spilled ink and smelled of centuries.

August waded through it with his boots held in one hand, his bare feet finding the slick curve of old paving stones beneath the flood. The catacombs beneath the capital had been a city once, or so Wren claimed — a first city, drowned on purpose when the founders raised the walls above it, its chapels and granaries and streets given to the water so that the new city could be built clean. *We bury what we don't want to look at,* Wren had said, and then he had looked away, because he was still counting the faces of the garrison men he'd left behind.

Now the buried city was the only place left to hide. Above them, the bells rang unstruck, hour after hour, and the crown-guard moved through the streets in patterns too smooth to be human, smiling the way everyone smiled now, and somewhere in the cathedral of glass and gold a god wearing a king sat down each evening to practice being loved.

August counted heads as they went. Sera, ahead of him, her broken order's sigil scraped off her pauldron but the scar of it still visible, a pale ghost of an eight-pointed star. Tam with Pip on his shoulders, the boy's small hands fisted in Tam's collar, both of them silent in the way children are silent when they have learned that silence is how you stay alive. Wren at the rear with a stolen lantern shuttered down to a coin of light. Behind them all, Juniper walked holding Marta's hand, and August did not let himself look at them longer than a breath, because looking too long at the children felt like showing a wound to the dark, and the dark, he had learned, took invitations seriously.

They made their camp in what had been a chapel — he could tell by the alcove where an altar should have stood, by the niche in the wall where a saint's statue had been pried out, leaving a hollow shaped like a kneeling man. Water lapped at the threshold. The air was cold enough to see your breath, and every sound they made came back to them doubled off the wet stone, so that even whispering sounded like a congregation.

Sera set the watch. Tam curled around the children like a dog around a litter. And August sat alone at the edge of the flooded nave with the last fragment of the prayer-stone in his palm and his mother's hymn folded against his heart, and did neither thing.

He had not read the hymn.

He told himself it was caution. That in a city where every bell listened, where prayers were answered before they finished, unwrapping a song written by a Stormbound woman who died smuggling it out of this very palace was a risk he couldn't take yet. He told himself he needed darkness and quiet and no one watching, and that none of those things existed yet.

But it wasn't caution. It was fear, plain as bread, and he knew it, and knowing didn't help.

Because songs demanded things. He had known that since he was six years old, since the first time the storm answered him and his mother went white as bone and gripped his shoulders and said, *never ask the sky for anything you're not prepared to receive.* Songs were prayers with the safety taken off. And his mother had died for this one. People did not die for things that asked nothing.

The lantern-light from the camp reached him only as a faint gold bruise on the water. Beyond it, the dark of the underways went on and on, street after drowned street, and August sat with his feet in the black water and finally, alone in a way he hadn't been since Hollowmere, he unfolded the hymn.

* * *

It was written on vellum gone soft as cloth, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood. His mother's hand. He knew it the way you know a voice — she had written him letters from the capital in the years before the exile, patient looping letters full of small domestic news, and he had burned them all when the hunters came for him, along with everything else, because burning was what exiles did.

He had thought, all these years, that he remembered her face. Sitting there in the dark with the vellum trembling in his fingers, he discovered he remembered her handwriting better than her face, and something in him cracked quietly at that, without sound, the way ice cracks under snow.

The hymn began simply. A rising phrase, four notes, like a question asked gently. Then an answering phrase, lower, like someone kind replying. He could hear it in his head without singing a note; the notation was the old storm-notation, the one his mother had taught him against his will, dots and slashes that weren't pitches so much as *pressures* — this much sky, this much weight, this much grief, hold, release.

He read it the way he'd once read weather. And the hymn read him back.

It was a binding-song. He had known that going in. What he had not understood — what no one could have understood without standing where he stood, with storm-blood humming in his ears and a god's hunger two hundred feet above him wearing a crown — was the shape of the binding. He had assumed a cage. Bars, a lock, a wall of thunder. Something you built around a god to hold it, the way you'd build a dike around a river.

It was not a cage.

It was a door. And doors need two sides.

The notation turned, halfway through, into something he recognized with a sick lurch: it became a summons. Not *hold this thing* but *come here.* Not *be contained* but *be welcomed.* The hymn's gentle rising phrase, the kind answering phrase — they weren't a conversation between singer and god. They were an invitation and an acceptance. The song bound Vessel the way a body binds a spirit: by giving the spirit somewhere warm to live.

A vessel. It required a vessel. A willing one — the notation was explicit about that, wound through with warnings in his mother's tightest hand, marginal notes crammed between the staves: *it must consent, it must consent freely, unwilling flesh rejects it.* The binding only held if the vessel opened themselves and said yes. Said it gladly. Said it the way you say yes to a guest you love.

His bloodline had made the original binding, the archives at the ruined shrine had said. He had thought that meant his ancestors had been jailers. Engineers of some great working, wardens with keys.

No. They had been hosts. Generation after generation, some son or daughter of the Stormbound line had sung this hymn and opened their own chest and let the god of obedience move in, and lived with it, and grown old with it, and died with it, passing the guest along like a candle flame passed hand to hand down a long dark corridor of years. That was the inheritance his mother died protecting. Not a weapon. A mouth. An open door with a family name carved over the lintel.

And then, somewhere in the last century — he could see exactly where, a gap in the marginalia, a generation of notes that thinned and stopped — the line had failed. Refused, perhaps. Fled, certainly. His own exile suddenly looked different: not a punishment, not a political accident, but a door slammed shut by people who had finally done the arithmetic. And a god left alone in the dark beneath the mountain, hungry, patient, waiting a hundred years for anyone at all to answer.

Until it found a king. Until it found Aldric, who must have prayed — who must have prayed *so sincerely*, some night of grief or doubt, some night any honest king would have — and something answered, and Aldric, alone and honored and desperate, said yes.

*Oh,* August thought, with a clarity so cold it burned. *Oh, you poor man. You said yes.*

The hymn was a trap with a kind face. That was the whole of it. It sang like mercy — *rest, be held, be carried* — and what it offered the god was a home, and what it asked of the singer was everything, forever, and it dressed the asking up in four gentle rising notes until you were inside it before you understood you'd been invited anywhere at all.

There was no clean way out. That was the second thing he understood, sitting in the black water while the drowned city breathed around him. He turned the pages twice more, looking for the trick, the third verse, the escape clause his mother must surely have hidden somewhere — and found instead her final marginal note, written smaller than the rest, the letters cramped as if her hand had been shaking:

*I looked for another way for eleven years. There isn't one. Forgive me. I ran out of time to find it, so I am leaving you the finding.*

She had known. She had carried this song across half a kingdom with hunters at her back, and she had known it was a door and not a cage, and she had smuggled it out anyway, because the alternative was the god loose in the world with no vessel at all — unbounded, unshaped, free to wear whoever it pleased, whole cities of yes. A trap was still better than an open pit. A kind face was still better than none.

August closed his eyes. The water moved against his ankles, patient as time.

He thought about Hollowmere, where magic could not follow, where he had spent seven years mending roofs and teaching himself to want small things. He had believed exile was a sentence. Now he saw it had been a reprieve — a cell with the door left unlocked, waiting for the day the family debt came due. Every quiet year had been borrowed. Every mended roof, every meal eaten in silence, every morning he had woken and *not remembered* the storms — all of it purchased on credit against this moment, here, in the dark, with the bill finally presented.

And underneath the cold clarity, quieter, almost unbearable: Sera's voice from three nights ago, low and fierce in a waystation loft. *You have to take the binding. You're the only one who can.* And his own voice, cracking: *I won't leave them.* And hers, terrible with honesty: *Then we'll lose everything anyway, just slower.*

He had thought she was asking him to die. She wasn't. She was asking him to do worse than die. She was asking him to say yes — gladly, the way the hymn required — to spend eternity as a house for a thing that ate obedience, to feel it moving through him the way it moved through Aldric, smiling with his smile, and to know that the children he loved would grow old and die outside him while he endured, door-shaped, in the dark beneath the mountain.

Duty had a face now. It was his mother's handwriting. It was four gentle notes. It was a yes he could already feel forming in his chest like a stone forming in a kidney, slow and inevitable and agonizing.

*Not yet,* he told it. *Not tonight.*

But the stone did not dissolve. It only waited, the way debts wait.

* * *

He heard the footsteps before he saw the lantern — small, quick, trying to be quiet the way children are quiet, which is to say loudly.

Tam came around the chapel's broken wall with a blanket over his shoulders and his eyes red-rimmed, and August had a heartbeat to wonder if the boy had followed him, or if the boy had simply known, the way Tam knew things, hearing prayers like other people heard rain.

"You've been gone two hours," Tam said. "Sera's pretending she isn't worried, which means she is."

"I'm fine."

"You're sitting in freezing water in the dark reading a dead woman's letter." Tam crouched at the edge of the flood, careful of the hem of his blanket. "That's not fine. That's the opposite of fine."

August almost laughed. It came out as breath. "Go back to sleep, Tam."

"Can't." The boy picked at the blanket's fringe. "Pip had a nightmare. Kept saying the bells were ringing inside his teeth." He was quiet a moment. "Everyone's having them. The kids. Bad dreams where something asks them questions." His jaw tightened. "Something's listening down here too, isn't it. It doesn't stop at the waterline."

August looked at him — this pickpocket, this prayer-hearer, this boy who had stitched a family out of orphans and deserters and a disgraced priestess and an exile, and who guarded it like a dragon guards a hoard it can't believe it owns. Two days ago August had torn a ritual out of Tam's hands and nearly torn out the boy's trust with it. The wound was still there. But Tam had come anyway, into the dark, toward the man who frightened him, because Pip was dreaming of bells.

"There's something I need to tell you," August said. "All of you. Tomorrow. But you first, because you'll understand it fastest."

Tam waited.

"The song my mother wrote," August said. "It binds the god. But it binds it *in* someone. Someone has to carry it. Willingly. Forever." He watched the boy's face do the arithmetic — quick, sharp, the same arithmetic Sera had done, the same arithmetic August himself had spent two hours refusing. "That someone is supposed to be me."

Tam was silent for a long moment. The water lapped. Somewhere far off in the drowned streets, a pipe knocked — or knocked itself — three times, softly, like a knock at a door.

"That's why you wouldn't sing it," Tam said at last. "In the catacombs. When Sera found it. You knew."

"I suspected."

"And now?"

"Now I know."

Tam hugged the blanket tighter. When he spoke again his voice had gone young, younger than August ever let himself hear it. "Is it going to take you? Like the king?"

"It only takes what says yes."

"Then don't say yes." It came out fierce, immediate, a child's solution to an impossible sum. Then Tam heard himself, and his face closed, because Tam was nobody's fool; he had survived the streets by understanding that wanting a thing did not make it true. He looked down at the black water. "There has to be another way," he said, flatly, without belief. "People always say there isn't, and then someone finds one. That's how stories work."

"Stories are written afterward," August said gently. "We're still in the middle."

Another silence. Then Tam lifted his chin, and the look in his eyes was the one he wore when he decided to steal something — bright, reckless, certain. "Then we find the middle of a different story. Because I'm not letting you become a *door*, August. I've slept in doorways. They're cold, and everyone kicks you, and nothing good comes in." He stood, blanket and all. "Come back to the fire. Sera's making the kids play the quiet game to keep them calm, and Marta cheats, and it's the best thing happening in this entire city right now. Whatever you decide — decide it tomorrow. Tonight you're just August, and you're coming to sit with your family."

August looked at the hymn in his hands. Four gentle notes. A door with his name over the lintel. Somewhere above, muffled by two hundred feet of stone and a drowned city, a bell rang the hour, unstruck, sweet as rot.

He folded the vellum along its old creases, carefully, the way you fold a letter you intend to answer, and tucked it against his heart, and stood, and let Tam pull him up out of the water.

"One more hour," he said. "Then I'm telling them everything."

"Everything?" Tam's eyes narrowed shrewdly. "Including the part where Sera's plan is 'August becomes a haunted house'?"

"Including that."

"Good." Tam started back toward the campfire-glow, then paused, glancing sideways. "For what it's worth — I prayed for you. Last night. Just a little one. Nothing answered." He shrugged, awkward. "First time in weeks a prayer of mine got ignored. Felt almost like privacy."

August laughed — really laughed, short and cracked and real — and followed the boy back toward the light, the hymn heavy against his chest, the cold clarity sitting in him like a stone at the bottom of a well.

Tomorrow he would tell them. Tomorrow they would argue, and Sera would be right, and he would refuse her, and the argument would go nowhere, because there was nowhere for it to go. He knew all of it already, the way you know the shape of a storm by the taste of the air.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was a fire, and Marta cheating at the quiet game, and one hour — maybe the last one he would ever get — of being only August.

Behind them, deep in the drowned dark, something knocked three times again. Patient. Polite.

Waiting to be let in.
