## 17. What Duty Demands

The underways had a heartbeat.

Sera had learned it in the first hours after they fled below — a slow, wet pulse of water moving through the flooded galleries somewhere beneath them, so deep and regular that at first she'd mistaken it for her own blood in her ears. Now, three days into their hiding, she knew better. The city above had its own pulse too: the bells, ringing unstruck every dawn and dusk, and the faint tremor of ten thousand people walking in step toward whatever Vessel was building. But down here there was only the water, and the dark, and the sound of August Kavanagh refusing to sleep.

She found him where she always found him — the dry alcove off the third gallery, where an old merchant's shrine had crumbled into a niche of weeping stone. He sat with his back against the wall, the bone-white tuning fork across his knees like something he was guarding rather than holding, and his mother's hymn folded into his breast pocket, unread. He hadn't read it. She'd watched him take it out twice and put it away twice, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

"You're not sleeping," she said.

"Neither are you."

"I'm on watch." She lowered herself onto the cold flagstones beside him, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Almost. "You're just sitting in the dark rehearsing arguments you haven't had yet."

His mouth twitched. It might have been a smile. In the lamplight — Wren's last stub of tallow candle, hoarded like treasure — his face looked carved out of the same pale stone as the shrine, all hollows and shadow. Three days underground had worn him thin. Three days since Tam took the blade meant for him. Three days since they'd scattered into the city's bowels with the crown-guard howling through the streets above like a second choir.

"How is he?" August asked.

"Sleeping. Finally. The fever broke this morning." She kept her voice level, clinical, the way she'd been trained to deliver hard news in the field. "Mira hasn't left his side. She's forgiven him, by the way. Or she's decided to pretend she has, which for a nine-year-old amounts to the same thing."

"And the one who—"

"The child who betrayed us is sitting with Wren, learning to clean a crossbow. Wren says keeping hands busy keeps hearts from drowning." Sera paused. "He's right more often than I'd like."

August nodded slowly. His thumb moved over the tuning fork's smooth stem, back and forth, a gesture so unconscious and so tender that it made her chest ache. That fork had been his mother's. It had crossed a kingdom hidden in a smuggler's false bottom while soldiers burned the house behind it. And now it sat in the lap of the last Stormbound, humming faintly whenever thunder passed far overhead — which it did even here, even through forty feet of stone, as if the sky were calling down into the dark looking for him.

"Sera." He said her name carefully, the way men say things when they already know what's coming. "You've got your sermon face on."

"I don't have a sermon face."

"You have three faces. The watch face, the wound-tending face, and the one you wear when you've decided what everyone else should do and are waiting for the right moment to say it." He turned to look at her then, and his eyes in the candlelight were the grey-green of deep water before a storm. "This is the third one."

She should have laughed. Instead she heard herself say, quietly: "Then let me say it."

* * *

She had composed the argument in the dark hours, walking the gallery rounds while the others slept, turning it over like a blade she was learning to hold without cutting herself. She had told herself it was strategy. Necessity. The cold arithmetic of war, which was the only arithmetic her order had ever taught her.

But now, facing him, the argument came out less like strategy and more like prayer.

"Vessel announced the consecration," she said. "Wren confirmed it this morning — heralds in every square. Nine days. They're going to bind the whole city's faith into the god, permanently. Every soul in the capital, smoothed flat, smiling, obedient. Forever." She drew breath. "And the only thing that can stop it is the storm-song. Your mother's binding. Which requires—"

"Don't."

"—a vessel," she finished, because she had never once in her life stopped halfway through a true sentence, not when her order cast her out for finishing them. "A willing vessel, August. You sang the hymn. You know what it asks. Someone has to stand in the place the god leaves empty, or the unbinding tears a hole in the world that Vessel will pour itself right back into. And there is exactly one person among us whose blood was made for that hole. Whose grandmother helped cut it. Whose mother died carrying the song that fits it."

"I know what the song requires." His voice was very quiet. Very controlled. She recognized the control; she wore the same kind herself, most days. It was the sound of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "I've known since the catacomb. You don't need to preach it to me like I'm a novice who missed the lesson."

"Then why won't you say it?"

"Because saying it makes it real, and because—" He stopped. His hand closed around the tuning fork until the knuckles went white. "Because I watched Tam bleed out on the bell tower stairs, Sera. Because Mira prayed to me — *to me*, like I was one of the gods — to make her brother stop hurting, and I couldn't, because my magic is the only thing in this world that answers prayers by killing things. Because I have spent eleven years being nobody, mending roofs in a valley where magic can't follow, and it was the first time in my life I was ever safe from myself." The words were coming faster now, cracking at the edges. "And you want me to walk up those cathedral steps and give all of it away. Not just the magic. Everything. The binding doesn't release a vessel, Sera. It *keeps* one. You know that. You read the archives same as I did. Whoever stands in that hole stands there until something stronger comes along to dig them out, and nothing stronger is coming, because we killed the last hope of that when we learned what Vessel really is."

"Yes," she said. "I know."

"Then how can you ask me?"

"Because someone has to!" It came out louder than she intended, echoing off the wet stone, and somewhere in the dark a rat skittered away from the sound of human grief. She lowered her voice, but the fire underneath stayed. "Nine days, August. Nine days and then it isn't Aldric in chains anymore — it's everyone. Every child in the choir. Tam. Mira. The baby born next spring who'll never know there was anything to fight. You keep talking about what the binding costs *you*. I'm asking you to count what it costs if you don't."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Duty never is. That's the first thing they teach you, before they teach you everything else wrong."

* * *

The silence afterward was long enough that the water-pulse filled it, beat after patient beat. August set the tuning fork down between them on the flagstone, deliberately, like a man laying down a weapon before a duel.

"Tell me about your heresy," he said.

Sera blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. You want to talk about duty? Then let's talk about what duty did to you. You told me once — the night after the wells, remember, when we were drunk on that awful plum wine — that the gods went silent years before anyone admitted it. That you said it out loud, at convocation, in front of the High Cantor." His eyes didn't leave hers. "So tell me the rest. Tell me what it cost. Because I think you've been carrying it this whole road and handing me pieces of it whenever you needed leverage, and I would rather have all of it now, in the dark, than keep pretending we're having a strategic conversation."

Her first instinct was anger — hot and familiar, the old armor. Her second instinct was to walk away, and she actually got one knee under herself before pride caught up with her and made her sit back down, because walking away would be a kind of lie, and she had sworn, alone, in the ashes of her old life, that she would never lie again. Not about the gods. Not about anything.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. You want it plainly?"

"Plainly."

"I was twenty-three. I'd spent six years listening at shrines — real listening, the discipline they train battle-priestesses in, putting your soul quiet enough to hear an answer if one comes. And the answers stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Like doors closing one by one across the whole country. And everyone around me kept praying and getting — something. Comfort. Warmth. Little coincidences they called grace. And I stood up at convocation and said: whatever is answering us now, it is not the gods we were promised. The gods are gone or sleeping or dead, and we are being consoled by something else, and we should find out what before it tells us what to do." She laughed, short and bitter. "They branded me within the hour. Heretic. Traitor to the covenant. Eleven years I carried that brand, August. Eleven years knowing I was right and being punished for it, and do you know what the worst part was?"

"What."

"Not the brand. The doubt. Every night, some small voice whispering: maybe you're wrong, maybe you were just deaf, maybe faith means believing the warmth is grace even when it feels like teeth. I doubted myself for eleven years because an institution told me to, and I *let* it, because obedience felt safer than truth." She turned to face him fully. "And now I know I was right. Vessel was answering prayers the whole time. Every warm little miracle for thirty years was that thing fattening itself on our consent. And I will be damned — properly damned, the old way — if I sit quietly one more time while the institution of *duty* tells someone I love to lie down inside a monster because it's the honorable thing to do."

The word was out before she could catch it.

*I love.*

August went very still. The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, impossibly far away, thunder rolled — or perhaps it was only the bells, practicing for the consecration.

"You love me," he said. Not a question. Not quite.

"You knew that," she said, and her voice shook and she let it. "Don't make me regret saying it plainly. You wanted plain."

"I did." He dragged a hand down his face. When it came away, he looked younger and more exhausted than she had ever seen him, all the storm drained out of him and only the man left. "Gods, Sera. Of course I knew. I've known since the river town, when you stood between me and that priest like my name was worth defending. I've known since the tower, when you held Tam's wound closed with your bare hands and prayed — *prayed*, you, the heretic — for the first time in eleven years, because you'd rather be wrong about the gods than lose him." He laughed, raggedly. "I love you too. I have for months. And I think that's exactly why you're wrong."

"August—"

"No. Let me be plain back." He leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the fork between them, throwing its long white shadow up the wall like a splinter of lightning frozen mid-fall. "My whole life, everyone who claimed to love me also claimed to know what I owed. My mother died for the song and never asked me to carry it — but the song asked anyway. The valley elders exiled me gently, lovingly, *for my own good*. The old priest at the wells called me heir of the Stormbound line like he was handing me a crown instead of a noose. Everyone who ever loved me dressed duty up as devotion and waited for me to put it on." His voice cracked and steadied. "I won't let you do it too. I won't. Not you."

"It's not the same."

"It's exactly the same. That's what terrifies you. You can hear it sounding like them, and you can't stop."

* * *

They fought for another hour. She would remember that afterward — not the sharp parts, though there were sharp parts, wounds opened and salted: her calling him a coward and instantly wishing the word dead in her mouth; him telling her that her order had broken her so thoroughly that she could only love people by sending them to die well. Cruel things, true-adjacent things, the kind of things the underways seemed built to amplify, echoing off the drowned stone until both of them flinched at their own voices.

But mostly what she remembered, later, was the ending. How it didn't end in victory for either of them.

It ended because Tam came.

The boy appeared at the mouth of the alcove without a sound — he could always move silently when he chose, a gift from a childhood spent surviving — and stood there wrapped in a borrowed blanket, fever-flushed, looking at the two of them sitting in the wreckage of their argument like survivors of a shipwreck.

"You're fighting about the binding," Tam said. Not a question either. He'd heard prayers his whole life; he probably heard the ones they weren't saying. "Aunt Sera wants August to do it. August won't."

"We're not—" Sera began.

"It's okay." Tam shuffled closer and sat down against August's side, uninvited, the way children do when they have decided the argument is over whether the adults admit it or not. "I prayed for strength once. To protect the kids. Something answered." He looked up at August, and his eyes were old and young at once. "You broke it. You saved me. And I hated you for like two days, and then I figured out that what you were really saying was: *you don't have to pay for love.*" He shrugged, small and enormous. "So. Same to you."

Then he simply leaned his head against August's shoulder and fell asleep, fever and all, trusting them with the total unconsciousness of the young.

Neither of them spoke for a long time. The candle burned lower. The water pulsed. Above, faint as memory, the bells rang the dusk — unstruck, sweet, obscene.

"Exhausted honesty," Sera said finally, very quietly. "That's what this is. Not victory. I came down here to win an argument and I've lost it and I'm glad, and I hate that I'm glad."

"I don't want to die in that hole," August said. "I want the roof-mending years. I want Mira's terrible singing. I want to grow old watching you argue with bishops." He picked up the tuning fork and pressed it into her hands, closing her fingers around it — the inheritance, the burden, the key. "But I'll tell you what I told the valley elders, the day they put me out. If it comes down to the family or the duty, I choose the family. Every time. Even if the family is nine days from being erased. *Especially* then. Because a duty that asks you to feed the people you love to the dark isn't duty, Sera. It's just the monster, wearing a nicer voice."

She held the fork. It hummed faintly against her palms, eager, patient, and she understood suddenly that it had been waiting for one of them to break — that the song itself was the trap's kindest face, and it would wait out any argument, any vow, any love.

"So we find another way," she said. "In nine days. With no plan, no army, and one deserter's garrison contacts."

"Wren's already working on the garrison," August said. A ghost of his old crooked smile surfaced. "And we do have one asset nobody's accounted for."

"Which is?"

"A child who betrayed us once because she was homesick." He nodded toward the dark gallery where Wren sat cleaning crossbows beside a penitent twelve-year-old. "She knows the cathedral better than any of us. She's been begging Wren for a way to make it right. And Vessel's guards will never imagine we'd trust her again."

Sera looked at him — at this stubborn, ordinary, extraordinary man who refused every crown offered to him, including martyrdom — and felt the last knot of the argument dissolve into something quieter and harder to name. Not agreement. Not surrender. Resolve, shared, like a blanket in the cold.

"All right," she said. "Another way. But August — if it fails, if it comes down to that hole in the world with no one else to fill it—"

"Then we'll fail honestly," he said. "Together. That's the only vow I can make you, and it's the only one worth making."

Above them, the last echo of the dusk bells faded into stone. And far off, at the edge of hearing, thunder gathered over the capital — real thunder, uninvited, unanswered — as if the sky itself had begun, at last, to take sides.
