## 19. Unbinding

The bells began before dawn.

August felt them in his teeth first—a low, sweet vibration rolling through the flagstones of the underways, through the flooded dark where they had hidden for two days with wet boots and fraying nerves. The sound was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard, a chord like sunrise given voice, and every note of it was a lie.

"They've started," Wren said. He stood at the mouth of the drainage tunnel, still in his garrison grays, his face gray to match. "Consecration at first light. The whole city's been summoned. They're singing in the streets."

"Of course they are," Sera said. She was checking the straps of her old battle-harness for the third time, though nothing needed checking. Her hands wanted work. "Vessel doesn't want worshipers. It wants a choir."

August sat apart from them on a dry ledge of stone, the bone-white tuning fork cradled in both palms like something newborn or dying. His mother's hymn lay folded inside him now—not on paper anymore, not since the catacombs. He had read it. He had sung it once, alone, in the black water-sound of the deep places, and it had gone into him the way rain goes into cracked earth, filling every fissure he had spent nine years pretending wasn't there.

He knew what it demanded. A willing vessel. A cage with a kind face.

He had told no one that he intended to pay.

* * *

They came up out of the underways through the crypt of Saint Ellery-the-Drowned, into air so thick with incense it tasted sweet and rotten at once, like fruit left too long in the sun. The cathedral square was full—gods, it was full. Tens of thousands of them, packed shoulder to shoulder, every face turned toward the great western doors, every face wearing the same soft smile. The lullaby-smile. August saw a woman holding an infant, and the infant was smiling too, and something in his chest clenched like a fist.

The bells overhead hung silent in their tower now, twelve of them, each tall as a house, bronze gone green-black with age. Unstruck. Waiting. He could feel them the way he'd once felt storms on the horizon behind Hollowmere's mountains: a pressure, a gathering, a held breath.

"The rite moves through three stations," Sera murmured as they joined the flow of pilgrims moving toward the square's edge. She wore no vestments; her order would have burned her for the presumption. Just a traveler's cloak, and beneath it the iron brand of her heresy, hidden against her heart. "The font. The altar. The crown. At each one, Vessel takes something. At the font, the city's tears. At the altar, its doubts." Her jaw tightened. "At the crown, its faith. Permanently. After today there will be no prayer in this kingdom that Vessel does not answer itself. Every god-touched child born after will be born into its choir."

"And us?" Tam asked. The pickpocket looked smaller than usual in borrowed pilgrim's robes, his sharp face shadowed by a hood. But his eyes were steady, and his hand rested briefly on August's arm as they walked—a touch that said what his voice couldn't say in a crowd: *I know what you're planning. Don't.*

"We sing," August said.

* * *

King Aldric appeared at the cathedral doors as the sun cleared the eastern rooftops, and the crowd's roar was not a roar at all but a single harmonized breath, ten thousand voices making one sound, and the wrongness of it raised the hair on August's arms.

Aldric wore white and gold. He moved down the steps slowly, graciously, laying his hand on the heads of children brought forward for blessing, and each blessed child's smile widened until August had to look away. From this distance the king might have been any beloved monarch. It was only when the procession passed within thirty feet of their place among the pilgrims that August saw his eyes.

They were begging.

They had begged in the mirror room, too, when August had reached him through the last shard of the prayer-stone—*I remember my name,* Aldric had whispered from the sealed vault of his own skull, *but I cannot remember my son's face, please, please, whoever you are, end it even if you end me*—and they begged now, those same eyes riding above that serene smiling mouth like a prisoner pressed to the window of a passing coach.

*I'm coming,* August thought at him, uselessly, desperately. *Hold on. Hold the door.*

As if the king heard—as if some drowned remnant of the man still had hands to press against the glass—Aldric stumbled. One step. A hitch in the perfect procession, gone almost instantly, smoothed over by the god's grace like a ripple vanishing from still water. But the bells above the square shivered without being struck, and for half a heartbeat every smiling face in the crowd flickered, like candleflames in a draft.

Vessel had noticed the stumble. Vessel was embarrassed.

*Good,* August thought, and felt the storm-song stir in his chest like a waking animal. *Be embarrassed. Be afraid. I'm going to give you something to be afraid of.*

* * *

The font station passed. The altar station passed. Each time, the bells hummed a little louder, and the crowd's unified breathing deepened, and August felt the pressure against his own mind—the lullaby trying to smooth away his doubt, his grief, his anger, everything sharp in him that made him *him*. He fought it with memory. Mending roofs in Hollowmere. Sera's terrible campfire coffee. Tam teaching the little ones to pick pockets with walnuts. The children asleep in a pile like puppies in the back of a waystation wagon. He built a wall out of small things, because small things were the only things the god couldn't unmake by loving them too much.

Then the crown station came, and there were no more stations after it, and it was time.

Wren moved first. That had always been the plan—the deserter turning deserter one final time, walking out of the crowd in his garrison grays with his hand raised in the officer's signal for *halt*, and the garrison troops ringing the square, men and women he'd drunk with and drilled with, hesitating, because half of them had been waiting months for someone else to raise that hand first. The line wavered. Somewhere near the west colonnade a sergeant threw down her spear, and then another soldier, and the wavering became a crack in the god's perfect morning.

And in the confusion, Sera struck the tuning fork against the altar rail.

The note it gave was thin and high and utterly clear, a thread of silver sound cutting through the choir-drone, and August felt it find the song inside him and pull, the way a fisherman's hook finds flesh. His mother's hymn rose in his throat before he decided to let it rise. The first words were in no language he knew, older than the valley, older than the kingdom, and they tasted of rain on hot stone.

The crowd did not hear it. The crowd was still smiling. But the bells heard it.

All twelve of them rang at once—unstruck, unmoved, screaming bronze—and the sound was no longer beautiful. It was the sound of a thing being torn, and everyone in the square clapped their hands over their ears, and smiles broke everywhere like ice on a thawing river, and ten thousand people remembered all at once how to be afraid.

Aldric stopped at the foot of the cathedral steps. The god turned the king's body to face August across the square, and the smile finally, finally fell away, and what was underneath was worse: vast, patient, hungry, wearing a dead man's grace like a stolen coat.

"*Stormbound,*" it said, and the voice came from the bells and the stones and the mouths of the nearest hundred pilgrims all at once. "*Little heir of the binding. You bring a fork of bone and your mother's arithmetic. We remember your grandmother's grandmother. We ate her patience. We will eat yours.*"

"You never bound us," August sang, and the hymn climbed, and thunder gathered in a cloudless sky. "You *waited us out.*"

Lightning came down.

It did not come from the clouds—it came from *him*, out through his arms, his open mouth, his weeping eyes, nine years of dammed storm breaking all at once, and it struck the cathedral's central bell and split it from crown to lip with a crack like the world's spine breaking. Bronze shards rained into the square. The second bell rang wild and discordant. The third exploded inward.

And Vessel screamed.

It screamed through Aldric's throat, and the sound peeled paint from doors three streets away, and the king's body arched backward on the steps as if hauled by hooks, and August sang harder, walked forward through the crowd's scattering panic, pouring the hymn into the fork, the fork into the air, lightning crawling over the flagstones in blue rivers. He could feel the god now—not as a presence in the bells or the king but as a shape in the world's fabric, an ancient knot of obedience, and the storm-song was finding the knot's loose end and pulling.

"*You cannot,*" Vessel said, and for the first time its voice was only one voice, only Aldric's voice, ragged and cracking. "*We are bound to him. Tear us loose and the tearing takes him. Tear us loose and the vessel must be refilled—this is the law your mothers wrote, Stormbound, the law you carry in your blood—SOMEONE MUST HOLD US—*"

"I know," August sang.

He had known since the catacombs. The hymn had told him plainly, in its kind-faced way: *a willing vessel, a cage with a door that locks from inside.* He had known, and he had kissed Tam's forehead that morning while the boy pretended to sleep, and memorized Sera's scowl, and counted the children twice, and come anyway.

He opened his mouth to offer himself—

—and the god moved faster than grief.

* * *

Later, August would understand that Vessel had known all along what he intended. Had let him sing. Had let him spend himself emptying the king, because an emptying leaves a hollow, and a hollow must be filled, and the god had never intended to go quietly into any cage August built. It had simply waited for the moment of maximum expenditure—the moment when the storm-song was spent, when the lightning lay crackling and dying on the flagstones, when August stood swaying and hollowed and *open*—

—and it leapt.

Not into him. Past him.

Toward the east colonnade, where Wren's soldiers had shepherded the smallest ones out of the crush, where the Choir of Little Saints huddled in their borrowed pilgrim robes—Maren with her crooked braid, the twins, solemn little Oskar who prayed for beetles—and toward Tam, who had never left the front, who had put himself between the god and the children the way he put himself between a blade and August, because that was the whole of Tam's heart and Vessel could taste hearts the way August tasted rain.

"*Small vessels hold longest,*" the god said through a hundred throats, and the hunger in it was bottomless. "*Children believe so easily. Children stay filled.*"

"No—" August's voice tore. He reached for the storm and found dregs, fumes, the last spit of a burnt-out fire. He had poured out everything. Everything, all at once, exactly as the plan demanded, and the plan had been a trap with a kind face after all, and the trap had not been set by his mother's hymn but by a god older than his mothers, patient as stone, and it was reaching for the children with hands made of the king's body and the bells' dying ring and the crowd's recovered terror—

Tam stepped in front of Maren and spread his arms.

"Do you hear prayers?" Tam shouted at it, chin up, thief's grin gone fierce. "Then hear mine: *not them. Not ever them.*"

And the god laughed, and leaned down—

—and August did the only thing left in his emptied hands.

He ran. Not away. Into. Between the god's descending attention and the children, arms wide the way Tam's were wide, and he did the thing the hymn had shown him in the catacombs, the thing he had sworn he would do, the thing he had hidden from every one of them because love makes everyone vulnerable and he had wanted one day more—

He began to sing the binding verse.

His voice was ash. His magic was spent. But the verse didn't need power—it needed *willingness*, a vessel opening its door from the inside, and August Kavanagh, exiled storm-caller, heir of the Stormbound, opened every door he had and stood in the threshold of himself and called the god home.

"*Come, then,*" he sang, blood on his lips, the sky weeping real rain at last onto the broken bells. "*Come into me. I am the end of your patience. I am the last of the line that caged you, and I am* willing—"

Vessel turned from the children.

It turned from the children, and looked at him, and began to pour itself into the space he had made, cold and vast and obedient to the oldest law of all, and August braced himself to hold the door shut behind it forever—

Across the square, Sera Vane was running toward him, screaming his name, and in her hand the bone-white fork was glowing like a falling star.
