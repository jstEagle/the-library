## 21. After the Rain

The rain came on the third morning after the unbinding, and it was only rain.

August stood in the palace courtyard with his face turned up to it, letting the water run down his cheeks and off his chin, and he wept without shame because no one could tell the difference between the rain and the grief, least of all him. The drops struck the flagstones with small ordinary sounds. No thunder followed them. No pressure gathered behind his sternum, no itch of charge crawling along his forearms, no whisper in the blood saying *more, more, you could have more.* The sky emptied itself over Vellharrow and asked nothing of him at all.

He had forgotten what that was like. He had forgotten that weather could be weather.

Behind him, the cathedral bells hung silent in their tower, three of them cracked clean through, the rest merely mute. Workmen had been at them since dawn, hauling rope and timber, and their voices carried across the courtyard in fragments — *mind the beam, mind the beam* — the most beautiful profanity August had ever heard. Ordinary men doing ordinary work under an ordinary sky. He could have stood there until dark listening to it.

"You'll catch your death," Sera said from the doorway, "and then I'll have to explain to six children why their storm-caller drowned standing up."

"I'm not a storm-caller anymore."

"No," she said, coming to stand beside him, hood back, letting the rain hit her too. Grey had come into her cropped hair where the ash of the consecration hadn't washed out, or perhaps it had always been there and he'd never looked closely enough. "You're not." She said it without pity, which was the only way he could have borne hearing it. "How does it feel?"

He considered the question honestly, the way she deserved. He turned his hands over. The old calluses from mending roofs in Hollowmere were still there; the faint silver scarring along his knuckles, where the lightning had lived just under the skin for thirty years, had faded to almost nothing.

"Light," he said at last. "Like something was sitting on my chest my whole life and I only noticed it when it got up." A breath. "And hollow. Like the same thing took part of the wall with it when it left."

Sera nodded slowly. "That's what heresy felt like," she said. "When they struck me from the rolls. For a year I kept reaching for the order the way you'd reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. Then one morning I reached and forgot to miss it." She glanced sideways at him. "It comes. The not-missing."

The doors opened behind them and Wren Ashfall came out at a half-run, still wearing his courier's coat even though no one had asked him to be a courier in days, because Wren wore duty the way other men wore skin. "They're ready," he said. "His Majesty's asking for you. Both of you. He's—" Wren hesitated, and August saw the deserter's old fear flicker through him, the trained instinct to soften bad news before delivering it. "He's having a good morning. But he wanted you there before the council arrives."

*Before the council arrives.* Because Aldric's mornings were his own and his afternoons belonged increasingly to ministers and petitioners and the slow machinery of a kingdom learning to trust its king again. Because whatever the man remembered by dawn might be worn smooth by noon.

August wiped his face with both hands, uselessly, in the rain. "Then let's not keep him."

* * *

Aldric received them in a small room rather than a grand one — a solar with tall windows looking east over the city, furnished plainly, almost sparsely, as though the king had ordered everything ornate removed and no one yet dared ask him why. August understood why. Every gilded thing in this palace had spent months smiling at him while something else wore his voice. Aldric wanted, for now, to sit in rooms that had never flattered him.

The king stood at the window when they entered, and August's chest tightened the way it did every time, because Aldric moved like a man relearning his own body. There was a pause before each gesture, a small deliberation, as if he consulted himself before lifting a hand. His hair had gone white at the temples in a matter of weeks. His eyes were his own now — tired, wet-bright, unbearably awake.

"August Kavanagh," Aldric said, turning. His voice caught on the name, and he smiled ruefully. "I've been practicing saying it. In the mirror. It sounds strange to say aloud the name of the man who saved your life. Stranger still when you can't remember whether you ever thanked him properly, because some of the days are—" He stopped. Started again. "Some of the days are missing. Whole seasons of them. I remember my son's christening and I remember waking on the floor of this room with bells falling out of the sky, and between those two things there is a fog with a few islands in it. You are on several of the islands." He gestured to chairs. "Please. Sit. I'm told kings who make people stand look like tyrants, and I've had quite enough of being mistaken for one."

They sat. Sera sat like a soldier, spine straight, hands open on her knees. August sat like a man who didn't know what his hands were for anymore, which was the truth of it.

Aldric remained standing. He paced once to the window and back, and August realized the king was nervous — genuinely nervous, a man about to do something difficult, and doing it anyway.

"The Stormbound line was exiled seventy years ago," Aldric said. "My grandfather signed the decree. My father upheld it. I inherited it the way I inherited everything — without reading it closely enough." He clasped his hands behind his back, a courtier's posture, then seemed to catch himself and let them fall loose. "The decree named your family oath-breakers and storm-witches. It stripped your lands, your name's honor, your right of return. It was wrong when it was written. I know now — better than any king has ever known anything — how much of what a crown calls justice is simply fear wearing robes." His jaw tightened. "Vessel taught me that. It wore my crown for two years and every law it loved was a law we already had. It didn't need to change us. We were already shaped for it."

No one spoke. Rain traced long fingers down the window glass.

"So." Aldric drew a folded document from the table beside him — heavy paper, sealed with wax the color of old wine. "By right and by remedy: the pardon of the Stormbound line, full and unconditional. Restoration of title. Lands in the eastern hills — good land, orchard country, the deed is drawn. And a seat at court, should you want it, because a kingdom that nearly died of obedience could stand to keep one man who has practice telling gods no."

He held it out.

August did not take it.

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and, far below, the workmen shouting at the bell tower. Aldric waited with a patience that cost him something — August could see it costing him, see the small tremor in the outstretched hand that the king willed still.

"I'm sorry," August said finally. "Not for refusing. For making you hold that out while I decide how to say it."

"Say it however you need to," Aldric said quietly. "I've spent two years unable to say anything in my own words. I will wait for yours."

August looked at the seal. His mother had died carrying a hymn out of a vault not so very different from the ones beneath this room, smuggling a song through checkpoints because someone had to, because duty was a fire that burned whoever held it. He thought of Hollowmere: the valley where magic couldn't follow, the roofs he'd mended, the elders who had told him to burn a dying man's prayer-stone and called it wisdom. He thought of the way the valley had felt when he crossed back out of it — not like leaving home, but like leaving a room he'd been locked in so long he'd started calling the lock a home.

"Hollowmere sent a letter last week," he said. "Did you know? They pardoned me too. Their own little decree, read out at the well. *August Kavanagh is welcome to return.*" He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Seventy years of exile and everyone's suddenly sorry. Everyone's holding out documents."

"It's easier to be sorry afterward," Sera said softly. "Sorry costs nothing once the danger's past."

"That's not fair to all of them," August said. "Some of them meant it. Marta meant it. She cried when she read it." He rubbed his eyes. "But here's the thing I learned on the road, Your Majesty. Home isn't a place that pardons you. I spent nine years in that valley telling myself I was safe, and I wasn't safe, I was *stored*. Like something packed away against need. And the moment I was needed, the valley opened its gates and pushed me out and called it fate." He looked up at Aldric. "You're offering me land and a title and a seat at court. I believe you mean it. I believe you'd defend it with everything you have. But I watched a god wear a crown, and I watched a whole city smile while it happened, and I don't—"

He stopped. Started again, plainer.

"I found people on the road. A disgraced priestess. A deserter. A pickpocket who hears prayers. Six children who call me the Thunder Uncle because Tam told them that's what I was and children don't revise." His voice went rough. "I gave up my magic to save them. That was the price and I paid it gladly and I'd pay it again. But I won't give them up too. Not for land. Not for a name. Not even for a pardon — especially not for a pardon, because a pardon means the exile was real, and I'm starting to think the exile was the lie. Hollowmere was never my home. It was the place I hid. My home is wherever those ridiculous children are arguing about breakfast."

The silence stretched. Aldric lowered the document slowly, and set it on the table between them, unaccepted but not withdrawn.

"And if the storms come back?" the king asked. "If the sky remembers you?"

"Then it rains," August said, "and I get wet like everyone else."

Something moved through Aldric's face then — not disappointment. Recognition. He laughed, short and startled, a sound that seemed to surprise him as much as anyone. "Two years I sat in that thing's grip watching it grant mercy generously and mean nothing by it. And now a man stands in front of me refusing a king's gift, and it's the first honest refusal I've been given since I woke up." He pressed his palm flat against the table, over the untouched pardon. "May I keep this, then? Unsealed. On file. So that if you ever change your mind — next year, in twenty years, on your deathbed — the offer doesn't expire with my memory of making it."

August blinked hard. "Yes," he managed. "Keep it."

"Good." Aldric straightened, and for a moment the king and the man were the same person, entirely. "Then I'll trouble you for one thing instead. Not a title. Not land." He hesitated, and the hesitation made him look young. "The god-touched children you travel with. The choir. Vessel fed on faith — I know that now, I felt it, the way it drank the bells. And there are hundreds like them in this city. Children who pray and get answers. Children nobody knows what to do with, now that the answers have stopped being lies." His eyes went to the window, to the grey bright morning. "I can give them bread and beds. I cannot give them what you have — the knowledge of what listens, and how to sing to it safely, and how to stop. Would you teach them? Not for me. For them."

August looked at Sera. Sera was already smiling, small and crooked, the smile of a woman watching a door close on one life and open onto another.

"Yes," August said. "For them."

* * *

He walked out of the palace alone in the late afternoon, when the rain had thinned to mist and the streets shone like hammered tin. Word had traveled ahead of him, the way word did in a city that had spent two years afraid of its own thoughts and was now, cautiously, remembering how to gossip. People stepped aside for him. Some bowed. One old woman pressed a heel of bread into his hand and would not take it back, and he ate it walking, and it tasted like the best thing he had ever eaten, which was absurd, and true.

At the corner of the Bellwrights' Row, where the underways stairs came up out of the ground, Tam was waiting for him, arms crossed, trying very hard to look casual and failing completely.

"Well?" Tam demanded. "Is your head still attached? Did he take it well? Wren bet me a silver piece you'd cry, and I bet him you wouldn't, and I need that silver, August, the twins have discovered sweets."

"I didn't cry," August said.

Tam squinted at him. "Your eyes are red."

"It's the rain."

"It stopped raining an hour ago."

August put his arm around the boy's shoulders and steered him toward the boarding house where the children were, and Tam talked the whole way — about the twins, about Wren teaching them drill formations in the yard, about the letter Sera had gotten from what remained of her order, the ones who'd believed her all along, asking her to come rebuild. About the shrine under the mountain, which Tam visited every week alone, keeping watch, because someone had to and Tam had decided it was him. August listened and answered and mostly just listened, and the city moved around them, loud and doubtful and alive, no longer smiling in unison.

That night, after the children were asleep — after Nell had demanded a story about the lightning and August had told her, truthfully, that the lightning was retired now, living out its days in the countryside eating grass — August climbed to the roof with a cup of wine and sat with his back against warm chimney brick.

Below him, Vellharrow lay scattered with lamplight. The broken bells would ring again someday; new ones were being cast already, and Sera had argued fiercely that they should be tuned lower, gentler, so that no one in the city would ever again feel a bell in their teeth. Somewhere out east, Hollowmere was mending its roofs without him, and the valley was green and quiet and magic-dead, and it was someone else's home now.

He raised the cup to all of it — the lost things, the kept things, the things he'd chosen.

And that was when he saw it: far off past the city walls, over the hills where the old road ran, a single flicker of light that was not lightning and not lantern-light. It pulsed twice, patient, deliberate, like something signaling. Like something that knew he was watching.

Tam's voice floated up the stairwell behind him, thin in the dark: "August? The little ones are saying the shrine's humming again."

August set down his cup and stood.
