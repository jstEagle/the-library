## 22. The Burning Choir, Singing

The schoolhouse had been a granary once, and before that a chapel, and before that — if the oldest stones were to be believed — a place where people had stood in the rain and shouted their grief at the sky until the sky answered.

August liked that lineage. It felt honest.

He stood now in the morning light that came slantwise through the new windows, glass thick and greenish and full of bubbles, watching eleven children arrange themselves on benches worn smooth by generations of grain sacks. The smallest, a girl named Pip with a cowlick that refused every comb in the village, sat cross-legged on the floor because her legs didn't reach the floor from the bench and she considered this an intolerable indignity. The oldest was nearly sixteen, a broad-shouldered boy called Merrick who had been touched by something river-deep and cold, and who still flinched when thunder rolled, though it had been a year since thunder had meant anything but weather.

"Again," August said. "From the top. And remember what I told you."

"Don't sing *at* anything," they recited, in the ragged unison of children who had memorized a rule without fully believing it mattered.

"Don't sing *at* anything," he agreed. "A song is a door. You don't prop a door open just because you like the breeze."

Pip raised her hand, which she did even when she had no question, simply because raising her hand made her feel official. "What if the song is sad?"

"Especially then." He crouched so he could look down the rows of them, all those faces still carrying the faint luminous residue of things that had listened to them once — a shimmer behind the eyes, a too-quiet quality to their breathing. God-touched, the world called them. The world had not yet found a kinder word, though Sera was working on one. "Sad songs are the loudest doors there are. Grief carries. That's why we learn the verses together. You never sing your sorrow alone into the dark. You sing it into a room full of people who will sing it back."

Merrick shifted on his bench. "Did you ever sing alone?"

The room went still in the way rooms did when the children sensed they had brushed against the true thing, the wound beneath the lesson. August let the silence sit for a moment, because lying to these particular children was impossible anyway — half of them could taste a falsehood the way other people tasted salt.

"Once," he said. "In a flooded place under a city, where no one could hear me but the dark." He straightened, clapped his hands once, brisk as a baker dusting flour. "Which is why we're going to practice the round now. Two parts. Pip, you're leading the high line, and yes, you may stand on the bench if you must, but if you fall off I'm telling Tam, and Tam will make it a story, and you'll never live it down."

Pip scrambled up. The first note rose, thin and clear, and eleven voices caught it and passed it along like a bucket at a fire, and the round began to turn.

August stood in the middle of it with his eyes closed and his hands empty.

That was the thing no one said aloud, the thing he felt every hour of every day the way a man feels the absence of a tooth: his magic was gone. Not sleeping, not muted, not waiting behind some door for a braver version of him to open it. Gone. He had poured it out into the binding like wine into thirsty ground, and the ground had drunk, and Vessel had starved and crumbled to a smell of ozone and old stone, and what remained of August Kavanagh was only a man. A man with a good voice and bad knees and a scar across his palm where the tuning fork had bitten him on the last morning of the old world.

And yet —

When the children sang, he felt something move through the room that was not quite power and not quite prayer. It was smaller than either. It was the feeling of a door being opened onto a garden instead of an abyss. He had spent a year learning its name, and the name, he had decided, was *company*.

The round turned. The light moved across the floor. Outside, the autumn fields lay gold and stubbled under a sky assembling its clouds with no intention whatsoever of doing anything dramatic with them.

When the last voice settled, Pip hopped down from her bench and marched up to him with the gravity of a general accepting a surrender.

"That was better," she declared.

"It was better," he agreed.

"Next time it'll be best."

"Careful," he said, crouching to her height again. "Best is a hungry word. Better is plenty."

She frowned at him, considering this, then shrugged with the magnificent indifference of the seven-year-old and ran out into the yard, and the others poured after her like water finding its level, and August stood alone in the warm dust-smelling light and let himself feel, for exactly as long as he allowed himself each day, the whole weight and wonder of the ordinary.

Then he banked the stove, closed the shutters against the afternoon heat, and went to find out why Wren Ashfall was standing at his gate looking like a man carrying news he'd rehearsed three ways and hated all of them.

* * *

Wren had changed in a year, the way soldiers changed when the war ended and left them holding the shape of themselves. He'd grown a beard he clearly hadn't decided to keep. He wore no colors at all, which in the capital's aftermath was itself a kind of uniform.

"They've finished the archive," he said, without preamble, because Wren had never learned to do anything with preamble except lose arguments to it. "Under the cathedral. All the order's records, everything Sera pulled out of the mountain shrine, everything the crown kept locked. They're calling it the House of Honest Answers." His mouth twisted. "Sera hates the name."

"Sera hates most names," August said. "It's how you know she loves the things themselves."

"She wants you there for the opening. Both of you. She wouldn't say why." Wren hesitated, and August watched a man who had once carried a king's secret letters decide whether to carry one more small truth. "There's a section sealed with storm-wax. Your mother's mark on it, August. The hymn. The original. What Sera found in the vaults was a copy — a fragment. This is the whole of it, and Aldric has ordered it preserved, and Sera says—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Sera says you should be the one to decide what happens to it. Read it or burn it. But decide it awake, and decide it free."

August looked past him, down the lane, where the village went about its business with the unhurried confidence of a place that had learned what peace cost and paid it gladly. Smoke rose straight from chimneys. Someone was arguing cheerfully about fence posts. A dog slept in the road with the total commitment of a creature who had never once been asked to save the world.

"I'll come," he said.

"You'll read it?"

"I'll decide it awake," August said, and Wren nodded slowly, the way men nodded at oaths they understood would take years to fulfill.

* * *

Sera Vane had taken a ruined priory on the coast road and was rebuilding it into something that had never existed before: an order with no hierarchy, no vows of silence, no doctrine except one sentence carved over the door in letters deep enough to outlast the weather. *We listen so that nothing else has to answer.*

August arrived at dusk and found her on the roof, because of course she was on the roof; some part of her had apparently concluded that gravity was another institution worth regarding with suspicion. She was re-laying slate with the efficient fury of a woman who trusted mortar more than people, though the people kept arriving anyway — deserters and doubters and god-touched adults who had spent decades afraid of their own shadows, all of them drawn to the priory like moths to a lamp that promised, for once, only light.

"You got my message," she said, without turning around.

"Wren delivered it with the subtlety of a thrown brick."

"He's improving." She came down the ladder with a slate still tucked under one arm, and up close August saw the year on her — gray coming in at her temples, a new scar on her forearm, and underneath all of it a steadiness that hadn't been there on the road out of Hollowmere, when she'd been a brand-marked heretic with nothing left but certainty and a grudge. "You brought the children?"

"Pip insisted. They're in the kitchen terrorizing Brother Anselm, who is discovering that hospitality is a discipline."

"Good." She set down the slate. For a moment she studied him with those pale, unsparing eyes that had seen through every wall he'd ever built, including the ones he'd built after the walls stopped working. "You look well."

"I am well. That's the strange part. I keep waiting for it to be a trick."

"It's not a trick. It's what you bought." She said it flatly, without ceremony, which was the highest register of tenderness Sera possessed. Then, quieter: "Have you decided about the hymn?"

He leaned against the doorframe. Below them, lamplight bloomed in the priory windows one by one, and somewhere inside, a child laughed — Pip, unmistakably, laughing the way she did everything, at full volume and without apology.

"My mother died keeping that song out of the wrong hands," August said. "I spent a year being furious at her for it. Then a year understanding her. Now I think—" He turned his hand over, looked at the scar. "I think she wasn't protecting a weapon. She was protecting a choice. The song can bind a god or free one, depending on who sings it and why. She wanted it sung by someone who chose it. So I'll read it. In the archive, with you there, with the king's seal broken properly. And then I'll put it somewhere the children can find it in a hundred years, when they're ready, when the world is ready — with a letter explaining that it's a door, and doors deserve honesty about what's on the other side."

Sera was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had roughened. "Your mother would have—"

"Don't," August said gently. "She'd have argued with me for six hours and then agreed. Let's leave her that. It's more fun."

Something cracked in Sera's face — not grief exactly, and not joy, but the seam between them where the truest things lived — and she gripped his shoulder once, hard, and let go, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

They ate together in the refectory, the whole strange congregation of them: Wren telling stories with his hands, Brother Anselm surrendering his kitchen with dignity, the children falling asleep in a heap like puppies, Merrick keeping watch over them without being asked, the way he always did. And later, when the priory had gone dark and the sea was making its ancient argument with the cliffs, August stood at a window with Sera beside him and neither of them speaking, because they had long ago used up all the words that mattered and discovered that the silence afterward was not empty but full.

"One year," she said finally.

"One year since the bells broke."

"Do you miss it? The storm?" She asked it the way she asked everything — plainly, without mercy, without cruelty either. "Be honest. You're allowed to miss it."

August considered the question honestly, letting it turn over like a stone in a stream.

"I miss the certainty of it," he said at last. "When the lightning came, there was never any doubt about what to do. Everything since has been choices. Small ones. Whether to fix a roof or write a letter first. Whether Pip gets honey or jam." He smiled at the dark window, at his own reflection ghosted faint against the night. "I don't miss the power. I miss thinking I needed it."

Sera nodded slowly. "That's the whole sermon," she said. "I couldn't have preached it better myself."

"Don't tell your order. They'll make you carve it over the door."

"We already have a door."

"Then get a second door. You're an order of honest answers. Answer honestly: you love collecting doors."

For the first time in the year August had known her, Sera Vane laughed — really laughed, unguarded, the sound startled out of her like a bird from cover — and the sound of it seemed to surprise her more than anyone, and August filed it away among the small treasures he had begun, cautiously, to permit himself.

* * *

Tam met him at the shrine on the last morning of the year, as they had met every month since the sealing, because some vigils you keep forever and some you keep until keeping them becomes a kind of homecoming.

The shrine stood in the hills above the capital, rebuilt in plain white stone, unadorned. Beneath its floor, behind three seals of storm-wax and one of Sera's devising and one that Aldric himself had laid with his own hands — the king insisting, saying, *I should carry some of the weight, I carried none of it when it mattered* — the crumbs of Vessel slept in the dark. Starved. Scattered. As dead as an old god could be made to be, which was dead enough for watchfulness.

Tam had appointed himself the watcher. No one had asked him to. He'd simply appeared one day, seventeen years old and sharp-eyed and impossible, and said, *Someone has to listen to this place, and I'm the best listener either of us knows,* and that had been that. He kept the records now, meticulous as a monk: temperature, tremor, the mood of the birds, the dreams of the nearby shepherds. Once a month he wrote it all down and sent it to Sera, and once a month Sera wrote back a single word — *quiet* — and once a month Tam pretended to be offended that his exhaustive reports earned one word, and everyone understood that the ritual was the point.

"You're late," Tam said, sitting on the shrine steps with his notebook on his knees.

"The roads were muddy."

"The roads are always muddy. You're always late. It's practically liturgy." But he scooted over to make room, and August sat, and for a while they watched the valley below, where the capital spread out along its river, bell towers rising clean and silent, waiting for human hands to ring them at human hours for human reasons.

"How are they?" Tam asked. Meaning the children. Meaning, as always, all of them, everywhere, the whole scattered flock of the god-touched whom Tam had adopted with the ferocious permanence of someone who knew exactly what it was to be a child nobody prayed for.

"Loud. Growing. Merrick starts training with Sera next season. Pip has decided she's going to be the first person to sing to the rain politely and get it to agree to fall only at night."

Tam snorted. "She will, too. That girl could negotiate water out of a stone." He flipped his notebook closed. Then, in a different voice, the careful one, the one he used maybe twice a year: "Anything? On your end? Since the last time?"

August shook his head. "Nothing. Silence where the storm used to be. Good silence. Room-shaped."

"And you're sure that's — you're sure it doesn't frighten you. Being ordinary."

August thought about the question seriously, because Tam deserved serious answers; he had earned them in a flooded catacomb and a burning tower and a hundred small midnights since.

"When I was young," August said, "my mother told me that the Stormbound were chosen because we could hold what would break anyone else. I believed that made us special. It took me thirty years and one very bad god to understand that what makes anyone special isn't what they can hold. It's what they refuse to put down." He looked at Tam — at this pickpocket-turned-warden, this boy who had heard prayers in the dark and answered them with his whole stubborn heart. "I put down the storm. I kept the rest. That was the trade, and I'd make it again tomorrow, and the day after, forever."

Tam was quiet. Then he cleared his throat, blinked hard at the horizon, and said, "If you tell anyone I got emotional, I'll deny it and also hide your boots."

"Your secret dies with me."

"It better. I know where you sleep."

They sat together as the sun climbed, two survivors keeping a gentle watch over a sealed dark, and the morning was so clear and so mild that neither of them noticed, at first, the change in the air — the softening of the light, the green smell rising off the hills, the first cool touch on their faces like a blessing from a source that required nothing in return.

Rain.

They both felt the old reflexive tension gather — Tam's hand tightening on his notebook, August's breath catching in the hollow where the storm-song used to live — and then, deliberately, consciously, they let it go.

The rain fell. Straight and soft and silver, drumming on the white stones, beading on the grass, running in bright threads down the shrine steps to feed the valley below. It fell on the capital and its waiting bells, on the priory and its carved door, on the schoolhouse and its sleeping stove, on the graves of the pilgrim who had crawled into Hollowmere and the mother whose song had finally been read, wept over, and given freely to the future. It fell without meaning. It fell without hunger. It fell the way rain had fallen before the gods woke and the way it would fall long after every name for it was forgotten.

Pip came running up the hill through it, soaked and triumphant, shouting that she'd made a deal, that the rain had *listened*, that it had agreed to stop by supper — and Tam laughed and August laughed, and the three of them sat together on the steps of the sealed shrine while the whole grey-gold world drank.

And the rain was only rain.

And it was enough.
