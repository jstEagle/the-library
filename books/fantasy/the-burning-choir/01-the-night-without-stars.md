## 1. The Night Without Stars

The candles had to be lit in order, and Petra Blackwood had been lighting them in order for twenty years.

Wax first, from the eastern sill, where the dawn came in over the orchard rows. Then the two tallow stubs by the hearth, then the tall white one on the mantel that only ever burned on the eve of the Founding Rite. Nine flames in all, and each one a small obedience. Mother Anselm had taught them the sequence when they were seven years old, her dry hands folding Petra's fingers around the taper: *Order is how we remember what we're allowed to want.* Petra had not understood it then. They understood it now, standing in their narrow stone house above the millrace with the ninth candle guttering, understanding it the way you understand a fence—by pressing your palm against it every day of your life.

Outside, the valley was dressing itself for the Rite. Lanterns swung from the eaves of Stillwater's crooked streets. Down at the meeting green, the elders were hauling the great bronze bowl into position, the one that would hold the storm-water tomorrow, when the whole town would gather to sing the valley safe for another year. Children were already running the boundaries with ribbons tied to hazel wands, touching them to the old ward-stones at the field edges—the squat gray teeth of them, mossed and lichen-eaten, standing in their patient ring around everything Petra had ever seen.

Everything Petra had ever seen. That was the phrase their mind kept returning to as they pulled on their choir robe, gray wool gone soft at the elbows, and it kept snagging there like a nail in cloth. Twenty-two years in the valley. Twenty-two years inside the ring of stones. They had never once crossed the line. No one had. You learned early not to ask what lay beyond the hazel woods at the northern rim; you learned that the question itself was a kind of rudeness, like asking an orphan about its mother.

Petra checked the candles a final time—order, order—and went out into the blue evening.

* * *

The rehearsal was held in the undercroft of the meeting hall, because the acoustics were better down among the roots of the building, and because Mother Anselm said a founding song should be sung with the earth listening. Forty voices of the Choir stood in their crescent, elders at the horns, children in the middle where their unbroken notes could be carried like cargo. Petra stood third from the left end, where they had stood since they were tall enough to see over the shoulder of the person in front of them.

Anselm raised her hand. The room went quiet the way water goes still.

"Tomorrow we sing the Rite," she said. Her voice was thin as winter paper but it filled the undercroft anyway; it always had. "Tonight we rehearse. And tonight I will ask you the same thing I have asked this Choir for fifty years: sing it as if it matters. Not because it does—" a dry pause, the ghost of her old humor— "but because the day it stops mattering is the day we find out why it did."

Someone laughed softly. Petra did not. There was something in her voice tonight, some hairline fracture under the glaze of routine, and twenty years of being taught by her had taught Petra to hear it the way farmers hear rain coming in the wind.

They began.

It was a plain melody, deliberately so—the Founding Song moved stepwise up and down the scale like a woman walking a fence line, checking the posts. The children took the ground note and held it, a long warm drone, and the adults wove above them, and the sound built in the stone until Petra could feel it in their back teeth, in the floor, in the soles of their feet. This was the part they loved, the part they would never have admitted to loving: the moment when forty separate breaths became one long animal breathing, when the song stopped being something they made and became something they were inside of.

And then, on the fourth verse—the verse about the sky, the one that named the stars as witnesses—

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*, all of them at once, through the high slit window above the crescent, the little strip of night sky that Petra could see between the heads of the second row. One heartbeat the valley's sky held its familiar scatter of lights, the Shepherd, the Loom, the three bright sisters of the Plough that Petra had navigated by their whole life without ever navigating anywhere. The next heartbeat: nothing. A black so total it looked less like absence than like a lid.

The song faltered. It did not stop—forty years of discipline does not stop—but it thinned, wavered, went ragged at the edges like cloth catching on brambles. Old Brother Callow lost his place entirely and stood mouthing silence. In the middle row a child began to cry and was hushed by three adults at once.

"Keep singing," Anselm said, and her voice did not shake, which was how Petra knew she was afraid. "Whatever you see, keep singing. The song holds the walls whether or not anything watches."

But Petra had stopped hearing the song. Because something else was happening inside them.

It began low, below the stomach, a warmth like swallowed coals, and rose. Twenty years of stillness broke open all at once—a door in their chest that they had never known was a door, swinging wide. Power. The word arrived whole and terrible, because there was no other word for it. It moved up their spine like water finding an old channel, and every channel in Petra's body was worn deep, worn smooth by use, though they had never used it, never once, not in twenty-two years of ordered candles and stepwise songs. Their body remembered. Their blood remembered. Something in them that had been folded away so long it had grown into the folding shook itself out and *rose*.

The air around Petra began to move. Loose hairs lifted from their braid. The candle flames on the undercroft ledge leaned toward them, all nine, like flowers toward sun.

*No,* Petra thought, with a clarity that cut through the roaring. *Not here. Not now. Not in front of—*

"Petra." Anselm's voice, quiet, from the head of the crescent. She had not turned around. She did not need to. "Breathe it down. Four counts in, eight counts out. As I taught you."

*As I taught you.* As if she had known. As if she had always known that the stillness in Petra was not emptiness but pressure, a lake behind a dam, and that someday the dam would hear a song.

Petra breathed. Four in. Eight out. The warmth sank, grudgingly, like a beast lying back down—but it did not sleep. It crouched. For the first time in their adult life Petra could feel it crouching, awake, attentive, listening through their ears to the broken rehearsal, waiting.

Above the slit window, the sky stayed black.

* * *

They dismissed the Choir early, which had never happened. Anselm told them to go home, light their candles, sleep, and say nothing of what they'd seen until the elders had spoken. The *until* did its work; by the time Petra reached the door, the undercroft was already humming with the particular hush of people deciding what not to say.

The streets were worse. Stillwater had come out of its houses to stand in the lanes and look up, and the looking was the frightening thing—not panic, not yet, but that terrible neighborly calm, aproned women murmuring *clouds, surely, festival clouds*, men saying the Rite would sort it, would mend it, the Rite always mended things. A boy of six stood in the middle of Mill Lane pointing straight up and shouting *it's gone, it's all gone* while his mother folded his arm down and smiled at passersby as if he'd said something charming.

Petra walked past the meeting green. The great bronze bowl sat empty in the torchlight, and beside it the elders stood in their knot, and their faces, lit from below, were the faces of people reading a letter they had hoped never to receive. Old Sister Maren was speaking fast and low, her hands cutting the air. Anselm stood apart from them, very still, looking north—looking, Petra realized, along the exact line of the valley rim, toward the hazel woods, toward the ward-stones.

Toward whatever had just gone out like a snuffed candle, all around the world.

"Mother Anselm." Petra hadn't meant to speak. The name simply left them, pulled out by the crouching thing in their chest.

She turned. Up close her face was composed, but her eyes had the wet brightness of someone who has been crying in private and recently. "Go home, child."

"The stars—"

"Will still be absent tomorrow, and the day after, and the Rite will proceed, and the valley will hold." She said it like a woman reciting a lesson she was trying to make herself believe. Then, softer, almost inaudible under the wind off the millrace: "God forgive us, it came due faster than I thought."

"What came due?"

For one moment—one long, suspended moment—Anselm looked at Petra the way you look at a door you are deciding whether to open. Petra felt the crouching thing lean forward against its cage, hungry for whatever was behind her face.

"Nothing," Anselm said. "Go home. Light your candles in order." And she walked away across the green to join the other elders, her gray robe moving in the wind like smoke refusing to rise.

* * *

Petra did go home. They lit no candles.

They stood instead at their eastern window with the shutters thrown open, looking up at the starless dark, and let themselves feel—for the first time, honestly, completely—what they had spent twenty years not feeling.

Fear. But underneath the fear, worse: a kind of vertigo, the ground-tilt of realizing that the floor has been a trapdoor all along. Because the magic rising in them had not come from nowhere. It had been *contained*. They understood that now with the new sense that had opened in their chest—they could feel the valley itself, feel the faint hum of the ward-stones ringing the rim like the rim of a struck bell, feel how that hum pressed down on everything living inside it, gently, ceaselessly, the way snow presses down a roof. Pressing Petra flat. Pressing the whole valley flat. For twenty-two years.

*Safety*, they thought, tasting the word and finding it rotten. Every ordered candle, every stepwise verse, every rule about not asking what lay beyond the hazel woods—all of it was not a life. It was a lid. And tonight someone—or something—had lifted the lid, and the stars had paid for it, and the power in Petra's blood had woken like a prisoner hearing keys.

Far to the north, along the dark rim of the valley, light flickered. Petra leaned out into the cold and watched it: a pale shimmer running along the line of the hills, following the chain of ward-stones, growing brighter, whiter, thinner—

It cracked.

There was no other word. The shimmer split with a sound that reached Petra a full breath later, a long descending tone like the last syllable of an enormous word, and the light ran jagged from hill to hill and died, all of it, everywhere at once, and the hum Petra had felt all their life—the gentle constant pressure of the wards—simply stopped.

The silence of it was physical. Petra gripped the windowsill. The crouching thing in their chest rose to its full height for the first time in twenty years, unfurled like a wing, and poured outward into the sudden space, and Petra gasped aloud at the sheer size of what they were, what they had always been, folded down and folded down into a shape the size of an obedient life.

Below in the town, dogs began to howl. Somewhere a bell started ringing with no rhythm and no reason. And on the northern rim, where the ward-stones stood in their ancient ring, Petra could see—or imagined they could see, or perhaps the new-woken thing in them could actually feel—stones toppling, one after another, like teeth leaving an old jaw.

The stars did not come back.

Petra stood in the cold a long time, watching the darkness where the sky's witnesses should have been, feeling their own power move through them like a tide with nowhere yet to go. Tomorrow was the Founding Rite. Tomorrow the elders would sing the valley safe, the way they had sung it safe for longer than anyone could say.

But the valley was not safe. It had never been safe. It had been *kept*—the way you keep a debt, the way you keep a secret, the way you keep a person somewhere by telling them the outside is a story.

Anselm's words came back to them on the wind: *it came due.*

Petra closed the shutters. In the dark, unlit room, they did not light the candles in order.

They did not light them at all.
