## 2. What the Wards Were For

The morning after the stars went out, Petra Blackwood swept their doorstep.

This was the thing to do. This was what people did. The broom moved in the same arcs it had moved for twenty years, pushing ash and valley dust off the flat stone, and Petra's hands performed the ritual of ordinary life while their mind screamed behind a wall of calm so complete it frightened them more than the dark sky had.

Across the lane, Maren Ostrow was beating her rugs with the same two-beat rhythm she used every Founding morning. She nodded at Petra without breaking stride.

"Strange night," Maren said. "Storm coming, I'd say. My hip's been singing since dusk."

Petra looked up. Where the sky should have been—where it had been, all their life, wheeling and salted with light above the rim of the valley—there was nothing. Not cloud. Cloud had texture, movement, the gray suggestion of weather deciding itself. This was a smooth black absence, like a lid set over the world.

"The stars," Petra said. Their voice came out level. They were almost proud of how level it came out.

Maren squinted upward, frowned, and went back to her rug. "Haze. It'll burn off." She struck the rug twice more. "You'll come to the Rite tonight? They're saying the Rite will go ahead as planned. Mother Cassia says a little darkness is no reason to shame the founders."

A little darkness. Petra gripped the broom until the wood bit into their palms. Last night they had stood in the choir loft with forty voices around them, mid-verse of the Founding hymn, when the sky had simply *stopped*—and every one of those forty voices had kept singing as though nothing had happened. Had Petra imagined it? For one vertiginous moment they had wondered. But then the wards—

The wards. Petra had felt them shatter. Felt it in their teeth, in the hollows of their bones, like standing beside a window when it breaks: that instant of pressure change, of air remembering it could move. Twenty years of living inside a hum so constant they had stopped hearing it, and last night the hum had died, and something that had been coiled tight inside Petra since childhood had stretched, unfurled, and *woken*.

They hadn't told anyone. They had walked home through streets full of neighbors lighting lamps against a darkness lamps did nothing for, and they had said *strange night* and *haze, likely*, and they had gone to bed and lain awake feeling their own blood sing in a key they'd been taught to forget.

"Petra?" Maren was watching them now, head tilted. "You've gone pale."

"Didn't sleep well," Petra said. "The quiet."

"There's always quiet before a storm." Maren smiled, satisfied, and carried her rugs inside.

Petra stood on their clean doorstep under the lidless sky and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that everyone in Stillwater would say exactly this, today and tomorrow and next week, right up until the lie became impossible or became permanent. *Nothing has changed. Haze. Storm coming.* The valley had spent twenty years practicing not-seeing, and they were very, very good at it now.

Their magic stirred beneath their breastbone like a second heartbeat, patient and enormous, and Petra made a decision they should have made last night.

They were going to the archive.

* * *

The House of the Choir sat on the valley's high shoulder, white stone gone gray with lichen, its roof half-collapsed where the fire had taken it eleven years ago. Officially the archive had burned in an accident—a lamp, a dry summer, bad luck. Unofficially, everyone knew better than to ask why the Choir had never rebuilt it, never cleared it, never let anyone but elders past the scorched threshold. In Stillwater there were questions you didn't ask the way there were foods you didn't eat during the Rite fast: not forbidden, exactly, just *wrong*.

Petra had been inside exactly once, as a child, on the day Mother Anselm had caught them humming a note that made the rain fall sideways. Anselm had marched them here—not to punish, Petra understood now, but to show them the cost. *Look,* she had said, gesturing at the blackened shelves, the fused glass of windowpanes, the smell of char that had never left the walls. *Look what knowing can do.* And Petra, seven years old and terrified, had believed the lesson was about fire.

The door was still sealed with the Choir's knot-work, wax and cord in patterns meant to turn away casual hands. Petra's fingers found the pattern without thinking—their hands remembered what their mind had been taught to release—and the seal came apart like bread.

Inside, the dark smelled of cold ash and old paper and, faintly, impossibly, of ozone. Of storms. Petra's skin prickled. Their waking magic leaned toward that smell the way a plant leans toward light.

They lit no lamp. They didn't need one; their eyes had adjusted, and besides, some instinct told them that light here would be a kind of trespass. They moved between the fallen shelves by touch and memory, boots crunching on char, and began to search.

They didn't know what they were looking for. That was a lie they let themselves keep for the first hour. What they were looking for was reassurance—some record, some ledger, some elder's dry footnote that would explain last night as weather, as astronomy, as anything other than what Petra's bones insisted it was. *We regret to inform the reader that the darkness is temporary.*

What they found instead, three hours in, wedged behind a shelf that had collapsed across a reading desk, was a fragment.

It was vellum, scorched at the edges, perhaps a third of a page surviving from whatever larger document it had once been. The hand was formal, ecclesiastical, the ink browned with age. Petra crouched over it in the dark and read by the gray seep of light through the broken roof.

*...therefore the terms are fixed and shall not be renegotiated, the Debt being accepted by the undersigned on behalf of the settlements entire. The sky is given. The valley is kept. Let the wards stand so long as the song is sung, and let no voice among us teach the children the fourth mode, which wakes what sleeps...*

The rest was ash.

Petra read it again. Then again, lips moving. *The sky is given. The valley is kept.*

Given to whom? Kept from whom? Given *how*—a sky was not a thing one gave, it was the thing above, the constant, the given beneath all givens—and yet here it was in someone's careful ecclesiastical hand, itemized like a debt entry, because that was exactly what it was. A payment. Terms. *Fixed and shall not be renegotiated.*

And the wards. *Let the wards stand so long as the song is sung.* The Founding hymn. The one they sang every year, the one the elders taught note by note, the one whose melody Petra had known longer than their own name. It wasn't a commemoration. It was maintenance. Every voice in Stillwater, raised every Founding Day, was a nail holding down a lid.

And the children. *Let no voice among us teach the children the fourth mode.* Petra sat back on their heels in the ash. There were four modes in the old training songs—or there had been; the elders taught only three, and when Petra as a child had asked about the gap in the numbering, Mother Anselm had said the fourth was lost, dear one, lost with the fire, don't fret it. Lost. Or *forbidden*. A mode that *woke what slept*.

Something was sleeping in Petra. Something had woken last night when the wards broke, and it knew the fourth mode the way the body knows breathing.

They were still kneeling there when their hand brushed the edge of something else beneath the fragment—a stiff rectangle, leather-edged, heavy. They drew it out into the gray light.

A map. Folded many times, cracked along the creases, but whole. Petra unfolded it with the care owed to a sleeping animal.

It was a country. That was the first shock—that it was a *country*, a full and detailed one, with mountain ranges inked in fine hatching, rivers named in a flowing script, towns marked with tiny confident dots. Petra's eye traveled across it, hungry for names, and found them strange and lovely and utterly unfamiliar: *Carrowmere. The Vell Steps. Ostmouth-on-the-Gray.* A whole nation rendered in loving cartographic detail, borders drawn firm and final.

And in the middle of it, a blank space.

Not empty—*erased*. The parchment there had been scraped, actually scraped with a blade, until the surface fuzzed and pilled, and over the scraped patch someone had written in a later, shakier hand: *Stillwater Valley.* Nothing else. No roads leading in. No rivers. The neighboring features—here a forest called the Weeping Reach, there a ridge named the Spine—all stopped at the edges of the scraped patch as if afraid to cross it. The valley was a hole in the shape of home.

Petra's whole life, rendered as a lacuna. Every road they had ever walked, erased by a blade. They had grown up inside a place that the world's own maps had agreed not to see.

The vertigo came then, properly, the way Anselm had once described seasickness of the soul. Petra knelt in the burned archive of the only home they'd ever known and understood that Stillwater was not a town. It was a container. The wards weren't protection. The hymn wasn't memory. The exile of storm-callers wasn't punishment—it was *storage*. They had all been put here, filed away, sung to sleep, and the sky above them had been sold to pay for the shelving.

*The Debt being accepted by the undersigned on behalf of the settlements entire.*

Someone had signed for them. Someone had traded the stars for survival, and no one had asked Petra, who had been a child, who would have been a child for another hundred years if the signing had happened that long ago—but it didn't matter, did it, because the bill had come due last night, mid-hymn, while forty voices held the lid down and the lid broke anyway.

Petra folded the map. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Inside, nothing was steady; inside, twenty years of certainty were collapsing like the ward-glass, and underneath the collapse something vast turned over and opened one eye.

They wrapped the fragment and the map in oilcloth from the desk drawer, tucked them inside their coat, and stood.

That was when they heard it: footsteps in the outer hall. Deliberate ones. Elder Cassia's cane, tapping stone in the rhythm she used when she wanted to be heard coming.

Petra had perhaps thirty seconds. The back wall of the archive had a gap where the fire had split the stones—Anselm had shown them that too, years ago, in another lesson Petra had thought was about fire. They went through it as the archive door groaned open behind them, and dropped into the gorse below the high shoulder with their heart hammering and the oilcloth crackling against their ribs.

Above, Cassia's voice drifted down through the broken roof, thin and cold: calling for the archivist, noting that the seal was broken, that the knot-work had been undone *from within the pattern*—and then, after a silence, softer, to someone Petra couldn't see:

"Wake the others. If the child took what I think she took, we have a problem older than any of us."

*Child.* Petra pressed their back against the cold stone, the stolen map against their heart, and breathed.

They had come looking for reassurance. They had found a Debt, a forbidden mode, and a hole in the world shaped like everything they loved. And somewhere out beyond the valley rim—in the erased country, in the dark where stars used to be—someone or something had come to collect.

Petra slid down through the gorse toward the lane, toward home, toward the Founding Rite they would attend with a straight face and a loaded coat, and did not yet know that the answers they needed were already walking toward Stillwater, following the same darkness, with a knife and half a key.
