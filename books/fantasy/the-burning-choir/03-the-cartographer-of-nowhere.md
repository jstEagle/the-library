## 3. The Cartographer of Nowhere

Petra had not slept. The night without stars sat in their chest like a swallowed stone, and every hour that passed without the sky correcting itself made the stone heavier. By dawn they were standing at the window of their narrow room above the cooper's shop, watching the valley light up in its ordinary way—the mist lifting off Stillwater, the terraced fields going from grey to green, the bell in the chapel tower ringing seven as it had rung seven for as long as anyone could remember—and trying to understand how everything could look so unchanged when nothing was.

The neighbors had an answer for that, of course. Everyone did.

*Cloud cover,* said the miller, squinting upward with the satisfied expression of a man who has solved a problem by refusing to look at it. *Weather coming in from the pass.*

*The Collectors took a tithe,* said old Marta Voss, and then laughed at her own joke, because the Collectors were a story told to children, a bogeyman with empty hands.

*You're pale, Petra Blackwood,* said the baker's wife, pressing a heel of bread into their hands unasked. *You should eat. You should stop looking up. Nothing good ever came of looking up.*

So Petra ate the bread and stopped looking up, and went instead to the one place in the valley where looking up had once been a discipline: the burned archive of the Choir, on the hill above the orchards, where the roof had caved in thirty years ago and no one had bothered to rebuild it because no one needed storm-callers anymore. That was the official story. Petra had grown up inside the unofficial one—had been carried through its doors at six years old with frostbite in their fingers and a voice that could crack ice, and had spent twenty years learning to sing quietly.

The archive smelled of char and wet stone, as always. Petra moved between the shelves with the ease of long habit, past the water-swollen ledgers, past the hymnals with their notation scratched out by careful hands, down to the back wall where Mother Anselm kept—or had kept—the things she called *the honest books.* She hadn't come to morning prayer. Neither had she come last evening, or answered the three times Petra had knocked at her cottage door. Anselm was old and stubborn and had survived two decades of being useless on purpose; she would not be felled by a dark sky. But her absence had a texture, and the texture was wrong.

It was in the honest books that Petra found the fragment.

It was half a page, torn from something larger, tucked inside a surveyor's atlas like a bookmark someone had meant to remove. The handwriting was Anselm's, but older—sharper, less resigned:

*—the Debt is not metaphorical. We signed. The terms are held beneath the First Door, and the First Door is held by the drowned cathedral, and the drowned cathedral is not on any map we are permitted to keep. If the wards fail, do not wait for instruction. There will be no instruction. There will only be what we agreed to forget.*

Below that, in different ink, different hand, cramped and urgent: *the valley is not marked. Check the border maps. They erased us first.*

Petra stood very still among the ruined shelves while the words rearranged the architecture of their life. *They erased us first.* They thought of the schoolroom lessons—the valley as refuge, the valley as sanctuary, the blessed stillness after the war—and felt the whole edifice tilt, gently, the way a floor tilts just before you understand you are standing on a ship.

They pulled the border atlases off the shelf with hands that were not quite steady. The first map showed the kingdom entire: coastlines, mountain ranges, the great river delta in the south. The second showed the eastern provinces in detail. The third—

The third showed the same territory, the same rivers, the same mountains folding down into lowlands—and where the Stillwater Valley should have been there was nothing. Not blank space, which would have been a statement. Nothing. The cartographer's hatching ran uninterrupted across the space where twenty thousand people lived, as though the land itself had declined to exist. A river that Petra had swum in every summer of their childhood appeared on the map, flowed confidently eastward, and simply stopped at the valley line, its blue ink ending in a clean vertical edge, as if the pen had been lifted by decree.

The vertigo came then, exactly as Anselm's marginalia must have known it would. Twenty years of containment dressed as care. A childhood of *sing softly, Petra, sing smaller* reframed in a single glance at a piece of vellum. Their whole life was not a life. It was a strategy.

The bell rang eight. Somewhere below the hill, the valley went about its morning, insisting on its own innocence.

And at the valley line, at that exact moment, a woman with a satchel full of impossible maps stopped walking, looked at the ward-ruins, and smiled the smile of someone who has finally caught up with a rumor.

* * *

Sefa Ilves had been walking toward the darkness for eleven days, and she was tired in the particular way that comes not from the body but from hope beginning, reluctantly, to function.

She had felt it first in the archive-house at Vell, where she'd gone to sell a forgery—a charming fake of a coastal chart of a country that had been unmade before Sefa was born, rendered so lovingly that collectors wept over it. She was good at that. It was the only honest work available to a person whose actual profession was extinct: cartographer of nowhere, mapper of the erased. Every country she had been trained to draw was gone. So she drew them anyway, from memory, from fragments, from the testimony of the last people who could name their own villages—and when memory ran out, she invented, and the inventions sold better than the truths, which told her everything about the world she lived in.

Then the stars had gone out, and Sefa had stood in the archive-house doorway with her forged coastline under her arm and felt something she had not felt since she was nine years old and soldiers burned her city's records to keep it from being remembered.

Recognition.

Because she knew what it looked like when something was taken out of the world deliberately. She had spent her whole adult life studying the wound-marks such takings left behind. And the sky, that night, wore them.

She had walked east for eleven days, following the dark the way other people followed rivers—because it led somewhere, and because there was nothing else to follow. On the twelfth day she reached a line of shattered white stones set into a mountain meadow, each one taller than a man, each one cracked straight down its center as neatly as if a giant had tapped them with a chisel. Ward-stones. Old ones. And beyond them, a valley so green and so orderly and so utterly unremarkable that Sefa knew immediately it was the most remarkable thing she had ever seen.

Because she had drawn this valley. Or rather, she had drawn the hole where this valley should have been—a hundred times, in a hundred commissions. *Here the sources disagree. Here the ink stops. Here there is a legend of a place of singers that no charter confirms.* Cartographers' guilds paid good money for anomalies, and the Stillwater Gap was the most persistent anomaly in the eastern archives. A country-shaped absence. And now here it was, filled in, real, smelling of woodsmoke and apples, its people moving through their morning as though the sky above them had not been switched off like a lamp.

Sefa stood at the ward-line for a long time, doing what she always did: mapping. Her eyes moved in the trained rhythm—landmark, bearing, distance, landmark—and her pencil moved in her pocket against her thigh, recording bearings she'd transcribe later. Habit. Craft. The one form of prayer she trusted.

In her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, half a key made of a metal she could not name lay against her hip like a second heartbeat. She had taken it from a dying man in a border town that no longer appeared on maps, who had pressed it into her hands and said, *Find the singers. The debt has a door. Tell them I'm sorry we waited so long.* She had spent four years failing to find the singers.

She had apparently stopped failing.

She was still smiling at the ward-ruins when she heard the footsteps behind her, and turned—too slowly, she'd note later, too slow by half—with her knife already clearing her belt, because eleven days alone in the dark country teaches a person certain reflexes.

A figure stood at the treeline. Young, maybe, or young-looking, with the hollowed watchfulness of someone who had not slept. They held a short iron rod—no, not a rod, a tuning-fork the size of a sword hilt, humming faintly, audibly, with a note Sefa felt in her teeth rather than heard.

"You crossed the line," the figure said. Their voice cracked on the last word and steadied itself with visible effort. "Nobody crosses the line."

"Someone built a road to it," Sefa said. "Roads are invitations. I read them professionally."

"That road hasn't been used in twenty years."

"Then it's overdue." Sefa let the knife sit loose in her grip—not raised, not lowered, a fact rather than a threat. "You're one of the singers. Don't bother denying it. You're holding a pitch-fork tuned to the ward frequency, your knuckles are white, and you've been watching me since I came over the rise, which means you felt me coming, which means the wards breaking woke something in you." She tilted her head. "How does it feel? Waking up?"

The figure—Petra, she would learn, though the name meant nothing yet—flinched as if struck. "Who are you?"

"Sefa Ilves. Cartographer." She said it plainly, because the plainness was the joke and she liked to give people time to find it. "I map countries that don't exist. And until yesterday, yours was the biggest one on my list."

Something moved behind Petra's eyes—the specific flicker of a person hearing a sentence they've been dreading and hoping for all at once. "That's not funny."

"No," Sefa agreed. "It never is. That's the trade."

They regarded each other across the meadow grass, the broken ward-stones between them like the ribs of some enormous dead animal. Wind moved through it, carrying apple-blossom smell up from the valley, absurdly gentle.

"I found a page," Petra said at last, slowly, the way a person walks on ice. "In our archive. It says the valley isn't on the border maps. It says we were erased first."

"Standard practice," Sefa said. "Erasure precedes extraction. You can't miss what you've been taught isn't there." She saw the words land, saw them hurt, and did not soften them, because softening was a lie and she had decided, sometime in the last eleven days, to stop lying to whoever these singers turned out to be. It was going to be difficult. Lying was her native language.

"What's in the bag?" Petra asked.

"Maps. Half of them true." Sefa crouched, keeping her eyes on the singer, and unwrapped the oilcloth with exaggerated slowness, laying the contents on the flat top of a fallen ward-stone: charts, sketches, the half-key gleaming dull silver-grey. "This came from a man named Oren Vashti, formerly of a town called Mereth, which you will not find anywhere, because it isn't anywhere anymore. He gave me this key and a message for the singers: *the debt has a door.* Do you know the phrase?"

Petra had gone very still. "There's a fragment. In our archive. It mentions a Debt. And a First Door." A pause. "Under a drowned cathedral."

Sefa exhaled. Four years of dead ends, and the end had been waiting behind a line of broken stones the whole time, tending apple orchards. "Well," she said. "Isn't that convenient."

"It isn't convenient at all."

"No," Sefa said. "It never is."

* * *

Later—much later, after the sun had climbed and the meadow shadows had shortened—they sat on either side of the fallen ward-stone like two merchants at a table, and conducted the negotiation that both of them had been circling since the knives came out.

Because the knives had mattered. Petra admitted it freely, in the privacy of their own head: they had walked up that hill intending, if the stranger proved hostile, to use the fork. To do whatever the fork did now that the magic was awake in them again—something they didn't understand, couldn't control, had felt only as a pressure behind the breastbone and a hum in the blood, terrifying and enormous, like a sea behind a door. They had lied to themselves about it the whole way up the hill. *I'll only warn them. I'll only frighten them.* Liars' arithmetic. And they had watched the stranger's hands and known, with a recognition that felt almost like relief, that the stranger was running the same arithmetic. *I'll only threaten. I'll only hold the line.* Two liars, each fluent in the other's language, each reading the other's tells like open text.

"So here's my offer," Sefa said. She spoke the way she drew lines—economically, every word load-bearing. "I know what's outside your valley. You don't. You've never been outside your valley—I can tell by how you look at the horizon, like it's a painting. I know which roads still exist, which towns remember themselves, and which ones have started eating their own names. I can get us to a drowned cathedral, if one exists, which—" she tapped the half-key, "—I now believe it does. In exchange, you get me through whatever's left of your Choir's defenses, and you teach me what the Debt actually is. Because I've been chasing its shadow for four years and I am tired, singer. I want to see its face."

"And what do you get out of it? Truly."

Sefa smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but it was an honest one, which from her was worth more. "My country was unmade when I was nine. I don't remember its name. I remember everything else—streets, songs, my mother's hands—but the name is gone, cut out, and the hole aches like a tooth. If this Debt can take a sky, it can perhaps give one back. Perhaps it can give back other things." She looked away, at the valley, green and oblivious. "I want to know if the world can be unmapped in reverse. That's my price. Hope. Cheap to print, expensive to carry."

Petra was quiet for a long moment. Then they said, "I have conditions. You don't lie to me. Not even the kind lies. Especially not those."

"Done. Same to you."

"And if it comes to choosing—if the Door asks a price—you don't pay it for me, and I don't pay it for you. We decide together or not at all."

Sefa studied them. "You've read the fragment. You know debts like this don't leave room for committees."

"Then we'll find out how much room there is." Petra extended their hand across the ward-stone, over the spread of impossible maps. "Bargain?"

Sefa looked at the hand. Then she took her knife and, with a flourish that was half mockery and half ceremony, laid it flat on the stone beside the offered palm. "Swear on steel instead. Hands shake. Steel remembers."

Petra hesitated—then laughed, a short startled sound, the first laugh in two days, and pressed their palm against the flat of the blade. "Two liars swearing on a knife. My mother would say that's how wars start."

"My mother would say that's how treaties start," Sefa said, gripping the blade's spine with her own hand. "Same thing, usually. With better paperwork."

The bargain sealed itself in the silence that followed—not with thunder, though thunder would have been fitting, but with the small click of two crafts recognizing each other across a table. Petra felt it settle into place the way a keystone settles: everything above it suddenly resting on it.

They gathered the maps. They turned toward the valley, toward the smoke of its chimneys, toward the chapel bell ringing ten.

And it was then that Petra saw them, on the road below the hill: five figures in the grey robes of the Choir elders, walking upslope with the unhurried certainty of people carrying a verdict. Behind them, at a distance that fooled no one, came six more of the Choir's sworn wardens, and the warden at the front carried the long staff of office banded in iron—the staff that meant a judgment had already been voted, sealed, and was now merely being delivered.

"They followed me," Petra breathed.

"Of course they followed you," Sefa said, and her hand had already found her knife again. "You're the only one in that valley who looked up."
