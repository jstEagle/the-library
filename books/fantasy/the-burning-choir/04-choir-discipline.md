## 4. Choir Discipline

The summons came at dawn, written in the old hand on paper that had not been made in the valley for a hundred years. *The Choir convenes. Attendance is discipline. Absence is confession.* Petra read it twice by candlelight, though the words did not change, and dressed in the grey habit they had worn since childhood without once deciding to wear it.

The chapter house still smelled of smoke. Three nights since the stars had gone out and the wards had shattered—three nights of neighbors sweeping glass-fine fragments from their doorsteps and calling it frost, of the baker opening his shutters at the usual hour to a sky like a closed lid and remarking only that it looked like rain. The valley had decided, with the smooth collective motion of a body pulling a blanket over its head, that nothing had happened. Petra alone walked through streets where every lamp burned yellow against a darkness that no longer ended, feeling like the last waking person in a town of sleepwalkers.

Inside the chapter house, the elders sat in their crescent beneath the great east window, which had been bricked over before Petra's birth. No one remembered why. Now Petra thought they might.

There were eleven of them left. Mother Cassian in the center seat, her hands folded like something being kept from escaping. The others arranged around her by seniority, faces lit from below by oil lamps so that their shadows climbed the walls like a second, truer choir. Mother Anselm sat at the crescent's far end, oldest of them all, her white braid coiled at her neck, her eyes half-closed as if she were listening to something the rest could not hear. Perhaps she was. She had always been listening to something.

Petra stood in the center of the floor, where novices stood to be questioned, and waited.

"You saw it," Mother Cassian said. Not a question. "Three nights ago. During the Rite rehearsal."

"I sang the descant," Petra said. "The stars went out mid-verse. The wards came down."

A murmur moved along the crescent like wind through wheat. Cassian raised one finger and it died.

"The wards," she said, "are maintenance structures. Old ones. Like the aqueduct, or the millrace. Things fail. Things are repaired." Her voice was level, reasonable, the voice of a woman explaining arithmetic to a child. "The Consistory has voted. We will treat this as a failure of maintenance."

"Mother, the sky—"

"The sky," Cassian said, "is weather. Weather passes." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the armrests, and Petra understood suddenly that the folding had been the effort, and this was the truth underneath. "The valley holds. The valley has always held. We will seal the passes, double the watch on the ward-ruins, and wait. When the dark lifts—and it will lift—we will resume the Rite calendar as though nothing occurred. There will be no discussion of stars. There will be no discussion of the Debt."

The word went through Petra like a hook through cloth.

They had found it only yesterday, in the burned archive—a scrap of vellum that had survived the fire that had taken the rest, tucked inside a ledger's binding like a moth pressed flat. *Let the Debt stand against the sky,* someone had written, in a hand centuries dead, *for we have bought the harvest of our children with the roof of the world.* And beneath it, a map. A map of the borderlands, drawn with loving precision, rivers and roads and mountain ranges all rendered faithfully—except for one valley, which had been left blank. Not omitted. Erased. Scraped down to the parchment's skin, so that the country flowed around an absence shaped exactly like home.

Petra had carried that absence under their coat for two days. They carried it into the chapter house now, against their ribs, like a coal.

"There was a Debt," Petra said. "In the archive. It names it."

Something happened to eleven faces at once. It was very small and very fast, and if Petra had not spent twenty years learning to read the Choir—the way you learn to read weather, or water—they would have missed it. But it was there: not surprise. Recognition. Grief, even. And beneath both, relief, the particular relief of people who have been waiting years for a knock at the door.

Mother Cassian's face did none of this. Hers had been prepared.

"The archive burned forty years ago," she said. "Whatever scraps you pulled from it are ash and rumor. The Choir does not deal in rumor." She rose, and the lamplight made her tall, made her shadow reach across the floor and touch Petra's feet like a hand. "Therefore the Consistory rules as follows. The valley is sealed. No one crosses the ward-line. Any who attempt it—" she paused, and chose the words the way you choose stones to throw, "—will be exiled without name. Struck from the rolls. Their songs struck from the canon. Their families will speak of them as one speaks of the dead, and then, in time, not even that. Do you understand what I am telling you, Petra Blackwood?"

"I understand you're afraid," Petra said.

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere above them, behind the bricked-over window, a bird landed on the sill and scratched at mortar it could not see through, and flew away again.

"We are the Choir," Cassian said quietly. "We were given voices so that we would know how to use them together. Alone, a voice is just noise the wind carries off. You are not alone, child. That is the entire meaning of your life. Remember it when the dark feels heavy." She sat. "This session is closed. Go home. Sing the morning office. Act as though the world is intact, and the world may take the hint."

* * *

Petra did not go home.

They walked instead to the edge of the choiryard, where the low wall overlooked the terraced fields falling away toward the river, and stood in the grey unlight watching the valley pretend. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight, obedient columns. A dog barked at some ordinary nothing. Down in the third field, old Hollis was already turning the autumn earth, his mule leaning into the collar, and the whole scene had the terrible competence of people who had done this ten thousand times and intended to do it ten thousand more, dark or no dark.

*Exile without name.* Petra turned the phrase over. It should have frightened them. It did frighten them—but underneath the fear was something worse and stranger: a small, cold lift, like the first breath after being underwater too long. Because the sentence assumed there was a name to lose. It assumed the rolls mattered, the canon mattered, the crescent of eleven faces mattered. And standing at the wall with a scraped-blank map against their heart, Petra found they no longer believed any of it did—not the way the elders believed, not as a shelter. The valley had never been a home. It had been a container. They had lived their whole life inside the shape of someone else's bargain, singing on schedule, and called it belonging.

That was the vertigo. Not the dark sky. The discovery that the floor had been a lid.

"Your stance is wrong."

Petra didn't startle. Twenty years of training had burned the startle out of them; Anselm's voice arrived the way weather arrives, already everywhere. They turned. The old woman stood at the yard gate with her hands in her sleeves, small and straight-backed, her face in the flat light like a page waiting to be written on.

"I wasn't standing any particular way," Petra said.

"You were standing the way you stood the day I found you at the ward-line, age six, with your palms bleeding because you'd been pressing them against the stones to hear it hum." Anselm came forward slowly, her sandals whispering on the flagstones. "You've been listening at doors your whole life, child. Today you finally heard something true. It changes the stance."

"Mother Cassian will have you reported for speaking to me."

"Mother Cassian reported me in the year of the granary fire, and the year the Vessin girl drowned, and the year I taught you the seventh mode, which is not in the canon." Anselm stopped an arm's length away. Up close, Petra could see what the lamplight had hidden in the chapter house: the fine tremor in her hands, the translucence of her skin, the way she breathed between sentences as though each one cost coin. She was dying. Not soon—perhaps—but certainly, and she knew it, and had arranged her face around the knowledge the way the valley had arranged itself around the dark. "I am past the reach of reports. You are not. So listen quickly."

She drew something from her sleeve.

It was a book. Small, thick, bound in grey leather gone soft as skin at the corners—which, Petra thought with a lurch, it probably was; Anselm had owned it longer than Petra had been alive. The cover bore no title. The spine was cracked in three places from decades of the same hands opening it at the same pages.

"Your songbook," Petra said. Every novice knew it existed. No novice had ever seen it. The songbooks of the Choir were supposed to be surrendered at death, returned to the archive, absorbed into the canon. "You can't—you'll be—"

"I will be an old woman who mislaid a book, which at my age is forgivable, expected, and impossible to punish." Anselm pressed it into Petra's hands. It was heavier than it looked. Warm, too, as though it had been held a long time. "Everything the Consistory voted tonight is in there. Everything they refused to say aloud. Everything I was too much a coward to say aloud, for fifty years, while I taught children to sing at a sky we had sold."

The words landed like stones in still water. Petra stared at her. "Sold."

Anselm smiled. It was the saddest thing Petra had ever seen on a human face, and Anselm had once sung the funeral office for Petra's own mother.

"Read the margins," she said. "Not the verses. The verses are the cage. The margins are the key—or half of one. Read them tonight, by candle, and then decide what you are, Petra Blackwood. Decide it before the valley decides for you." She glanced once, swiftly, at the blank eastern sky, and for a moment her composure slipped and Petra saw underneath: terror, old and worn smooth as a river stone, terror she had carried so long it had become her posture, her breathing, her life. "And whatever you choose—leave before the new moon. The Collectors keep schedules. We taught them that ourselves, God forgive us."

"Collectors? Mother, what are—"

But Anselm had already turned away, walking back across the yard with her hands in her sleeves, small and straight and unhurried, an old woman going in to sing the morning office in a valley that had agreed to pretend. At the gate she paused. She did not look back.

"I reported you myself, once," she said, to the air, to the blank sky, perhaps to no one. "Years ago. For humming at the ward-line after lights-out. Did you ever wonder why your punishment was kitchen duty in my wing, rather than the penitent's cell?" A pause. "I have been buying you time since you were six years old. I appear to have run out. Spend what's left well."

Then she was through the gate, and gone, and Petra stood alone in the choiryard holding a dead woman's future weight in their hands—no. Not dead. Dying. Dying, and spending her last authority not on the Consistory, not on the canon, not on the valley she had served for seventy years, but on one exile-not-yet-declared novice with bloodied palms and a scraped map against their ribs.

Betrayal Petra had expected. The crescent had delivered it cleanly, in proper form, with votes and rulings and the comfortable language of maintenance. What Petra had not expected, what they had no shelf inside themselves to store, was this: grace arriving from the exact direction the betrayal should have come from, wearing the same grey habit, walking with the same unhurried step.

They opened the book right there, standing at the wall, because their hands would not wait.

The first page held the morning office, flawless, canonical. The second held the descant Petra had sung since childhood. But the margins—narrow, cramped, ink faded to brown—the margins were another country. Anselm's handwriting changed across the decades, young and sharp, then weary, then fierce again near the end, and the notes ran alongside the sacred verses like water running alongside a road:

*We did not hide from the war. We paid.*

*The Door is real. I have seen the drawings. Under the cathedral, under the water.*

*A voice who paid. A voice who forgot. A voice who stayed. Three, always three. Why three? Who is the third?*

*If you are reading this, I failed to be brave in time. Be brave faster than I was.*

Petra looked up. The valley lay below in its obedient morning, smoke rising, mule turning earth, the whole contained world humming its one note. Somewhere beyond the ward-line, past the sealed passes, the erased country waited with its fragmentary towns and its boy who spoke in borrowed words—though Petra did not know that yet; they knew only that outside the line, everything the Choir had suppressed in their blood for twenty years was awake and rising like floodwater.

And somewhere south, following the same darkness across the dead maps of the world, a woman with half a key and a cartographer's hunger was already walking toward the valley that officially did not exist.

Petra closed the songbook, held it against the scraped map, two secrets pressed together like cupped hands around a flame, and began, quietly, to plan how to betray everyone who had ever loved them—in the only direction left that led anywhere at all.

Outside the wall, at the valley's rim, the first light of a sun with no stars to precede it crept over the ridge, and it looked, Petra thought, exactly like the light at the beginning of a story they had not yet agreed to survive.
