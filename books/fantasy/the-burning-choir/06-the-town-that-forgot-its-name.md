## 6. The Town That Forgot Its Name

They came down out of the hills on the third morning, and the road simply stopped.

Not faded, not washed away—stopped. One moment there was packed clay and wheel-ruts and a milestone worn smooth by generations of thumbs; the next, the road ended in a field of barley as if it had been cut off at the waist. The ruts ran to the edge of the grain and then the grain grew straight through where they should have been, unbroken, indifferent, as though no cart had ever passed this way at all.

Sefa crouched at the boundary and pressed her palm flat against the earth. "It's not that the road was destroyed," she said quietly. "It's that the country stopped remembering it."

"You keep saying things like that," Petra said. "As if a country remembers anything."

"Everything remembers." Sefa stood, brushing dirt from her knee. "Roads remember feet. Houses remember fires. Names remember mouths. You take away the remembering, and what's left isn't emptiness. It's something worse. It's the shape of a thing with nothing inside it." She looked down at the barley, and for a moment her face did something Petra had never seen it do—it looked young, and frightened, and very far from home. "I've mapped countries like this. On paper they look fine. Rivers go where rivers go. But you stand in them and your boots don't quite touch the ground."

Petra said nothing. They were thinking of the valley, of twenty years of wards humming overhead like a held breath, of a life lived inside someone else's strategy. At least the valley had known what it was hiding from. This land had been *eaten*, and didn't know enough to grieve.

They walked the last mile into the village along the edge of the field, because there was no other way to walk.

* * *

The village had a well, a shrine, a smithy with a cold forge, and forty or fifty people who all turned to look at them with the same mild, unfocused courtesy—the look you give travelers when you cannot quite recall whether you were expecting any.

"Welcome," said a woman by the well, drawing water. She was perhaps fifty, with flour dusted up both forearms. "Welcome to—" She paused. Her mouth stayed open a moment, working at nothing, like a door trying hinges that had been removed. Then she smiled again, serene as milk. "Welcome," she said.

"What's this place called?" Sefa asked.

"It has a name," the woman said, with gentle certainty. "Of course it has a name. Every place has a name." She looked at the well, at the shrine, at the sky—which was empty, starless even in daylight, a pale bruised ceiling—and her smile held. "We've always been here."

Petra tried the shrine. It was a stone post carved with interlaced knots, the kind raised at crossroads in half the countries of the old maps, and at its base lay offerings: bread, a copper pin, a child's shoe filled with wildflowers. Someone here still felt devotion. Someone here still knew there was something to be devoted *to*. But when Petra asked an old man sitting in the shrine's shade who the offering was for, he considered the question with real patience, the way one considers a child's puzzle.

"For the one it's always been for," he said.

"And who is that?"

He smiled at them kindly, and Petra understood, with a slow cold turning in their stomach, that behind his eyes there was a hole shaped exactly like an answer, and that the hole did not hurt him, and that this was the most horrifying thing they had ever seen.

They asked the baker what bread was called here. He told them. They asked what the river to the east was called. He told them that too—but when Sefa wrote the words down and showed them back to him an hour later, he read them with polite puzzlement, as though she'd shown him a recipe in a foreign tongue. The words fell out of people like water through cupped hands. Nothing stayed. Nothing accumulated. A town is a pile of shared memory stacked so high it looks like stone, and here the pile had been scattered, and everyone was standing calmly in the wreckage, calling it a village.

"They're not sad," Petra whispered to Sefa as they walked the single street. "Why aren't they sad?"

"Because grief needs a before," Sefa said. "You can't mourn what you can't compare. They're not sad. They're *shallow*." Her jaw tightened. "I told you I map dead countries. This one isn't even dead. Dead would be a mercy. This one's been *skimmed*."

At the far end of the street, where the houses gave out and the barley began again, a boy sat on a fence rail, watching them.

* * *

He was perhaps twelve, barefoot, with the kind of stillness Petra had only ever seen in herons and in Mother Anselm on the mornings before a rite. While every other soul in the village had greeted them with that soft unfocused warmth, the boy watched them the way one watches weather—as something that might turn.

"He's different," Petra murmured.

"He's watching us like he knows what we are," Sefa murmured back. "Which means either we're obvious, or he is."

They approached slowly. Up close the boy was thin and sun-browned, with a smudge of ash on his cheekbone and eyes too old for his face—not hard old, but *full* old, the way a cup is full to the brim and must be carried carefully or not at all.

"Hello," Petra said. "What's your name?"

The boy opened his mouth. What came out was not his voice answering. It was a voice—a woman's voice, warm and worn, speaking words in a cadence that belonged to no one present:

*"Ask me tomorrow, love, and tomorrow, and tomorrow after that."*

Petra went cold. Sefa's hand drifted toward her knife without appearing to move at all.

"That wasn't an answer," Sefa said evenly.

The boy closed his mouth. Opened it again. Another voice this time, a man's, gruff and amused:

*"Names are for selling things and hanging people. Call me what you like."*

"Stop that," Sefa said sharply.

But Petra had gone very still, because they had heard this before—or its cousin. In the Choir, in the oldest exercises, in the training songs meant to teach a storm-caller to hold another voice inside their own without losing the borders of themselves. *Ventriloquy of the soul,* Anselm had called it, and had forbidden its practice outside the deep vaults, because a singer who lost their own name to the exercise could not be given it back.

This boy was not doing ventriloquy. This boy was *made of it.* He had no voice of his own left at all—he spoke entirely in borrowed words, in the salvaged speech of other people, and yet his eyes—

His eyes remembered everything.

"I think," Petra said slowly, kneeling to bring their face level with his, "that you can't say your own name. But you know it. Don't you?"

The boy looked at them for a long moment. Then he reached out and touched Petra's collarbone, once, gently, right over the breastbone where a singer's resonance lives, and spoke in a third voice—an old one, cracked and formal, the voice of someone long dead giving a lecture:

*"The vessel that knows itself may carry the flood. The vessel that does not is merely spilled."*

"He's quoting," Sefa breathed. "He's quoting Choir doctrine. From before the valley. Before the wards." She took a step back. "Petra. Those texts burned. Anselm told us those texts burned eighty years ago."

"The archive burned," Petra said. "That's not the same thing." They kept their eyes on the boy. "Who taught you that? Can you show me? Can you tell me where the words come from?"

The boy's mouth worked. His face strained—Petra could see the effort of it, a swimmer fighting a current, reaching for a word that belonged to himself and finding only the sea of everyone else's. Finally he exhaled, and gave up, and simply pointed: at the empty sky, and then at the shrine, and then—at the last—straight at his own chest, and spoke in the warm woman's voice again, softer now:

*"He keeps everything. That's his trouble. Everything goes in and nothing comes out that's his."*

"Whose voice is that?" Petra asked. "Was that your mother?"

The boy flinched. It was answer enough.

* * *

They sat with him through the afternoon, on the fence rail, while the village moved around them in its shallow, pleasant loops. Sefa sketched—she could not help herself, mapping the street, the well, the shrine, annotating each with the words people had used and the words people had lost, building on paper the skeleton of a place whose flesh had been taken. The boy watched her draw and, when she finished, tapped the margin and produced, in a dry scholarly voice none of them had heard yet:

*"A map is a memory with ambitions."*

Sefa's pencil stopped. She stared at the boy for a long time. "Yes," she said finally, roughly. "It is. Who said that to you?"

He shrugged—the first ordinary, childish gesture she'd made him produce—and pointed at his own head, and then made a slow pouring motion, as if decanting.

"He's a vessel," Petra said quietly. "Someone poured into him. Or he caught what was spilled." They thought of the fragment in the burned archive, the word that had started them down this road, written in a hand that shook. *The Debt.* They thought of the dark stars, the shattered wards, the searching presence in the night. And they thought of what Sefa had said in the barley field: *the country stopped remembering.*

"Sefa. If someone wanted to take a country's memory—its whole identity, its name, its sky—where would it go? It couldn't just vanish. Memory doesn't vanish. It's *weight*."

"It gets collected," Sefa said. "Stored. Carried." Her face had gone gray. "There are stories. In my trade we call them sailors' nonsense—beings that harvest what places know. I always thought they were how cartographers explain gaps in the record." She looked at the boy, at his brimming, burdened eyes. "I never thought the gaps might have a keeper."

Petra turned back to the boy and asked, as gently as they knew how, the question underneath all their other questions: "The sky. The stars. Do you know what happened to them?"

The boy went still—that heron-stillness again. He looked up at the blank pale ceiling of the world for a long, long moment. And when he spoke, it was in the gruff man's voice, but slower now, weighted, each word set down like a stone on a grave:

*"Collected. All of them. Every light. Picked clean like a carcass by the roadside, and carried away in jars."*

His own hand rose—his own gesture, his own trembling finger—and completed the sentence in silence, pointing north.

Toward the drowned lowlands. Toward the broken spire visible on the horizon like a needle through the world's skin.

Toward the cathedral under the water.

And then the boy did one more thing. He leaned close to Petra, close enough that they could see their own reflection doubled in his overfull eyes, and spoke in the warm woman's voice one final time—but the words were new, urgent, pressed out of him like blood from a wound, and Petra would spend years wondering whether some corner of that voice had belonged to a mother who had died knowing:

*"They'll come for what he's keeping. They always come for what's kept. Run, singers. Run, and don't look back at what follows you."*

Far to the north, faint and patient, something turned toward the sound of true names being spoken in a forgotten town—and began, unhurried, to walk.
