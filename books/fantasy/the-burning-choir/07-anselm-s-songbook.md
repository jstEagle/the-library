## 7. Anselm's Songbook

The songbook smelled of tallow smoke and river water, and Petra had not dared open it for two days.

It sat on the table between the cold lamp and the window where the starless dark pressed like a hand against the glass. Two days of pretending it wasn't there. Two days of sweeping a floor that didn't need sweeping, of mending a coat that had no holes, of listening to neighbors in the lane say *lovely weather for the Rite* and *the wards will be rebuilt by autumn, you'll see* as though the sky had not been peeled away above them like the lid off a pot. Two days of Sefa Ilves sharpening her knives at the other end of the room with the patience of someone who understood that some doors only open when the person standing before them stops being afraid.

On the third morning, Petra opened it.

* * *

The book was older than Petra had ever guessed. In all their years of lessons—of sitting cross-legged on the choir-house floor while Mother Anselm's shadow fell long across the boards—they had never seen this volume. Anselm had kept her teaching songs on loose sheets, cheerful things about rain and sowing, melodies designed to sit lightly on a child's tongue. This was different. This was bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its spine stitched with thread that might once have been silver, and its pages were dense with notation Petra half-recognized: the storm-caller's staves, but older, wilder, with marks above the lines that looked less like instructions and more like warnings.

*Do not sing past the third rest,* said one such mark, in a hand that was not Anselm's.

*This verse was paid for,* said another.

Petra turned pages with fingers that trembled slightly and hated themselves for trembling. Here were the songs they had learned as children—but wrong, or rather, cut. Every melody they'd been taught appeared in the book whole, entire, running on past the point where Anselm had always stopped them, always with the same gentle laugh: *That's enough for today, little storm. Save your voice.* Petra had thought it kindness. A teacher sparing young throats.

Now they saw the cuts for what they were. Amputations. Someone had taken scissors to these songs the way a surgeon takes scissors to a limb, and the stumps sang silence.

* * *

The marginalia began quietly, the way rot begins quietly.

In the margins of the third song—a round about thunder that every child in the valley could hum before they could write their own name—Anselm's small, precise script: *They will tell you the valley chose to be hidden. Ask them who did the choosing.*

Petra read it three times. The fire needed tending; they did not tend it.

Further in, beside a lament whose final verses had been struck through so hard the paper had torn: *We did not flee the war. We surrendered. There is a difference, and the difference is the sky.*

Sefa looked up from her maps. "You've gone white," she said. Not unkindly. She had a way of delivering observations like a physician delivering diagnoses—flat, factual, already moving to the next thing.

"It's Anselm's handwriting." Petra's voice came out strange. "She wrote in this book. She knew. She—" They stopped, because the sentence that wanted to come out was *she knew and she taught me songs anyway*, and they could not yet bear the shape of it.

"Read me the parts that matter," Sefa said, and went back to her inking, giving Petra the mercy of not being watched.

So Petra read aloud, haltingly, translating Anselm's cramped script into speech while outside the lightless afternoon deepened into a lightless evening:

*The Collectors came in the last year of the war, when the borders were burning and the choirs were dying. They offered what we could not refuse: an end to the killing. Their price was not gold. It was memory—not ours, but the world's. They would take the old country out of the sky itself. No stars over our land. No names written in heaven. And in exchange, the war would pass us by, because a country that cannot be seen from above cannot be marched into.*

*The founders agreed in a single night. One night to sell a sky.*

*But a bargain is a door, and doors swing both ways. Beneath the drowned cathedral there is a Door older than the bargain—the First Door, through which the Collectors themselves once entered. What was given can be sung back. What was sung can be unsung. I have spent my life deciding whether I am brave enough to try, and I have decided, each morning, that I am not. Forgive me, little storm. I am leaving you the decision instead.*

Petra stopped reading. The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the lane a neighbor laughed at something, warm and ordinary, and the sound was unbearable.

"The drowned cathedral," Sefa said slowly. She had set down her pen. "I've charted the lowlands south of here. There's a spire breaks the floodwater at certain seasons—locals call it the Drowned Lady. Nobody goes near it. Nobody remembers why." Her eyes came up, sharp and bright. "Except now we do."

"She knew," Petra said. The words scraped coming out. "All those years. She stood at the front of the choir-house every Founding Rite and sang the same cut songs, and she knew we were singing inside a cage, and she never—" Their voice cracked and they let it. "She gave me the book instead of reporting me. That's grace, isn't it? So why does it feel like being handed a debt?"

"Because it is one," Sefa said simply. "Grace usually is."

* * *

That night Petra dreamed of the choir-house, and the dream was memory, exact and merciless.

They were seven years old again, kneeling on the cold floorboards in a row with the other children, and Mother Anselm walked the line with her hands folded into her sleeves. She had been beautiful then, in the way of old stone—weathered, patient, impossible to imagine young. When she reached Petra she crouched, so that her face was level with theirs, and her eyes were the grey of the valley sky in November.

"Sing me the rain," she said.

Petra sang the rain. It was the first song every child learned, four notes rising and falling, meant to do nothing at all—or so Petra had believed. As they sang, something moved in their chest, a warmth like a swallowed coal, and the lamp flames in the choir-house guttered though the windows were shut.

Anselm's hand closed gently over theirs, and the song stopped. "Again," she said, "but smaller this time. Sing it like a secret. Sing it like you're hiding it even from yourself."

"Why?" Petra had asked, seven years old and honest. "Why hide it?"

And Anselm had smiled—that smile, the one that made every child feel chosen—and said: "Because the best hiding place for a loud thing is inside a person who has learned to be quiet. Now again. Smaller."

Smaller. All those years, *smaller*. Petra woke with the word lodged in their throat like a fishbone and lay staring up into the dark where stars should have been, understanding at last that their entire childhood had been a lesson in containment, and that the kindest person they had ever known had been its most devoted warden.

And yet.

And yet she had pressed the book into their hands in the burned archive's shadow, had said *go south, little storm*, had spent her whole life too afraid to act and had still, at the end, found the courage to arm someone else. Inheritance, Petra thought, staring at the ceiling. It comes to you as a wound. Whether it becomes a weapon is your decision.

* * *

Morning brought Sefa to the table with her half-key laid beside the songbook, and the two objects looked at each other like strangers discovering a shared language.

"I found this in the ruins of a map-house in a country that no longer exists," Sefa said, turning the broken metal so it caught the gray light. "Half a key. I've carried it eleven years without knowing what it opened. Your book mentions a Door."

"The First Door. Under the drowned cathedral." Petra traced Anselm's words: *a lock that wants voices, not hands.* "She doesn't say how it opens. Only that it can."

"Then we have half a key and a full reason," Sefa said. "That's more than either of us had yesterday." She paused, and something flickered across her face—hesitation, almost shyness, absurd on a woman who had held a knife to Petra's throat five days ago. "My country had songs too. Before it was unmapped. I don't remember them anymore. I remember that I used to remember them, which is worse." She closed her fist around the key-half. "If your Door can give back what was taken—I want to be there when it opens. Even if it opens for you and not for me."

Petra looked at her for a long moment. Then down at the songbook, at Anselm's last page, where the writing grew larger, less controlled, as if the hand that made it had finally stopped being afraid:

*Little storm—if you are reading this, then the dark has come, and my cowardice has run out of road. Go to the water. Sing what I could not. And when they ask you who gave you leave, tell them: the one who stayed, who watched, who remembered everything and did nothing until it was too late to do anything but this.*

*You were never taught to be small. You were taught to wait.*

Petra closed the book. Outside, the starless night was lifting into a starless dawn, and far to the south, beyond the ward-ruins and the fields that ended mid-furrow, something beneath the floodwater waited with the patience of stone.

"We leave tomorrow," Petra said. "Before the elders finish sealing the passes."

Sefa nodded once and began rolling her maps.

And in the silence after, Petra heard it—faint, from the direction of the choir-house, threading through the lanes like smoke: the elders' voices raised in the Founding hymn, the cut version, the safe version, singing their valley deeper into its sleep. But underneath it, so low it might have been imagination, another sound. A second melody, walking beneath the first like a river under ice.

Someone else in Stillwater Valley was singing the whole song.

And they were singing it getting closer.
