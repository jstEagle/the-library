## 8. Hunters of the Quiet

The road remembered being a road. That was how Vess found it—by listening to what the ground still knew.

She walked with her boots off, as she always did in harvested country, because the soles of her feet were better instruments than any compass her order had ever built. The earth beneath her was thin with memory now, scraped nearly clean, and what remained came up through her skin in fragments: the ghost-pressure of cart wheels, the phantom warmth of a hearth long since carried away, the dull ache of a name that had been spoken here ten thousand times and was spoken nowhere anymore.

*Harrowmere*, the ground almost said. *Harrow—* and then nothing, like a bell struck once and never again.

Vess stood at the edge of the empty field where Harrowmere had been and let the silence finish its work on her. This was the part of the trade no one sang about. The Collectors took memories, yes—that was the ledger's language, the language of the contracts signed in the years when the world was ending and everyone was desperate enough to sign anything—but what they actually took was *having been*. A town did not lose its recollections the way a person loses a key. It lost the fact that it had ever existed to remember anything at all.

And she had helped. She had walked behind the harvest-wains for eleven years, singing the quieting songs, holding the resonance-forks steady while the older Collectors drew the memory up out of the ground like water from a well. She had been very good at it. She was, she suspected, still very good at it, which was the worst thing about her.

Something was burning in the east. Not fire—the sky there had gone dark weeks ago, starless, a hole punched in the night that the Collectors themselves had made and could not explain. But under the darkness, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, something was *singing*. A songbook, opened. Marginalia read aloud by lamplight. Two voices arguing over an old notation, one of them wrong about the tempo.

Vess turned toward it the way water turns downhill.

* * *

The Collectors kept their chapterhouse in a place that had agreed to forget itself completely—a valley so thoroughly emptied that even its stones had stopped being stones, becoming instead a kind of patient gray fog that held the shape of walls out of habit. Inside, the fog was warm. Inside, the fog listened.

Vess reported to the Choir-Supreme in the Long Room, where the ceiling was hung with bells that had no clappers. They rang anyway, sometimes, when someone lied.

"The songbook is moving," she said. "West of the Stillwater line. Two carriers. One of them sings like trained choir—the old discipline, the pre-War forms. The other doesn't sing at all but hums maps. I've heard her do it in her sleep, through three miles of dead country."

The Choir-Supreme was named Ossian, though he had not used the name in two hundred years; names were the first thing collected, the first payment, and those who gave theirs up entirely were said to be most trustworthy. He sat in a chair that might have been carved from the fog or might have been the fog pretending, and his face was the face of a man who had been handsome once and had then lived too long without anyone to be handsome for.

"Two carriers," he repeated. "Not three?"

"There was a boy in the forgotten village. Tam. He remembers everything but owns none of his own words—he speaks only in what other people have said. I marked him as a vessel, not a carrier."

Ossian was quiet for a moment. In the silence, one of the clapperless bells shivered, very slightly, and went still again.

"A vessel," Ossian said. "You were a vessel once, Vess. Before we finished you."

"I know what I was."

"Do you?" His voice was gentle, which was worse than cruelty. Cruelty you could hate. Gentleness you had to carry. "Tell me what you remember of before."

And this was the trap she walked into every time, knowing it was a trap, walking in anyway, because some part of her believed that if she told the story often enough, carefully enough, she would eventually find the seam in it where her real life was hidden.

"I remember a choir loft," she said. "I remember cold stone under my knees and my mother's voice teaching me descant. I remember a war coming over the mountains like weather. I remember the elders telling us there was a way to survive it, and the price was—" She stopped. She always stopped here. There was nothing after this. The story simply ended, mid-sentence, the way a song ends when the singer's throat is cut.

"The price was," Ossian agreed softly. "Yes. That is all any of us have. We are the price, Vess. We paid ourselves." He rose, and the fog-chair sighed and forgot him a little. "Follow the songbook. Do not take it. Watch who carries it and what they intend. If they mean to reopen the First Door—if they mean to *unpay* the Debt—then you will bring them to me, and we will show them why the bargain cannot be undone."

"And if they won't come?"

Ossian smiled. It was the smile of a man remembering a smile rather than smiling. "Then you will sing them quiet, little vessel. As you were sung quiet. As I was. It is the only mercy left in us, and we spend it well."

* * *

She caught up with them on the fourth day, at the drowned lowlands, and she did not reveal herself. She watched instead from the reed-beds, wrapped in a Collector's cloak of unremembered gray, and she learned more in one evening of watching than eleven years of harvesting had taught her.

The trained singer—Petra Blackwood, the archives called them, last voice of the Stillwater Choir—sat on a fallen spire-stone with a songbook open across their knees, and they were crying. Not elegantly. Ugly, gasping, furious crying, the kind that comes from reading your own history in someone else's handwriting.

"They *knew*," Petra said to the cartographer, Sefa Ilves, whose voice Vess recognized from the humming-through-dead-country. "Anselm knew. She wrote it in the margin like a recipe. *We gave them the sky and they gave us the morning.* Like it was arithmetic. Like it was fair."

"It bought eighty years," Sefa said. She was sharpening a knife against the stone, unhurried. "Eighty years my country didn't get. Don't tell me it wasn't fair. Tell me it was expensive. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," Petra said. "They aren't." And then, quieter, in a voice that went through Vess like a fork through soft earth: "Do you think they knew? The ones who became the Collectors. When they signed. Do you think they understood what they'd stop being?"

Sefa's knife paused. "Does it matter?"

"Yes," Petra said. "It matters to me whether the thing hunting me remembers being a person. Because if it does, then it can grieve. And things that grieve can choose."

In the reeds, Vess put her hand flat against the mud and felt her own heartbeat travel down through it, out into the drowned ground, and she thought: *they're talking about me.* She thought: *I am the monster in their story, and their monster is sitting in the rushes wondering if she can still cry.*

She tested it, quietly, the way you test a numb limb. She reached for grief the way she'd once reached for the high descant, expecting nothing.

It was there. That was the horror of it—it had always been there, buried under two hundred years of professional quiet, intact as a coin dropped in a well. Grief for a choir loft. Grief for cold stone under her knees. Grief for a mother's voice teaching her a line of music that ended, in her memory, mid-phrase, forever mid-phrase, because the rest of the song had been the first installment of the payment.

She could cry. She simply hadn't, in eleven years, because crying was not in the ledgers, and the ledgers were the only self she had left.

* * *

That night she dreamed, which was also not supposed to happen.

She dreamed of the signing. Or—not the signing itself; that was collected, gone, spent into the great gray account. She dreamed of the *after* of the signing, the part that survived because it had happened inside her body and bodies keep poor books.

A hall of young singers. Hundreds of them, kneeling in rows on stone, and the elders moving down the aisles with the resonance-forks, and the sound the forks made—not a sound, really, a *subtraction* of sound, a hole in the air shaped like whatever note you loved best. One by one the singers stood and sang their names, and the forks drank the names, and the singers sat back down with pleasant, emptied faces, already forgetting they had ever had anything to forget.

Her turn had come. She had stood. She had opened her mouth.

And here the dream always offered her a choice it had never actually given her: *sing, or run.* In the dream she ran, every time, barefoot down the mountain road with the whole choir's harmony falling away behind her like snow off a roof, and she woke with her heart slamming and her cheeks wet and no idea, ever, whether the dream was memory or mercy.

Ossian would say it was neither. Ossian would say the dream was residue, the way fat renders from meat, and that a good Collector rendered clean.

But Ossian had clapperless bells in his ceiling that rang when people lied, and Vess had heard them ring once, very softly, during one of his own reports. He had pretended not to notice. She had pretended not to notice him pretending. That was the whole architecture of the Collectors, really: everyone pretending not to notice the ringing.

* * *

On the fifth day she made a mistake.

She had closed the distance too far, drawn by the songbook's resonance the way a moth is drawn by anything bright enough to die on, and when Petra began to practice the opening verse—just the opening, eight bars, barely voiced—the sound went through the dead country like a plow through fallow field, and everything the harvest had left behind *woke up.*

Grass remembered growing. Water remembered running. A dead tree on the floodline remembered blossoming, all at once, out of season, out of time, white flowers opening on black wood like a scream made gentle. And Vess, standing in the middle of it with her hood back and her face bare, felt eleven years of professional quiet crack down the middle—and she *sang back.* Reflex. Training. The quieting song, rising out of her before she chose it, counter-melody to counter-melody, dampening the verse, pressing the waking land back down into sleep.

Petra stopped mid-bar. Turned. Looked directly across the flooded field at the gray figure in the reeds.

"You," Petra called, and their voice carried over the water with terrible clarity. "You're one of them. You've been following us since the lowlands."

Vess did not run. Running was for people with somewhere to be, and her orders were to watch, and watching required only standing. She stepped out of the reeds into the open, hands visible, cloak open, showing them the truth of her: a woman-shaped absence, a person with the outline worn through.

"You woke the ground," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her—rusty, actual, like a door opening in a house everyone believed empty. "Don't do that again. Not until you understand what hears it."

"What hears it?" Sefa had the knife out, naturally, and had moved to flank, naturally, and Vess noted the geometry with the detached approval of a professional admiring another professional.

"Everything we took," Vess said. "Everything we *are.* We were choirs once—all of us, every Collector, whole companies of singers who paid too much and couldn't stop paying. The Debt doesn't end when the contract does. It ends when the last voice that owes it goes silent. We didn't become hunters because we wanted to hunt. We became hunters because the hunger is what's left when a song is taken out of a singer and the shape of it stays." She looked at Petra, and let herself say the true thing, the thing she had carried alone through eleven years of gray country: "I remember a descant. My mother taught it to me. I can't recall a single note of it, but I remember *that there was one,* and the wanting of it has been eating me alive since before you were born."

The wind moved across the floodwater. Somewhere behind Petra, the boy—Tam, the vessel, the one who spoke only in borrowed words—said, very quietly, in a voice that was not his own:

*"Things that grieve can choose."*

Vess flinched. It was her own sentence, thrown back at her in a child's mouth, and it landed like a bell with a clapper after all—one clear, accusing, unavoidable ring.

"That's why I'm telling you this," she said. "Before my order decides for me. The Choir-Supreme is coming. Ossian. He was singing lead in the First Cathedral when the bargain was struck; he has been paying longest, and he has nothing human left except the wanting, and the wanting has curdled. He believes the Door must never open—not because of what it costs, but because if the Debt is reversed, then everything we paid was wasted. Everything we *became* was for nothing." She swallowed. "He is coming with every Collector who can still walk, and he will not negotiate, and he will not grieve, because he has spent two hundred years making sure he can't."

Petra studied her for a long moment. Then, in the tone of someone testing ice: "Why warn us? What do you want?"

And Vess opened her mouth to give the tactical answer, the sensible answer, the answer about leverage and survival—

What came out instead was: "I want to hear the descant. Yours. Anselm's. Whatever's in that book—I want to stand in the front row one more time and listen to a song I'm not allowed to take." Her voice broke on the last word, and she was too tired to be ashamed of it. "I have been the hunter of the quiet for eleven years. Let me be quiet for one hour. That's all. That's the whole of my treason."

Tam spoke again, in someone else's words, words gathered from a hundred forgotten mouths, and they fit the moment so exactly that Vess's knees nearly went: *"Come home. All is forgiven. Come home."*

Behind Petra, Sefa lowered the knife—not all the way, but lowered it—and said, in the voice of a woman making the second-worst decision of her life: "If this is a trap, I'll kill you slow."

"If this is a trap," Vess said, "you'll be doing me a kindness."

Far to the east, under the starless hole in the sky, the horizon had begun to fill with a gray that was not weather. They were coming. All of them. The whole grieving, hungry, hollowed-out company of the paid.

And Vess Ishmael—whoever she had been before the forks drank her name—turned and walked toward the enemy's campfire, carrying the only thing she had left to give them:

a warning, and an hour.
