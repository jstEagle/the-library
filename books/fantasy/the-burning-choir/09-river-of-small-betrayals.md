## 9. River of Small Betrayals

The river had no name anymore. Sefa said so on the second morning of their journey downstream, in the flat voice she used for facts that hurt.

"Every river I've mapped since I left home has been like this. The water remembers its course. The banks remember their shape. But the name—" She crouched at the reedy edge and touched two fingers to the current, as if taking its pulse. "The name goes first. Names are the lightest thing a country owns. They blow away before anything else does."

Petra stood behind her with their arms crossed against the cold coming off the water and thought about how much they hated this woman's habit of saying true things in a voice like a ledger being read aloud.

They had been traveling for six days now, out past the ward-ruins, out past the fields where roads ended mid-stride like sentences no one finished. The songbook rode against Petra's chest in an oilcloth wrap, warm as a sleeping bird. Tam was three days behind them somewhere—no, ahead, they'd decided; the boy moved like weather, appearing where he pleased—and Vess was nowhere and everywhere, a silence shaped like a person walking parallel to them through the world.

It should have been a good morning. The sun had come up the color of hammered brass, the first honest sunlight Petra had seen since the stars went out, and the magic that had flooded back into them at the valley line still hummed under their skin like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. They could feel the river's water table through their boots. They could feel the pressure of rain gathering over hills they couldn't see. Twenty years of suppression, and now every hour brought some new sense online, some new way of hearing the world's wet machinery turn.

So it should have been a good morning. Instead Sefa had spread her maps on a flat stone at the campsite and was correcting them, and Petra had leaned over her shoulder to look, and that was how it started.

"What's this?" Petra asked, tapping a small notation in the corner of the oldest sheet—a symbol like a closed eye drawn in ink so faded it was almost a stain.

Sefa's hand stopped moving.

"That's nothing," she said. "A surveyor's mark."

"It's not a surveyor's mark." Petra knew cartography well enough by now to know what they didn't know, and they knew this wasn't it. "I've seen it before. In Anselm's margins. It's a Collector sign."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the river to say something, which rivers do when people don't—the small persistent argument of water over stones, indifferent and eternal.

"Take your hand off my map," Sefa said quietly.

"Tell me what the mark means."

"I said take your hand off—"

"Or what?" Petra heard their own voice come out harder than they intended, edged with something new, something the returning magic had sharpened in them without asking permission. "You'll draw a knife on me again? We're well past that, Sefa. We crossed the line together. You watched me drown in reverse. Whatever we are to each other now, we're not strangers bargaining at knifepoint."

"No," Sefa said. "We're worse than that."

She rolled up the map with a snap that cracked the morning open, and Petra saw her hands were shaking—not with fear, they realized, but with the effort of holding something inside that wanted very badly to be out.

* * *

They walked all day without speaking, which is its own kind of conversation, and a cruel one. By late afternoon the river bent east into a country of drowned orchards—trees standing hip-deep in black water, their fruit gone soft and ghostly pale, feeding fish that fed herons that stood in the shallows like patient clerks waiting for accounts to settle. The drowned lowlands were beginning. Somewhere ahead, under all this water, the cathedral waited with its spire breaking the surface like a drowning hand.

Petra made camp on a shelf of dry ground between two orchards and built a fire small enough to hide, and it was there, staring into the flames, that Sefa finally broke.

"You want to know about the mark," she said. "Fine. I'll tell you about the mark. But you have to understand that everything I'm about to say is true, and that truth is the only currency I have left, and I am spending all of it right now, and you will never be able to give it back to me."

"Sefa—"

"My country was called Ilvenmark." She said the name the way you set down something heavy: carefully, with bent knees. "You won't have heard of it. That's rather the point. It was north of everywhere, a country of fen and birch and salt marsh, and it had the finest mapmakers in the world because we were a nation of people who needed to know exactly where things were—we'd lost things before. Plagues. Wars. A whole century where the sea took a mile of coast every generation. Mapping was how we held on. My family had kept the great survey for nine generations. I was apprenticed to my grandmother at six years old. I could draw the Ilvenmark coastline from memory with my eyes closed. I did draw it from memory, later, with my eyes closed, over and over, because drawing it was the only way to keep it."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the flooded orchard, a heron lifted off with a sound like a door opening.

"The Collectors came in my nineteenth year," Sefa went on. "Not armies. Nothing so honest as armies. They came the way rot comes into timber—from inside, along the grain. First the outlying villages stopped answering letters. Then the letters themselves started arriving blank. Then people started forgetting, village by village, and the forgetting spread along the roads like oil along cloth, and the government—such as it was—did the only thing governments ever do. It bargained. It paid. It gave them the eastern provinces, then the western ones, then the capital, buying time the way you buy time with anything: badly, and on credit."

"And you?"

"I ran." Her voice didn't waver, but it went thin, stretched over something enormous. "I ran south with my grandmother's survey rolls strapped to my back, and I told myself I was saving them, saving the country in paper if not in fact. And then in a border town called Vessik—before Vessik forgot itself, while it still had three days left of being itself—I met one of them. Not Vess. Another one. Older. Its face was like a face you'd seen in a dream and couldn't place. And it looked at my maps, at the survey rolls, at nine generations of my family's work, and it made me an offer."

Petra felt cold that had nothing to do with the evening.

"It offered me passage," Sefa said. "Out. South, past the reach of the harvest, to lands the Collectors hadn't gotten to yet. Safe conduct. Food. All of it. In exchange for copies of the survey rolls. Because here is the thing nobody tells you about the Collectors, Petra: they don't just take memory. They *use* it. Memory is how they navigate. A map of a country is a map of everyone who ever remembered it, and my grandmother's survey was a net thrown over ten thousand minds. Every town marked on those rolls was a town they could find. Every road was a road they could walk down into the dark of someone's head."

"You gave them the rolls." Petra's voice came out flat and terrible.

"I gave them the rolls." Sefa met their eyes, and her own were dry in the way that eyes get when crying would be a mercy and she wouldn't allow herself one. "My country died either way. It was dying while I copied those maps. But I told myself the difference didn't matter, that Ilvenmark was already a ghost and ghosts can't be murdered twice. I told myself I was just a survivor. I have told myself that every day for eleven years, and every day it has sounded less like the truth and more like a coin I bite to check if it's real."

"The mark," Petra said. "On your old sheets. What is it?"

"A receipt." Sefa laughed, and it was the worst sound Petra had ever heard from her, worse than any cry—a laugh with nothing in it, a laugh like an empty room. "That's what the closed eye means in their accounting. *Paid.* I drew it myself, on every sheet I sold them, so I'd know which ones were clean of my hands and which weren't. Eleven years, Petra. Eleven years of selling maps of countries that were going dark anyway, telling myself I was only selling what was already lost. And then I found your valley on a Collector's route list—your little valley with its wards and its stolen sky—and I came here, and I told myself this time was different. This time I was selling nothing. This time I was buying something back."

"Were you?" Petra asked. "Buying something back? Or were you running again, with a different bundle on your back?"

The words landed like a slap, and Petra was glad, in some small vicious part of themselves, to have landed it—and ashamed of that gladness in the same breath.

"I don't know," Sefa said simply. "That's the honest answer. I don't know what I am anymore. I know I led you out of the valley. I know I haven't sold anything since Vessik. I know that when I look at your songbook I feel something in my chest that isn't hunger or fear, and I don't have a word for it, because the language I'd have used for it is buried under a fen in Ilvenmark." She reached up and pulled the cord at her neck, and from beneath her shirt she drew a small brass tube, battered, sealed with wax. "Half a key. I told you I carried half a key. I never told you where I got it. My grandmother gave it to me the night before the Collectors reached our town. She said: *when you find the door, and you will find the door, because doors are the last things countries leave behind—when you find it, give back more than you took.*"

She held it out across the fire.

Petra looked at it and did not take it. Their fury was a live thing in their chest, huge and simple and clean: *she fed countries to them, she fed the darkness its own road map, she may have fed it ours.* And underneath the fury, worse, was the vertigo of realizing that every kindness Sefa had shown them—the knife-point bargain, the wary truce, the shared watchfires—now had to be re-read, re-weighed, re-priced. Trust, once repriced, never returns to its old value.

"I can't do this," Petra heard themselves say. "I can't walk beside someone who—do you understand what they are? Do you understand what they took from Tam's town, from your country, from mine? And you handed them the addresses."

"Yes," Sefa said. "I understand exactly what they are. Better than you do. You've seen them for weeks. I grew up under them." She set the brass tube down on the flat stone between them, deliberately, within Petra's reach. "Keep it or throw it in the river. Either way, tomorrow I'm walking to the cathedral, because my grandmother told me to give back more than I took, and the debt is the only inheritance I have left. You can come or not. I'm done lying to you, which means I'm done pretending I can make you stay."

She rolled into her bedroll with her back to the fire, and Petra sat alone with the brass tube and the flames and the enormous slow anger of the water going by in the dark.

* * *

The ambush came an hour past midnight.

Petra woke not to sound but to absence—the fire's crackle suddenly wrong, the night insects gone silent in a spreading ring, the way a room goes quiet around a held breath. They were on their feet before they were fully awake, the songbook clutched to their chest, and they saw it at the orchard's edge: a figure standing hip-deep among the drowned trees, and it was not Vess.

This one was older. Its outline kept trying to be a person and giving up, like a word on the tip of the tongue. Where its face should have been there was a smoothness that nonetheless suggested features the way fog suggests hills. Around its feet, the black water had gone perfectly still, mirror-still, and in that stillness Petra could see reflections that didn't match—reflections of orchards in blossom, of a village with lit windows, of a coastline with a name.

*Ilvenmark,* Petra thought, and understood: the thing was wearing the memory of what it had eaten, the way a snake wears the bulge of its meal.

"Sefa!" Their voice cracked across the water. "Sefa, wake up, it's—"

The Collector moved, and moving, it was fast the way falling is fast. It crossed the flooded orchard in three strides that didn't splash, and the water where it stepped didn't ripple, and Petra felt it reaching—not for their body, for the *songbook*, for the resonance of Anselm's marginalia, a hook of attention sinking toward them through the air like a fisherman's line finding flesh.

Petra did the only thing twenty years of suppressed training had left them. They sang.

It wasn't a verse. It wasn't words. It was raw note, storm-note, the first sound their magic had ever made before the Choir taught it manners—and the Collector flinched. Actually flinched, its outline shivering like a reflection disturbed. Sound, Petra realized with a lurch. Sound was memory made audible. Sound was the one medium the Collectors couldn't quietly erase, because to hear was already to keep.

But one raw note wasn't enough. The thing recovered, and its attention came down on Petra like a weight of deep water, and Petra felt memories start to loosen—not big ones, small ones, the edges first: the smell of the Choir dormitory soap, the color of Anselm's second-best shawl, the name of the boy who'd shared their table at twelve years old. They felt these things sliding, going soft, going—

—and then Sefa hit the Collector with a burning brand from the fire.

It was, objectively, an absurd gesture. Fire meant nothing to a thing like that. But it startled the creature for half a heartbeat, half a heartbeat was all anyone ever got, and in that half-heartbeat Sefa shouted: "The water! Petra—it wears reflections—break the water!"

And Petra understood.

They dropped to their knees at the flooded orchard's edge and drove both hands into the black water and *sang at it*—a chord, a rising third, the simplest working the Choir taught children, the one that made rain puddles tremble. The whole flooded orchard shivered. The mirror-stillness shattered into ten thousand broken fragments of reflected blossom-village-coastline, and the Collector, which had been standing on its own stolen memories, suddenly had no floor. It staggered. Its outline tore like wet paper. And in the gap, Sefa was there with the knife—not striking at the body, which wasn't a body, but cutting the air in a precise line, a cartographer's line, a border, and where her blade passed, Petra felt the creature's attention shear away like cloth cut from cloth.

The Collector recoiled to the orchard's far edge and stood there, ragged, regarding them. When it spoke, its voice was many voices slightly out of sync, and one of the voices was young and northern-accented and afraid.

"*Ilves,*" it said. "*Cartographer. Your account remains open.*"

Then it folded sideways into the dark between the trees and was gone, and the insects came back all at once, roaring, and the river resumed its small eternal argument with the stones.

* * *

They sat by the rebuilt fire until dawn, close enough that their shoulders touched, neither of them willing to be the one to move away.

"It knew my name," Sefa said at last. "My family name. I haven't used it in eleven years."

"It keeps accounts," Petra said. "Vess talked like that too. Like everything was owed."

"Everything is owed, to them. That's the whole of their religion." Sefa turned her hands toward the flames. They were steady now, or steadier. "When I sold the rolls, the one I dealt with told me: *nothing is given, everything is traded, and the trade is always fair.* I believed it for years. Fair. As if fairness could be measured in what survives instead of what's lost." She was quiet a moment. "You sang tonight. At the water. I've heard trained singers my whole life—well, I heard them, before—and none of them could have done that. Broken a hundred yards of still water with a nursery rhyme."

"It's not a nursery rhyme. It's the first working. It's what they teach you when you're seven, before they teach you control." Petra hesitated. "Anselm used to say the first working was the truest one. Everything after is just fear wearing technique."

"Your mentor sounds like my grandmother."

"They'd have liked each other," Petra said, and was surprised to find it was true, and surprised more by the ache that came with it.

The brass tube still lay on the flat stone where Sefa had left it. Petra picked it up now, weighed it, and held it out.

"This is yours. Your grandmother gave it to you."

"She gave it to whoever reached the door." Sefa didn't take it. "I'm not the one who can sing. I'm the one who knows the way. Keep it until we're under the water. Then we'll see whose hands it fits."

Petra closed their fingers around it. The metal was cold and then, slowly, wasn't.

"I'm not forgiving you," Petra said. "I want to be clear about that. What you did—those countries, those people—they're not abstractions to me anymore. I've seen Tam. I've seen what's left of a town when the Collectors finish the shopping list your maps helped write."

"I know."

"But." Petra stared into the fire, hunting the exact shape of the truth. "When it came out of the trees, you didn't run. Eleven years of running, and you didn't run. You grabbed a stick and drew borders in the air and fought a thing that eats nations, for someone who five hours ago told you they were done with you." They shook their head slowly. "I don't forgive you. But I trust you. Tonight I learned those aren't the same thing, and I learned I'd rather have the second one anyway."

Sefa was silent for a long moment. Then she laughed—short, rusty, real this time, the first real laugh Petra had ever heard from her.

"Tactical forgiveness," she said. "The Ilvenmark school of diplomacy. My grandmother would approve. She always said the best alliances are the ones nobody's proud of."

Dawn came up gray-gold over the drowned orchards, and the water lay quiet, and far to the east, visible now between the trees for the first time, a spire broke the surface of the flood—slender, dark, hung with dead ivy, tilted like a needle in the world's fabric.

Neither of them spoke. Neither of them needed to. They broke camp in silence, side by side, and took the first steps of the last approach together.

Behind them, in the mud of the abandoned fire-ring, Petra noticed a single footprint that hadn't been there before. Narrow. Deliberate. Facing the cathedral.

Vess had found them. Vess had stood here in the night, watching them fight, watching them choose each other—and had walked on ahead.

Toward the Door.
