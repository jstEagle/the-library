## 10. The Drowned Approach

The land began to lie to them three days before they saw the water.

It lied gently, at first. A road that bent where no road needed to bend, curving around the ghost of a hill that had been quarried away a century ago—or so Sefa said, tapping her pencil against the blank place on her chart where the hill should have been. A field of barley growing in rows that spelled nothing, that were simply too straight, too obedient, as if the ground itself were trying to remember what order looked like and getting it wrong. A fence that ran for a mile and then stopped, mid-air, its posts planted in nothing, and beyond the fence more barley, and beyond the barley the lowlands, gray and breathing.

Petra had learned to read the wrongness the way they had once learned to read weather: by the feel of it against the skin, the small electric prickle at the nape of the neck. Magic pooled in the low places now, seeping up through the soil like groundwater, and where it pooled, the world grew uncertain. A crow flew overhead and cast two shadows. Petra watched the second one lag behind the first, a half-step slow, and decided not to mention it. There were things Sefa didn't need to hear, and things Petra didn't need to say aloud to make them more true.

"Water's rising," Sefa said anyway, on the morning of the third day. She stood at the crest of a levee that some dead generation had built with more confidence than skill, her map-case slung across her body like a bandolier. "It's not rain. There hasn't been rain. The land's *letting it in.*"

Petra climbed up beside her. Below, the lowlands spread out in a sheet of dull pewter, broken here and there by the crowns of drowned trees and the upper floors of farmhouses, their windows dark and full of reflected sky. The flood stretched to a horizon that shimmered, and out of the middle of it—impossibly far, impossibly tall—rose a single gray spire, slender as a needle, tipped with a crooked iron cross that caught no light at all.

The drowned cathedral.

Petra had seen it in Anselm's marginalia, in a woodcut so old the ink had gone brown. They had thought the picture exaggerated. It had not. If anything, the spire looked *smaller* than it should have been, the way a mountain looks small from a distance, and Petra's body understood before their mind did: the building beneath the water was vast. A city had been built around it once. The city was under their feet, under the flood, under the silt, and the water had risen to keep it company.

"It's real," Petra said, and hated how their voice shook.

"You thought I was lying?"

"I thought *everyone* was lying. It's a reasonable default." They pulled Anselm's songbook from their coat, careful of the damp, and turned to the page where the marginalia thickened into something like a route: *follow the bell-line; the bells remember the way down.* "There's supposed to be a road. Under the water. The old processional road. Anselm wrote that it stays dry."

"Roads don't stay dry under ten feet of flood."

"Roads that are *remembered* do." Petra met her eyes. "You map countries that don't exist. You tell me what remembering can do to a road."

Sefa looked at the spire for a long time. Wind moved her hair off her forehead, and in the flat gray light Petra could see all the small ways she had been worn down—crow's feet, a scar through one eyebrow, the permanent squint of someone who had spent her life looking at horizons that weren't there anymore.

"I've seen a bridge hold because everyone on it believed it would," she said finally. "I've seen a city gate that wouldn't open for a man who'd forgotten which city he came from. So. Yes. Maybe the road remembers." She shouldered her pack. "And maybe we swim. Either way, we walk until the water tells us to stop."

They walked down into the flood country, and the water told them to stop only once, at their ankles, and then, grudgingly, let them pass.

* * *

The processional road revealed itself the way a fish reveals itself in murky water: not by appearing but by the disturbance around it. The flood shallowed over a long, straight causeway of black stone, and where the stone ran, the water lay flat as poured glass, holding the drowned sky in perfect stillness, while on either side the flood churned with a wind that never reached the road itself. Walking it was like walking through the memory of walking—each step landing exactly where ten thousand processions had landed before, their feet finding the worn hollows in the stone without being told.

Sefa kept stopping to sketch. "The stone's *singing*," she said at one point, crouched with her palm flat against it. "Feel that? It's not a hum, it's—words. Too worn to make out. Like a hymn sung by a congregation of ten thousand years."

Petra could feel it too, faintly, through their boot soles: the road murmuring its old processional, *come, come, come*, worn down to a single syllable. They thought of the Choir's discipline, the hours of unison, the way a hundred voices singing the same note stopped being a hundred people at all. They thought of how much of their life had been that note, held so long it had become silence.

They made camp that night on a rise where the causeway widened into a plaza, its paving stones printed with the faded ghosts of a labyrinth. Petra took first watch and sat with the songbook open on their knees, reading Anselm's handwriting by the light of a lamp that burned with a small, apologetic flame. The marginalia had grown strange in the last pages—tighter, faster, the letters leaning as if written on a moving cart:

*The Door does not want a key. It wants a witness. Three kinds: the one who paid, the one who forgot, the one who stayed. We were all three once. That was the whole of our power, and we spent it.*

*We were all three once.*

Petra read the line four times. Around them the flood lay motionless, and far out, the spire stood against the stars—or against the place where stars should have been. The dark up there was total now, a black so complete it had texture, like felt. Petra had stopped looking at it, mostly. Tonight they made themselves look.

Somewhere in that dark, the old country's sky was folded up like a cloth. Somewhere in that dark, whatever had been paid for their valley's survival was still being counted.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said from her bedroll, without opening her eyes. "Where you read and go rigid and I have to guess whether you've found something terrible or just a spelling error."

"Both, possibly." Petra closed the book. "The Door needs three voices. Someone who paid. Someone who forgot. Someone who stayed."

"And us?"

"I don't know yet. I keep hoping the answer is that we're none of them."

Sefa was quiet a moment. "I'm the one who forgot," she said, in the flat voice she used for facts. "My country isn't unmapped, Petra. It's *unmade*. There's not even a blank space where it was. Blank spaces are honest." She rolled over. "Whatever the Door wants from that, it can have it. I stopped grieving it years ago. There's no floor to that well."

Petra wanted to say something kind. They said nothing, which they were learning was sometimes the kinder thing, and kept the watch until the water turned silver with a dawn that had no sun behind it, only a general brightening, like a memory of light.

* * *

They heard the boy before they saw him.

It was mid-morning of the second day on the causeway, the flood stretching monotonous on either side, when the singing started—thin, cracked, a child's voice carrying impossibly across the water. Sefa's hand went to her knife. Petra's went to the songbook. Then they both heard what the boy was singing, and neither of them moved.

It was the litany of the forgotten town. *The miller's name was Oskar. The miller's name was Oskar. The well was dug in the year of the two comets. The well was dug in the year of the two comets.* The same handful of sentences, over and over, in a voice that was hoarse from days of it.

He came around the bend of the causeway at a stumbling run, barefoot, soaked to the waist, his lips gray with cold—and he was walking on the water. Not on the glass-flat road. On the churned flood beside it, his feet finding purchase on the surface with every step, as if the water were remembering him right back.

"Tam," Petra said.

He saw them and his face did something complicated—relief and terror and the particular blankness of a boy holding too many borrowed words to have room for his own. He reached the causeway and his legs simply stopped working; he sat down hard on the wet stone and kept singing, because he did not know how to stop, until Petra knelt and put a hand on his shoulder and said, gently, "You can rest now. We'll hold the names for a while."

The singing stopped. The silence that followed was enormous.

"I walked," Tam said. His voice was ruined, a whisper dragged over gravel. "I walked out. On the road. Only there wasn't a road, only I *remembered* one, so there was." He looked up at Petra with the terrible lucidity she had seen in him before, the lucidity of someone carrying a weight no child should be asked to lift. "The town started forgetting me back. Did you know it does that? If you remember too hard, it forgets you harder. Mama couldn't say my name this morning. She looked right at me and asked if I'd eaten." His chin trembled. "So I left. Before she forgot the leaving too."

Petra gathered him in before they had decided to, and he came stiffly, the way children do when they have been brave too long, and then not stiffly at all. Over his head, Petra met Sefa's eyes. She had not put her knife away. She was looking at Tam the way she looked at unmapped territory: with hunger and fear in equal measure.

"You walked on remembered feet," Sefa said quietly. "Across open flood. For three days."

"Four," Tam said into Petra's coat. "I counted the dawns."

* * *

They did not speak of the Door while the boy ate. Sefa had hard bread and dried fish and a flask of something that claimed to be tea; Tam consumed all of it with the mechanical thoroughness of a body catching up on its own maintenance, and then, abruptly, fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, in the middle of a sentence about the well with the two comets.

Petra caught him before he toppled and laid him out on their own bedroll, and stood looking down at him for a long moment.

"He shouldn't have been able to do that," Sefa said. She had her chart spread out and was not looking at it. "A boy with no training, no discipline, no—nothing. He walked four days on a road he invented. The Choir spends twenty years hammering children to make them capable of a *fraction*—"

"He remembers," Petra said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. He remembers, and out here, remembering *works*." They looked at their own hands. Under the skin, faint as veins of ore in dull stone, the old light moved when they breathed. Twenty years of the wards had taught them to keep it down. Out here, past the ward-ruins, it rose in them like water finding its level, and every day it was harder to press flat. "The Collectors took the sky. But they didn't take what people paid with. It's all still here. In the land. In him. In—" they flexed their fingers— "in us. It's just waiting for someone to call it by name."

"Then we're close." Sefa rolled up the chart. "The spire's a day out, maybe less, if the causeway holds."

They stood together at the edge of the plaza while the boy slept. The flood lay flat to the horizon. The spire had grown in the night, or seemed to; it rose now with a terrible intimacy, close enough that they could see the bells in the belfry, hanging motionless in their frames, each one furred with a century of drowned silt. *Follow the bell-line,* Anselm had written. *The bells remember the way down.*

"Petra." Sefa's voice had changed. Petra turned. She was staring not at the spire but at the water beside the causeway, at a place where the glass-flat surface had begun, very slowly, to ripple—outward, in rings, from a point far below.

"Something down there is waking up," she said. "Or something down there knows we've arrived."

Tam stirred in his sleep and, without waking, began to hum—the first notes of a song Petra had not yet taught him, a song that existed nowhere but in Anselm's book and in the sealed dark beneath the water, waiting to be sung.

The bells, far out in the belfry, shivered once, though there was no wind.

All of them, together, without being struck.
