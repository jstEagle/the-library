## 11. Doors Below

The spire broke the water a mile out, black against the grey of dawn, and Petra could not stop looking at it.

It leaned. That was the first thing. Not the proud vertical thrust of the cathedral engravings in Anselm's songbook—those had shown a tower rising like a held breath—but something bent, as if the building had been trying to look down at its own foundations for three hundred years and had slowly, patiently succeeded. The flood had taken everything else. Rooftops showed here and there where the water ran shallow, dark triangles like the backs of sleeping animals, but the spire alone stood clear of the surface, and it stood *crooked*, and somehow that was worse than if it had fallen.

"You keep staring," Sefa said. She was re-lashing the coracle's frame with wet cord, her fingers moving in the quick economy of someone who had repaired boats in worse places than this. "Staring doesn't get us across."

"I'm deciding whether to be afraid yet."

"Decide faster. The water decides for you otherwise." She nodded toward the shallows, where the flooded lowlands spread out flat and featureless to the horizon. There were no waves. That was the second thing Petra noticed about the water, after the leaning spire: it did not move. A lake this size should have breathed at least a little—wind-chop, current, the slow swell of some distant rain. But the surface lay smooth as poured wax, and their wake behind the coracle faded not by spreading but by simply ceasing, each ripple stopping mid-curve like a sentence cut off.

"It's holding its breath," Tam said from the bottom of the boat.

He sat wrapped in Sefa's spare cloak, his thin legs drawn up, his eyes on the water with an expression Petra was beginning to recognize as his particular kind of courage—not fearlessness, but fear that had been thoroughly catalogued and filed away. He spoke little, and when he spoke it was often in borrowed words, phrases he'd gathered from other people the way other children gathered stones. But sometimes, lately, a word came out that was only his, and those words landed heavier than all the rest.

"The whole sea is one held breath," he said. "Like before a song."

Petra looked at him. "Is that yours?"

The boy considered. "I think so. I think I made it just now." And there was such quiet astonishment in his face—at himself, at the fact of having made anything—that Petra had to look away, back at the spire, because the alternative was to weep in front of a child who would file that away too.

They crossed through the morning. The water stayed shallow enough in places that drowned fences rose beneath them, row after row of pale posts marching into the deep, and once—a church bell, hanging from nothing, its rope trailing upward into air as if the tower around it had been drunk rather than destroyed. Sefa charted as they went, because she charted everything; her hands were never still unless they were drawing. She marked the fence lines, the bell, the place where the depth suddenly fell away beneath them and the water went from green-glass to a blue so complete it seemed less like color than like absence wearing color as a disguise.

"There," she said quietly. "That line. That's the old nave wall. We're over the roof now."

Petra felt it before Sefa said it—a pressure in the chest, a hum in the teeth, the same sensation that had woken in them the night the stars went dark, but inverted, denser, like standing inside a struck bell. Their magic, which had slept twenty years behind the wards and then woken wild and unmanageable when the wards shattered, settled here into something almost like attention. The valley's stolen magic was below them. It was not dormant. It was *waiting*, the way a choir waits in the dark before the precentor lifts a hand.

* * *

They moored the coracle to the spire itself, looping rope around a jutting gargoyle whose face the water had worn to a suggestion—a brow, a hint of a mouth, an expression Petra decided was patient and Sefa decided was smug. They'd argued about it. It was the kind of argument they had now, small and constant and oddly comforting, two people who had learned each other's rhythms well enough to quarrel about gargoyles.

The descent began at a ledge below the waterline where the cathedral's flank offered a broken window, tall as a door, its glass long gone. The water beyond it was clear in a way water should not be—clear as air, clear as the space between stars—and Petra could see straight down into the drowned nave: pillars going down like the legs of some enormous wading creature, shafts of light falling in slanted columns, dust or silt or something finer than either drifting through them like snow that had forgotten which way was up.

"We dive," Sefa said. "We follow the central aisle. The Door should be under the high altar—the maps I've seen of the old country put the First Doors at the founding-stones, and founding-stones go under altars, always. The builders knew what they were sealing."

"How deep?"

"Farther than you'll like. Shallower than you'll fear." She checked the length of cord she'd brought, coiled at her hip, its end weighted with a scrap of iron. "We stay on one line. We breathe when we can and we don't panic when we can't. Tam—" She stopped.

Tam was already looking at the water with an expression of perfect calm. "I don't have to breathe much," he said. "I forget to, usually. Forgetting is mostly what I am." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather, and it went through Petra like a fishhook.

"You're staying on the boat," Sefa said.

"I'm coming." And then, in someone else's voice, some dead priest's cadence Petra would never trace: "*Suffer the small ones to come unto the deep places.*" He shrugged. "I didn't make that up. Somebody here says it all the time. Says it right under us. All day, every day, forever." He tilted his head, listening to something none of them could hear. "She sounds tired."

* * *

The water took them gently, which was somehow more frightening than violence would have been. There was no cold shock, no resistance—Petra slid beneath the surface the way a hand slides into a glove, and the world above—the boat, the leaning spire, the flat white sky—bent and wavered and became another country seen through glass.

Down was easy. Down was almost willing. Petra swam the central aisle between the pillars, and the light fell around them in columns, and the drowned cathedral opened above and below like a throat. Fish moved through the windows—through the empty frames where glass had been, fish moved freely where congregations once filed in, silver shoals threading the stone tracery, and Petra thought: *this was meant to be beautiful*. Someone built this to make human beings feel small in a holy way, and the water had finished the work, and the holiness remained, and the humans were gone.

Their magic woke fully at forty feet.

There was no gentleness to it this time. It came up through Petra's chest like water through a cracked hull, and for a moment they hung in the blue middle-distance with their mouth open and their heart slamming, feeling every note they had ever been trained to suppress rise at once—the storm-song, the keening-song, the low names for wind that the elders had beaten out of them with silence and discipline and the flat statement *we do not sing that here*. Twenty years of containment, and then the wards broke, and since then the magic had been a wild animal pacing in a too-small room. Here, in the drowned nave, it lay down. Here it recognized something.

*You're home,* it seemed to say. *You're late.*

Petra's vision spotted at the edges. They kicked for the light.

* * *

The high altar came up out of the gloom like a mountain range seen from the air—no, like a table set for a meal no one would ever eat, because it was still laid. Candles in stone holders, unburned. A cloth over the altar-stone, heavy with silt but folded with care. Chalices. A book, swollen to twice its size, its pages fused into a single block of pulp. Everything arranged, everything waiting, three hundred years of patience crystallized into an interrupted service.

And beneath the altar-stone, reached by steps that descended into a round chamber floored with black stone, the First Door.

Petra had imagined it a hundred ways since Anselm's marginalia had first named it—in their imagination it had been vast, a gate for giants, iron-banded, terrible. It was none of those things. It was human-sized. It was, in fact, almost modest: a door of dark wood gone black with age and water, set in a frame of the same black stone as the floor, carved with a single line of script that circled the frame like a wreath. The letters were old-country letters. Petra couldn't read them. But they could feel them, the way you feel a chord before it's played—a pressure, a readiness, a held breath.

And in the center of the door, where a handle should be, was the lock.

Three rings of metal, nested one inside another, each ring etched with lines that might have been script or might have been musical notation—Petra genuinely could not tell, and suspected the distinction was a later invention. The rings did not turn. They did not need to. As Petra watched, treading water in the round chamber with Sefa beside them and Tam floating above the steps with his hair moving like weed, the innermost ring *shivered*. Just once. Like a plucked string dying.

"It knows we're here," Sefa whispered. Her voice in the water was strange—carried not through air but through the stone itself, arriving as vibration in Petra's jaw and breastbone. Sound traveled strangely down here. Everything they said, the door heard.

"How does it open?" Petra asked, and felt their own question buzz through the black floor and out into the dark beyond the walls, out into whatever waited there.

Anselm's marginalia had been maddeningly specific and uselessly vague at once: *three voices, the old payment reversed, sing what was taken back into the world.* Three voices. Petra had assumed it meant three singers, three bodies, three throats. They had spent the whole journey south half-dreading the arithmetic of it—that they would arrive and find themselves short, that the door would demand a third stranger, that they would have to trust some drowning farmer or wandering Collector with the last verse of everything.

Now, floating in the round chamber, Petra read the lock properly for the first time.

Each ring bore not one inscription but three, set at thirds around the circle like stations. And beneath each inscription, in the old script that Petra could not read but suddenly, horribly, *understood*—the way you understand a lullaby in a language you never learned, the way the melody carries the meaning whether or not the words are foreign—each station named its singer.

The outer ring: **the one who paid**. The one who gave something up. The one whose hands signed the bargain, whose throat sang the sky away, whose descendants carried the debt like an inheritance of ash.

The middle ring: **the one who forgot**. The one from whom the memory was taken—or who gave it, or lost it, or sold it. The hollowed country. The unmapped name. The boy who speaks only in other people's words.

The innermost ring, smallest, closest to the keyhole of the world: **the one who stayed**. The one who remained behind the wards. The one who kept the valley. The one who lived their whole life inside the lie because the lie was the only shelter anyone would give them.

Petra read it three times. The water pressed close. The shivering ring stilled, waiting.

"No," Sefa said. She had read it too; of course she had; she read everything. "No, it wants three different people. Three voices. That's the whole point of a lock like this—you don't let one person hold every key."

"It doesn't want three people." Petra heard their own voice come out flat and strange, vibrating through the floor. "It wants three *truths*. Look at the stations again. Look at what they actually say."

Because that was the thing, the thing Petra had been circling since the words resolved, the thing they had been trying not to understand: the stations weren't descriptions of strangers. They were descriptions of a single life, split into three.

*The one who paid.* The Choir paid. The founders sang the sky into the Collectors' keeping, and every storm-caller born afterward inherited the account. Petra's magic was the debt, living and breathing. When Petra sang, they paid.

*The one who forgot.* Petra had spent twenty years in the valley being taught not to remember. Not the old country—they'd never known it—but their own magic, their own nature, the entire first language of their own soul. The wards hadn't just kept the valley in. They'd kept Petra *out* of themselves. What was that, if not forgetting? What was exile-without-name, the elders' punishment, if not the valley's own disease turned inward?

*The one who stayed.* Petra had never crossed the valley line until this season. Petra had obeyed. Petra had sung the Founding Rite every year of their life, had believed in the safety, had defended the wards to Sefa with genuine anger in the early days of their partnership—*they kept us alive, you don't know what it was like outside*—had stayed, and stayed, and stayed.

One life. Three stations. The lock didn't need three strangers.

It needed one person who contained all three truths, and two witnesses to sing it true.

"They're all me," Petra said. The words left them and the chamber took them and the door's rings trembled, all three at once now, like a dog hearing its name. "The one who paid—the Choir's debt runs in my blood, my voice is the payment. The one who forgot—I forgot myself for twenty years, they saw to that. The one who stayed—I stayed. Gods help me, I *stayed*, I loved the ward-wall, I called it home." Their voice cracked, and the crack rang through the black stone and came back changed. "It's not a lock that needs three keys. It's a lock that needs one person broken exactly three ways."

Tam drifted down the steps to float at Petra's shoulder. He looked at the door with his head tilted, listening.

"She's still saying it," he reported softly. "The tired lady under the floor. She says: *it was never meant to be easy, only possible.*" He paused. "That one's hers. That one's really hers."

Sefa had gone very still. In the blue light her face was unreadable, cartographer's face, the face she wore when a map revealed terrain she didn't want shown. "Then you sing all three," she said at last. "And we stand witness. That's the shape of it."

"That's the shape of it." Petra turned in the water, looking at the door—at the modest, patient, terrible door that had waited three hundred years for someone broken in precisely the right configuration. They had spent the journey believing they were walking toward a destiny. Everyone always said *destiny* like it was a gift, like it was a crown, like the chosen one gets to feel chosen. Standing before the First Door, Petra understood at last what the word had always actually meant.

Not a gift. A burden, fitted to your shoulders at birth, that you spend your whole life walking toward so you can finally set it down—or lift it.

"I used to think finding the Door would mean I was special," Petra said. The confession came out small, swallowed instantly by the water and given back to them through the stone, so that they heard it as if someone else had said it. "Chosen. Marked. All the songs about it—the hero comes to the door and the door *wants* them, and that wanting makes them worth something." They touched the outermost ring, and it was cold, and it shivered under their fingers like a lip about to speak. "But it doesn't want me. It *fits* me. The way a wound fits a knife. Every terrible thing that ever happened to me—the wards, the exile, the forgetting—it's all just... joinery. I was built for this lock. Piece by piece. My whole life was the carving."

Tam put his hand over theirs on the cold metal. His grip was stronger than it looked, and warm, impossibly warm, down here in the breathless blue.

"The tired lady says," he began, and stopped, and started again, and this time the words were nobody's but his own, plain and young and true: "Being the key isn't the same as being locked. You get to turn."

Above them, far above, through three hundred feet of patient water, the flat white daylight dimmed—as if a cloud had passed, except there were no clouds anymore, there was nothing up there anymore but the empty socket where the sky used to be.

And in the dark beyond the door, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the black stone floor like a note sustained by a throat that never tires, Petra heard it begin: the opening verse. Hummed. Waiting. Asking, in the old tongue they somehow knew, a single question:

*Who will pay? Who will forget? Who will stay?*

And from somewhere above—far above, at the surface, at the rim of the drowned world—came a sound that did not belong to the water or the cathedral or any of them.

Oars. Many oars, moving in time.
