## 12. Vess Descends

The water in the nave had gone still.

Not calm—still. There was a difference, and Petra felt it in their teeth before they understood it in their mind. Calm water breathed with the current, shifted with the moon, carried the small gossip of fish and silt. This water had stopped listening to anything but itself. It lay across the drowned cathedral floor like a sheet of black glass laid over a sleeping face, and beneath it the pillars rose pale as bones, and between the pillars the light of Sefa's lamp moved like something alive that did not wish to be seen.

They had made camp on the gallery above the transept, in what had once been the singers' loft. The stone benches were still carved with the names of choristers dead four hundred years, and Tam had spent the evening running his fingers along them, reading aloud in borrowed voices—the voice of a woman long dusted, the voice of a boy his own age who had drowned in this very room when the river came. He could not make sounds of his own. Every word he owned belonged to someone else first. But he gave each name its voice back anyway, one by one, like returning coats left behind at a party no one remembered attending.

Sefa sat cross-legged with her maps spread around her, the half-key from her pack resting on the oldest of them, the one where the valley was a blank space shaped like a held breath. She was redrawing the coastline from memory, the way she did every night now, though she never said why and Petra never asked. Some rituals explained themselves if you let them stay silent long enough.

Petra sat apart, near the broken rose window, holding Anselm's songbook open against their knees. The pages were soft as cloth from a lifetime of hands. In the margin beside the opening verse of the Reclamation—*We paid the sky, we paid the sky, we bought the morning with the night*—Anselm's handwriting crawled small and urgent: *It is not a payment you make once. It is a door, and doors swing both ways, and whoever stands in the doorway must choose which way the wind blows through them.*

Petra had read those words a hundred times since the valley. They had thought they understood them. They had been wrong, the way you are always wrong about a wound until it is your own.

Below them, through the flooded arches, the First Door waited. They had seen it that afternoon on the first dive: a slab of green-black stone taller than three men, set into the cathedral's eastern wall where the altar had been, carved with a lock unlike any lock—a ring of three hollows, each hollow shaped like an open mouth. One mouth for the voice that paid. One for the voice that forgot. One for the voice that stayed. The divers' lamps had slid across its face and the carvings had seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it, and Tam, watching from the boat, had pressed both hands over his heart and said, in his grandmother's voice, *There it is. There is the throat of the world.*

Tonight they would go down again. Tonight, if the songbook told true, Petra would sing the opening verse into the water and see whether the mouths would open.

And somewhere above them, coming down through the dark in slow spirals, something else was singing too.

* * *

Vess had been descending for six hours.

She did not think of it as falling. Falling was what bodies did; she had not been certain she had a body in the way other people meant it. She thought of it as being drawn, the way a plumb line is drawn down—not by weight but by truth, by the simple physics of finding what is level in a crooked world. The resonance of the songbook pulled at her the way north pulls at a needle, and she had stopped resisting it days ago, somewhere over the flooded lowlands, when she had understood that resistance was just another way of being moved.

She came down through the cathedral's broken tower, hand over hand along stones that had been saints once, before the Collectors unmade their faces. Her kind had done that. She knew it the way you know your own signature—without pride, without horror anymore, the way a knife knows cutting. The Collectors had been human once. That was the first thing they took from you, the knowledge of it, and the last thing you ever got back, if you ever got it back at all, was the memory that there had been a time before the taking.

Her name had been taken thirty-one years ago, in a village whose name had been taken forty years before that, in a country whose name had been taken so long ago that the maps had healed over the absence like skin over an old burn. She had been a child then. She remembered—no. She did not remember. She had been *shown* fragments, during training, the way you show a dog the thing it is meant to fetch. A kitchen. A woman's hands kneading bread. A song in a language that tasted of rye and smoke. These were not memories. They were bait, hung inside her by her handlers so that she would know what she was hunting: anyone who still kept such things whole.

The Collectors called it harvest. The word was chosen carefully, the way all their words were chosen, to make the taking sound like agriculture instead of theft, like the memory-keepers were wheat and the Collectors merely the season. Vess had used the word herself for thirty-one years. It was only lately, tracking the songbook's resonance south through the drowned country, that the word had begun to taste wrong in her mouth, the way a familiar stair tastes wrong underfoot when one step has rotted.

She paused on a ledge halfway down the tower and looked out through a window that had lost its glass centuries ago. The sky was wrong. Everyone said so now—the sky was wrong, the stars were gone, the darkness up there was not night-dark but absence-dark, a ceiling where a heaven should be. Vess had looked at that absence every night of her service and felt nothing, because feeling had been the second thing they took. But tonight, hanging in the black ribs of a drowned church, she looked up through the empty window at the starless dark and felt something move in her chest that she had no name for, because they had taken the names too.

It might have been grief. It might have been hunger. With her kind, the two had always been difficult to tell apart.

She continued down.

* * *

In the singers' loft, Petra heard the descent before they saw anything—a scrape of boot on stone, high up, rhythmic, patient. Then silence. Then the scrape again, closer, circling.

"Sefa." Petra's voice barely carried. "Someone's coming down."

Sefa looked up from her map with the particular stillness of a woman who has been hunted before and has learned that panic is just prey behavior with better posture. She closed the map. She put the half-key in her boot. She picked up her knife—not to fight, she would say later, but to have something honest in her hand.

"Collectors?" Tam asked, in the voice of the drowned boy.

"Or worse," Sefa said. "Company."

The shape resolved at the mouth of the loft stairs the way shapes do in bad light: wrong first, then human, then wrong again. Vess stood in the archway with lamplight sliding off her gray coat, and for a long moment none of them moved, because all of them were doing the same arithmetic—how many seconds to the water, how deep, how fast can a thing like that swim—and all of them arrived at the same answer, which was: not fast enough.

"You have it," Vess said. Not a question. Her voice was flat and precise, an instrument tuned to a single note. "The book. The old woman's book. I have followed its voice for nine days."

"It doesn't belong to you," Petra said.

"Nothing belongs to anyone. That is the first lesson." Vess took one step into the loft, and the lamplight found her face, and Petra saw that her eyes were the color of weather—gray shot through with something darker, moving, like clouds seen through clouds. "Give me the book and I will let you walk out of here. I am permitted to say that. I have never said it before. I want you to understand what it costs me to say it."

"Why?" Sefa's knife was low and steady. "Why let us walk?"

"Because the book wants to go where it's going," Vess said, "and things that want are dangerous, and I am tired."

The last two words came out of her like splinters, like things that had worked loose from a wall. She seemed surprised by them herself. Her hand went to her collar—to a collar, Petra realized, a thin band of dull metal fused to her throat, seamless, scarless, grown there—and her fingers touched it and fell away as if burned.

"The book," she said again, and the note in her voice reassembled itself, flat and single. "Now."

"No," Petra said.

And because Petra Blackwood had never in twenty years of enforced silence done the brave thing at the right time—they had done the reckless thing at the wrong time, which was nearly the same thing and much more useful—they opened Anselm's songbook to the first page and began to sing.

* * *

*We paid the sky, we paid the sky,*

*we bought the morning with the night.*

*Four hundred winters underground,*

*four hundred summers without light.*

Petra had sung the verse before—in the valley, as a child, in the choir rows where the magic slept in them like a stone at the bottom of a well. They had sung it a hundred times and felt nothing but the ordinary lift of harmony, the ordinary ache of a minor key. That was what the wards had been for, they understood now. Not to cage the storm-callers. To mute them. To turn a nation's thunder into lullabies.

But here, under a sky with no stars, above a door with three mouths, the verse came out of them whole.

The sound filled the loft the way water fills a hull. It moved through the stone. It moved through the standing flood below, and the black glass of the water shivered into rings, and the rings spread outward from the eastern wall where the First Door stood, and the drowned pillars began, faintly, to hum—each one pitched to a different line of the verse, the whole cathedral revealed as an instrument that had been waiting four hundred years for someone to play it.

And Vess screamed.

It was not a battle cry. Petra had imagined, in the long nights since the wards broke, what a Collector's attack would sound like, and it had sounded like wind, like teeth, like the tearing of paper. It had not sounded like *this*—this raw, cracked, human sound, torn out of her as if the song had reached into her chest and taken hold of something buried there and *pulled*. She dropped to her knees on the stone. Her hands went to her collar and clawed at it. The band of metal glowed a dull red where her fingers scrabbled, and it did not break, but beneath it, beneath her skin, something was breaking, and everyone in the loft could hear it happening.

"What did you do?" Sefa had her knife up, then lowered it, confused, because Vess was not attacking. Vess was *weeping*, silently, terribly, the way people weep who have forgotten how and are remembering all at once.

"I sang," Petra said. "I only sang."

"No." Vess's voice had come apart into layers. Underneath the flat Collector note there was another voice, older, rougher, a girl's voice from a kitchen that smelled of rye and smoke. "No—you *reminded* me. Do you understand what you've done? Thirty-one years. Thirty-one years I have been—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "My name. I remember my name."

The loft went very quiet. Even the humming pillars seemed to lean in.

"Say it," Tam said gently, in the voice of the old woman whose name was carved on the third bench. "Names want saying. That's the whole trouble with them."

Vess took her hands from her eyes. She looked at each of them in turn—the cartographer with her honest knife, the boy made of borrowed words, the singer with the book—and when she spoke, her voice was small and ruined and entirely her own.

"Vess," she said. "My name is Vess. It was my mother's mother's name. There was bread. There was a stove. There was a window with frost ferns on it, and my sister drew a bird in the frost with her fingernail, and I have not thought of that bird in thirty-one years and now I will never stop thinking of it." She laughed, or sobbed, or both; the sound had not decided. "Do you know what they do? They don't take everything. They leave you the *shape* of a life, a hollow, so you keep walking. So you keep hunting. You hunt the thing you lost in everyone who still has it."

"The harvest," Sefa said quietly. "You call it harvest."

"We call it that because the alternative word is *theft*," Vess said, "and theft requires a thief, and a thief requires a self, and we are not permitted selves." She looked down at her glowing collar, and her expression turned grim and clear. "It will close again. Whatever your song opened, it opens only while the song is in the air. When the singing stops, I will be theirs again, and I will do what I was sent to do, and I will not be able to stop myself, and I will remember afterward. That is the cruelest part. We always remember afterward."

"Then don't let it close," Petra said. "Help me finish the verse. Help me open the Door."

Something moved across Vess's face—fear, real fear, the first honest emotion she had worn in three decades—and she shook her head slowly.

"You don't know what you're asking. You've read the old woman's margins. You think the Door is a payment you reverse, a debt you cancel, a sky you buy back." She got to her feet, unsteady, one hand braced against the carved names on the bench. "I have stood on the other side of that Door. My handlers send Collectors down here every generation, do you know why? Not to guard it. To *listen*. The Door is not sealed. It is *swinging*. Slowly, over centuries, like a gate in a wind you can't feel. And everything that comes through it from the other side—" she gestured upward, at the starless dark, at the whole wrong ceiling of the world— "*comes through it.*"

Tam made a small sound. In the voice of the drowned boy, he said: "The stars weren't collected."

"They were *spent*," Vess said. "Paid through the Door. And the Door opens both ways, singer. Everything your founders pushed through it to buy their war—every star, every season, every name of the old country—it didn't vanish. It's all still *there*, on the other side, pressed against the hinge. Waiting. And the Collectors—" her hand rose to her collar again— "we are what leaks through when it swings. We are what the other side sends back instead of change. You sing that Door open wide enough to take your sky back, and yes, the stars return. And everything else comes home too. Everything that has been pressing on the other side of the wall for four hundred years."

The lamplight guttered. Below them, through the floor, the First Door hummed with the residue of the verse, and the three carved mouths seemed, in the shifting light, almost open.

"So it's a choice," Sefa said. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around the knife. "A starving country, or whatever's been starving on the other side. Is that it?"

"It's a door," Vess said simply. "Doors don't choose. Singers choose." She swayed, and caught herself, and her eyes were already graying over, the weather closing back in, the flat note rising in her throat like water rising in a lock. "It's closing. I can feel it closing. Listen to me quickly—when I am hers again, when the handler's voice comes back, run for the water, dive, don't fight me, I cannot be fought, only *outlasted*. The verse takes time to work. The Door needs all three voices. And the elders of your Choir are two days behind me and they know exactly where you are, because I let them follow me. I thought I was herding you." Her mouth twisted. "Perhaps I was. Perhaps I was leading you to the only place you could survive. I no longer know which of us is using which, and that, I think, is what mercy looks like from where I stand."

"Vess—" Petra started forward.

"Don't." She raised one hand, and it was already steadier than it had any right to be, already someone else's hand. "Sing. Sing it soon, and sing it loud, and when I come down those stairs with killing in my face, remember that somewhere underneath the gray there is a girl drawing a bird on a frosted window, and she is rooting for you." Her eyes found Petra's and held them for one last unclouded moment. "The Door opens both ways. Make sure you're standing on the side you can live with."

Then the weather closed over her face completely, and the flat note settled into her voice like a stone settling into a well, and Vess straightened, and looked at them without recognition, and said, in a voice like a blade being sheathed:

"You have the book. Give it to me."

* * *

Below them, far down in the black water, the First Door shivered on its ancient hinges—and from somewhere on the other side of the world, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something answered.
