## 13. The Price Read Aloud

The stair down into the drowned cathedral was a spiral of black stone, and the water came with them.

That was the strangest part, Petra thought, as they descended hand over hand along a rope slick with ten centuries of silt. The flood had not simply filled the nave like water filling a cup. It had *followed* them—drawn down into the deep places as if the cathedral itself were drinking, as if the building remembered being thirsty. Their lantern light bent strangely through it, and every surface they passed wept a slow condensation that smelled of old incense and river mud.

Sefa went first, knife between her teeth when she needed both hands on the rope. Tam came last, walking where there was no floor to walk on, his feet finding purchase on steps that had crumbled away decades before he was born. He did this without comment. He had done many impossible things without comment since they'd pulled him dripping from the flooded lowlands, and Petra had learned not to ask. Asking made him reach for words, and reaching for words cost him something they could see him paying.

Behind them, far above, Vess held the stair.

They had left her at the third landing with her sword drawn and her back to the dark, because she had said—I remember my name, she had said, and then, quieter: *I don't know what I'll do if I hear the Collectors coming and I'm standing next to you.* It was the most honest thing anyone had said to Petra in weeks, so they had honored it.

Now the stair ended, and the First Door stood before them in the flooded dark.

It was smaller than Petra had imagined. All their life, the First Door had been a thing of sermon and rumor—a gate beneath the world, vast as a mountain's mouth. But this was only a door, human-scaled, set into a wall of stone that was not stone at all but compressed centuries, strata of prayer pressed hard enough to fossilize. The door itself was pale wood gone gray with drowning, banded in bronze gone green, and across its face ran three keyholes arranged like the points of a triangle.

In the center, where a fourth point would close the shape, there was nothing. A smooth blank place, worn concave by some ancient touch.

The half-key in Sefa's pocket grew warm as they approached. She drew it out—a jagged length of bronze no longer than a finger—and it pulled toward the upper-left keyhole like a compass needle finding north.

"One who paid," Sefa murmured. "One who forgot. One who stayed." She looked at the two lower holes, then at Tam, then at Petra. "Anselm's notes said the lock wants voices, not keys. But the mapmaker who cut this half of the key thought otherwise."

"Maybe both," Petra said. "Maybe the key is just the first word."

Tam drifted past them both and laid his palm flat against the blank center of the door.

He shuddered. Not the small shudder of cold water. His whole body seized once, like a struck bell, and when he turned back to them his eyes were wide and wet and full of a terror so pure it seemed almost holy.

"It's listening," he said. And then, because he could not say what he meant in his own words, he borrowed someone else's—an old woman's voice, cracked and kind, the voice of some grandmother in the town that had forgotten its name: *"Come in, children. Supper's getting cold."*

* * *

The Door opened onto a room that should not have existed below the waterline.

It was dry. That was the first obscenity of it—the air inside warm and still, tasting of dust and lamp oil, as though the flood had never dared cross this threshold. The chamber beyond was round, low-ceilinged, lit by no source Petra could find, and its walls were covered edge to edge in writing.

Not carved. Not painted. The words moved.

They crawled across the stone in slow constellations, gathering and dispersing, and as Petra stepped through the doorway the nearest cluster of them lifted off the wall entirely and hung in the air, rearranging itself, waiting.

"The Door shows the price," Sefa said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for maps of countries that no longer existed. "Anselm wrote that much. She didn't write how."

Petra reached out—not touching, but near—and the hovering words settled into an order:

**THE SKY WAS SOLD TO BUY THE HARVEST. THE HARVEST FAILED. THE DEBT STANDS.**

"I know," Petra whispered. "We know all this. Show us something we don't—"

The words scattered and reformed:

**TO REOPEN THE BARGAIN IS TO UNMAKE IT. WHAT WAS GIVEN IN MEMORY WILL BE RETURNED IN MEMORY. THE SINGER WILL CARRY ALL OF IT. EVERYONE ELSE WILL CARRY NOTHING.**

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled through fathoms of held-back water, they could hear—or imagine they heard—the distant percussion of Vess's blade meeting something that did not want to be met.

"Everyone else," Sefa said slowly. "Define everyone."

The walls obliged:

**EVERY SOUL WHO REMEMBERS THE OLD COUNTRY WILL FORGET IT ENTIRELY. ITS NAME. ITS SONGS. ITS GRAVES. THE WARS THAT DROWNED IT AND THE HANDS THAT SOLD IT. ONLY THE SINGER KEEPS. ONLY THE SINGER MOURNS.**

Petra read it twice. Three times. The words did not change, though the crawling script never stopped moving, letters trading places like soldiers on watch.

"Well," Sefa said at last, into the silence. "That's tidy. That's very tidy. You sing, the sky comes back, and the whole country dies a second death—this time in the one place it still lived." She laughed, one short bark with no humor in it. "And you keep it. Alone. Forever. Do you understand what forever is, Petra? I do. I've mapped countries that lasted less time than your grief will."

"Sefa—"

"No." She rounded on them, and her eyes were bright and furious and afraid. "No, you don't get to do the noble face yet. Listen to me. I sold maps to the Collectors to stay alive after Ilves was unmade. I know exactly what it costs to be the last person who remembers a place. I wake up every morning and I have to *decide* to keep carrying it, and some mornings the deciding takes until noon. You're twenty-three years old. You think you can carry a whole nation?"

"Somebody has to."

"*Somebody* has to breathe too!" She caught herself, dragged a hand through her wet hair, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to something rawer. "There has to be another way. There's always another way; I've found passages on maps that weren't drawn, I've—"

"There isn't," said Tam.

They both turned. He was standing against the far wall, and the moving words had gathered around him like moths, and he was reading them with an ease that frightened Petra more than anything the Door had shown them. He understood this place. Whatever the Collectors had left of him, whatever they had taken, they had left the part that spoke to doors.

"You could split it," he said, in the voice of Sefa's dead cartographer father—Petra knew it was his because Sefa flinched. "Three singers. A third each. That's what the founders did, the first time. That's why the Debt took so long to come due—they paid in thirds, and thirds are small, and small payments buy small time."

"And now?" Petra asked.

"Now the Door wants it whole." He shrugged, a boy's shrug, terrible in its simplicity. "One singer. One keeper. One archive."

"Then it's me," Sefa said immediately. "I've already lost a country. I'm already halfway hollow. Take the rest, it's—"

"You'd be singing *my* country's names," Petra said. "You don't know them. You can't give what you don't—" They stopped. Because that wasn't true, was it. Sefa had spent six weeks learning Anselm's songbook by lamplight, mouthing syllables in the dark of their camps, mapping the old country the only way left to map it: note by note. She knew more of it than any living soul except Petra and the elders.

Which was exactly the problem.

"If you sing it," Petra said quietly, "you burn out everything you built. Every map you ever memorized. Ilves. Your father's voice. All of it goes into the working and comes out as sky, and you won't even know to miss it." They shook their head. "And I would know. That's the worst part, Sefa. I'd stand there afterward remembering your father's voice while you smiled at me like a stranger."

Sefa opened her mouth. Closed it. For once in her life, the defector who always had three exits mapped had none.

* * *

It was Tam who broke the stalemate, and he broke it the way he broke everything: gently, and at the worst possible moment.

"I should do it," he said.

"Don't," Petra said.

"I'm already mostly gone." He said it without self-pity, which was somehow infinitely worse. He counted on his fingers, a child's gesture. "The Collectors took my town's name first. Then my mother's lullabies. Then my own name—I got it back from Vess, mostly, but it's thin, it's like a coat that doesn't fit anymore. What's left?" He spread his hands. "Other people's words. Borrowed rooms. If I sing, I lose things that are already lost. It's efficient. My grandmother used to say waste was a sin." He smiled, and it was an old woman's smile on a boy's face. "*Waste not, want not, little bird.*"

"That's not sacrifice," Petra said, and their voice cracked on it. "That's just dying with extra steps. Tam—you're twelve years old. You're supposed to grow up and forget things *slowly*, the normal way, one summer at a time. Not—all at once, for a country you barely—"

"I remember it better than you think." And his voice changed—not borrowed this time, but his own, young and steady, and for a moment the flat affect fell away and he was only a child standing in a dry room under the sea. "I remember the harvest songs. The elders sang them wrong on purpose so the wards wouldn't hear. I listened under the table. Nobody ever listens to children; that's how I kept so much." He looked up at them. "If I sing, I keep nothing. But if *you* sing, Petra, you keep everything, and I've seen what keeping does to people. I've seen the Collectors. They were choirs once. They kept too much, and it turned them into—that." He gestured upward, toward the dark and the hunters and the hunger. "I'd rather be nothing than become hungry."

The words hung in the warm dry air.

Sefa sat down abruptly on the floor, back against the wall, and put her head in her knees. "This is insane," she said, muffled. "We're arguing over who gets to be erased. This is what victory looks like. Two liars and a ghost-child divvying up oblivion like bread."

Then she lifted her head, and her eyes had gone sharp again, the cartographer's eyes, hunting for the flaw in the terrain.

"Wait," she said. "The Door said *the singer keeps*. It didn't say the singer keeps *everything*." She was on her feet now, crossing to the wall, jabbing a finger at the crawling text. "Look. Look! *What was given in memory will be returned in memory.* Given. Past tense. The founders gave the sky away in three voices. The Debt is three voices deep. So the return payment—" she spun to face them, triumphant and terrified in equal measure— "the return payment might take three voices too. One verse each. The Door reads the whole song as one singer because the song *is* one. We pay together, and maybe—maybe the keeping splits along the seams."

"Or maybe it doesn't," Petra said. "Maybe we each lose everything and the sky stays shut."

"Yes," Sefa agreed cheerfully. "Or that. Shall we find out?"

Above them, faint but unmistakable now, came a sound that froze all three of them: a long, grinding groan of stressed stone, and beneath it, rising through the held-back water like bubbles through honey, a chorus. Wordless. Patient. Many-throated.

The Collectors had found the cathedral.

And with them, distantly, another sound—human voices, raised in the rigid unison of Choir discipline. The elders had come too. Of course they had. Petra could almost hear Mother Anselm's absence in it, the hole where her contralto should have been.

"They'll try to stop us either way," Sefa said, already moving to the door, knife out. "So decide fast. Who sings first?"

Petra looked at the Door—at the three empty keyholes, at the blank worn place at the center where a fourth point should be. And the answer arrived not as a decision but as a recognition, the way a tune arrives after years, whole and inevitable:

One who paid. One who forgot. One who stayed.

"Tam first," they said. "He paid most and kept least—his verse opens the lock. Then Sefa—she forgot a country; hers holds it open. Mine last. I stayed. I finish it." They swallowed. "And if the keeping doesn't split—if the Door was lying, or if we read it wrong—then it lands on me, because I'm last, and I'll carry it. All of it. That part isn't a bargain. That part you don't get to argue with."

For a moment Sefa looked like she would argue anyway. Then she crossed the room and gripped Petra's shoulder, hard, once, the way you grip a rope over a drop.

"Together, then," she said. "Badly, probably. But together."

Tam was already standing before the Door, small hands rising, and around him the crawling words on the walls began to gather, to lean inward, to listen—

—and the chorus above them swelled, and the first Collector's shadow slid down through the flooded stairwell like ink poured into milk, and the First Door, patient as debt itself, waited to be sung open.
