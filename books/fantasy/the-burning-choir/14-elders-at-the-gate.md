## 14. Elders at the Gate

The floodwater had gone still.

Petra noticed it first as a wrongness in the sound of the drowned cathedral—the spire's broken bell no longer chimed against the water, the ripples that had lapped the flooded nave for a day and a night simply stopped, as if the whole lowland were holding its breath. They stood on the cathedral's western gallery, where the stone had crumbled into a stair of rubble rising just above the surface, and Petra could see their own reflection in the flat grey water: a thin figure in a soaked coat, a songbook wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and behind them, faint and impossible, the reflection of stars that did not yet exist.

Sefa saw it too. She had her half-key out, turning it over in her fingers the way she did when she was thinking instead of speaking. "Water doesn't go quiet on its own," she said. "Something told it to."

"Or something's coming," Tam said. He sat with his back against a fallen pillar, knees drawn up, hands folded in his lap like a child at lessons. He had barely spoken since the Door showed them the price—since it had shown him, specifically, how little of him there was left to lose. When he did speak now, it was often in borrowed voices, fragments of sermons or market cries or his mother's lullabies, whatever words his ruined memory could still reach. But this he said in his own voice, small and clear: "Something's coming."

Petra felt it before they heard it. A resonance in the sternum, old and familiar, like a tuning fork struck in another room. The Choir. Not one voice—a chorus of them, disciplined and harmonized, moving through the drowned lowlands in the particular cadence storm-callers used when they wanted their magic to carry without being heard by anyone who wasn't trained to listen.

Petra had been trained to listen since before they could walk.

"They followed the songbook," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. "The marginalia. Anselm's handwriting has a signature in it—I told you, every mapmaker leaves a signature. Hers is in every verse."

"I know." Petra's mouth was dry. "I hoped they'd be slower."

"Hope is not a strategy," Sefa said, but gently, for her. She tucked the key away and drew her knife—not threatening, never threatening anymore, just present, the way a lamp is present in a dark room—and moved to the narrowest point of the gallery stair, where one person could hold against many. "How many?"

Petra closed their eyes and listened. "Nine singing. One silent."

"One silent," Sefa repeated. "That's your elder."

"That's worse than nine," Petra said. "That's Mother Anselm."

* * *

They came out of the mist an hour past dawn, wading the flooded causeway in single file, robes hitched up around their knees, and they made no attempt at stealth because they had never needed stealth in their lives. The Choir walked the way weather walked. Elder Maren led them, tall and silver-braided, her voice already raised in the discipline-cant that made the air itself stiffen and resist change—the same cant that had held the valley's borders for two hundred years, sung now in miniature around eight bodies advancing across the water without a ripple.

Behind her came the others. Petra knew every face. Sister Ovelin, who had taught them breath-control when they were seven. Brother Cassian, who had once wept at Petra's first solo and then reported them, three years later, for humming forbidden intervals in the dark. Old Father Ilex, who was ninety if he was a day and whose hands shook too badly now to shape a chord, so he sang only the drone beneath everyone else, the note that held the floor.

And behind them all, carried in a chair of woven willow on the shoulders of two novices Petra didn't recognize—

Mother Anselm.

She was dying. There was no other word for what Petra saw. The woman who had taught them to sing the storm down out of a black sky, who had been the fixed point of their entire childhood, was reduced to a husk wrapped in white wool, her skin the color of candle tallow, her eyes sunken so deep they seemed to look out from inside her skull rather than from its surface. Her hands lay folded in her lap, and even from thirty paces Petra could see the tremor in them—not age-tremor but song-tremor, the shake of a body that had spent everything it had.

"She's alive," Tam whispered beside them. He had come to stand at Petra's shoulder without being asked. "You thought she was dead."

"I saw her die." Petra's voice came out strange. "I stood at her deathbed when I was twelve. I sang the passing-verse myself. She was cold, Tam. I *felt* her go cold."

"Then either you were lied to," Sefa said from the stair, not turning around, "or she was."

Elder Maren halted the column ten paces from the gallery stair. The discipline-cant faded, and in the sudden ordinary silence her voice carried across the flat water with terrible clarity.

"Petra Blackwood." She did not use any title. She did not soften it. "By vote of the surviving elders of the Stillwater Choir, sealed under seal and witnessed under witness, you are named exile-without-name. Your songs are stricken. Your name is struck from the rolls. Whatever you intend in this place, you are commanded to desist, withdraw, and return to the valley, where mercy may yet be considered."

"You can't strike my name," Petra said. "In three days, no one will have names to strike."

"We know what the Door offers." Maren's composure cracked, just at the edges, the way ice cracks before it goes all at once. "We read the archive fragment. We know the bargain can be reversed. And we know the price. Erasure, Blackwood. Every soul who remembers the old country unmade in their remembering. Do you understand what that *is*? It is not peace. It is damnation. It is the second death, the death the founders refused even in the famine years, even when the Collectors were at the gates and the sky was bleeding. They chose survival with the debt unpaid rather than survival bought with souls. And you would undo their sacrifice for—for what? Stars? Pretty lights for children to wish on?"

"For the world," Petra said.

"The world got along without them." Maren spread her arms, and behind her the elders echoed the gesture, eight pairs of hands opening in the old argumentative sign, the gesture choir children learned before they learned letters. "Look around you. The valley lives. The people live. They plant and harvest and marry and bury. They do not miss what they never had. You would take from them the only sky they've ever known and call it rescue."

"It isn't their sky," Petra said. "It's collateral. It was never theirs to keep, and it was never yours to give."

"And who are *you*," Maren said, and now the crack widened, and underneath it Petra heard something that frightened them more than anger—grief, enormous and unprocessed, grief that had been waiting twenty years for this conversation, "who are you to say? A child we exiled. A name we struck. You come back with a defector and a broken boy and a dead woman's songbook and you think suffering has made you wise. It hasn't. It's made you *willing*. Willingness is the cheapest thing in the world, child. Every Collector in the dark was willing, once."

Silence, then. The water lay flat as poured lead. Somewhere far off, a bird made a sound and thought better of it.

Tam stepped forward.

He did it without ceremony, just walked out from behind Petra and stood at the edge of the gallery, looking down at the nine singers and the dying woman, and when he spoke, it was in Elder Maren's own voice, pitched perfectly, the way he spoke everything—in other people's words.

*"Do you understand what that is?"* he said, in her voice, giving her back her own question. Then, in his mother's voice, soft: *"Sleep, my heart, the stars are counted."* Then, in a voice none of them knew, older, cracked with age and distance: *"We paid too much. We paid too much. We paid—"*

"Stop," Maren said, and her discipline broke entirely, and Petra saw her flinch as if struck.

"He remembers," Petra said quietly. "Everything. He can't speak except in what other people remember, and he remembers the old country, Maren. He's eleven years old and he carries more of it than your whole archive did before it burned. If we don't open the Door, the Collectors finish taking him. Is that your mercy? Is that the founders' sacrifice? Letting a child be eaten verse by verse because eating him slowly is more dignified than paying the debt?"

"You don't know what erasure is," Maren whispered. "None of you know. I've read the founders' letters. They describe it. The ones who considered it, in the last year of the war. One of them wrote—" her voice failed and steadied— "*it is not that we forget. It is that we were never. Our mothers never sang to us. Our graves are empty because our bones were never laid. Damnation is at least a place. Erasure is no place at all.*"

"I know," Petra said.

And they opened the oilcloth and took out the songbook, and held it up so the morning light caught the cover, and watched nine faces and one dying one fix on it at once.

"But I also know what Anselm wrote in the margins. And I think she'd like to tell you herself."

* * *

The willow chair came forward across the causeway, the two novices wading carefully, and then they lifted her—carefully, the way you lift something already broken—and set her down on the gallery stones three feet from Petra, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

Up close, the dying was worse. Petra could smell it, the sweetish smell of a body burning through its last fuel. Anselm's eyes found theirs and focused with effort, and in them was none of the fog Petra had feared. She was entirely, lucidly present. She had probably fought to stay lucid for exactly this.

"You sang my passing-verse," she said. Her voice was a ruin, a whisper dragged over gravel. "At twelve. You cried through the whole thing and hit every note anyway. I lay there listening and I thought: this one. This one will do what needs doing, someday, and it will cost them everything, and they'll do it anyway, because they cried and kept the pitch."

"You let me believe you were dead." Petra's voice shook. "Twenty years. I lit candles for you. I said your name at the winter remembrance. Every year, Anselm. Every single year."

"I know." Something moved in her ruined face that might have been a smile. "I heard you. The wards carry sound strangely, but they carry it. Every winter, a child's voice saying my name into the dark, and I couldn't answer, because answering meant the elders would know I doubted, and a doubting First Voice can't protect anyone. So I let you mourn me. Forgive me. It was the cruelest useful thing I ever did."

"It wasn't useful. It was just cruel."

"Yes," Anselm said simply. "Often those are the same thing. Ask me about the wards sometime. Or don't—we haven't the time." She turned her head, with visible cost, toward Maren. "Help me stand."

"You can't stand—"

"*Help me stand*, Maren. That is not a request from your equal."

Maren hesitated one heartbeat too long, and Anselm's eyes flashed with something of the old fire, and the elders moved as one body, lifting her upright between them, her white wool robe hanging off her frame like snow off a branch. She swayed. She did not fall. She planted her shaking hands on nothing at all, and Petra felt it happen—the air around her thickened, gathered, *listened*. Even dying, even hollowed, the First Voice of the Stillwater Choir could still make the world pay attention.

"I voted for the exile," Anselm said clearly, and Petra's heart dropped like a stone. "I want that understood. When the vote was called, I voted yes. Exile-without-name for Petra Blackwood, for consorting with defectors, for trafficking in forbidden knowledge, for intent to unmake the founding bargain." She looked at Petra, and her ancient eyes were wet. "Because exile-without-name means the Collectors cannot find you through us. It means when they came asking—who remembers this traitor?—we could honestly say *no one*. It was the only protection the Choir had left to give you, and I gave it, and I would give it again."

"You protected me by erasing me," Petra said.

"I protected you the only way a drowning woman protects a child—by pushing it under." Her breath rattled. "But that was before I read what you took from the archive. That was before I understood the Debt has come due regardless. Listen to me, all of you. All of you, elders of the Choir, hear your First Voice." The air tightened further; even the water seemed to lean in. "There is no waiting out the dark. There is no sealing the valley. The Collectors have finished harvesting the outer country—they are in the valley's dreams already, I have felt them feeding for a year. The choice was never between the Door and safety. It is between the Door and the slow unmaking of everything, beginning with that boy and ending with the youngest novice in Stillwater. The founders refused the price in the famine years because refusing might still have won the war. Refusing now wins nothing. Refusing now is only pride wearing the founders' robes."

"MOTHER ANSELM." Maren's voice cracked across the gallery. "You would sanction this? You would sanction *damnation*? You taught me the founders' letters yourself—you made me memorize the passage about the empty graves—"

"I did." Anselm's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "And I have thought about that passage every day for sixty years, Maren, and I will tell you what I finally understood on my deathbed, which is the only desk I have left. The founder who wrote it was wrong. Not faithless—wrong. She believed erasure was no place at all. But I have died once already, in a bed, with a child singing over me, and I will tell you what I found there: nothing. No place, no punishment, no watching dark. And I woke from it, and I was glad, because it meant there was no hell—but I have thought since, what if that nothing is what erasure is? What if it is not torment? What if it is only *rest*?" She drew a breath that whistled. "I am not certain. Hear me: I am not certain, and neither were the founders, and neither are you. None of us knows what the old country's people would have chosen. But I know what *I* choose. I choose the child who cried and kept the pitch. I choose the debt paid. I choose the sky."

She raised one trembling hand, and pointed it—not at Maren, not at the elders, but at the flat grey water, at the drowned spire below it, at the Door beneath.

"By the authority vested in me as First Voice of the Stillwater Choir, in the two hundred and eleventh year of the Founding, I overrule the vote of the elders. The exile of Petra Blackwood is revoked. The seal on the valley is dissolved. The Choir will stand down." Her hand began to shake harder; she steadied it against her own chest, over her heart. "And I invoke the Last Verse. The final authority of a dying First Voice. Whatever happens in this place, in this hour, is done under my name. If there is damnation in it, it is *my* damnation, and I will carry it into whatever nothing is waiting for me, gladly, because I have missed the stars for sixty years and I am tired of pretending the ceiling was enough."

No one moved. The water did not move. Even the mist seemed to have stopped mid-drift.

Then old Father Ilex, ninety and trembling, opened his mouth and began to hum the drone. Low, wavering, imperfect—the floor-note under everything, the note that holds the hall. Sister Ovelin joined him, tears running freely down her face. Then Brother Cassian, who had reported Petra at fifteen and could not meet their eyes now but sang anyway. One by one, the elders of the Stillwater Choir took up the drone beneath their dying First Voice, and the sound rolled out across the drowned lowlands like a tide deciding to come home.

Maren did not sing. She stood rigid, her jaw working, her hands fisted in her sleeves—and then, slowly, she knelt. On the wet stone, in the flood-mist, before the willow chair and the child she had exiled, she bowed her head.

"Not forgiveness," she said, to the stones. "I haven't earned the word. But I will hold the stair. Whatever comes up out of the dark while you sing—it goes through me first."

Anselm's legs gave then, and the novices caught her, and lowered her back into the chair, and Petra knelt beside her and took her shaking hands in theirs.

"How long do you have?" Petra asked, though they already knew.

"Long enough to hear the first verse." Her grip, for all its frailty, was iron. "That's all I ever asked of any of my students. Give me long enough to hear it."

Petra looked up. Sefa stood at the stair with her knife sheathed and her half-key in her fist, watching the Choir with the expression of a woman recalculating every map she'd ever drawn. Tam stood at the water's edge, looking down at his reflection, and in the flat grey mirror Petra saw what he saw: not one reflection but many, layered faint as watermarks—the faces of everyone whose words he carried, standing behind him like a congregation.

Below them, deep beneath the flood, something answered the drone.

A vibration rose through the gallery stones, through their knees, through their teeth. The First Door, feeling the harmony gather above it, had begun—slowly, patiently, the way a lock begins—to turn.

Petra unwrapped the songbook. Opened it to the first page. And above the drowned cathedral, in the east, the sky began, very faintly, to lighten in a way that had nothing to do with dawn.
