## 15. First Verse

The water in the drowned nave was warm.

Petra had expected cold—expected the flood to hold the chill of the deep places, the way the Stillwater Valley held its long gray winters—but the water that lapped at their waist as they waded toward the First Door carried a heat like a body's. Like something sleeping under it, breathing. Sefa noticed it too; Petra saw her pause on the second step of the sunken aisle, her half of the key held above the surface, and press her free hand flat against the water as if taking its pulse.

"It's not water," Sefa said quietly. "Not all of it. Feel that?"

Petra felt it. Beneath the taste of stone and silt there was something else, something that prickled against the skin like the air before lightning. Twenty years of suppression had taught Petra to read magic the way a deaf child learns to read lips—by attention, by inference, by longing. But this they did not have to infer. This pressed against them from every side, patient and enormous, the way the sea presses against a door it has been knocking on for centuries.

*The valley's stolen magic.* Anselm's marginalia had said it plainly enough, once Petra learned to read the old notation: *We did not survive the war. We purchased survival, and the coin was the sky.* All those years of gray, starless nights over Stillwater—all those nights Petra had lain awake as a child counting the three stars the wards still permitted, thin and apologetic, like coins left on a dead man's eyes—that had never been the whole sky. It had been the change left over after the bill was paid.

And now the bill had come due, and the change was being called back, and everything the valley had bought with it—the safety, the silence, the small unremarkable lives—was about to be repossessed.

"Petra." Tam's voice came from behind them, thin across the water. He stood on the last dry step of the stair with Vess, who had insisted—had *demanded*, in the flat voice of someone who remembered what she was for—that she take the stair alone when the singing started. "It's time."

Petra turned. The cathedral rose around them in its drowning: pillars thick as ancient trees going up into black water, their capitals lost somewhere far overhead where the flooded roof let down shafts of gray-green light. Fish moved through the transept like thoughts through a mind trying to sleep. And ahead, at the end of the nave, where the altar should have been, the First Door stood in the flooded dark—a rectangle of older night, older than the water, older than the country that had built the church to hide it.

The lock waited at its center. Three rings of metal, three keyholes shaped like open mouths.

One who paid. One who forgot. One who stayed.

*I am all three,* Petra thought, and the thought did not feel like destiny. It felt like a debt collector reading your name off a ledger.

Sefa reached them first. She was pale—not frightened, Petra realized, but *spent*, already, though she had not yet sung a note. She had spent the dive down telling them what she would lose. She had made herself say it aloud, every name of her homeland she could still recall, so that the singing would find them ready, like coins counted out in advance. "I'm ready," she said now, which was a lie, and they both knew it was the kind of lie you tell so that someone else can keep standing.

"I know," Petra lied back.

Tam came last, walking on the water's surface.

He had done it since the lowlands—walked out onto rivers and marshes and finally the flooded plain itself as if the water were a road he'd known as a boy, because somewhere inside him, in the place the Collectors had gutted, a road still existed. He stepped from the stair onto the flood and the flood bore him up, dimpling gently around his bare feet, and he crossed the nave toward them wearing an expression of mild surprise, as he always did, as if he himself found it remarkable each time.

"The choir stands," he said—which was Mother Anselm's phrase, borrowed from her mouth years ago, meaning *we are here, we are enough*. He stopped beside them and looked up at Petra with his terrible clear eyes. "Sing loud," he said, in a voice that belonged to no one Petra knew. "They are coming. I can hear them not-hearing."

Above, faint through fathoms of water and ruined stone, came a sound like a bell struck underwater. Then another. Not bells. *Voices.* The Collectors had found the resonance of the songbook, just as Vess had, and they were coming down through the broken world toward the sound the way sharks come to blood—not from hunger, Vess had said, but from grief so old it had forgotten its own name and taken hunger's shape instead.

"Vess is on the stair," Sefa said. "Alone."

"She asked for alone." Petra looked back once, up the length of the nave, to where the stair climbed out of the flood toward the light. A small figure stood on it, sword drawn, hair plastered dark against her skull, and she did not look back at them. She had said one thing before they dove, gripping Petra's wrist hard enough to bruise: *"When you sing the opening verse—if it cracks me again, don't stop. Whatever you hear me do, don't stop."*

Petra had promised. It was the only promise they had ever made that they were afraid they might be grateful to break.

The Door's lock took their voices before they were offered. As Petra raised the songbook—Anselm's songbook, its margins dense with her dying handwriting—and opened to the first page, the three rings of metal began, very softly, to hum. Each ring in a different pitch. Waiting to be filled.

"One who paid," Sefa said. Her voice shook. She steadied it the way she steadied a compass needle, with pressure and practice. "That's me. My country paid everything and got nothing back. I'll take the first ring."

"One who forgot," Tam said, in Anselm's voice, then in his own: "Me. Mostly." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Petra had ever seen, that smile, because it was entirely, lucidly *his*.

"And one who stayed." Petra's own voice sounded strange to them—too calm, the calm of the eye of a storm they had spent twenty years learning to be. "I never left the valley. I stayed my whole life without knowing why."

"Then we begin," Sefa said, "and we do not stop. Whatever happens. Whatever we see. Whatever we—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Whatever I beg you to do. We do not stop."

"We do not stop," Tam echoed.

Petra set two fingers against the first ring of the lock, felt the metal's hum climb into their bones, and began to sing.

---

The opening verse was not a song Petra had learned. That was the first impossible thing about it.

Anselm's notation gave the shape of it—the intervals, the holds, the places where the voice must fall silent and let the silence itself carry the line—but the moment Petra committed to the first note, the verse took over, the way a river takes a dropped leaf. Their voice went somewhere they had never put it, down into registers their childhood training had told them did not exist, and the note that came out was not Petra Blackwood's voice at all. It was the valley's. It was the sound the Stillwater Valley would make if it could open its throat after twenty years of holding its breath.

The water answered.

It began as a trembling, a shiver running outward from the Door through the flooded nave, and then the light changed. The gray-green shafts from the broken roof turned gold—not sunlight, nothing so honest—gold like memory, gold like the color of harvest fields in a country Petra had never seen and suddenly, terribly, could see. The stolen magic was rising. It came up through the floodwater in slow luminous columns, threading between the drowned pillars, gathering around the singers like a congregation returning to a church it had been locked out of for a generation. Where it touched the water, the water remembered being rain. Where it touched the stone, the stone remembered being a mountain. Everything the valley had sold was coming home, and it was coming home *singing*, harmonies rising out of the deep places that no human throat had produced, the counter-voices of earth and water and the long-buried foundations of the world.

Petra sang the second line and the columns of light began to move upward, toward the surface, toward the sky that had been sold out from under everyone they loved.

And the Collectors heard it.

Petra felt them arrive the way you feel a shadow cross the sun—not seeing them, not yet, but feeling the light falter. The harmonies rising from below stuttered. Somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms of flood, came a sound like tearing silk, and then Vess's voice, clear and furious and afraid, ringing down the drowned nave:

*"THEY'RE IN THE WATER! PETRA—THEY'RE IN THE WATER—DON'T STOP!"*

Don't stop. As if stopping were possible. The verse had Petra now entirely; their body was a bell that had been struck, and the ringing would continue until the song was done or the bell was broken. They sang the third line with tears sliding hot down their face and mixing with the flood, and the tears were not from sorrow. They were from the sheer unbearable *size* of it—the awe of feeling, for the first time in a life of gray containment, what they actually were. A storm-caller. A piece of the sky's own weather, walking around in a person's shape, kept in a valley like a coin in a miser's fist.

The first Collector broke the surface thirty feet away.

It had been human once. That was the horror Vess had tried to prepare them for, and it was worse than preparation—it was *obvious*, now, in the way it moved, in the remnants of a face turning toward the sound of the singing with an expression that was hunger and homesickness fused past separating. Its mouth opened. What came out was not a scream but a *name*—a name in a language Petra somehow understood, a woman's name, spoken by the thing that had eaten her, spoken with such raw longing that Petra's verse nearly shattered with pity.

*Don't stop.*

The second Collector surfaced closer. The third. They came through the golden water like shadows through smoke, converging on the sound, and where they passed the light died behind them, columns of returned magic guttering into gray. They could not reach the Door—the old wards around it, the founders' last honesty, still held—but they could reach the singers' minds, and they did. Grief poured across the water in waves. Petra saw Sefa stagger, saw her grip the first ring of the lock with both hands and force her own verse out through the assault, singing the names of her unmapped homeland while her eyes streamed and her voice cracked and repaired itself and cracked again.

"They're pulling at Tam," Sefa gasped between lines. "Petra—they're pulling at him—"

Petra couldn't look. Looking was stopping. They sang the fourth line, the fifth, felt the Door's first ring grow hot beneath Sefa's hands as her verse neared completion, felt the second ring waiting under Tam's palms, felt the third humming against their own sternum like a second heart—

Above, on the stair, Vess screamed.

It was not a scream of pain. Petra knew pain-screams. This was the other kind—the kind Vess had begged them not to stop for—the sound of conditioning cracking, of a name forcing its way up through decades of harvested silence into a mouth that had forgotten it owned anything. Through the golden failing light Petra glimpsed her on the stair, sword in one hand, the other pressed flat against her own breastbone, holding something in—or letting something out—while shapes crowded the water below her, climbing, always climbing, drawn by the verse that was undoing them and saving her in the same breath.

*"MY NAME,"* Vess was shouting, at the Collectors, at the water, at the world. *"MY NAME IS—"*

A Collector reached the stair. Another. The light in the nave was failing faster now, the golden columns winking out one by one as the things in the water drank the returned magic even as it rose, gorging themselves on the very sky coming home, and Petra understood with a clarity that felt like falling: the song was working, and the song was killing them, and both of these things would happen at exactly the same speed unless someone finished it.

The first ring of the lock flared white.

Sefa's voice rang out one final time—her homeland's true name, sung whole and complete and burning—and then she went silent, swaying, hollow-eyed, her lips still moving around words that were no longer there. The first ring blazed like a captive star and went dark, sealed.

Two remained.

Tam lifted his face toward Petra. His expression was no longer surprised. It was serene, and it was terrified, and it was *his*, entirely his, the last unharvested field of him, and he opened his mouth to give the second ring the only thing he had left—

—and the water beneath him went black.

* * *

Petra sang on.

There was no choice in it anymore; there had perhaps never been any choice in anything, only the long illusion of it, and the verse carried them forward through the horror the way a river carries a boat over a waterfall—inevitably, mercilessly, with the roar growing louder. They watched, through tears they could not spare the attention to wipe away, as the Collectors closed around Tam like a hand closing around a candleflame. They watched the blackness climb him, unmaking, unraveling, eating the boy outward in from his edges. They watched him stand on the water's surface in the middle of his own erasure, and they watched him *smile*.

His mouth moved. No sound came out at first—the Collectors had taken his voice, were taking it, syllable by syllable—and then, from somewhere deeper than voice, from the vault where he kept the words of everyone he had ever met, Tam spoke.

The word that came out was not in any language Petra knew.

It was the true name of the old country.

It entered the second ring of the lock like a key entering a wound. The metal drank it, flared, sealed—and the change was instantaneous and total. Every column of stolen light in the drowned nave reignited at once, gold flooding back through the water, and the Collectors recoiled as if scalded, shrieking in the voices of the women and men they had been. The harmonies from below surged. The Door itself seemed to lean toward Petra, the third ring blazing, waiting, hungry, *ready*—

Tam was gone.

Where he had stood there was only water, closing gently over the place where a boy had walked on remembered feet, and the ripples spread outward and stilled, and the song demanded the final verse, and Petra Blackwood opened their mouth to give it everything that was left.

Above them, on the stair, Vess stood alone against the dark, holding the line, screaming her recovered name into the flood.

Below them, the Third Ring waited for the voice of the one who stayed.

And Petra—who had never crossed the valley line until this week, who had stayed their whole life without knowing why, who was about to become the only soul in the world who would remember any of this—began to sing the last verse of the old country, knowing that when the final note faded, they would be singing it to no one.
