## 16. Tam's Word

The song had a floor, and Petra found it.

It was not a note so much as a place—a depth beneath the flooded nave where the water itself began to hum, where the drowned cathedral's stones remembered being quarried and sang along in their slow mineral voices. Petra stood chest-deep in the dark water on the stair below the broken rose window, and the first verse moved through them like a river through a channel cut long ago for exactly this purpose. The valley's stolen magic rose to meet it. They could feel it coming up through the floodwater in threads and tendrils, silver-bright against their skin, answering the way a struck bell answers its own echo.

Above them, faint through the water's surface, the sky was still black. But it was a different black now. A listening black.

*One who paid,* Anselm had said, her dying voice steady as a plumb line. *The verse of payment is yours. Sing what was given. Do not flinch from what it cost.*

Petra did not flinch. That surprised them—the steadiness of their own throat, the way grief could be carried like ballast instead of drowning you. The old country's name was not yet in the song; that word waited at the keystone of the working, the third voice, the one that named what had been lost so the sky would know what it was getting back. But everything else was there: the wards, the bargain, the centuries of hushed magic pressed down into the valley like flowers in a book.

Sefa stood one step above them, wading, her half of the key held aloft in its cradle of light. She had already begun her part—low, ragged, the names of her unmapped homeland rising out of her one by one like birds leaving a burning wood. Each name cost her something visible. Petra could see it happening: a flicker behind her eyes, a small collapse at the corner of her mouth, as if she were swallowing embers and smiling around them. Her memories were burning as fuel, and she fed them willingly, and the song grew fat and bright on the offering.

And Tam—

Tam stood on the highest dry step, the boy who spoke only in other people's words, his hands open at his sides. He was not singing. He was *holding*. That was the only word for it. He had told them once, in his borrowed voice—Anselm's voice, actually, though he'd never met her then—that remembering was a kind of carrying, and someone had to carry the far end of the thing or the bridge fell down. So he carried. His whole thin body was taut with it, a living guy-wire for the working, and the third voice slept inside him coiled and waiting: the true name of the old country, the last word anyone alive still knew.

The Collectors came at the second turning of the verse.

Petra felt them before hearing them—a wrongness in the water, a cold that was not temperature but *absence*, moving through the flooded nave the way oil moves through cloth. Vess was holding the stair. They had heard her voice above, cracked and furious and human, shouting words that were hers again, all hers: *Not this door. Not while I have a name.* There was a sound like hail on bronze. Then another. Then a sound like a wall giving up.

"Petra." Sefa's voice, tight, mid-name. She was singing *Ilves, Ilves, the river called Amme, the hill of nine pines*—and each word left her lighter and more frightened. "They're through."

The water went dark in a spreading ring.

Petra kept singing. There was nothing else in the world to do; the verse had them now, had their breath and their heartbeat, and to stop would be to let the valley's stolen magic fall back into its grave. But their eyes found Tam on his step, and Tam's eyes found theirs, and in the boy's face was no fear at all—which was somehow worse than fear, because Tam knew better than any of them what was coming. He had been mostly unmade since childhood. He knew the shape of the thing that took you apart.

They came up out of the water like herons made of winter.

There were seven of them, tall and narrow, their forms flickering between shapes the eye refused to keep—once-choirs, once-human, worn down to instruments of harvest. Their faces were the worst part: not featureless but *erased*, smooth as pages scraped for reuse, and yet tilted with terrible attention toward the sound. Toward the song. Toward the three small loud lives standing in a dead god's house trying to give the sky back.

Vess came down the stair ahead of them, bleeding light from a gash along her arm, her sword of cartographer's steel—not a weapon really, just a tool sharpened past its purpose—held in both hands. "I slowed them," she said, and her voice broke on the ordinary human pride of it. "I slowed them and they didn't care."

"They never do," Sefa said. "That's how I know they're what I sold maps to." And she sang louder.

The nearest Collector reached the dry steps.

It did not walk so much as *arrive*, folding distance the way a hand folds paper. It stopped before Tam, and its blank face inclined, almost courteously, the way a tax collector inclines to a debtor. And Petra understood, with a lurch that nearly broke their verse, that this was not malice. This was arithmetic. The boy was full of remembered words—stolen ones, hoarded ones, a whole town's worth of other people's speech—and to the Collectors he was not a child. He was a granary.

"No," Petra sang, and made the note a wall.

The Collector passed through the wall like weather.

What happened next happened slowly, which is the cruelty of such things. The Collector did not strike Tam. It simply *began*, the way frost begins on a window—an edge of whiteness at his shoulder, creeping, and everywhere it touched him he grew fainter, less argued-for, a sketch losing its artist's confidence. His hands went translucent at the fingertips. His outline softened. He looked down at himself with mild interest, the way one looks at a stain.

"Oh," Tam said. It was Sefa's voice he used, her word, her *oh*, borrowed weeks ago on the road and kept safe. "So that's what it feels like from outside."

"TAM!" Sefa's song shattered. She lunged for him and the water fought her, dragging at her skirts, and two more Collectors folded into being between her and the step, patient as tide.

"Keep singing." Tam's voice now was his own—or as close as he ever came to it, that quiet grain underneath all the borrowed words, the one voice that belonged to no one else because everyone else had forgotten it existed. "Please. If you stop, it was all for—" He smiled, and the smile was Anselm's smile, borrowed too, and none the less true for it. "You need the third voice. You need the word."

"I can't take it from you like this—"

"You're not taking it." The whiteness reached his collar. His hair was going pale at the edges, not gray but *unwritten*, color draining out of it like dye out of cloth in a flood. "I'm spending it. There's a difference. Mother Anselm taught me that—no wait." A flicker of confusion crossed his fading face. "No. Someone taught me that. Someone kind. I don't remember who anymore." He laughed, and the laugh was thin as ice over deep water. "That's all right. I remember the word."

The Collectors pressed closer. Vess was fighting at the stair's head, swinging her blade in wide desperate arcs, and where it touched them the Collectors recoiled—not harmed, but *reminded*, flinching from the cold honest iron of a thing that had been used for measuring honest ground. "Petra!" she shouted. "Whatever he's about to do—be ready to catch it!"

Tam knelt on his dissolving step. The whiteness had taken his legs; he knelt on air and memory and it held him, because he believed it would. He put his fading hands together, cupped, the way children cup water, the way singers cup a flame against wind.

"The elders think erasure is damnation," he said. "The Collectors think memory is currency. Everyone's wrong." His eyes were bright and utterly clear. "A word isn't yours. It never was. A word is a *bridge*, and bridges are meant to be walked across by people going somewhere you'll never see."

He drew one long breath.

And Petra, still singing, still pouring the second verse's payment into the rising water, saw the boy gather every word he had ever carried—the town's forgotten sentences, the travelers' phrases, the hymns and curses and market-calls of a hundred borrowed voices—and set them all down except one. One word. The oldest one. The one he had been keeping at the very bottom of himself like a coin sewn into a coat lining, saved for exactly this, for the moment when a bridge was needed and there was no time to build one any other way.

He looked at Petra and mouthed it once, silently, showing them the shape.

Then he gave it away.

The word left him like a bird leaving a snared hand. It crossed the dark water not as sound but as *meaning*, striking Petra's sternum, sinking in, finding the hollow place the third voice had waited in all their life without knowing it was a hollow. And Petra suddenly knew the old country's name—knew it the way you know your own pulse, complete and ancient and warm, a name that meant *the place where the rain agrees to fall*, a name that had not been spoken aloud in four hundred years because everyone who could speak it had paid it away or been emptied of it.

The third voice opened in Petra's chest like a door.

Tam, meanwhile, was almost gone. The whiteness had climbed past his shoulders. His face was the last of him, and even that was softening now, features gentling toward blankness—but he was not afraid. He was looking upward, past the broken rose window, past the listening black sky, at something only he could see.

"There you are," he said—in a voice no one recognized, his true voice, the one that had never been borrowed because it had never been needed until now. "Hello. I brought it back."

And then the Collectors finished their harvest, and the step was empty, and the water closed over the place where a boy had knelt with the smallest possible splash, polite as a dropped pebble.

For one horrible half-second, Petra's song faltered.

The whole working shuddered. The rising silver threads of the valley's magic hung suspended in the floodwater, waiting, and the Collectors turned their blank faces toward the silence with the hunger of things that had finally, after four hundred years, smelled the whole meal.

Sefa caught Petra's eyes across the dark water. Her face was wrecked, hollowed, half her own history burned away in the second verse and now this on top of it—and still she lifted her chin, and still the names kept coming, *Amme, the nine pines, my mother's kitchen, the road east*, because grief performed is still devotion, and she would not let the bridge fall.

"Sing," she said. "He spent it so you'd sing. Don't you dare waste him."

Petra breathed in.

The breath went all the way down, deeper than lungs, into the keystone place. And Petra Blackwood—the exile, the contained, the last archive not yet written—opened their mouth and spoke-sang the true name of the old country for the first time in four centuries:

*"Serenvale."*

*The place where the rain agrees to fall.*

The word went through the flooded cathedral like dawn through a prism. Every stone woke. The water itself turned to light from beneath, and the valley's stolen magic—every suppressed storm, every hushed thunderhead, every lullaby the Choir had ever swallowed—rose through the flood in a single towering column of silver, and the First Door beneath the nave began, at last, to open.

But the Door swung inward as well as outward. Cold poured through the widening seam—cold and vast and *attentive*—and the Collectors all turned toward it at once, and Vess screamed from the stairs:

"It opens both ways! I told you—it opens BOTH WAYS—"

Something on the other side had heard the name *Serenvale* spoken aloud.

And it was answering.
