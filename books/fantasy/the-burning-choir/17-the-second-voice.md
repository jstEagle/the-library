## 17. The Second Voice

The first verse was still hanging in the water when Petra's voice began to fail.

Not from exhaustion—though their throat was raw, though the floodwater around the drowned cathedral trembled with every note they gave it—but because the song was taking something as it went. Petra could feel it leaving them in thin bright threads, drawn up through the chest and out through the mouth: the smell of Anselm's kitchen, the weight of a childhood winter, the name of the street where they had first learned to whistle. Small things. The song took small things first, the way a fire takes the edges of a page before it reaches the words.

They kept singing anyway. That was the bargain. That was the whole terrible architecture of it.

And beneath them, on the flooded stair where Vess stood alone with a broken pike and a voice gone hoarse from screaming warnings, the Collectors were coming.

Petra felt Sefa move before they saw her. The cartographer had been braced against a pillar of the submerged nave, one hand pressed flat to the stone as if she could take its pulse, her half of the key still warm against her sternum. Now she waded forward through waist-deep water that glowed faintly with the risen magic of the valley—all that stolen storm-light climbing toward the surface like bubbles—and she stood beside Petra in the ruined choir loft where the First Door's song had first been loosed.

"Your verse is ending," Sefa said. Not a question. She had heard the shape of the working now for three verses' worth of silence and one verse of singing, and she heard things the way she drew coastlines: precisely, without mercy, without flinching.

"I know."

"You can't hold both voices. No one can. Anselm's marginalia says—" Sefa stopped herself, and Petra watched her stop, watched the cartographer's habit of precision war with something larger moving behind her eyes. "I've known since the river," Sefa said quietly. "Since before the river. Since the night I read what your mentor wrote about the second voice. *One who paid. One who forgot.* I've been walking toward this since before you asked me to."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice under a boot. "Don't tell me there's another way. You're the one who taught me there isn't. You're the one who said the Debt doesn't negotiate." She laughed then, a short, awful sound, and wiped her face with the back of her hand—though whether it was water or something else, neither of them would ever say. "I sold maps, Petra. I sold the shapes of dead places to the things that killed them. I have spent eleven years being paid in bread for the erasure of everything I loved. Do you understand? This is the only transaction left to me that isn't theft."

Below them, something screamed—not human, not anymore, but wearing the memory of a human throat—and Vess answered it with a word in a language Petra had never heard and somehow understood: *stop*. The stair shuddered. Light burst upward through the floodwater in veins of gold and green, the valley's returned magic rising faster now, feeding on the song, and through that light Petra could see Vess silhouetted against the dark shapes descending, small and fierce and mortal, holding a line no one had asked her to hold.

"She can't last," Petra said.

"No one lasts." Sefa stepped closer. In the glow her face looked carved out of old ivory, all planes and shadow, and her eyes—the eyes of a woman who had mapped countries that no longer existed, who had drawn coastlines from memory because memory was all that remained—were steady as a compass needle. "Sing me the line, Petra. Teach it to me the way Anselm taught you. Quickly. Before I lose my nerve, which will be in approximately ten seconds, because I am a coward with excellent posture."

Petra's throat closed. They thought of the river of small betrayals, of the knife at dawn, of two liars recognizing each other's craft across a campfire. They thought of Tam, already mostly gone, spending his remembered words like a miser's final coins. They thought of the price read aloud in the Door's cold light: *no one who remembers the old country will survive to mourn it*—and understood, with a lurch, that the song did not distinguish between countries. It would take whatever the second voice offered. It would take hers.

"It will burn," Petra said. "Everything. Every name, every road, every face. You'll finish the verse and you won't know why you're crying."

"I cry anyway," Sefa said. "At least this way it will mean something."

She smiled. It was the worst thing Petra had ever seen, and the most beautiful: grief performed as devotion, worn openly, unashamed, the way other people wore medals.

So Petra sang her the line.

It was not long. Three phrases, rising, the middle one bent strangely—the old country's tongue had a grammar for longing that no living language kept—and Sefa listened with her whole body, the way she read maps, the way she memorized coastlines she would never walk again. When Petra finished, she nodded once, businesslike, a surveyor confirming a bearing.

Then she opened her mouth and began to sing the names of her own unmapped homeland into the working.

* * *

Her country had been called Ilvesmark. Almost no one alive knew that now; soon even she would not.

Sefa had been eight when it ended. She had never told Petra the whole of it—had given them fragments, the way she gave everyone fragments, because fragments were safe and whole truths were doors that opened both ways. But the song did not want fragments. The song wanted everything, and she gave it, pouring eleven years of hoarded memory into the rising water like a woman emptying her pockets into a river.

She sang the names of the harbors first. *Vessik. Karja. The Little Salt Gate.* She sang the fishing villages whose names had never appeared on any map but her father's hand-drawn ones, the ones he'd burned the night the Collectors came, feeding his life's work page by page into the stove while the family watched, because a map in enemy hands was a people undone. She sang the name of the hill where he'd taught her to read stars, and the name of the dog, and the name of the tune her mother hummed while gutting fish, a tune with no words that Sefa had carried in her head for twenty years like a coal carried in cupped hands, afraid to let it go out, afraid to let it burn.

The song took the coal.

She felt it go. That was the horror and the mercy of it—there was no anesthesia in the working, no numbness. Each name left her the way a tooth leaves the jaw: a moment of resistance, a tearing, then a strange clean absence that the tongue keeps returning to. She sang *Vessik* and the harbor vanished from her—not the idea of it, which remained like a photograph of a photograph, but the harbor itself, the smell of tar and herring, the particular slap of grey water on grey stone. She sang her mother's tune and the melody guttered mid-phrase, and she heard herself humming emptiness, and she wept without stopping the song, because stopping was the one thing the working would not forgive.

Beside her, Petra sang harmony and grieved. This was the cruelty folded inside the gift: Petra retained everything. Every name Sefa surrendered passed through the song and settled into Petra like sediment into a riverbed, so that even as Sefa's homeland died in her mouth it was born again in Petra's keeping. Petra now knew the weight of the Little Salt Gate's bell. Petra now knew that Sefa's mother had called her *little compass* when she was small, because she always found her way home. Petra would know these things forever, alone, and Sefa would stand beside them afterward and feel nothing where a country used to be.

*I'm sorry,* Petra sang, weaving the apology into the harmony where no one but the Door could hear it.

*Don't you dare be sorry,* Sefa answered in the same hidden thread, her voice fraying, blazing. *This is the first honest work I've done in eleven years.*

The water around them had begun to rise in slow spirals, answering the second voice the way it had answered the first. Above, through the broken vault of the nave, the drowned sky churned—still starless, still black, but black the way a held breath is black, black with pressure, black with something waiting to be released. The valley's stolen magic climbed the cathedral walls in luminous ropes, and everywhere it touched, the old stones remembered themselves: carvings surfacing from beneath centuries of silt, saints' faces emerging blinking into the glow, and among them, high on the eastern wall, a frieze of singers that no history book had ever mentioned—robed figures with their mouths open, their hands linked, singing down a sky that the sculptor had rendered full of holes.

Sefa saw it too. She laughed through her tears—a real laugh, cracked and astonished—and sang louder.

She sang the names of the dead. There were so many. Her father, whose maps had bought the family three days of running. Her brother, who had not made the fourth. The schoolteacher who had hidden children in a root cellar and been found. She sang each name and the song drank each one, and with every name her voice grew thinner and brighter, a candle burning down to give light to a room, until Petra could hardly bear to harmonize with her, could hardly bear to be the vessel into which all this treasure was pouring.

And still the Collectors came.

They poured down through the flooded clerestory like ink dropped in water, shapes that had once been choirs—Petra could see it now, see the horror at the heart of them, the way their tattered forms still moved in patterns that had once been harmony, still reached for one another with hands that had once been linked in song. They had paid too much, Anselm's notes had said. They had sung away too much of themselves and what remained had forgotten it was ever anything but hunger. They descended on the sound of Sefa's burning memories the way moths descend on flame, and above them, on the stair, Vess screamed a warning that broke in the middle, and the first of them slipped past her—

—and Sefa turned her face toward it and sang its name.

Not a Collector's name. The name it had borne before: a young man's name, a chorister's name, from a country Petra had never heard of, and how Sefa knew it none of them could say except that the song was generous in strange ways, giving back as it took, handing the singer keys to doors she hadn't known were locked. The Collector stopped. Shuddered. For one instant its tattered darkness resolved into the ghost of a human face—young, frightened, mid-note—and then the others closed over it like water, and it was gone, and Sefa sang on.

"The third phrase!" Petra gasped, when the verse's end rose toward them like a reef. "Sefa—the third phrase—"

"I know." Her voice was almost nothing now. A whisper with a spine. "Little compass finds her way home."

She sang the third phrase.

It was the bent one, the one with the grammar of longing, and she bent it further than Anselm's notation required, bent it past the edge of the old country's music into something new, something that belonged to no nation because it belonged to grief itself, and the whole drowned cathedral rang with it. The spiraling water leapt. The frieze of singers blazed. Far above, beyond the broken vault, beyond the black sky, something vast turned over in its sleep—the Debt, the ancient bargain, feeling the second of its three locks give way.

And then it was done.

Sefa's voice stopped. Not failed—stopped, cleanly, the way a clock stops, and she stood in the glowing water swaying, her mouth still open on the shape of a note that no longer had anything behind it. Her eyes were open too, and they were hollow—not empty, Petra would insist later, never empty, but hollowed, the way a house is hollow after the family has gone, still holding the shape of everything that used to live there.

She blinked. She looked at Petra with the polite blankness of a stranger on a road.

"I'm sorry," she said slowly. "Do I know you? You seem—I feel as if I should know you."

And then her gaze drifted down to her own hands, splayed against the glowing water, and to the cartographer's tools still strapped at her belt, and something in her—something deeper than memory, some bedrock layer the song could not reach, craft and courage and the stubborn animal will that had carried her across an erased world—rose up through the hollowness like a spring through rock.

She began, very softly, to hum.

No tune. Nothing. Just the act of humming, the shape of music without its content, and her face crumpled and reformed and crumpled again, and finally she said, in a voice scraped raw and wondering:

"I don't remember what I lost. But I remember that I loved it. Is that—is that enough? Tell me that's enough."

Petra caught her as her knees buckled, held her up in the shining water, and could not answer, because the answer was yes and no and the whole unbearable arithmetic of the price. Around them the cathedral thundered with the risen magic, the third lock waiting, the final verse coiled in Petra's chest like a storm that had waited twenty years for permission.

Above, on the stair, Vess went down under the dark.

And far overhead, through the broken vault, the black sky split—one hairline crack of silver, widening, widening—as the Collectors, maddened by the second voice's sacrifice, abandoned the stair entirely and came down into the nave like a landslide of night, all of them, everything, converging on the last singer standing.

Petra laid Sefa gently against the pillar, turned to face the dark, and opened their mouth to sing.
