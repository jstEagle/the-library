## 18. Climax: The Sky Remembers

The water was rising with the song.

Petra felt it before they saw it—the floodwater in the drowned nave climbing the pillars in slow silver ropes, drawn upward by the verse Sefa had just finished singing. Her voice still hung in the flooded dark like a struck bell that refused to stop ringing. She stood chest-deep at the base of the First Door, hollow-eyed, smiling, her hands open at her sides as though she had set something down and could not remember what it had been.

"Sefa," Petra said.

"I'm here." Her voice came out scraped thin, a reed blown through. "I'm—here." She said it twice, as if testing whether the words belonged to her or to someone she had once read about. "Go on, Petra. Finish it."

Above them, through the broken roof where the cathedral's spire had punched its way out of the flood years ago, the stairwell groaned. Stone cracked. Somewhere up there, Vess was holding the line alone, and the sounds coming down through the water were not the sounds of a woman winning.

Petra turned back to the Door.

It filled the end of the nave like a wound in the world—a slab of black stone older than any country, older than the war that had made the bargain necessary, carved with a lock that was not a lock but a mouth, waiting for the third and final voice. The first voice had been Tam's: *Vessarien*, the true name of the old country, spent from his mouth like his last coin, given while the Collectors unmade him word by word. Petra could still hear the shape of it. They would always hear the shape of it. The second voice had been Sefa's, sung in the tongue of her drowned homeland, her memories burning off her like mist off a morning field until all that remained was a woman who knew she had loved something and could no longer say what.

Now the third voice. Now the singer. Now Petra Blackwood, who had stayed—who had been kept, who had been contained, whose whole life had been a wall built around a debt—and who would remain when everything else was ash and starlight.

They opened Anselm's songbook one last time.

The pages were soft as cloth now, swollen with floodwater, the ink of her mentor's hand bleeding gently into the margins where she had written the truth of the Choir in letters small enough to hide from God. *We did not survive the war,* Anselm had written. *We purchased survival, and the price was the sky, and we let our children believe the walls were kindness.* Petra pressed their thumb against the final notation—the last verse, the one only the singer could carry, because it required a memory that would not be erased but *concentrated*, gathered, kept, like all the light of a burned house held in a single lamp.

*You will be the archive,* Anselm had written. *Forgive me. I could find no other way to keep you safe except to make you the safest loneliest person alive.*

"Mother," Petra whispered, and did not know if they were praying or cursing, and understood finally that those had always been the same act.

Then they began to sing.

* * *

The first note left Petra's body like something that had been waiting twenty years for permission.

It rose through their chest, their throat, their teeth—it tasted of Stillwater rain and of the ward-song they had been forced to swallow every dawn of their childhood, the discipline hymns designed not to call storms but to bury them. But buried things do not die. Buried things wait. And every suppressed thunderhead of Petra's girlhood and boyhood and the long ungendered years between—all of it woke at once and came up through the song like a river remembering the sea.

The floodwater answered.

It rose around them in columns, in spirals, glowing faintly gold where the first two verses still resonated in the stone, and above the broken roof of the drowned cathedral the darkness itself began to thin. Not brighten—thin, the way fog thins when wind decides it has indulged the fog long enough. Through the gap where the spire had shattered, Petra could see the sky, and the sky was beginning to remember.

A single point of light kindled far overhead. Then another. Then a scatter of them, tentative as the first stars any child had ever named, and each one that lit sent a shudder through the water, through the stone, through Petra's bones, because every star was a payment returned, a coin taken from a country's purse and given back with interest paid in grief.

The Collectors came down the stairwell like a tide of quiet.

Petra sang and watched them come. There were dozens—no, more, dozens upon dozens, pouring through the breached doors and down into the flooded nave, and they did not walk so much as *arrive*, folding space around themselves the way a man folds a letter. They had been human once. Vess had told them that in the dark below, her conditioning cracked open by the sound of the opening verse: choirs, once, whole orders of singers who had paid too much of themselves in too many wars until nothing remained but the harvest and the hunger. Their faces were smooth as unfinished sculpture. Where their eyes should have been there were places where eyes were remembered.

And they were afraid.

That was the thing no one had ever said about the Collectors, the thing Vess had barely been able to articulate even after her own name came back to her: they were terrified. Every one of them. Because the song Petra was singing was not an attack—it was a *reversal*, and everything the Collectors had harvested, everything they had swallowed of a hundred unmapped countries, was being called home. They clutched at the water, at the pillars, at each other, mouths opening on soundless screams, and from each of them the stolen memories poured upward in ribbons of pale light, streaming toward the reigniting sky like rivers running backward into the mountains.

*Let it go,* Petra sang, in the old tongue, in the language Tam had died to complete. *What you took was never yours. What you were is not lost. Come down out of the hunger. Come home.*

The nave shook. The pillars groaned. Somewhere above, the last resistance on the stairwell ended—not with Vess falling, Petra realized, but with Vess stepping aside, standing down, her sword-hand open, letting the tide of her former masters pour past her toward a Door that was about to unmake them all.

Good, Petra thought, and hated how much it cost to think even that much, and sang louder.

* * *

The Collectors reached the water's edge and stopped.

Not because they chose to. Because the song had built a shore inside them—the memory of being human, dredged up from whatever sediment lay beneath the hunger—and against that shore the hunger broke and broke again like a wave that has finally met land it cannot climb.

One of them came apart first. It happened gently, horribly: the smooth face fissured, and behind the fissures was a woman's face, ordinary, middle-aged, weeping without knowing what century had taken her children. Then another Collector folded, and another, the whole tide of them collapsing inward like puppets whose strings had been cut by the same knife, and where each one fell there knelt a person—just a person, soaked and shaking and grieving, surrounded by the ghosts of everything they had eaten to avoid feeling anything at all.

The nave filled with weeping. Dozens of voices, then hundreds, the sound rising through the broken roof and out into the night, and Petra understood with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between ribs: this was the sound the old country must have made, at the end, when the Choir signed the bargain and the stars went out one by one and no one was permitted to mourn aloud. This was the grief that had been banked for three hundred years, gathering interest in the dark.

The Debt was not being reversed, Petra realized. The Debt was being *paid*—finally, fully, in the only currency it had ever truly been denominated in.

They sang the last lines with tears running freely into the floodwater, and did not stop singing, because the song could not be stopped now any more than a storm can be stopped once the pressure breaks, and because stopping would mean hearing the weeping clearly, and hearing it clearly would mean understanding what they had done, and they could not afford understanding yet. Understanding could wait. Grief could wait. Even solitude could wait, though it was already waiting patiently at the edge of the light, patient as an heir.

Above the broken water, the sky caught fire with stars.

Not the thin borrowed lights of the valley's warded decades—real stars, whole constellations, the sky of Vessarien restored in a single breathless instant, wheeling back into place above a country that no longer existed to name them. The floodwater turned to hammered silver. The First Door, its lock satisfied, its mouth full of three voices, dissolved—not opened, not shattered, simply *finished*, the way a sentence is finished, leaving silence where meaning used to stand.

And in that silence, Petra felt it happen.

It came like a tide going out in reverse. Every memory of the old country—every name, every song, every hearth-fire and harvest rite and lullaby, every street of every town, the names of rivers that no longer ran anywhere, the faces of people three centuries dead—did not vanish. It *arrived*. All of it. At once. In Petra.

They gasped. The song ended; the silence rushed in; and Petra Blackwood stood chest-deep in silver water under a reborn sky, holding a nation.

There was no other way to describe it. It was not knowledge. It was weight. Three hundred years of a country settled into them like snowfall, layer upon layer, and Petra felt their spine bend under it and refused to fall, refused, because someone had to hold it, because that had been the price written into the last verse in Anselm's careful hidden hand: *the singer keeps.* Everyone else forgets. The singer keeps. Forever. Alone.

Around the nave, the survivors were surfacing from the weeping into confusion. Sefa blinked at the stars with wet, wondering eyes. "They're beautiful," she said, and her voice was steady and empty and new. "I don't—I've never seen—" She looked at Petra, and smiled, and the smile was the smile of a stranger meeting a stranger. "Did you do this? Whatever it was—you did something. I can tell by your face."

"Yes," Petra said. "I did something."

Tam was gone. There was no body to bury, because he had already been mostly unmade when he gave his last word; the song had simply finished what the Collectors started, and the water where he had stood held nothing but starlight. Petra looked at that patch of silver flood and memorized it too—added it to the archive, filed it beside the name *Vessarien* and the taste of festival bread and the sound of Anselm young and laughing in a courtyard that existed now nowhere in the world except behind Petra's eyes.

The elders would arrive soon, if they weren't already climbing the flooded approach. They would weep without knowing why. Sefa would map a world she couldn't remember losing. The Collectors-turned-human would wander out of the lowlands carrying griefs they couldn't source, and some of them, maybe, would build something better out of the ache.

And Petra would remember. All of it. Every day. For the rest of a life that suddenly looked very long and very quiet, like a library after closing.

Petra tipped their head back and looked up through the broken roof at the sky their song had bought back, at the stars of a country that lived now in exactly one place, beating inside one tired chest.

"Vessarien," they said aloud, softly, testing the weight. It held. It would always hold. That was the curse of it, and the gift, and there was no longer any difference between the two.

From the flooded doorway behind them came the sound of boots in shallow water, and lantern-light, and the voices of the Choir elders arriving to see what their exile had wrought—and among them, thin and ragged but unmistakable, a voice Petra had thought never to hear again in this world:

"Petra." Mother Anselm, dying, alive, standing at the edge of the silver flood with her arms open. "Oh, child. What have you done?"

Petra turned, the whole country turning with them, and went to meet her.
