## 19. Aftermath of Light

The water went still before the last note did.

Petra felt it happen in their chest first—the song ending not like a door closing but like a hand unclenching, finger by finger, until the valley's stolen magic had passed through them and out into the world where it belonged. The floodwater around the drowned cathedral settled into something almost gentle, lapping at the broken stair in small silver tongues. Above them, through the wound in the roof where the spire had collapsed a century ago, the sky was full.

Full. Petra had no other word for it and no better one. Stars beyond counting, stars in rivers and clusters and slow wheeling orders, stars so dense along the horizon that the dark between them looked like the exception rather than the rule. Light fell through the ruined nave and lay across the water in long pale columns, and in those columns floated dust and ash and the fine grey residue of what had been the Collectors—no longer hunting, no longer anything but human-shaped grief dissolving into the current.

Sefa stood waist-deep beside them, hollow-eyed, her hands still raised from the second verse as if she'd forgotten they were hers. Her lips moved once, twice, shaping a word that wouldn't come. Then she lowered her arms and said, very quietly, "I can't remember what I sang."

"I know," Petra said.

"It was my country. It was—" Sefa pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead, hard, as though she could press the memory back in through the bone. "I know that it was mine. I know I loved it. I know there were birches, or I think there were birches, or someone told me once about birches—" Her voice cracked and steadied, cracked and steadied, the way ice does over moving water. "Why can't I mourn it? Why can't I even find it to mourn?"

"Because you gave it away," Petra said. "Every name of it. You sang it into the working so the Debt could be reversed." They heard how cruel it sounded and could not soften it, because softness would have been a lie, and they had sworn—at the Door, inside the Door, in whatever place the Door actually was—they had sworn not to lie anymore. "You're standing in the shape of a sacrifice you'll never be able to see."

Sefa turned and looked at them for a long moment. The starlight caught the wet grey at her temples, the knife-scar on her jaw, the cartographer's squint she'd never quite lost even now that there was nothing left to map. Then she laughed—a short, terrible, genuine laugh—and waded toward the stair.

"Well," she said. "At least I'm alive to complain about it."

* * *

They found Tam at the top of the stair, sitting with his back against a fallen pillar, and for one wild heartbeat Petra thought he was whole.

Then they saw his eyes.

The boy who remembered everything but spoke only in other people's words had spent his final remembered word giving Petra the true name of the old country—*Vessalune*, sung in a child's clear voice straight into the heart of the working, completing the third voice, sealing the Debt reversed. And whatever he had been made of, it had gone with the word. He sat in the starlight looking like a boy who had been very thoroughly emptied and then, carelessly, refilled with someone else's weather.

He saw them coming and smiled. That was still there. The smile was still his.

"You came back," he said, and the voice was his too, or near enough. But the words—Petra listened with the whole of their trained ear, the ear Mother Anselm had spent twenty years sharpening against storm-song, and knew: *you came back* belonged to someone else. Some mother, some soldier, some stranger dead these hundred years whose sentence Tam had carried out of a burning archive of a town and now wore because it was all he had.

"Tam." Petra knelt in front of him. Water ran off their coat and pooled between them, black and star-flecked. "What do you remember?"

He considered this with the grave patience of a much older person. "I remember that I'm cold," he said. "I think that one's mine. Cold feels like mine." He frowned. "There was a dog. Was the dog mine? Someone loved a dog very much. I have the loving of it but not the dog."

Petra's throat closed. They had known this was coming. They had watched him begin to unravel mid-verse and kept singing, kept singing, because the alternative was a sky that stayed dark forever, and they had told themselves the price was paid by everyone equally, spread thin across a nation's worth of forgetting. But it wasn't equal. Nothing about it was equal. Tam had been mostly memory to begin with—he had told them so himself, cheerfully, horribly, *I'm already mostly gone*—and when the old country dissolved out of every mind that held it, there had been very little left in him to hold him together.

"The dog was yours," Petra said. "His name was Bracken. He was brown with one white paw, and he slept under your bed, and he was afraid of thunder, and you used to sing to him during storms because your grandmother taught you a song for it."

Tam's face lit up like a window. "Bracken," he breathed. "Yes. Yes, that's—" And then the light guttered, because Petra could see him reaching for the rest and finding only the single stone they'd placed there, an island in all that water. "How do you know that?"

Because I am the last living archive of a country that no longer exists, Petra did not say. Because every name in it lives in me now and nowhere else, and I will carry them until my lungs give out, and there is no one on this earth I can share them with without stealing from them the mercy of not knowing what was taken.

"I read about him somewhere," they said.

It was the first lie they'd told since the Door. It tasted like ash and like kindness, both at once, and Petra understood that they would be telling it, or ones like it, for the rest of their life.

* * *

The elders came down from the rim of the valley at dawn—or what would have been dawn, except that dawn hardly mattered anymore beneath a sky so crowded with light that the horizon itself seemed to glow.

Petra met them at the edge of the floodwater. There were eleven of the survivors of the Choir's ruling circle, walking two by two down the old pilgrim road with their hoods up against a cold that no longer existed, and behind them straggled perhaps forty others—singers, tenders, the few children too young to have been sealed inside the valley's vote. They stopped when they saw the water. They stopped when they saw the cathedral's broken spire rising from it like a black finger accusing heaven. And then, as one body, they looked up.

Petra watched the moment the starlight hit them.

It was not like anything they had expected. Petra had imagined rage, perhaps, or terror—the elders who had voted to seal the valley, to wait out the dark, to exile-without-name anyone who left, confronted at last with proof that the dark had been a debt and the debt had been payable. Petra had rehearsed the argument on the walk up from the nave. *You chose cowardice. You chose containment. I chose the sky.*

But the elders did not argue. Elder Maren, whose voice had led the vote for sealing, whose verdict had nearly put Petra's name on the exile rolls—Elder Maren dropped to her knees in the mud of the pilgrim road and began to weep.

Not softly. Not the composed weeping of a woman moved by beauty. She wept the way people weep at gravesides, doubled over, one fist pressed to her mouth, making sounds that were barely human. And around her the others went down too, one after another, like a harvest falling before a wind no one else could feel, until the whole road was strewn with kneeling singers crying their hearts out at a sky they could not stop staring at.

"We don't know why," Elder Maren gasped when Petra reached her. She grabbed Petra's wrist with shocking strength, her wet eyes wide and frantic. "Child, tell me why I'm weeping. Tell me what I've lost. I feel it—I feel it like a limb, like a house burned down while I slept, I know that something is *gone*, I know that I loved something and cannot—"

And Petra stood there, holding all of it—the whole country, Vessalune, every river name and hearth-song and grave-marker and recipe and curse and lullaby, every one of the eleven thousand souls who had bargained their sky away to buy their grandchildren's survival, every field and festival and feud, held entire and alone in Petra Blackwood's single skull—and looked at the woman who had sentenced them, and could not decide whether mercy or cruelty was the greater gift.

"You're grieving the war," Petra said finally. "Everyone is. It ended tonight. Grief comes after."

It was true the way a map is true. It showed the shape of the thing without any of the territory.

Elder Maren nodded slowly, accepting it, and wept harder, and Petra stood among the kneeling elders in the starlight and felt the loneliest thing they had ever felt—not the loneliness of being unloved, which they had known since childhood, but the loneliness of being the only mourner at a funeral attended by ten thousand grieving strangers who didn't know whose funeral it was.

*I know your names,* Petra wanted to shout at them. *All of them. I know the name of the village Elder Maren's grandmother came from—Coriel-on-the-Water, it was called, and the bell in its chapel had a crack that made it sound like laughter, and there was a woman there named Odile who made plum brandy and cheated at cards, and her descendants are kneeling in this mud right now, three of them, and none of them will ever taste that brandy or hear that bell or know Odile's name again unless I say it, and I will say it, I will say it every day until I die, and it will save nothing and comfort no one but me.*

They said none of it. They helped Elder Maren to her feet instead, and the old woman gripped their arm and searched their face and said, with the strange gentleness of the newly broken, "You did this. Whatever happened—you did this, didn't you. I can see it in you. You look like someone carrying something heavy down a long stair."

"Yes," Petra said. "Something like that."

* * *

By full morning the floodwater had begun its slow retreat, draining south through channels that had waited a hundred years for permission, uncovering the drowned fields in strips of glistening black earth. The survivors of the Choir made camp on the high ground above the cathedral, and Petra walked among them doing the work that needed doing—counting heads, finding blankets, sending Sefa to organize the mapping of the new terrain, because Sefa had announced, with the ferocity of the recently gutted, that she intended to draw every road and river of this world before she died and would thank no one to pity her about it.

Vess found Petra at the water's edge near dusk.

The Collector-turned-woman sat on a half-submerged wall with her boots off and her feet in the retreating flood, and she looked—ordinary. That was the strangest thing. Two days ago she had been a hunter of the Quiet, a conditioned instrument of beings who harvested memory itself; now she was a tired woman in a travel-stained coat with a healing bruise on her cheekbone, swinging her feet in the shallows like a girl.

"They're all asleep down there," she said, nodding toward the Collectors' remains—or rather, toward the scattered men and women the Collectors had collapsed into, ordinary grieving humans now, some of whom had wandered into the Choir's camp and been fed, because the Choir's people were kind in the way of people who had just survived something. "Sleeping like the dead. First time in—well. A long time." She glanced up at Petra. "I dreamed last night. Do you understand? I haven't dreamed since I was made. I dreamed about a kitchen."

"What was in it?"

"Bread. Someone singing off-key. I don't know whose kitchen." Vess smiled, and it was a real smile, and it hurt to look at. "I keep waiting for the conditioning to come back and take it. It doesn't. It's just gone. You broke it with one verse." She shook her head. "Do you know what you are, Petra Blackwood? Do you have any idea?"

"A singer," Petra said. "A debtor. An archive." They sat down on the wall beside her. The water was cold against their palms. "Mostly tired."

Vess studied them for a while. She had sharp eyes, the kind that missed nothing—it was what she'd been built for, in the way that instruments are built—and Petra felt her seeing it anyway, the thing they were hiding, the weight behind the eyes.

"There's something you're not saying," Vess said. "About the cost. Everyone's celebrating and grieving and building fires, and you're sitting here like a woman at her own wake. What did the Door take from you?"

"Nothing," Petra said.

And then, because Vess had held a stair alone against the converging Collectors, because she had remembered her own name at the worst possible moment and chosen to spend it on a warning, because she was the only person in the world who might understand—Petra heard themselves add, quietly:

"Nothing was taken from me. That's the cost. Everything was given to me. Every memory of the old country, every name, every song—all of it lives in me now and nowhere else. I'm the only one who remembers Vessalune. I'm the only one who can mourn it. And I will carry it alone until I die, and when I die it dies, unless I find a way to make it otherwise." They looked out at the water, at the spire, at the impossible stars beginning to prick through the brightening sky like lamps lit early. "That was the bargain I made. I just didn't understand until afterward what *alone* meant."

Vess was silent a long time. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"My makers—the things I served—they started as choirs too. Did you know that? Human choirs who paid too much, who gave and gave until there wasn't enough left to stay human, and what remained learned to feed on memory because memory was all it had ever lost." She pulled her feet from the water. "You could become that. You understand. One person holding a whole country—if it curdles, if the loneliness turns you—"

"I know," Petra said.

"So what will you do?"

Petra looked down at their own hands, the singer's hands, scarred and steady. Somewhere behind them the camp was lighting its fires, and the smoke rose straight up into a sky so full of named and nameless lights that it looked like the whole history of the world written out in a script no one could read—except, now, one person.

"I'm going to write it down," Petra said. "All of it. Every name I hold. And then I'm going to decide who deserves to read it."

Far off across the black fields, where the flood had drained furthest, something glinted that had not glinted before—a curve of pale stone emerging from the mud, deliberate, shaped, man-made. Sefa was already walking toward it with her satchel and her measuring cord, calling over her shoulder for paper.

Petra stood, and followed, and did not look back at the cathedral.
