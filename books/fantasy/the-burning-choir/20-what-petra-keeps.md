## 20. What Petra Keeps

The book was ordinary. That was the first thing Petra decided, and the decision mattered more than they could have explained to anyone—though there was no one left to explain it to.

It was a ledger, once. Sefa had found it in the flooded customs house at the valley line, its pages swollen and warped into soft waves, the columns of tolls and grain weights bleached to ghosts. She had pressed it flat beneath stones for a month, then oiled the cover until the leather remembered how to be leather again. "Every map starts as a blank page," she'd said, setting it on Petra's table without ceremony. "Yours just happens to be a country."

Petra had not touched it for six days after that.

They sat now in the small stone house above the terraced gardens—the house that had been Mother Anselm's, that the elders had offered them with averted eyes and hands that would not quite meet theirs—and watched the light move across the blank first page. Morning came late to Stillwater now. The stars were back, all of them, burning in their ancient stations, but the sun seemed to have learned caution. It rose the way a survivor rises from a sickbed: slowly, testing the floor.

Outside, someone was singing. It was one of the younger storm-callers, a girl named Odile who had been seven when the sky went dark and eleven when it returned, running through the morning scales the way children everywhere ran through anything—badly, joyfully, with no idea what the notes had cost. The melody drifted up through the shutters and settled over Petra like dust.

*That is the fourth mode of the Vesperine,* Petra thought. *You are singing it a fifth low. Anselm would have rapped your knuckles.*

And then, as always, the thought arrived behind the thought: *No one else alive knows that.*

The knowledge sat in Petra's chest the way a stone sits in a streambed—worn smooth by repetition, immovable, quietly reshaping everything that flowed past it. They knew the name of every village between the valley rim and the salt coast. They knew that the people of Harrow Fen tied blue thread to their doors in the last week of autumn, and that the thread was not decoration but a ward against a spirit whose name meant *the thing that counts your breaths*. They knew seventeen words for rain in the dialect of the drowned cathedral's city, and which of those words were considered unlucky to speak indoors. They knew the old country's true name—not the name on maps, not the name the Collectors had catalogued, but the name Tam had spent his final word to give them, the name that had completed the third voice and opened the First Door.

They knew it the way a widow knows her marriage: completely, uselessly, alone.

Petra dipped the pen.

The first word took an hour. Not because they didn't know it—they knew ten thousand words—but because writing felt like a kind of betrayal, or perhaps a kind of burial, and they needed to be certain which. In the end they wrote the name of the capital city, Vessamare, in careful letters, and beneath it: *Population before the Debt: unknown to me. I will write down everyone I ever met from there, and mark the rest uncounted.*

Then the dam broke, as dams do, all at once and forever.

* * *

They wrote through the spring.

They wrote the songs first, because songs were the most fragile things—paper held words well enough, but melodies lived only in throats, and throats were mortal. Petra transcribed every piece of the Choir's hidden repertoire, the modes and counter-modes, the storm-calls and the still-calls, the lullaby Anselm had used to teach them breath control by making them sing it lying down with a stone on their chest. Beneath each song they wrote its history, its regional variants, the occasions on which it was forbidden. The margins filled with phonetic notes for dialects that no longer existed anywhere but in Petra's mouth.

Then the names. This was slower, and worse. Petra made lists—birth records reconstructed from memory, market days, the names of ferry-men and midwives and the woman in Vessamare who sold roasted chestnuts outside the assize courts and called every customer *little bird* regardless of age or rank. When memory failed, Petra wrote *I do not remember*, and found, to their surprise, that writing the failure down eased something. A gap acknowledged was a gap honored. A gap ignored was a grave dug shallow.

Sefa came most evenings. She never asked to see the pages, and Petra never offered, and this mutual restraint was one of the deepest kindnesses either of them had ever performed. Instead Sefa spread her own work across the other end of the table—her new maps, the ones she was drafting of the world as it now was, the world with a hole in it shaped like everything Petra carried.

"The coastal survey is done," she said one night, tapping a sheet dense with soundings and coastline. "Or as done as it gets. There's a bay south of the old border that isn't on any surviving chart. It just... wasn't recorded. Or it was, and the recording went with everything else." Her pen hovered. "I've named it Tam's Reach. I hope that's—I wanted you to know before I ink it permanent."

Petra looked at the bay. It was beautiful, the way Sefa's work always was—every contour lovingly argued, every depth sounding rendered like a confession. "He would have hated having water named after him," Petra said. "He was afraid of deep water. He told me once, using my own words from three years earlier, that drowning was just forgetting too fast."

Sefa's laugh cracked in the middle. She inked the name anyway.

It had taken months for the fury to burn down to ash, and longer for something green to grow through the ash. Petra still remembered the night by the river—the night Sefa had confessed to selling maps to the Collectors, the night Petra had stood with their hand around a knife hilt and their whole body shaking with the arithmetic of betrayal. *She fed them the shape of countries,* Petra had thought. *She helped them eat.* And Sefa had stood there and taken it, hollow-eyed, saying only: *Yes. And I am still here, and I am sorry, and those two facts are both true, and you may weigh them however you like.*

What had saved them—not that night, but eventually—was the understanding Petra had resisted longest: that Sefa's sin and Petra's survival were the same sin wearing different clothes. Everyone in the old country had bargained with the dark in some currency. The Choir had paid in sky. Sefa had paid in maps. Petra had paid, in the end, in solitude, and was paying it still, daily, at a rate they had set themselves.

"You're staring at page four hundred," Sefa observed. "The one with the funeral rites."

"I'm deciding whether to include the mourning bread recipe," Petra said. "It feels small. Next to everything else."

"Include it." Sefa said it with the flat certainty she reserved for cartographic corrections. "A country isn't its wars. It's what people ate while the wars happened. Trust me—I've mapped enough places to know what survives and what doesn't. Recipes survive. Borders don't."

So Petra included the mourning bread: barley flour, honey, a coin baked into the loaf so the dead would have passage-money, the coin given afterward to a child so the living would remember that grief circulates. They wrote it out twice, once in the old script and once plain, and their eyes blurred on the second copy, and they let them.

* * *

The book was finished in the first cold week of autumn, fourteen months after the sky reopened. Four hundred and sixty-one pages. Petra read it through once, aloud, alone, in a voice that grew hoarse by the third day and did not stop until the last line—which was not a grand statement but a note in the margin style Anselm had taught them: *If you are reading this and I am gone, the words are yours now. Sing them badly. Sing them often. Badly and often beats beautifully and never.*

Then Petra closed the book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and did not publish it.

The offer had come, of course. It came the way offers come to people who have survived remarkable things—gently at first, then persistently. Scholars from the coastal universities wanted copies. A printer in the river towns proposed a fine edition with engravings. Even the reformed Choir, humbled and half-remade, petitioned Petra to deposit the book in their new archive, promising climate-controlled rooms and scholarly access and the full weight of institutional memory.

Petra declined all of it, and understood, as they declined, that the reasons would sound like madness if spoken plainly.

Because a published book is a book that can be argued with. A deposited archive is an archive that can be burned—it had been burned once already; Petra had sifted the ashes of the Choir's library with their own hands. An institution's memory is an institution's hostage. Anything given away can be corrected, edited, dismissed, or seized, and the Collectors might be grieving humans now, ordinary and broken and pitiable, but the hunger that had made them was not extinct. It was only sleeping. Somewhere, someday, something would get hungry for memory again, and Petra refused to leave a feast laid out on the table.

But underneath the strategy, honest as a stone at the bottom of a well, lay the truer reason: some things cannot survive being common. The old country had died once by theft. If its memory became a curiosity—a chapter in a survey, a footnote in a ballad collection—it would die a second time by dilution, and Petra could not bear to be the agent of that. Better one keeper than a hundred forgetters. Better a single flame tended nightly than a bonfire abandoned after the festival.

So Petra kept it. Kept the book under the floorboard beneath the hearthstone, where the warmth would keep the damp out. And kept the country the only way left that could not be stolen: in the body, in the throat, in the daily discipline of recitation.

Every morning, before the sun cleared the valley rim, Petra stood at the window and recited. The names of the nine provinces. The seventeen words for rain. The Vesperine modes, all twelve, sung at proper pitch. The list of the dead as far as memory allowed—this took the longest now, nearly an hour, and Petra did not shorten it even when rain came through the shutters or when Odile knocked impatiently for her lesson. The dead had waited long enough. They could wait an hour more.

The neighbors noticed, of course. Stillwater was a small place built on the ruins of a lie, and its people had developed a keen ear for private rituals. Some thought Petra mad. Some thought them holy. The elders, who had voted to seal the valley and sentence the defiant to exile-without-name, now deferred to Petra in matters of doctrine with a humility so complete it curdled into avoidance. They wept sometimes, Petra knew, without knowing why—weeping at odd moments, at certain chords, at the sight of the northern horizon. Grief with no address. The whole valley carried it, a homesickness for a home that had been erased from them along with itself.

Only Sefa understood what the recitation was. She had heard it through the wall on her visits, and one evening she had said, without preamble: "You know what you're doing, don't you? You're not remembering it. You're keeping it alive the way you keep a fire alive. Feed it or lose it. Every single day, feed it or lose it."

"Yes," Petra had said.

"And when you die?"

Petra had looked at the fire for a long time. "Then I had better find someone to hand the poker to."

* * *

Winter came, and with it the question Petra had been circling since the day the book was finished.

It arrived in the form of Odile, standing in the doorway with snow in her dark hair and the particular stubbornness of a fifteen-year-old who has decided something. Behind her, half-hidden, stood two others—Bram, the ferry-man's son, and a quiet girl named Isbet who had lost her parents to the flood and spoken maybe forty words a week.

"We want to learn the real songs," Odile said. "Not the ones the elders teach. The ones you sing at your window."

"They're not mine to teach," Petra began, and stopped, because the sentence was a lie, and lies had cost this valley enough.

They were not mine, Petra thought. They were Anselm's, and hers were the founders', and theirs were the old country's, and the old country is dead except for me. Which means they are mine now, entirely, the way a wreck washed ashore belongs to whoever finds it. And a wreck belongs to the finder only if the finder does something with it. Leave it on the beach and the sea takes it back.

"Come in," Petra said. "All three of you. Take off your wet boots."

They sat at the table where Sefa's maps had dried and the ledger had been written, and Petra brought out nothing—no songbook, no manuscript, nothing written, because the written thing was for keeping and the living thing was for giving. "What I'm going to teach you," Petra said, "you will think are inventions. Songs I made up. Modes nobody uses. Words in a language that doesn't exist." They looked at three young faces, snow-damp and waiting. "Let yourselves think that. It's kinder. But learn them exactly. Every note, every syllable, exactly. Because they are older than this valley, and they are all that's left of somewhere that was real, and one day—one day far from now, if I've done this right—you'll understand what you're carrying, and you'll carry it anyway."

Isbet, the quiet one, spoke her weekly allotment of words then, and chose them well: "Why us?"

Petra had asked themselves the same question for a season. Why these three, out of all the valley's children? The answer, when it finally surfaced, was simple and terrible and true.

"Because you're already unmade, a little," Petra said gently. "Odile, you were born in a valley that turned out to be a cage, and you've had to rebuild what 'home' means from scratch. Bram, your father's trade died with the old roads and he pretends it didn't, and you've learned to hold a truth and a kindness in the same hand. Isbet—" and here Petra's voice softened, "—you know what it is to lose everything and keep speaking anyway. That's the qualification. Not talent. Talent we can train. What can't be trained is knowing that memory is a labor, and choosing it."

Outside, the snow fell on the terraced gardens, on the ward-ruins at the valley line where wildflowers now grew through the shattered stone, on the whole quiet world that did not know what it had lost and was building something new on top of the not-knowing. Inside, Petra opened their mouth and began to teach the first mode of the Vesperine, properly pitched, and three young voices stumbled after theirs into the dark of a dead country, carrying it forward one wrong-but-eager note at a time.

Later—much later, when the lesson was done and the children had gone laughing down the snowy lane—Petra heard footsteps on the path. Deliberate ones. Familiar ones, though they came only once a year.

Petra did not turn from the window. They smiled instead, and reached for the kettle, and said to the empty room, to the book beneath the hearthstone, to the nine provinces and the seventeen rains and the little bird who sold chestnuts outside the assize courts:

"She's early this year. Good. I have a true story I've been saving."
