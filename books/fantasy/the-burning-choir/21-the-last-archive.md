## 21. The Last Archive

The schoolhouse at Stillwater had once been a granary, and before that, if the beams were honest, a chapel to some god whose name had gone out with the old country. Petra Blackwood had chosen it for exactly that palimpsest quality—the way the walls held their history in layers, plaster over stone over older stone, so that a child pressing her palm against the cool interior might, without knowing why, feel that she was touching something with patience in it.

Twenty-two years now since the sky came back. Twenty-two years since the water of the drowned cathedral had parted like a held breath released, and the stars had kindled overhead one by one, each one flaring into being with a sound Petra alone could hear—a sound like a name spoken by someone who loved you. The others had stood in the flood-mud and wept without knowing what they wept for. Sefa had finished hollow-eyed and smiling. Tam was gone, spent down to his last remembered word, which he had given away freely, generously, the way he had given everything: other people's words, all his life, until the final one was his own gift.

Petra remembered all of it. That was the arrangement. That was the price, still accruing interest every single day.

"Teacher." Maren tugged at Petra's sleeve. She was nine, gap-toothed, impatient in the way of children who had never known a dark sky and therefore could not imagine one. "You're doing the faraway face again."

"The faraway face," Petra said, "is a professional instrument. Like your pitch pipe."

"It's a sad face."

"It's an *old* face." Petra smiled and set down the chalk. Eleven children sat on benches salvaged from three different buildings, arranged in a rough semicircle around the practice circle Petra had scored into the floorboards—concentric rings, because storm-calling began with circles, always had, though none of these children knew that the rings they learned were copies of copies of a design whose original had been sung into existence by people whose names existed now only in Petra's mouth. "Where were we?"

"The rain song," said Oskar, who was solemn and heavy-browed and could hold a low note like a stone holds cold. "The one you made up."

"The one I made up," Petra agreed, and felt the small familiar dishonesty settle into place, worn smooth as a river stone by two decades of handling. It was not entirely a lie. Every song they taught was an invention in the way that a translation is an invention—faithful to something no living soul could check them against. They had taken the great working of the First Door, the verse that had unmade the Debt and reopened the sky, and broken it apart like bread, distributing it among these children in pieces small enough to carry safely. The rain song was four bars of the second voice. The wind exercise was a fragment of the third. The lullaby they sang at winter solstice—that was the opening verse itself, disguised, slowed, tucked into words about sheep.

The elders would have called it blasphemy, if any of the elders lived. What remained of the Choir was a handful of gray heads in the valley townships, women and men who wept sometimes at festivals without knowing why, who felt a grief move through them like weather and could not chart its source. Petra had stopped trying to explain. Grief without an object, they had learned, does not want explanation. It wants company.

"From the top," Petra said. "Maren, you're the thunder line. Don't push it—thunder isn't loud because it tries, it's loud because it's *far*."

Maren screwed up her face and produced a note that cracked the window nearest the door, which everyone pretended not to notice, per tradition.

* * *

Vess arrived, as she always did, on the first hard frost of autumn, walking up the valley road from the direction of the pass with a traveler's pack and a face that had grown neither older nor kinder in twenty years, only more settled, the way a scar settles. She came to the schoolhouse door at dusk, when the children had gone home to suppers they'd complain about, and she knocked the way she always knocked—three times, evenly spaced, like someone reporting rather than visiting.

"You're late," Petra said, opening the door. "Frost came eight days ago."

"I was in the east. There's a village past the marshes where they've started singing harvest songs in rounds, and nobody remembers teaching them the rounds." Vess stepped inside, stamping the cold from her boots. She looked around the room with the same expression she brought every year—not nostalgia, nothing so warm, but inventory. Checking that the world was still holding its shape. "I wanted to see if the pattern was spreading or if I'd imagined it."

"And?"

"And it's spreading." She sat down heavily on a child's bench, knees complaining, and for a moment she looked less like a former agent of the Collectors and more like what she had been underneath all along: a tired woman who had once paid too much for something she couldn't name. "Songs are leaking through, Petra. Fragments. A round here, a refrain there. Things that sound like inventions but land in the ear like memories."

Petra poured tea from the pot that never quite went cold—storm-callers' hearths held heat the way other hearths held grudges—and handed her a cup. "That's the working settling. The sky didn't just come back; it came back *distributed*. Everything we sang is woven into the weather now. Of course it leaks."

"Mm." Vess drank. Outside, the first stars were coming out over the valley rim, and both of them, by long habit, paused to watch. Petra counted them the way a miser counts coins—all present, all named. Vess counted them the way a former jailer counts prisoners—relieved, and unable to stop checking the locks. "You know why I come."

"One true story," Petra said. "Same terms as always."

"Same terms." Vess set down the cup. Her hands were steady; they had been steady even in the cathedral, even holding the stair against the Collectors while the floodwater rose and sang. "Tell me one true thing about the old country. Anything. You choose."

This was the ritual, and like all rituals it had hardened into form without losing its weight. Once a year, Vess climbed out of whatever life she had made for herself in the rebuilt cities of the coast—she worked with the grieving now, the Collectors' human remnants, teaching them to mourn instead of harvest—and once a year she sat in this schoolhouse and asked to be told one true thing, because she had spent thirty years taking memories and could not bear to hold a single false one anymore.

Petra considered. They had told her about the harbor at Vel Anseth, where the tide came in silver. About Mother Anselm's hands, cracked from chalk and cold, writing marginalia by candlelight. About the taste of plum wine at the Founding Rite, before the Founding became a lie. About Tam's laugh, which sounded like other people's laughter assembled into something new.

Tonight they said: "There was a hill above the choir school called the Listening Place. Children were sent up there in storms to learn pitch—you had to find the exact center of the thunder's frequency and hold it, alone, in the dark, with lightning close enough to taste the metal in the air. And every child who ever did it came down changed. Not braver. Quieter. Because once you've matched your voice to a storm, you understand that the storm isn't angry. It's just large. Most things we call anger are just largeness we haven't learned to sing to yet."

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she let them be wet, which had taken her perhaps fifteen years to learn. "The Collectors had a word for that," she said. "Before we were Collectors. Our own teachers used to say it. *Largeness.*"

"I know," Petra said gently. "I've heard it in the fragments you hum."

They sat in silence while the frost ticked against the windows. Then Vess stood, shouldered her pack, and paused at the door. "Next year," she said, "I may bring someone. There's a girl on the coast—one of ours, third generation, born after. She hears things in the wind that she shouldn't be able to hear. Songs with structure. I thought—" She stopped, jaw working. "I thought maybe it was time she heard where the structure comes from. If you'll have her."

Petra looked at this woman who had hunted them across half a drowned country, who had held a stairway against her own kind so that a song could finish, who remembered nothing of the country she had helped destroy but came every year to collect one true piece of it, honestly, the way other people collected stamps. Belonging, Petra had learned, was not something you recovered. It was something you built afterward, on honest terms, smaller than before, and real in the way that only small real things are real.

"Bring her," Petra said. "Tell her to bring her own pitch pipe. We're short on thunder lines."

* * *

After Vess left, Petra climbed to the loft where they slept and took down the book.

It was not a grand object. Leather gone soft as cloth, corners rounded by handling, a strap of oiled canvas to keep it shut. Inside, in Petra's hand, in ink refreshed twice over the decades, was the old country entire: every township and its founding song, every elder of the Choir back six generations, the names of the drowned streets of Vel Anseth, the recipe for plum wine, the eleven tones of the Listening Place, Tam's true name and the word he had spent, Sefa's homeland rendered in prose because Sefa herself had burned hers singing and asked Petra, afterward, hollow-eyed and smiling, *tell me what I lost—I want to hear it in someone else's voice.*

Every night, before sleep, Petra recited a portion of it aloud. Not reading—reciting, eyes closed, testing the archive against memory the way a bellringer tests a rope. Tonight it was the section on the harbor towns, and as they spoke the names—Vel Anseth, Corrin's Landing, the Nine Steps—they felt the usual thing happen, the thing that never dulled: the names rose up out of them and hung in the dark room like lanterns, and somewhere far off, in the weave of the restored sky, something answered. Not words. Just acknowledgment. The way a chord answers its root.

Sefa had offered, once, early on, to help publish it. *Let them know what they lost. Let them mourn properly.* And Petra had refused, and the refusal had nearly ended a friendship already forged in floodwater, until Sefa understood: a published book becomes a curiosity. A recited memory stays alive. As long as Petra carried it in the body—in breath, in daily speech—the old country was not dead, merely quiet, kept the way embers are kept, banked and tended and passed hand to hand. One day, when the right child appeared with steady hands and a voice that could hold largeness, the whole of it would go to her whole, and she would think she was learning songs, and she would be learning a country.

Below, in the schoolroom, the chalk lay where Petra had left it, beside the practice circles scored in the floor. Tomorrow the children would come again, gap-toothed and impatient, and learn the rain song they believed their teacher had invented, and complain about supper, and crack a window or two. Petra banked the fire, and touched the book once before closing the strap, and stood a moment at the small window looking up.

Over the valley rim, low in the northeast, a star was burning that had no business being there. Petra knew every star in the restored sky by name—had named, in the privacy of twenty-two years, each one for someone the old country had lost. This one was new. Or rather, it was old, and newly returned, flickering at the edge of vision like a word on the tip of the tongue.

Petra watched it for a long time, tea going cold in their hands, and felt the archive stir inside them, turning toward that faint light the way a sleeping child turns toward a familiar voice.

"That's not one of mine," Petra murmured to the dark. "Whose are you?"

The star flickered—once, twice—like an answer forming.
