## 22. Coda: One Star, Named

The climb took Petra Blackwood the better part of the morning now, though once—forty years ago, on the night the stars went dark—it had taken them no time at all to run this same slope in terror, barefoot, their songbook clutched against their chest like a heart they'd been handed by someone else.

They stopped twice on the way up. The first time was for breath; the second was for the view, which had earned its toll a thousand times over and never once grown old. The Stillwater Valley spread below them in its autumn colors, gold and rust stitched together with the silver thread of the river, and beyond it the lowlands where the drowned cathedral's spire still broke the water like a finger raised in permanent question. The flood had never fully receded. The cartographers—Sefa Ilves chief among them, though she'd long since trained others to hold her instruments—had mapped the drowned district down to its doorways, but no one had drained it. It seemed right, somehow, that some part of the old world should remain underwater, sleeping where it had been paid away, even after everything that had been sung back.

Petra leaned on their walking stick—a plain thing of ash wood, unadorned—and let the wind move through their white hair. Seventy-eight years old. Their hands had gone knuckled and thin, spotted like old paper, but they were steady. They had made sure of that. Every morning for forty years, before tea, before speech, before anything, Petra had stretched those hands and flexed them and told them what they were for. *You are the last archive,* they would say. *Be worthy of it.*

The valley below was full of life. Smoke rose from the chimneys of Stillwater town in straight, unhurried columns. Children were visible as small bright dots along the river path, walking home from lessons. Somewhere down there, a bell was ringing the hour—not the Founding Rite bell, which had been melted down decades ago at Sefa's dry suggestion that a country ought not to celebrate the day it sold its sky, but an ordinary bell, for an ordinary hour, in a country that finally knew its own name again.

Not the old name. Never the old name. That name lived in exactly one place in all the world, and it was climbing this hill.

* * *

They reached the rim as the light began to fail.

There was a flat stone up here that generations of shepherds had used as a landmark, and Petra had claimed it as a kind of study, though it held no books and admitted no students. They sat down slowly, arranging their coat beneath them against the cold, and looked east, where the first stars were beginning to arrive.

Forty years, and Petra had never gotten over the arrival of stars. Other people treated them as furniture—fixed, assumed, barely noticed except when clouds hid them. But Petra had seen the sky empty. Had stood in the choir loft of the burned archive on the worst night of the century and watched the darkness come down not like night but like a lid, total and deliberate, a ceiling instead of a heaven. They had felt the wards shatter through their teeth. They had heard Mother Anselm's voice, weeks later, dying and certain, explain what the founders had done: *We bought the war with the sky, child. Debts don't die just because the debtors do.*

And so every clear evening since, Petra had looked up and counted. Not all of them—they'd never been able to fix a number, the sky being what it was—but enough. Enough to know the difference between a sky and a memory of one.

Tonight the counting went quickly. The season was turning toward winter and the air was clean and hard, and the stars came out in their hundreds, then their thousands, sharp as nailheads driven into black velvet. Petra found the ones they always found first: the Shepherd's Yoke low in the south, the three sisters of the Loom, the pale wanderer that Sefa had spent two years proving was not a star at all but something farther and stranger, and had then drawn onto her maps anyway, because a map should tell the truth about what it doesn't understand.

Then Petra did what they had come to do.

They stood. It took a moment. The stone helped.

And they spoke the name of the old country aloud.

It was not a word that existed anymore in any living mouth but theirs. It had been a country of terraced hills and salt-roads and choirs who sang weather into being, a country whose language had folded itself around storm-calling the way a hand folds around a tool. When the Debt was reversed—when Petra had stood in the flooded nave with Tam's last word burning in their throat and Sefa's voice cracking beside them and the Collectors pouring down the stairwell like black water—the magic had gone home, the stars had reignited, and every soul who remembered the old country had forgotten it in the same instant, everyone but the singer. The elders had wept in the shallows without knowing why. Sefa had finished her verse hollow-eyed and smiling, and by morning she could not have said what she'd lost, only that she'd given it gladly. Tam—

Tam was a star now. Not literally. But Petra had named one for him thirty-nine years ago, working quietly through the proper channels, which in those days meant writing letters until someone with authority got tired of saying no. The star sat in the southern sky above where his forgotten town had been, small and steady, and every year on the anniversary of the singing, Petra climbed here and greeted it first.

But tonight, as on every night of this particular ritual, they spoke the country's whole name—all nine syllables of it, each one a room in a house only they still kept—and they watched the sky.

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. Petra had never expected it to. The ritual was not a prayer; it was maintenance. A name spoken daily does not fade. A name spoken alone, into the dark, by the last person who owns it—that name stays *alive*, which is a different thing from staying, and harder work.

But tonight—perhaps it was the clarity of the air, perhaps the angle of the season, perhaps only the treachery of old eyes—one star flickered.

Just once. Just slightly. Low in the east, in a region of sky Petra had never particularly claimed.

Petra went very still.

Wind moved through the dry grass along the rim. Down in the valley, the ordinary bell rang the half-hour, indifferent and lovely. And the star did not flicker again, and Petra understood—or chose to understand, which at seventy-eight amounts to the same thing—that the sky had not answered them. That stars are furnaces older than any country, and countries are brief candles, and the dead do not speak through pinpoints of hydrogen fire.

But they had seen it. And for one long moment, standing alone on the rim of the valley that had been their prison and then their salvation and then simply their home, Petra Blackwood allowed themselves the luxury of believing, completely and without evidence, that somewhere in all that burning distance, something had heard its name and turned toward the sound.

"Goodnight," they said to the star. "I'm coming to you eventually. There's no hurry."

* * *

The child was waiting at the bottom of the hill, as arranged.

She was eleven years old, called Wren, and she had the particular quality that Petra had spent three years watching for among the valley's children: steady hands. Not talented hands—talent was common, and mostly useless. Steady hands. Hands that did not fidget when the world got strange. Petra had seen her sit through a thunderstorm without flinching, had seen her copy out a map of the drowned district in Sefa's schoolroom with lines so true that Sefa herself had grunted, which was the highest praise her former partner ever issued in public.

Wren stood at the foot of the path with a lantern, because she was sensible, and a satchel, because she was curious, and she did not rush up the hill to meet the old storm-caller, because she had already learned the most important lesson: that some things must be approached at the speed they permit.

"You saw the star," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been told to watch the eastern sky tonight, and she had, and she'd seen the old woman stop halfway down the hill and stand motionless for the space of fifty heartbeats. Wren had counted.

"I saw a star," Petra corrected gently. "What I *saw* and what happened are two different subjects, and knowing the difference between them is the first thing I'm going to teach you."

"Teach me?" Wren's voice did something complicated—hope trying very hard to look like composure.

Petra lowered themselves onto the flat boulder beside the path and patted the stone beside them. The child sat. Up close, in the lantern light, Petra studied her face: the patience in it, the watchfulness, the faint stubborn set of the mouth that reminded Petra painfully of another stubborn child, decades ago, exiled to a valley they didn't understand, holding a songbook they couldn't read.

"The songs I teach," Petra said, "the other children think are inventions. Old nonsense I've dressed up. Storm-songs, naming-songs, a lullaby in a language nobody speaks." They paused. "That language is real. It belonged to a country that no longer exists anywhere in the world except in one book and one head. Mine. Tonight I'm going to tell you why, and tomorrow we begin your first lesson, and if you listen well, in twenty or thirty years, when my voice is done, there will be two heads. Then four. Then more. That is how a country survives its own death—not by walls, not by wards. By pupils."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then, with the directness Petra had chosen her for: "Is it a heavy thing? Carrying a whole country?"

Petra laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of them, echoing off the dark hillside.

"Yes," they said. "It is the heaviest thing there is. And I will tell you a secret about heaviness, Wren, one it took me most of a lifetime to learn: a weight carried *together* stops being a burden and becomes a belonging. I carried it alone for forty years because I thought that was the price. It was only the price of doing it badly."

They opened the satchel.

The book inside was not large. It had been rebound twice; the leather was new but the pages were soft as cloth at the edges, worn by forty years of daily handling. On the cover, tooled in a script no living reader could parse, was a title. Inside were maps in Sefa's precise reconstructed hand, drawings of terraced hills and salt-roads, diagrams of choir formations, and page after page after page of names—every name Petra had saved, cross-referenced, annotated, loved. And threaded through it all, in Petra's own aging script, the songs. All of them. Even the one that had reopened the sky.

"This is yours to learn," Petra said. "It is not yours to keep. Nothing in this book belongs to any one of us. That's rather the point of it."

Wren took the book with both hands, and her hands did not shake, and Petra felt something in their chest loosen that had been tight for forty years—not grief, exactly, and not relief either, but the specific peace of a sentry hearing footsteps that mean the watch is changing.

"Read me the first line," Petra said. "Don't worry about getting it right. You can't get it wrong yet. Wrong comes later, and it's useful."

Wren opened the book. The lantern threw their two shadows long across the path, down the hill, toward the valley with its lit windows and its honest bell and its stars overhead past counting. The child sounded out the first syllables of a dead country's name, mangling them beautifully, and above the rim of the world, in the east, the small pale star that might have been listening held its place in the dark—steady, patient, keeping its light for whoever came climbing next.

Petra closed their eyes and listened, and for the first time in forty years, the archive was not the only one who remembered.

The lesson had begun.
