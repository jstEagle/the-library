## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The waystation smelled of tallow, old smoke, and the particular sourness of a place where people had been drinking to forget things for a hundred years. Casper Reyes sat in the corner farthest from the door with their back against a wall of rough-hewn pine and their mistress's stolen cloak pulled up around their ears, and they told themselves—for the eleventh time that night—that they were not hiding.

They were resting. There was a difference. Mistress Ivenna had always said so, in that voice like gravel poured over honey: *Resting is what you do when you've earned it, girl. Hiding is what you do when you haven't.* And then she would look at Casper over her spectacles until the distinction became very clear.

Well. Casper had run from the cottage three days ago with nothing but the clothes on their back, two heels of black bread, and a burn on their palm shaped like the sigil for *obedience*, which Ivenna had seared there as a lesson during their last argument. So perhaps it was hiding. Perhaps it was both. The candle-glow of the waystation was low and greasy, and the smugglers at the long table played dice with the grim economy of people who had killed over less than a copper bit, and nobody looked at the thin figure in the corner twice. That was the whole point of places like this. That was why Casper had walked half a night through freezing rain to find it.

For one night—just one—nobody in the world was looking for an apprentice named Casper Reyes. Nobody was saying *where is the girl, where did she go, fetch her back, she's needed.* For one night, Casper belonged to no one's ledger, no one's lesson plan, no one's disappointment. They let the guilty warmth of that thought spread through their chest like cheap wine, and they hated themselves a little for how good it tasted.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the waystation's single window—a square of horn and oilcloth, yellowed with age—Casper could see a sliver of the mountain pass and, above it, the sky.

That was where the trouble started. Though of course Casper didn't know it yet. At that moment the trouble was still forty miles east, sitting politely on the brow of a king who had forgotten its own name.

* * *

It began with the wards.

Casper felt them before anything else—the way you feel a struck bell in your teeth before your ears catch the sound. Every hair on their arms lifted. The dice-players went quiet mid-throw, one man's hand frozen above the cup, because whatever ran beneath the skin of the world had just shuddered, and even people with no craft in them at all felt the floor of things tilt.

Then the horizon lit.

It came from the east, beyond the pass, beyond Thornfell's border stones: a wall of pale gold light rising into the clouds like dawn arriving from the wrong direction, at midnight, from the wrong quarter of the world. The light crawled along the ridgelines, tracing paths Casper recognized with a lurch in their stomach—ley-lines, ward-channels, the great standing circuits that ringed the whole of the Thornfell marches. Ivenna had made Casper copy their geometry a hundred times. *Learn the shape of the cage before you ask what's inside it,* she'd said, and Casper had rolled their eyes and copied it anyway, never once asking what the cage was *for*, because asking questions got you extra lessons and burned palms.

Now those channels blazed like rivers of molten glass pouring uphill, and the whole eastern sky turned the color of a fever.

"Gods' teeth," someone said near the fire. "What is that?"

Nobody answered. In a room full of smugglers, silence was its own kind of answer.

Casper stood without deciding to stand. Their body knew something their mind refused to say aloud: *that's her work. That's her circuitry. That's the great ward-net, and it's dying.*

Because it wasn't burning bright the way a healthy ward burns, steady and sure as a hearthstone. It was flaring—the wild, thrashing flare of a thing fighting. Casper had seen a rabbit caught in a snare flare exactly like that, all legs and terror, spending everything it had in one terrible minute. The light stuttered. Pulsed. Somewhere in its rhythm Casper could almost hear a voice, or the ghost of one, shouting words too large and too old for human throats.

Then, all along the eastern ridge, the light went out. Not fading—*out*, like fingers pinching wicks, section by section, running north along the mountains faster than any rider, faster than any bird. The darkness chased the gold across the sky and swallowed it, and the last Casper saw of the border wards of Thornfell was a single knot of brilliance hanging over the high pass like a lantern in a drowning man's fist—

—and then that too guttered, and died, and the night came down over the mountains like a lid.

The dice-players stared at each other. Someone laughed, high and uncertain. "Storm," he said. "Ward-storm. Happens sometimes." His voice said he didn't believe it either.

And that should have been the worst of it. A dead ward-net was catastrophe enough; Casper's hands were already shaking with the arithmetic of it, because if the wards were down then everything Ivenna had spent her life maintaining was down, and Ivenna would rather die than let that happen, and—

Casper's thoughts stopped. All of them. Because they had glanced at the window again, at that little square of horn and oilcloth, at the slice of sky above the pass.

And the stars were going out.

Not being covered. Not clouding over. Going out—one by one, then ten by ten, then in whole constellations at once, like a city seen from a hilltop at dusk where the lights are extinguished street by street by some careful, methodical hand. The Shepherd's Yoke vanished. The Riverwife dimmed to embers and then to nothing. Casper watched the North Nail—the star sailors steered by, the star carved over every temple door in the kingdom, the star that had hung in every sky of every story since stories began—flicker once, twice, like a candle in a draft.

And go dark.

The waystation was utterly silent now. Even the fire seemed to burn quieter, as though it understood company. Casper stood in the middle of the floor with their mouth open and their heart hammering somewhere up around their teeth, watching the last stars fail, and the feeling that rose in them was so enormous and so strange that it took a long moment to name it.

Dread. Yes. Dread, cold and total, the dread of a world whose ceiling had just been removed. But underneath it, threaded through it, shamefully intertwined with it:

A thrill.

Because the world was ending—or something like ending—and in all that vast unraveling, not one single person anywhere on the roads or in the towns or behind the desks of the Guild was thinking about a runaway apprentice. The whole sky had gone black, and Casper Reyes was, for the first time in their life, gloriously, terribly unimportant. They could feel the guilt of that thrill sitting in their stomach next to the fear, two stones in the same well, and neither one would sink.

"They'll come back," said the innkeeper, from behind his bar. He said it the way men say *it's just weather*. "Stars come back."

But Casper was already doing the other arithmetic, the worse one. Wards didn't fail on their own. Ward-nets the size of the Thornfell marches weren't built to fail—they were built to be *broken through*, or broken *from inside*, and either way somebody had done the breaking. And Ivenna—Ivenna who had worked those wards until her knuckles cracked, Ivenna who had gone gray early and never said why, Ivenna who had looked east every evening of Casper's apprenticeship with an expression Casper had always mistaken for simple habit—

Ivenna would have been standing at her cottage window right now, if she'd been alive to stand anywhere. Watching the same sky. Knowing exactly what it meant.

*I know what it means,* Casper thought, and the thought arrived with the certainty of a key turning. *I don't know how I know, but I know. Something has gotten out. Or something has gotten in. And she spent her whole life holding the door.*

The last star went out. The sky over the mountains was a perfect, seamless, absolute black, blacker than any night Casper had ever seen, a blackness with weight and texture, like velvet laid over the world by a hand that did not intend to lift it again. In that lightless vault, the mountains lost their shapes. The road below the window simply ceased to exist.

In the waystation, a smuggler quietly put down his dice and began, very seriously, to drink.

* * *

Casper didn't sleep. They sat by the fire after the others stumbled off to their bunks, feeding it splinters of crate-wood, and they tried not to think about the cottage—which was, of course, the only thing they could think about. Three days' walk back south. Three days through a countryside whose wards had just died, whose night had just been amputated. Ivenna was alone out there. Ivenna was old. Ivenna was—

Ivenna was the reason Casper had run, and the guilt of that sat down beside the guilt of the thrill, and now there were three stones in the well.

Toward the grey hour before dawn, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the cold crept in through every seam of the building, Casper heard boots on the stairs. Heavy ones. Deliberate ones.

Sefa Vane came down into the common room the way she came into everything: like weather. She was broad and brown and scarred along one jaw, with rings on six fingers and a knife visible in three places, and she looked at Casper sitting alone by the dead fire and did not look surprised.

"You're the witch's brat," she said. It wasn't a question. "Ivenna Okonkwo's stray. Saw you slip in past midnight, soaked through, wearing her cloak." She crossed the room and stood over Casper, blocking the firelight. "She paid my crew's blood-debt seven years ago, when the crown's men took my brother's memory and left him a stranger in his own kitchen. Your mistress put back what the enforcers scraped out of him. Cost her, too—I saw her hands shake for a month after. So." She crouched, bringing her face level with Casper's, and her eyes were sharp and dark and entirely awake. "Here's what I know, stray. Tonight the wards died. Tonight the stars went out. And tonight, of all nights, a runaway apprentice crawls into my waystation looking like the end of the world followed them down the mountain."

"I didn't—" Casper started.

"You didn't do it. No. But you might know what did." Sefa Vane held out one ringed hand, palm up. Not threatening. Waiting. "Your mistress is dead, girl. Word came up the valley road yesterday. Cottage burned to the foundation stones, no body found, and crown riders asking questions in every village between here and the ford."

The words went into Casper like the burn-sigil had gone into their palm: white, total, erasing everything else. The fire. The empty cottage. The mistress who had seared lessons into their skin and slipped honey-bread into their pack when she thought they weren't looking, and who was now ash and absence and a question mark on a burned foundation stone.

"So," Sefa said softly, watching Casper's face crumble and not looking away from it, because apparently she was the kind of person who never looked away. "You can sit here by this dead fire and hide, or you can come with me and my crew, and we can go find out what killed the sky. Your choice, stray. But choose fast. Crown riders reached the crossroads an hour ago, and they're asking after a runaway in a green cloak."

She stood, and held out her hand again, and waited.

Above the waystation, through the little horn window, the sky stayed perfectly, impossibly black—and somewhere out in that new darkness, something was already moving toward the mountains, patient as winter, hungry as a crown.
