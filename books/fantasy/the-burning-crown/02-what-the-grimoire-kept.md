## 2. What the Grimoire Kept

The cottage smelled of ash and cold tea when Casper came back for the body.

They had told themselves they wouldn't. They had walked three miles down the mountain road with a stolen loaf in their coat and a plan to keep walking, all the way to the coast, all the way out of Thornfell and its dying wards and its mistress who was dead on her kitchen floor—but their feet had turned at the fork without asking permission, and now here they were, standing in the doorway of the only home they'd ever known, watching dust drift through light that had no business being this gentle.

Mistress Yew lay where she had fallen, one hand still curled around the handle of the copper kettle, as if she'd been mid-pour when her heart gave out. Her silver hair had come loose from its braid and spread across the flagstones like frost. She looked small. That was the worst part. Forty years of Casper's life she had been enormous—her voice filling the rafters, her temper scorching the wallpaper, her presence so vast it seemed the cottage itself leaned toward her like a plant toward sun—and now she fit inside a patch of morning light barely wider than a hearth-rug.

Casper knelt. The floor was cold through their trousers. They touched her wrist out of some stubborn, childish hope and found what they already knew: nothing. Not even the residual warmth magic leaves behind when it goes out of a body quickly. Whatever had taken her had taken everything, cleanly, the way a snuffed candle takes the flame but leaves the smell of smoke.

"You could have waited," Casper said. Their voice cracked on the second word. "You absolute— you could have *waited*."

The kettle had spilled when she fell. A dark stain spread across the flags, long dried, shaped like nothing at all or possibly like a hand reaching. Casper found themselves staring at it until their eyes burned, then made themselves stand, made themselves move, because grief that stood still would drown them and there was work in this house that would not wait for mourning.

She had no family. She had never spoken of any. Which meant the cottage and everything in it—the jars of dried nettle and moonwort, the bundles of warding chalk, the tax ledgers she'd falsified with cheerful incompetence—was Casper's now, if they wanted it, and they did not know how to want anything this large.

They started with the kitchen because it was closest.

* * *

By noon they had buried her.

There was a plot behind the herb garden where the soil went soft and dark, and Casper dug with a spade whose handle fit their mistress's grip, not theirs, so that every stroke felt borrowed. They wrapped her in the good quilt from her bed—the blue one with the stars embroidered on it, which she'd claimed was a wedding gift from a husband who had never existed—and they said the words from the burial rite as best they remembered them, which was badly, because Mistress Yew had never once taken Casper to a funeral. *"We return you to the ground that fed you."* Something like that. The ground took her without complaint.

Casper sat on the fresh-turned earth afterward and ate the rest of the stolen loaf and felt the guilt of that settle somewhere under their ribs: eating bread over a grave, crumbs falling on the mound. But she would have laughed. She'd have said something sharp about waste and sentimentality, and then she'd have eaten half the loaf herself.

It was while they were washing the grave-dirt from their hands at the rain barrel that they noticed the study door standing open.

Mistress Yew kept that door locked. Always. Eleven years an apprentice and Casper had never once seen past it—had been threatened with extra chores, with docked meals, with one memorable threat involving a hex that made your eyebrows fall out in alphabetical order. The lock was a simple thing, iron, old-fashioned, and it hung open now like a mouth mid-sentence.

Inside, the room was smaller than Casper had imagined. They had built it up over the years into something grand—a vault, a treasury, a place where the real secrets lived—and instead it was narrow and low-ceilinged, lined with shelves that sagged under the weight of ordinary books. Herbals. Almanacs. A treatise on drainage ditches. Dust lay thick on everything except the desk, which was clear, and bare, and held exactly one object.

A book. Bound in grey leather gone soft with age, its corners worn round, its spine uncracked in years. On its cover, tooled in faded silver that caught the grey daylight and refused to shine, were two words:

*FOR CASPER.*

Their own name. Their name, in her handwriting, in silver, on a book locked behind a door she'd never opened for them.

Casper's hands shook so badly they could barely lift it.

* * *

The grimoire did not open easily. The spine resisted, stiff with disuse, and the first pages were dense with diagrams Casper half-recognized—ward-lattices, the kind Mistress Yew had taught them to draw in chalk and salt, though these were vaster, more intricate, drawn by a surer hand. Each lattice covered a full page and each was annotated in her cramped, slanting script, and it took Casper three pages of turning before they understood what they were looking at.

These were not practice drawings. These were the border wards of Thornfell. All of them. Every ward-stone from the Pass of Hollow Pines to the sea-cliffs at Greywater, mapped and keyed and maintained, page after page after page, in her handwriting, going back decades.

"She *made* them," Casper whispered. "She didn't just tend them. She *built* them."

Eleven years of sweeping floors and grinding herbs and copying recipes for cough syrup, and the whole time the woman upstairs had been holding up the sky of an entire province with her bare hands. Casper thought of the night three days ago—the wards flaring green and then guttering, the stars going out all at once like a lamp pinched between wet fingers—and felt something turn over in their chest.

They kept reading.

The later pages changed. The handwriting grew tighter, faster, the ink blotted with hurry. And the subject shifted. No more lattices. Instead, page after page of notes in margins, corrections, fragments, and always returning, circling back like a dog to a wound, to the same two words:

*The Crown.*

*"The crown is not regalia. It never was. It is a binding, and bindings are hungry by nature. This one has been feeding for longer than the kingdom has kept records, and it has learned efficiency. It does not take memories all at once—it takes them the way tide takes sand, grain by grain, so slowly the victim polishes his own cage and calls it peace."*

*"I have confirmed it again tonight. The king remembers his coronation and nothing true before it. His councilors remember only what serves the crown. The city remembers what the city is told. Aldmere is not governed by a man; it is digested by an object, and the object is nearly finished with him."*

Casper turned the pages faster. The entries grew ragged, undated, desperate.

*"If the wards fail, it will be because I failed. I have stretched them across forty years and they were meant to last a hundred, but the crown's hunger reaches further every winter, and I am old, and my hands shake, and there is no one—"*

The sentence stopped. Below it, in different ink, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*"There is Casper."*

Casper set the book down on the desk very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"No," they said aloud, to the empty room. "No, no, no. You don't get to do that."

But the book wasn't finished, and neither, apparently, was she.

The final entry occupied a single page near the end, written in a hand so steady it must have cost her everything, every letter placed like a stone in a wall:

*"Casper—if you are reading this, then I am dead, and the wards are broken, and you are angrier at me than you have ever been at anyone in your short, stubborn life. Good. Anger will carry you further than grief; grief sits down halfway and weeps.* 

*I knew what was coming. I have known since the night I first read the crown's signature in the ward-lines, eleven years ago, the day I took you in. Yes—I chose you because of this. I am not sorry. You have the blood for it, though you do not yet know what blood that is, and I ran out of time to tell you gently, so I will tell you plainly:* 

*The crown of Aldmere devours memory, and it is devouring the king alive, and when the last of him is gone the last star goes with him, and then the dark does not lift. Someone must unbind it. I built walls against the dark for forty years because I could not face the dark itself. You are younger than I was, and braver, and you deserve better than the cowardice I am handing down to you.* 

*Everything you need is in this book. Everything I could not say is in it too. Forgive me or don't—the work does not require forgiveness, only hands.* 

*Go to the capital. Do not travel the King's Road. Trust no one who looks at you with empty eyes, and trust, carefully, the ones who owe me debts—they exist, and they are fewer than they used to be.* 

*Burn nothing. Forget nothing. Remember everything, Casper Reyes, because remembering is the only weapon the crown cannot take unless you give it."*

The last line was smudged, as if a drop of water had fallen on it before the ink dried. Casper looked at the page and realized, distantly, that the drop was theirs, falling now, four days late.

They closed the grimoire. They held it against their chest and stood in the narrow dusty room where a woman had spent eleven years writing letters to a child she was preparing to orphan twice—once from her life, once from her lies—and Casper felt the grief curdle, thick and hot, into something with edges.

She had known. She had *known*, and she'd handed them floor-sweeping and cough syrup and bedtime stories about hedge-witches, and all the while she'd been drafting them like a soldier, like a tool, like the next pair of hands in a war she hadn't the stomach to finish. *Trust no one who looks at you with empty eyes.* What did that mean? Who were the people who owed her debts? What blood did Casper carry, and why had she written *though you do not yet know what blood that is* in ink that looked almost afraid?

"I'm not doing this," Casper said to the room, to her, to the grave visible through the window with its raw turned earth. "I'm a runaway apprentice with a stolen loaf and no boots worth the name. Find someone else."

The room said nothing. Dead women are excellent at silence.

Casper stood there until the light through the little window went amber, then grey, arguing with a book. And somewhere in the middle of the argument they heard themselves say *find someone else* and understood, with a lurch, that there was no one else. There had never been anyone else. She had spent forty years being the someone else, and she had died at a kettle with the work unfinished, and the stars were still dark, and somewhere east of here a king was forgetting his own name one grain at a time.

Casper looked down at the grimoire. At the silver letters worn soft by her thumbs, *FOR CASPER,* pressed there years ago by hands that had known this day was coming and prepared for it anyway, the way you store away blankets for a winter you're praying won't arrive.

"Fine," they whispered. It came out wrecked. They said it again, louder, and it came out angry, which was better. "Fine. But I'm keeping score, old woman. Every mile, every scar, every sleepless night—you owe me, and I'm collecting."

They took the grimoire, and her good cloak from its peg, and the warding chalk from the kitchen shelf, and they banked the hearth fire and left the door unlocked, because there was nothing left inside worth locking away.

Outside, the sky was doing the thing it had done every night since the wards fell: sitting there, starless, black as a closed eye. Casper pulled the cloak tight and looked east, toward the capital, toward a king they'd never met and a crown that ate memories, and felt the anger settle into something steadier—a coal, banked, patient, ready to be carried.

They were three steps down the garden path when they saw the lights.

Two of them, maybe three, bobbing along the mountain road below—not lanterns, the movement was wrong, too smooth, too even, like things being *pulled* rather than carried. Coming up the road. Toward the cottage. Toward the fresh grave and the open door and the girl-shaped silhouette standing in it.

Casper killed the thought of firelight, snuffed the candle of their own presence the way Mistress Yew had drilled into them a thousand times—*small, small, be small*—and slipped into the hedgerow with the grimoire against their heart, as the hollow lights came on up the road toward everything she had left behind.
