## 3. The Hollow Patrol

Casper heard them before they saw anything: a silence moving up the mountain road, the way snow silences things, swallowing the sound of their own footsteps until Casper could not tell if they were walking or floating. The pines stopped dripping. The wind forgot its business. Even the stream below the switchback seemed to hold its breath.

Then the lanterns came around the bend below—six of them, burning white and cold, without any warmth in the light at all. And behind each lantern walked a figure in the grey livery of the crown, and behind the figures walked nothing at all, because no shadow followed them. That was the detail that made Casper's stomach drop through the mountainside: six lanterns, six men, and not one shadow on the road, though the moonless dark pressed close enough to touch.

They had empty eyes. Not blind—Casper had seen blind eyes, milky and wandering. These were open, wet, working eyes that saw everything and held nothing, like windows into a house where all the furniture had been carried out. They swept the road as they came, unhurried, thorough, and one of them paused beside a shrine to the old king that some farmer's grandmother had built before Casper was born—a little stone niche with a weathered carving of a bearded man holding a sheaf of wheat.

The enforcer looked at it. Then he reached out and put his hand flat against the stone, and the carving *ran*, like wax, like butter left in sun, the wheat and the beard and the kind old face sliding down into grey puddles that soaked away between the cobbles. He did this gently. That was the worst part. He did it the way you'd smooth a wrinkle from a bedsheet.

*Everyone who remembers,* the grimoire had said. Casper clutched it tighter under their coat, felt its leather heartbeat against their ribs, and did the only sensible thing a runaway apprentice could do.

They ran.

* * *

Sefa Vane counted her crew twice a day, once at dawn and once whenever trouble started, because trouble was when people wandered off. Trouble had started an hour ago, when the stars went out, and now, from the ridge above the waystation, she counted four: Brannic on the mules, the twins Ilo and Petra arguing over the last of the dried plums, and old Wren smoking by the fire with the serenity of a woman who had survived three wars by refusing to worry about any of them.

Five, when the kid came stumbling out of the treeline.

Sefa had been watching the mountain road since the wards died two nights back. She'd watched the white lanterns come through the pass at Harrow Gap, and she'd watched what they left behind: a toll-keeper who smiled at her with a smile like a painted mask and could not say his own daughter's name; a shepherd's dog howling over a man who sat in the grass, whole and healthy and hollow, asking everyone he met, politely, endlessly, *who am I?*

So when a thin figure in a dead witch's coat burst from the pines with a book hugged to their chest like a lifeboat, Sefa did the arithmetic fast. Kid meant witnesses meant lanterns. She was already moving before the kid reached the fire.

"Up," she said. "All of you. Now."

The kid skidded to a stop, wild-eyed, and looked at her like she'd spoken a foreign language. "The road," they gasped. "There were six of them, they unmade the shrine, they don't have shadows, they—"

"I know what they are." Sefa kicked dirt over the fire. "Brannic, cut the mules loose. Ilo, Petra, pack it *ugly*, we're leaving half of this." She turned to the kid. "You led them here?"

"No! I crossed the stream, I broke my trail—" But even as they said it, the kid's face went grey, because down the mountain road, small and cold as far-off stars, six white lights flickered into being among the trees.

"Congratulations," Sefa said. "You broke your trail straight to us."

* * *

Casper had imagined being caught many times since fleeing Mistress Ellery's cottage—imagined the enforcers' hands, the white light, whatever happened to the shepherd in the grass—but imagining was nothing against the reality of the lanterns climbing toward the waystation, patient as tide. What surprised Casper was the smugglers. They didn't argue. They didn't panic. They moved like water finding downhill, and the tall woman with the scarred knuckles—Vane, someone called her—grabbed Casper by the collar and hauled them along like luggage.

"Who are you?" Casper managed, feet barely touching ground.

"Sefa Vane. And you're the witch's stray." Something flickered across her face—not kindness, exactly. Recognition. Debt. "Ellery took a knife out of my side eleven years ago and asked nothing for it but a favor owed. I've been waiting for the favor. I hoped it would be smaller than this."

"You knew her?"

"Everyone on this mountain knew her. She fixed what was broken and looked the other way when we moved what needed moving." Sefa shoved Casper behind a rock wall older than the kingdom and peered back at the waystation. Below, the first enforcer stepped into the firelight, lifted his lantern, and passed his hand over the ground where the fire had been. The charred stones went pale and new, as if no fire had ever burned there. "She never mentioned an apprentice."

"She never mentioned smugglers either." Casper's voice shook. They hated that it shook. "She never mentioned anything. She just *died* and left me a book full of warnings."

"Then we're both carrying something she left us." Sefa's jaw tightened. "Move. There's a path off this ridge that isn't on any crown map, because I made it myself, out of goat trails and spite."

* * *

The path was a lie told to the mountain itself—a seam in the rock that opened only when you already knew it was there, dropping through a crack so narrow the mules had to be blindfolded and coaxed with honey. Sefa went last, watching the ridgeline, watching the lanterns reach the waystation and find ash where a fire had been and nothing else, because Wren, bless her unbothered heart, had salted the clearing with a handful of forget-powder she kept for customs men. Let the hollow ones stand in that clearing and remember nothing worth erasing.

By the time they came down into the hidden valley, the crew had formed opinions, and they delivered them around a cold camp, no fire, voices low.

"We can't take a stray," Brannic said. He was a big man built like a door, gentle with the mules and suspicious of everything on two legs. "Strays get people erased."

"The stray is why they came," Petra pointed out. Her twin Ilo nodded, mouth full of plum.

"The stray is also the witch's," Sefa said, and the way she said *the witch's* ended the argument the way a dropped anchor ends a drift. She looked at Casper across the dark. "Here's how it will be. We feed you, we move you, we keep you breathing until we reach somewhere the lanterns don't go. In exchange, you do for us what your mistress would have done. You watch for ward-craft. You read whatever needs reading. And when this is over, the debt is paid, both directions, and nobody owes anybody grief."

It wasn't rescue. Casper understood that. It was commerce, cold and clean, and some bruised part of them wanted to spit at it—to say *she trusted you, she bled for you*—but they had run from the only home they'd ever known two nights ago, and they had nowhere else the arithmetic worked.

"Both directions," Casper agreed. "Nobody owes anybody grief."

Wren laughed softly from the darkness, smoke curling from her pipe toward a sky with no stars to catch it. "Oh, child," she said. "That's what everyone says at the start."

* * *

Sefa slept in shifts and dreamed in fragments. In the gray hour before dawn she woke to find the kid sitting upright, the grimoire open across their knees, lips moving over pages that seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it.

"It talks about the king," Casper whispered, when they noticed her watching. "Not the king now. The old one. It says the crown doesn't just erase—it *eats*. And it says the wards on Thornfell weren't border wards at all. They were a leash. And somebody cut the leash."

Sefa looked north, toward the capital she'd spent her whole life avoiding, where a man sat on a throne wearing a crown that ate memories, and every soul in the country had agreed, quietly, without ever saying it aloud, to remember less and less.

"Get some sleep, stray," she said. "We've got hard roads ahead, and I mean that in every sense the word owns."

Above them, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. And far off, at the edge of hearing, or perhaps only at the edge of imagination, something vast and patient began, very slowly, to turn its attention south.
