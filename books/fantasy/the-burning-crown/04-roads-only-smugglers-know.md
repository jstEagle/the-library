## 4. Roads Only Smugglers Know

Sefa Vane had driven the Salt Road at night for eleven years, and she had never once seen it this dark.

Not dark like a moonless night—dark like a held breath. The mountains on either side of the pass should have been silvered by starlight, their snowfields glowing faintly blue even in deep winter. Instead they were shapes without edges, black cutouts pasted against a blacker sky. Her mule, Bramble, kept shying at nothing, ears flat, and Sefa couldn't blame him. A world with no stars was a world that had forgotten how to be looked at.

"Left fork," Casper said from the seat beside her.

"I know."

"You were going to take the right."

"I was *considering* the right." Sefa flicked the reins. "Considering is not taking."

Casper said nothing to that, but Sefa felt them settle back against the wagon's rail, arms crossed, wearing that particular silence of theirs—the one that meant *I'm right and we both know it*. Eleven days on the road together and she'd already learned to read the apprentice's silences the way sailors read weather. It was an uncomfortable skill. It meant she was paying attention.

The left fork dropped into a river valley that no map still printed had ever bothered to record. Sefa knew it because her mother had known it, who'd known it from a Kellish runner before the war, who'd known it from someone whose name was lost the way all smuggler knowledge was lost—not written down, just carried, hand to hand, like contraband. The path was barely wider than the wagon wheels, stitched along the riverbank between alder roots and fallen scree. In daylight it would have been beautiful. Tonight it was simply a way through, which was all any road needed to be.

Behind them, the second wagon creaked under Odo's weight and the weight of everything Odo insisted on bringing: salt pork, tarred rope, three crates of unlicensed lantern-oil, and a lute he could not play. Wren rode up top with him, small and sharp-eyed, keeping watch on the rear trail. And in the third wagon, tucked among bolts of grey cloth that had never once been sold honestly, rode the reason for all of it: a runaway apprentice, half a grimoire's worth of dangerous knowledge, and a debt Sefa owed to a dead woman named Maren Solenne.

She didn't regret paying it. She wanted that said clearly, at least inside her own head. She just hadn't expected the debt to come with a person attached—a person who flinched at loud noises and hoarded bread crusts in their pockets and looked at Sefa's crew sometimes like a cat looks at a lit hearth, wanting to come closer and not quite believing the fire was real.

"Stop," Casper said suddenly.

Sefa hauled the reins. "What?"

"There's something across the path." Casper was already down off the bench, one hand raised, and Sefa saw the faint shimmer of light gather at their fingertips—ward-light, pale as frost. "Don't move the wagons."

Odo's voice drifted forward, low. "Trouble?"

"Maybe. Maybe just rabbits." Casper picked their way ahead, and Sefa, cursing quietly, followed with a hooded lamp.

What the lamp found was a boundary stone, waist-high, furred with moss—and around it, a ring of old ward-marks carved into the rock, so worn they were nearly invisible. Except they weren't invisible anymore. They were glowing, faintly, the way embers glow when you blow on them.

"That's impossible," Sefa said. "These marks are older than my grandmother."

"They're waking up." Casper knelt, not touching, tracing the air above the carvings. "Someone renewed them recently. Recently-ish. Within the last year." A pause. "It's a good pattern. Clean anchoring, balanced flow. Whoever did it knew what they were doing."

"Is it going to stop us?"

"No. It's a warning ward. It tells whoever set it that something crossed here." Casper glanced up, and in the lamplight their face was unreadable. "Which means somebody's watching these roads. Somebody who remembers enough to be afraid."

Sefa stood there a moment, feeling the starless dark press against her shoulders like wet wool. Then she turned and whistled twice, sharp, the signal for *move quiet, move now*.

They made the far side of the valley by midnight and camped in the hollow beneath a collapsed watchtower, a bowl of old stone hidden from every direction but the sky—which, Sefa thought grimly, was the one direction that couldn't spy on anyone anymore. Small mercies.

* * *

The fire was Odo's doing, because the fire was always Odo's doing. He built them like shrines, small and precise, ringed with stones he arranged by some private geometry, and he fed them slow so they burned long. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he braided and hands that had done every kind of work there was, honest and otherwise. He'd been with Sefa since before she'd been captain, back when she'd been nobody's daughter running messages over the Thornfell passes for coin and the occasional beating.

He was tuning the lute now, badly. Wren sat cross-legged nearby, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, which was what Wren did instead of sleeping. And Casper—

Casper was drawing in the dirt.

Sefa watched from where she sat cleaning her boots. The apprentice had cleared a patch of ground near the firelight and was scratching lines into it with a stick: circles within circles, anchor-points marked with little stars (a bitter joke, given the sky), flow-lines connecting them like veins. Their mouth moved as they worked, half-chanting, half-muttering, entirely absorbed.

"What's that, then?" Odo asked, giving up on the lute.

"Ward-craft." Casper didn't look up. "Basic perimeter. If I'm going to be traveling with people who snore loud enough to wake enforcers, I'd rather know if anything crosses our line before it's standing over us."

"And you're drawing it in the dirt because...?"

"Because chalk's expensive and I'm practicing." Casper sat back on their heels, studying the diagram, then frowned at it, rubbed out a section with their palm, redrew it. "Mistress Solenne always said wards drawn in earth remember longer than wards drawn in air. Earth has patience."

"Maren said that?" Odo's voice went gentle in a way Sefa rarely heard it.

"She said a lot of things." The stick paused. For a moment Casper's shoulders drew up around their ears, that flinch again, grief arriving like weather. Then it passed, or was made to pass, and they went on. "Most of them were insults. But the ward-craft was good."

Wren set down the knife. "Teach us."

Sefa blinked. So, apparently, did Casper.

"Teach you," the apprentice repeated flatly.

"To draw your dirt pictures." Wren shrugged, all seventeen years of them radiating indifference so carefully constructed it fooled no one. "You said yourself the roads are being watched. Odo can't fight, Sefa won't let me take first watch alone, and none of us knows a warning ward from a hole in the ground. Seems stupid not to learn."

"It takes years," Casper said. "Proper ward-craft. Years and—"

"We're not asking for proper," Sefa heard herself say. She hadn't planned to speak. The words came anyway, in her captain's voice, the one that ended arguments. "We're asking for enough. Enough to know when something's coming. Enough to buy ten seconds." She met Casper's eyes across the fire. "Ten seconds is the difference between a story and a grave. You know that better than anyone."

Something shifted in the apprentice's face. Not softening exactly—more like a door unlocking from the inside.

"All right," Casper said. "But you'll do it my way. And if you botch the anchor-lines, the ward will slap you unconscious, and I will laugh."

"She laughs at our pain already," Odo confided to Wren. "This changes nothing."

* * *

It should have been tedious. It was not.

Casper taught the way Maren Solenne must have taught—ruthlessly, precisely, with a dry humor that surfaced like a fish and vanished again—but underneath the rigor was something else, something almost startled. Sefa saw it every time one of them got a line right. Odo, of all people, turned out to have steady hands and a feel for the flow-lines, murmuring that braiding rope and braiding light weren't so different, and every time he got one clean, Casper looked at him like he'd performed a miracle. Wren learned the anchor-stars fastest, quick fingers and quicker temper, swearing fluently each time the ward-light stung them for sloppy work, and each time Casper corrected the grip rather than the swearing, patient as bedrock.

And Sefa—Sefa, who had not been a student of anything since she was nine years old—found herself kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves pushed up, drawing circles by firelight, and finding that she cared whether they were true.

"There," Casper said, behind her shoulder. "Feel it?"

She did. Where the last line closed, the air changed—thickened slightly, like the surface tension on a full cup. A hum below hearing. The completed ward sat over their camp like a cupped hand.

"That's yours," Casper said quietly. "You made that. It'll hold until dawn."

Sefa looked at the faint shimmer only she and Casper could see, and felt something in her chest do an unfamiliar thing. Pride, maybe. Or its cousin.

"My mother would've called it witchery," she said.

"Your mother sounds like she'd have gotten along with mine," Casper said, and then went very still, as if surprised by their own words. *Mine.* Sefa let it lie. Some doors you don't knock on; you just leave them unlatched and walk away whistling.

Later, when the others slept—Wren curled against Odo's side like a barnacle, Odo snoring as prophesied—Sefa took the middle watch and found Casper awake beside her, knees drawn up, looking at the empty sky.

"Can't sleep either?"

"Sleeping's when they get you," Casper said, which was not an answer, and also was.

They sat in silence awhile. The fire ticked. Somewhere downslope, the river talked to itself in the dark.

"Sefa." Casper's voice came low. "Why did you really come for me? At the patrol. The debt—that's a reason, but it's a thin one. Debts get weighed against risk. I was a lot of risk."

Sefa considered lying, found she didn't want to, and hated that a little.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "I got caught running untaxed wool through Thornfell. The magistrate was going to take my hand. Standard penalty, first offense, they were very proud of it." She turned her boot in the firelight. "A hedge-witch I'd never met came to my trial and swore I'd been in her cottage with fever the whole week. Lied under seal of her craft, which could've cost her everything. She never told me why. Never asked for anything. Just—" Sefa snapped her fingers softly. "Gone, after. I spent years wondering what I'd done to earn it. Then I stopped wondering and started trying to be worth it." She glanced sideways. "Then she died, and left a note saying her apprentice might need a road south. So. There it is. You're not the debt, Casper Reyes. You're the chance to keep being worth it."

Casper stared at the fire for a long time. When they spoke, their voice was rough at the edges.

"She never told me about you either."

"Maren Solenne," Sefa said, "kept more secrets than a confessor's box."

That earned a ghost of a smile. Then Casper reached into their coat and brought out the grimoire—or what remained of it, the half that had survived the Salt Stair, its spine charred, its pages soft as moth wings. They'd hardly let it out of their sight since. Now they opened it across their knees and turned, slowly, to a page near the end.

"Sefa. Look at this."

The page showed a map. Not of roads—of the capital. Aldmere City, rendered in fine brown ink, its streets and walls and the great spiral of the palace hill all drawn in a sure, practiced hand. And around the palace, in ink so fresh it still gleamed, someone had added annotations in a different script: measurements, symbols Sefa half-recognized from tonight's lesson, and at the center, circled twice, two words in Maren Solenne's handwriting:

*Mirror Room.*

"She mapped it," Casper whispered. "Years ago. She mapped the whole route. She was planning this before I ever ran away. Before she—" The breath caught. "Sefa, she knew she wasn't coming. She built this whole thing for me to finish. Every road, every contact, every—" Their finger stopped on a line of smaller text beneath the map, and Sefa watched the blood leave their face.

"What?" Sefa leaned closer. "What does it say?"

Casper read it aloud, barely above the fire's crackle: "*If you are reading this, the wards are down and the crown is nearly finished. Do not go alone. Trust the Vane woman; she is better than she believes. And child—whatever you find in the Mirror Room, remember that the king was my friend before he was their king.*"

The fire settled. Above them, the sky stayed black and starless, and somewhere east, beyond three more days of forgotten roads, a city slept under charms, and a king sat in a room full of mirrors remembering nothing at all.

Sefa put her hand briefly on the apprentice's shoulder.

"Then we'd best not go alone," she said. "Get some sleep, Casper. Tomorrow I teach you the river crossing—and tomorrow night, you teach Wren anchor-lines. We're going to need every ward you can draw."

Because in three days, they would reach the city that forgot its name—and Sefa Vane intended to walk in knowing exactly who she was.
