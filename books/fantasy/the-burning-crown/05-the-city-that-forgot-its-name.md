## 5. The City That Forgot Its Name

They smelled the capital before they saw it.

Sefa had warned them it would be so—*a city of a hundred thousand souls smells like a hundred thousand suppers cooking, and none of them for you*—but what drifted down the river valley to meet them was something else entirely. Beneath the woodsmoke and the fish-stink of the Aldmere's lower reaches there ran a sweeter note, cloying and faintly wrong, like lilies left too long in a closed room. Like flowers arranged over something buried.

Casper stood at the rail of the flat-bottomed barge as it slid through the morning fog, the grimoire heavy against their ribs where it rode in its sling beneath their coat. The mist parted in slow rags, and the city assembled itself out of nothing: first the bridge-towers with their green copper crowns, then the terraced roofs climbing the hillsides in pale tiers, then the palace at the summit, white as a pulled tooth, its spires lost in a sky that held no stars even now, even in daylight, because Casper knew—they *knew*, in the ache behind their eyes—that the stars were not merely hidden but eaten.

"That's it," Breck said beside them, chewing the end of a rope he was supposed to be coiling. "Aldmere City. Biggest collection of people pretending not to look at each other in the world."

"It's beautiful," Casper said, and meant it, and hated that they meant it.

The spires caught the grey light and threw it back silver. Bells were ringing somewhere, three towers out of step with one another, and the sound rolled across the water like weather. It would have been easy—so easy—to believe the city was only a city, that the wrongness in the air was tanneries or lamp-oil or the ordinary rot of ten thousand lives pressed close together.

But Casper had spent six years learning wards from a woman who could hear a lie in a warding-sigil from across a room. And the whole city hummed.

Not with life. With *drawing*.

"Do you feel that?" they asked quietly.

Breck spat his rope-end into the water. "Feel what?"

"Nothing." Sefa appeared at Casper's elbow, silent as she always was on a deck, her braids beaded with river-damp. "That's what they feel. Or rather—" she tilted her head, listening to whatever smugglers listened to, "—that's what they don't. Look at the docks, little warden. Tell me what you see."

Casper looked.

The docks were busy enough. Cranes swinging, barrels rolling, gulls screaming their grievances. But the stevedores moved strangely—not slowly, not clumsily, just *evenly*, all of them, all at the same pace, like figures in a music box wound to a single tempo. A woman hauling nets paused to wipe her brow, and four men nearby paused in the same instant, hands rising to their own brows, and then all five resumed as one.

"They're wearing them," Casper said. Their mouth had gone dry. "Memory-charms."

Around nearly every neck, every wrist: small tokens. Copper discs stamped with the royal seal. Glass beads that caught the light with an oily shimmer Casper now recognized—the same shimmer as the residue the enforcers left behind, the same shimmer as the scorch-marks in Mistress Yew's ruined ward-circle. Charms. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

"Sold at every shrine in the city," Sefa said. "Cheap as bread. *Wear the king's favor and be at peace.* That's what the charm-sellers cry." Her voice did something complicated around the word *peace*. "My mother wore one for thirty years before I got her out. She couldn't tell me my own name without it."

* * *

They moored at the Tanners' Stair, a smuggler's berth if ever there was one, where the customs-men took their bribes in smoked eel and asked no names. Sefa went to haggle; Breck went to find out which warehouses were watched; and Casper, against everyone's better judgment and their own, went walking.

They told themselves it was reconnaissance. They told themselves a lot of things, in those first hours inside the walls.

The streets climbed away from the river in crooked switchbacks, and the higher Casper walked, the quieter the city became. Not empty—never empty—but quiet in the way a room is quiet when everyone in it has agreed upon something unsaid. Market-cries rose from the squares, but they were the *same* cries, vendor after vendor calling prices in identical cadences, identical words, as though the whole city had learned its lines from a single script. A baker called *fresh loaves, warm from the king's ovens!* and two streets later a second baker called *fresh loaves, warm from the king's ovens!* in the very same voice, the very same lift at the end, and when Casper stopped to buy one, the man smiled at them with perfect warmth and perfect emptiness, like sunlight on glass.

"How long have you baked here?" Casper asked.

"All my days, and my father's before me, blessed be the crown."

"And before the crown?"

The smile did not falter. It did not flicker. It simply continued, the way a painted smile continues. "There was no before, friend. There is the crown, and there is the peace."

Casper bought the loaf because their hands needed something to do. It was good bread. That was somehow the worst part—that everything here functioned, flour and yeast and fire doing their honest work, while the people moving among them had been scooped out like gourds and filled with the same warm, harmless nothing.

In a chapel square off the Cordwainers' Row, a priest was blessing new charms by the basketful, dipping copper discs in holy water and murmuring words that made Casper's teeth ache. They were not prayers. Casper knew ward-craft when they heard it, even corrupted, even inverted—and this was a ward turned inside out, a ward that didn't keep memory safe but kept it *out*, a sieve strung across the mind. The faithful queued in orderly rows to receive them. A young mother held her infant up to be touched with a disc too large for the baby's small fist, and the priest laughed kindly and tied it instead to the carriage-blanket, and the mother thanked him in the same voice as the baker, the same voice as everyone, and Casper stood in the crowd with their heart hammering and understood—

Understood that this was not a city under a spell.

It was a city being *eaten*. Slowly. District by district, street by street, memory by memory, the way a fire eats a house from the inside while the chimney still draws. The crown wasn't content with one king's mind. It farmed. It grazed. Every charm was a tap into a vein, and the veins ran all the way up the hill to the white palace, carrying sweetness to something that was always, always hungry.

Mistress Yew's wards had held the border for forty years. Casper had thought the stakes were a broken ward-line, a darkened sky, a dead woman's unfinished work. Personal things. *Their* things—their guilt, their grief, their anger at a mistress who had known and stayed silent.

Standing in that chapel square, watching a baby's blanket drink from a copper disc, Casper felt the mission change shape in their chest. It stopped being about Yew. It stopped being about Casper Reyes at all.

*One hundred thousand souls,* they thought. *And every one of them queuing up to be hollowed.*

* * *

They found Sefa at dusk in the back room of a dye-house that fronted as a chandler's shop, the crew gathered around a guttering lamp, maps spread over barrels of tallow. Casper came in smelling of river mud and other people's bread and could not make their face behave.

"You saw it," Sefa said. Not a question.

"The charms. The voices. They all speak the same, Sefa. Word for word. A baker, a priest, a beggar—I tested six of them, and six of them told me there was no *before*." Casper sat down hard on a tallow-crate. "I thought the crown was eating the king. One man. One terrible thing. But it's not one man. It's a *harvest*. Whole districts, stripped and replanted with nothing, and the people line up for it because the nothing feels like peace."

Silence around the lamp. Old Wrenn, the crew's navigator, a woman older than the mast she'd sailed under, spoke first. "The Lowquarters went quiet eleven years back. We all said plague. Plague makes sense. Plague lets you not ask."

"And the Lantern District," Breck said slowly. "My cousin lived there. Stopped writing. I told myself ink was dear." He looked at his own hands. "We've been sailing past this our whole lives and calling it weather."

"That's how it works," Casper said. "That's how it *wants* to work. Nobody asks, because asking means remembering there's something to ask about, and remembering hurts, and the charm is right there, cheap as bread." They pulled the grimoire from its sling and set it on the map, and the lamplight seemed to bend toward it, greedy. "Yew wrote that the crown must be fed or it feeds itself. I thought she meant the king. She meant *all of this*. The king is just the root it grew from."

Sefa studied Casper across the lamp, and for once there was no transactional arithmetic in her eyes, no weighing of debts owed to a dead hedge-witch. There was something rawer.

"When we take the crown off him," she said, "what happens to them? The charmed ones. Does it come back? Everything they gave?"

"I don't know." Casper's throat was tight. "Maybe. Maybe it's gone forever, and they wake up hollow with holes where their lives used to be. Maybe some of them can't survive getting themselves back." They looked down at the grimoire's cracked leather, at the sigil burned into the cover that matched the scar on their own palm. "Yew never wrote that part. Maybe she didn't know. Maybe she couldn't bear to."

"Then we'd best find out," Sefa said, "before we do it. Because I'll not free a city into a massacre." She tapped the map, on the white smudge of ink that marked the palace hill. "There's a way in. There's always a way in. The old servant stairs off the Salt Court, if the stories hold. Three nights from now the court keeps the Vigil of the Founding—every noble drunk on ceremony, half the guard marching in it."

Old Wrenn snorted. "And the other half?"

"The other half," Sefa said, "will be us."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the bells rang the hour, three towers out of step, and beneath the bells Casper could still hear it—the great soft hum of the city drinking, patient as roots, everywhere and endless. They had come down from the mountains thinking they carried a debt to one dead woman and a warning about one stolen king.

Now the weight was a hundred thousand names nobody remembered, not even the people who'd borne them.

Three nights, Casper thought, pressing their scarred palm flat against the grimoire until the old sigil bit cold into the skin. Three nights, and then the mirror room, and then whatever waited behind the king's empty eyes.

Above the dye-house roof, through the smoke-hole, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

And somewhere in the dark above it, Casper would have sworn they felt a single point of light—faint, failing, furious—holding on.
