## 6. The King in the Mirror Room

The palace of Aldmere did not look like a place where a king lived. It looked like a place where a king had been *kept*.

Casper saw it from the rooftops of the tanners' quarter, where Sefa's crew had rented a room above a dye-house that reeked of vinegar and old blood. Even from this distance, even through the river haze, the palace seemed wrong—not grand, exactly, though it was that too, all pale spires and banners gone gray as ash—but *quiet*. No music spilled from its windows. No lamplight moved behind its glass. The banners hung so still they might have been painted onto the air.

"It doesn't breathe," Casper said.

Sefa Vane stood beside them on the rooftop, arms crossed against the cold, her braids beaded with river-damp. "Palaces don't breathe, apprentice. They're buildings."

"This one doesn't." Casper had spent eleven years learning to feel wards the way other people felt weather—pressure before rain, a prickle before lightning. The border wards of Thornfell had sung to them the night they died, a chord snapping string by string. What came off the palace now was nothing like a song. It was a hum. Low, patient, and *hungry*, like a wasp nest wrapped in wool. "There's something inside it that's eating."

"Charming." Sefa didn't argue further. She'd stopped arguing with Casper's senses somewhere on the Salt Road, after Casper had walked the crew around a collapsed bridge by feeling the ward-remnants still clinging to its stones. "Pike says the servants' gate on the east face changes guard at moonrise. After that, ninety heartbeats of dark stair. Can you do what you said?"

Casper looked down at their hands. In the grimoire—the half of it that remained, torn down the spine by a traitor's knife—they had found a veil-cantrip their mistress had written in the margin in her cramped, impatient hand: *For sneaking apprentices. Do not tell me how you used it.* It required only a candle stub, a spoken name, and the willingness to be forgotten for an hour.

"I can do it," Casper said. "It'll hurt."

"Most things worth doing do." Sefa clapped their shoulder once, hard, the way she did instead of saying anything soft. "Ninety heartbeats. If you're not back at the postern by the third bell, we burn something expensive and come get you."

"You'd get caught."

"Probably." Her teeth flashed white in the starless dark. "But we'd do it anyway. That's what crew means, little witch. Write it down in your book."

Casper wrote nothing down, but they kept the words.

* * *

The servants' gate smelled of lye and turnips. Casper slipped through it in the space between one guard's yawn and the next, veiled in a shroud of un-noticing that made eyes slide off them like rain off waxed canvas. The cantrip cost more than the grimoire had promised—a thread of warmth pulled out of them with every step, memories going slightly gray at the edges, the color of things seen through fogged glass. They held tight to three anchors as they climbed: Sefa's laugh, rough as gravel; the smell of Mistress Yewen's cottage, woodsmoke and dried lavender; the sound of the wards of Thornfell dying, which they carried like a stone in the chest because it was the last thing she had ever asked them to witness.

The palace interior was worse than the outside. Corridors stretched long and lit by lamps that burned without flickering. Servants glided past with trays of food no one ate. And everywhere—everywhere—were mirrors.

That was the first thing Casper understood about the palace: it was full of mirrors, and none of them were clean. Every polished surface wore a faint gray film, like breath on glass that never faded. When Casper passed, their reflection lagged. Half a heartbeat. A full one. Once, turning a corner, they caught their own face looking back at them from a mirror they had already passed—and the reflection was smiling when they weren't.

They did not look at any more mirrors.

The hum grew louder as they went deeper. Casper followed it the way one follows a scream through a burning house—not because it was wise, but because something in it was *human*. Down a stair worn hollow in its center by centuries of feet. Through a door banded in iron so old the metal had gone black and bloomed with roses of rust. Into a room that took Casper's breath away, though there was nothing in it but light.

The mirror room was round, and its walls were glass from floor to vaulted ceiling—but not ordinary glass. Each pane showed a sky. Not the real sky, which had been empty and black since the night the stars died, but remembered skies: summer constellations over Thornfell's hills, the winter crown of stars above Aldmere's capital, skies from places Casper had never been, hung in panels like paintings in a gallery of night. Hundreds of panes. Thousands of stars. All of them stolen, all of them shining here in this sealed room while the world outside went dark.

And in the center of the room, beneath the false stars, sat an old man feeding bread crumbs to pigeons that were not there.

He was thin as a winter branch. His robe was fine once—Casper could see the ghost of gold thread in its weave—but it hung off him like skin off a fruit left too long in the sun. His hair was white and unwashed and fell past his shoulders. He sat on a plain wooden chair in the middle of all that stolen glory, murmuring to his invisible birds, and when he lifted his head at the sound of Casper's step, his eyes were the same empty gray as the enforcers'.

Almost the same. There was one difference, small and terrible: where the enforcers' eyes were empty all the way through, this man's emptiness had a shape. Something had been scooped out of him, again and again, for years—and at the very bottom of the hollow, like water at the bottom of a well, one thing remained.

He looked at Casper and smiled.

"Oh," he said. His voice was rust and honey. "You have her eyes."

Casper's whole body went cold. "You know me?"

"No." The king shook his head slowly, apologetic, as if failing some test. "No, I don't know anyone. I knew someone. I think I knew someone. But—" he gestured vaguely at the paneled skies, at the room, at himself, "—it keeps taking it back. Whatever I find. Like counting coins in a dream." He frowned then, concentrating, and Casper watched him reach into the well of himself with both hands, straining. "But I keep one. They can't seem to take the one."

"What do you keep?" Casper whispered, though they already knew. They had known since the smile. Some part of them had known since the door.

"A woman," the king said. "On a hill. There's wind, and she's angry at me—no, not angry. Frightened for me. She has mud on her hem and lavender in her sleeves and she's saying—" his brow furrowed, and his voice dropped to a child's hush, "—she's saying, *Aldric, you have to let me finish it, or it will finish you.*"

The name hit Casper like a fist. Not *Your Majesty*. Not *the king*. She had called him Aldric, the way you call a friend.

"She was a hedge-witch," the king went on, warming now, hoarding the memory aloud the way a miser counts coins. "From the mountains. Thornfell—yes, Thornfell, I remember the word if not the place. She came down to the capital to warn me, and everyone laughed, and she didn't. She grabbed my wrist in front of the whole court and told me my crown was killing me, and I—" his face crumpled, "—I believed her. I remember believing her. That's the last true thing I have. I don't remember her name. I've tried. I say *tell me your name* every time I visit her, and every time the room takes it before morning."

*Every time I visit her.* Casper's throat closed. He came here—to this room, to this paneled sky—to visit a memory because it was the only place she existed anymore. The mistress who had never spoken of her past except in fragments. Who had wards stitched into every fencepost of Thornfell. Who had died alone in a cottage that smelled of lavender, leaving a grimoire addressed to an apprentice by name.

Yewen. Her name had been Yewen, and this broken man had loved her like a friend, and he couldn't keep even that.

"She was my mistress," Casper said. The words came out cracked. "She raised me. She taught me wards. She died in the spring."

The king stared at them. The pigeons that weren't there scattered from hands that weren't moving. Slowly, wonderingly, he reached up and touched Casper's cheek with fingers light as moth wings.

"The eyes," he repeated. "I thought—I hoped. She spoke of an apprentice sometimes. *My stubborn fledgling*, she called you. She said you ran away." A pause, and something like grief moved across the ruin of his face, grief for a person he could not name in a life he could not hold. "Did you go back? Did you tell her you were sorry?"

"I never got the chance." Casper's vision blurred. "She died before I could."

The king nodded, slow and solemn, absorbing this the way sand absorbs rain—as soon as it landed, it began to drain away. Casper could see it happening. Could see the connection forming and dissolving, forming and dissolving, a man trying to hold water in his fists. In a minute he would forget them. In an hour he would forget this conversation entirely, and be alone again under his stolen stars with one memory and no name.

Unless.

Casper pulled the torn grimoire from their coat. Half a book, ragged-spined, smelling of lavender and woodsmoke. The king's gaze snagged on it and *held*—held the way nothing else held—and Casper understood with a jolt that went through them like lightning: the crown could strip everything from him except the things bound up in her work. Her wards. Her craft. Her book. The crown had eaten a king down to the seed, but the seed was her, and it could not crack it.

"Aldric." Casper used the name deliberately, watching it land, watching him cling to it. "Listen to me. Your crown was made by my family. Made to cage a tyrant—and something went wrong, or something changed, and now it's caging you. My mistress spent her life building the wards that kept it contained, and they broke the night the stars went out. She left me the way to end it. But I need the rest of the book, and I need to know what happened the day she came to warn you. Everything you remember. Before it takes it back."

The king's hands trembled. For a moment the emptiness in his eyes churned, and Casper felt the hum of the crown rise around them like a hive disturbed, the gray film on a hundred mirrors thickening, the remembered stars guttering—

Then Aldric gripped Casper's wrist, hard, with the desperate strength of a drowning man, and said:

"Sit down, fledgling. We haven't much time. It always comes back at the third bell—and this time, I think it knows you're here."

Far above them, through floors of stone and silence, a door boomed shut.
