## 7. Betrayal on the Salt Stair

Sefa Vane had been betrayed before. She'd been robbed in Rell's Crossing, double-crossed off the Amber Coast, and once left tied to her own mast by a first mate with a taste for other people's ships. She had thought herself fluent in the language of treachery—its accents, its tells, the way a liar's hands moved when they counted coins that weren't theirs yet.

She had never been betrayed by family.

That was the difference she kept circling, three days out from the capital, as the crew made camp in the lee of a collapsed watchtower and the salt wind came up off the flats smelling of old iron and drowned things. She watched Brenn across the fire. He was laughing at something Doss said, his big shoulders shaking, his scarred hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea he'd brewed too weak because he always did, because he was a man who never took more than the water could spare.

*He owes you nothing,* Sefa told herself. *You owe him everything.*

It had been Brenn who carried Casper over the pass at Hollowmere when the apprentice's legs gave out. Brenn who stood between them and the hollow-eyed patrol on the fen road and lied so smoothly that even Sefa half-believed him. Brenn who had wept—actually wept, silent and ashamed of it—when Casper read aloud the passage about the king's memories being eaten one year at a time, like fruit off a tree.

And it was Brenn whose sister had been taken by the enforcers eleven years ago, outside a village called Marrow's End, for the crime of remembering the old king's face.

Sefa turned her cup slowly in her hands and thought: *There is the door they'll walk through. There is the wound they'll press.*

---

The Salt Stair came down out of the hills two days later—a road no map remembered, cut into the cliffside above a dead sea, its steps furred white with mineral bloom so that in the starless dark they glowed faintly, like the spine of some enormous buried creature. It was the last approach to Aldmere that the crown's watchers didn't cover. The grimoire said so. Casper had traced the ward-marks along its length with reverent fingers, murmuring that their mistress must have walked this very path, decades ago, toward the same throne room.

They descended at dusk, single file, boots whispering on salt-crusted stone. Below them the capital lay spread across the plain like a spilled jewel-box, ten thousand lights burning behind walls that ate memory. Above them, nothing. The sky had been an open wound for weeks now, and Sefa still caught herself looking up, still flinching at the absence where stars should have been. A sky without stars was a sea without a floor. You could fall forever into it.

Brenn walked at the rear. He had been quiet since the watchtower. Quiet in a way that Sefa recognized now, too late, as a man rehearsing something.

Halfway down, the wind changed.

Sefa felt it before she heard anything—the particular wrongness in the air that every smuggler learns in their gut before their head catches up. The salt smell thinned. Underneath it came something colder, cleaner, like snow falling in an empty room.

"Off the stair," she breathed. "Now."

Too late. Lanterns bloomed along the cliff-top above them—not firelight but that pale, sourceless glow the enforcers carried, light without warmth, light that cast shadows pointing the wrong way. And down the steps below, rising to meet them, came more.

They were caught on the spine of the world with nowhere to go but sideways, and there was no sideways.

"Hold," said a voice from above, and it was not the flat chorus-voice of the enforcers. It was human, rough, familiar. "Hold, and no one gets touched."

Sefa's blood went cold and then colder.

Brenn stepped forward, away from the crew, into the edge of that pale light. His face was wet. He wouldn't look at any of them. He looked at the enforcers instead—at the tall one in front, whose empty eyes reflected nothing—and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"You said if I gave you the route, you'd give her back. What they took out of her. All of it. Every year."

"We restore what is erased," the enforcer said. Its mouth moved; its eyes stayed vacant as coins. "Memory returned whole. This is promised."

"You *promised.*" Brenn's voice cracked on the word like ice underfoot.

Doss made a sound low in his throat, half snarl, half sob. Little Wren, who was seventeen and had followed Sefa out of the dredger-slums because Sefa had once bought her a hot meal and never mentioned it again, grabbed Sefa's sleeve and whispered, "Cap. Cap, what's happening?"

What was happening was that family was coming apart on a stair made of salt, which was fitting, because salt was what you rubbed into a wound, and Sefa had taught Brenn that trick himself, years ago, on a night when he'd come back from a job bleeding and shaking and she'd sat up with him till dawn.

"Brenn." She kept her voice level. Captain's voice. The voice that had talked them past customs cutters and toll-gangs and one very persistent dragon-cult. "Look at me. Not them. Me."

He looked. That was the worst part. He looked at her with his whole ruined face, and she saw that he hadn't done this lightly—that he had lain awake nights doing sums no man should have to do, weighing one sister against five friends, and that the arithmetic had broken him either way.

"They have her," he said simply. "Eleven years, Sefa. They've had her eleven years, and I couldn't even remember her face anymore until—" His hand shook toward Casper, toward the grimoire slung across the apprentice's back. "Until I heard what the crown does. What it *took*. I thought she was dead. I built her a cairn in my head and grieved her and now she's not dead, she's just *empty*, and they can fill her back up. They said they can. They said—"

"They're liars," Sefa said. "Brenn. They're holes wearing men's coats. They don't restore anything. They harvest."

"I know." His jaw worked. "I know what they probably are. But if there's a chance—one chance—then it wasn't a hard choice, was it? That's the thing nobody tells you. Everyone says betrayal is a hard choice. It isn't. It's the easiest thing in the world. That's why it happens all the time."

The enforcers began to descend.

---

Afterward, Sefa would remember it in fragments, the way a storm breaks itself into pieces small enough to survive.

Casper shouting words from the grimoire, and the salt on the steps flaring blue-white, holding the first rank of enforcers frozen mid-stride like flies in amber. Doss hauling Wren backward up six steps, eight, ten, toward a crack in the cliff-face that might or might not go anywhere. The tall enforcer moving through Casper's wards as though they were cobwebs, unhurried, inevitable.

And Brenn—Brenn turning at the last moment, breaking his own bargain, throwing his arms around the enforcer's neck and dragging it back from the others, roaring, "GO, you idiots, GO—" while its pale hands closed on his forearms and light poured out of his open mouth, out of his eyes, years of him streaming away into those coin-blank sockets, and the last thing he shouted, the thing Sefa would hear every night for the rest of her life, was not his sister's name.

It was hers. *"Vane! Keep them safe!"*

Then Sefa was shoved through the crack in the cliff by Casper's small strong hands, and the dark swallowed them, and the screaming stopped.

---

They ran through the bones of the mountain for what felt like hours—old smugglers' tunnels, airless and blind, guided only by Casper's witchlight and Sefa's memory of routes her mother's mother had cut before the crown ever darkened the sky. They didn't speak. Wren cried silently the whole way, tears cutting clean tracks through the salt dust on her face. Doss walked like a man carrying timber.

When they finally surfaced, in a ravine choked with grey bracken, Sefa called a halt and made herself count heads. Herself. Doss. Wren. Casper.

Four. Out of seven, counting the ones left on the ship at anchor. Five, counting Brenn.

"Casper." Her voice came out sanded flat. "The book."

The apprentice went white. Hands flew to the sling across their back—and stopped. Sefa saw it happen, saw the moment the fingers found the tear. The grimoire came out into the witchlight in two halves, ripped raggedly down the spine, pages loose and drifting like wounded birds. Casper held the pieces the way you'd hold a bird with a broken wing.

"It caught on—" Their voice failed. They tried again. "When I went through the crack. The rock. It caught and I didn't—I didn't stop, I just ran, I *ran*—"

"Hey." Sefa crossed to them and closed the apprentice's fingers over the torn halves. "Look at me. You ran because running was right. Book can be mended. People who get taken by those things—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Book can be mended."

"But the unbinding rite spans both halves," Casper whispered. "Mistress Okonkwo split it deliberately—said no one person should carry the whole of it. The second half has the words that end it. The words that *free* him. And I don't know if they're—"

A sound from the ravine's mouth stopped them all.

Not an enforcer. Enforcers didn't whistle. But someone was whistling—three notes, low, repeated. A smuggler's signal. *Friend in the dark. Come careful.*

Sefa's knife was in her hand before the echo faded. She traded a look with Doss, who nodded and put himself between Wren and the ravine mouth. Then the bracken parted, and a figure limped into the witchlight: a woman, gaunt, grey-streaked, dressed in eleven years out of fashion, with eyes that were not empty at all but blazing, blazing wet and furious and alive.

Behind her, half-carried, hung a big man with a scarred face and a smile flickering uncertainly on it, like a lamp trying to remember how to burn.

"Sefa Vane?" the woman said. "My brother wrote your name somewhere he couldn't reach anymore. Said if any part of him survived, it would be trusting you."

In Casper's hands, the torn grimoire's loose pages stirred, though there was no wind—and on the topmost page, ink was rising fresh and deliberate, forming letters none of them had written:

*The crown knows you are coming. It has known since the stair. Hurry.*
