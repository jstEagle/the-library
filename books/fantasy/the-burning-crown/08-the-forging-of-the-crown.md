## 8. The Forging of the Crown

The grimoire's torn edge wept.

Casper had not known a book could bleed, but there it was: where the enforcers' blade had sheared the binding in half, the pages leaked a thin silver light, like marrow from a bone. They sat in the smugglers' safehouse beneath the tannery district, where the air smelled of lime and old salt, and turned the wounded thing over and over in their hands, looking for whatever half remained.

The first half—the half they'd kept—held the wards. The theory of them, at least: diagrams of Thornfell's border stones, the knot-patterns of protection Mistress Verity had woven across three kingdoms, each one anchored to a single star. Casper had studied these for years without understanding what they anchored *to*, or why. Now the answer lay in the missing pages, somewhere in Sefa's captured satchel or scattered along the Salt Stair where the enforcers had cornered them.

*Think,* they told themselves. *She trained you to think.*

Mistress Verity had been many things—sharp-tongued, impatient, fond of burning bread and calling it "rustic"—but she had never been careless. If the grimoire's deepest secret could be lost to a knife-stroke on a stairway, then it was never meant to live only in the book.

Casper held the torn half close to the lamp and looked again at the wards. And saw, for the first time, that every diagram was drawn in two hands. The main lines were Verity's—she'd signed her work the way she signed everything, with a small hooked *V* hidden in the flourishes. But beneath those lines, faint as breath on glass, ran an older script. A different hand. A hand Casper knew better than any handwriting in the world, because it was the hand they'd spent ten years copying letters in, grinding ink for, being scolded for smudging.

Their own. Or near enough that the difference was a matter of centuries rather than blood.

*No,* Casper thought. *Not near enough. Exactly. It is exactly your hand, because it is your blood's hand, and you have known this since the night the stars went out and felt the wards die like teeth pulled from your own jaw.*

They had told no one that part. That when the border wards collapsed, Casper had felt it—not heard, not seen, but *felt*, a snapping behind the sternum, as if something strung through their body had been cut. They had blamed fear. They had blamed grief. They had blamed everything except the obvious.

The obvious was inked in their ancestors' hand on every page.

* * *

It was Brannic who found the seam.

The old smuggler had appointed himself Casper's shadow since the Salt Stair, since Sefa went into the enforcers' wagon with her wrists bound and her chin up, since Casper had run instead of fighting and hated themselves for it with a clean, clarifying hatred. He came down the safehouse stairs now with a stub of candle and an expression like a man who has found a hole in his boat.

"Your book," he said. "It's warm."

"It's always warm."

"It's warmer *here*." He set the candle on the table and tapped the back cover, near the spine, where the leather had buckled into a ridge. "Feel."

Casper felt. Beneath the ridge, the leather was hot as a fevered brow, and when they pressed, the cover sighed open along a line Casper had never seen—a seam finer than any stitch, sealed with ward-work so old it had become indistinguishable from the material itself. Their thumb, moving along it, left a trail where the seal dissolved. Only their thumb. When Brannic tried, the leather stayed shut as a clam.

"Right," Brannic said, in the tone of a man deciding not to ask questions he'd regret the answers to. "I'll be upstairs. Shouting if anyone hollow-eyed comes down the lane."

The hidden pages slid free like a tongue from a mouth.

There were seven of them, written in that familiar-unfamiliar hand, and the first bore a title in letters that seemed to sink into the parchment even as Casper read:

*On the Forging of the Crown, and the Debt of Our Line.*

*We were not wardens,* the page began. *Understand this first, whoever reads: we were jailers. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

*The king we bound was no king. He was a hunger wearing a crown it had taken from a dead man, and by the time the stars began to go out above his marches, he had eaten the memories of four villages entire—left them standing, breathing, smiling, empty as cups. We could not kill him. We tried. What he had eaten made him too large; every death we dealt him, he wore like a coat. So we did the worse thing. We forged him a crown of our own making, iron and oath and starlight, and we set it upon his head, and it ate him instead—slowly, memory by memory, until what remained in the throne room was a gentle, bewildered man who could not remember why he deserved to be caged.*

*That is the mercy and the horror of it. The crown does not destroy. It keeps. Every memory it takes from Aldric goes into the crown itself, and the crown holds them the way a dam holds water—and the wards of the border hold against the dark the way a dam holds against the flood. While the crown fills, the wards stand. When the crown is full—*

The page ended there. Not torn. Ended, deliberately, mid-sentence, and the next page began:

*When the crown is full, someone must choose. This is the debt of our line. Each generation, one of our blood must come to the throne room and renew the binding—feed the crown a portion of their own remembering, so that it never reaches its fill. My sister did it. Her daughter did it. I have done it twice, and I will tell you honestly, reader, child of mine, that I do not remember my wedding day, or the sea, or the sound of my mother's voice, and I would do it again tomorrow.*

*But hear the other truth, which I have hidden even from myself until now: the crown can be broken. Not renewed. Broken. The tyrant's hunger died with his last true memory, long ago; what remains in the crown is only storage, only weight. Break it, and everything it holds pours back into the world—the king's memories to the king, the stolen years to the sky, star by star by star.*

*And the price. There is always a price, and ours is paid in the coin we know best. The breaker must give the crown something equal to what it releases. A life of remembering for a kingdom's worth. You will not get to choose what it takes. It takes what you hold dearest, because that is what balances the scale.*

*I write this hidden because I am a coward about one thing only: I do not want to know which of my children will read this. I love you. I am sorry. The forge is yours now.*

Casper read it three times. Then a fourth, because the third time their eyes had blurred and they needed to be sure of the words *the forge is yours now*, which sounded less like an inheritance than a sentence being passed.

* * *

They did not sleep. Toward the grey hour before dawn, Brannic came down again and found them sitting exactly where he'd left them, the seven pages spread in a fan across the table, the lamp burned to a puddle.

"Well?" he said.

"The enforcers," Casper said. Their voice came out scraped. "The ones with the empty eyes. Do you know what they are?"

"Dead men walking, by the look of them."

"Worse. Better." Casper gathered the pages with hands that would not quite steady. "They're bearers. People of our line—or people the crown chose when our line ran thin—who came to renew the binding and failed. Who gave too much, or too little, or broke under it. The crown empties them out and uses what's left. Hollow hands to do hollow work." They looked up at Brannic, and found it strangely easy to say the rest aloud, now that it was written down in an ancestor's ink. "Brannic, I'm descended from the people who built that thing. The wards, the crown, all of it. The reason the stars went out over Thornfell is that nobody of my blood has stood in that throne room in forty years. My mistress was the last bearer. She was buying time. She sent me away to keep me from the choice, and then she died, and the choice just—" they made a fist, opened it, "—waited for me."

Brannic lowered himself onto the bench across the table, joints cracking like green wood. For a long moment he said nothing, and the silence of the safehouse pressed in, thick with tallow-smell and the distant groan of the tannery vats.

"My da was a debt-collector," he said at last. "Not the violent kind. The patient kind. He used to say a debt doesn't care whether you knew you owed it. It just sits there, growing interest." He nodded at the pages. "That what this feels like?"

"Yes."

"And what do you owe, exactly?"

Casper looked at the seventh page, the last one, which was not prose at all but a diagram: the throne room of Aldmere rendered in fine lines, the dais, the mirror-wall behind the throne, and at the center a circle marked with the same knot-pattern Casper had been drawing since they were six years old. In the middle of the circle, two words in their ancestor's hand:

*THE FORGE. THE CHOICE.*

"Two roads," Casper said. "Renew the binding, and the crown keeps eating Aldric until there's nothing left of him at all, and then it starts on the country, and some child of mine in a hundred years sits where I'm sitting reading this same page. Or break it. Pour everything back. The king gets his memories, the sky gets its stars, the wards get their anchors." They swallowed. "And I pay for all of it with the best of what I have. It takes what you hold dearest. Those are the terms. Written by my own blood, so I suppose they'd know."

Brannic was quiet a while longer. Outside, the first cart of the morning rattled past, and somewhere a tannery worker coughed.

"Sefa's up there right now," he said finally. "In whatever hole they've got her. With her memories in some enforcer's ledger, waiting to be fed to that thing or traded back to her for a price. You said the crown keeps everything it takes." His eyes, rheumy and shrewd, fixed on Casper. "Everything it took from her is still in it. Everything it took from the districts. From the king."

Casper went still.

"I hadn't—" they started, and stopped, because they had, actually, they'd known it since the first page, and grief had simply been sitting on the knowledge like a hen on an egg. "Yes. It's all still in there. Nothing the crown eats is ever destroyed. That's the whole horror of it. It *keeps.*"

"Then it's not just a cage you're deciding about." Brannic rose, knees popping, and took up his candle. "It's a vault. And everything everyone's lost is locked inside, waiting on whether a runaway apprentice decides to pay the toll." He paused at the foot of the stairs, silhouetted against the grey light from above. "For what it's worth, girl—boy—Casper. My da also said the only debts worth having are the ones you'd pay twice."

He climbed the stairs. Casper sat alone with the seven pages and the dying lamp and the weight settling onto their shoulders—not crushing, not yet, but real, the weight of a thing that fits because it was made for you.

They thought of Mistress Verity, who had carried this alone for forty years and never once let it show behind her sharp tongue and her burnt bread. Who had addressed the grimoire to Casper by name because she had known, all along, whose turn it would be. Anger flickered in Casper's chest, old and tired, and guttered out. She hadn't abandoned them to the choice. She'd trained them for it, every scolding, every lesson, every ward drawn and redrawn until the patterns lived in Casper's fingers deeper than their own name.

*The forge is yours now.*

"All right," Casper said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead, to the ancestor whose hand matched theirs. "All right. I'll pay it."

They folded the seven pages into their shirt, over their heart, and went upstairs to wake the crew, because there was one more thing the hidden pages had shown them—a route into the palace, through the old forge-tunnels beneath the mirror room, a door that answered only to their blood.

And on the horizon beyond the tannery roofs, where the sky should have been paling toward dawn, the darkness had begun, very slightly, to deepen—as if the night itself were leaning in to watch what the last of the forging line would do.
