## 9. Unbinding

The palace had a heartbeat.

Casper felt it through the soles of their boots as they came up out of the underways with Sefa's crew at their back—slow, vast, wrong, like the pulse of something sleeping in a flooded cellar. The grimoire's torn halves rode against their chest, bound together with smuggler's twine and prayer, and every step toward the throne room made the pages warm against their sternum until it felt less like carrying a book and more like carrying a coal.

Above them, the Salt Stair rang with boots and shouting. The raid was already moving without them; that had been Sefa's design. *You're the key,* she'd said, pressing her knife into Casper's hand handle-first, an old smuggler's blessing. *We're just the noise around you.*

The noise was magnificent. Dree's smoke-pots bloomed violet in the colonnades. Oskar's voice rolled down the marble throat of the great hall, bellowing about fire and the king's justice and other lies delivered with such conviction that even Casper half-believed him. Enforcers poured toward the sound, hollow-eyed and silent, moving like puppets whose strings had been cut but who hadn't yet noticed.

Casper went the other way, up the servants' stair, through a door that remembered being oak and had forgotten how to be locked.

* * *

They found Sefa before they found the throne room—which was to say, they found her cage.

It stood in the antechamber beneath the dais: not iron, not wood, but a column of pale light shaped roughly like a person's absence, and inside it Sefa Vane hung like a moth pinned to glass. Her coat was gone. Her knives were gone. Her mouth moved when she saw Casper, shaping words the light wouldn't let out, and her eyes—her sharp, laughing, untrustworthy, beloved eyes—were still entirely her own.

That was the worst part. The crown didn't want her memories. It wanted hers as bait.

"Casper." Her voice came through the light warped, as if shouted up from underwater. "Trap. It's a trap, you idiot, it *knows* you're—"

"I know." Casper set both palms against the column. It bit them—not cold, not hot, but *hungry*, a sensation with teeth. "I know it knows. I'm coming anyway."

"That's the stupidest—" Sefa laughed, ragged, furious, alive. "Gods. You really are one of ours now."

The unbinding for a captive ward was simple, which did not make it easy. Casper spoke the words from the grimoire's second half—the half that had cost them blood and a week of Oskar's terrible cooking to decipher—and felt the crew-mistress's craft rise in their throat like water finding its level. The light screamed. Not audibly. In the way a starved animal screams, all appetite and no voice.

Sefa fell out of it into Casper's arms, shaking, lighter than she should have been, as if the ward had been drinking more than memory.

"Throne room," she gasped. "It's waiting for you. It's been *waiting*."

"Good," said Casper, who did not feel brave at all, and opened the doors.

* * *

The throne room of Aldmere was a wound dressed as architecture.

Gold leaf peeled from the walls in long curls. The great windows showed a sky with nothing in it—not clouds, not night, just an absence wearing the shape of sky. And on the throne sat King Aldric, crowned, his hands flat on his knees like a man holding himself down, his face turned toward the doors with an expression Casper had last seen on their mistress in her final days: the look of someone listening to a voice only they could hear.

"You came," Aldric said. His voice was rusted from disuse. "She said you would. The hedge-witch. She said—" He stopped. Pressed his palms harder against his knees. "It lets me keep almost nothing. But it keeps her. Do you understand? It keeps her because keeping her hurts me most."

"I understand," Casper said, and began to walk.

The crown felt them coming. Casper knew it the way one knows a hand rising behind them in the dark—and then the dark rose instead, and the crown reached into them with fingers made of everything they had ever tried to bury.

*Mistress Ilse's cottage, door hanging open, the smell of burnt rosemary.* Their first night running, huddled in the waystation, the guilty little thrill of being unfound. *The wards flaring green over Thornfell and dying like struck matches.* Every star going out at once while Casper stood in the yard and thought, shamefully, wonderfully: *for one night, nobody is looking for me.*

And underneath all of it, older, deeper, the one the crown saved for last:

*Their mother's face, the day they left. Not angry. Worse. Relieved.*

Casper staggered. Kept walking. The memories came again, sharper, edited by something that had studied them for years, and this time they brought company—the enforcers' emptiness, whole districts of the capital blinking out sentence by sentence, a city forgetting its own name one charm at a time. *This is what you are,* the crown said, in a voice assembled from every cruel thing anyone had ever said to them. *A runaway. A thief of other people's craft. Your line built me and your line abandoned me and you are nothing but the last coin of a debased inheritance—*

"Yes," Casper said aloud, and kept walking, and the words tasted like blood and freedom. "All of that. And I'm here anyway."

That was the trick of it. They understood it suddenly, wholly, the way the grimoire had been trying to tell them all along in its cramped dead woman's hand: force fed the crown. Fear fed it. Grief fed it. It was a thing forged from coercion, and coercion was its only food. But a choice freely made—

A freely given sacrifice was a language it had never learned to eat.

"Aldric." Casper reached the foot of the dais. Up close the king looked less like a monarch than like a held breath. "I can end this. But I need you to help me, and helping me will cost you everything it's given back. Every memory it's let you keep. Her. All of her."

Aldric looked at them for a long moment. Then he smiled—a small, cracked, human smile, the first true thing in that room.

"She tried to save me once," he said. "She failed. She spent forty years failing better. And now she sent me you." He rose from the throne, and his hands were steady. "Take it. Take all of it. I would rather lose her a second time as myself than keep her a moment longer as his prisoner."

"Not his," Casper said gently. "Mine. My family forged this. That's why it hates me most."

"Then it chose its enemy well."

They climbed the dais together.

* * *

The unbinding, done properly, required three things: the bearer's consent, the heir's blood, and the words.

Aldric gave the first without hesitation, kneeling on the steps below his own throne like a man at prayer. Casper opened their palm on the second, drawing the blade Sefa had pressed on them across the lifeline, letting the grimoire drink. The book drank greedily. The torn halves knitted themselves under the blood as if the wound were thread.

The words were the third, and the crown fought them with everything it had.

It threw Casper's mother at them. It threw the empty road out of Thornfell, the last look back at a cottage already going grey in memory. It threw forward, too—showed them the price before they'd agreed to pay it: themselves standing on the palace steps tomorrow, looking at Sefa's face and feeling nothing of home, hearing *Thornfell* as a stranger's word. It showed them the stars returning and themselves unable to remember why they'd wanted them.

Casper spoke through it. Voice cracking, hands shaking, blood dripping onto gold—they spoke every word, and where the crown stole a word from their mouth they wrote it in the air with their bleeding finger, and where it stole that too they simply started again, stubborn as any hedge-witch's apprentice ever taught to mend a fence-line ward.

"It won't hold," Aldric whispered, and Casper couldn't tell if he meant the binding or himself. He was flickering. With each syllable, memories poured out of him visibly, like heat-shimmer off summer stone—a coronation, a betrayal, a woman with rosemary-burnt hands laughing in a kitchen—given up one by one, freely, terribly, his face wet and serene. "Keep going. Don't stop for me. *Don't stop for me.*"

"I'm not stopping."

The crown began to scream.

Not with the king's voice. Not with Casper's. With the voices of every bearer who had ever failed—generations of them, layered like sediment, all the hollow-eyed enforcers who had once stood exactly where Casper stood and chosen wrongly. The sound filled the throne room, filled the windows, filled the starless sky outside, and Casper understood with sudden awful clarity that the scream wasn't rage.

It was gratitude. Or the closest thing a cage could feel to it.

"*Unbind*," Casper said, the final word, the word their ancestors had written and never dared speak, and put both bleeding hands on the crown.

Light went through them like winter.

For one endless moment Casper was every Reyes who had ever lived—the tyrant-crowners and the tyrant-makers, the renewers and the refusers, a long red thread of blood and burden running back through centuries to a forge built in fear. They felt the original sin of it: good people, frightened people, choosing imprisonment over justice because imprisonment was easier to maintain. They felt the debt. They accepted it.

And then, with Aldric's hands over theirs, with Sefa's voice shouting their name from the doorway, with the whole found weight of their strange new family at their back—

They broke it.

* * *

The crown did not shatter like glass. It shattered like ice on a spring river—all at once, everywhere, with a sound like the world exhaling.

The pieces fell as light. Casper watched them go, streaming up through the broken windows, threading through the ruined gold, pouring out of the palace like a flock of released birds, and high above Aldmere the sky began—began—to remember itself.

One star. Faint, trembling, absurdly beautiful.

Then two. Then ten. Then a scatter of them, then a spill, then a flood, constellations cascading back into their ancient seats like a country's exiles coming home, and below in the streets of the capital people were pouring out of their houses, screaming, weeping, pointing upward at lights most of them had never seen.

Casper slid down the dais steps and sat, abruptly, on cold marble, next to a king who no longer knew his own name but was smiling anyway, peaceful as a man finally allowed to sleep.

"Casper." Sefa dropped to her knees beside them, gripping their shoulders, searching their face. "Hey. Hey, look at me. What did it take? What did it *take*?"

Casper opened their mouth to answer and found the answer missing.

Thornfell was gone. Not the knowledge of it—they knew the word, knew it meant mountains, a border, a cottage—but the texture, the smell of rosemary, the green flare of dying wards, the guilty thrill of the unfound night, Mistress Ilse's voice saying their name. Gone, paid out like coin, spent to the last piece of silver. They searched for grief and found only the shape where grief should live, smooth and vacant as a scar.

"I don't know yet," Casper said honestly, and looked up past Sefa's stricken face, past Oskar and Dree crowding the doorway, past the king dreaming on the steps, to the sky.

The stars were still arriving. Thousands of them. And among the oldest constellations, in a place no chart had ever named, a new pattern was burning itself into the dark—bright, unfamiliar, unmistakably shaped like a small crew of liars and thieves standing on a palace staircase.

"Look," Casper whispered, and pointed. "I think it's us."
