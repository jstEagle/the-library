## 10. New Stars

The crown did not shatter the way glass shatters.

It came apart like a held breath finally released—slowly at first, then all at once, a sound that was not sound but the absence of one, a silence so complete Casper felt it in their teeth, behind their eyes, in the hollow of their throat where a scream had been living for days.

They stood in the center of the throne room with both hands raised and the crown's fragments turning to light above them, and the light was gold, then white, then something beyond color entirely, and through it Casper could see the throne room as it must have looked a hundred years ago: banners on the walls instead of bare stone, a floor of polished cedar instead of cracked slate, and Aldric—young, unburdened, laughing at something a hedge-witch had said—standing where Sefa now stood with her sword drawn and her face turned up toward the brightness like a woman watching rain end a drought.

*You cannot,* the crown said, or the memory of it said, because the crown was already mostly gone. *You are ours. You were always ours.*

"No," Casper said, and their voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring. "I'm theirs."

And they thought of Sefa's laugh, rough as gravel and twice as warm. Of Tam teaching them dice on the third night out, cheating shamelessly and letting them win anyway. Of Wren humming in the dark of the wagon while the road unspooled beneath them toward a city that had forgotten its own name. Of a fire built under a starless sky, and hands passing a shared cup, and the astonishing, terrifying discovery that a person could belong somewhere without earning it first.

The crown broke.

Not into pieces—into stars.

Casper felt it happen inside themselves before they saw it happen in the air: a great loosening, a pouring-out, the thing they carried spilling upward through the shattered skylight and the broken windows and every crack in the ancient walls, streaming into the night like water finding its way home. And it took things with it. It took the smell of Mistress Yewen's cottage, woodsmoke and dried lavender and ink. It took the path down to the creek where Casper had learned to float on their back and watch the constellations turn. It took her voice saying *careful, child, magic remembers what we forget*, and the shape of her hands, and the name of the village two miles east, and—

No. Not all of it. Not yet.

The last star—the last true memory, the grimoire had said, though the grimoire lay in halves on the flagstones now, its work finished—hovered at the edge of Casper's mind like a lamp in a far window. They reached for it and found, with a strange calm, that they already knew what it was.

Thornfell. The whole of it. The cottage and the wards and the woman who had raised them and lied to them and loved them and left them a book with their name on the first page. All of it gathered together in one small burning place, and the crown's last hunger curled around it like smoke around a wick.

"Let me," said Aldric.

He had come down from the dais. He stood beside Casper in the ruin of his own long captivity, an old man in a young man's body, and his eyes—for the first time since the mirror room—were not empty. They were full to overflowing, brimming with everything the unbinding had poured back into him: a kingdom, a betrayal, a friend with dirt under her fingernails who had tried to save him and failed and died trying.

"You shouldn't have to pay alone," he said. "She wouldn't have wanted that."

"She'd have wanted me to be smart about it." Casper laughed, and it came out wet. "This is me being smart."

"It isn't."

"No," Casper agreed. "But it's mine."

Aldric bowed his head—a king's bow, deep and formal and utterly sincere—and stepped back.

Casper opened their hand. The last memory rose from them like heat off summer stone, and they watched it go without flinching, because someone had told them once that magic remembers what we forget, and if that was true—if any of it was true—then Thornfell wasn't gone. It was just somewhere Casper couldn't follow. Somewhere safe.

The last star went home.

* * *

They walked out onto the palace steps at dawn, or what should have been dawn, except that the sky was still black and salted with darkness and the crowd below—the whole city, it seemed, spilled out of doors and into the streets, drawn by whatever ripple the breaking had sent through the world—stood looking up, waiting for nothing, expecting nothing, because no one alive remembered what a sky was supposed to hold.

Then the first star returned.

It came back the way a candle comes back when relit: sudden, small, absolute. A single point of silver fire high over the eastern towers, and the crowd made a sound Casper had never heard before—not a cheer, not a gasp, but something older, a sound the body makes before the mind can decide anything, a sound like recognition.

Then another star. Then ten. Then a hundred, a thousand, the whole spilled treasury of them pouring back across the heavens in rivers and clusters and slow wheeling constellations whose names half the crowd suddenly remembered mid-gasp, shouting them out to strangers—*the Plow! the Salt Road! the Weaver's Loom!*—and strangers answering back with other names, other stories, until the square below the steps was a riot of recovered sky and recovered language and people holding each other and pointing and weeping without quite knowing why.

Casper stood at the top of the steps and felt the hole where Thornfell had been.

It didn't hurt the way they'd feared. It hurt the way an amputation hurts after the healing begins: a ghost-ache, a cold spot, a certainty of something missing rather than the missing itself. They knew, abstractly, that there had been a cottage. That there had been a woman. That the woman had been important—that she was, in some way Casper could no longer access, the reason for all of this. But the knowing was like reading about a stranger's life in a borrowed book. The pages were there. The warmth was not.

"Casper." Sefa's hand landed on their shoulder, heavy and real. "Hey. Look at me."

Casper looked. Sefa's face was streaked with soot and dried blood and tears she would deny to her grave, and her eyes were steady as a keel.

"Do you remember the fire?" she asked. "Third night out. You burned the flatbread."

"I remember the fire," Casper said, and their voice held. "I remember you telling Tam he cheated even though you taught him how."

"I never taught him how. He has no honor." She pulled them in, and Casper let her, and over her shoulder Wren arrived and then Tam and then the rest of them, a tangle of arms and salt-stiff hair and laughter that shook loose something in Casper's chest that had been clenched since the night the stars went out.

"You're crying," Tam said gleefully.

"I'm experiencing weather," Sefa said into the top of Wren's head.

Above them, the sky turned slowly on its ancient axis, hung with more lights than Casper had ever seen, and Casper understood—with the part of them that remained, the part that was new-made and unwritten—that this was the trade. This was what the crown had taken as its toll: not their future, not their found family, but their past. One life's worth of yesterdays, spent to buy back everyone else's tomorrows.

It seemed, standing there in the crush of Sefa's crew, absurdly cheap.

* * *

They stayed in the capital nine days, because kings insisted, and because Wren discovered the harbor markets and refused to leave them. Aldric—King Aldric again, though he flinched at the title and asked them, privately, to keep using his name—sat with Casper each evening in a small room overlooking the eastern wall, and between them they rebuilt what could be rebuilt.

"I remember her hands," he said on the fifth night. "Dirt under the nails. She was always digging in something. I don't remember her face anymore—I think I lost that years ago, to the crown—but the hands I kept. She'd have liked this, I think. The stars coming back. She used to say the sky was a ledger, and someday everything written there would be read aloud."

Casper turned their cup in their fingers. "Tell me about her. Please. I know there's—I know there's a shape where she should be, and I can't fill it."

So he told them. About a hedge-witch named Yewen who had come to court with mud on her boots and opinions no one asked for, who had seen what the council intended for their young king and opposed it, who had spent thirty years maintaining wards along a border no one thanked her for, waiting for an apprentice worth trusting with the truth. He told it slowly, in pieces, across those nine evenings, and Casper listened the way you drink water in a desert: greedily, gratefully, letting each detail settle into the empty place like sediment filling a riverbed.

It wasn't memory. But it was something. It was hers, given freely, and it was enough.

On the ninth morning, Sefa found Casper on the palace steps, a packed bag at their feet and the two halves of the grimoire wrapped in oilcloth beneath their arm.

"The *Kestrel* sails on the evening tide," she said. "South, then west. There's a stretch of coast where the old wards are failing—same rot as Thornfell had, if the rumors hold. Nobody's watching it. Nobody's fixing it." She shrugged elaborately. "Seems like the kind of thing a smuggler's crew might need a ward-crafter for. If we knew one."

Casper looked up at the morning sky, blue and clean and crowded with invisible stars waiting for nightfall. They thought of a cottage they could no longer picture, and a woman they knew only through a king's remembering, and a book with their name on the first page that was, they suspected, not finished writing them after all.

"West," Casper said. "I've never seen the western coast."

"No," Sefa agreed, hauling them up by the arm. "You haven't. Time we gave you something new to forget."

They went down the steps together, into the noise and the morning, while overhead the stars waited patiently for dark—and when dark came, they would find Casper Reyes on the deck of the *Kestrel*, looking up, learning the sky again one story at a time, in the company of everyone who mattered.
