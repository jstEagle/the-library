## 1. The Cartographer's Rule

The rule was simple, and Oscar Dravenmoor had built her life upon it the way a mason builds upon bedrock: *never draw what you cannot afford to lose.*

She had learned it at nine years old, ink-stained and solemn, when her father had caught her copying one of his charts—the real ones, the ones that lived in a locked drawer beneath his drafting table. He hadn't shouted. He had simply knelt beside her chair, taken the pen from her small fingers, and said, "A map is a confession, little wren. Every line you draw tells someone where you've been, what you know, what you love. So before you put ink to paper, ask yourself: is this a truth I can survive them having?"

She had not understood then. She understood now, ten years after his death, as she sat in the back room of her shop in Greyhollow with three finished maps spread before her on the scarred oak table, deciding which of them would be allowed to exist.

The first was honest work—a survey of the lowland river country commissioned by a wool merchant named Aldous Fen, who wanted to know which fords would bear his carts in spring. Rivers, roads, market towns marked in tidy brown and blue. A map any journeyman cartographer in the capital might have drawn. Safe. Forgettable.

The second was nearly as safe: a hunting chart of the Thornfell foothills, game trails and water sources rendered in greens so muted they seemed to have grown from the paper itself. The kind of thing a lord's steward might pin up in a study and never look at twice.

The third map was the problem.

It showed the high passes of Thornfell—all of them, including the six that appeared on no other chart in existence. The Shepherd's Crack, a fissure barely wide enough for a man with a pack, that wound through the mountain's shoulder and emerged above the treeline. The Hollow Stair, carved by hands unknown centuries before the first kingdom rose, its steps worn smooth as glass by feet that had never stopped climbing. And the Weeping Way, where snowmelt bled through the rock face year-round and froze into curtains of ice that could be shattered and climbed like a ladder to heaven.

Oscar had found these routes herself, over a decade of walking the mountains under the pretense of survey work. She had measured them, memorized them, committed every switchback and scree field to a memory she kept oiled and sharp as a blade. And then she had done the thing that defined her more than any bloodline ever could:

She had not written them down.

The third map on her table showed only the passes the Crown already knew about—passes that were watched, taxed, and in two cases deliberately collapsed by royal engineers decades ago. It was accurate. It was useless. It was exactly what she intended to sell.

*Never draw what you cannot afford to lose.* Her father's voice, warm and tired, from a decade of graves ago.

She rolled the merchant's maps, sealed them with plain wax, and set them aside. Then she pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began, as she did every night, the work that actually mattered: drawing nothing at all.

This was her private discipline, stranger than any smuggler's ritual. Each evening she took a blank page and drew the mountain from memory—not the polite mountain of her sold maps, but the true one, every crevice and goat path and abandoned watchtower, holding it all in her mind's eye the way a musician holds a symphony. When the page was full—and it was always full, dense with notation only she could read, a cipher of her own devising that looked like decorative flourishes to any untrained eye—she fed it to the stove and watched a decade of knowledge curl into ash.

Some nights she wondered if she was a cartographer at all, or merely a woman who burned things.

* * *

Greyhollow woke slowly, the way mountain towns do, shrugging off fog before it shrugged off sleep. By the time Oscar unlocked her shutters and turned the sign to read *FAIRWEATHER FINE MAPS & CHARTS*, the morning market was already grumbling to life below—fishwives arguing over the night's catch, a carter swearing at his mule, the baker's boy weaving through it all with bread stacked to his chin.

Fairweather. She had chosen the name herself at seventeen, standing in this same doorway with paint still tacky on the sign. It sounded like nothing. It sounded like a woman who had blown in on an ordinary wind and would blow out again just as easily. In ten years, no one in Greyhollow had ever asked where Mistress Fairweather had come from, because the answer—*here and there, doesn't everyone come from somewhere?*—was so dull that asking again would be an admission of having nothing better to do.

Dullness was armor. She wore it like her father had worn his.

Her first customer arrived before the fog had fully lifted: Aldous Fen himself, red-faced and smelling of lanolin, eager to see the river charts he'd paid half up front for. Oscar walked him through them with the patient patter she'd perfected—spring levels here, solid fords there, avoid the eastern bank after thaw—and watched him nod along, pleased with his purchase and entirely unaware that the woman selling it to him could walk from Greyhollow to the coast without touching a single road the Crown maintained.

"Fine work," Fen declared, tucking the roll under his arm. "You've a gift, Mistress Fairweather. Ever thought of taking commissions in the capital? Cartographers of your caliber are rare."

"The air disagrees with me," she said, smiling the smile that ended conversations politely. "Mountains suit me."

"They say you've mapped half of Thornfell," he pressed, leaning in with the appetite of a man who smelled profit. "There's talk in Lowmarket of silver veins in the northern reaches. If a man had a guide who knew—"

"You'd want a mining surveyor," Oscar said. "I sell maps, Master Fen. I don't sell myself."

He left slightly offended and thoroughly satisfied with his charts, which was the ideal outcome of most of her transactions. She watched him go from the doorway, arms crossed against the morning chill, and felt the familiar shape of the day settle over her: customers until midday, survey sketching in the afternoon light, the evening burn, the nightly memory-drawing. A wheel turning smoothly on well-greased axles.

It had been enough, once. She tried to remember when it had stopped being enough, and couldn't find the moment—it had happened the way water wears stone, invisibly, a grain at a time.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the mountains, as it always did. Oscar shouldered her survey pack—brass instruments, blank folios, the harmless tools of a harmless trade—and climbed the switchback trail behind Greyhollow to a granite shelf she called her office. From there the whole southern face of Thornfell unfolded below her: grey peaks going gold at the seams where the late sun struck, valleys pooled with shadow, the thin silver threads of streams stitching it all together.

She worked for two hours on a legitimate commission—a boundary dispute between two sheep farmers that would pay for a winter's coal—and let her eyes wander where they wished across the true map in her head. There: the smoke of the high shepherd camps, three days' climb north. There: the notch in the ridge that led down into the Vale of Thorns, where the smugglers ran their cargo on moonless nights. She knew the men who used those paths by silhouette and boot-print rather than name; she sold them route information sometimes, always vague, always deniable, always paid in coin that went into a floor cache she told no one about. Not because she needed the money. Because refusing them would have meant questions, and questions were the one commodity she never traded in.

That was the arrangement her whole life rested on: she gave the world exactly as much of herself as kept it from looking closer.

Below her, Greyhollow smoked gently, forty-odd rooftops huddled together like sheep against the vast indifference of the peaks. Somewhere beyond the mountains lay the capital, the Crown, the Inquisitors with their white cloaks and their lists of forbidden names. Somewhere in the old stories her father had never quite finished telling lay the reason a girl with a gift for maps had been taught to erase herself line by line.

She had stopped asking why at fourteen. She was starting to ask again at twenty-seven, and she didn't like the answer that was forming: *because a cage that fits perfectly is still a cage.*

The tremor came at dusk.

It began as a sound she felt before she heard—a deep, subterranean groan, like some enormous thing rolling over in its sleep beneath the world. Then the shelf beneath her shuddered, once, twice, a long rolling shudder that sent a chip of granite skittering off the edge and tumbling into the dark. Below, Greyhollow's dogs began to howl, all of them at once, a ragged chorus of alarm.

Oscar went still, hand flat against the stone, reading it the way she read everything—by touch, by pattern, by instinct honed on ten years of mountain weather. Earthquakes happened in Thornfell; the range was old and restless and occasionally grumbled about it. But this—

This had not moved like stone settling. Stone settling was sharp, percussive, a crack and a slump. This had been a *roll*, slow and deep and rhythmic, three pulses like a heartbeat. Like something breathing.

By the time she'd packed her instruments and descended to town, the tremor was the only topic in Greyhollow. The fishwives had reclassified it as an omen; the carter whose mule had bolted classified it as the mule's fault; the blacksmith, who fancied himself a scholar, announced that the mountain was shedding winter's frost-heave and everyone could stop panicking. Oscar bought a loaf of bread, agreed mildly with the blacksmith, and walked home through streets where lamps flickered in windows and children were called indoors earlier than usual.

In her shop, she lit the stove, ate her bread, and did not draw.

She sat instead before the blank page for a long while, quill poised, listening to the silence of the house around her. Ten years of routine, and tonight the routine felt less like armor and more like a shell she'd outgrown—tight at the shoulders, pressing at the ribs. She thought of the high passes no map held, and how she knew them better than she knew her own neighbors. She thought of the floor cache of coins she never spent. She thought of her father's last words to her, spoken feverish and half-lucid in the final week: *"When the mountain wakes, little wren, run the ways I taught you, and don't look back."*

She'd assumed it was fever talking. Fever talked a great deal, near the end.

But the mountain had groaned tonight, three pulses deep, like a heart remembering its rhythm. And somewhere far to the north, past the ridgelines and the frozen passes, in villages she'd never visited, she would later learn that a child had knelt by a dry well and prayed to a god three hundred years dead—and the sky had listened.

Oscar blew out the lamp, fed the day's blank page to the stove unread, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the wall, feeling the stone hold its breath.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would climb higher than usual. Tomorrow she would check the northern approaches, the old routes, the places her father's lessons had carved deepest.

Just a survey, she told herself. Just caution. Just the rule, applied as always: *never draw what you cannot afford to lose.*

She fell asleep wondering, for the first time in ten years, whether the thing she couldn't afford to lose was her secret—or her life.
