## 2. Prayers Answered

The god of the well had been dead for three hundred years, and Wren knew it the way she knew her own hands—absolutely, without needing anyone to tell her.

Her grandmother had taught her his name. *Vess of the Deep Water*, she'd said, kneeling Wren down at the well's lip when Wren was small enough that her chin barely cleared the stones. *Older than the village, older than the Crown, older than the mountains themselves. The priests burned him out of their books, but you can't burn a name out of water.* Then she'd made Wren whisper it into the dark below, just once, so it would live somewhere even if nowhere else.

Grandmother was four years in the ground now, and the drought was in its second summer, and Wren knelt at the same lip with her knees aching against sun-hot stone and whispered the name again.

*Vess of the Deep Water.*

It felt foolish. It always felt foolish. The well was down to a cupful of green-scummed water at the bottom, and the barley stood in the fields like an army of skeletons, and the priest, Father Aldous, said from his pulpit every seventh-day that the drought was a test of faith and that praying to anything else was a sin that would crackle nicely on a pyre. He didn't say *pyre*. He said *consequences*. But everyone in Millbrook understood him.

Wren prayed anyway.

She didn't pray like Father Aldous prayed—all standing and booming and telling the sky what it owed them. She prayed the way Grandmother had shown her: quietly, with her palms flat on the warm stone, as if she were pressing her hands against the chest of something sleeping and trying to feel whether it still breathed.

*Please,* she thought, not even saying it aloud anymore because there was no one to hear and the words embarrassed her even alone. *We're not asking for much. Just enough. Just rain. Just so Ma stops crying over the seed-corn she had to eat, and so little Tammy Halloway stops looking gray around the mouth, and so the well doesn't go dry before the solstice. Please. I'll give anything. I don't have anything, but if I did, I'd give it.*

The stone under her palms was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. It was evening, the hour when the mountain shadows came down the valley like spilled ink, and the stone should have been cooling. Instead it warmed beneath her hands, slowly, steadily, until it felt less like rock and more like skin—like the flank of some enormous animal waking beneath the earth.

Wren snatched her hands back.

The warmth stayed. She could feel it through her skirts where her knees pressed the well's rim, a pulse, faint and slow, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. And down in the dark of the well, in that last cupful of scummed water, something *moved*.

Not a reflection. Not a frog. The water itself shifted, turning, though there was no wind to turn it, and for one impossible moment Wren looked down into it and saw not the bottom of the well but *depth*—vast, cold, blue-black depth, the kind of depth that lived in the sea or in dreams—and somewhere far down in that depth, something opened what might have been an eye.

The warmth pulsed once more.

Then it was gone. The stone was cold. The well was a well again, shallow and dying, and Wren sat back on her heels with her heart slamming against her ribs and told herself, very firmly, that she had imagined all of it. That she was tired. That hunger did strange things to a person's head.

But when she walked home through the dusk, past the skeleton barley, she found herself glancing up at the sky. Checking. The way you check on someone you've spoken to.

The clouds had begun to gather over Thornfell.

* * *

It rained that night.

Wren woke to the sound of it—a sound so foreign after two years that for a moment she couldn't name it, lying in the loft listening to the drumming on the thatch above her head, wondering if the roof was falling in. Then understanding arrived, whole and enormous, and she was out of bed and down the ladder and out the door before she'd thought to grab a shawl.

Rain. Real rain, thick and silver in the moonlight, hammering the dust into mud, filling the wheel-ruts, running off the eaves in ropes. She stood in the yard with her face tipped up and her arms out and laughed out loud, alone in the dark, laughing and crying at once because it was *raining*, it was actually raining, and she had asked, and it had—

The laugh died in her throat.

The rain was black.

Not shadow-dark, not night-dark. Black the way ink is black, black the way nothing natural is black, each drop catching the moonlight and giving none of it back. Where it struck the dust it didn't bead and run—it *sank*, vanishing into the ground as if the earth drank it greedily, hungrily. Where it struck her outstretched arm it was cold—not rain-cold but deep-cold, well-cold, the cold of water that has never seen the sun.

And it whispered.

At first Wren thought it was the wind in the chimney, or the river coming back to life in the gorge, or her own pulse in her ears. But the whispering was *in* the rain, in each drop as it fell past her ear, a thin dry voice like a page turning, like breath drawn through teeth, and when she stilled herself and listened—really listened, the way Grandmother had taught her to listen to water—the whispers resolved into words.

Names.

They were names. Dozens of them, hundreds, murmured over and over in voices that overlapped and tangled, some deep as landslide-rumble and some thin as a child's, and none of them were names Wren knew. They sounded old. They sounded *hungry*. One voice near her left ear said a name that bent her thoughts sideways just to hear it, and another, closer, wetter, said a name that made her think of drowning, and a third said—

Wren clapped her hands over her ears.

The whispers kept going. She could feel them through her palms, through the bones of her skull, patient and endless, a congregation reciting a liturgy no living mouth had taught them. And underneath all the strange names, underneath the rumble and the hiss, one voice repeated one word with a persistence that raised the hair on her arms:

*Wren. Wren. Wren.*

She ran inside. She barred the door, which was silly, because rain doesn't use doors, and she lay awake in the loft until dawn with the blankets over her head, listening to the black rain whisper her name against the thatch, over and over, gentle as a mother calling a child home.

By morning, the clouds had broken apart, and the sun rose on a village that glistened and steamed and, inch by inch, began to scream.

* * *

They noticed the crops first.

The barley hadn't drowned. That was the terrible thing, the thing Wren understood only later, standing in the crowd at the edge of the Halloways' field. The rain had been exactly what they'd prayed for—enough, plenty, a soaking gift—and the barley had drunk it and straightened overnight, green shooting up through the pale stalks so fast you could almost watch it happen. By noon the field looked like a painting of health. By the next morning it looked like a painting of something else.

Because the barley had come up *black*.

Not withered, not blighted—healthy, glossy, vigorous, each stalk standing tall and heavy-headed, and every head of grain as black as the rain that fed it. When Tammy Halloway's father threshed a handful and poured the grains out into his palm, they gleamed like beetle shells. When he tasted one, he spat for a full minute and then sat down hard in the dirt and wouldn't say what it tasted like, except that his wife kept asking and finally he said, in a voice like a man describing a nightmare, "*names*," and nobody asked him again.

The well was worse.

The rain had refilled it—brimming, overflowing, the first time in two years the bucket went down and came up dripping. But the water came up dark, dark as tea left too long, and it smelled of cold stone and something underneath that, something mineral and alive, and when Mother drew a bucket at dawn and set it on the step, the surface of the water shivered though the morning was windless, and rippled outward in rings, in patterns, in shapes that might have been letters in a script no one read anymore.

Father Aldous took one look at the well and went white as whey.

"It's the old one," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the sentence, which frightened Wren more than the black water did, because Father Aldous had never in his life been afraid of anything except unpaid tithes. "Vess. The drowned god. This is his doing. This is—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and looked out across the square at the villagers gathered there, at their faces turned up to him hungry for an explanation, and Wren watched him choose, right in front of her, to be afraid of the Crown instead of the god. "This is witchcraft," he said. "Someone has called on the forbidden powers. Someone has *summoned* this."

The square went quiet in the way a forest goes quiet.

And then, one by one, in ones and twos and then in a slow tide, they all turned and looked at Wren.

She didn't understand it at first. She stood there with her hand in her mother's grip and her heart going like a rabbit's, and she thought, absurdly, *they're looking behind me*, because surely they weren't looking at *her*, surely eleven years of being the quiet Halloway-neighbor girl who mended nets and fetched water and never said a word above a murmur hadn't earned her this. But they weren't looking behind her. They were looking at her, and their faces were doing something she had never seen faces do before, something that made her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

They were looking at her the way you look at a snake in the woodpile. Carefully. From a distance. Recalculating everything you thought you knew.

"She was at the well," someone said. Old Marta, whose memory was going but whose grudges never did. "Evening before the rain. I saw her there. Kneeling. Whispering."

"I saw her too," said Joss Tanner, and he wouldn't meet Wren's eyes, which somehow was worse than meeting them. "Been seeing her there for weeks. Every evening. Talking to it."

"Not talking," Marta said. "*Praying.*"

"Praying to *what*, though?" That was Tanner's wife, and her voice had a tremor in it that wasn't fear, quite. It was something hungrier. "To what does a child pray, that the sky answers?"

Wren opened her mouth. Nothing came out. Her mother's hand tightened around hers hard enough to hurt, and her mother stepped half in front of her, shoulders squared, and said, "She prays like any Christian girl prays, you superstitious—"

"To Vess."

The word dropped into the square like a stone into still water, and every ripple that spread out from it touched Wren. It was Tammy Halloway who'd said it—Tammy, seven years old, gap-toothed, who used to follow Wren around begging for stories about the mountain. Tammy stood by her father's knee with her thumb in her mouth, and she said it again, louder, with the terrible clarity children have: "Wren talks to Vess. Grandma said so. Grandma said Wren knows his name."

Grandmother. Dead four years, and still handing knives to neighbors.

"Wren." Father Aldous came toward her across the square, and the crowd parted for him, and his face had arranged itself into its pulpit-expression, stern and sorrowful, sorrowful and stern. "Child. Tell us it isn't so. Tell us you haven't been trafficking with the drowned one. Tell us, and be forgiven."

And here was the moment, Wren would think later, lying sleepless in whatever bed or barn or ditch her life came to next. Here was the moment where she could have lied. She was good at silence; she could have been good at lies. Say *no*, say *I pray to the Ascended, same as everyone*, and maybe they'd believe her, maybe the black barley would fade and the well-water run clear and the whole terrible week fold itself up and blow away like chaff.

But the rain had whispered her name. All night, gently, patiently, the way you'd call someone you loved. And whatever slept under the well had opened one eye and looked at her, and she had looked back, and some stubborn, doomed, Grandmother-given honesty in her chest refused to call that nothing.

"I prayed for rain," Wren said. Her voice came out small and steady, which surprised her. "We needed rain. So I prayed. To whoever was listening."

The square was silent.

Then Father Aldous sighed, long and theatrical, the sigh of a man burdened beyond bearing, and said, "Take her to the shrine house," and the tide of villagers came forward, and Wren's mother screamed.

* * *

They locked her in the shrine house—not the church, which was Crown-built and whitewashed, but the old shrine house at the village's edge, the round stone building that had been there before the church, before the village, maybe, with its single window slit and its floor worn smooth by knees that had knelt there centuries before Father Aldous's God had a name. They meant it as a prison. Wren suspected it had been built as something else entirely, and that the smooth-worn floor remembered other prayers, older ones, and perhaps that was why, sitting in the dark with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, she felt less afraid in there than she had in the square.

Outside, the argument went on. She could hear her mother's voice rising and cracking, and Father Aldous's rising over it, and the lower murmur of the crowd, and once, distinctly, the word *Inquisitors*, spoken in the hushed tone people used for plague and tax-collectors, and her blood went cold, because everyone knew what happened to villages that reported witches and everyone knew what happened to villages that *didn't*.

Inside, the air smelled of rain and old stone.

And the water came to her.

It seeped up between the flagstones first—impossible, the shrine house stood on high ground, the well was a hundred yards off—but there it was, a thin dark gleam spreading across the smooth-worn floor, gathering in the hollows, finding the channels that generations of kneeling worshippers had polished into the stone. It moved with purpose. It pooled in front of her, a puddle the size of a serving plate, black and still, and in its surface she could see not the ceiling beams above her but depth again, that vast cold blue-black depth, and far down in it, the shape that might have been an eye.

*You called,* the water said, without sound, the words arriving directly behind her eyes like memories that weren't hers. The voice was layered, many-voiced, the congregation from the rain folded into one throat. *Little one. You called, and we answered. Why do they put you in a stone box?*

"You're dead," Wren whispered. Her voice shook. She made herself keep looking into the pool, because Grandmother had said *never look away from water that looks at you*, though she'd never said why, and now Wren suspected she'd never learn why. "Father Aldous says you're dead. Everyone says the gods are dead. Three hundred years."

A sound moved through the water that might have been laughter, if laughter could be sad.

*Three hundred years is a long nap,* the water said. *Not death. Death is different. Death is quieter. Something has cracked open, little one, something above us, and through the crack the old names are leaking back into the world. We are weak. We are starving. We are remembering ourselves one whisper at a time.* The eye-shape drifted closer to the surface, and the cold coming off the pool made Wren's breath fog. *You gave us your need, and your need was honest, and honest need is food and drink to the starved. So we answered. Forgive us the manner of it. We have forgotten how to be gentle.*

"The barley," Wren said. "The rain. The—the names. People are frightened."

*People should be frightened,* the water said, with terrible mildness. *Frightened is how the living stay living. But hear me, little caller, because I am old and my time in your world may be short.* The pool trembled; the eye pressed nearer the surface; the whispering congregation of voices all leaned in at once, and Wren felt them like a wind through her hair. *Others will wake too. Others who slept longer, and hungrier, and meaner. What I ask of you is small: remember me kindly. Speak my name to the water sometimes, so I do not fade back down. And when the burning comes—and it will come, they always burn—run. Run uphill. Run toward the mountains, toward Thornfell, and keep running until you find the one who maps the ways that aren't on maps.*

Wren's mouth went dry. "What one? Who—"

*The heir,* the water said, and the word rang through the shrine house like a bell through fog, and every flagstone in the floor seemed to hum with it. *The ember keeps what the ember binds. Tell no one else. Promise me.*

"I promise," Wren whispered, because there was nothing else to say to a god, even a drowned one, even a starving one.

The pool shivered. The eye sank. And as the black water drained away between the flagstones—backwards, upward, flowing against everything water ought to do—its last whisper settled against her ear like a cold kiss:

*They're coming for you at dawn, little one. Don't let them brand you before you run.*

* * *

They came at dawn, as promised.

Wren heard them from the loft of the shrine house—she'd climbed up among the rafters rather than sleep on the cursed-smooth floor, though sleep hadn't come regardless. Torches, boots, Father Aldous's voice organizing men the way he organized the harvest-tithe. Through the window slit she watched them cross the misty square: six men with staves and rope, and behind them Father Aldous in his vestments carrying the iron brazier, and her mother being held back by two women who were, Wren noted distantly, the same women who'd wept at Grandmother's funeral.

Brand her. The god had known. Had *seen* it, maybe, the way water sees everything that falls into it.

There was no way out of the shrine house. One door, one window slit too narrow for a cat. Wren pressed her back against the cold stone and listened to the boots stop outside the door and thought, with a strange floating calm, *run uphill, run toward the mountains, keep running* —and understood, with the same calm, that she was about to fail her very first promise.

The door groaned open. Torchlight spilled across the worn floor, across the place where the black water had been, and Father Aldous stood silhouetted in it with the brazier glowing at his feet and sorrow carved into his face like a decoration.

"Come down, child," he said. "It will only hurt for a moment, and then you'll be clean."

Wren looked at him. She looked past him, at the men with the rope, at the gray dawn sky above the rooftops, at the far white shoulder of Thornfell rising over the valley with the sun kindling along its ridge like a line of fire.

And the brand on her wrist—though no iron had touched her yet, though she was still unmarked, unburnt, untouched—began, softly, patiently, to glow.
