## 3. A Stranger on the Salt Road

The rule was simple, and Oscar Dravenmoor had built her whole life inside it like a bird building a cage one twig at a time: *see nothing, be nothing, owe no one.*

She repeated it to herself now, standing in the wind on the Salt Road pass with a dying girl at her feet, because the rule had just begun to crack and she wanted very much to know why.

* * *

Three days after the tremor, the mountain still hadn't settled. Oscar felt it in her teeth when she bit down, a faint wrongness in the bedrock, as though Thornfell were a sleeping animal that had dreamed something unpleasant and couldn't quite return to sleep. The merchants in Lowmark talked of nothing else—landslips on the eastern face, wells gone cloudy, a shepherd's flock that refused to graze above the treeline. Oscar listened, nodded, sold them maps of roads that were safe and roads that weren't, and said nothing about the way her ink had behaved the night of the shaking.

She'd left a fresh pot of walnut-ink open on her desk. When she woke, it had dried into a shape she hadn't drawn: a spiral, tight and inward-turning, like water going down a drain. She'd scraped it off with a knife and told herself that temperature changes did strange things to pigment.

That was the trouble with a double life. You became an expert at telling yourself stories.

So she'd taken the high road out of Lowmark that morning—the Salt Road, the old caravan route that switchbacked up through the grey shoulders of the mountains toward the passes no merchant used anymore—for reasons she could justify to herself if she tried. Surveying. The tremor might have opened new routes or closed old ones; a cartographer who didn't check her terrain was a liar with better penmanship. That was the story. The truth was simpler and less comfortable: she needed to walk where no one could see her, needed the thin cold air and the silence, needed to feel the mountain under her boots and pretend it wasn't whispering.

The Salt Road earned its name from the white crust of mineral bloom that furred its stones in dry years, and today it wore that shroud fully, the whole pass dusted pale as bone. Wind came down off the peaks carrying the smell of snow and something else—something scorched and sweet, like sugar burnt in a pan. Oscar catalogued it without meaning to. Old habit. A mapmaker's mind filed everything: *scorched-sweet smell, source unknown, elevation roughly nine thousand feet, note for future reference.*

Then she rounded the shoulder of the pass below the Widow's Tooth, and the smell resolved into blood.

The girl lay crumpled against a fallen slab of granite like a doll dropped by a careless child. Young—young, thirteen at most, all elbows and hollows, dark hair matted with frost and dirt. Her clothes were village-made, homespun wool gone grey with wear, torn at the knee and shoulder. One boot was missing. Frost had gotten into her exposed skin and painted it in ugly white-and-black patches, and there was blood—a lot of it—frozen in a fan across the stone beneath her, though Oscar's eye went first to the tracks: small footprints in the salt-bloom coming up from the south, staggering, uneven, a drunkard's line drawn by someone too exhausted to walk straight. And behind those prints, higher up the pass, other prints. Booted. Evenly spaced. Deliberate. They came down the trail, circled the place where the girl had fallen—

—and turned back. Whoever had been following her had stopped. Had looked at her lying there. And had walked away, leaving her to the mountain.

Oscar stood very still, and the rule rose up in her like a wall: *see nothing, be nothing, owe no one.* A dead girl on a pass was a problem. A living girl on a pass was a catastrophe. Inquisitors followed survivors the way wolves followed blood. Every instinct she'd honed over ten years of careful invisibility said *walk away, mark the location, forget the coordinates.*

Her feet didn't move. That was the strange part. Ten years of discipline, and her feet simply declined to obey.

"Damn it," she said quietly, to the wind, to the mountain, to whatever had dried into a spiral in her inkpot. "Damn it."

She knelt. The girl's lips were blue-grey, her breathing so shallow the frost on her breath barely formed. But alive. Alive, and burning up with fever even as the cold took her extremities, which made no sense at all until Oscar peeled back the ruined collar of the girl's dress to check for wounds and saw the brand.

It sat over her heart, or where a heart would be in a drawing—a mark the size of a coin, seared into the skin in the shape of a flame, or an ember rather: not the clean teardrop of fire but something coiled and glowing, rendered in colors that had no business existing in scar tissue. Red, yes, but underneath the red a deep orange-gold that seemed to shift when Oscar wasn't looking directly at it, the way embers breathe. The skin around it wasn't merely burned. It was *lit*, faintly, from within, and the edges of the wound were not closed and not open—there was no scab, no healing, no festering either. Just a wound held permanently at the moment of its making, three days old and three hours old and three years old all at once.

Oscar had spent a decade among smugglers' secrets and pre-Covenant maps. She knew every symbol her father's generation had used to mark dangerous ground, sealed places, things best forgotten. She had never seen this. And yet some wordless part of her, some inheritance older than her training, looked at the ember-brand and whispered a single word she didn't understand and couldn't repeat aloud.

The girl's eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, human, and full of a terror so complete it had passed through fear and come out the other side into a terrible calm.

"You see it," the girl rasped. Her voice was wrecked, days of screaming or days of silence, hard to say which. "Everyone sees it. That's why they—" A cough racked her, wet and awful. "They called me witch. My own mother crossed the street. I prayed for rain and it rained black and it *whispered*, and then they—" Her eyes fixed on Oscar's face, searching. "Are you going to finish what they started?"

And there it was. The whole terrible arithmetic laid out between them on the frozen stone. Finish what they started. The booted footprints that had circled her and turned away. The village that had driven a child into the high passes in early winter with one boot.

Oscar should have said yes. Should have said *I'm no healer, I can't help you,* and walked down the mountain and locked her door and told herself stories until the stories held. Instead she heard herself say, in a voice flat with self-disgust:

"Hold still. If you puke on me, you're walking the rest of the way yourself."

* * *

Getting her down the pass was two hours of the hardest work Oscar had done in years. The girl—Wren, she said, just Wren, no family name offered and Oscar didn't press—could stagger a few dozen steps at a time before her legs quit, and each halt cost them body heat neither of them had. Oscar gave her her cloak, her gloves, half her provisions, and a shoulder to lean on, and kept up a low stream of curses the entire way, mostly at herself.

Every hundred yards, the rule whispered its arithmetic. *You are leading a god-touched fugitive directly past the approaches to your own territory. Every person who sees you together is a thread that leads back to your door. She is a lit candle and you have spent ten years learning to be smoke.*

And every hundred yards, Wren's weight leaned a little heavier on her shoulder, and the ember-brand glowed through the borrowed cloak like a coal wrapped in cloth, and Oscar kept walking.

"Why?" Wren asked once, during a rest stop huddled in the lee of a cairn, her teeth chattering so hard the word came out in pieces. "Why help me? Everyone else—"

"I don't know," Oscar said, and was surprised to find it was true. She'd prepared several lies—*I'm a healer, I'm going that way anyway, the Church pays for strays*—and discarded them all, because this freezing child had been lied to enough by people who claimed to love her. "I had rules. You're making me break all of them. I'm trying not to think about it too hard."

Wren almost smiled. It was a small, cracked thing, but it was the first thing on her face that wasn't terror, and Oscar found, to her considerable irritation, that she'd have climbed another pass to see it again.

"The people who followed me," Wren said suddenly, as they started moving again. "Soldiers. Crown men, with a gold ribbon on their coats. They caught up to me two days ago, near the shrine at Vess's Ford." She stumbled; Oscar steadied her. "One of them touched my brand. Just touched it, through my dress. He screamed. Like it burned him right through his glove. Then their captain looked at me for a long time and said—" her voice dropped into a rough imitation, flat and cold, "'Let the mountain have her.' And they turned around and left."

Oscar's blood went colder than the wind. Crown soldiers, retreating from a child. Leaving her deliberately to die rather than touching her again. Which meant somewhere in the command structure of the realm, someone knew exactly what this brand was—and feared it.

"They'll send others," Oscar said. "Men who don't touch."

"How do you know?"

"Because that's what I'd do." She said it before she could stop herself, and Wren looked at her with those too-old eyes, and Oscar realized how it sounded. "I mean—that's how hunters think. Anyone who hunts thinks that way." Smooth, Dravenmoor. Very smooth.

But Wren only nodded, accepting it the way the desperate accept anything, and let herself be steered down the last miles of the pass as the light failed and the scorched-sweet smell came again on the wind, stronger now, from the east, from the direction of the deep ranges where no one lived and no map Oscar had ever sold marked anything at all.

* * *

She took Wren not to Lowmark—never to Lowmark—but to the waystation, the abandoned survey hut in the fold of the mountain where she kept the second life, the true one: the archive of pre-Covenant maps, her father's instruments, the locked chest of things that would get a person flayed if the wrong eyes saw them. It was a violation of every protocol she'd built. Bringing an outsider here was the single most reckless act of her decade of careful living, and she did it anyway, because the alternative was leaving a feverish child alone in the dark with a wound that wouldn't heal and soldiers who'd already decided she should die.

The hut was low-ceilinged and smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and Wren looked around it with the dazed wonder of someone who'd expected a grave and found a home instead. Oscar got a fire going, forced broth and willow-bark tea into her, and finally, with the girl sleeping in a nest of blankets by the hearth, sat down across from her and allowed herself, for the first time in three days, to actually look at the problem.

The brand hadn't changed. That was the horror of it. Fever came and went, frostbite faded to red, exhaustion surrendered to sleep—but the ember-mark over Wren's heart remained precisely, perfectly, unnaturally itself. Held. Suspended. As if time had been instructed to leave it alone.

Oscar fetched her father's journal from the locked chest—not to read, she told herself, just to hold, just to feel its weight while she thought—and sat turning it over in her hands. The leather was worn soft at the corners where his thumbs had rested. She hadn't opened it properly in months. Tonight, with a branded child breathing raggedly six feet away and Crown soldiers somewhere behind them on the pass, the journal seemed less like a relic and more like a question she'd been avoiding for ten years.

*What did you know, Da? What did you hide in these pages?*

Outside, the wind changed. The scorched-sweet smell flooded through the gaps in the shutters, thick enough to taste, and by the hearth Wren cried out in her sleep—a small, bitten-off sound—and the brand flared. Not metaphorically. Flared, throwing ember-light across the ceiling of the waystation, bright enough that Oscar saw her own shadow leap huge against the far wall, and in the glow she could have sworn the light bent toward her, toward the journal in her hands, like iron filings finding a lodestone.

Then it guttered out, and Wren whimpered, and the wind moved on, and Oscar sat alone in the dark with her father's book and the growing, unwelcome certainty that the tremor in the mountain, the spiral in her inkpot, the black rain the girl had prayed down—all of it was one thing, one vast slow thing beginning to wake, and that she, Oscar Dravenmoor, mapmaker of no particular importance, was standing directly in its path.

She should send the girl away at first light. Give her food, a map of safe roads, directions to Duskhollow and Mother Quill's dubious mercy, and wash her hands of the whole affair. That was the rule. That was survival.

Oscar looked at the sleeping child, at the faint coal-glow pulsing under the blankets with each breath, steady as a heartbeat.

"First light," she said aloud, to no one, knowing even as she said it that she was lying, and that the mountain, which had started collecting her lies, would remember this one too.

Far to the east, beyond the shuttered window, something answered—a pulse of heat on the wind, brief and patient, like an eye opening.
