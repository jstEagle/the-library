## 4. The Gilded Inquisitor

The rain had followed Seraphine Locke for three days, and she had begun to suspect it was not entirely natural.

It fell now in fine grey sheets over the border town of Marrow's Rest, beading on the gilded pauldrons of her armor without ever quite wetting them—a small vanity of consecrated oil and blessing that her superiors called practical and she privately called a mercy. A dry Inquisitor was a patient Inquisitor. And patience, today, would matter more than fire.

She sat her white courser at the head of the column and let the town come to her, as towns always did. First the dogs, slinking along the fence lines with their ears flat. Then the children, peering from doorways with the particular hunger of the young for spectacle. Then the men—farmers mostly, and one fat factor with ink-stained fingers who had ridden out to meet them an hour past and now hovered at the edge of the square like a moth unsure whether the lantern would warm or consume him.

"Your Holiness." The factor bowed too deeply. "Marrow's Rest is honored. We hadn't word of your coming."

"No," Seraphine said. "You wouldn't have."

She dismounted without assistance, though the squire scrambled anyway, and stood a moment in the mud of the main square letting the town look at her. She knew what they saw: a woman of perhaps forty years, tall, grey-eyed, her hair bound back beneath a hood of white silk shot through with gold thread—the vestments of the Order of the Unblinking Eye. The gilding on her armor caught what little light the day offered and threw it back in dull coins. They saw authority. They saw the Church made flesh.

What they did not see—and this was the point, this had always been the point—was the ledger behind her eyes, already filling with names.

"The shrine," she said. "Take me to it."

* * *

It stood at the town's western edge, where the fields gave up and the foothills began: a low stone structure older than any building in Marrow's Rest, older than the Church itself if the masons' marks were honest, which they rarely were. A country shrine. One of thousands scattered across the frontier like seeds no one remembered planting. The locals called its patron the Shepherd Below, or sometimes just the Old Man of the Hill, and left him offerings of bread and wool and, judging by the state of the threshold, the occasional chicken.

Seraphine circled it slowly while her soldiers waited at a respectful distance, their torches hissing under the rain-covers.

Inside, the god's icon was crude—a carved figure whose face time had worn into featurelessness, so that it looked less like a deity than like everyone you had ever failed to remember. Offerings crowded the alcove: bread gone green, a child's knitted mitten, coins of three defunct kingdoms. Someone had left a jar of honey, sealed with wax and a pressed flower. Someone had written a prayer on birchbark in a careful child's hand and tucked it beneath the icon's foot.

Seraphine read it.

*Please let my brother's cough get better. I will give you my blue ribbon.*

She folded it once, precisely, and put it in her coat pocket.

"Holiness?" Captain Vess had come up beside her, rain streaming off his helm. He was a good soldier, Vess—dull enough to follow orders and honest enough to hate some of them. "Is there... something here? Signs of the touched?"

"There is always something here," Seraphine said. "That is rather the difficulty."

She stepped out into the rain and looked up at the hills beyond, where Thornfell's shoulders rose grey and immense against the cloud. Three hundred years ago, her order's founders had done the great work: the Sundering's aftermath swept clean, the covenant sealed, the gods bound behind the veil where their hungers could never again wear human faces. The Church taught it as salvation. The Church was correct to teach it as salvation. Seraphine had read the accounts of the Godswars—had read them more closely than any living member of the hierarchy, she suspected—and she knew what walked the world before the binding. Things that answered prayers. Things that loved. Things that ate.

The veil was thinning. The reports had been arriving for months now, each more implausible than the last, and her superiors had responded the way bureaucracies always respond to apocalypse: with committees. Seraphine had been dispatched instead—with a warrant broad enough to drive a cart through, a fragment of the old covenant seal in a lead-lined case against her heart, and instructions that amounted to *discover the truth, but do not embarrass us discovering it.*

She meant to do considerably more than that.

"Burn it," she said.

Vess went still. Rain ticked against his helm. "Holiness?"

"The shrine. Burn it."

"It's a country shrine, Holiness. Half these people can't read. It's—" He stopped himself, which she appreciated. He had been about to say *harmless*, and they both knew what happened to men who finished sentences like that in her presence.

"It is a door," Seraphine said, "and doors are only harmless until something comes through them." She turned to face him fully, and let him see—not the anger, because she felt none, but the certainty, which was worse. "Captain, three weeks ago a village two days north of here received rain after a child prayed for it. Black rain. Rain that spoke. Do you imagine the Old Man of the Hill did not hear about it? These shrines are roots of the same tree. When one wakes, they all begin to stir. I am not punishing these people. I am amputating before the rot spreads."

"And the offerings? The folk who tend it?"

"They will grieve," Seraphine said. "Grief is survivable. What came out of the Godswars was not." She raised her voice. "Kindling. Now."

They burned it well, her soldiers; they were practiced. Oil first, then flame, and the wet wood fought them for a while before surrendering with a crack like a bone breaking. The townsfolk gathered at a distance and watched with the expressions of people watching a house fire on someone else's street—horror and relief braided together, gratitude that it wasn't theirs, shame at the gratitude. An old woman wept without noise. A boy of maybe ten stared at Seraphine with a hatred so pure and so total that she made a note of his face out of professional habit, then dismissed him. Children hated her everywhere. It was practically a rite of passage for the office.

The icon itself resisted longest. Even as the flames took the alcove, the carved face seemed to hold its blankness, its terrible everyman patience, and Seraphine found herself standing closer than safety recommended, watching it burn, waiting—

For what? For it to scream? To speak?

It did neither. But as the roof beam collapsed inward and sparks climbed the rain in a black column, she felt it—that faint pressure against her senses, like a hand laid flat against a door from the other side. Not anger. Worse. *Attention.* Something far away and very old had noticed the fire, and noticed *her*, and filed her away somewhere in its vast diminished memory.

Good, she thought. Let them notice. Let them learn that the door swings both ways.

"Holiness." Vess again, quieter now, subdued by the burning as men often were. "The factor says—there's been talk. A girl. Up-mountain villages, they're saying a girl called down the rain. Wren, they call her. The factor thought you'd want—"

"I want everything," Seraphine said. "Names, ages, the village, who sheltered her, who denounced her. Bring me the factor."

The factor came at a trot, sweating despite the cold, and gave her everything he had, which was little and secondhand and stank of gossip: a girl of eleven or twelve, a shepherd's daughter, prayers answered too well, the village turned her out, some said her own family drove her into the pass with stones, some said worse. No one knew where she'd gone. Into the Thornfells, most likely. Into the high passes, where the weather killed the unprepared and the prepared alike.

"A god-touched child loose in the mountains," Seraphine said softly. "Alone. Frightened. Praying to whatever will listen."

The factor blinked. "Holiness?"

"Do you know what frightened children are, master factor?" She did not wait for an answer. "They are loud. They pray loudly, and desperately, and constantly, and every prayer is a knock on a door that should have stayed shut. That child is not a heretic. She is a beacon." She looked back at the burning shrine, at the black column of smoke bending northward into the hills. "And beacons draw things. Both kinds."

* * *

That night, quartered in the factor's best room with the shutters rattling, Seraphine opened the case.

It was a modest thing, the fragment—no larger than a coin, a shard of some substance that was neither metal nor stone nor glass, warm to the touch in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. The old covenant seal, broken into seven pieces when the Dravenmoors completed their great work, distributed among the founding houses of the Church so that no one hand could ever reforge it—or so the official history ran. Seraphine had long suspected the official history was a comfort rather than a record. Seals were not broken for safekeeping. Seals were broken because breaking them served someone.

Against her palm, the fragment pulsed, slow as a sleeping heart.

She had carried it for eleven years, since the day the Grand Provost placed it in her hands and said, *You will know when it wakes.* Everyone assumed he meant it would grow hot, or hum, or shine. Seraphine had understood him differently. You would know when it woke the way you know a sleeper has woken in the next room—not by any sign from the thing itself, but by the silence changing quality around it.

Tonight, for the first time, the silence had changed.

She held the fragment up to the candlelight and watched the shadows it cast—wrong shadows, shadows that moved a half-beat behind her hand, as though reluctant to obey. Somewhere out in the dark, in the high passes of the Thornfells, a child was praying. Somewhere, a dead god was answering. Somewhere, the seal her order had guarded for three centuries was remembering that it had once been whole.

And somewhere—this was the thought she saved for last, the thought she kept like a blade kept sharp—somewhere there might still be blood. Covenant-keeper blood. The Dravenmoors were recorded extinct, of course; the Church had seen to the recording personally, and the Church was thorough in its records the way arsonists are thorough with their matches. But extinction, in Seraphine's experience, was less a fact than a claim, and claims required verification.

She wrapped the fragment away, snuffed the candle, and lay down armored except for her gauntlets, as she did every night, listening to the rain.

It had stopped whispering. That was the thing she would not write in her report. For three days the rain had murmured at the edges of her hearing—names, she was almost certain, names in no living tongue—and tonight, over Marrow's Rest, it had gone quiet, as though whatever spoke through it had found what it was looking for.

Or as though it had moved on. Northward. Into the mountains.

Seraphine closed her eyes and composed tomorrow's orders in her head: ride at dawn, take the north road, question every priest, peddler, and smuggler between here and the high passes. Somewhere ahead of her was a frightened girl with a brand that would not heal. And somewhere beyond the girl, patient as a buried coal, was the bloodline her Church had sworn was ash.

*Let them all come out of hiding,* she thought, on the edge of sleep. *The gods, the heirs, the liars. I have fire enough for every one of them, and a world worth saving requires exactly that much burning.*

Outside, the last smoke of the Shepherd Below's shrine drifted north over the peaks, a grey thread pointing the way—though whether it marked her path or warned the mountains of her coming, Seraphine Locke could not have said, and did not care to guess.
