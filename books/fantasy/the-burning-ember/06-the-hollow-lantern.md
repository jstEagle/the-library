## 6. The Hollow Lantern

The lantern above the bar had gone hollow years ago.

Cassian Vale noticed such things now. He had once been a man who noticed nothing but the shape of his own devotion—the clean geometry of prayer, the architecture of liturgy, the way candlelight fell across an altar like a benediction. He had been a priest of the Church of the Sundered Veil for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once looked up at the lamps and wondered what burned inside them. Now he looked at everything. Now he looked at everything because looking was all that was left to him, and because the things he saw were always, somehow, wrong. The lantern's glass was smoked black on one side where the flame had licked too long against it, and its oil reservoir held perhaps a finger's width of fuel, and still someone had hung it there over the bar of the Drowned Meridian as if light were a thing you could promise forever and never refill.

He sat at the end of the bar with his back to the wall, which was habit rather than caution—no one in Duskhollow troubled themselves to rob a drunk priest, and no one in Duskhollow believed he was a priest anymore anyway, which was the same thing as being safe. His cup was half-empty and had been half-empty for an hour, because drinking was another thing he did badly now, another rite he could not complete. He would lift the cup. He would smell the rotgut brandy they distilled somewhere in the warrens below the harbor. He would remember that his father had died with this same smell on his breath, and that Cassian had sworn at fourteen, kneeling at the graveside with his mother's hand heavy on his shoulder, that he would never be a man who needed anything so much as he needed to be needed by God.

Then he would drink anyway. Slowly. Like penance.

The Drowned Meridian smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular staleness of air that had been breathed too many times by too many people who had nowhere else to breathe. It was built into the cliffside below the smuggler town, its windows facing not the sea but the stone, so that the only light came from lanterns and from the great iron stove in the corner, around which the evening's regulars arranged themselves like debris after a flood. A one-eyed pilot named Brannoc was arguing with a fishmonger about tide-tables. Two women—sisters, or something closer than sisters—sat in the corner with their heads bent over documents, forging bills of lading with the serene concentration of monks illuminating scripture. And behind the bar, Mother Quill—a title she had awarded herself, no one knew when, and no one dared dispute—polished glasses that did not need polishing and watched her den with the flat, patient eyes of a woman who had buried more friends than she could count and intended to outlive the rest.

"You're dripping," Mother Quill said, without looking at him.

Cassian touched his chin. Condensation from his cup. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to my bar." But there was no edge in it. There never was, for him. That was the cruelest kindness anyone showed him these days: they had stopped expecting anything of him, and so they had stopped being disappointed, and so they were gentle, the way one is gentle with a lame dog.

He turned the cup in his hands. On its base, worn nearly smooth, was the stamp of the seminary at Vaelmoor—his seminary, once, a lifetime of prayers ago. He had stolen the cup on his last night there. Or not stolen; taken, the way a man takes a splinter from his own flesh. Eleven years he had served. Eleven years of offices and oblations, of the Litany of the Veil sung at dawn and dusk, of hearing confessions from farmers whose sins were small and sorrowful and real. And on the twelfth year, in the autumn of the great drought, he had done the one thing a priest must never do.

He had prayed for himself.

* * *

It had been a girl first. That was how he remembered it, though memory had a liar's tongue. A girl from the river villages, nine years old, brought to his chapel coughing blood into a scrap of linen her mother clutched like a talisman. The fever-flux, the physicians called it, and shrugged, and charged fees. The mother had come to him because the physicians had failed and because the desperate will try anything, even a god three centuries dead, even a Church that had long since stopped pretending its prayers were answered by anything but the discipline of the men who spoke them.

Cassian had prayed. He had knelt beside the girl's pallet in the chapel of Saint Verene-the-Forgotten—for that was the joke of his parish, that its patron saint had been struck from the rolls before his grandmother was born—and he had prayed with everything in him. Not the rote offices. Not the measured cadences of the Litany. He had prayed the way he had prayed as a boy, face-down on the flagstones, fists clenched, bargaining and begging and finally simply *asking*, the naked wordless ask of a child who believes someone is listening.

And the girl had lived.

That was the horror of it, the part he had never told anyone, the part that had unraveled him thread by thread through the following weeks: the girl had lived, and the physicians called it a remission, and the mother called it a miracle, and Cassian had stood in his empty chapel at midnight and understood, with a certainty that felt like falling, that it was neither. Something had heard him. Something old and vast and *other* had turned its attention through the cracked veil of the world and looked upon a dying child in a river village, and had decided—or been moved, or been bargained with; he would never know which—to let her stay.

He should have reported it. That was the doctrine, drilled into every seminarian: manifestations are to be documented, sealed, and surrendered to the Office of Inquisition, for unbound divinity is a fire that consumes the house it warms. He had written the report. He had sealed it with wax and the signet of his office. He had carried it to the courier's post himself.

And then he had stood outside the post for two hours in the rain, and he had thought about the girl laughing again, and he had burned the report in the chapel brazier, and he had told himself it was mercy.

It was not mercy. He knew that now. It was pride. It was the intoxication of having been *heard*—of having spent eleven years speaking into a silence he had been trained to call faith, and then, one night, hearing the silence answer. He wanted it again. Gods forgive him, he had wanted it again, and so he had prayed, alone, at his own altar, for the smallest and most selfish of intentions.

He had prayed for confirmation. For a sign. For God—any god, the dead ones, the sundered ones, whatever waited behind the veil—to speak to *him*, to tell him he had not wasted his life addressing an empty room.

The night of the solstice. He remembered the cold coming up through the floor. He remembered the candles guttering in a wind that did not exist. He remembered kneeling until his knees went numb and then numbness spreading upward like frost, filling him, until he was a hollow lantern with no flame inside.

Silence.

Not the ordinary silence of an indifferent universe—that silence he could have borne, had borne, had built a career upon. This was different. This was the silence of a room full of people who have all agreed not to look at you. This was silence with *attention* in it, silence leaning toward him, silence that knew his name and declined to say it. Whatever had answered for the girl had heard him too. And it had chosen not to answer.

Or—it had been forbidden. Or it had been feeding elsewhere. Or it had looked into Cassian Vale, priest of the Sundered Veil, and found nothing worth the cost of a word.

He never learned which. He resigned his orders within the month—walked out of the chapel, left his vestments folded on the altar like a shed skin, and walked down out of the hill country until he reached the coast, and kept walking until he reached Duskhollow, where a woman called Mother Quill looked at him for a long moment and said, "You'll do. You can carry crates and keep your mouth shut, and if you ever preach in my den I'll throw you into the harbor."

He had kept his mouth shut for four years.

* * *

"Vale." Mother Quill's voice cut across the den's murmur, and Cassian surfaced from his memories like a drowning man breaking water. She was looking past him, toward the door, and her eyes had changed—not alarmed, exactly. Mother Quill did not alarm. But sharpened, the way a blade sharpens. "We've got company."

The door swung open on a gust of cold mountain air and a figure wrapped in a travel-stained cloak, hood back, and Cassian's first thought was that he had never seen anyone look so tired and so determined at the same time. A woman, mid-thirties perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and the wind-scoured complexion of someone who lived high and traveled hard. She carried a leather map-case slung across her body like a weapon, and she moved like a soldier moving through territory she knew but did not trust.

Behind her, half-hidden in the folds of her cloak, was a child. Small, dark-haired, maybe ten years old, with the grey-faced exhaustion of someone who had walked too far on too little. The child's right hand was wrapped in bandages, and through the wrappings, faintly, as if lit from beneath the skin, glowed a dull orange light.

Cassian set down his cup.

He told himself it meant nothing. Brands festered; festering wounds could glow with infection's heat; he had seen fevers do stranger things. He told himself that while Mother Quill came around the bar and exchanged low words with the woman—Oscar, someone said, Oscar Draven-something, the cartographer, the map-witch of Thornfell—and while the child was steered to the warmth of the stove and given soup she stared at as if soup might be a trap. He told himself that while the woman unrolled a map on the bar top to show Mother Quill some route through the high passes, and Cassian, despite four years of practiced indifference, leaned forward despite himself, because the map was *beautiful*, drawn in a fine sure hand with elevation lines like fingerprints of the earth itself.

And there, in the lower margin, in tiny precise script, was a notation he recognized. Not the words—the words were surveyor's shorthand, coordinates and bearings. The *form*. A small sigil, a stylized flame enclosed in a circle, the mark that seminary students used to annotate passages concerning the pre-Sundering shrines. He had drawn that mark ten thousand times. Every student at Vaelmoor drew it. It was called the ember-mark, and it was older than the Church, older than the Sundering, and no working cartographer alive had any reason to use it unless—

Unless she knew what it meant. Unless she had been annotating shrine sites on her maps for years, hiding them in plain sight, waiting for—what? For whom?

His eyes went to the child.

The bandaged hand lay on the table beside her bowl, and the glow through the wrappings pulsed, slow and steady, like breathing. Like a coal that would not die. Cassian had seen that light exactly once before, in an illuminated manuscript in the restricted archive at Vaelmoor, a book so old its pages crumbled at the touch, depicting the covenant-keepers of the old world with brands of living fire on their hands—the Ember Covenant, the binding of the gods, the seal that the Church taught had been broken and buried and blessedly forgotten.

The child looked up and caught him staring. Her eyes were enormous and frightened and, beneath the fright, stubborn as bedrock.

"You're staring at my hand," she said. Her voice was hoarse. "Everyone stares at my hand."

"I'm sorry," Cassian said, and found, to his surprise, that his voice worked, that it sounded like a priest's voice, steady and warm, a voice he had thought lost in the silence of that solstice night. "May I?" He knelt beside her chair, slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way one approaches a wounded animal—or a wounded god. "I'm not going to touch it. I just want to look."

She glanced at the woman—Oscar—at the bar, and got a nod, wary and reluctant. Then she unwound the bandage, and the whole den seemed to lean in, and there it was: a brand shaped like a flame, sunk into the skin of her palm, glowing a deep banked orange, and the skin around it was unbroken, unblistered, untouched by any burn he had ever seen. It was not a wound. Wounds healed or worsened. This simply *was*, permanent as a birthmark, patient as a coal.

Cassian knelt there on the filthy floor of a smugglers' den with the mark of the Ember Covenant burning in a child's palm, and he felt something move in him that had been still for four years. Not hope—he had renounced hope along with his orders, had buried it in the same grave as his pride. Something older than hope. Recognition. The feeling of a key turning in a lock he had mourned as rusted shut.

"It doesn't hurt," the child said, watching his face. "It burns, but it doesn't hurt. Is that strange?"

"No," Cassian said quietly. "No, I don't think it is." He looked up and found Oscar Dravenmoor—though he did not yet know the name, would not learn it for days—watching him from the bar with an expression of dawning alarm, as if she had just realized she had led her secret into a den full of strangers, one of whom was kneeling before it with tears standing in his eyes.

"Cassian," Mother Quill said, and her voice was soft and very dangerous. "What do you know?"

He rose. His knees cracked. Four years of silence rose in his throat like floodwater, all the things he had never said, the burned report, the answered prayer, the solstice silence, the shame that had hollowed him out and left him sitting in dens nursing cups he couldn't finish. He looked at the map on the bar, at the little ember-marks scattered through the mountains like seeds, at the child with the coal in her hand, at the woman who had drawn them all together without knowing what she was drawing.

"I know," Cassian Vale said, "that the gods are waking up. And I think—" His voice broke, and he steadied it, the way he had once steadied it over a hundred congregations. "I think I've been waiting four years for someone to ask me that question."

The lantern above the bar flickered. Outside, far off in the peaks of Thornfell, thunder rolled where no storm cloud hung—and beneath it, so faint it might have been imagination, something that was not thunder at all.

Something that sounded, unmistakably, like a voice saying his name.
