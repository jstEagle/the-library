## 7. What the Journal Buried

The journal did not want to be read.

Oscar had suspected as much from the moment she'd pulled it from the burning waystation's wreckage—her father's handwriting had always been a fortress, but this was something else. The first forty pages were innocuous enough: survey notes, elevation figures, a tedious argument about the proper treatment of vellum. Her father the cartographer, meticulous to the point of madness. She could hear his voice in every measured line, that dry rasp like a quill dragged over slate.

*Always draw what is there, little ember. Never what you wish were there.*

She sat in the back room of Mother Quill's boarding house in Duskhollow, a single tallow candle guttering beside her, and turned pages until her fingers ached. Below her floorboards, the smuggler haven murmured its midnight business—the clink of coin, Brannoc's low laugh, someone badly playing a fiddle two doors down. Wren slept in the next room. The girl still cried out in her sleep sometimes, whispering names the black rain had taught her.

Oscar worked by habit and lamplight, and it was habit that saved her.

Because on page forty-one, her father's neat hand changed.

Not dramatically. A stranger flipping through would never notice. But she had spent ten years forging documents, copying seals, aging ink with tea and smoke. She knew when a hand was performing. The letters grew slightly rounder, the spacing slightly looser—a man writing for an audience that might one day inspect his work. And threaded through the dull survey notes, certain words appeared at intervals that no survey required: *ember*. *Ash*. *Vessel*. *Thornfell's heart*.

A cipher key hidden in plain sight.

Her mouth went dry. She set down the journal, walked to the washbasin, splashed water on her face, and made herself come back slowly, the way she'd approach a snare in the underbrush. Some part of her already understood what she was about to find. Some part of her had understood since the waystation burned, since she'd stood in the road watching ten years of careful anonymity go up in smoke and felt—not grief, not yet, but a terrible kind of relief, like a corset cut away.

She took a fresh sheet of paper and began extracting.

The method was old, older than the Church, older than the Crown: take every seventh word from the false text, or every word following a marker, and the true sentence surfaces like a drowned body rising. It took her three hours and two candles to find the pattern. Her father had used *ash* as his anchor. Every time the word appeared in the innocent text, the words bracketing it belonged to the letter beneath.

The first true sentence she assembled read:

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, and the binding is thinner than I feared.*

Oscar did not move for a long time. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere below, the fiddle stopped.

She kept working. What else was there to do? Grief could wait; it had waited ten years already, folded small and buried deep, and it knew how to be patient. Her hands were steadier than she deserved. She copied out the anchor words, built the extraction grid, and fed the false journal through it page by page, and the true journal rose out of it like a second manuscript written in ghost-ink, and with every page her father's real voice came through—no longer dry, no longer careful, but urgent, frightened, and furious.

*They tell the story wrong, Oscar. They have always told it wrong. Listen.*

*After the Sundering, when the gods tore Vaelith open and their remnants fell ravening upon the world, it was not the Church that ended it. It was not the Crown. It was seven families, and ours was first among them. We were keepers of the Ember Covenant. We did not destroy the hungry gods—we bound them. Sealed them behind the veil with our own blood as the lock, generation after generation, each heir renewing the seal at the altars so the door would hold.*

*Your grandmother renewed it at Vess. Your grandfather at Aurel. I renewed it the year you were born, and I paid for it in ways your mother begged me never to tell you.*

*I am telling you now because I may not get another chance, and because a lie kept out of love becomes a cage built around the one you love most.*

Oscar read that line four times.

Then she got up, crossed the room, and pressed both palms flat against the cold wall, and breathed, and did not scream, though everything in her wanted to. The plaster was damp and flaking. She focused on that. Damp plaster. Fiddle music starting up again. Wren turning over in the next room with a small, unhappy sound.

*A lie kept out of love becomes a cage.*

Ten years. Ten years of maps sold honestly and routes memorized dishonestly, of a name shed like a snake's skin, of *Oscar Dravenmoor* reduced to a signature on documents that no longer existed because the waystation had burned with all of them. Ten years of believing herself a coward in hiding, and she had been—what? A sentry? A wick waiting to be lit?

She went back to the table. She kept reading.

*The seal does not run on blood alone, whatever the old rites claim. Blood is the key, but consent is the hand that turns it. My father never understood this. He performed the renewal at Aurel like a man paying a tax, resentful and rote, and I watched the veil shudder where it should have held fast. The gods are not animals behind a fence, little ember. They are prisoners who must choose to stay imprisoned, and they will only choose it if the one who binds them binds them fairly. This is the secret at the heart of everything: the Covenant was never meant to be inherited like land. It was meant to be chosen like a vow.*

*And the bloodline thins. You are the last. After you, there is no key at all.*

There was more—pages of it. Practical things, terrible things. The locations of the altars, which she had half-guessed already: Vess in the drowned valley, Aurel on the cliff, and a third whose name had been scraped off the page so thoroughly it left a gouge in the paper, as if her father had attacked it with a knife. Instructions for the renewal rite, written in a shorthand she recognized from her mother's recipe books, which meant her mother had known too, had helped encode it, had sat across from him at this same kind of table doing this same kind of work while Oscar slept upstairs believing her parents were merely tired.

And then, near the end, a passage that made her blood run cold in a way the rest had not:

*Be wary of the Church. Not the parish priests—they know nothing, poor lambs—but the Inquisition. They have a fragment of the original seal. Stolen, before your birth, from the vault at Winterfell. I do not know what they intend with it. I know only that a man came asking questions about our family the winter your mother died, and he wore grey gloves, and he smiled too easily, and after he left we burned nothing but papers for a month.*

*If they ever learn what you are, they will not try to use you, Oscar. They will try to erase you. A covenant cannot be destroyed while its keepers live. Remember that. Understand what it means about how long you have.*

Grey gloves. She had seen grey gloves recently. She had seen them on a woman with a gilded badge and a voice like honey poured over a blade, standing in the market square of Harrowgate while a shrine burned behind her, and the memory arrived whole and horrible: Seraphine Locke, holding up a shard of dark metal to the crowd, saying, *the age of meddling gods is over*, and the crowd cheering, and Oscar standing among them with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird.

The fragment. Locke had carried the fragment in her gloved hand, and Oscar had walked past it close enough to touch.

She laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, and she pressed her fist against her mouth until it stopped.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the table, surrounded by extraction grids, the candle burned to a puddle. She had not slept. She doubted she could have. Every time she closed her eyes she saw her father's face—that patient, closed-off face she had spent her childhood trying to read—and reinterpreted it. All those silences. All those times he'd gone up the mountain alone on the solstice and come back looking wrung out, telling her it was survey work. All those years her mother had flinched at thunder.

They had made her a stranger to her own name and called it protection.

No. That wasn't fair, and she was a fair-minded woman if she was anything. They had made her a stranger to her own name because the alternative was an Inquisitor's pyre, and they had been right about the danger—Locke was proof of that, the burned shrine was proof, the grey gloves were proof. Her parents had bought her thirty years with their silence. It was impossible to be furious at them without also being grateful to them, and the two feelings ground against each other inside her like millstones, and neither would win, and she was so tired.

But fury needed somewhere to go. Fury was a fire, and her whole life she had banked hers down to coals, and now the coals had air, and they were eating through her chest.

She heard footsteps on the stairs—light ones, hesitant—and a knock.

"Oscar?" Wren's voice, small through the door. "I heard you moving all night. Are you sick?"

"Not sick." She swept the grids into a pile and covered them with the journal. "Come in."

Wren crept in the way she did everything, like a creature that had learned rooms could turn on her. She was carrying the chipped cup she'd adopted, and she climbed onto the bench across the table and looked at Oscar with those grave eyes that had seen too much black rain.

"You look like Brannoc after he loses at cards," Wren said.

"I feel like it, too."

"Is that book why?" The girl nodded at the journal. "You've been reading it like it bites."

Oscar considered lying. It was reflex, ten years of reflex, and she caught herself doing it—composing the deflection, the shrug, the *old survey notes, nothing interesting*. And then she thought of her father's words. *A lie kept out of love becomes a cage.* She had spent one night discovering that her entire life had been a lovingly constructed cage. She would not build another one around this child, not even a small one, not even kindly.

"It belonged to my father," she said. "And it turns out he wasn't who I thought he was. Or rather—he was exactly who I thought he was, and also someone else entirely, and I don't know yet which one I'm angry at."

Wren absorbed this with the unnerving seriousness of children. "My da used to say everyone's two people. The one at supper and the one at prayer."

"Your da sounds wise."

"He was mostly wrong about sheep." A ghost of a smile. Then, quieter, staring into her cup: "Do you pray, Oscar?"

The question landed harder than the girl could know. Oscar looked at the journal, at her father's hidden hand, at the word *binding* repeated down the margin like a heartbeat.

"I think," she said carefully, "that someone has to. Soon. Whether anyone wants to or not."

Wren nodded as if this were a perfectly reasonable answer, drank her water, and asked if she could go help Petra with the forgery inks, because Petra had promised to show her how gold leaf worked, and Mother Quill said it was fine as long as she didn't breathe the fumes. Oscar let her go. She listened to the small feet clatter down the stairs, and she sat alone again with the truth.

* * *

By full light she had finished the final pages, and the final pages changed everything again, because of course they did. Her father had never once given her a gift that didn't come wrapped in a burden.

*One thing more, and it is the hardest thing I have ever written.*

*The renewal can fail. Not through weakness—through strength. If an heir comes to the altar with power enough, the binding holds absolutely, and the holder of that power becomes something other than human. My grandmother warned of it before she died: the Covenant offers its keeper godhood as a wage. The old Dravenmoors refused it. That refusal is the only reason there is still a mortal world worth saving, because a god who binds the other gods answers to nothing at all.*

*You will be offered it, little ember. I know you will, because the offer grows stronger as the bloodline thins—it is the seal's desperation speaking. Refuse it. Whatever it shows you, however beautiful it makes itself, refuse it. Power taken at the altar is power the altar keeps.*

*I loved you more than the Covenant. That is the last true thing I know. Let it be enough.*

Oscar sat back. The morning sun had crept across the table and lay over the journal like a laid-out hand.

Refuse it. He'd written it like an instruction, simple as *always draw what is there*. As if she wouldn't stand in front of that offering someday with her whole starving heart open. As if she hadn't spent ten years learning exactly how much a person could want something while pretending not to want it.

She gathered the extraction grids, every scrap of paper bearing the true text, and fed them one by one into the cold stove, striking a match. The paper caught with a soft whump and curled into black lace. She kept only the journal itself, and even that she wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord, and she was weighing where to hide it when she realized the answer was obvious and had been all along.

Nowhere. There was nowhere left to hide. The waystation was ash; her archive was ash; her name was ash. Hiding was finished. Everything her father's generation had built—every concealment, every careful erasure—had failed not because it was done poorly but because it was done at all. Secrets thinned like bloodlines. The only thing left was to carry it openly, in her own two hands, to the altars, and pay what was owed.

Which meant Duskhollow. Which meant the crew. Which meant telling a one-eyed pilot and a pair of twin forgers and a matriarch with a voice like gravel in honey that the quiet map-woman paying her board was the last heir of a covenant older than the Crown—and that the road they'd need to run led straight past the Inquisition.

It meant, she thought grimly, trusting people. She'd rather walk the Trial of Embers barefoot.

Below, the fiddle struck up something fast, and Brannoc's laugh rolled through the floorboards, and Wren's lighter laugh tangled with it, and Mother Quill shouted something about breakfast that was technically a threat. Ordinary sounds. Found-family sounds, though she hadn't let herself call it that yet.

Oscar tied the oilcloth bundle shut, tucked it inside her coat against her ribs where it sat like a second heartbeat, and went downstairs to decide whom to trust first—and how much of the truth a smuggler's den could hold before it started selling.

She had reached the third stair when the door banged open and Petra burst in, wild-eyed, ink to the elbows.

"Oscar—you'd better come. There's a man in the common room asking after a map-witch from Thornfell. Grey gloves."

The coals in her chest flared white-hot.

"He's alone," Petra added, "but he smiles too easy."
