## 8. Company of Strays

Wren had never seen a town that lived inside a mountain before, and for the first hour after they descended through the hidden gate, she was too busy staring to be afraid.

Duskhollow did not sit in the valley like Thornfell village did. It *hollowed* itself into the rock, a honeycomb of lantern-lit caverns and stairways carved into living stone, so that from the outside—if there even was an outside—no one would ever guess that three hundred souls lived here beneath the roots of the world. Chains of paper lanterns swayed along the high vaulted ceiling of the main cavern, hundreds of them, gold and red and one defiant green, and their light pooled on the flagstones like spilled honey. The air smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions and something sweeter underneath—spiced wine, maybe, or the resin the locals burned in little clay dishes outside every door.

"Close your mouth, sprout," said Brannoc, steering her by the shoulder with a hand the size of a dinner plate. "You'll catch flies. Or worse, you'll look like a tourist."

"I've never seen—" Wren began.

"Nobody's ever seen," Brannoc agreed cheerfully. He was a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, with a beard braided into two greying ropes and a black silk patch over his left eye. The other eye was quick and brown and missed nothing. "That's rather the point of Duskhollow. First rule of the Hollow: what you see here, you didn't see. Second rule: if anyone asks, you're my niece."

"I'm not your niece."

"You are now. Congratulations on the family. We're terrible, but we feed well." He steered her down a crooked lane between buildings that leaned into each other like old drunks, past a smithy ringing with hammer-song, past a woman selling candied plums who winked at Wren with both eyes, which somehow felt more scandalous than anything else she'd seen all day.

Wren's hand kept drifting to her sleeve, where the brand sat wrapped in linen bandages Oscar had changed that morning. It had stopped whispering since they'd crossed into Duskhollow—or perhaps it hadn't, and she'd simply stopped listening—but it still burned, low and constant, like a coal banked under ash. The people here glanced at her sometimes. She caught them doing it. A boy about her age stared openly until his mother tugged him behind a doorway. A man mending nets by the cistern looked up, saw her face, and went pale before turning away very deliberately.

They know, she thought. They know what I am.

But no one pointed. No one shouted *witch*. No one came at her with the kitchen knife that Old Marta had raised when the black rain started falling and wouldn't stop whispering names. Here, in the lantern-light under the mountain, being strange seemed almost ordinary.

* * *

Mother Quill's establishment occupied the best-lit corner of the third cavern, a sprawling warren of rooms above a taproom called the Gilded Lantern, and Mother Quill herself turned out to be less a person than a force of nature wearing shawls.

She was small. That was the first shock. Wren had imagined someone vast and terrible, given how Brannoc spoke of her, but Mother Quill barely reached Wren's chin, and most of her apparent height was grey hair piled into a tower secured with what appeared to be three knitting needles and a fishhook. Her face was a topography of wrinkles, deep as river-cut canyons, and her eyes were sharp black beads that took in Wren—the mud, the bandaged arm, the hollows under her eyes—and rendered judgment in a single glance.

"So," Mother Quill said. "The map-witch drags home a stray."

"She found me," Wren said, before she could stop herself. "I mean—I wasn't—she didn't drag me. I followed."

"Mm." Mother Quill circled her slowly, the way Wren had seen farmers circle a horse they were thinking of buying. "Can you cook?"

"No?"

"Wash dishes?"

"I—yes? I think so."

"Read?"

"Yes."

"Write?"

"Yes."

"Lie?"

Wren hesitated. "I don't... I'm not very good at it."

Something flickered across the old woman's canyon-face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might have lived once, long ago. "We'll see how long that lasts. Everyone in this house lies professionally except me, and I lie better than any of them because I only do it on Sundays." She jerked her chin toward the stairs. "Third door on the left. There's a cot and a basin and a window that looks at solid rock, so don't expect a view. You eat when we eat, you work when we work, and if you bring trouble through my door, you'd best pray it's the kind that can be stabbed."

"That's not comforting," Wren said faintly.

"Comfort's for the rich and the dead. Go on. Supper's at sundown, and if you're late, Petra will eat your portion and lie about it with a straight face, and you'll believe her, because everyone believes Petra."

* * *

The twins found her before supper found her.

Wren was scrubbing a pot the size of a rowboat in the taproom kitchen when a voice directly beside her ear said, "You're doing it wrong," and she shrieked loud enough to slop suds over the floor and possibly summon the militia.

The girl attached to the voice grinned at her. At least, Wren thought it was a girl—it was hard to tell, because a second identical girl appeared on her other side a heartbeat later, also grinning, and for one vertiginous moment Wren felt like she was standing inside a mirror.

"Ilsa," said the first.

"Petra," said the second.

"We're twins," they said together, and then, catching themselves, glared at each other.

"Don't do the thing," said Ilsa.

"You did the thing first," said Petra.

"The *thing* is our thing. You can't claim the thing."

"It's a shared thing. Everything of ours is shared. Except the blue ribbon, which is mine by right of conquest."

"You *stole* it while I slept!"

"A conquest is a conquest."

Wren stood dripping suds, forgotten, watching the argument unfold with the particular fascination of someone watching weather. Eventually the twins remembered she existed and turned in unison, which was deeply unsettling.

"You're the god-touched girl," Petra said.

"Petra!" Ilsa elbowed her.

"What? Everyone's saying it. Half the Hollow knows by now. The other half will know by tomorrow's breakfast, and by Friday there'll be a ballad, and by next month the ballad will have us rescuing you from a dragon, which I for one would support."

"There's no dragon," Wren said.

"Not yet," said both twins, and glared at each other again.

Ilsa rolled her eyes and slung an arm around Wren's shoulders, suds and all. "Ignore her. What she means is: are you all right? Really. Not the polite answer."

And that—that simple question, asked without fear or reverence or the flat animal hatred Wren had run from—cracked something open in her chest that she hadn't known was sealed. She opened her mouth to say *fine*, because she was fine, she was fine, she was—

"I watched my whole village decide I was a monster," she heard herself say instead. "In one morning. One rain. Marta held a knife on me and I'd known her my whole life. She taught me to bake. And now I'm here and I don't know why any of this is happening and my arm burns all the time and I keep waiting for all of you to figure out what I am and do the same thing."

The kitchen was quiet except for the drip of the tap.

Then Petra, of all people, sat down on an overturned crate beside the wash-tub, patted the space next to her, and said, "Sit. Scrubbing's better with company. And for the record—we're smugglers, forgers, thieves, and at least one deserter. Nobody in this house gets to throw stones about being a monster. It's practically our coat of arms."

"Brannoc lost the eye to a customs officer he was trying to bribe *badly*," Ilsa offered, resuming Wren's abandoned pot with practiced efficiency.

"He tells it differently every time," Petra said. "Last month it was a kraken."

"It was not a kraken," said a voice from the doorway, and Brannoc filled it entirely, arms crossed, one eye twinkling. "It was a very large goose."

* * *

Supper at the Gilded Lantern was nothing like any meal Wren had ever eaten.

At home, meals had been silent, orderly affairs—father at the head, grace said to gods who never answered, children speaking only when spoken to. Here, the long table in the taproom's back room roared and argued and passed dishes without ceremony. Brannoc told a story about running grain past the Crown blockade during the famine years that involved, at various points, a wedding dress, a forged bishop, and a barrel of live eels, and by the end of it Ilsa was wheezing and even Mother Quill's mouth had twitched in a way that might, generously, have been called amusement.

Wren ate roast fowl and buttered turnips and bread still warm enough to steam, and halfway through the meal she realized her cheeks hurt from smiling, and that she couldn't remember the last time that had happened.

It was Petra who noticed her staring at the lantern above the table—a plain thing, glass and tin, its candle guttering low.

"You flinched," Petra said quietly, under the noise. "When the flame dipped. Why?"

Wren's hand had gone to her bandage without her permission. Under the linen, the brand pulsed once, softly, like a heart remembering its rhythm.

"Since the rain," she said. "Fire does... things. Near me. Little things. Candles lean toward me. Lamps brighten. I thought—I thought at first I was imagining it, but I wasn't, was I? Something answered my prayer. Something's *in* me, or following me, or—" Her voice wobbled. "My mother used to say the old gods were dead. Everyone says they're dead. Dead things shouldn't be able to answer."

The table noise seemed very far away suddenly. Ilsa and Petra exchanged a glance over her head—one of those twin conversations that happened entirely in eyebrows—and then Ilsa reached across and, matter-of-factly, moved the guttering lantern further down the table, away from Wren, and lit a fresh candle instead.

"There," Ilsa said. "New candle. If it leans toward you, let it. Fire's honest. It goes where it wants, and it doesn't apologize after."

"And anyone who calls you monster for it," Petra added, "has to get through us first, and we're *very* annoying. Ask anyone."

Wren laughed—a wet, surprised sound—and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the burning there subsided.

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and the taproom below had gone soft with drink and song, Mother Quill found Wren on the back steps, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of cedar, watching the paper lanterns sway against the cavern dark.

The old woman lowered herself onto the step beside her with the care of someone negotiating with her own knees, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The mountain hummed around them—water somewhere in the deep stone, the distant clink of the night shift at the forgers' benches.

"In three hundred years," Mother Quill said at last, "this house has taken in deserters and debtors and one actual pirate queen, may she rest, and every single one of them arrived thinking they were poison. That the world had good reason to spit them out." She picked a bit of lint off her shawl. "And every single one of them was wrong. Damaged, aye. Frightened, often. Poison, never. Poison doesn't worry about whether it's poison."

Wren's throat worked. "What if I'm different?"

"Sprout, you're a child whose prayers got answered by something old and hungry and stupid, through no fault of your own, and you walked down a mountain on a burned arm to tell the truth about it. In my professional opinion"—the black bead-eyes glittered sideways at her—"that makes you the least poisonous thing to walk through my door in twenty years."

Above them, the lanterns swayed. One of them—the defiant green one, far up in the vault of stone—flickered, just once, in a wind that did not exist underground.

Neither of them mentioned it. But Mother Quill shifted slightly, putting her small, stooped body squarely between Wren and the light, and stayed there until the flickering stopped.

* * *

Wren woke in the third room on the left to the smell of frying bread and the sound of the twins arguing about the blue ribbon, and for three full seconds, half-asleep, she forgot everything. She was just a girl waking up in a warm house where people knew her name.

Then the brand woke too.

It came gently this time—not the searing agony of the mountain pass, but a slow, deliberate warmth spreading up her arm, across her shoulder, settling behind her breastbone like a second heart learning its beat. And beneath the warmth, faint as a voice heard through a wall, something whispered.

Not names this time.

A direction.

Down. Deep. Toward the mountain's roots, where the oldest tunnels ran and no lanterns hung. Toward something that had been waiting a very long time, and had just begun, patiently, to listen for her.

Wren lay still beneath the blankets, listening to the ordinary sounds of the house—Petra laughing, Brannoc swearing at a kettle, the world going on—and understood, with a child's terrible clarity, that whatever warmth she had found here, she had led it straight through the hidden gate.

Down in the deeps, something had caught her scent.

And it was already climbing.
