## 9. The Price of Passage

The Greywater did not appear on any map Oscar had ever drawn, and she had drawn every map worth owning in the Thornfell marches.

That was the first thing to understand about it: the river was not hidden so much as *unwilling*. It ran through a fold of the mountains where the valleys doubled back on themselves like a sentence that refused to end, and travelers who stumbled into it by accident found themselves walking out again three days later with no memory of the intervening country and an unaccountable grief sitting on their chests like wet wool. The smugglers called it the Greywater because of the color—no, that was the lie they told strangers. They called it the Greywater because water that remembered everything it carried eventually turned the color of ash.

Oscar had known about it for six years. She had mapped its approaches in her head, never on paper, and she had promised herself she would never need it.

Promises, she was learning, were the first casualties of covenants.

She came down from the high passes alone, as the Ferryman required. Wren had wanted to come; Cassian had insisted; Mother Quill had simply looked at Oscar over the rim of her pipe and said, "You don't bring a crowd to a debt negotiation, girl." So Oscar had left them at the waystation above the fold with a map drawn in disappearing ink—a trick Ilsa had taught her, vinegar and heat—and a promise to return before the moon filled out.

The valley announced itself by silence. One moment there was wind in the pines and the small industrious noise of water somewhere below, and the next moment both stopped, cut off as cleanly as a door closing. The trees here grew pale and close together, their bark grey as old skin, and no birds crossed overhead. Oscar's breath made clouds in air that had gone cold without changing temperature, which was impossible, and she had learned over the past weeks to stop flinching at impossibilities. They were simply the world showing its seams.

The river appeared all at once, the way a thought appears. One step she was walking through birch-shadow; the next, the ground fell away into a broad grey flood moving without current, flat as poured metal, stretching upstream and down until the mist took it. There was no far bank. There was only the near one: a shelf of dark stone, and moored against it, a boat.

It was not a ferryman's boat. It was too fine for that, too old—black wood scarred with use but lovingly maintained, a single lantern hanging from the stern post burning with a flame that gave off light and no heat and cast no reflection on the water. And standing in it, one hand resting on the pole he did not need, was the Ferryman.

He had been handsome once, or had worn a handsome shape. His face had the smoothness of something carved rather than born, features arranged with a craftsman's care but no particular conviction, as though he had tried on this appearance centuries ago and never bothered to update it. His eyes were the color of the river. When he saw her, he inclined his head with the courtesy of an innkeeper greeting a guest he already knew would not pay in coin.

"Cartographer," he said. His voice was quiet and carried perfectly, the way voices carry in empty churches. "You've come farther than most who stand where you're standing."

"I need passage."

"Everyone who comes here needs passage." He gestured, and the lantern swayed though there was no wind. "Fewer need it enough."

"The drowned shrine of Vess." She kept her voice level. She had rehearsed this on the mountain, walking, muttering to herself like a madwoman, and now the rehearsal held. "Below the falls on the north fork. Every road to it crosses your water."

"It does." He studied her, and being studied by him felt like being read—like pages turning somewhere behind her ribs. "And you know my toll."

"A memory." The word came out smaller than she intended. She straightened. "A memory. Chosen by me?"

"Chosen by you, yes." Something moved across his carved face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if he'd bothered to install one. "That is the mercy of the Greywater. We do not take what you refuse to give. That is also the cruelty. You must choose."

"And if I choose nothing? If I turn back?"

"Then you turn back, and the shrine stays drowned, and whatever wakes beneath Thornfell keeps waking." He said it without judgment, without urgency, the way a man reports weather. "I am not your conscience, cartographer. I am only the price."

* * *

She made him wait while she walked the bank.

Not because she was deciding—the decision had been made somewhere on the high passes, maybe further back than that, maybe the night she'd pulled a burned child out of the snow—but because she wanted to look at what she was about to lose while she still owned it. That seemed important. That seemed like the kind of thing a person should do properly, with attention, the way she inked a coastline: slowly, twice, checking the truth of it against the land.

Her mother's face.

It was strange how memory worked. She could not remember her mother's voice anymore—that had gone years ago, worn away like a coin passed through too many hands—but the face remained, vivid and specific in a way almost nothing else in her childhood was. The laugh lines first, deep crescents at the corners of eyes the same grey-green as Oscar's own. A small scar through the left eyebrow, from a fall off a horse her father had sworn was gentle. The way her whole face reorganized itself when she laughed—eyes narrowing, nose crinkling, chin tipping up as if she were laughing at the sky itself. The way it went utterly still when she was afraid, which was rare, and which Oscar had seen only once, the night soldiers came to the house, the night her mother pressed the journal into her hands and told her to run and did not say goodbye because goodbyes were for people who expected to be found again.

Oscar had been nine. She had built her entire adult life inside the absence that woman left behind. Every map she drew was drawn in the shape of that absence. Every rule she lived by—invisibility, anonymity, the careful erasure of the name Dravenmoor—was a wall built around a grave.

And now she was going to hand the face itself to a dead river.

"There are other memories," she said to the Ferryman, when she came back to the stone shelf. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it. "I have ten thousand others. I could give you the taste of the stew at Duskhollow. I could give you the name of every smuggler's route east of the pass. I could give you—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—I could give you the face of every Inquisitor who ever looked at me sideways. No one would miss those."

"No," he agreed. "No one would miss them. And the toll would not be paid."

"That's not how bargaining works."

"That is exactly how bargaining works." He stepped aside, unhurried, and the boat drifted to the shelf and touched it soft as a kiss. "The Greywater takes what costs. A memory you can spare is not a memory; it is pocket lint. The crossing requires weight, cartographer. It requires that you arrive on the other shore lighter in a way you will feel for the rest of your life." He looked at her, and for a moment the craftsmanship of his face failed and something older looked out—something tired beyond the reach of language. "I have taken the day someone learned to walk. I have taken a first love's name. I have taken a soldier's knowledge of why he deserted. In three hundred years, no one has ever paid me something cheap and gotten across. The river knows the difference. Believe me. The river has made me know the difference."

"How?" The question escaped before she could cage it. "How does it feel, being the one who collects?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "Like holding water. Everything passes through, and nothing stays, and you remember the shapes of things you can no longer name." He turned his face toward the mist upstream. "Your mother's face. You've decided. You've been decided since the birches."

"You can't know that."

"I collect memories, child. I know what people look like when they're saying goodbye to one."

* * *

She boarded the boat.

The black wood was warm under her boots, warmer than the air, warmer than blood, and when she sat on the thwart the whole vessel settled around her weight like a living thing adjusting its posture. The Ferryman took up his pole—unnecessary, she understood now, purely ceremonial, the way a priest's vestments are unnecessary—and pushed off from the shelf, and the near bank slid away into the birches and was gone.

The crossing was silent. Not quiet—silent, the absolute hush of a place where sound had been spent long ago and none remained. Even the water made no noise against the hull. The lantern burned. The mist stood in walls on either side. Somewhere beneath them, impossibly deep, the river carried its cargo of everything anyone had ever chosen to forget, and Oscar found herself wondering—with a detachment that frightened her—whether her mother's face would sink down there among them, laughing at the sky, forever.

"Tell me what you're taking," the Ferryman said. It was not a request. "The toll must be named aloud. Named and described. The river requires specifics."

She closed her eyes. Opened them. Some things deserved to be witnessed with open eyes.

"My mother's face," she said. "All of it. The scar through her left eyebrow. The lines at the corners of her eyes. The way she laughed—chin up, nose crinkled, like the joke was aimed at heaven. The way she went still when she was afraid. The night she gave me my father's journal and told me to run." Her throat closed. She forced the words through it one at a time, each one a stone pulled from a wall that then leaned dangerously. "I give you her face. All of it. Every time I have ever seen it and every time I will imagine it. Let the river keep it. Let me cross."

The lantern flame bent toward her, though nothing moved.

"Paid," said the Ferryman.

It did not hurt. That was the worst part. She had braced for pain—for the tearing sensation the smugglers' stories promised, cold hooks behind the eyes—and instead there was only a softness, a loosening, like a knot untying in a place she couldn't point to. The mist brightened. The boat moved faster, riding some current that had finally consented to exist.

And then she reached for her mother's face and found—

Nothing.

Not darkness. Not a blank. Worse: a *shape*. She knew there had been a woman. She knew the woman had been her mother, that she had loved her with the whole dumb ferocity of a nine-year-old, that she had died—or worse than died, been erased—on a night of soldiers and fire. The facts remained, filed and orderly. But when she reached for the face itself, the way you reach for a familiar object in a dark room, her hand closed on air. There had been laugh lines. She knew that the way she knew the capital of a country she'd never visited. There had been a scar. Which eyebrow? The knowledge was gone, and with it the eyebrow, and with it the whole architecture of a human face that had once been the first thing she saw every morning of her childhood.

She tried to reconstruct it from mirrors—from her own face, which everyone said favored her mother's. But a mirror gives you a face at rest, a face performing itself, and her mother's face had lived in motion, in laughter, in that terrible stillness, and all of that was gone, gone, gone, sunk into grey water under grey mist, and Oscar sat in the silent boat with tears running down her cheeks and no idea what she was weeping for except that she knew, she *knew*, it was the most expensive thing she had ever bought.

"This is the cost," the Ferryman said quietly. He did not look at her. He watched the water, always the water. "This is what I meant by weight. You will spend the rest of your life knowing you loved a face you cannot see. Grief without an object. It is the hardest cargo any of my passengers carry, and they carry it forever, and most of them tell me, in the end, that they would pay it again." A pause. "Would you?"

"Yes." It came out instantly, and it surprised her with its truth. "She'd have done worse for me."

Something flickered across his carved features—respect, perhaps, or its fossil. "Then you may yet survive what's coming. Few who reach Vess's shrine do, you know. The drowned gods ask harder questions than I do." He lifted the pole, and ahead, impossibly, a far bank materialized out of the mist: grey shingle, grey grass, and beyond it the dark shoulder of the mountain with the falls' distant murmur—the first real sound she had heard since the birches. "The shrine lies two miles north. Follow the water's voice." The boat kissed the shingle. "One more thing, cartographer."

She stood on legs that didn't trust themselves. "What?"

"The toll runs both ways, in a sense. What the river takes, the river keeps—and the river is not sealed away. It runs under everything. Under Duskhollow. Under the shrines. Under the thin places where the veil is wearing through." His river-colored eyes fixed on her, and for the first time there was something in them that might have been warning. "What you gave me today is part of the water now. And the water is stirring. Things that drink from the Greywater learn what it carries." He paused. "Including your name."

Cold walked up her spine. "Who drinks from the Greywater?"

"Everything hungry," said the Ferryman, and pushed her gently ashore, and the boat withdrew into the mist without a ripple, and the lantern light lingered a moment—two points of gold in the grey, like eyes—before it, too, was gone.

* * *

She stood on the shingle for a long time.

Two miles north, the drowned shrine of Vess waited with its rite and its blood and its watching dark. Behind her, days south, a burned child and a disgraced priest and a crew of strays waited for her to come back with answers. Somewhere between those two points lay the rest of her life, and she had just paid for passage into it with the face of the woman who made her.

She tried once more, standing there, to see it. Laugh lines. Scar. Chin tipped up toward heaven. She got the words but not the picture, a song remembered without its melody, and the grief that rose in her had no face to fasten to and therefore no bottom, and she let it move through her because the alternative was letting it stay.

Then she wiped her cheeks, checked her satchel—journal, knife, the vial of Wren's blood Ilsa had drawn at Mother Quill's instruction, because the brand was a key and keys opened doors—and turned north, toward the sound of falling water.

Behind her, far downstream, the Greywater carried its new treasure into the dark under the world.

And something in that dark, patient and vast and growing less patient by the day, tasted it—and smiled around a mouthful of a dead woman's face, and began, very slowly, to swim toward the light.
