## 10. Gods in the Gutters

The rain came to Duskhollow sideways, as it always did, driven up from the Greywater in grey sheets that smelled of silt and old fish and something faintly, stubbornly holy. Cassian Vale stood beneath the crooked awning of the Salt Cellar with a cup of something that pretended to be wine and watched the water run through the gutters like it was looking for something.

He had been watching water for three days now. He told himself it was habit—the seminary had trained him to notice omens, and old training died hard—but the truth was uglier. The truth was that since the night on the mountain pass when he'd first seen the ember-brand glowing on a half-dead child's wrist, he had not been able to stop looking for signs. And signs, lately, were everywhere.

A candle guttering leftward in still air. A dog that howled at nothing at the exact hour of vespers. The way the rain, this morning, had fallen in a perfect circle around Mother Quill's house and nowhere else, for eleven minutes, while the rest of Duskhollow drowned.

*Coincidence,* said the part of him that wanted to keep drinking. *Weather is weather.*

*Nothing is weather anymore,* said the other part, the one that remembered kneeling on cold stone at nineteen years old, praying until his knees bled, and receiving silence so vast and complete it had felt less like absence than like being *unmade*.

"Brooding again?" Brannoc settled beside him under the awning, one eye squinting out at the downpour, the other—a puckered seam of scar tissue where an eye used to be—doing whatever it was that ruined eyes did. "You've got the look. Seminary look. I knew a man who went to seminary once. He brooded himself into a hernia."

"I don't brood."

"You've been staring at that same puddle for a quarter hour like it owes you money."

Cassian didn't answer. In the puddle, the rain made rings, and the rings made patterns, and the patterns almost—*almost*—resolved into script. He blinked and it was just water.

That was the trouble with gods returning after three hundred years. Everything started to look like them.

* * *

Duskhollow at dusk was a different animal than Duskhollow by day. By day it was a smugglers' haven: narrow streets stacked like spilled dice against the mountainside, warehouses full of goods that had never seen a customs seal, taverns where the ale was honest even if everything else wasn't. But at dusk, when the lamps came on—fat, smoky things behind horn panes—the town seemed to *settle*, the way an old dog settles before a storm it can smell coming.

Cassian walked the evening rounds because walking helped, and because Mother Quill had asked him to check on Wren, and because checking on Wren was the closest thing to a prayer he permitted himself these days.

She was in the loft above the chandlery, sitting cross-legged on a cot too big for her, and she was drawing. The girl drew constantly—little figures, little houses, little suns with faces—and Cassian had learned not to startle her. He knocked twice on the ladder rail and waited.

"It's me."

"I know." She didn't look up. "You always knock like you're sorry to be here."

"That's fair."

She glanced up then, and his chest did the thing it always did—the tightening, the old reflexive grief. She was nine years old and she had walked out of a village that called her witch, out of black rain that whispered names, and she carried on her wrist a brand shaped like a flame that would not heal, would not fade, and sometimes—only sometimes, only at dusk—glowed.

"How's the arm?"

She pulled her sleeve down over it, quick and practiced. "Fine."

"Wren."

"It's fine," she said, louder, and then, smaller: "It hums. At night. Like a bee got under the skin but the bee is singing."

Cassian sat down on the edge of the cot, careful, the way you'd sit near a bird with a broken wing. "Does Oscar know about the singing?"

Wren's face did something complicated. "I didn't want her to make that face."

"What face?"

"The face where she's scared but pretending she's doing sums instead."

Despite everything, Cassian smiled. "She does do that face."

"She thinks I don't notice. Brannoc says she's been making that face since the day she was born."

"Brannoc says a lot of things." He hesitated. "May I see it? The brand. Just for a moment."

Wren studied him with the flat, measuring gaze of a child who had learned that adults' kindnesses came with hooks. Then she pushed back her sleeve.

There it was. The ember-mark, no longer raw the way it had been on the pass, but settled now into the skin like an old scar that refused to age—except that tonight, in the lamplight, it was *moving*. Faintly. The way coals move when they're dying but not dead. And beneath it, audible if you held your breath, a sound like a hymn sung very far away through very thick walls.

Cassian had spent twelve years as a priest of the Concordance. He had studied the old liturgies, the pre-Sundering rites, the prayers the Church had officially declared apocryphal and unofficially burned. He knew what a vessel looked like. He knew what a *doorway* looked like.

This was neither. This was a *beacon*.

"Wren," he said carefully, "when it sings—does it ever sing words?"

Her eyes came up, wide and dark. "How did you know?"

*Because I've read the forbidden commentaries. Because I once watched an old woman in Vespergate speak in a voice three centuries dead before the Church took her away.* "Lucky guess. What words?"

She bit her lip. Then, in a whisper, in a cadence that was not any language Cassian knew and yet which he understood perfectly, she sang:

*"What was buried wants its name. What was stolen wants its heir."*

The lamp flame bent toward her. Just slightly. Just enough.

Cassian stood so fast the cot scraped. "Don't sing that again. Not anywhere. Not to anyone. If it starts on its own, you come find me or Oscar immediately. Do you understand?"

"You're doing the face," Wren said miserably.

"Yes," Cassian admitted. "Yes, I am."

* * *

It happened two nights later, in the gutters, as the rain had promised.

Cassian was coming back from the chandlery past midnight, drunk enough to be steady rather than unsteady—he'd long ago found the exact weight of drink that let him walk straight while his thoughts stayed suitably drowned—when he heard the coins.

That was the only way to describe it. Coins. The sound of copper and silver spilling across cobblestones, a bright ringing scatter of it, coming from the alley between the tannery and the salt store. Except there was no wind to carry it, and no footsteps, and the sound kept going—kept *spilling*—long past any purse's capacity.

Every hair on his arms rose. Twelve years of priesthood and six years of drinking had taught him the difference between fear and *recognition*, and what moved through him now was recognition, cold and total, like stepping into a river that turned out to be full of ghosts.

He should have walked away. Every rule of his new life—the rules he'd written himself, carved into his own bones over six bitter years—said walk away. Don't look. Don't kneel. Don't *pray*.

He looked.

In the mouth of the alley, by the light of a single guttering lamp, stood a figure that was trying very hard to be a beggar and failing at the fundamentals. It wore rags, yes—layered, patched, greasy rags—but the rags hung wrong, draped like royal vestments on a frame that couldn't quite remember how bodies worked. Its feet were bare and filthy and did not quite touch the ground. Its face was kind and terrible, the face of every beggar who had ever held out a hand and every king who had ever refused it, and its eyes were full of small round lights, hundreds of them, thousands, turning slowly like coins dropped into a well of dark water.

Around its bare feet, real coins lay scattered across the wet cobbles. Coppers, silvers, a few tarnished golds—coins of mints that hadn't struck currency in three centuries. Cassian recognized, absurdly, a Dravenmoor half-crown among them, its ember sigil worn nearly smooth.

"Beggar-king," Cassian breathed. The old texts called it the Lord of Lost Coins, patron of the discarded, the forgotten, the thrown-away. A minor god. A *small* god, in the old hierarchy—barely worth a footnote, a shrine in an alley, a superstition among thieves. "You shouldn't be here. You're bound. You were all bound—"

The god's head tilted. When it spoke, its voice was the sound a coin makes spinning to rest on stone, multiplied into speech.

"*Priest.*"

"I'm not. Not anymore."

"*The collar remembers what the man forgets.*" One rag-draped arm extended, palm up, fingers curled inward except the index finger, which pointed—not at Cassian. Past him. Through him. Down the street, toward Mother Quill's house, toward the loft above the chandlery where a nine-year-old girl slept with a burning brand on her wrist. "*Drawn up the road like smoke to a chimney. Drawn by the little light. The little light that calls and calls.*"

"She's a child," Cassian said, and heard his own voice crack on it. "Whatever you want from her—"

"*Want?*" The god's coin-eyes spun faster, and something like sorrow moved across its impossible face. "*We do not want, priest. We are want. We are what remains when wanting is all that's left of you.*" It took a step forward, bare feet never touching ground, and the coins at its feet rang softly, rearranging themselves into patterns Cassian's eye refused to follow. "*But the light wants. The light reaches. And we answer, because answering is all we remember how to do.*"

"Then don't answer. Go back. Go back to sleep, go back to whatever grave the Sundering put you in—"

"*There is no grave deep enough anymore.*" For the first time, the terrible kindness in its face cracked, and underneath was something exhausted beyond mortal comprehension. Something *starving*. "*The seal thins. The heir wakes. Can you not feel it, priest? Even your dead God feels it, wherever dead Gods go.*"

Cassian's mouth went dry. "What heir? What are you talking about?"

The god drifted closer, and up close Cassian could smell it—not rot, not incense, but the smell of a temple abandoned for centuries, dust and cold stone and the ghost of ten thousand candles. Its coin-eyes fixed on him, and in their slow-turning depths he saw reflections that weren't his own: a mountain, a map, a woman's hand tracing ink lines by lamplight.

"*The mapmaker,*" the beggar-king whispered, and every coin in the alley rang at once, a great bright tolling chord. "*The last of the line that took our names away. Oscar Dravenmoor. The heir of embers comes to the altars, and the altars wake, and we wake with them, and oh—oh—we remember what her family did to us.*"

The world narrowed to a point. The rain, the cold, the wine—all of it fell away, and there was only the alley and the god and the name hanging in the air like a blade.

Oscar. *Oscar Dravenmoor.* The name he had not known, the name she had not given, spoken aloud in a smuggler's alley by a thing out of a burned book.

"You're wrong," Cassian said hoarsely. "You have the wrong—she's a cartographer. She sells maps. There's no—"

"*We do not err,*" the god said gently. "*We only remember.*" It began to thin then, at the edges first, its outline fraying like wet paper, the coin-lights in its eyes winking out one by one, dozens, hundreds, a treasury going dark. "*Tell the heir: we know what she carries. Tell her the debt is called in. Tell her—*"

Its voice stretched, thinned, became the sound of a single coin spinning slower and slower on stone.

"*—tell her we are coming to be paid.*"

And it was gone. The last coin-light winked out. The scattered coins remained, though—dozens of them, ancient and gleaming on the wet cobbles, arranged in a pattern that Cassian looked at exactly once and then made himself stop looking at, because it was a map, it was unmistakably a map, and the place it marked was the heart of Thornfell.

Then, one by one, as he watched, the coins began to rust. Fast, impossibly fast, centuries of decay compressing into seconds, until nothing remained but stains of green and red oxide on the stones, and the rain came and washed even those away.

Cassian Vale stood alone in the alley for a long time. He did not pray. He had promised himself he never would again. But his hands, he noticed distantly, had folded themselves into the old shape without asking his permission, and his lips were moving around words he'd sworn off six years ago, and the word they kept circling, over and over, like a coin that wouldn't stop spinning, was *Oscar*.

* * *

He found her at dawn in Mother Quill's kitchen, hunched over her father's journal with a pot of tea gone cold beside her, ink on her fingers, that particular frown she wore when she was translating something she hated. She looked up when he came in, and whatever was on his face made her set down the journal very slowly.

"Cassian. What happened?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Six years of silence, of solitude, of *never telling anyone anything*—all of it warred with the memory of coin-eyes and a voice like a bell.

"There was a manifestation last night," he said finally. "In the alley by the tannery. A minor deity—the Lord of Lost Coins, if the old texts have it right. A beggar-king. Small god, harmless in the old days. Shrines in alleys, offerings from thieves, that sort of thing."

Oscar had gone very still. "Manifestation. Here? In Duskhollow?"

"Drawn here. It said—" His throat worked. "It said it was drawn by Wren's brand. That the child 'calls and calls.' And then—" He stopped. Started again. "Oscar, it spoke a name. Before it dissolved. It looked at me—or through me—and it said the heir of embers comes to the altars, and it said—"

"Say it."

He made himself hold her gaze. "It said *Oscar Dravenmoor*. It said her family took the gods' names away. It said the debt is called in." His voice dropped. "It said they are coming to be paid."

The silence that followed was long and terrible. Somewhere outside, the rain kept falling, patient as a creditor.

Oscar looked down at her father's journal, open on the table to a page of cramped, coded script, and when she spoke again her voice was flat and quiet in a way Cassian had learned to fear more than shouting.

"Three nights ago," she said, "the first altar woke under my blood. Two nights ago, the journal confirmed what my father spent his whole life hiding—that the Dravenmoors bound the gods after the Sundering, and that the binding is failing." Her thumb moved over the page, back and forth, back and forth. "Last night, a god crawled out of whatever grave we put it in, dragged itself to a smuggler's den in the middle of nowhere, and said my *name*."

"Oscar—"

"It knows." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and bright and more frightened than he had ever seen them, and she was doing the face, the sums-instead-of-fear face, and failing at it. "It knows who I am, Cassian. Which means the others know. Whatever else is stirring out there in the dark—it isn't stumbling around blind anymore. It's *looking* for me." She laughed, short and joyless. "Ten years I spent making myself invisible. Ten years of maps and false names and never sleeping in the same bed twice. And one rite at a drowned shrine, and every hungry thing in Vaelith can see me clear as a bonfire on a peak."

"So we move," Cassian said. "Tonight. Now. Take Wren, take the crew, get ahead of—"

"Get ahead of *what*?" She gestured at the window, at the rain, at the whole grey waking world. "It's a god, Cassian. You can't outrun a god. You can barely outrun the *weather*." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "And it said debt. It said *paid*. My family stole something from those beings—stole their names, their freedom, maybe worse—and now the bill has come due, and I'm the last Dravenmoor standing at the counter."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then, from the loft above, faint through the floorboards, came a sound that stopped both their hearts: a child's voice, humming. A slow, sweet, unfamiliar melody, winding up out of sleep and dream, and beneath it—so faint Cassian might have imagined it, might have *prayed* he imagined it—the soft, endless ring of distant coins.

Oscar's head snapped up. "Is she—"

"Singing," Cassian said grimly. "In her sleep." He was already moving for the ladder. "The brand's getting stronger. Whatever called that thing to Duskhollow, it's calling louder."

Behind him, he heard Oscar rise, heard the journal snap shut, heard her say, low and fierce, to herself or to the gods or to the ghost of her father:

"Then we'd better find out what she's saying before something else answers."

Above them, in the grey light of a rain-washed dawn, Wren slept on—and hummed—and somewhere far below the roots of Thornfell, in the dark where the old kings of Vaelith lay bound, something ancient turned its attention toward the sound, and began, with the patience of the buried, to climb.
