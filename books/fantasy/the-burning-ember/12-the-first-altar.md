## 12. The First Altar

The drowned shrine of Vess lay three days into the Greywater marshes, at the end of a smuggler's route that existed on no map Oscar had ever sold—and on only one map she had ever drawn, in ink that faded within an hour of drying, in a cipher of her own devising, folded into the lining of her father's journal.

She had not wanted company for this. She had lost that argument twice: once to Cassian, who had simply looked at her over the rim of his tin cup and said, "You don't get to die alone in a bog, that's not how redemption works," and once to Wren, who had not argued at all, which was worse. The girl had only looked at her with those too-old eyes and said, "It burns less when you're near. I don't know why."

Oscar knew why. She had known since the night by the fire when she'd finally opened her father's journal past the maps and read what he had buried beneath them in a hand gone shaky with age or fear or both. *The binding is blood. The blood is thinning. God forgive us for what we made our children carry.*

So the four of them went—Oscar, Cassian, Wren, and Brannoc, who had appointed himself escort with the reasoning that "someone has to mind the boat, and none of you lot can tie a cleat worth a damn." The Greywater did not forgive mistakes. It swallowed them.

* * *

They poled through channels where the water ran the color of old pewter and the reeds grew taller than a man on horseback. Mist sat on the marsh like a lid on a pot. There were no birds. That was the first wrongness, though it took Oscar half a day to name it: a marsh this rich should have been deafening with life, herons and rails and the wet gossip of frogs. Instead there was only the sound of their own passage—the knock of poles against hull, the lap of water—and underneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, a hum. Not a sound, exactly. A pressure. Like standing near a struck bell that had not yet finished being a bell.

"Feel that?" Brannoc muttered, around midday of the second day. His one eye kept moving, cataloging. "Teeth-ache weather."

"It's the shrine," Oscar said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "We're close enough that it—" She stopped, because she didn't have words for what it *did*, only the feeling of it, which was like walking toward a hearth in winter: warmth that promised and warned in the same breath.

Cassian, in the bow, had gone very still. He had been still more often lately, she'd noticed. A man listening to a silence he knew intimately. "Vess," he said quietly. "Goddess of thresholds and safe arrival. We sang to her at every departure, in the seminary. *Keep the door, keep the way, keep us whole between.*" His mouth twisted. "Three hundred years dead, they told us. Three hundred years of singing to a corpse."

"Maybe not a corpse," Oscar said. "Maybe just sleeping. There's a difference."

"There is," Cassian agreed. "I've been both."

Wren said nothing at all. She sat in the middle of the boat with her hands wrapped around the brand on her forearm as if cradling a small hot animal, and she watched the mist, and once, when Oscar glanced back at her, the girl was mouthing something—counting, Oscar realized. Counting under her breath. When she caught Oscar looking she stopped, embarrassed.

"What are you counting?"

"The hum," Wren said. "It goes up and down. Like breathing." She hesitated. "It got faster when we turned east."

* * *

They found the shrine on the morning of the third day, and it was exactly as drowned as its reputation promised, and nothing at all like Oscar had imagined.

She had pictured ruins—a few broken columns, a sunken altar, the usual melancholy arithmetic of fallen things. What rose out of the Greywater instead was a dome, whole and unbroken, of green-black stone furred with weed, breaking the surface like the back of some sleeping leviathan. Water poured endlessly off its crown in bright thin sheets, catching the light. And set into the dome's face, above the waterline, was a door.

Not a doorway. A door—carved stone swung open on hinges that should have rusted away centuries ago, hanging patient as a held breath, revealing a stair that descended into lit water. Not reflecting light. Emitting it. A soft gold glow came up from below, diffused through the flooded stairwell, and where the glow touched the surface of the marsh, the water cleared—not brown, not grey, but glass, down to pale stones far below.

"Safe arrival," Cassian whispered. He had taken off his hat without seeming to notice he'd done it. "She kept the door."

Brannoc tied the boat to a stunted willow with the exaggerated care of a man who did not trust the ground to stay put. "Right," he said. "Rules. Nobody touches anything shiny. Nobody drinks the glowing water. And if anything down there starts talking, nobody answers it." He looked pointedly at Wren. "*Especially* you, sprout. Things that talk to children are never up to any good."

"They usually aren't," Wren agreed solemnly, and climbed out of the boat first, because she was eleven and immune to wisdom.

* * *

The stair went down farther than the dome's size could account for, which was the second wrongness, and Oscar noted it the way she noted everything—coldly, precisely, mapping—while another part of her, a part she kept locked in a room at the back of herself, screamed softly at every step.

The water did not touch them. That was the third wrongness, and the worst. They walked down a submerged staircase, fully immersed in glowing gold, and the water parted around each of them in a trembling skin, a bubble shaped like a person, so that they moved dry-shod through the deep as if the shrine itself were holding its breath around them. Fish hung suspended beyond the barrier, watching with flat silver eyes. Light came from everywhere and nowhere.

At the bottom, the stair opened into a chamber the size of a village square, and the chamber was the altar.

There was no table, no plinth, no carved basin. The floor itself was the working: concentric rings of inlaid metal—copper, silver, and a dark red-gold Oscar recognized with a lurch in her chest, because she had seen that color only once before, in the locket of her mother's ashes, in the metal her father had refused ever to name. Ember-gold. Covenant-metal. The rings spiraled inward toward a central point where the stone rose in a low smooth mound, worn glossy, like a place where many hands had rested, or one hand had rested many times.

And everywhere—on walls, on ceiling, in the seams between stones—were carvings of Vess. Not the stern iconography Oscar knew from church fragments: not the veiled woman with the keys. These were older, warmer, stranger. Vess laughing. Vess wading hip-deep in a river of stars. Vess holding a door open with her shoulder while an endless procession of small figures—travelers, refugees, the lost—filed through. In every carving, her face was turned toward the ones coming through the door, not the ones going out.

"She wasn't a gatekeeper," Cassian said. His voice had gone rough. He stood before the largest carving with water-light sliding across his face. "The Church taught she guarded the threshold against what was outside. But look at her. She's not guarding it. She's *holding* it. Holding it open." He pressed his palm flat against the stone, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "Gods. What did we do to you?"

"You bound her," Oscar said. The words came out flat and hard, and she let them, because if she didn't keep her voice flat she was going to feel what was underneath it. "My family bound her. All of them. To stop the Sundering, the journal says. To cage the gods that had gone mad with hunger before they ate the world." She walked to the center of the chamber, to the smooth-worn mound, and stood looking down at it. "And now the cages are rusting, and everyone in them has spent three hundred years getting hungrier."

"So we're here to fix your family's cage," Brannoc said, arms crossed, planted near the stair like a man guarding the only exit. "Good. Fix it and let's go. This place gives me the prickles."

"It's not a cage-repair," Oscar said. Her heart was going hard against her ribs. She pulled off her right glove, finger by finger, and the air of the chamber seemed to lean toward her. "The journal is specific. The binding was sealed in blood at three altars—Vess, Aurel, and the third, unnamed. Renewal requires the same. Blood of the keeper's line, freely given, at each altar, before solstice." She flexed her bare hand. The scars across her knuckles—from ten years of pens and knives and cold mountain nights—looked suddenly like a rehearsal. "This is the first door."

"And the cost?" Cassian asked quietly. He had come to stand at the edge of the innermost ring, not crossing it. He knew better. Even disgraced, even drunk half his life, some knowledge lived in the bones. "Rites like this always have a cost, Oscar. The old liturgies are full of them. Blood freely given is never just blood."

"I know." She had read that page four times. *The altar takes what the giver holds dearest of the moment given,* her father had written, *and returns nothing but the work.* "I know what it might take. That's why you're all staying up there by the stairs."

"No," said Wren.

Oscar turned. The girl had come down the stair despite everything, despite Brannoc's sputtered oath, and stood at the chamber's edge with her chin up and her brand bared—glowing now, faintly, ember-orange against her skin, pulsing in time with the hum that filled the drowned hall.

"It got faster," Wren said. "When you took your glove off. It knows you're here. And—" her voice wobbled and steadied, "—and I'm not waiting up there alone with Brannoc, no offense, while you do god-magic. You saved me. I want to see."

Brannoc arrived behind her, red-faced with the effort of having failed to stop her. "I'll watch the stairs," he growled, defeated. "Somebody sensible has to."

Oscar looked at them—Cassian with his bare head bowed, Wren with her burning arm, Brannoc grumbling at the door—and felt the strange double weight she'd carried since the waystation burned: grief for the hidden life, and gratitude, unwelcome and enormous, for the people who had marched into the drowning dark beside her. *A covenant reforged by choice*, something whispered in her, in a voice that sounded like her father and nothing like him at all. *But that lesson comes later. First, the blood.*

"All right," she said. "You can watch. From the rings' edge. If anything happens—if the water moves wrong, if I say run—you take Wren and you go. Swear it."

Cassian met her eyes. Something passed between them that neither of them named, the thing that had been building since the fight in Duskhollow, since the night he'd told her about his prayers and the silence that answered. "Sworn," he said.

* * *

Kneeling at the central mound, Oscar drew her knife.

The chamber hushed. Not silent—the hum remained, that vast slow bell-note pressing against her skull—but attentive. She felt it the way she imagined a deer feels the forest attend it: every unseen thing turning its face toward her. The fish beyond the water-skin had stopped swimming. They hung in ranks, dozens of them, facing inward.

"Vess," she said aloud, and her voice rang strangely in the flooded dome, doubled and trebled, as if the water itself repeated it. "Keeper of doors. I am Oscar Dravenmoor. Last of the line that caged you. I come to renew the binding—not to strengthen your prison, but—" she faltered, because the journal's words were her father's words and she no longer trusted them, "—but to hold the door shut until a better answer can be built. Blood of the keeper's line, freely given."

She opened her palm along the old scar-line, deep enough to matter, and pressed her bleeding hand to the smooth-worn stone.

The world went gold.

Not light—gold. It came up through her knees, her spine, her teeth; it poured into the wound in her hand and did not hurt, which frightened her more than pain would have, because things that replace pain are taking something. She saw—no, she *received*—a flood of knowing: the shape of the seal, vast and intricate, a lattice laid across the skin of the world like frost on a window, and at every node a knot of captive divinity burning in its sleep. She felt the three altars as three fingers pressed against a pulse. She felt the thin places, the tears, the hungry dark seeping through. And she felt, intimate as her own heartbeat, the mechanism her ancestors had built, and understood in one vertiginous instant how to turn it.

Her blood sank into the stone. The ember-gold rings began to move.

They rotated—slowly, majestically, counter to one another, copper against silver against that deep red-gold, grinding out a note below hearing that she felt in her marrow. Along the walls, the carved processions of travelers seemed to shift, to walk, their small stone feet finding their ancient rhythm again. The hum resolved into something almost like music. Almost like a voice humming a departure-song, low and endless, the song of someone keeping a door.

"It's working," Cassian breathed. He was gripping Wren's shoulders; the girl's brand had flared to full brightness, throwing their shadows huge against the carvings. "Oscar—it's *working*—"

And then the lattice turned its attention toward her.

There was no other way to describe it. One moment she was a hand on a machine; the next, the machine was a gaze, and she was inside it. Something on the other side of the seal—something vast and patient and *awake*—became aware of the hand turning the lock, and looked at her.

She couldn't see it. That was the horror of it: she perceived it only as a direction, a pressure, an absence in the gold with a shape, the way you perceive the sun through closed eyelids. It regarded her across a distance that was not distance, through a veil grown thin as paper, and it was *interested*. Curious. Old. So old that its curiosity felt geological, the interest of a mountain in a single climber's footprint.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, from the water and the stone and the marrow of her own bones, a voice—or the memory of a voice, or the space where a voice had been worn smooth by centuries of silence:

***Dravenmoor.***

One word. Her family's name, spoken by something that had last heard it three hundred years ago, spoken with a weight that cracked two of the smaller rings clean through. The chamber shuddered. Silt bloomed in clouds through the golden water. The fish scattered like flung coins.

The presence withdrew—not gone, she understood that with sick certainty, never gone, merely *turned aside*, the way a lighthouse turns—but the word hung in the water after it, ringing, ringing.

Oscar came back to herself on her hands and knees on the altar-stone, gasping, her hand healed to a thin pink seam as if the knife had never touched it. Above her, the great rings had settled back into stillness—but changed. Brighter. Whole where they had been corroded. One thread of the vast frost-lattice across the world, taut again.

The first door, held.

"Oscar." Cassian was kneeling beside her, his hand hovering an inch from her back, afraid to land. "Talk to me. What happened? What did it say?"

She lifted her head. Through the water-skin, in the gold-lit gloom, Wren stared at her with her brand guttering slowly down to embers, and Brannoc had his axe out for all the good it would do, and the drowned shrine of Vess hummed its long note around them like a held breath.

"It said my name," Oscar whispered. "The old name. The family name." She pushed herself upright, and her legs shook, and she made them hold. "It knew me, Cassian. Whatever's behind the seal—it's been waiting three hundred years for a Dravenmoor to turn that lock again." She looked up, through the flooded stair, through forty feet of glowing water, toward a sky she could no longer see. "And now it knows I'm coming."
