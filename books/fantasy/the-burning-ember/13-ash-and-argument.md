## 13. Ash and Argument

The fire in Duskhollow's common room had been built to last the night—stacked high with bog-oak that hissed and spat resin like a thing alive—and Cassian Vale sat apart from it anyway, as he sat apart from everything now, nursing a cup of Mother Quill's bitter blackbrew and watching Oscar Dravenmoor pretend she was only a mapmaker.

She stood at the long table by lamplight, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink-stained fingers moving over a fresh sheet of vellum with the kind of precision that looked like devotion. Wren had fallen asleep an hour ago in the corner nook, curled beneath Brannoc's spare coat, and even sleeping the girl's fist stayed clenched around the hem of it as if letting go might cost her something. The ember-brand on her wrist had stopped weeping. It had not stopped glowing.

Cassian had spent eleven years learning to read people the way priests read scripture—looking for the verse beneath the verse, the meaning under the meaning. And what he read in Oscar Dravenmoor, night after night, was a woman performing a role so long and so well that she had forgotten there was an audience at all.

Except she hadn't forgotten. That was the thing. She checked the door when strangers came in. She angled her maps away from eyes that lingered too long. When the beggar-king had spoken her name in the gutter three nights past—a god's voice cracking like river ice, saying *Oscar* with a weight no cartographer's name should carry—she had gone white as bone-ash and lied about it within four breaths.

*It's a common enough name,* she'd said.

It was not. He had checked. He had spent two days checking, quietly, through the ledgers in Quill's back room, through the trade-tongues of every caravan master who passed through Duskhollow hauling salt and secrets. There were Oscars in the lowlands, sure enough—farmers, farriers, one fat customs clerk in Greyharbor—but none of them drew maps like scripture, none of them knew the Thornfell passes better than the smugglers who'd bled for them, and none of them made old gods speak their names before dissolving into coin-shadow and smoke.

He turned the blackbrew cup in his hands. The clay was chipped where his thumb worried it, a groove worn smooth by years of exactly this gesture—his confessor's habit, the one he'd never managed to bury with the rest of his vocation. Waiting for someone else's sins to surface while drowning his own.

Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something. A name, maybe. The brand pulsed once, faint as a dying coal, and settled.

Cassian set down the cup and crossed the room.

* * *

"You're holding the pen wrong."

Oscar didn't look up. "I've held it this way for ten years."

"That's how I know you're not drawing a map." He stopped at the edge of the lamplight, close enough to see the fine tremor in her hand that she would have denied existed. "You're drawing a boundary. Maps don't have walls in the middle of open country, Oscar. Boundaries do."

Her pen lifted. For a moment the lamplight caught her face full on—the sharp cheekbones, the mouth set in a line that could hold back a flood, the eyes that were doing arithmetic with exits. He'd seen that look before, years ago, on the faces of penitents deciding whether to lie to God. Most of them lied. God forgave them anyway, or pretended to, which amounted to the same economy.

"Go to bed, Cassian," she said.

"We need to talk."

"No. We need sleep, and provisions, and for you to stop looking at me like I'm a puzzle you're entitled to solve."

"Entitled." He laughed, and it came out uglier than he intended. "That's rich. You want to talk about entitlement? Let's talk about the crew. Brannoc gave up his ship for you—for *her*—" he nodded toward the sleeping girl, "—and you still haven't told him why the Maw hunts her. Why it hunts *you*. The twins are forging papers tonight on the assumption that we're running cargo, and Mother Quill is spending coin she can't spare buying silence from the harbor-watch, and not one of them knows they're ferrying the last heir of the Ember Covenant into a war between gods."

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife.

Oscar went very still. Not the stillness of guilt—he'd expected that, braced for it—but the stillness of a drawn bow. "Say that again," she said softly, "and I'll deny it so thoroughly you'll doubt your own ears."

"I already said it once. You didn't deny it."

"I'm denying it now."

"Now doesn't count. Now is after I watched you flinch at the word *heir* in Quill's ledger. Now is after I watched you bleed into a bowl behind the shrine at Vess and come back with light under your skin." He stepped into the lamplight fully, arms crossing, and hated himself for the posture even as he took it—the lectern stance, the pulpit stance, all the old architecture of judgment rising in him like water into a cellar. "You want to keep your secrets, fine. Keep them. But you're not keeping them alone anymore. You're keeping them inside other people's lives, and that's not secrecy, Oscar. That's just lying with extra steps."

Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, quick and animal, gone before it finished arriving. Her jaw tightened. "And what would you know about honesty? You sit in corners drinking Quill's swill and cataloguing everyone's failures because it's easier than facing your own. You haven't told them either, have you? What you were. What you did. The great fallen priest of the Hollow Lantern, dispensing judgment from the cheapest seat in the house."

The words found the crack in him on the first strike. They always did; she was precise the way avalanches are precise.

"My failures," he said, "never got anyone's ship burned."

"Not yet."

"*Not yet.*" His voice cracked upward and he let it, let her hear the fraying edge of it. "Gods, listen to yourself. You've already decided how this ends. Everyone burns, everyone pays, and you stand in the middle of the ash telling yourself it was necessary. That's not duty, Oscar. That's just pride wearing duty's clothes."

"And what is this?" She swept a hand between them, at the fire, at the sleeping child, at the whole smoky room. "What is standing here at midnight tearing me open in front of a sleeping girl? Concern? Or do you just need someone else's sin to be bigger than yours for one night?"

The fire popped. Somewhere above them a floorboard groaned under Brannoc's weight as the old pilot turned in his bunk. Wren slept on, mercifully, her breath a small steady tide.

"I prayed," Cassian said.

The non sequitur hung there. Oscar blinked.

"For six years, before the Lantern fell, I prayed every morning at first light and every night at last. Proper prayers. The liturgy, the forms, the whole architecture of it. I prayed for my congregation, for rain, for the sick, for the war in the south to end. And then one night—" his throat worked, "—one night a fever came through my parish. Took forty-one people in a month. Forty-one, Oscar. I buried them myself when there weren't enough hands. And I prayed harder than I had ever prayed in my life. I knelt until my knees bled through my trousers. I offered Him anything. Everything. My ordination, my voice, my name. Take me instead. Take *anyone*. Just give me one miracle, one, and I will spend the rest of my life paying for it."

The firelight moved on his face. He watched her watch him, and saw the anger in her guttering into something more dangerous: attention.

"Nothing answered," he said. "Three centuries of silence, the Church called it. The Long Quiet. We were taught it was a test of faith—that the gods had withdrawn to some higher purpose and our prayers were seeds planted in winter. Beautiful doctrine. Very comforting. And then a dead god answers a *child's* prayer with black rain, and a beggar-king crawls out of a gutter to say your name, and I find out the silence wasn't a test at all. It was a *binding*. Your family put a lock on heaven and threw away the key, and every prayer I ever sent died against that lock, and forty-one people—" his voice failed, steadied, failed again, "—forty-one people might be alive if your ancestors hadn't decided the world needed saving from its own gods."

The silence afterward was enormous. The fire ate its oak. Outside, wind worried the eaves of Duskhollow, and somewhere down the ravine the Greywater muttered over its stones.

Oscar set down her pen. Carefully. The way one sets down a weapon one has decided not to use.

"You think I don't know that?" she said, and her voice had changed—lower, rougher, stripped of the performance. "You think I learned about the covenant from a journal and felt nothing? I learned it three weeks ago, Cassian. Three weeks ago I was a mapmaker. I sold charts to merchants and memorized smuggler roads and told myself I was free. Then my father's journal told me my whole bloodline was a prison wall, and every soul who ever prayed into that silence was praying at a grave my family dug." She laughed, short and joyless. "You think your forty-one haunt you? I did the arithmetic the first night. Three hundred years. Every drought, every plague, every fevered child whose mother begged the sky for mercy. All of it unanswered because a Dravenmoor decided mortals couldn't be trusted with miracles. I carry all of them. You carry forty-one. Do not stand there and tell me about weight."

"So tell them." He gestured at the room—at Brannoc's ceiling, at the twins' lamp burning late in the loft, at Quill's office with its ledger-light still on. "Tell the crew. Tell them what's coming for us. Tell them why the Maw wears the faces of the eaten and why it follows a nine-year-old girl across half a mountain range. They've earned the truth. *I've* earned—"

"What? What have you earned, Cassian?" She was close now, closer than she'd been since Vess, and he could smell the ink and woodsmoke and the iron tang that clung to her since the first rite. "You want honesty as payment? Fine. Here's mine: I don't tell them because the moment I say the words out loud, they become real. The moment I say *heir*, I'm not a mapmaker anymore. I'm the last of a line of jailers, and everything I love becomes either a weapon pointed at me or a hostage held against me. Locke already burned my archive. She already took the only life I built. If I name what I am to these people—if I make them *mine*—then I'm handing the Inquisition a list of everyone I cannot bear to lose." Her breath shuddered. "So yes. I lie. I lie because loving people is how you kill them, and I have decided, selfishly, to love them anyway, and I am trying—*trying*—to keep that from being a death sentence."

The words rang in the rafters and slowly faded.

Cassian found he had no sermon left. It was a strange sensation, the absence—the old machinery of response, the comforting cascade of doctrine and correction, simply gone quiet. What remained was smaller and much older: a man looking at a woman who was afraid of exactly what he was afraid of, and both of them too proud to say so in plain language.

"They're going to find out anyway," he said finally. Quietly. "Locke. The Church. The gods themselves already know. Secrets like yours don't keep, Oscar—they metastasize. Better they hear it from you by firelight than from an Inquisitor's blade."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Yes, I know. I just need—I need a little more time where I'm still just the person who draws the maps. Is that so unforgivable?"

"Yes," he said, and then, because the truth had a momentum now and he was too tired to fight it: "No. Gods help me, no. It's the most forgivable thing I've heard in eleven years."

She lowered her hands. Looked at him. In the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious about being wet, and there was a rawness in her face that no map had ever shown, and Cassian understood with sudden terrible clarity that he was in trouble—not the kind that bled, the kind that stayed.

"Why do you care?" she asked. "Truly. You've known me two months. You owe me nothing but passage and a share of whatever's in the strongbox. Why does my lying keep you up at night?"

Because you pray like I used to pray. Because you flinch at altars. Because when you think no one is watching, you touch the scar on your palm the way I used to touch my collar, checking that the thing that defined you is still there. Because I watched you feed Wren broth spoonful by spoonful for three nights without once making the child feel like a burden, and I thought: *there but for the grace of a god who wasn't listening.*

"Because I recognize the architecture," he said aloud. "The walls. I built the same ones. Mine had stained glass."

A sound escaped her—half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. The rest is—" He stopped. Started again. "The rest is that when you walked into Quill's den with that girl in your arms, bleeding and half-frozen and breaking every rule of survival you claim to live by, I thought: *there's a person who lies to herself worse than she lies to anyone else.* And I have been unable to look away since. It's not concern. It's not duty. I don't have a clean word for it, and I resent that profoundly."

The distance between them had shrunk to the width of a decision. He could see the pulse in her throat, quick and uneven. The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and the shadows leaned in around them like conspirators.

"This is a mistake," she said.

"Almost certainly."

"We're running for our lives. There's an Inquisitor behind us and a hungry god ahead of us and a child asleep ten feet away wearing a brand that—"

"—that we don't understand, yes, I'm aware of the tactical situation, I was a priest, not an idiot." His hand had risen without his permission and hovered near her jaw, not touching, asking a question his mouth wouldn't form. "Tell me to stop."

"Don't stop," she whispered, and then, anguished: "*Don't stop.*"

He kissed her, or she kissed him—the geometry of it dissolved instantly into mutual ruin—and it was nothing like peace. It was argument continued by other means, grief and fury and relief all tangled together, two guarded people finding the single unguarded place in each other and pressing there like thumbs on a wound. Her hand fisted in his collar. His found the back of her neck. The fire ticked. The wind sang in the eaves. Somewhere in the dark beyond the windows, far off in the high passes, thunder rolled where no storm should be—a deep, rhythmic sound, almost like breathing.

They broke apart gasping, foreheads together, her breath ragged against his mouth.

"That changes nothing," she said.

"Everything," he corrected. "But we can argue about which later. I'm told arguing is something we do well."

Despite herself—despite everything—she laughed, wet and broken and real, and hid her face against his shoulder, and he held her among the ashes of both their certainties and thought: *this is what I broke my vows for, if I'm honest. Not pleasure. This. Being known.*

Then her body went rigid against him.

"Cassian." Her voice had gone flat and cold. "The thunder."

He listened. The sound came again, rolling down from the high country north of Duskhollow—too regular, too deep, shaking grit from the rafters. Wren woke with a small sharp cry, sitting bolt upright, and the brand on her wrist flared bright enough to throw her shadow huge against the wall.

"She's awake," Wren whispered, staring at the window, at the darkness beyond. "The hungry one. She smells the altar-blood. She smells *us*."

Outside, the night split open with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and every dog in Duskhollow began to scream.
