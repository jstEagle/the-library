## 14. The God That Hungers

Wren had learned to sleep with one ear open.

It was a habit from the village, from the weeks after the black rain, when every knock at the door might be a neighbor with a torch or a neighbor with a question she couldn't answer. In Duskhollow the habit had softened—Mother Quill's den was warm and loud and full of people who laughed too much to be dangerous—but it never quite left her. So when the brand woke her in the small hours, she was awake enough to feel it happen: a slow unfurling of heat beneath the skin of her forearm, like a coal breathing under ash.

She sat up in the narrow bunk Ilsa had rigged for her beside the drying racks, and pressed her palm over the mark. The ember-shape pulsed once. Twice. Like something with a heartbeat on the other side of her skin, knocking.

*Not now,* she thought at it, the way you'd think at weather. *Please not now.*

The brand did not answer. It never answered. But it burned a little brighter, the way a dog lifts its head before the storm arrives, and Wren lay back down and stared at the rafters and listened to the den breathe around her—the twins' muffled bickering through the wall, Brannoc's snore like a saw working green wood—and told herself that Thornfell was full of old fires under old stones, and that a warm arm in a warm room meant nothing at all.

She almost believed it by the time she slept again.

* * *

They came down out of the hills two days later, following the Greywater road toward the mining town of Colderhatch, because Mother Quill needed salt and Brannoc needed a new rudder-pin and everyone needed to stop looking at each other across the same four walls. Wren rode in the cart between barrels, her knees drawn up, watching the mountains do what they always did—stand there, patient and enormous, keeping their own counsel.

The brand had been quiet since that night. Quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

"You're doing the face," Petra said from the driver's bench. She didn't look back; she never needed to. "The one where you're counting all the bad things."

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting." Petra flicked the reins over the mule's broad back. "Ilsa counts with her fingers. You count with your eyebrows. Same thing."

Wren tried to smooth her forehead and failed. The truth was she'd been counting. Three days since the ember woke. Three days since Cassian had gone very still over his tea and asked her, casually, far too casually, whether she'd dreamed anything lately. Three days since Oscar Dravenmoor had looked at her map-work for a long moment and then folded it away without a word, which from Oscar was louder than shouting.

Ahead, the road bent around a shoulder of grey rock, and Colderhatch came into view below—a scatter of slate roofs in a fold of the valley, smoke rising from the mine-heads, the headframe standing against the sky like a gallows that had decided to be useful instead.

Except the smoke was wrong.

Wren saw it before anyone else, because she had spent a season learning what smoke could tell you. Hearth-smoke rose thin and blue-grey and honest. Forge-smoke was darker, greasier. The columns drifting up from Colderhatch were pale and sluggish and *low*, hugging the rooftops as if they were afraid of the open air, and they moved against the wind.

"Petra," Wren said.

"I see it." Petra stood up on the bench, shading her eyes. Then, over her shoulder, sharp as a whip-crack: "Brannoc! Get up here!"

The cart stopped. They gathered at the bend in the road—Brannoc with his one eye narrowed, Petra with her hand already inside her coat where she kept the knife she pretended she didn't have, Wren with both arms wrapped around herself because the brand had begun, very softly, to sing.

Not burn. Sing. A thread of heat running up from her wrist into the meat of her thumb, the way a plucked string hums after the note is gone. And underneath the heat, fainter than memory, a pulling. As if somewhere ahead of her something had turned its attention around, the way you turn your head when someone says your name across a crowded market.

"It wants me to look," she whispered.

Nobody asked her what she meant. That was the strange mercy of the strays—they had learned, each of them, which silences to respect.

"Town's not burning," Brannoc said at last. "Smoke's wrong, but nothing's alight. Could be the mine venting."

"Mines don't vent uphill," Petra said.

They went down anyway, because that was the other thing about the strays: they went toward trouble the way water goes downhill, complaining the entire time. Mother Quill had a rule about it—*you don't leave a town wondering*—and though Quill had stayed behind in Duskhollow, her rules traveled with them like fleas.

* * *

Colderhatch was empty the way a shed skin is empty.

That was the thought Wren kept circling and failing to land, walking the high street with her boots making the loudest sound in the world. Doors stood open. Meals sat on tables, gone cold and grey. A cradle rocked gently by a hearth where no fire burned, though the coals in the grate were still warm when Petra touched them—one hour, maybe two. Laundry hung on lines between houses, shirtsleeves turning slowly in the wind like men waving goodbye.

"No blood," Brannoc said. He'd taken the lead without discussion; he always did when things got bad, as if his missing eye made him the one entitled to see the worst of it. "No signs of a fight. No tracks leaving, neither. Ground's soft enough to hold 'em and there's nothing but bird-marks."

"People don't just—" Petra started, and stopped, because everyone in that street was thinking the same word and nobody wanted to say it.

*Eaten.*

Wren said it. Inside her own head, only inside, but the brand flared at the shape of the thought like a dog hearing its name, and she gasped and clutched her arm and Brannoc was beside her in two strides.

"It knows we're talking about it," she managed. "I think—I think it can hear me. Through this." She held out her arm. Beneath the skin the ember-mark glowed now, actually glowed, a dull orange light like a lantern behind cloth, and the sight of it made her stomach drop through the floor of the world. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I led us—"

"You didn't lead us anywhere," Brannoc said. "We walked ourselves down this road with our eyes open. Difference matters." But he'd put himself between her and the end of the street as he said it, and Wren understood that the difference mattered less than he claimed.

Because at the end of the street, outside the mine-head, people were coming out of the dark.

* * *

There were perhaps thirty of them, and Wren knew even before her mind finished objecting that they were wrong. They walked like people walk when they've forgotten how—too smooth, no wasted motion, feet finding the ground without ever seeming to search for it. Their clothes were miners' clothes, patched and slate-dusted. Their faces—

Their faces were fine. That was the horror of it. Whole, unmarked, ordinary. A heavyset woman with flour still on her forearms. An old man with a miner's lamp hooked on his belt, unlit. A boy of maybe twelve with a smudge of soot on one cheek, exactly the kind of boy who'd been climbing where he shouldn't.

They stood in a loose half-circle at the mouth of the adit and looked at the three strangers in the street, and they smiled, all together, all the same smile, and Wren felt the brand go from singing to screaming.

"Hello," said the woman with flour on her arms, in a voice like a well—deep, and echoing, and with something moving at the bottom of it. "You brought the little key."

"We're just passing through," Brannoc said, steady as bedrock, and Wren loved him for it. "Cart's on the road above. We'll be on our way."

"The little key," the woman said again, ignoring him entirely, and thirty heads tilted at once, thirty necks bending the same degree, and Wren's skin crawled off her body and kept crawling without her. "Three hundred years we starved behind the door. Three hundred years of thin gruel and thinner light. And then someone *prayed*, little key. Someone opened their heart like a window, and we remembered the taste of being wanted." The smile widened past the limits of the woman's face—not tearing, just *wrong*, the way a reflection is wrong. "And now the door stands ajar, and everything behind it is hungry, and here you come walking down our road wearing the lock on your arm."

"That's enough." Petra's voice, hard and flat. She'd moved to Wren's other side, knife low against her thigh. "Whatever you are, you're wearing dead people, and that's a debt you'll answer for."

"Wearing?" The old man with the lamp spoke now, and he laughed, and the laugh came out of all thirty mouths at once, a chord of amusement with nothing human in its harmony. "Child, we *are* them. We ate them so thoroughly there's nothing left over. No ghost, no grief, no grave to visit. We wore their faces home to their neighbors first, you know. Knocked on doors. Said *come quick*. Such trusting faces." He patted his own cheek fondly, with the woman's hand. "Faces are just doors too. Everything is doors. And you—" the heads swung back to Wren, all of them, the whole half-circle aligning on her like compass needles finding north, "—you are the finest door we have ever seen."

The brand detonated.

Wren went to her knees. Fire ran from her wrist to her elbow to her shoulder, and through the pain she felt the *pull* again, vast now, irresistible, a hook set behind her sternum reeling in. The Maw wasn't calling her. It was *coming*, gathering itself out of thirty borrowed bodies and the dark of the mine behind them, and it was using her mark as the road.

"Run," she heard herself say. "Run, it's using me, it's using me to find—"

Brannoc hauled her up by the collar like a sack of meal and ran.

* * *

Later, Wren would piece the flight together from fragments: the street tilting past, Petra screaming at the mule, the cart lurching up the switchback road with the whole town's worth of smiling faces flowing after them—not running, *flowing*, pouring along the ground like spilled mercury, fast as fear. She would remember Brannoc cutting the mule loose rather than let it slow them, and Petra swearing at him with real feeling while she did exactly the same thing on the other strap. She would remember the moment the faces stopped.

At the edge of the town boundary, where the last slate roof gave way to gorse and scree, the tide of bodies simply halted, all thirty of them, mid-stride, as if they'd reached the end of a leash. And they stood there in the fading light and watched the cart climb, and they did not stop smiling, and the last thing Wren saw of Colderhatch was the boy with the soot-smudged cheek raising one small hand in a wave.

Then the brand went dark, all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the silence it left behind was worse than the burning.

* * *

They made six miles before Brannoc would allow a halt, and then only because the mule-track narrowed and the cart couldn't turn and night was coming down black and moonless. He built no fire. They sat against the cart's wheels with their knees up, three people pretending to be calm, and the mountain breathed around them.

"They knew my name," Wren said finally. Her voice came out small. "Not my name-name. What I am. The key. How?"

"Doesn't matter how," Petra said.

"It matters to *me*."

Petra was quiet a moment. In the dark, Wren heard her sheath the knife, the small oiled click of it. "Then here's what matters more," she said. "It stopped at the town line. Things that hungry don't stop unless they have to. Which means it can't reach you yet—or it doesn't want to show us what reaching you looks like. Either way, we've got time to think, and thinking starts with getting you to Oscar. Tonight."

"She'll know what it is," Brannoc rumbled. "Or that priest will. God-botherers know god-things." He shifted, and his eye found Wren in the darkness, and his voice gentled into something she'd rarely heard from him. "You did nothing wrong, girl. Whatever's written on your arm, you didn't write it."

But that wasn't true, and they all knew it, and the knowing sat among them like a fourth passenger. Because Wren *had* prayed. Wren had knelt in the dust behind her father's house and begged a dead god for rain with her whole heart, and something had answered, and everything since—the rain that whispered names, the village, the torches, the pass, the brand—everything was fruit of that one seed. She had opened the window. The Maw had said so itself, in a dead woman's voice, and it had thanked her.

"I want it cut off," she said suddenly. "The arm. I want—"

"You want to sit still and stop being foolish," Petra said. "Cutting bits off children is how we lose arguments. We keep the arm. We keep the child. We get answers." She stood, brushing off her skirts, brisk as morning. "Brannoc, take first watch. Wren—come here."

Wren came. And Petra, who forged documents and lied beautifully and trusted no one further than she could throw them, wrapped her thin arms around Wren and held her against a coat that smelled of ink and woodsmoke, and said nothing at all, which was somehow the exact right thing.

Over Petra's shoulder, Wren watched the dark mass of Thornfell against the stars, and the brand lay quiet on her arm, and she understood—with the terrible clarity that comes after horror—that quiet was not the same as gone. Somewhere behind them, in an empty town full of cold meals and rocking cradles, something vast was waiting at the end of its leash. And it had called her *key*.

It took them until dawn to reach Duskhollow, and when they stumbled into the den, gray-faced and hollow-eyed, Oscar Dravenmoor took one look at Wren's arm and went white as paper.

"What happened?" Oscar demanded. "Wren. Look at me. What did you see?"

And Wren, shivering, said the only thing that mattered:

"It's coming for me. And it said—it said I was the lock. Oscar, it said the door was already open."
