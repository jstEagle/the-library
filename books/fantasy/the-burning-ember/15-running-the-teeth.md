## 15. Running the Teeth

The Maw did not run. That was the worst of it.

Oscar had imagined pursuit as a thing with legs—hounds, riders, the pounding rhythm of boots behind her. She had spent ten years planning escapes from men, and men could be outlasted, outclimbed, out-thought. But the thing that had eaten Hollowmere came down the valley like weather. It wore the town's faces when it moved: she had seen them in its bulk, half-formed and screaming, the miner with his lamp still lit inside his chest, the baker woman whose mouth opened where her eye should have been. It did not need to be fast. The mountain itself seemed to lean toward it, stone softening, trees bowing their heads like mourners, and everything that leaned too far simply *went*—swallowed without sound, without even the dignity of a scream.

"Left fork," Oscar said. Her voice came out flat and steady, which surprised her, because inside she was nothing but white noise. "Take the left fork."

Brannoc took the left fork.

The smuggler road was not a road at all by daylight standards—a shelf of rock cut into the mountainside, wide enough for one cart if the cart's driver had made peace with God. Below them the Greywater gorge dropped away in ribbons of mist; above, the pines stood black and dense as a congregation. The mule train plodded on, breath steaming, and behind them—far below now, back along the river flats—the air had begun to shimmer the way heat shimmers over a forge.

It was coming up the valley. Eating as it came.

"How far to the Teeth?" Cassian rode close, one hand on Wren's shoulder where she sat pressed against him on the pack-mule. The girl hadn't spoken since Hollowmere. Her brand glowed through her sleeve like a coal wrapped in cloth, and every few minutes her whole body flinched, as though something were calling her name from very far away.

"The narrows are two hours at this pace." Oscar didn't look back. Looking back was a luxury. "Less if we ditch the cargo."

"We're not ditching the cargo." Brannoc said it mildly, the way he said most things, but his one eye was fixed forward and his knuckles were white on the reins. "That cargo is three months' work."

"That cargo is going to get us eaten."

"It's *my* cargo, map-witch."

She turned then, and what she saw in his face stopped the argument in her throat. Not stubbornness. Something older and sadder. The *Kestrel's Grace* was moored at the Greywater landing they'd abandoned an hour ago—the flat-bottomed river barge he'd piloted for twenty years, patched and re-patched, painted with faded mermaids that Ilsa used to mock and Petra secretly loved. Every plank of it was Brannoc's history. And it was sitting at the landing directly in the path of the thing behind them.

He knew. Of course he knew. He'd known since the first scout came back pale and babbling, and he'd said nothing, because saying it would make it real, and because a captain does not abandon ship until there is no other choice.

"We'll come back for it," Petra said quietly, riding up beside him. "When it passes. When we know more."

"Aye," Brannoc said. "We'll come back for it."

Nobody believed it. Everybody let him have the lie anyway. That, Oscar thought, was the moment she understood what a crew was—not the oaths or the articles or the split of shares, but the collective agreement to carry each other's lies when the truth was too heavy.

* * *

The unmapped ways began at a cairn no honest surveyor would ever record.

Oscar had found it seven years ago, following a rumor from a dying smuggler who'd traded it to her for a bottle of plum brandy and someone to listen to him. The cairn looked like a hundred others scattered through Thornfell, raised by pilgrims or herdsmen or simple vanity. But behind it, screened by a curtain of birch grown thick as a wall, a goat track descended into the gorge itself—down switchbacks so steep the mules had to be led blindfolded, down past a waterfall frozen mid-plunge into a lace of blue ice, down to a ledge that ran beneath the roots of the mountain.

No map held it. She had never drawn it. In ten years of selling cartography to merchants and magistrates, she had kept perhaps forty roads like this one locked in her skull alone, and she had told herself it was caution, professionalism, the cartographer's rule: *what keeps you alive stays off the page.*

Riding it now with the Maw's silence pressing against her spine, she understood for the first time that it had never been caution. It had been preparation. Some part of her—the part that was Dravenmoor blood and ember-brand inheritance whether she wanted it or not—had always known she would need roads that existed nowhere but in her own head.

"Where does this go?" Cassian asked. He'd given Wren his cloak against the spray of the falls, and the girl rode huddled in it like a bird in a fist.

"Nowhere," Oscar said. "That's the point. It goes under the world and comes out three miles east, and there's nothing there worth mapping, which means nobody's ever looked for us here."

"And the thing behind us?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. They all felt it—the pressure of it, like a thumb pressed against the base of the skull. Wren whimpered once, small and terrible, and the brand flared bright enough to light the tunnel-mouth ahead of them, and Oscar felt the answering pull in her own chest, the ember-heritage turning over in its sleep like a dog scenting another dog.

*It knows what you carry,* whispered something that might have been memory or might have been the mountain. *It knows what she carries. It will follow the taste of you both to the end of the world.*

"Move," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on it. "Everyone move *now.*"

* * *

They ran the under-road for six hours.

It was a strange, airless passage—stone overhead close enough to touch, water seeping through seams in silver threads, the only light the lanterns Ilsa carried and the sick glow of Wren's brand. The twins took turns at the front with Oscar, reading the old waymarkers she'd memorized: a notch cut in the rock shaped like a fishhook, a seam of red quartz that meant the ceiling would drop, a hollow where smugglers a century dead had scratched tally marks counting cargoes long since sold or seized. Once they passed a skeleton curled against the wall with a rusted lantern beside it, and Petra crossed herself with a gesture that wasn't quite the Church's rite, and nobody spoke.

The Maw did not enter the tunnels. Oscar began, somewhere around the fourth hour, to hope.

Then the hope died all at once, the way a candle dies.

Wren screamed.

Not a child's scream—an alarm, involuntary, the body sounding a bell the mind couldn't stop. The brand burned white through the cloak, and the stone around them *shivered*, dust sifting down, and Oscar felt it through her boot-soles: a vast, patient weight settling onto the mountain above them, spreading, searching. The Maw had reached the ridge. It was pouring itself across the surface of the earth like spilled ink, filling every crack, and the cracks led down.

"It can feel her," Cassian said. His arm was around Wren, his lips near her ear, murmuring something—old liturgy, maybe, or just nonsense syllables, anything to give the girl a human voice to hold onto. "Every time it gets close, the brand calls to it. We're dragging a beacon through the dark."

"Then we go faster." Oscar was already moving, hauling the lead mule by its halter, calculating distances she'd measured years ago in idle hours that suddenly weren't idle anymore. "There's a junction ahead. Left goes to the eastern outlet. Right—" She hesitated.

"Right goes where?"

"Right goes down. Old mine workings. Flooded, mostly. There's a sump channel that runs under the Greywater and comes up in a sea cave on the far bank." She heard how it sounded and pushed on before anyone could object. "If the Maw follows the brand, we split the trail. Someone takes Wren down the right-hand way, fast, wet, and miserable. The rest of us go loud and bright toward the eastern outlet and give it something else to chase."

"No," said four voices at once.

"Yes," Oscar snapped. "Because the alternative is that it eats all of us in a dead-end tunnel, and I did not spend ten years learning these rocks to die in one."

"It's not happening," Brannoc said. His voice had gone quiet, which those who knew him understood was worse than shouting. "We don't split. We don't leave anyone. That's not how this crew works."

"You'd rather we all die together? Very noble. Very stupid."

"I'd rather," Brannoc said, "you stop talking about my people like they're ballast."

The word landed between them and sat there. *My people.* Not crew. Not hands. People. Oscar opened her mouth to argue and found, to her fury, that her eyes were stinging, and that the argument had gone out of her like air from a punctured lung.

"There's another way," Ilsa said.

They all turned. She stood at the junction with her lantern raised, and her twin face—Petra's face, the face they shared like a coat worn on alternate days—was grim.

"The eastern outlet opens onto the scree slope above the Greywater landing," Ilsa said. "Above the *Grace*."

Silence. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

"She'll draw it straight to the ship," Petra said softly.

"She'll draw it past the ship," Ilsa corrected. "And a barge makes noise, sister. A barge full of oil and powder makes a great deal of noise."

"No," Brannoc said again. Just that. Just the word, flat as a coffin lid.

"Captain." Ilsa's voice gentled. "It's going to take her anyway. You know that. The question is whether it takes her for free, or whether she buys us the hours we need to put a gorge between it and that child."

Brannoc looked at his ship the way other men look at graves. Then he looked at Wren—small, shaking, nine years old and carrying a god's signature burned into her skin—and something in his weathered face closed like a door.

"Get her to the water," he said.

* * *

What followed, Oscar would remember afterward in fragments, the way trauma shatters time into shards.

Ilsa and Petra running the mules hard for the eastern outlet, torches blazing, shouting and singing at the top of their lungs to make themselves a feast of noise. The Maw's attention swinging toward them like a compass needle—Oscar felt the pressure lift from her own skull and nearly wept with relief. The scramble down the sump channel, waist-deep in freezing water, Cassian holding Wren above the flood with arms that shook but did not fail. The sea cave, black and roaring, the Greywater's mouth breathing cold salt-and-stone wind. And above them, on the scree slope, the world turning orange.

The *Kestrel's Grace* burned like a pyre.

Brannoc had rigged her himself in the last hour—Oscar had watched him do it, moving through the familiar motions of loading cargo, except the cargo was every barrel of lamp-oil they carried and the powder stores besides, and his hands never trembled once. When the Maw came down the slope wearing fifty stolen faces, drawn by the twins' racket, the barge had been waiting at the landing like bait on a hook. Brannoc touched a taper to the fuse line, stepped back, and took off his hat.

The explosion lifted the front third of the Maw into the air.

It screamed with a hundred mouths. It recoiled, boiling backward up the slope, its borrowed faces flickering and dissolving, and for one impossible moment the night was bright as noon and Oscar saw the thing entire—a cathedral of hunger, a landslide given appetite—and then the burning wreck of the *Grace* settled into the shallows, and the light went out of the world, and the Maw withdrew into the dark hills to lick its wounds and hate.

Brannoc watched until the last ember died on the water.

Then he put his hat back on, wiped his face with a sleeve—it might have been spray, it might not have—and said, "Well. She always said she'd go out spectacular."

Nobody laughed. Nobody cried either, though it was a near thing on several counts. Instead Petra put her hand on his shoulder, and Ilsa took his other one, and the four of them stood in the cave mouth in the cold while the river ran black past the bones of his ship, and Oscar understood that she was watching something being built that no covenant, no altar, no god had ever made: a family, forged in the exact shape of a loss.

"I'm sorry," she said. It was inadequate. She said it anyway.

Brannoc shrugged. "You'll owe me a ship, map-witch. Interest accruing daily." He turned, and his one eye caught the faint glow of Wren's brand, and softened. "Come on, little ember. Let's get you warm. Can't have the bait catching cold."

* * *

They made camp at dawn in a hollow above the far bank, screened by thorn scrub, with no fire because fire drew eyes and the Maw was not the only hunter in these mountains. Locke was out there too. Somewhere behind them, patient as winter, the Inquisitors followed the same trail of rumor and ruin.

Cassian found Oscar standing watch at the hollow's edge, watching the sun come up over the peaks—the peaks she had mapped, peak by peak, for ten years, and which had turned out to be hiding a god-eater in their roots and a destiny in her blood.

"You saved them," he said.

"I got a man's ship burned and a child marked for death. If that's your definition of saving—"

"That's exactly my definition." He came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Almost. "I spent eleven years as a priest, Oscar. Do you know how many people I saved? None. I recited beautiful words over a machine designed to grind them down, and I called it faith. You've known this crew for a season, and they'd walk into fire for you. That's not failure. That's the only arithmetic that matters."

She wanted to tell him. Standing there in the grey dawn light with exhaustion stripping away her defenses layer by layer, she wanted to say *my name isn't Oscar Dravenmoor the mapmaker, it's the last heir of the Ember Covenant, and the gods are waking because of my blood, and I am terrified.* The words climbed her throat like climbers on a rope.

Below the hollow, Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured, and the brand pulsed once—twice—

And went dark.

Not dim. Dark. Extinguished, like a snuffed candle, for the first time since the village of Ashford. Oscar was moving before she'd decided to move, sliding down the slope, kneeling beside the girl. Wren's eyes flew open. They were clear. Clearer than they'd been in weeks.

"It stopped calling me," Wren whispered. She sat up, clutching Oscar's sleeve with both hands. "The hungry one. It stopped. But—" Her brow furrowed, and she looked east, toward the high passes, toward the drowned shrine of Vess and everything beyond it. "Something else is calling instead. Something old. It says—" She frowned harder, listening to a voice no one else could hear. "It says *the heir runs true. Come home.*"

Oscar's blood turned to ice water.

Behind her, Cassian had gone very still. "Wren," he said carefully. "Who's speaking? What name does it give?"

The girl looked up at Oscar, and in the dawn light her eyes held a reflection of something that was not the sunrise.

"It doesn't give a name," she said. "It gives yours."
