## 16. Interrogation by Firelight

The cell smelled of wet stone and old blood, and Seraphine Locke found both comforting in their honesty. A room like this never pretended to be anything other than what it was.

She stood at the barred door for a long moment before entering, watching the old woman by the light of the single lantern her soldiers had left burning on its iron hook. Mother Quill sat on the cot with her hands folded in her lap as though she were waiting for a tea service rather than an interrogation. She was smaller than Seraphine had expected—most dangerous people were—and her silver hair was braided back with the kind of severe practicality that spoke of a woman who had never once wasted a moment on vanity. Her knuckles were swollen with age and work. There was a smudge of ink on her left wrist that she had not bothered to wash away.

They had taken her at the river crossing outside Duskhollow, three days after the burning of the waystation. She had not run. That was the first thing Seraphine had noted in her ledger: *the subject did not run.* Most smugglers ran. Most smugglers begged. This one had looked at the Inquisitors surrounding her cart, sighed like a grandmother delayed by weather, and said, "Well. You'll want to make yourselves comfortable, then. I don't come cheap."

Now she looked up at Seraphine through the bars, and her eyes—pale grey, sharp as awls—took the measure of her captor in a single unhurried sweep.

"You're younger than I expected," Quill said. "They usually send someone with more scars to do this sort of work."

Seraphine pushed the door open and stepped inside, letting it swing shut behind her. "Scars are evidence of error. I try not to accumulate them."

"Mm." The old woman's mouth twitched. "And how's that working out for you?"

* * *

Seraphine had prepared for this interrogation the way she prepared for everything: exhaustively, methodically, with every contingency mapped and every question sequenced so that each answer would open the door to the next. She had read the captured smuggler's confession twice—the one who had spoken of a map-witch in Thornfell, of a woman who sold routes no honest cartographer should know. She had cross-referenced the name Dravenmoor against parish records four centuries dead. She knew, now, what walked those mountains wearing a merchant's coat.

But Mother Quill was not a question about maps. Mother Quill was a question about people.

"Your crew," Seraphine began, drawing the single stool from the corner and sitting at a careful distance—not too close to threaten, not so far as to seem afraid. "Brannoc. The pilot. One eye, bad temper, sentimental about his ship. The twins—Ilsa and Petra—forgers, good ones, by the quality of the documents we've seized. And the girl. Wren."

At the girl's name, something moved behind the old woman's eyes. It was small—a flicker, a tightening—but Seraphine had spent eleven years learning to read faces the way scholars read scripture, and she did not miss it.

"The girl is sick," Quill said flatly. "Whatever you think she is, she isn't. She's a foundling we took in off the salt road because leaving children to die on mountainsides is what separates us from crows."

"She carries a brand shaped like an ember that will not heal."

"She carries a burn from a cooking fire. Children are clumsy."

"Inquisitor Voss examined the wound before he reported to me. He described scarring in the shape of a stylized flame, ringed by script in no living alphabet, raised above the skin as though pressed there from within." Seraphine let the words sit in the damp air between them. "I have read the Church's oldest texts on god-touched marks, Mother Quill. I know what they look like. And I know what it means when one appears on a child in the same season that dead gods begin answering prayers."

The old woman was silent for a moment. Then she laughed—a dry, rusted sound, genuine in its contempt.

"You say 'dead gods' like it's a comfort. Like the world was better with them sleeping." She leaned forward slightly, chains clinking soft against the cot frame. "Tell me, Inquisitor. When your Church burned the shrines three hundred years ago—when your blessed founders put out every hearth-fire of the old faith and called it salvation—did anyone ask what the gods were *for?*"

"They were for nothing," Seraphine said evenly. "That is precisely the point. They were hunger wearing crowns. They fed on worship and gave back madness. The Sundering happened because men tried to hold power that was never meant for mortal hands—"

"No." Quill's voice cut through hers like a blade through wet canvas. "The Sundering happened because *your* founders wanted what the gods had, and couldn't take it, so they broke it instead. I've run cargo through these mountains for fifty years, girl. I've heard things in smuggler dens that would curdle your pretty certainty. The Church didn't seal the covenant to save the world." Her pale eyes fixed on Seraphine's with terrible steadiness. "It sealed it to keep the rest of us from ever touching what it couldn't have."

"That is heresy."

"That is *bookkeeping.*" The old woman smiled, showing teeth worn yellow with age. "Fifty years of ledgers, Inquisitor. I know a falsified account when I see one. And your whole Church—it's built on a lie someone cooked the books to hide."

* * *

Seraphine changed tactics.

This was the discipline her superiors had never understood about her—that conviction alone was a blunt instrument, and blunt instruments shattered against certain kinds of stone. Mother Quill was stone. So Seraphine set the stool aside, rose, and did something no Inquisitor was supposed to do in an interrogation cell.

She poured two cups of wine from the flask she'd brought, and handed one through the bars of the cot's ankle-chain to the prisoner.

Quill looked at the cup. Looked at her. "Poisoned?"

"If I wanted you dead, I'd have saved the wine for myself."

A dry chuckle. The old woman drank.

"I'm going to offer you mercy," Seraphine said. "Not the word—the thing itself. You are past seventy. You have spent your life outside the law, but I have found no record—no *record*, Mother Quill, and my records go deep—of you ever spilling innocent blood. You ran cargo, forged papers, moved refugees when it suited you and contraband when it paid. The Crown calls that crime. I call it survivable." She sat again, cup balanced on her knee. "Give me the girl. Give me the map-witch. Tell me where they're going, and you will walk out of this cell. Not into a cell of your own. Not into exile. *Out.* With a pardon under seal, and enough coin to die warm."

For a long moment, the only sound was water dripping somewhere in the dark beyond the lantern's reach.

Then Mother Quill set down her cup, folded her swollen hands in her lap again, and looked at Seraphine with an expression the Inquisitor had never seen on a prisoner's face in eleven years of service.

Pity.

"Oh, child," the old woman said softly. "You really do believe all of it, don't you? Every word. The seal, the heresy, the mercy of the Crown." She shook her head slowly. "You came in here thinking you were offering me a bargain. But here's what your ledger doesn't show, because whoever keeps it burned the page: that girl is a *child*. She prayed for rain because her village was dying, and something answered her, and now grown men with torches hunt her through the snow. And you—" the pale eyes glittered, hard as river ice, "—you want me to hand her to you so your Church can do to her what it did to every soul who ever touched the old fire. Burn first. Bury the ash. Call it order."

"I want to prevent a catastrophe."

"No." Quill's voice dropped, gentle and merciless. "You want to prevent being *wrong*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference between you and me."

* * *

Seraphine had interrogated fanatics, cowards, zealots, and liars. She had a protocol for each. What she did not have a protocol for—what no seminary text had ever covered—was a woman who looked at her with pity and meant it.

"You misunderstand your position," Seraphine said, and heard the frost enter her own voice. "Mercy is not infinite. It has terms, and those terms expire. If you will not speak willingly, there are methods—"

"There always are." Quill settled back against the wall, unbothered, arranging her chains as though settling blankets. "Go on, then. Do what you came to do. But understand something first, because I'll only say it once." She lifted her chin, and for a moment—just a moment—she seemed to fill the whole small cell, this tiny, ink-stained, grey-haired woman. "Everything you take from me tonight, you'll take from silence. My crew knows it. The girl knows it. And someday, Inquisitor—maybe soon, maybe when it's too late for you—you'll know it too: you can break a body a hundred ways, but you can't break a woman who's already decided what she's worth."

Seraphine rose. "You'll die in this cell."

"Everyone dies somewhere, dear. At least mine has good company in the walls." A pause, and then, almost kindly: "Ask me anyway. Ask your questions. I'll enjoy watching you waste the candle."

So Seraphine asked. For six hours, by the guttering lantern, she asked—about Duskhollow's hidden approaches, about the route the fugitives had taken south, about the map-witch's face, her name, her blood. And Mother Quill answered everything with the same magnificent, infuriating irrelevance: recipes for plum brandy, the proper way to mend a sail, an increasingly detailed opinion about which of Seraphine's superiors was embezzling from the Inquisition's own coffers ("Check the requisitions for chapel candles, dear. Nobody burns that many candles honestly.").

Somewhere past midnight, the old woman began to sing. Low, cracked, half-remembered—an old mountain hymn, one of the forbidden ones, about an ember kept alive beneath the ash. Seraphine recognized it from the Church's index of proscribed songs. She had never actually heard it sung before.

It should have sounded like defeat. Instead it filled the cell like a hearth.

* * *

Near dawn, the coughing started.

Seraphine noticed it first as a hitch in the hymn, then as a wet rattle that did not belong to any performance. She had assumed the prisoner's frailty was theater. It was not. Mother Quill had been dying since before they took her—the river crossing, the cold, the weeks of flight before it—and whatever the cell had done, it had only hurried along what winter had already begun.

By the time the guard fetched the medicus, the old woman's breathing was a shallow, laboring thing, and her lips had gone the color of slate.

Seraphine knelt beside the cot. This too was against protocol; she did it anyway.

"Last chance," she said quietly. "The girl. The witch. Where."

Mother Quill opened her eyes. They were still clear. Still pale and sharp and utterly, maddeningly unafraid.

"You still think this is about information," she whispered. Each breath cost her; she spent them like a miser spending gold, only on what mattered. "It was never... about information. You wanted me to beg. Because if I beg, then everyone can be made to beg. Then your whole rotten... beautiful machine works." A ghost of a smile. "I won't beg, child. None of us will. That's the thing you'll never... put in your ledger."

Her hand moved—slowly, with enormous effort—and closed around Seraphine's wrist. The grip was astonishingly strong for a dying woman. Ink-stained fingers, swollen knuckles, holding on.

"Listen to me," Quill breathed. "You wear that fragment of the seal at your throat. I've seen it. You think it's a trophy. It's a *debt*." Her eyes bored into Seraphine's, and in them was not hatred but something far worse—certainty, offered as a gift. "The Church didn't win the Sundering. It *broke* something it doesn't understand, and it's been paying interest ever since. When the bill comes due—and it's coming due now, girl, that's why the gods are waking—you remember the old woman in the cell. Remember she told you true."

The grip loosened. Slid away.

"Tell Brannoc..." A breath. Another. The pale eyes drifted toward the ceiling, toward something Seraphine could not see. "...he still owes me for the *Kestrel*. Thieving old..."

The sentence never ended. The breath after it simply didn't come.

The cell went very quiet. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. The lantern flame bent and straightened, bent and straightened, as though the room were breathing for her now.

Seraphine stayed kneeling a long time. Then she reached out with two fingers and closed the old woman's eyes, and found, to her own surprise, that her hand was not entirely steady.

* * *

She wrote the report herself, before sunrise, standing at the desk in her quarters with the seal-fragment cold against her sternum. *Prisoner expired of natural causes prior to yielding intelligence. No methods employed. No confession obtained.*

Every word true. Every word a concealment. She was growing accustomed to the sensation.

What she did not write—what she could not write—was the thing that kept her standing at the window afterward, watching the frost crawl across the glass while the garrison slept.

*It's a debt,* the old woman had said. *The Church broke something it doesn't understand.*

Seraphine had dismissed it as a dying smuggler's spite. That was the correct response. The disciplined response. She had written it into her ledger accordingly.

But she had also spent eleven years learning to tell the difference between a lie and a truth told by an enemy. And Mother Quill had died the way no liar dies—unhurried, unbroken, spending her last breaths not on pleas or bargains but on a warning, delivered free of charge, to the very woman sent to destroy everything she loved.

Liars bought their lives with words. This woman had refused to sell.

Which meant either she had believed every word—or she had known something.

Seraphine touched the seal-fragment through her uniform. Cold. Always cold. Three centuries, and the sacred relic of her order had never once held warmth.

*When the bill comes due.*

In the east, over the black teeth of the Thornfells, the sky had begun to lighten—and at the horizon's edge, faint as a coal breathed upon, there was a glow that might have been dawn, and might not.

Seraphine watched it for a long time. Then she opened her ledger, turned to a fresh page, and wrote a single line in her precise, unforgiving hand:

*The map-witch travels south. Find out why the mountain is glowing.*

She was beginning, despite herself, to ask real questions.
