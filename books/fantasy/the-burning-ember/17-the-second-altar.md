## 17. The Second Altar

The shrine of Aurel clung to the cliff face like a swallow's nest, three hundred feet above a sea that had never learned gentleness.

Oscar saw it first from the switchback trail below—a pale smudge against the grey granite, half-swallowed by ivy and salt-bleached lichen. Her father's journal had described it in his cramped, careful hand: *Aurel-of-the-Windward-Face, second of the three. Built where the wind cannot decide whether to be sea or mountain. Approach with an honest tongue or not at all.*

She had thought that last part was poetry. Cartographers' journals were full of poetry; it was how men who spent their lives alone kept themselves company. But the closer they climbed, the more she suspected her father had been writing instructions.

The wind up here did not blow so much as argue with itself, gusting off the water and then reversing without warning, shoving them toward the edge and then away from it as if unable to make up its mind. Below, the waves detonated against black rocks in long white explosions, and the spray rose high enough to taste on the air—brine and iron and something older underneath, something that smelled faintly of struck flint.

"You could have mentioned the climb," Brannoc wheezed behind her, one hand pressed to the rock face, his single eye screwed shut against the wind. The loss of his ship still sat on him like wet wool; he moved like a man carrying a coffin he hadn't finished building. "Ferryman takes memories. This cliff just takes your legs."

"Your legs are fine."

"My legs are *lying* to me." He spat over the edge, watched the wind steal it sideways, and grunted. "Everything up here lies. Bad place for a shrine. Good place for a grave."

Oscar didn't answer. She was watching Wren.

The girl climbed like she'd been born on cliffs—which, Oscar remembered, she nearly had been; there had been goat-paths around her village steeper than stairs. But she climbed slowly now, deliberately, one hand pressed flat against the stone beside her as though listening to it. The ember-brand on her forearm was visible even through her sleeve, a dull orange glow like a coal wrapped in cloth.

"It's warmer here," Wren said when Oscar drew level with her. Not frightened—that was the strange thing. Since Duskhollow, since the Maw, since everything, the girl had stopped being frightened of the brand and started being curious about it, which worried Oscar far more. "Not burning-warm. Like... standing near a hearth someone just left."

"The shrine," Oscar said. "It knows you're coming."

"Does it know you're coming?"

Oscar looked at her. Ten years old, hair full of salt, eyes too old for the face they lived in. She thought about lying, and found—to her mild horror—that she didn't want to. That was new. That was dangerous.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I'm hoping it doesn't look too closely."

Wren nodded as if this were a perfectly reasonable answer and climbed on.

Cassian brought up the rear. He'd barely spoken all morning—not sulking, not wounded, just quiet in the way of a man who has decided that silence is the only honest currency he carries. Last night by the fire he had told her about the seminary letters, about decades of Church knowledge buried under decades of Church silence, and she had watched something in him finish breaking and begin reassembling along different lines. He was not the same man who had drunk himself blind in the Hollow Lantern. She wasn't sure yet what he was instead. She wasn't sure what *she* was anymore either.

They reached the shrine as the sun cleared the morning haze.

* * *

It was smaller than she expected. Shrines in stories were always vast—pillared halls, gold leaf, the weight of empire. This was a hollow in the cliff perhaps twenty feet across, its back wall the raw granite of the mountain itself, its front open to the sea and the sky. Someone had once carved an archway at the entrance, but the sea-wind had eaten most of the detail; what remained suggested wings, or hands, or simply the shape of something opening.

Inside: a floor of fitted stone swept clean by centuries of wind. A low basin at the center, carved from a single block of white stone gone grey with salt. And on the back wall—

Oscar stopped breathing.

A map.

Not painted. Not carved. *Inlaid*—threads of copper and silver and some dark metal she didn't recognize, set into the granite in lines fine as spider silk, depicting the Thornfell range in miniature. Every peak. Every pass. And threaded through them, in the dark metal, routes she knew. Routes she had spent ten years walking and memorizing and never once putting down on paper because no map should hold them.

Smuggler roads. Her smuggler roads.

"That's—" Brannoc had come up beside her, and his voice had gone very soft. "That's the Kettle. And that spur there, the one that shouldn't hold a goat—that's the Widow's Step. I've run that step twice in my life and nearly died both times." He looked at her, eye wide. "How does a three-hundred-year-old shrine know my roads?"

"Not yours," Oscar said quietly. "Mine." She touched the wall, tracing the dark line that wound beneath Thornfell's roots—the road she had never told anyone about, the one she'd found at nineteen and sworn to take to her grave. It was there. Precise. Patient. "It knows mine."

"The shrines remember what the keepers knew," Cassian said from the archway. He had his journal out—her father's journal, actually; they had begun trading them somewhere on the Greywater without ever discussing it—and he was reading by the flat grey light. "Aurel was called the Witness, in the old rites. God of things seen truly. Not sun-god, not storm-god. Just... seeing." He looked up. "The first altar asked for blood. This one asks for something else."

"How do you know?"

He turned the journal toward her. Her father's hand, a page she hadn't reached:

*Aurel does not want your blood. Blood is easy. Any fool can bleed. Aurel wants the thing under the blood—the thing you say when no one is listening. Come to the Windward Face with a lie in your mouth and the rite will fail, and worse than fail. The Witness sees truly, and being seen truly is not the same as being forgiven.*

Oscar read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept sliding off the last sentence the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

"Wonderful," Brannoc said. "First altar wants blood, second wants therapy. What's the third want, our firstborn?"

Nobody laughed. The joke landed in the hollow of the shrine and lay there, and the wind came through the archway and carried it out to sea.

* * *

They prepared the way the journal described, because whatever else her father had been—liar, thief of gods' power, architect of a three-century silence—he had been thorough.

The basin filled from a spring that seeped through a crack in the granite, sweet water tasting faintly of mineral and lightning. Cassian chalked the old circle around it, the interlocking rings of the covenant script, and his hand shook only a little. Wren sat against the far wall with her brand-covered arm bare, watching everything with the ferocious attention of a child memorizing a world that kept trying to end. Brannoc stood watch at the archway, facing outward, because he had announced that if any god, Inquisitor, or weather event wanted to interrupt, it would have to go through him first, and he was in exactly the mood for it.

Oscar knelt at the basin.

The water was still. In its surface she could see the strip of sky above the shrine, the wheeling white shapes of seabirds, and her own face—thin, wind-burned, ink-stained even now, because some habits survived the apocalypse. Behind her own shoulder, reflected, Cassian stood at the circle's edge, and she found she was glad he was there and hated that she was glad, because gladness was another kind of exposure.

"Rite of Renewal, second station," Cassian read, low. His voice took on the cadence of liturgy without his seeming to notice—as if the words themselves remembered the shape of prayer even after he'd renounced it. "*Before the Witness, the heir speaks. Not the rehearsed truth. Not the safe truth. The truth that costs.*" He paused. "There's a marginal note in your father's hand."

"Read it."

"'I lied here. It worked anyway. Do not do what I did.'"

Silence, except the wind and the sea below, arguing their endless argument.

"He lied," Oscar said. "At the altar of the god of true sight. And it *worked anyway*."

"And he wrote *do not do what I did*," Cassian said. "Which means it worked the way a rotten beam holds a roof. It held until it didn't." He crouched beside her, close enough that she could see the exhaustion worn into his face like watermarks. "Oscar. Whatever you're going to say—you should decide now whether you're saying it to the god or to yourself. Because I think they're the same audience."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do." He said it gently. That was the worst of it. Cassian Vale had a talent for gentleness that should have required a license. "You've known since the first altar. You bled into that drowned shrine and felt it answer, and I watched your face. That wasn't only duty answering. Duty doesn't make a person look like that."

"Like what?"

"Like someone coming home to a house she's been told burned down."

She wanted to tell him he was wrong. The lie assembled itself readily enough—she'd had ten years of practice at lying, mostly to herself, mostly about scale and consequence and the exact dimensions of her own wanting. She opened her mouth to say it.

And the wind stopped.

Not died down. *Stopped*—the whole argument of sea and mountain falling silent at once, the spray hanging in the air, the seabirds gone quiet, the world holding its breath the way a room holds its breath when someone says the true thing at a dinner table. In the basin, the water went mirror-still and then, impossibly, began to rise—a thin column, a finger's width, lifting out of the stone bowl and standing in the air between them, catching the light.

On the back wall, the copper-and-silver map began to glow.

"Oh," said Wren, from the wall, in a small voice. "Oh, it's awake."

Oscar knelt before the column of standing water and understood, with total clarity, that she was not going to be permitted the lie. Not because the god would punish it—her father proved it could be gotten away with—but because she had spent ten years lying in every direction at once, to merchants and smugglers and Inquisitors and herself, and she was so *tired*. She was tired down to the marrow. The windless hush of the shrine felt less like a trap than like a room where, for the first time, no one was listening to eavesdrop.

Only to witness.

"All right," she whispered. To the water. To the god. To herself. "All right."

She thought about where to start, and realized there was nowhere to start except the middle, the ugly middle, the part she'd never said aloud even in the privacy of her own skull.

"I want it," she said.

Her voice sounded strange in the hush—too loud, too human, cracking on the second word.

"The power. My inheritance. I want it." The words came faster now, tumbling, ten years of dammed-up honesty breaching all at once. "Everyone keeps talking about duty—duty, obligation, the burden of the bloodline—and I have let them, because duty is respectable, duty makes you a martyr instead of a monster. But that's not the whole truth. The whole truth is that when I bled at Vess's shrine and felt the seal answer me, I didn't feel burdened. I felt *alive*. For the first time in ten years of hiding, of mapping other people's roads while my own life went unmapped, I felt like I had been *meant* for something, and I loved it. I loved it the way you love a fire in winter. Not sensibly. Not safely."

The column of water trembled. She kept going, because stopping now would be worse than anything the god could do.

"I want to walk into the Court of Cinders and have them kneel. I want the voice that stops storms. Some nights—I'll say this too, since we're being honest—some nights I imagine Seraphine Locke on her knees in front of me, and I imagine letting her *feel* what it costs to burn a shrine, to hunt children, and I enjoy imagining it. That's what's in me. That's what this blood is. My family stole power from dying gods and I am the last drop of the theft, and when I touch it, it doesn't feel stolen. It feels like it *fits*." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "And I'm terrified that if I reforge the covenant—if I pour myself into it—I won't stop at sealing the veil. I'll want to *rule* what's behind it. I want the power. That's the truth. Not duty. Want. Ugly, human, hungry want."

The silence afterward was absolute.

Even the sea seemed to have stopped below the cliff. The column of water hung in the air, catching light it shouldn't have been able to catch, and in its surface Oscar saw not her own reflection now but something else—a vast dim shape, winged or many-handed or simply *open*, regarding her from the other side of the skin of the world.

Then the water spoke. Not in sound. In sensation—in the feeling of being seen so completely that there was nowhere left to hide, and discovering, in the same instant, that being seen completely did not kill her.

*Yes,* the shrine seemed to say. *That.*

Warmth spread through the basin, through the chalked circle, through the floor stones, through the whole cliff. On the back wall, the great map blazed—every route, every pass, every hidden road lighting up copper-bright—and then the light gathered itself inward, condensing, compressing, flowing out of the wall entirely and streaming into the basin, into the water, into the seal-work that was not a seal-work at all but a *wound*, a suture across the skin between worlds.

And the suture pulled tight.

Oscar felt it the way you feel a knot drawn snug—not pain exactly, but tension resolving into strength. Somewhere behind the sky, something that had been leaking for three hundred years stopped leaking. The pressure she had carried since the first altar—that sense of a door bulging inward—eased by a fraction. A fraction was enormous. A fraction was everything.

"You told the truth," Cassian said quietly. He was staring at the fading glow on the wall, at the basin, at her. "The ugliest truth you had. And it made it stronger."

"That's not how locks work," Oscar said. Her voice was hoarse. "Locks don't get stronger when you confess."

"No," Cassian said. "But covenants aren't locks. I've been thinking about this since the letters. A lock is something you impose. A covenant is something you *agree to*. All parties. Even the parts of yourself you'd rather weren't invited to the table." He looked at her, and there was something in his face she couldn't name—awe, maybe, or grief, or the particular tenderness of a man watching someone survive something he'd failed to survive himself. "Your father lied here and built a covenant out of duty and denial, and it rotted in three hundred years. You told the truth and—" he gestured at the humming, tightening, *alive* presence in the stone "—and it took you seriously. All of you. The wanting included."

From the wall, Wren spoke up, matter-of-fact as a child reporting the weather: "The brand likes you better now."

Oscar looked over. The girl was holding her arm up to the light, examining the ember-mark with a scholar's frown. "It's not pulling anymore. Before, it always felt like it was reaching for something. Now it's just... resting." She lowered her arm and looked at Oscar with those too-old eyes. "You fed it something it was hungry for."

"Honesty," Brannoc said from the archway, disgusted. "The altar was hungry for *honesty*. I've sailed with forty years of smugglers and not one of them would've survived five minutes in this hole." But he said it softly, and he crossed himself with the wrong hand and the wrong god, the way sailors do, and nobody corrected him.

* * *

They camped that night in the shrine's mouth, because descending the switchbacks in the dark was suicide and because none of them, privately, wanted to leave yet. The seal's warmth lingered in the stone like a hearth long after the fire should have gone out. Wren slept against the back wall with her bare arm pressed flat to the glowing map, breathing slow and deep, and the brand pulsed gently in time with her—resting, as she'd said. Resting.

Oscar sat at the edge of the archway with her father's journal open on her knees, not reading it, just holding it. Below, the sea went on with its work, patient and indifferent, the waves dismantling the coast grain by grain the way they had for longer than gods had names.

Cassian settled beside her. Close. Not touching. The not-touching had become its own language between them, fluent and excruciating.

"Two altars," he said. "One to go."

"One to go," she agreed. "Except the third one's a ruin. We don't even know if—"

"We'll know when we get there." He was quiet a moment. Then: "What you said today. The part about Locke on her knees."

Oscar's stomach dropped. "You heard that."

"I heard all of it. So did the god, which is rather the point." He turned to look at her, and the moonlight caught the scar of his old life on his face. "I'm not telling you it makes you monstrous. I'm telling you I recognized it. I knelt in a seminary chapel for eleven years praying to a silent heaven, and do you know what I wanted? Not grace. *Answers*. I wanted to stand in front of whatever ran the universe and demand the ledger—every prayer unanswered, every child unfed, every silence justified, line by line. That's not faith. That's hunger wearing faith's coat." He exhaled. "We're the same animal, Oscar. You just stopped pretending sooner."

She looked at him for a long time. The sea boomed below. Inside the shrine, the map glowed faintly, keeping its vigil.

"Cassian," she said finally. "When this is over. If this is over. If we reforge the covenant and I give up—" she gestured vaguely at everything, the power, the blood, the inheritance humming in her veins like a second heartbeat "—all of it. What's left? Who's left?"

He smiled, tired and real. "Someone worth mapping," he said. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we go find a ruined altar, and I have a bad feeling about what we're going to find there."

She might have asked him what he meant. But far below them, at the base of the cliff where the waves broke black, a light appeared—a cold, steady, deliberate light, moving along the shore road in the unmistakable formation of a column of soldiers.

And at its head, a lantern of gilded glass, burning white as judgment.

Seraphine Locke had found the second altar.
