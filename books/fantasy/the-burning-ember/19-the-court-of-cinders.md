## 19. The Court of Cinders

The veil took her between one heartbeat and the next.

Oscar had been kneeling at the edge of the drowned shrine of Vess, wringing lake water from her sleeve, when the world simply—folded. Not like falling through a door. Like being a word in a book that someone closed and opened again in a different chapter. The cold of the mountain water was still on her skin; the smell of wet stone still in her hair. But the shrine was gone, and the sky above her was not a sky at all.

It was ash.

Not grey, not dead—a living ash, drifting in slow spirals across an endless vault, glowing faintly from within like embers carried on a wind too gentle to exist. Each mote pulsed with its own small light, and where they drifted low enough to touch the ground, the ground remembered them: veins of fire kindling briefly in black glass before dimming again. The air tasted of woodsmoke and rain and something older underneath both—the smell of the first fire ever struck, perhaps, or the last.

"Vaelith," she said aloud, and her voice did not echo. It was *kept*, instead, gathered up by the ash and carried away somewhere, as though the realm itself wanted to hold what she said.

She stood on a plain of black glass that stretched to horizons made hazy by distance and drifting light. Ruins rose from it here and there—not ruins as mortals built them, but ruins as gods might: a colonnade whose pillars were petrified lightning; a staircase ascending to a doorway that opened onto nothing, or onto everything, depending on how she tilted her head. Everything was beautiful the way a burned forest is beautiful. Everything was beautiful the way grief is beautiful.

And everything was *diminished*. She felt it the way you feel a room where a great fire has gone down to coals—the shape of the heat still there, the presence of it, but only a fraction of what it had been.

They were waiting for her.

* * *

She counted eleven of them, though counting felt wrong, the way counting waves feels wrong. They stood scattered across the glass plain at distances that refused to stay fixed—one moment a figure was ten paces off, the next it was beside her without ever crossing the space between. They had shapes, but the shapes were suggestions. A woman whose hair was a slow river running uphill. A tall figure robed in frost that never melted, never cracked. Something vast and patient with antlers of smoke, crouched like a heron at the edge of a pool. A child sitting cross-legged, turning a handful of dying stars over in her palms like river stones.

The diminished gods of Vaelith. The bound ones. The ones her family had chained three centuries ago and called it salvation.

Oscar's hand went to the knife at her belt out of pure habit, and the child-god laughed—a sound like ice cracking on a spring pond, delighted rather than cruel.

"She reaches for iron," the child said. "In the house of fire."

"You would too," Oscar said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. Ten years of lying to merchants and outrunning Inquisitors had taught her that steadiness was not the absence of fear but the decision to speak anyway. "If strangers dragged you somewhere against your will."

"No one dragged you," said the woman with the river-hair, and her voice was the sound of water over stones, unhurried, certain. "The veil is thinning, daughter of Dravenmoor. It thins around *you* especially. We did not pull. We only... left the door unlatched, and you leaned."

"I didn't lean."

"You have been leaning your whole life," said the frost-robed figure, and its voice was winter wind through a keyhole. "Every map you drew of places no map should hold. Every night you memorized roads that lead nowhere mortal. You thought you were hiding. You were *reaching*."

Oscar's jaw tightened. She looked around the court—the Court of Cinders, some deep-buried instinct named it, a name that arrived in her mind already worn smooth by use—and forced herself to do what she did best. Observe. Catalogue. Map.

The gods stood in a rough circle, and at the circle's center, half-sunk in the black glass like a coin dropped in tar, was an altar. Her heart stumbled. She knew altars now; she'd bled on two of them. But this one was different—larger, older, its surface carved with the same interlocking knotwork she'd found in her father's journal, the same sigils that had branded themselves into Wren's skin. The Ember Altar's elder sibling, or its shadow. And rising from it, faint as heat-shimmer, she could see threads. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Fine as spider silk, radiating outward and upward and *through*—through the ash-sky, through the veil, into the mortal world.

Into people. Into prayers. Into Wren's brand, maybe. Into Cassian's silence.

"The covenant," she said quietly.

"What remains of it," said the heron-thing, and its voice was so deep she felt it in her back teeth. "You see our state, heir. Look at us. Truly look."

She did. And looking, she understood why the realm smelled of dying fires. These were not gods at their height. These were portraits painted over other portraits, faces worn thin by centuries of being *bound*—their power siphoned through the covenant knots into the seal, their names locked behind blood-oaths, their worship forbidden until even remembering them became heresy. The Church hadn't destroyed them. The Dravenmoors had. Her family had taken living divinity and wound it tight as a tourniquet, and for three hundred years the tourniquet had held, and the limb beneath had slowly died.

"We are owed," said the river-woman gently. "That is all we came to say. That, and one offer."

"There it is." Oscar crossed her arms. "I wondered how long the pleasantries would last."

The child-god giggled again. "We like her."

"We are not going to threaten you," the river-woman continued, as if the interruption hadn't happened. "Threats are for the hungry ones, the remnants, the things that forgot their own names and kept only their appetites. We remember our names. Remembering is nearly all we have left. So we offer instead. Hear it, weigh it, refuse it if you can. But hear it."

The ash-motes began to move.

They drifted inward from every horizon, converging above the sunken altar, gathering, thickening, until the drifting embers formed a shape—a figure, human-shaped, standing where the altar's threads converged. And the figure was *her*. Not a copy. A possibility. This Oscar stood straighter, unburdened, her eyes lit from within with a low gold light, and when she raised her hand, the threads of the covenant rose with it, obedient as puppet-strings, and far away—Oscar felt it rather than saw it—the trembling veil began to *settle*, to knit, to heal.

"This is what we can give," the river-woman said. "Your inheritance, whole. Not the thin trickle your blood has carried for generations—not the dregs of a diluted line—but the full draught, as the first Dravenmoors drank it. Take it, and you need no altars. No rites. No solstice deadline ticking toward disaster. You could mend the veil with a word. You could bind the Maw before it eats another town. You could unmake Seraphine Locke's warrants with a thought and scatter the Inquisition like leaves." The ember-Oscar smiled, and it was Oscar's own smile, the rare one, the one she almost never spent. "You could save everyone. All at once. Today."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing in the realm.

Oscar made herself breathe. Made herself think of the drowned shrine, of her blood hissing on wet stone, of the *attention* that had turned toward her afterward—vast, curious, terrible. Made herself think of Wren's brand burning in the night. Of Brannoc's ship going down in the Teeth. Of Ilsa's body on the Weeping Stair, and Cassian's blood soaking through her hands, and Mother Quill's ashes, and Petra's face when they'd turned the traitor out into the snow rather than kill him.

Everyone she'd lost. Everyone she could keep, if she only reached out and took.

"That's the trap," she said finally. "Isn't it? That's always the trap."

"Is it?" asked the frost-figure, mild as morning. "A trap requires deception. We deceive you in nothing. The power is real. The salvation is real. Ask us to prove it and we will show you the Maw's death a hundred ways."

"Oh, I believe you." Oscar began to walk—slowly, along the rim of the sunken altar, because her legs needed to do something and standing still felt too much like surrender. "I believe every word. That's what makes it a trap. My father's journal says the first binding cost the Dravenmoors everything they loved, and they did it anyway, and then—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—and then they wrote it all down in cipher and hid it from their own children and let the whole thing rot, because admitting what they'd done meant admitting what they'd *taken*. Power doesn't stay borrowed. It starts making demands. I've felt it already, the little I carry. It whispers. It wants. And you're offering me the whole voice instead of the whisper."

"Yes," said the river-woman, simply.

Oscar stopped walking. "You're not even going to argue?"

"What would arguing gain? You are correct. Power wants. That is not a flaw in the offer, heir—that is the nature of power, mortal or divine, and any who tell you otherwise are selling something." The river-woman's current slowed, grew almost tender. "We offer it knowing what it is. We offer it because we are *dying*, and the seal is failing, and you are the last door left. We would rather live bound to a willing keeper than scatter into the hungering dark as remnant-things. Even a cage looks different when you choose the lock."

Something in the words snagged on her, sharp and unexpected. Pity. They were asking her for pity, these beings who could unmake mountains, and the pity was *real*, and that was somehow worse than any threat.

"And if I say no?"

"Then you take the slow road," said the child-god, tossing a dying star and catching it. "Altar by altar. Rite by rite. Drop by drop, the way water cuts stone. It might work. It might finish too late. The Maw grows fatter every day. The veil tears wider every week. Your priest bleeds, your pilot mourns, your little brand-marked girl walks around wearing a key that every hungry thing in two worlds can smell." The child looked up, and her eyes were old, so old, older than the mountains Oscar had mapped. "The slow road costs less of your soul and more of everyone else's. That is the honest arithmetic. We give it to you honestly because we want you to choose with open eyes. Chosen chains hold better. Your family proved that much."

Oscar's breath went out of her.

Because that was the poison in the honey, wasn't it. Not a lie anywhere. Just arithmetic. Just the weight of every life balanced against her one stubborn, scrappy, hard-won determination to remain *herself*—to remain the woman who sold maps to merchants and memorized smuggler roads and slept in waystations and wanted, desperately, ordinarily, to be allowed to love people without the world ending.

The ember-Oscar stood waiting at the center of the web, patient as a portrait. Gold-eyed. Unafraid. Everything she could be if she stopped flinching from what she was.

And beneath the fear, beneath the fury at her father and his fathers, beneath the grief stacked like cordwood—Ilsa, Quill, the archive burning, ten years of hiding turned to smoke—something in her *leaned*. Just slightly. Just the way a cold hand leans toward a fire. She hated it. She felt the hatred and the longing at once, tangled past separating, and understood with terrible clarity that this was the true temptation: not the promise of power, but the promise of *certainty*. No more impossible choices. No more watching people die while she crept along the slow road doing fractions of what was needed. One word, and the arithmetic ended forever.

"Show me something," she heard herself say.

The gods stilled.

"If I'm to weigh this—if you want me choosing with open eyes—then show me what it costs. Not the pretty version. Show me the keeper. Show me the last heir who took the full inheritance, wherever they are, whatever's left of them." Her voice cracked and she let it. "Show me my father."

The ash stopped moving.

For a long moment the Court of Cinders held its breath—all eleven diminished shapes frozen in attitudes that suddenly looked less like patience and more like dread. The river-woman's current ran backward. The frost-figure's robe crackled.

"He is not here," the river-woman said at last, and for the first time her voice was not certain. "The dead of your line do not come to Vaelith. We are bound, heir, not omniscient. What your father became after he sealed his own inheritance away—we do not know. We were not permitted to watch."

"*Permitted.*"

"The binding has terms," said the heron-thing, deep and slow. "Terms written by your ancestors. Terms that forbid us much, and forbid us most of all to speak plainly of what the keeping does to the keeper. We circumvent where we can. Tonight, with you, we circumvent greatly. But that door is shut even to us."

"Convenient."

"It is *terrifying*," said the child-god, with sudden fierce simplicity, and the star in her palm guttered out. "You think we enjoy this? We remember what we were. We remember skies we wore like clothing. And we remember the first keeper, the one who took us whole, and what the taking built in him, and what he did with the building." She looked down at her empty hands. "Ask us anything but that."

The ash hung motionless. Somewhere beyond the veil, impossibly distant, Oscar thought she heard thunder—or cannon-fire—or the Maw, feeding.

She stood at the center of the Court of Cinders with her inheritance offered on a golden platter and the truth locked behind her own family's locks, and she realized that either answer would break something. Taking the power might break the world. Refusing it might break it slower. And there was no third option, no clever map-maker's route around the choice, because she was standing in the one country she had never been able to chart.

"Then I'll ask something else," she said quietly. "The little girl. Wren. The brand on her arm—you've seen it. You know what it opens."

The gods exchanged a silence that had weight and texture.

"We have seen it," the river-woman admitted.

"Is she bait? Or is she a key? Because those aren't the same thing, and I've been treating her like the first, and if I'm wrong about that—" Oscar's hands curled into fists. "Tell me what she is."

The child-god raised her head. And in her eyes, for just a moment, Oscar saw reflected not the ash-sky but a door—old, iron-banded, standing alone in darkness, with light leaking through its seams.

"She is what happens," the child said softly, "when a prayer is answered by something that should have stayed asleep. And she is coming here whether you bring her or the Maw does. The veil is thinning fastest around *her* now, heir. Faster than around you." A pause, delicate as frost forming. "You should hurry home. I think someone is about to knock."

The Court of Cinders folded.

* * *

She came back to herself on her knees in the shallows of the drowned shrine, gasping, soaked to the waist, the mountain night shockingly ordinary around her—real stars, real cold, the small honest sounds of water and wind. Her blood had dried brown on the altar stone. Everything was exactly as she'd left it.

Everything was completely different.

She pushed herself upright, wading for the shore, and her mind was already moving the way it moved over a blank stretch of map—fast, hungry, connecting points. The veil thinning fastest around Wren. Around *Wren*, not around her, which changed every plan she'd built since the pass, which meant the girl wasn't cargo to be protected but a door to be guarded, or answered, or—

A sound rolled down from the peaks. Not thunder. Too rhythmic. Too deliberate.

Hoofbeats. Many of them, descending the switchbacks toward the valley where, twelve miles east, a stolen wagon sat hidden in a stand of pines containing a wounded priest, a one-eyed pilot, a forger, and a brand-marked child who was, apparently, the thinnest point in two worlds.

Oscar broke into a run.

Behind her, in the black water of the shrine, the reflection of the stars shivered—and for half a heartbeat, among them, glowed a single ember that no night sky should hold, watching her go with something that might have been hope, or hunger, or both.
