## 20. Refusal and Ruin

Wren had learned to count her fears the way other children counted sheep.

There was the fear of the dark, which she'd outgrown in Duskhollow's lantern-lit warrens. There was the fear of soldiers, which she'd carried since the village turned on her, since Mother's face went strange and hard behind the door that never opened again. There was the fear of the brand, which lived under her skin like a second heartbeat, warm and wrong. And there was the newest fear, the one she hadn't yet found a name for: the fear that all of this—the smugglers, the maps, the woman with ink-stained fingers who'd pulled her half-dead from a mountain pass—would be taken away before Wren figured out how to keep it.

She was counting them now, standing at the edge of a meadow that should not have existed.

They had climbed for two days above the treeline, following Oscar's father's journal through passes so narrow Brannoc had to walk sideways with his one eye squinted against the wind. And then, without warning, the mountain opened like a hand uncurling, and there it was: a bowl of green cradled in black stone, flowers blooming in defiance of the altitude, the air thick and sweet as summer though frost rimmed every rock beyond its edge.

"It's a fold," Oscar said quietly. She stood at the meadow's center beside a ring of standing stones, each one taller than three men, each carved over every inch with script that hurt to look at directly—Wren's eyes kept sliding off it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "A pocket of Vaelith bleeding through. The veil is thin here. Thin enough to walk through, if it wants you."

"Or if you want it," Cassian murmured. He hadn't let go of his iron lantern since they'd made camp the night before. The flame inside burned steady and colorless, the way priest-lanterns were supposed to burn, and Wren had noticed he checked it every few minutes like a man checking whether his heart was still beating.

The brand on Wren's shoulder had been singing since dawn.

Not burning—singing. A low, sweet note beneath her skin, like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup. She'd told no one. She'd learned what happened to people who told things. But the note grew louder as they neared the stones, and by the time the sun touched the peaks it was less a sound than an instruction, patient and enormous, and Wren's feet wanted very badly to walk toward the ring.

She dug her heels into the grass instead and watched Oscar.

Oscar Dravenmoor—who was not called Oscar Dravenmoor, who was called nothing at all, whose name Wren only knew because names leaked in small places like Duskhollow—stood before the tallest stone with her father's journal open in her hands. The wind moved her hair but not her coat. Around her, the air had begun to shimmer, faint as heat off summer stone, and where it shimmered Wren could see *through*—glimpses of somewhere else. Towers of pale glass. Rivers running silver and slow. Shapes moving in the distance that might have been people or might have been weather.

The gods' country. Vaelith. The place the prayers went when the world still answered them.

"You don't have to do this part," Cassian said. "The rite at the drowned shrine worked. The cliff shrine worked. This one's just—"

"Just the place where the veil is thinnest," Oscar finished. "Which means it's the place I can hear them best." She looked down at the journal, and Wren saw her thumb press flat against the page, hard, as if holding something down. "And they can hear me."

Ilsa shifted at the meadow's edge, her twin's absence a shape she kept leaning into and finding empty. Petra had her arm through Ilsa's and didn't let go. Brannoc stood with his arms crossed, jaw working around words he wasn't saying. They had buried Mother Quill's effects in a cairn two valleys back; they had not buried their anger, and it sat on all of them like snowfall.

Wren understood anger. Anger was easy. It was the other thing underneath it that frightened her—the way Brannoc had started carrying extra rations without being asked, the way Petra had begun sleeping between Wren and the fire, the way all of them had quietly rearranged themselves around a eleven-year-old girl as if she were something breakable and precious and *theirs*.

Nobody had ever rearranged themselves around Wren before.

The shimmering thickened.

It happened slowly and then all at once, the way ice lets go of a pond. One moment the meadow held only wind and standing stones; the next, the far side of the ring was simply *elsewhere*—a courtyard of white glass under a sky the color of deep water, and figures standing in it. Wren counted seven before her eyes watered and she lost track. They were beautiful the way avalanches are beautiful. They wore robes that might have been woven light or woven smoke, and their faces kept almost resolving into shapes Wren knew—a grandmother, a river, a door—and then sliding away again.

One of them stepped forward. It did not walk so much as arrive, gradually, the way dawn arrives.

*Heir.*

The voice did not come through the ears. It came up through the ground, through the bones of the feet, through the teeth. Wren's brand flared hot and she bit her lip bloody to keep from crying out.

*Little ember of a stolen hearth,* the god said, and its voice was kind, and that was the worst thing Wren had ever heard. *You carry our inheritance like a coin clutched in a fist. Three centuries we have waited in the dimming. Come. Take what bleeds through your blood. Take all of it. You need not crawl altar to altar like a beggar at a gate. We will open our hands, and you will be more than mortal, and nothing that hungers will ever touch you or yours again.*

"*Yours*," Oscar repeated. Her voice came out strange—flat, careful, a cartographer's voice, measuring terrain. "*Mine.* That's an interesting word for it."

*It is your birthright.*

"It's my *grandfather's theft*." Oscar's hands were steady on the journal, but Wren saw her knuckles whiten. "You know it. I know it now too. My family didn't bind you to save the world—we bound you to *rob* you. Every prayer answered since the Sundering was answered from a purse that wasn't ours to hold. So don't stand there calling it a birthright. Call it what it is. Stolen goods."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Then the god laughed, and the laugh rolled through the meadow and set every flower nodding, and it was not an unkind laughter, which somehow made it worse.

*Yes,* it said. *Yes. The clanmother told you true. And still the offer stands. What does it matter how the coin was minted? It spends the same. Take it, heir. Remake the covenant with yourself at its crown. Or refuse, and spend your short mortal life crawling toward an altar you may never reach, while the Maw eats its way across the world one town at a time.* The god's face almost became a woman's face—almost became, for one terrible instant, someone Wren had prayed to in a dry summer three years ago. *We are dying, little mapmaker. Pride is a luxury of the living.*

Wren saw it happen. She was the only one close enough to see it, and she would remember it for the rest of her life: the exact moment Oscar Dravenmoor wanted to say yes.

It was in her eyes first. They went wide and bright, lit from inside, gold creeping into the brown like dawn into night. Her breath caught. Her chin lifted. For one heartbeat she stood taller, as if something vast had poured itself into the shape of her and found the shape good. The shimmering air bent toward her. The flowers turned to face her. Even the standing stones seemed to lean.

She wanted it. God help them all, she wanted it—wanted the power to end the Maw with a word, to bring Mother Quill back from her cairn, to make the world safe enough that a branded child could sleep without screaming. Wren could see all of that wanting, laid open like a map, and it was the most frightening thing she had ever witnessed, because it was *reasonable*. Because anyone would. Because Wren herself, offered the same, would have said yes before the echo faded.

"No."

Oscar said it quietly. The word fell into the meadow like a stone into a well.

The gold drained from her eyes. She swayed, caught herself, and planted her feet like a woman planting a flag.

"No," she said again, louder, and now her voice shook and she let it shake. "I won't take it. Not the inheritance. Not the crown. Not any of it. You want a keeper? Find another bloodline—I'm the last one, so I suppose you're stuck with a keeper who *refuses*. I'll give everything back. All of it. Freely. That's the only covenant worth having—one made by choice, not by chains. Even mine. *Especially* mine."

The god regarded her for a long, long moment. When it spoke again, its voice had gone soft and strange.

*Do you know why we offer, heir?*

"Because you're desperate."

*Because we are afraid.* The figure's edges frayed, smoke pulling loose from smoke. *Something is coming up the dark roads of Vaelith, eating the diminished ones one by one. It ate the Ferryman. It ate the beggar-king's last coins. It grows fat on divinity, and it has smelled you—your blood, your refusal, the hole you mean to tear in the world's order. When you unbind the power, little mapmaker, you will leave a door open. And the Maw is already leaning on that door.*

"Then I'll close it after."

*With what?* And here the god did something Wren had not thought possible. It smiled sadly. *You cannot pour out a well and then drink from it. Choose, heir. Choose quickly. The door does not wait on human courage.*

"I choose anyway."

The word had barely left her mouth when the sky cracked.

Not the real sky—the *other* one, the deep-water sky beyond the glass towers. A seam split it from horizon to horizon, black as a pupil dilating, and through that seam came a sound Wren knew in her marrow. The whispering. The rain-whispering, the name-whispering, the sound that had fallen on her village the day the dead god answered her childish prayer for rain.

The gods in the courtyard recoiled. The nearest one simply *went*, snuffed like a candle, and its going made a sound like a bell underwater.

*Too late,* the god said, and its sadness was bottomless. *It followed the offer. It always follows the offer. Heir—run. Run and do not look—*

The vision tore shut. The meadow snapped back to itself—wind, stones, frost-rimmed rock, ordinary afternoon light—and Oscar staggered as if released from a great weight, catching herself against the standing stone, gasping, alive, mortal, *refusing*.

For three heartbeats, everything was fine.

Then the brand on Wren's shoulder stopped singing.

It *screamed*.

She went down into the grass, folding around the pain, and the pain was not like before—not the warm wrongness she'd carried since the pass, not the itch of a wound that wouldn't heal. This was a *taking*. Something pouring itself through the brand and into her, writing on her from the inside, and she felt it move down her arm and across her collarbone and up her throat, cold lines drawn in fire, and she heard herself making a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice.

"WREN—" Oscar was there, on her knees in the grass, hands hovering, not touching, because the last person who'd touched the brand had blistered. "Cassian! *Cassian!*"

He came fast, lantern swinging, and dropped beside them. "Hold her shoulders. Don't touch the marks." His face had gone the gray of old ash. "Gods below. Gods *below*, it's spreading."

"What is it?" Petra's voice, high and tight. "What's happening to her?"

"It's finishing the job," Cassian said, and his voice cracked on the words. "The Maw marked her partially at the mining town—it's been pulling at her ever since, drawing her along like bait on a line. Now—" He swallowed. "Now it's completed the mark. While the veil was thin. While the door was open. It used *this*—" he gestured at the standing stones, at the fading shimmer, at Oscar, "—it used *her refusal*. The moment she turned away from power, she was vulnerable, and the Maw poured itself through the gap."

Wren couldn't speak. The lines had reached her face now; she could feel them there, cool and precise, tracing her cheekbones like a mask being fitted. Through the red haze of pain she heard Brannoc swearing steadily, creatively, in three languages.

And then, beneath the pain, she heard the whispering.

It was inside the mark now. Inside her skin, her blood, the space behind her eyes. *Little key,* it whispered, in a voice like rain on a roof, like rain on a coffin lid. *Little key that opens every lock. We know where the altar sleeps. We know the road beneath the mountain. And when the heir comes to unbind the power, we will be there, and we will drink, and we will wear her face afterward, and yours, and yours.*

"Wren." Oscar's face swam above her, ink-stained fingers gripping her shoulders, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her cheeks. "Wren, stay with me. Look at me. Look at *me*."

Wren looked. And what she saw in Oscar's face frightened her worse than the whispering—not pity, not horror, but something harder. Something being forged in real time, the way a smith hammers a blade.

"She's marked," Cassian said quietly. "Fully. It can find her anywhere now. Track her. And through her—" He stopped.

"Through her, it can find *us*," Petra finished. Her arm was around Ilsa, and Ilsa's face was blank in the way it had been blank since the Weeping Stair, since her sister's—no. Since the ambush. Wren sometimes mixed them up, the dead ones. There were getting to be so many.

"No," Oscar said.

Everyone turned. She was still kneeling in the grass, still holding Wren's shoulders, but her head was up now, and her voice had changed. It was the voice she used when she sold maps to merchants—pleasant, reasonable, absolutely immovable.

"It thinks she's bait," Oscar said. "It thinks marking her gives it a leash on us—that we'll run, or hide her, or worse, trade her. That we'll make its choices for it." Her thumb brushed Wren's hair back from the new-cool lines on her temple, gentle as anything, and her eyes over Wren's head were arctic. "It's wrong."

"Oscar," Cassian said carefully. "If it can track her—"

"Then we stop running *from* it." She rose, lifting Wren with her, one arm under the girl's knees, and Wren, who had not been carried since she was very small, who had walked out of her village alone with a burning shoulder and a bag of bread, put her arms around this stranger's neck and held on. "We've been reacting since the waystation burned. Running its roads, fighting on its ground, grieving its casualties. No more. It gave us a gift tonight and doesn't know it."

Brannoc's eye narrowed. "Enlighten us."

"It marked her to draw us. Fine. Then we go where *we* choose, and it must follow *us*." Oscar's gaze swept the circle—Cassian, Petra, Ilsa, Brannoc, and last, longest, Wren. "The living map completes at the heart-mountain. The final page said it draws true only for a willing heir. Well, I refused the throne, and the mountain hasn't stopped humming since. We descend. We take the smuggler roads—the ones no Inquisitor knows, the ones no *god* knows, because I never mapped them, I only memorized them." A grim ghost of a smile. "It can feel her. Let it come. Every mile it chases us through my mountains is a mile it travels blind, hungry, and angry. And tired things make mistakes."

"And if it doesn't make mistakes?" Ilsa asked. Her voice was flat as slate. "If it just takes her, the way it took the mining town?"

The silence stretched. Wren felt the answer coming and pressed her face into Oscar's shoulder, into the smell of ink and woodsmoke and cold air.

"Then it learns," Oscar said, "what the Duskhollow crew does to things that touch our family."

*Family.* The word went through Wren warmer than any brand.

They broke camp in twenty minutes, because the Duskhollow crew could break camp in their sleep and nearly had, these past weeks. Brannoc took point with his crossbow slung across his back. Petra and Ilsa flanked the rear. Cassian walked beside Oscar, and the colorless flame in his lantern burned steadier than it had in days, as if even it had decided.

Wren rode in Oscar's arms until the slope steepened, then walked between them, one small hand swallowed in Oscar's ink-stained grip. The lines on her face had gone quiet, cool as riverbed stones, and the whispering had receded to a murmur at the edge of hearing—but it did not stop. It would never stop now. She understood that with a child's clear, unsentimental clarity. She was a marked thing. A keyed thing. A door with legs.

But she was also, apparently, a door that a whole crew of dangerous, grieving, stubborn people had decided to guard with their lives.

Below them, the sun sank behind Thornfell's western teeth, and the mountain's shadow reached out across the valleys like a hand opening. Somewhere in that darkness, something vast was turning toward a small girl's heartbeat, tasting the air, beginning to move.

Wren squeezed Oscar's fingers.

"It's coming, isn't it," she said. Not a question.

"Yes," Oscar said. No lies, not even kind ones. That was one of the thousand reasons Wren loved her.

"Good," said Wren, and the cool lines on her face prickled, and she bared her teeth at the gathering dark like the smallest, fiercest wolf in the pack. "Let it come tired."

* * *

Deep beneath the roots of the world, in the drowned dark where the Ferryman's boat drifted empty and ownerless, the Maw turned its attention north.

It had worn ten thousand faces and eaten a hundred towns, and it remembered none of them, because hunger keeps no ledger. But it kept one thing now, one fixed point in the swirling dark: a small bright brand, walking north with a company of mortals, toward a door in the heart of a mountain.

It began to climb.

And far above, in a smuggler's camp pitched in the teeth of the high passes, Wren woke once in the night, briefly, to see Oscar sitting awake by the banked fire with her father's journal open on her knees, watching the final page as the living map drew itself—line by patient line, downward, inward, toward the mountain's burning heart.

The map had begun to show a second route, branching from the first.

A route no one had chosen.

A route that was drawing itself in the Maw's own handwriting.
