## 22. Ambush at the Weeping Stair

The stair had been weeping for a thousand years before any of them were born to name it.

Cassian felt the water before he saw it—a fine cold mist drifting up from the falls below, beading on his eyelashes, slicking the ancient steps to a glassy sheen. The Weeping Stair clung to the eastern face of Thornfell like a scar of white stone, switchbacking up through the cloud layer in forty-one turns. He'd counted them on the way down, months ago, back when he was still a man who counted things as a way of not feeling them.

Now he counted them again out of habit, and because counting kept his eyes forward and off Oscar's shoulders.

She walked three paces ahead of him, hood back despite the spray, her dark hair plastered to her neck. She hadn't spoken in an hour. Since the third shrine—since they'd crested that last ridge and found nothing but ash fused into the shape of a doorway, Church fire written in every scorch mark—she had gone quiet in a way that frightened him more than shouting would have. Grief he could answer. But this silence was the silence of a woman doing arithmetic with ruin, and the sums kept coming out wrong.

*Without all three altars,* she had said at the edge of the burned shrine, her voice flat as slate, *the covenant can't be renewed the old way.*

*Then we find a new way,* Wren had said, small and fierce, and Cassian had wanted to kiss the child's stubbornness and weep at it in equal measure.

Behind him, Ilsa was singing.

She always sang on descents—or ascents, or anywhere her feet were moving and her hands were empty. Old Duskhollow river songs, bawdy in the verses Mother Quill pretended not to hear, sweet in the refrains no one pretended anything about. Her twin sister Petra walked beside her, harmonizing low, their voices twining the way twins' voices do, as if they'd been one voice split carelessly in two at birth and had spent thirty years learning to live apart.

"—and the grey gull cries where my love lies sleeping, and the water takes what the water keeps—"

The song bounced off the wet stone and came back strange, doubled by echo, so that it seemed the mountain itself was singing with them.

Cassian let himself believe, for eleven steps, that everything was ordinary.

On the twelfth step, the birds stopped.

It wasn't gradual. One moment the crags above were loud with choughs and rock-wrens, and the next there was nothing but water-sound and Ilsa's voice trailing off into the hush like a candle going out. Petra's hand found her sister's arm. Brannoc, hauling the rear with his one eye sweeping the heights, went still in the particular way of a man who has spent forty years reading weather in small silences.

"Down," Brannoc said quietly. "Off the stair. Now."

They were in the open—the whole length of the stair, forty turns of pale stone against dark cliff, nowhere to hide, nowhere to run except up or down and both ways long. Cassian's hand closed around the iron haft of his mace, the old seminary weapon he'd never learned to love and never learned to put down.

"Contact," Petra breathed, and pointed.

They came over the rim of the pass above like ink poured into water. Black-lacquered armor, white surcoats bearing the sun-and-scales of the Inquisition, ropes and grapples and the terrible economy of professionals who had done this a hundred times. Cassian counted twelve. Then fourteen. Then two more appearing on a ledge below—below, they'd been climbed past, hidden in the spray-shadow of the falls—and his stomach turned to lead.

Twenty-six. On a stair forty feet wide at its broadest.

"Inquisitors," Oscar said. Not a question. Her hand was already inside her coat, on the map-case, on whatever desperate thing she carried there.

And then, from the top of the stair, a voice like a bell rung in a tomb.

"Oscar Dravenmoor."

Seraphine Locke stood at the head of the stairs, gold-trimmed white cloak snapping in the wind off the falls, her face composed into something worse than cruelty: certainty. In her raised hand, something glinted—a fragment of metal, old and dark, and even at this distance Cassian felt it pull at something behind his ribs, the way a magnet pulls at iron in the blood.

"You are named in the Ledger of Extinction," Locke called down, and her voice carried impossibly, blessed or cursed by the acoustics of the gorge. "By order of the Holy Conclave, your line ends today. Kneel, and it ends cleanly. Run, and it ends anyway—but messily, and others will bleed for it."

"She means the child," Petra said, very softly. "Others. She means Wren."

Wren stood at the center of their little cluster, and Cassian watched the girl's chin come up, watched the ember-brand on her wrist glow faintly through her sleeve like a coal breathing. "I'm not kneeling," Wren said. "I've knelt enough for one lifetime."

For one absurd, blazing moment, Cassian loved all of them so much he couldn't breathe.

Then the arrows came.

* * *

The first volley shrieked down the stairwell of air and struck stone, sparks skating across wet rock. "Shields!" Brannoc roared—he had no shields, none of them did, and the word was pure habit from a life spent on decks—and they scattered instead, pressing into the shallow alcoves where pilgrims had once sheltered from the spray.

Cassian moved without deciding to move. That was the thing about the body; it remembered its training even when the soul had renounced it. He got Wren behind him, got Oscar behind him, put his broad back to the cliff-face and his mace up, and the world narrowed to angles and distances.

"Split!" Brannoc bellowed. "Up is death—they hold the high ground! Down, through the falls!"

"The falls are a lie," Petra shouted back. "There's a ledge behind the curtain—Ilsa and I forged the survey, remember? It holds maybe four people and it's a hundred-foot drop to the pool—"

"It holds six if six don't breathe deep," Brannoc said. "GO."

They went. And the Inquisitors came down the stair like judgment itself, boots striking wet stone in unison, swords drawn, and the battle of the Weeping Stair began with no declaration at all—just steel finding flesh in the spray and the thin places between heartbeats.

Cassian fought the way he prayed: badly, sincerely, and with his whole self.

A swordsman in black came at him high; Cassian caught the blade on the mace's iron flange and drove his shoulder into the man's chest, sending them both skidding. Another tried to flank toward Wren and met Petra's knife instead, quick and ugly. Ilsa fought with a boat-hook, of all things, swinging it in wide singing arcs, holding the width of the stair at the fourth turn while the others funneled behind her—and gods, she was *good*, better than good, she fought the way she sang, like the motion was older than thought—

"Hold the turn!" Ilsa shouted, laughing, actually laughing, blood already on her sleeve. "Hold the turn and mind your footing, it's slippery as a bishop's conscience—"

An arrow took Brannoc through the meat of his thigh and he went down swearing magnificently. Cassian dragged him into an alcove, tore the man's own belt off to bind the wound, and looked up in time to see the second wave coming—not soldiers now but something worse: two figures in white-and-gold, Inquisitors of the inner rank, moving with the flat grace of people who had trained past exhaustion into something else entirely.

And past them, descending with unhurried steps, came Seraphine Locke herself, her boots never slipping on the wet stone, the seal-fragment glowing in her fist like a dying star.

"Cassian Vale." She knew his name. Of course she knew his name. "The Hollow Lantern himself. They still tell your story in the seminary halls, you know. The priest whose god went deaf." She smiled, and it was almost gentle, which made it monstrous. "Come back to us. Kneel with me, brother, and I'll speak for you at your trial."

"I stopped being your brother," Cassian said, "the day I understood what you were brothers *of*."

He swung. She parried without seeming to move, and the counterblow opened his cheek, and then there was no more talking because there was no breath for it.

* * *

Later—much later, in the smoke and the rain and the terrible quiet after—he would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory broke the fight into shards: Petra screaming somewhere below. Brannoc crawling, dragging his leg, cutting a rope with his belt-knife. Wren's brand flaring so bright it lit the spray red. Oscar at the center of it all, fighting with a cartographer's precision, knowing the stair's every angle because she had *drawn* it, using the wet stone the way she used ink—

And Ilsa, alone at the fourth turn, holding the pass.

Because that was the shape of it, in the end. The Inquisitors had split them, and the only road down ran through the fourth turn, and Ilsa stood at the fourth turn with her boat-hook and her river songs and her sister's name in her mouth, and she held it.

Cassian saw it happen from twenty feet away and thirty years away and no distance at all.

Three of them came at her together, having learned at last that she could not be taken singly. She killed the first—truly killed him, boat-hook through the gap beneath the helm—and staggered the second, and the third stepped inside her reach and put a sword through her belly, and Ilsa, twin of Petra, singer of the grey gull song, *laughed at him*, wrapped both arms around the blade and around the man holding it, and pulled him off the edge of the stair with her.

They fell together into the weeping mist, into the roar below, and the sound of them landing was swallowed by the falls as if the mountain were ashamed of it.

Petra's scream cut through everything. It would live in Cassian's ears until he died. It was not a human sound; it was the sound of one half of a divided whole tearing along the old seam.

"Ilsa—" Petra was running the wrong way, up the stair, toward the fall, toward nothing, arrows whipping past her, and Cassian caught her around the waist and hauled her down and she fought him like a drowning woman fights the sea. "Let go, let GO, she's—she's—"

"I have her," he lied, because there was nothing else to say. "Petra. I have her. Look at me. LOOK at me. If you die here she died for nothing."

That stopped her. Grief is a fire and sometimes only colder grief will smother it. Petra went rigid in his arms, then limp, then turned and buried her face against his shoulder and made a sound he would never describe to anyone, and Cassian held her and watched the stair and waited to die, and found, to his astonishment, that he didn't much mind the waiting anymore.

Which was when he saw the blade meant for Oscar.

* * *

She had driven Locke back. Gods knew how—she fought with a short knife and a mapmaker's geometry, giving ground by inches, always angling, always turning the Inquisitor onto the worst footing—and for one heartbeat Cassian thought, *she might do it, she might actually—*

But Locke had not come alone, and while the golden Inquisitor held Oscar's eyes, her shadow did not. A soldier Cassian hadn't seen—hadn't *been able* to see, tucked into the blind angle of the sixth turn—rose from the rocks above the path with a sword already falling, and Oscar was looking at Locke, and Locke was looking at Oscar, and nobody in the world was looking at the blade except Cassian Vale.

He did not think. Thinking was for afterward.

He crossed the distance the way a man falls crosses a ledge—fast, graceless, committed past recall. He got his body between the sword and hers. He heard Oscar scream his name, heard it the way you hear thunder, felt it more than heard it, and then the blade went into him below the left shoulder blade, angled down, and the world went white and then very, very quiet.

Strange, the things the mind chooses. As he fell he thought of the seminary orchard, of the taste of stolen pears, of the novices' laughter. He thought: *so this is what I was saving it for.* All those years of hoarding his life like a miser who'd forgotten what money buys—and it bought this. Thirty pounds of falling priest. One heartbeat's worth of wall.

He hit the wet stone. The cold of it was enormous.

"Cassian—" Oscar was on her knees beside him, her hands pressing the wound, her blood mixing with his on the rock, and her face was the last beautiful thing in a world rapidly simplifying itself into pain. "No, no, look at me, keep your eyes on me—"

"Still here," he managed. His mouth was full of copper. "Stubborn. You know how I am."

Above them, the tide of battle turned—not won, never won, but broken loose, the way ice breaks loose from a river. Brannoc, bleeding from the thigh, had cut the mooring rope of the old signal-tower at the stair's mid-point, and the whole rotten structure came down the mountainside in an avalanche of timber and stone, scattering the Inquisitor line, sealing the lower turns under a tangle of wreckage. Locke's voice rang out once—*"Fall back! Regroup at the high camp!"*—and then the white cloaks were withdrawing, and the mist was filling in behind them like water closing over a dropped stone.

And then it was just the rain, and the wounded, and the place where Ilsa had been.

* * *

They carried their dead and their dying down to the ledge behind the falls, because there was nowhere else. The hollow behind the waterfall was small and silver-lit, walled in rushing water, loud enough that no one had to speak and quiet enough that everyone could be heard.

Brannoc sat propped against the stone, gray-faced, binding his own leg with the competence of a man who had stitched sails and skin alike. Wren pressed herself against his side, her brand finally dimming, her eyes huge. Petra knelt at the lip of the ledge, looking out through the curtain of falling water at the pool far below, and she had not made a sound since the ledge, and that silence was the loudest thing Cassian had ever heard.

Oscar worked on his wound with shaking hands and steady knowledge—knife-heated over a lamp, needle threaded, the wound probed and packed and closed while Cassian bit down on a strap of leather and stared at the ceiling of falling water and thought about prayer.

He had spent ten years believing his prayers went unheard. Lying on cold stone with an Inquisitor's steel in his back, watching a woman he had no right to love sew him shut like a torn sail, he understood at last that he'd been praying to the wrong thing all along. Not to the silent heavens. To this. To the found and ragged family of the fallen, to the smuggler's oath and the forger's loyalty and the child's stubborn mercy. Whatever covenant bound *them*, it had never gone deaf. He simply hadn't known how to listen.

"Oscar." His voice came out ruined. He tried again. "Oscar. Before I'm too cowardly to say it twice."

"Don't talk. Save your strength."

"No." He caught her wrist, careful of the needle, careless of everything else. Blood loss makes honest men of us all; there was nothing left in him now but truth. "I spent my whole life on my knees asking the sky for answers, and the sky said nothing, and I thought I was broken. I wasn't broken. I was just asking the wrong direction." He laughed, and it hurt, and he didn't stop. "You're the answer, you impossible woman. You've been the answer since the salt road. I love you. I think I've loved you since you argued with me about contour lines. I needed you to know it while I still—"

"Stop." Her voice cracked clean through. She bent over him, forehead pressed to his, her tears falling warm on his face and mingling with the spray. "You're going to live, Cassian Vale. You're going to live and I'm going to make you say it again when you're sober and unbloodied, and then you're going to help me find a new way to save the world, because the old way just died on a staircase." Her hand tightened on his. "Do you understand me? I refuse. I *refuse* to lose anyone else today."

Behind the falling water, Petra rose from the lip of the ledge at last. She walked to the center of the hollow, and she took from inside her coat a scrap of paper—one of her forgeries, some perfect counterfeit of a noble seal—and she held it up to the lamplight, and her face was the face of a woman standing at the edge of a country she'd never visited before.

"They wanted a war," Petra said quietly. "Locke and her conclave and their ledgers of extinction. Fine." She folded the paper away. "I spent thirty years forging documents. I can forge one more. And when we get to that altar of yours, Oscar Dravenmoor, I want to be there when we write the Inquisition's obituary—in *their* handwriting."

Through the curtain of the falls, far above on the ruined stair, something glinted and was gone: a flash of white and gold, watching, waiting.

Locke had not finished with them. And the solstice was nine days away.
