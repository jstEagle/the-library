## 23. Triage and Truth

The cave mouth breathed cold air over them like a dying animal's last exhale.

Oscar had dragged Cassian up the Weeping Stair on her own back for the last hundred yards—after Brannoc carried him the first three hundred, after Petra had bound the wound with strips of Ilsa's own cloak because they had nothing else clean enough and Ilsa was past needing it. The others were still below, holding the pass against pursuit that might or might not come, buying them time with torches and noise and the particular ferocity of people who had already lost too much to lose anything else.

Now it was just the two of them in a smuggler's cache she'd mapped at nineteen—a hollow behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, stocked with old blankets gone musty, a rusted brazier, half a jar of grain spirit, and the kind of silence that only existed this high up the mountain, where even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

Cassian lay on his side on the blankets, his coat balled beneath his head, his shirt cut away from the wound. The blade had gone in under his ribs, angled up and back—the kind of wound that killed slowly if it killed at all, filling a man up with his own blood one breath at a time. It had missed everything vital by what Oscar could only call a mercy, though she'd stopped believing in mercies somewhere around the drowned shrine of Vess.

She knelt beside him with needle and gut-thread and the grain spirit, and her hands, which had never once shaken over a map, shook now.

"You're staring," Cassian said. His voice was rough as gravel under snow. "I've seen you reforge a covenant seal without blinking. You can look at one knife wound."

"I'm not staring." She soaked a cloth in the spirit. "I'm planning."

"Planning."

"Where to put the needle. It's an exact science. Like cartography." She pressed the cloth to the gash and he hissed through his teeth, his hand flying up to grip her wrist—not to stop her, she realized, but to hold on. To anchor himself to something while the fire did its work. His fingers were cold. Everything about him was cold except the blood, which was still warm, which kept coming.

"Ilsa," he said, when he could speak again.

"I know."

"She held the pass. She—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again in a lower voice. "She died holding it. I saw her fall and I couldn't—I couldn't get to her. There were four of them and you were ahead of me and I chose—"

"You chose right." Oscar said it flatly, the way she'd say *north is up*. "You took the blade meant for me. If you'd gone for Ilsa we'd both be dead and the pass would've fallen anyway."

"That's not how it feels."

"No," she said softly. "It never is."

She threaded the needle by feel, her eyes adjusting to the dim blue light that came through the ice curtain. Outside, the waterfall groaned and shifted, shedding its skin of frost a layer at a time. Somewhere far below, faint as memory, she thought she heard shouting—Brannoc's voice, maybe, carrying up the stone—but it might have been the mountain settling. It had been doing a lot of that lately. The whole world had.

"Cassian." She set the needle's point against his skin. "This is going to hurt worse than the blade did. Sewing always does. The body resents being reminded it can be mended."

"Do it."

She sewed. She worked the way her father had taught her to work—with patience, with economy, with a strange tenderness toward the material in her hands—and Cassian bore it the way he seemed to bear everything: silently, jaw tight, eyes fixed on some middle distance where shame lived. She'd noticed that about him weeks ago, back in Duskhollow, back before any of this. He looked away from things. From mirrors, from fires, from the faces of people who spoke kindly to him. As if he'd been excommunicated not just from his church but from his own reflection.

Seven stitches. Eight. On the ninth, his breathing went ragged and she paused, let him ride out the wave, pressed her free hand flat against his sternum until it steadied.

"Breathe," she told him. "In through the nose. Out slow. You've done penance before. This is just another form."

A ghost of a smile touched his mouth. "Penance was easier. Penance, at least, knew what it was punishing me for."

"And what was that?"

His eyes came to hers then—grey eyes, tired, stripped of all their usual careful deflection by pain and blood loss and whatever had happened on that stair when he'd stepped into a blade's path without hesitation, without calculation, without anything but the raw reflex of a man who had decided, somewhere deep and wordless, that her life mattered more than his.

"You know what it was," he said.

"The night your prayers weren't answered."

"The night my prayers *were* answered," he corrected, "and the answer was no." He laughed, and it cost him; she felt the wound pull under her hands. "Three hundred years the gods were silent, Oscar. Three hundred years the Church taught us that silence was holiness—that faith meant speaking into the dark and expecting nothing back. And I believed it. I built my whole life on it. And then one night, when I was twenty-three years old, kneeling in the sanctuary at Vespergate with my sister dying of fever thirty miles away, I prayed harder than I have ever prayed in my life. And something answered."

Oscar went very still.

"It said *no*," Cassian whispered. "Not with words. With absence. A shape of absence, like a hole cut in the world. And my sister died at dawn, and I knew—I *knew*—that the silence everyone called holy was just a door held shut from the other side. That someone, something, had heard me perfectly well and chosen to let her die." His hand found hers where it rested against his chest and gripped it. "So yes. I left the priesthood. They called it disgrace. It was the only honest thing I ever did."

Ten stitches. Eleven. She tied off the thread and sat back on her heels, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the waterfall sang its one long note outside the ice.

* * *

She should have bandaged him and let him sleep. That was the triage, the discipline: treat the wound, rest the patient, save the truth for morning. Her whole life had been built on deferral—tomorrow she'd tell them, someday she'd explain, when the time was right she'd say who she really was. Ten years of tomorrows, folded and filed like the maps she sold, every secret pressed flat between pages.

But Ilsa was dead on a mountain stair. Mother Quill was in an Inquisitor's cage or a shallow grave. Wren was sleeping below with a brand on her arm that pulled hungry gods across the world like a lodestone pulls iron. And Cassian Vale had taken a blade in the ribs for a woman whose real name he didn't even know.

The time was right. The time had been right for miles of road and weeks of lies, and she had simply been too much a coward to see it.

"Cassian." Her voice came out strange. Smaller than she intended. "There's something you need to hear before you sleep. Because if you wake up hating me, I want you to do it with a clean bandage and full knowledge, not rumors."

He shifted against the blankets, wincing, and studied her face. Whatever he saw there made him go quiet. Made him wait.

"My name isn't Oscar Dravenmoor the mapmaker," she said. "Well—it is. But it's more than that. My name is Oskara Dravenmoor, last heir of the Ember Covenant. My family bound the gods after the Sundering. My blood is the lock on the door between this world and Vaelith, and the lock is failing, and everything that's happened since the black rain fell on Wren's village has been falling toward me like water toward a drain." She heard her own voice shake and hated it and kept going anyway. "I've known since I opened my father's journal. I've known since the first altar. I've known since Duskhollow, since the Greywater, since the beggar-king spoke my name in the gutter—and I let you all walk into Inquisitors and hungry gods and a burning stair without telling you what you were carrying. What *I* was carrying."

The silence stretched. The ice curtain glowed and dimmed with the light behind it.

"Why?" he asked finally. Not accusing. Just asking, the way he asked everything—as if the answer might be a kind of scripture.

"Because the moment I say it out loud, it becomes true in a way it wasn't before." She wrapped the bandage around his ribs, her hands moving on autopilot while the rest of her stood at the edge of a cliff she'd been circling for ten years. "As long as it stayed in my head, I could pretend I was still a mapmaker who'd stumbled into something. That there was a version of this where I handed the problem to someone else and walked away. Saying it makes me the heir. Saying it means I can't be anything else." She tied the knot. Smoothed the linen down over the wound with her palm, feeling his heartbeat underneath, stubborn and quick. "And because I was afraid."

"Afraid of what?"

"Afraid you'd look at me the way the Church looks at god-touched things. Afraid you'd see the bloodline instead of the woman. Afraid—" and here her voice did break, finally, cleanly, like ice giving way, "—afraid that you'd realize the only reason you stepped in front of that blade was the covenant, duty, the last heir, and none of it was about *me* at all."

Cassian moved. Slowly, with the care of a man whose every rib was a negotiation, he pushed himself up onto one elbow, and then his hand came up and caught her jaw—gently, so gently, thumb brushing the tear she hadn't known was there.

"Oskara Dravenmoor," he said, and her true name in his mouth sounded like a rite, like something consecrated, "I stepped in front of that blade for a woman who argues with me about theology at two in the morning. Who memorizes smuggler routes for fun. Who paid a ferryman her mother's face and never once complained about the price. Who sews a wound like she's writing poetry and pretends it's an exact science." His forehead came to rest against hers. His breath was warm against her cold lips. "I didn't know your name. I didn't need it. I would have taken that blade for you if you'd been a cobbler's daughter with no drop of divine blood in your whole body. Do you understand? The covenant can have what's left over. *You* were first."

She kissed him—or he kissed her; afterward neither of them could say, and it didn't matter. It was careful and it was fierce and it tasted of grain spirit and copper and ten years of solitude ending all at once, and when they broke apart she was laughing wetly, absurdly, pressing her brow hard against his as if she could weld them together.

"Stay," she said. It came out like a command and a plea in the same breath. "When this is over—if I survive the altar, if the reforging works, if there's any world left worth mapping—stay. Not because the covenant needs a priest. Because I need—"

"Yes," he said.

"—a partner, someone who—"

"*Yes*, Oscar." He smiled, and it transformed his whole exhausted face, wiped years of shame off it like weather off glass. "You don't have to argue me into it. I chose you on a mountain stair with a knife in my ribs. I'm choosing you again now, with eleven stitches and no church and no god's permission required. Wherever you're going—to the heart of Thornfell, to the Ember Altar, to the end of the world and back—I'm going. That's not duty. Duty I know how to recognize, and this isn't it. This is the other thing. The thing I stopped believing existed."

Outside, the light through the ice curtain changed—warmed, reddened, flickered. Oscar turned her head and saw, with a lurch, that it wasn't sunset. Sunset was hours away.

Firelight. Torches, coming up the pass. Many of them.

And beneath the crackle of flame, threading through the roar of the waterfall, she heard it: a sound like vast jaws opening somewhere inside the mountain itself, deep below the stone, deep below the roots of the world—

—and Wren's scream, thin and terrible, rising from the camp below.
