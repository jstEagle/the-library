## 25. The Traitor's Toll

Petra had counted the salt twice.

That was the thing about grief nobody warned you about—it turned you into a counter. After Ilsa died on the Weeping Stair, Petra had discovered that if her hands were busy tallying, weighing, dividing, they could not shake. If her mind was full of numbers, it had no room left for the sound of her sister's last breath, or the way the blood had steamed in the cold, or how Ilsa's hand had still been curled around a half-finished forgery when they buried her under the cairn with Brannoc's ship-coin on her eyes.

So Petra counted. She counted everything.

Rope: eleven coils, three frayed. Arrowheads: forty-one. Dried meat wrapped in waxed cloth: enough for nine mouths across eighteen days of hard climbing, if the mountain did not make them linger. Lamp oil: six flasks. Salt for preserving whatever they might hunt in the high passes: two full sacks from the Duskhollow stores, each one marked with her own chalk sigil so no one would touch them by accident.

Which was why, on the third morning after they'd made camp in the hollow beneath the split crag, she stood over the second sack and knew—with the cold, absolute certainty of an accountant staring at a ledger that refuses to balance—that someone had opened it.

The chalk sigil was still there. That was the clever part, and the damning part. Whoever had done it had redrawn the mark after them. But they'd drawn it *left-handed*, and Petra was right-handed, and Ilsa had been right-handed, and every sigil in this camp that Petra had not personally made in the last week now curved just slightly wrong—a hair's breadth of difference no one else in the world would ever have noticed, because no one else in the world had spent twenty-six years forging documents beside a twin who could tell a forged "e" from a true one at ten paces.

Petra crouched in the grey pre-dawn light and did not move for a long time.

The sack was lighter. Not by much. A careful thief's weight—two handfuls of salt, maybe three, scooped from the bottom where the loss would hide until the sack ran low and by then the camp would be days deeper into the mountains with nothing to preserve meat with and winter coming down off the peaks like a closing fist.

She untied the drawstring and plunged her hands in to the elbow, feeling through the coarse crystals the way she'd once felt through a merchant's ledger for pages shaved thin. Her fingers found it near the bottom: a small cloth bundle, knotted tight, buried like a seed.

She drew it out. Unknotted it with hands that would not tremble, because trembling was a luxury and she had retired all her luxuries the day Ilsa died.

Inside: a folded scrap of paper, and a coin.

The coin was Church-minted. A sunburst on one face, the scales of judgment on the other, gold so bright it looked obscene against the salt and the dirt. The paper held four lines in a neat clerk's hand:

*Route confirmed. They make for the heart-mountain by the old ways. The witch-child travels with them. Payment upon delivery of position. Mark your next signal at the standing stone.*

Below it, a date. Three days past.

Petra read it four times. Then she folded it along its original creases, tucked it inside her coat against her ribs, and sat back on her heels in the freezing dark and let herself feel, for exactly as long as it took the sky to lighten, the full weight of what she was holding.

Someone in this camp was selling them to Seraphine Locke.

* * *

She spent the day watching.

It was a strange, terrible discipline, watching people she loved—or had loved, or had eaten beside and argued beside and pulled out of ditches—for the small betrayals that add up to a large one. She watched Cassian Vale tend his bandaged side and say nothing to anyone about the pain, because that was Cassian, and pain was a debt he insisted on paying privately. She watched Oscar Dravenmoor hunch over her father's journal by the fire, lips moving around dead languages, ember-light catching the new burn-scar that climbed her forearm from the Trial of Embers like a vine of gold. She watched Wren sit close to Oscar without touching, the way a frightened animal sits near the one warm thing it trusts, the faded ghost of the brand still visible on the girl's wrist like a scar of light.

And she watched the others. Brannoc, one-eyed and quiet since he'd lost his ship running the Teeth, mending harness with more care than the harness deserved. Tam and Odile, the young ones, bickering over whose turn it was to haul water. And Doss.

Doss, who had come to Duskhollow four years ago with frostbitten feet and a story about deserting a river-barge crew, and who had turned out to be steady, cheerful, tireless Doss—the man who'd taught Wren to gut fish and laugh while doing it, who'd carried Ilsa's body down from the Stair because Petra's legs had stopped working and no one had asked him to and he had simply done it, weeping, without being asked.

Doss, who was right-handed, and who had spent the last hour sharpening a skinning knife with his left hand.

Petra felt something crack quietly behind her sternum. Not surprise. She had already known, in the way the numbers had known before she did. But knowing a thing in your ledger and watching it breathe in front of you were different countries, and the border between them hurt going across.

Because there was another detail, wasn't there. There always was. Doss had volunteered for water duty both mornings since the camp was set—and the standing stone was by the stream. Doss had taken the second watch, alone, unbothered. Doss had been the one to suggest this hollow beneath the split crag, sheltered, defensible—

—and invisible from the valley road, except to someone who already knew where to look.

* * *

She told Oscar first. Not because Oscar was the leader—Oscar would have bristled at the word—but because Petra had watched her for weeks now, and she had decided that this woman, who gave away godhood the way other people gave away bread, would do the least unforgivable thing with the information. It was a strange kind of trust to place in someone. Petra placed it anyway, because the alternative was placing it in herself, and she no longer trusted what she might do alone with a name and a knife and her sister's grave still fresh.

They told the others at dusk, around the fire, with the paper passed from hand to hand and the Church coin sitting in the dirt where everyone could see it gleam.

No one spoke for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the mountain exhaling.

"Could be a plant," Brannoc said at last, gruff, hopeful. "Could've been planted on us. Someone slips it into the salt in Duskhollow before we left—"

"The sigil was redrawn here," Petra said. "Three nights ago. Left-handed." She looked at the fire, not at Doss. "I checked every mark in camp."

"And the signal?" Cassian asked quietly. He was pale; the wound from the Weeping Stair had gone hot again despite Oscar's poultices, and he sat propped against his pack like a man conserving coin. "Is there a signal already placed?"

"I went at noon," Petra said. "Under pretense of checking the trail. There's a strip of red cloth wired to the north face of the stone. Fresh."

Tam swore. Odile put a hand on his arm. Wren had gone very still, the way she did when the world confirmed its cruelty, and Oscar reached back without looking and found the girl's shoulder and rested her hand there, anchor and stone.

"Doss," Oscar said.

Not a question. Petra watched the man's face across the fire and saw the whole architecture of him collapse in the space of a breath—the cheerfulness going first, then the steadiness, until what remained was just a tired man in his forties with a face like wet paper.

"How long have you known?" Doss asked. His voice came out cracked.

"Since dawn," Petra said. "You?"

He laughed. It was the worst sound Petra had heard since the Stair. "Since before Duskhollow," he said. "Since before any of you." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Gods. Four years. You want to hear it?"

"No," said Tam.

"Yes," said Oscar.

* * *

He told it plainly, which was somehow worse than if he'd lied beautifully.

There had been a daughter. A girl named Mirei, born in the river towns, seven years old when the sweating fever took the whole street. Doss had prayed—to the sanctioned gods, the Crown's gods, the tidy gods of the Church liturgy—and gotten nothing, because for three hundred years the gods had given nothing to anyone. And then the old things had started waking, and word had come upriver of a village where rain fell when a child asked for it, and Doss—who had lost his faith and then his daughter and then, in some order he could no longer reconstruct, his mind—had gone looking for miracles the way a drowned man looks for air.

The Inquisition had found him first. Or rather, he had found them; he'd walked into a garrison chapel and asked a priest whether the stories were true, and the priest had seen in his ruined face exactly what the Church always saw in ruined faces: material.

"They didn't threaten me," Doss said. He was looking at the fire, but Petra doubted he saw it. "That's the thing none of you will believe. They offered. They said: somewhere out there is a girl who can talk to what's left of the divine, and she's traveling with a woman of the Dravenmoor line, and if the covenant comes back—if the old powers come back whole—then everything the Sundering burned stays burned forever, and every prayer anyone ever whispers goes into a hole. They said the girl is the door, and doors get used by whatever walks through them." His jaw worked. "My daughter died screaming for a god to save her and nothing answered. Nothing. And these people—" his hand swept the camp, the maps, the brand-scarred child, the heir, the priest, all of it— "these people are going to open the door again. And I thought: what comes through? What comes through this time?"

"So you sold us," Brannoc said flatly.

"So I sold you," Doss agreed. "For a promise they'll never keep and coin I haven't touched. For the chance that maybe Locke's way is kinder than the alternative." He finally looked up, and his eyes found Wren, and Petra saw the girl flinch and hold her ground, chin up, furious and terrified and brave, twelve years old and already carrying more weight than the whole camp. "I'm sorry," Doss said to her, and his voice broke clean in half. "I'm sorry. I told myself you weren't real. I told myself you were a story they were using. And then I watched you gut fish and laugh, and I kept telling myself it anyway, because the other thing was unbearable."

Silence. The fire burned. High above, the peaks held the last violet light, and somewhere in that light, Petra knew, the Church's riders were reading a strip of red cloth and adjusting their route toward a hollow beneath a split crag.

"What did you tell them?" Oscar asked. Calm. Terribly calm. Petra had learned to hear what lived underneath that calm: a woman deciding, moment by moment, not to become what her ancestors had been.

"Where we camped. How many. That you're bound for the heart-mountain." Doss swallowed. "I didn't tell them about the map. I don't know anything about the map. I couldn't give them what I don't have." A bitter almost-laugh. "Turns out I'm a bad traitor. Couldn't even do that right."

"You told them about Wren," Cassian said. Quietly. Not accusing. Just setting it on the table between them, where it had to lie.

"I told them about Wren," Doss whispered.

* * *

The argument happened in two places at once: out loud, around the fire, and inside Petra, where it was much shorter.

Out loud, Tam wanted him dead. Tam was nineteen and had lost friends to Inquisitor fires before Duskhollow took him in, and he stood with his fists shaking and said the word *hanged* and meant it, and no one could call him wrong. Odile said nothing but stood beside him with her arms crossed, which from Odile was a shout. Brannoc paced the perimeter of the firelight like a caged bear, growling that every hour Doss breathed was an hour the Church knew their position, that sentiment was a luxury the mountains didn't stock.

Cassian argued for mercy, but badly—he was feverish, and his mercy came out sounding like doctrine, and doctrine was the one currency Doss had already proven worthless. Wren said nothing at all. She sat with her knees pulled up and her eyes fixed on Doss with an expression Petra couldn't read until suddenly she could: the girl was trying to decide whether to be afraid of him or sorry for him, and hating herself for considering the second one.

And Oscar listened. That was the thing about her that Petra kept having to relearn: she listened the way stone listens to water, patiently, wearing down. When the voices finally exhausted themselves, she spoke.

"He led soldiers to the waystation," Oscar said. "Before we ever met him. Before any of this. People died there." She looked at Doss. "Did you know that?"

"I heard," Doss said. "After. I heard what happened at the waystation and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I told myself it was necessary. That's what they teach you, in the chapel, when they're turning you. Everything is necessary. Every fire is a wall against a bigger fire." His voice dropped to ash. "I stopped believing it somewhere on the Salt Road. I've been walking with you for weeks knowing what I am and unable to figure out how to stop being it. Because the moment I confessed, one of you would do exactly what Tam wants to do. And I—" he spread his empty hands— "I didn't want to die. Isn't that the filthiest part? All of it, everything I did, and at the bottom of it I'm just a coward who didn't want to die."

Petra stood up.

Every head turned. She hadn't spoken through the whole argument, and they knew better than most what her silence weighed. She walked around the fire until she stood over Doss, close enough to see the pulse jumping in his throat, and she let him see what she was carrying—not the paper, not the coin, but the other thing, the thing under her ribs where she kept Ilsa.

"My sister died holding a pass," Petra said, "so that a child she'd known for two months could live. Mother Quill died in a chair with her spine straight and her mouth shut, and the last thing she gave Locke was contempt, because that was the only thing left in the larder that was hers to give." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that, in a distant, sick way. "I have spent every night since the Stair imagining what I would do to the person who made those deaths cheap. And now here you are. And here's the joke, Doss. Here's the filthy joke." She crouched, eye level with him. "Killing you costs us nothing. That's why it's worthless. A blade is free. Anyone can pay it. Even me. Especially me—gods know I want to. But mercy—" and here her voice did finally shake, one hairline fracture, and she hated it— "mercy is expensive. Mercy means I carry you in my ledger forever as a debt unpaid. Mercy means every mile from here to the heart-mountain, I sleep with one eye open wondering. Mercy means I let my sister's death stay expensive instead of making yours cheap to balance the books." She straightened. "I vote exile. And I want it recorded that I hate it. I want it recorded that it tastes like rot. Because if we kill you, we're just the Church with worse singing."

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then Oscar nodded, slow, like a verdict settling. "Exile," she said. "Tonight. South fork, down to the lowlands. You'll have three days' food and no weapon, and if you signal the Church—if so much as a scratch mark reaches them—we will know, and there won't be a second council." She paused. "But you'll live. And you'll live with it. That's not kindness, Doss. That's the sentence."

"It's worse," Doss whispered. He was crying now, openly, without shame, a middle-aged man weeping in the firelight in front of the family he'd sold. "Gods forgive me, it's worse."

"That's rather the point," said Cassian.

* * *

They sent him off at first light, before the sun cleared the eastern ridge. Brannoc packed his food himself, grimly generous. Tam refused to say goodbye and stood facing away, shoulders rigid, and Odile put a hand briefly on his back, and that was the whole of the farewell from them. Wren was the surprise. She walked up to Doss at the edge of camp while the others pretended not to watch, and she said something to him too low for anyone else to catch, and whatever it was, it hit him like a hammer; he folded forward over her small hand where she'd gripped his wrist, and then she let go and walked back to the fire and didn't look at him again.

Petra walked him to the south fork. Neither of them spoke for the first mile. Frost silvered the grass; their breath hung in flags.

At the fork, where the trail dropped toward the river country and everything Doss had chosen over them, he stopped. "Your sister," he said. "On the Stair. She was laughing. Ten seconds before. At one of my stupid jokes." His voice was wrecked. "I need you to know I know what I spent."

Petra looked at him for a long time. The sunrise came up over the ridge and lit the frost to fire, and far to the north, faint as a coal under ash, Thornfell's highest peak wore its perpetual crown of cloud.

"Ilsa forgave people for a living," Petra said finally. "Forgeries, debts, betrayals—she used to say everyone's handwriting shakes when they're scared, you just have to learn to read it anyway." She turned back down the trail. "I'm not her. Read that however you like."

She didn't look back. But she counted his footsteps fading behind her—all the way to thirty, all the way to silence—and she hated herself for counting, and she counted anyway, because the counting was all she had left of the habit of caring, and she was not yet ready to put that down.

When she reached camp, Oscar was waiting at the fire with the journal open on her knees and an expression Petra had learned to dread: the look of a woman holding news that would cost them all something.

"We have a problem," Oscar said. "The clans control the only pass to the heart-mountain. And they'll want proof of who I am before they open it." She closed the journal. "They've summoned me to a trial by fire."

Petra sat down heavily beside her, took out her chalk, and began, out of pure stubborn habit, to count the arrows again.

"Then we'd better make sure you survive it," she said.
