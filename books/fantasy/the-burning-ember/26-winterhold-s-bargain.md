## 26. Winterhold's Bargain

The mountain clans did not send invitations. They sent a raven with a strip of birch bark tied to its leg, and on the bark, burned into the wood rather than written in any ink, was a single mark: an ember inside a circle.

Oscar held it up to the firelight in the ruined waystation they'd made camp in and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"That's the old covenant sigil," Cassian said quietly. He was standing at the window, watching the snow come down over the pass in long grey sheets. "The one your family used. The one that's supposed to be extinct."

"It's a summons." Brannoc took the bark from her fingers and turned it over with his one eye narrowed. "Clan Winterhold doesn't burn marks for strangers. They burn them for debts."

"Then someone remembers the Dravenmoors." Oscar said it before she could stop herself, and the name sat strange in her mouth—her name, the buried one, the one only four living people now knew. It had been three weeks since she'd spoken it aloud to Cassian by firelight with his blood soaking her hands, and still it felt like handling a blade by the edge.

"They remember more than that," Mother Quill would have said. But Mother Quill was ash in an Inquisitor's brazier, and every time Oscar reached for the old woman's voice—the gruff certainty of her, the way she'd say *girl* like it was a title and not an age—the grief caught in her throat like a fishhook.

They went anyway. There was nowhere else to go.

The third altar was cinders. The Church had seen to that, and Locke's defection had bought them truth but not time: without all three shrines, the renewal could not follow the old road, which meant the Ember Altar beneath Thornfell was no longer merely their destination but their only one, and the Maw was growing fat on everything between here and there. They needed the clans. The clans controlled the deep roads—the smuggler ways were Oscar's, but the *mountain's own* veins, the heat-vents and hollow galleries where even her father's maps grew vague, belonged to the people who had lived inside the rock since before the Sundering.

And the clans, the raven made clear, would give nothing to a stranger.

* * *

Winterhold was less a village than a wound in the mountainside—a vast mouth of black stone opening above the tree line, its entrance hung with banners of cured hide dyed rust-red, its thresholds carved with spirals so ancient the wind had begun to eat them back into suggestion. Torches burned in niches along the approach, and Oscar noticed, as they climbed the switchback path, that none of them guttered in the wind. The flames stood perfectly straight, like soldiers at attention.

"They're fed from below," Wren whispered beside her. The girl hadn't left her side since Duskhollow; since the brand; since the night the Maw had marked her and the crew had looked at one another across her small sleeping form and silently agreed, without a word spoken, that nothing would take her. "The mountain breathes warm. Brannoc told me. There's fire under everything here."

"There's fire under everything everywhere," Oscar said. "Most places just keep it buried better."

A hundred eyes watched them climb. She could feel them the way she'd learned to feel weather coming—pressure behind the eyes, a prickle at the nape. Faces in the high windows. Shapes on the ledges. Children peering around their mothers' legs with the frank, unblinking stare of children who had never been taught that staring was rude, because in Winterhold nothing came up the mountain uninvited, and anything invited was worth looking at.

At the threshold stood the gate-warden: a woman built like the mountain herself, broad and weathered, grey braids bound with iron rings, a scar splitting one eyebrow into two white wings. She carried no weapon that Oscar could see, which somehow made her more frightening, not less.

"You're the map-witch," the warden said. Not a question.

"I'm a cartographer."

"You're the last Dravenmoor." The warden let the name fall into the cold air like a stone into a well, and Oscar heard it drop and drop and never hit bottom. Around the threshold, the watching faces went very still. Somewhere above, a child stopped fidgeting. "We have songs about your house. None of them kind."

"I've heard some of them," Oscar said. "I'm hoping you'll tell me the rest."

Something flickered in the warden's face—not warmth, exactly, but the acknowledgment one predator gives another. She stepped aside. "The Clanmother will see you. Leave your steel at the post. All of it. Yes, even the little knife in your boot, cartographer, we counted it when you were two hundred paces out."

Oscar surrendered her blades—one, two, and the third, with dignity—and walked into the mountain.

* * *

The Clanmother's hall was a cathedral of firelight. The ceiling arched up into darkness where the smoke vanished without staining, and the walls were hung with generations of clan history rendered in woven wool and hammered copper: hunts and weddings, avalanches survived, wars remembered mostly through who had died standing. At the far end, on a seat carved from a single rib of black stone, sat a tiny woman wrapped in furs, so old that her skin had gone translucent as vellum, so that when the firelight moved behind her Oscar could see the faint map of veins at her temples, blue rivers on pale country.

Her eyes, though. Her eyes were young. Black and quick and utterly unsentimental, and they fixed on Oscar the moment she entered and did not let go.

"So," the Clanmother said. Her voice was dry paper, but it carried. "The ember writes letters again after three hundred years, and it sends us a thin woman with ink on her fingers. Kneel, if your knees work. I'm old, not blind—I can see you want to argue, and arguing is done standing, so kneel first and then be disappointed."

Oscar knelt. Beside her, she heard Cassian do the same, and Brannoc, with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and Wren, small and solemn, copying the adults because that was how children learned reverence.

"The seal is failing," Oscar said. "You know this. Your torches are fed by the mountain's blood, and the mountain's blood runs close to Vaelith—you've felt the tremors, you've lost hunters to things in the deep galleries that shouldn't have faces. I've come to ask for passage through the clan roads to the heart of Thornfell. And for your spears, if you'll give them, though I'll take the passage alone if that's what's offered."

"And what do you offer?"

"My name. My blood. Whatever the covenant requires of me."

The Clanmother laughed. It was a terrible sound, like ice cracking off a roof in spring. "Your blood. Child, do you know how many people have come into this hall offering their blood? Miners with blacklung. Mothers with starving babes. Men who'd sold their sisters' teeth to buy drink. Everyone offers blood, because blood is the one coin everyone carries and no one counts the cost of until it's spent." She leaned forward, and the firelight slid across her face like water. "The Dravenmoors bound the gods. That much is true in our songs, whatever else is true besides. And the binding is failing because the binders' line has thinned to—you." Her black eyes traveled down Oscar's body, taking inventory: the ink stains, the calluses, the new scar along her forearm from the Weeping Stair. "So we will not take your word, or your blood in a cup, or your pretty confession. You will walk the Trial of Embers. Survive it, and the deep roads open to you, and forty spears march under your banner. Fail it—" a shrug, delicate and awful, "—and the mountain keeps what it keeps."

"What is the trial?" Cassian asked. His voice was level, but Oscar heard the strain under it, the priest's instinct to interpose himself between danger and other people worn smooth by years of failure into something quieter and more stubborn.

The Clanmother smiled at him with genuine pleasure, the way a cat smiles at a bird that talks. "Ah. The fallen priest. We know you too, Cassian Vale. Your Church burned a shrine of ours in the year of your birth—did they teach you that in seminary? No? They leave such things out of the nice books." She turned back to Oscar before he could answer. "The trial is simple. There is a vent in the mountain's flank, an old throat where the first fires came up when the world was young. The clan walks it once in a generation, when a chief must be chosen or a debt must be paid. The fire is real. The fire is holy. Walk from the low arch to the high arch without turning back, without screaming, without dying, and you are clan-friend forever—marked and witnessed. Turn back, scream, or die, and we bury what's left of you with respect and speak of you kindly, which is more than most get."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave the way you came, and the Maw eats the lowlands, and eventually the lowlands' trouble climbs up here, and we deal with it ourselves, as we always have." The Clanmother settled back into her furs. "We do not need you, Dravenmoor. Understand that clearly before you choose. We are offering you a door, not begging you to walk through it."

Oscar knelt on the cold stone and thought about her father's journal, and the lies folded into it like letters into envelopes; about Mother Quill's contempt for mercy that cost nothing; about the Ferryman's toll and her mother's face, gone now, traded away, so that when she tried to remember the woman who had sung her to sleep she got a warm shape and no features, a portrait with the paint scraped off. Everything worth having on this road had been paid for in advance, and the price had only ever gone up.

She thought, too—with a shame that arrived late but arrived honestly—about the whisper of power that lived under her sternum now, the inheritance waking in her blood, and how part of her, a small hot part she didn't like to look at, wanted to stand up and say *I don't need your trial, I am the heir of the Ember Covenant, kneel to me.* The power would do it, too. She could feel it coiling, eager, offering.

That was exactly why she couldn't use it. And exactly why the trial frightened her less than it should have, and more.

"I'll walk," she said.

"Of course you will," said the Clanmother. "You're your grandmother's granddaughter, whether you know it or not. We'll begin at moonrise. Eat first. The fire is less cruel to the well-fed—it's a superstition, probably, but our dead were all hungry, so we don't risk it."

* * *

They fed them smoked fish and dark bread and a broth that tasted of pine and iron, and afterward Cassian found her on a ledge overlooking the great vent field, where the mountain exhaled columns of shimmering heat into the freezing night, so that the snow above the vents fell upward instead of down, a slow silver rain ascending.

"You don't have to do this," he said.

"Yes, I do."

"That's not what I mean." He came to stand beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, not quite. He had gotten good at that distance—near enough to be felt, far enough to be refused. "You could refuse. We'd find another way. I'd find another way. I've spent ten years finding ways around impossible things for people with worse odds than yours."

"And how many of those ways worked?"

He was quiet for a moment. Below them, a vent sighed and shifted, and the upward snow bent sideways. "Some," he admitted. "Enough to keep trying."

"Cassian." She watched the heat-shimmer rather than him; it was easier to say things to the light. "Every hard thing on this road, I've paid for with pieces of myself, and I've noticed something. The pieces grow back different. The memory-toll took my mother's face, and what grew in the empty place was resolve, or stubbornness, or scar tissue—I can't tell which. The ambush took Ilsa, and what grew in was this—" she touched her own chest, "—this cold arithmetic I do now, counting who's expendable, hating myself for counting. If I let the clans' fear make my choices, or the Church's cruelty, or the Maw's hunger, then everything I am when this ends will have been authored by them. This trial is the first hard thing anyone's asked of me that I actually get to choose." She finally turned her head. "Let me choose it."

He looked at her for a long moment, firelight and vent-light moving on his face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly. "Then I'll be at the low arch," he said. "And if you turn around—if you decide halfway that it's too much—I will be the first person cheering, and I will fight anyone who calls it cowardice. Do you understand me? Walking in isn't the brave part. Walking out alive is the only part I care about."

"That's very unhelpful, spiritually speaking."

"I was a very bad priest," he said, and the ghost of a smile crossed his face, "and I intend to keep improving."

* * *

The vent field at moonrise was a landscape from the world's first morning. Forty vents breathed in staggered rhythm, some whispering, some roaring, the ground between them glowing faintly through cracks like veins of lava in black glass. The clan had assembled along both rims—hundreds of them, silent, their rust-red banners hanging motionless in the windless cold. The path itself ran straight as a spearcast between the burning throats: perhaps two hundred paces of stone that the Clanmother, borne on a litter to the rim, described as "warm, no warmer than a baker's oven at full proof, mostly, except where it isn't."

"Mostly," Oscar repeated.

"Faith is mostly," said the Clanmother serenely.

Cassian stood at the low arch. He didn't touch her—he knew better, the wardens had made the rules plain—but he caught her hand in the last instant before the threshold, squeezed once, hard, and said, so quietly only she could hear: "Come back and tell me what it's like. That's all. Come back and tell me."

Wren pressed something into her other palm: a twist of thread, dyed red. "It's from my sleeve," the girl whispered fiercely. "From home. It's not magic or anything. It's just—so you carry something that wants you to come back."

Oscar closed her fist around it and stepped through the arch.

The heat hit her like a hand.

Not gradually—there was no gentle gradient, no merciful acclimation. The first ten paces were a wall, and she walked into it the way she imagined a swimmer walks into winter surf, breath punched flat, every nerve shrieking that this was wrong, wrong, wrong, that flesh was not meant for this, that the body's whole ancient wisdom said *turn around, turn around now.* Sweat sprang up all over her and began to steam. The hem of her coat browned. The air itself tasted of copper and struck at her lungs like swallowed sparks.

She kept walking.

Ten paces. Twenty. The vents roared on either side, and within the roar—she would swear this later, under oath, to anyone who asked—she heard voices. Not whispers, not the seductive murmur of the power in her blood, but something older and more indifferent: the mountain's own voice, or the memory of gods passing through it in the world's childhood, syllables too vast to parse. The heat pressed on her not like punishment but like attention. Like being read.

Thirty paces. Her boots were smoking at the welts. A blister opened on her right palm where the thread was clenched, and the pain was oddly clarifying, a bright clean line drawn through the roar. *This is real,* the pain said. *You are really here. Now be here.*

Forty. Fifty. Halfway. The crowd on the rims had begun a low chant, rhythmic, wordless, a heartbeat in a hundred throats, and the sound reached her through the fire like a hand reaching through a window. She thought of Mother Quill saying *family isn't the blood you're given, girl, it's the blood you'd spill,* and her eyes stung, and the sting might have been smoke.

Seventy. Eighty. The heat changed. It stopped pushing against her and began, impossibly, to move with her, flowing around her shoulders like a mantle, and she understood with a lurch that this was the test's true face—not endurance but recognition. The fire was deciding. The fire was reading her blood, the thinned and tangled and lied-about blood of the Dravenmoors, and asking itself a question she couldn't hear, and the chant of the clan rose and rose—

—and somewhere in the last thirty paces, she stopped fighting.

She didn't stop walking. But she stopped bracing, stopped clenching, stopped holding herself apart from the heat as a thing to be survived, and simply let it be what it was: the oldest thing she would ever meet, older than the covenant, older than the binding, older than the theft her family had dressed up as salvation. She walked through it the way she walked through unmapped country—attentive, humble, taking notes with her whole body.

The high arch received her like a doorway home.

She stumbled through it into the blessed cold and stood there steaming, shaking, half-deaf from the roar, and became aware gradually that the chanting had become cheering, that hundreds of voices were shouting her name—not *Dravenmoor*, but a clan-name she didn't yet know, a word that meant, she would learn tomorrow, *she who walked and was not consumed*—and that Cassian had crossed some line he wasn't supposed to cross and was holding her upright, laughing, actually laughing, his forehead pressed to her singed temple.

Wren was crying. Brannoc was roaring something celebratory and profane. On her right forearm, where the heat had kissed her longest, a mark was rising through the reddened skin: a spiral, fine-lined, ember-colored, the clan's oldest sign of welcome.

"She's marked!" the gate-warden bellowed, delighted, terrifying. "Marked and witnessed! Forty spears for the Dravenmoor! Feast tonight! And someone get the poor woman a coat, she looks like a roasted pigeon!"

Later—much later, when the feast had burned down to embers and the hall had gone quiet and Wren slept against Brannoc's shoulder—Oscar sat alone by the hearth, turning her marked arm in the firelight, when the Clanmother's litter whispered up beside her.

"You did well," the old woman said. Then, softer, in a voice stripped of all its performance, all its theater: "Too well. The fire recognized you faster than it has any walker in nine generations. Which means the blood in you is stronger than we were told the Dravenmoor line had become." Her black eyes glittered. "Which raises questions, child. Questions about what your ancestors *really* did at the Sundering, and why our songs about your house are not kind. You've earned answers. Come to my chamber at dawn, and I will tell you what the fire told me tonight when it sang your name."

She paused at the edge of the firelight, a small bundle of fur and shadow.

"And pray to whatever you still pray to, Dravenmoor, that you can live with it."
