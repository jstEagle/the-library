## 27. The Clanmother's Secret

Wren had never seen a hall built from a mountain's own bones before.

The clanhold of the Emberkin was carved into the living rock above the tree line, its walls ribbed with veins of pale stone that caught the firelight and held it, so that the whole chamber seemed to breathe with a slow amber pulse. Wren sat close to the great hearth, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of juniper smoke and sheep's wool, and tried not to stare at the clanmother.

It was difficult. Old Maren Ashveil was not a woman one could easily stop staring at. She was small and bent as a winter branch, her face seamed like old leather left too long in the sun, but her eyes—her eyes were young and sharp and dark as flint, and they missed nothing. She had watched Wren all through supper without pretending otherwise, and now, while the others drifted toward their sleeping alcoves and Oscar stood at the far end of the hall speaking in low tones with Brannoc about the morning's road, the clanmother rose from her carved stone chair and crossed the floor to sit beside Wren on the hearth bench.

She moved slowly, but there was nothing frail in the way she settled herself. Her hands, knotted like roots, folded in her lap.

"You're the one who prays," she said.

Wren's stomach dropped. "I don't—not anymore. I stopped."

"Mm." The clanmother poked the fire with an iron rod, sending up a fountain of sparks. "Aye, I'd expect you'd say that. But the brand on your arm says different. Brands are prayers that got answered too loud, girl. They don't fade just because you've learned to whisper."

Wren pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders. Under it, against her skin, the ember-shaped mark lay quiet for once—a dull warmth rather than the burning ache it had been since the Maw had marked her fully in the valley below Winterhold. She had learned, in these last weeks, to read its moods the way she'd once read the sky over her village for rain. Tonight it felt almost drowsy. Almost content.

"Oscar says you'll help us," Wren said carefully. "That you know the old roads under the mountain."

"I know more than roads." Maren Ashveil turned those flint eyes on her again. "I knew your Dravenmoor, when she was a cartographer's daughter who didn't know what she was. I knew her father, too, before the Church burned his work out of him. And before him, his father. My people have kept watch on the Dravenmoor line for nine generations, child. That's what my title means. Clanmother of the Watch." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was a tired one. "We were promised we'd only ever need to watch."

"Watch for what?"

"For the day the binding failed." The old woman said it plainly, the way you'd say *the well has run dry* or *the snows came early*. "And now it's failing. And now here you all come down out of the passes, asking for our roads and our spears, because the last Dravenmoor means to reforge the covenant at the Ember Altar." She shook her head slowly. "You should eat more. You're thin as a winter wren. Fitting name, that."

"Please," Wren said, and hated how small her voice came out. "If you know something's wrong—if you know why we shouldn't do this—you have to tell us. People are dying. Whole towns. The Maw ate Harrowgate, it ate—" Her throat closed. She made herself go on. "It ate the Ferryman. If Oscar can fix the seal, all of that stops."

The clanmother was quiet for a long moment. The fire cracked and spat. Somewhere deeper in the hold, a baby cried and was hushed.

"Do you know what the Sundering was?" Maren asked at last.

"The war. When the gods went mad and broke the world. Everyone knows that story. The priests tell it—the ones who still bother. The gods grew proud, and they fought each other, and mortals suffered, and so the heroes rose up and bound them behind the veil to save us all."

"A fine story." The clanmother nodded approvingly. "Clean. Simple. Heroes and mad gods. Every village has a version of it, and every version ends the same way: the good folk won, and the bad gods lost, and everyone lived safer ever after." She leaned closer, and her voice dropped low enough that Wren had to lean in too, close to the juniper smoke and the smell of old stone. "Now let me tell you the version my grandmothers told their daughters, in this hold, where no priest ever came to correct them. Are you listening?"

Wren nodded.

"The gods did not go mad first," Maren Ashveil said. "They were robbed first. And then they went mad."

* * *

The words hung in the smoky air between them, and Wren found she had stopped breathing.

"The Dravenmoors were not heroes," the clanmother continued, settling back as though she had merely remarked on the weather. "Oh, they were powerful—that part of the story is true. In the last age, the line of Dravenmoor were god-speakers. Intercessors. The veil between Vaelith and the mortal realm was thin in the high places, and the Dravenmoors were the ones who walked through it, who carried mortal prayers up and divine answers down. Trusted by both sides. Beloved by both sides." Her mouth twisted. "And both sides trusted them exactly as far as a lamb trusts a shepherd."

"What happened?"

"What always happens, when a door stands open and only one family holds the key." Maren's voice hardened. "The Dravenmoors looked at the gods—all that power, all that terrible shining weight—and they asked themselves a question no honest person should ever ask. Not *how do we serve?* but *why should they have it and not us?*"

Wren thought of Oscar. Of the way she'd stood at the drowned shrine of Vess, blood running down her wrist into the black water, her face lit with something that wasn't quite awe and wasn't quite fear. Of the way she'd admitted, at the cliffside shrine of Aurel, in a voice that shook: *I want it. Not just the duty. I want the power.* And how the seal had strengthened anyway, as if honesty were a kind of payment.

"They made a covenant," the clanmother said. "But understand what that word meant to them. Not a promise between equals. A snare dressed in holy language. They invited the gods to a great rite—a sharing, they called it, a mingling of essence, mortal and divine, to bind the two realms in harmony forever. And the gods, foolish and trusting, because trust was the whole nature of them, came to the altar." Her flint eyes fixed on Wren. "And the Dravenmoors poured them out like wine."

"Poured them—"

"Took them into themselves. All of it. Every scrap of divinity the gods consented to give—and more, far more than any consented to give. The covenant was a vessel, and the vessel had a false bottom, and everything the gods poured into it drained away into Dravenmoor veins instead of back into Vaelith." The old woman's hands tightened on her iron rod until the knuckles whitened. "The gods woke up hollowed. Emptied. What was left of them tore at the veil in agony and rage, trying to take back what was stolen, and the tearing is what your priests call the Sundering. The world didn't break because the gods went mad, child. The world broke because they were mugged by the very family that claimed to be saving them."

The fire popped. Wren became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking.

"But—but the seal," she managed. "The seal keeps them out. Keeps everyone safe. If the Dravenmoors stole the power, why would they make a seal that holds the gods back?"

"Because thieves build locks too." Maren's laugh was short and bitter. "Once you've emptied a house, you bar the door so the owners can't come home. The binding was never mercy. It was a vault door. The Dravenmoors locked the starving remnants of the gods outside the world and kept their stolen inheritance inside their own bloodline, generation after generation, thinning with each passing heir, hoarded like a dragon's pile. And the Church—" she spat, deliberately, into the flames, "—the Church either never knew or chose not to, because a world with a locked heaven and a licensed priesthood suited them just fine. Three centuries they've preached the hero-story while the true heirs hid in plain sight, drawing maps."

Wren's gaze went, without meaning to, across the hall—to where Oscar stood with her arms crossed, firelight catching the ink stains on her fingers, laughing at something Brannoc had growled. Laughing. As if the ground beneath her whole life weren't about to open.

"She doesn't know," Wren whispered.

"No. Her father likely half-knew and buried it deep; men often learn the truth of their houses late and choose the lie that lets them sleep. But the blood remembers what the mind forgets." The clanmother reached out and took Wren's chin in her root-knotted hand, gentle but immovable, turning her face so their eyes met. "And here is the part that concerns *you*, little wren. That brand on your arm. You think it's a curse. You think it's the Maw's hook in your flesh. But brands are keys, and keys answer to locks, and the oldest lock in this world is the covenant itself. The Maw hunts you because the Maw is hungry—and what does a starved thing hunger for most?"

Wren's mouth was dry. "What was stolen."

"What was stolen." The clanmother released her chin. "Every remnant god scratching at the veil can smell the Dravenmoor inheritance in the world like a dog smells blood under a floorboard. And the strongest scent of all comes off the one marked to witness the reforging. You're not bait, child. You're a bell. And every hollow god in Vaelith can hear you ringing."

* * *

Across the hall, Oscar laughed again at something Brannoc said, and the sound carried in the stone room, warm and unguarded, and Wren felt tears rise hot and sudden in her eyes.

Because she liked Oscar. She loved her, in the fierce, astonished way you love people who pull you half-dead out of a snowdrift on a mountain pass and ask nothing in return. Oscar, who gave up her mother's face to the Ferryman without hesitation. Oscar, who had confessed at Aurel's shrine that she wanted the power—and had chosen the slow, hard, honest path anyway. Whatever poison was in the Dravenmoor blood, it hadn't poisoned *her*. Had it?

"You're going to tell her," Wren said. It wasn't a question. "Tonight."

"I'm going to tell her," Maren agreed. "My people swore an oath of watching, not an oath of silence, and the hour for watching is done. But I'll tell you something else, wren, and you mark it well." She gripped Wren's shoulder with surprising strength. "The truth will cut her. Truths like this always cut. But look at her—look at what she's already paid, what she's already refused. A thief's gold spends the same whether the spender knows its history or not. What matters is what she *does* with it. And if she pours that stolen inheritance back into the altar freely—if she gives the gods back what her house took—then the sin of the Dravenmoors dies with the last Dravenmoor, and something new is born in its place. Something clean."

"And if she can't?" Wren's voice cracked. "If learning this breaks her? If the power in her hears this and—and likes it?"

The clanmother looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she rose, joints cracking like green wood, and beckoned Wren to follow.

They crossed the hall together. Oscar saw them coming and straightened, her easy expression folding away into alertness—the mapmaker reading new terrain. Behind her, Cassian set down his cup. Brannoc's one eye narrowed.

"Clanmother," Oscar said. "You wanted to see me alone earlier. I assumed the trial terms—"

"The trial is done and you passed it. This is another matter." Maren Ashveil planted herself before Oscar, small and bent and utterly unafraid, and Wren saw that the old woman's hands were trembling now, just slightly, as though the words she carried had weight enough to shake even her. "Nine generations my family watched yours, Oscar Dravenmoor. We watched because we were promised the truth would only ever be needed once. I am the last of the Watch, and the hour has come, and I find—" her voice roughened, "—I find I am sorry for it. Sit down, heir of the Covenant. There is a story you must hear, and it is not the one your father's journal tells."

Oscar glanced at Wren—at her tear-streaked face, her white knuckles—and Wren saw the exact moment the mapmaker understood that the terrain ahead had changed. She saw Oscar's jaw tighten. She saw her glance at Cassian, and Cassian step closer without being asked.

Then Oscar pulled out the bench and sat, folding her ink-stained hands on the table like a woman preparing to chart a coastline she already suspected was haunted.

"Tell me," she said.

And the clanmother began to speak, and the fire burned low, and Wren sat beside the last Dravenmoor and listened to the foundation of everything crumble—one stolen god, one poisoned inheritance, one buried century at a time—while somewhere far beneath them, in the mountain's heart, the Ember Altar waited to learn what its heir was truly made of.
