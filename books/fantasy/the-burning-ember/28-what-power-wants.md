## 28. What Power Wants

The fire had burned down to its red arithmetic when Oscar finally sent Wren to sleep.

It had taken an hour of gentle insistence—the girl had wanted to keep watch, wanted to be useful, wanted, Oscar suspected, to stay close to the one person in the world who might understand what it meant to learn that your whole inheritance was a theft. *Your legacy is stolen goods.* The clanmother's words had lodged in Oscar like a splinter too deep to draw. She had smiled at Wren, told her that watching was a skill and sleeping was a weapon, and the girl had gone reluctantly to her bedroll beside Ilsa's empty place—the space they still left for her, every night, because no one had found the words to stop.

Now the camp slept. Brannoc snored like a landslide deciding whether to happen. Petra murmured numbers in her dreams, always numbers. Somewhere beyond the ring of stones, the mountain wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages turning.

Oscar sat alone with her father's journal open on her knees, and the thing inside her sat alone with her, and neither of them was sleeping.

*You could make her take it back.*

The voice did not come through her ears. It never did. It arrived the way warmth arrives through a wall—already inside the room, already hers, wearing her own cadence like a borrowed coat. It had been doing this since the Trial of Embers, since she walked through living flame and came out marked from wrist to shoulder with lines like cooling lava, since the clans pressed their fists to their chests and called her *heir* as if the word were a blessing instead of an accusation.

*The old woman lied,* the warmth said. *Or she misremembered. Old women do. Go back. Ask again. Or better—don't ask. Look. You have the sight now. Reach into her mind the way you reach into a map, and see the truth for yourself.*

"I don't reach into minds," Oscar whispered. "I'm not—that's not what I do."

*You reached into Vess's drowned shrine. You reached into Aurel's cliff. You are reaching right now, little cartographer. Every rite you performed opened you wider. You are a door that has forgotten it can close.*

She looked down at her hands. In the firelight they were ordinary hands—ink-stained, nicked by compass blades, the knuckles scarred from a decade of hauling herself up scree slopes no sane person climbed. But beneath the skin, faint as veins under river ice, the ember-marks glowed. They had been glowing more often lately. They glowed when she was angry. They glowed when she was afraid.

They were glowing now.

"That's not an answer," she said to the dark. "That's a threat dressed as an observation."

*It is an invitation.*

She closed the journal. That was a mistake, she realized a moment too late, because closing the journal left her hands empty, and empty hands were an invitation of their own.

*Here is what I offer,* the power said, settling closer, and oh, it was seductive, it was so terribly seductive, not because it flattered but because it understood. It understood the shape of the wound the clanmother had cut. *You spent ten years being no one. Ten years selling maps to merchants who never learned your name, memorizing smuggler roads you could never tell anyone about, burying Dravenmoor so deep even you forgot where you'd put her. And now you learn the burial was deserved. Your blood bound the gods not to save the world but to cage it. Your ancestors were thieves. Everything you might have been proud of is a crime scene.*

"Stop."

*But I can fix it,* the warmth said gently. *Not the past. The past is stone. But the knowing—I can soften the knowing. One small working. You would barely feel it. Take the splinter out. Take the doubt out. Take the memory of the clanmother's face, the pity in it, the contempt—take all of it and set it down somewhere far away, and wake tomorrow light as you were before Duskhollow, before the waystation burned, before any of it. You would still do the work. You would still reforge the covenant. You would simply do it without this grinding weight. Is that not mercy? Are you not allowed mercy?*

And here was the horror of it, the thing Oscar would later struggle to explain to Cassian without sounding mad: the offer was good. It was genuinely, reasonably good. She turned it over in her mind the way she'd turn a suspected forgery in lamplight, looking for the flaw, and the flaw kept hiding. What was wrong with setting down a grief? People prayed for exactly this. Whole religions were built on the promise of it. *Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden.*

But she had read enough of her father's journal now to know how the old Dravenmoors had talked. *For the greater ease of the realm*, they had written, in their beautiful hands, as they chained gods into the dark. *That none need suffer the burden of the divine.* They had been merciful too. Mercy was just another word for taking something away and calling the absence peace.

"No," she said aloud, and her breath fogged though the night wasn't cold. The ember-lines brightened along her forearm, and the warmth gathered itself, patient as erosion. "No. Because once I start editing—" Her voice cracked. She pressed on. "Once I start editing, where does it end? Today the doubt. Tomorrow the grief for Ilsa. Next week the fear of Locke. And one morning I wake up and I'm not me anymore, I'm just—a smooth thing wearing my face, walking toward your altar with nothing left in her worth saving."

*Worth saving,* the power repeated, tasting the word. *You believe there is something in you worth saving apart from me?*

"That's the entire point," Oscar said, and hated how small her voice sounded against the mountains. "That's the entire point of everything I'm trying to build. A covenant made by choice. Which means there has to be a *me* left over to do the choosing."

The warmth went quiet. Not gone—she could feel it coiled beneath her sternum, patient, vast, older than the language it borrowed—but quiet, the way a predator is quiet when it has decided the prey isn't going anywhere tonight.

Oscar hugged her knees to her chest and stared into the coals and let herself shake. It was the first time she'd allowed it since the clanmother's hearth. Alone, with no one to steady, she shook like a struck bell, and the sound of it seemed to go on and on.

Because the worst part—the part she would never say out loud—was that she *wanted* it. God help her, she wanted it. There was a version of her, only a few hours old, that had already said yes. That had already reached for the working the way a starving woman reaches for bread, and felt the doubt begin to dissolve like sugar in warm water, and felt—for three heart-stopping seconds—*light*. Clean. Certain. Ready for the altar, ready for anything, unburdened by the knowledge that her name was built on a robbery.

Three seconds. Then she had thought of Wren's face when the girl asked, *Was your family good?*—asked it with such naked hope—and Oscar had known that if she took the working, she would lie to that child forever, fluently, painlessly, and never even know she was lying.

She had slammed the door on it. But doors, she was learning, did not stay slammed. Not these doors.

"You're awake."

She hadn't heard him cross the camp. Cassian stood at the edge of the firelight in his shirt sleeves, hair flattened on one side, eyes shadowed with the particular exhaustion of a man who had stopped expecting to sleep well sometime around his defrocking. He held two tin cups. Steam rose off both.

"I'm awake," she said. Her voice came out scraped. She cleared her throat. "How could you tell?"

"You argue in your sleep." He came around the fire and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, far enough that the almost was a choice. He handed her a cup. Chamomile, from the smell of it—Petra's hoard, which meant he'd either charmed her or robbed her. With Cassian it was usually both. "Tonight it was in Old Covenant liturgical. Which is impressive, because I'm fairly sure you've never studied it."

"How would you know what I've studied?"

"Because I taught it," he said, "for six years, to seminarians who mostly wanted to be elsewhere, and none of them conjugated *to bind* in their sleep." He sipped. "You're getting worse, Oscar. Don't tell me you're not. You flinch when the fire pops. You've started holding your left arm against your body like it's someone else's. And yesterday, when the clanmother said *stolen goods*, your eyes went silver for half a heartbeat. I've seen that before. In Vaelithic texts. In paintings of the Sundering."

She stared into her cup. The chamomile trembled with her pulse.

"It talks to me," she said.

He didn't move. Didn't gasp, didn't reach for her, didn't do any of the things a frightened man does. He just waited, the way he waited during confessions, she supposed—though he'd never actually confessed to hearing any, back when he had a pulpit. Just waited, present, unhurried, as if her worst thing were a thing he had all the time in the world for.

"Not out loud," she went on, because the silence was worse than saying it. "Inside. It sounds like—it sounds like *me*. That's the trap. It knows all my arguments before I make them. It makes better arguments than I do. Tonight it offered to take the doubt away. The clanmother's words, the shame, all of it. Just—lift it out. And I nearly—" Her throat closed. She tried again. "Cassian, I nearly said yes. Three seconds. Maybe four. I was *lighter*. I could feel the doubt dissolving and it felt like grace, it felt like everything the hymns promised, and the only reason I stopped—"

"Why did you stop?"

"Wren," she said. "I thought about lying to Wren and not knowing I was lying."

Cassian set his cup down on the stone with a small deliberate click, and then, without ceremony, he took her left hand.

Her marked hand. The one she'd been guarding against her own ribs like a wound. His fingers were warm and ink-stained too—he still wrote his useless beautiful letters no one would ever send—and he didn't grip hard, didn't perform anything. He just held it, thumb resting over the pulse point where the ember-lines ran thinnest, as if to say: *here, this is yours, this beat is yours, no god gave it to you.*

The warmth inside her recoiled.

It was subtle—like a snake drawing back from a flame it hadn't expected—but she felt it, unmistakably, a cold spot opening behind her sternum where a moment ago there had been heat. The whispers didn't stop entirely. But they lost their footing. The borrowed voice of herself faltered, because it had mapped her solitude perfectly and had no strategy for this: a second hand. A witness. Someone whose presence made the editing impossible, because whatever she became, he would see it, and she would know he saw it.

"Oh," she breathed. "It doesn't like that."

"What doesn't like what?"

"The power. When you—" She flexed her fingers inside his. "When you hold on. It pulls back. Like it needs me alone."

"Of course it does," Cassian said quietly. "Isolation is the first tool of every corruption I ever read about. The Church's exorcism rites spend nine-tenths of their length separating the afflicted from everyone who loves them—not the demon's work, note, the *rite's* work. Get them alone. Get them doubting their own senses. Convince them they're a burden." His mouth twisted. "I stood at an altar for years reciting those words and never once noticed we'd written the demon's playbook ourselves."

"Cassian—"

"I'm not finished." He turned to face her fully, and the fire caught the grey at his temples, the scar through his eyebrow, the terrible gentleness that he wore like a penance. "Listen to me, Oscar Dravenmoor. Whatever it offers you—and it will keep offering, it will get cleverer, it will start offering things you're ashamed to want—you come find me. Wake me. Any hour. I don't care if it's the middle of the night and I'm drooling on Brannoc's shoulder. You put your hand in mine and you hold on until the voice gets bored. That's not a request. That's the deal. That's what I'm for."

"And what are you for the rest of the time?" she asked, and it came out softer than she intended, almost teasing, almost breaking.

"Maps," he said. "Bad jokes. Arguing with Petra about whether the twins' forgeries count as art. And loving you, which I mention only because you seem determined to carry everything alone, and I am constitutionally incapable of allowing that."

The word landed in her chest like a coal dropped in snow—hissing, bright, undeniable. He'd said it before, in the dark after the Weeping Stair, with blood between them; he'd said it triage-soft, a confession squeezed out alongside the truth of her name. But this was daylight logic, firelight logic, said plainly, with no wound to hide behind, and it terrified her more than the Maw, more than Locke, more than the vast patient hunger coiled beneath her ribs.

"I nearly erased you," she whispered.

He frowned. "What?"

"Just now. Before you came. When it offered to take the doubt away—I thought about what else I could take away. The fear of losing people. And I realized if I kept going, eventually I'd sand off the parts of me that—" She laughed, wet and unsteady. "The parts that are inconvenient about you. The priest who failed. The man who drinks too much chamomile and writes letters to a church that betrayed him. If I edited myself clean, I'd edit out the reasons I—" She stopped. Took a breath. Said it. "The reasons I love you. Because loving you is the most inconvenient thing I do. It slows me down. It gives the world leverage. A smart heir would cut it out."

"A smart heir," Cassian said, "built the old covenant. Look how that turned out." He lifted her hand—her marked, guarded, traitorous, human hand—and pressed his mouth briefly to her knuckles, over the glowing lines, and the light there guttered like a candle in a doorway, steadied, and dimmed to something almost warm and almost bearable. "We're not building a smart covenant. We're building a true one."

They sat like that while the fire settled and the mountain wind turned its long pages. She felt the power withdraw—not defeated, nothing so hopeful as defeated, but outmaneuvered for the night, retreating to some deep gallery of her blood to wait and sharpen. She knew it would be back. She knew it would come back wearing better disguises: duty, maybe, or justice, or love itself, twisted into justification. Corruption was patient. It had three hundred years of practice.

But she also knew this: it needed her alone. And she was done being alone. Ten years of meticulous invisibility, and it turned out the whole elaborate architecture of her solitude had been the enemy's fortification all along.

"Cassian," she said, as the sky east of the peaks began to bruise grey. "The third shrine. Aurel was cliffside, Vess was drowned. The journal says the third is Kethrys, in the high pass above the Weeping Stair—we can reach it in two days if the weather holds."

"Then we'll reach it," he said.

She nodded. But she didn't sleep, and neither did he, and when the sun finally cleared the ridge it found them both already standing, packed, ready—because far below in the valley, thin as a thread of black thread through gold, a column of smoke was rising from the direction of the high pass.

Smoke that rose in a straight, deliberate line.

Smoke that smelled, even at this distance, of church-fire.

* * *

They broke camp in ninety seconds flat. Brannoc didn't ask questions; he simply pointed at the smoke and said, "That's no beacon. Beacons are fed green wood to make it thick. That's a pyre." Petra counted their remaining supplies twice, lips moving, and came up with a number she didn't like. Wren stood at the trailhead with her brand bared to the morning, and the ember-mark on her small wrist was burning in perfect sympathy with the marks running up Oscar's arm—two flames leaning toward each other across the distance like hands reaching.

Oscar looked at the smoke, then at the map in her head—the real one, the one no paper held, the network of smuggler cuts and goat tracks and collapsed-mine bypasses that threaded the high pass like veins through a body. There was a way. There was always a way; she had spent a decade making sure of it. A route that would bring them to Kethrys in a day and a half instead of two.

It ran through country that had been unmapped for a reason.

"Cassian," she said, rolling the journal and slinging her pack. "How do you feel about walking through the dark side of the mountain?"

His answer was to check the blade at his hip and fall into step beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not drift apart.

Behind them, the smoke stood straight as a candleflame in windless air, and waited.
