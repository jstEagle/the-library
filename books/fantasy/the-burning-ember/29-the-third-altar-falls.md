## 29. The Third Altar Falls

Cassian Vale had spent eleven years learning to read the language of ruin. He'd seen what fire did to a chapel—how it peeled the gilt from the saints, how it left the smoke-stains in shapes that looked, if you were tired enough and guilty enough, like faces. He had stood in the ashes of his own seminary chapel after the fire he'd started with one overturned lamp and a prayer no one answered. He knew ruin the way other men knew their mothers' kitchens.

So when the trees thinned at the top of the Vesswater ridge and the smell reached him—oil, not hunger; tallow and consecrated pitch, not the sweet-rot stink of the Maw—he knew before anyone said a word.

He stopped on the trail and let the others come up around him. Oscar was ahead, as she always was now, her father's journal strapped against her ribs like a second heart. Wren rode doubled behind Brannoc, her small hands fisted in the back of his coat, the ember-brand on her arm wrapped in linen against the cold. Petra brought up the rear with the mules, humming one of Mother Quill's road songs under her breath—the way she did when she was frightened and didn't want to be.

The shrine of Aurel sat in a bowl of granite where three ridges met, a place the old maps called the Listening Hollow because sound behaved strangely there—prayers whispered at the eastern wall could be heard, faintly, at the western one, hours later. Cassian had never been here. But he had dreamed of it, once, in the years after his fall: a circle of standing stones, a spring that ran silver even in winter, an altar of white mountain-glass worn smooth by ten thousand pilgrim hands.

What lay below them in the hollow was none of those things.

It was a crater.

Not a wide one—the shrine had been modest, a waystation of faith rather than a cathedral—but deep, and precise, and wrong. The Church burned things messily when it burned in anger. This had been burned in method. The standing stones were gone entirely; not toppled, not shattered, but *gone*, hauled away or ground to powder, and the ground where they'd stood was fused to glass in a ring so perfect it might have been drawn with a compass. At the center, where the altar should have been, there was only a pit filled with ash so fine and so pale it looked like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

And driven into the glass at the crater's rim, planted like a flag on conquered soil, was an iron stake. From it hung a banner Cassian knew too well—the sun-and-key of the Inquisitorial Office, its gold thread catching the grey morning light.

"Nobody move," Oscar said quietly.

She was already moving. She came down the slope with her hood back and her jaw set in the particular line that meant she had stopped feeling things in order to function, and Cassian followed because following her had become, somewhere over the last months without his noticing, simply what he did.

The ash crunched underfoot. It was still warm. That was the detail that made his stomach turn—not the banner, not the glass, but the warmth. They had missed the Inquisitors by hours. Perhaps less. Somewhere on these same trails, men in gilded half-masks were walking away from this place at a soldier's pace, unhurried, certain, already thinking about their supper.

Oscar knelt at the edge of the altar-pit and pressed her palm flat against the fused glass. Cassian watched her face as she did it, watched the color drain out of her cheeks in stages, like water finding its level.

"It's not just broken," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "They didn't just burn it. Look at the cut lines." She traced a finger above the glass without touching it. "They quarried the altar stone out whole. Took it with them."

"Why would they take it?" Brannoc had come down behind them, one eye narrowed against the light off the glass. "Burnin' I understand. Burnin's a message. Stealin' a rock is—" He stopped. Cassian watched him arrive at it a heartbeat before he said it. "Stealin' a rock is *use*."

"They're not destroying the altars," Cassian said. The words tasted like copper. "They're collecting them."

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins' shadow, because there was nowhere else within six miles and because Oscar refused to leave, and no one had the heart—or perhaps the courage—to argue with her. She sat apart from the fire with the journal open across her knees, turning pages with a care that looked almost violent, as though the paper might confess something if she handled it roughly enough.

Cassian brought her tea he'd made himself, bitter and oversweet the way she pretended not to like it, and sat down beside her without asking. That was another thing he'd learned: you didn't ask Oscar Dravenmoor for permission to keep her company. You simply arrived, and stayed until she remembered how to want company again.

"The rite needs all three," she said, before he'd even settled. Not looking at him. Looking at the crater. "Vess for the waters. Aurel for the flame. And the third—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The third for the breath. The old texts are clear. Three altars, three elements, three renewals, and then the Ember Altar itself to bind them. Two shrines renewed means nothing. Less than nothing—it's a door opened halfway. It would draw them faster than leaving everything sealed."

"I know."

"You don't know." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and she hated every drop of it, he could see that, hated being seen in this state more than she feared anything the Maw could do. "I have spent ten years learning every smuggler track and goat path in these mountains so that when this moment came I would know exactly where to go. I had a *route*, Cassian. I had contingencies. I had a plan with four layers of fallbacks, and every single layer assumed the third shrine would still exist, because who burns a shrine to a god everyone believes has been dead for three hundred years? Who looks at a pile of old stones nobody prays at anymore and sees a threat?"

"The Church does," Cassian said softly. "Because the Church stopped believing the gods were dead a long time ago. We just didn't tell anyone."

That landed. He saw it land. Because he hadn't told her yet—not all of it—and the look she gave him was sharp enough to draw blood.

"What do you mean, *we*?"

He set down his cup. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Eleven years of practice at confessing things had taught him at least that much control.

"When I was in seminary—before, you understand, before the fall, when I was still Father Cassian and my prayers still had somewhere to go—there was a document. A restricted commentary on the Sundering. Novices weren't supposed to see it. I saw it anyway, because I was nineteen and arrogant and I thought rules were for people with less faith than me." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "It described the binding rites in detail. The covenant architecture. And in the margin, in the Archlector's own hand, there was a note. One line. I've carried it in my head for fifteen years like a splinter I couldn't dig out."

"Say it."

"*The seal holds while the blood holds. Watch the last house.*"

The fire popped. Over by the mules, Petra had stopped humming. Cassian realized, distantly, that they had an audience—that the whole camp had gone quiet around them, Brannoc and Wren and Petra all pretending very badly to be asleep or busy or elsewhere.

"Watch the last house," Oscar repeated. Her voice had gone very quiet. "The Dravenmoors."

"The last house of the covenant-keepers. They knew, Oscar. The Church has known for generations that the binding was mortal, that it lived and died with a bloodline, and instead of helping maintain it they wrote a note in a margin and locked the book away. And now—" He gestured at the crater, at the banner, at the whole ruined shape of the world. "Now the seal is failing, and their answer isn't renewal. It's collection. Take the altar stones, take the relics, take every piece of the old covenant into Church vaults where no heir can ever reach them, and let the gods starve behind a door they build themselves. A covenant with no altar is a covenant no one can ever reforge. Not you. Not anyone. Forever."

"That's madness."

"That's *theology*." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and didn't try to soften it. "Madness at least admits it's sick. Theology calls the sickness salvation. Seraphine Locke probably believes, right now, tonight, as sincerely as I ever believed anything, that she is saving the world from a second Sundering. That she is the mercy and we are the plague."

Oscar was silent for a long time. When she spoke again, the fury had drained out of her voice and left something worse underneath—something flat and exhausted and terribly young.

"Then it doesn't matter what I do. If they have the third stone, the traditional rite is dead. There is no path through the three altars. There's no—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I gave up my mother's face for the first one, Cassian. Do you understand? I paid a memory to a river ghost and I can't even remember what I lost anymore, I just know there's a hole in me shaped like a woman singing, and I did that for a plan that no longer exists."

He wanted to touch her. He wanted it so badly his hands ached with it. Instead he said, carefully, "There may be another way."

"Don't." She dropped her hand and looked at him with red-rimmed eyes. "Don't offer me hope like it costs you nothing. Everyone offers hope like it's free. Hope is the most expensive thing there is."

"I'm not offering it." He turned the cup in his hands. "I'm telling you what I know, and what I know is this: the traditional rite is dead, yes. Three altars, three renewals, the slow path your ancestors built. But your father's journal doesn't describe the traditional rite, does it? It describes the *first* one. The original. What the Dravenmoors did at the Ember Altar before there were shrines, before there was architecture, back when the covenant was new and improvised and desperate." He had spent three nights reading over her shoulder while she slept, guiltily, hungrily, and the pages had seared themselves into him. "The shrines were built later. Waystations. Amplifiers. The first binding happened at the heart-mountain with nothing but an heir, an altar, and a price. Everything else is scaffolding."

"Scaffolding that tells me *what the price is*." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Without the shrines I don't know the steps. I don't know the words. I'd be walking into the deepest place in Thornfell with a book of half-legible notes and a head full of inherited power that wants things, Cassian. *Wants*. You've felt it. You've seen what it does to me when I let it up for air."

"Yes," he said. "I have." He had seen her stop a rockslide with a word and liked it. He had seen her eyes catch firelight wrong, twice, briefly, and say nothing about it either time. "And I'm going to be standing next to you when you do it anyway."

"Cassian—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've been finishing nothing for eleven years and I'm tired of it." He set the cup down and turned to face her fully. "I prayed for nine years and got silence, and I decided that meant God was dead or I was damned, and both answers nearly killed me. Then I met a girl with a brand that won't heal and a woman who draws maps of places that don't officially exist, and I learned that silence isn't absence. Sometimes silence is a door holding. Your ancestors bound the gods to save the world, and the world forgot, and the Church buried the knowledge, and the whole weight of it came down to one cartographer who thought she was alone. You were never the plan's last hope, Oscar. You were the plan's *only* hope, and the plan was always going to come down to this: one person at one altar making one choice no one else can make. The shrines were the easy version. This is the real one. It was always going to be the real one."

She stared at him. The firelight moved on her face, and for a moment she looked less like the composed stranger he'd met on the salt road and more like what she actually was—a young woman carrying a dead family's debt through a freezing country with enemies on every side, doing it anyway, hour after hour, day after day.

"The third stone," she said at last, mechanically, grasping for the practical because the emotional was drowning her. "If they're collecting them, they'll take them somewhere secure. The Inquisitorial vaults are beneath the Bastion in Corvane. That's four hundred miles the wrong direction."

"Then we don't need the stone."

"We need *something*."

"We need what the stone *is*," Cassian said slowly, feeling the idea assemble itself as he spoke, terrifying and bright. "Aurel's flame. Vess's water. The third is breath—is spirit, is the living witness. Wren."

Across the fire, the girl's head came up sharply. She had been listening to every word; of course she had. The linen wrap on her forearm had slipped sometime during the night, and the ember-brand glowed faintly in the dark, pulsing in a rhythm that Cassian had long since stopped pretending was random. It pulsed now, quick and eager, like a heart hearing its name.

"No," Oscar said instantly.

"She's been keyed to every shrine we've visited. The brand burned before we reached Vess. It burned on the approach to Aurel. She is a living relic of the old covenant, Oscar—maybe the last one the gods themselves marked. If the shrines are gone, maybe the covenant doesn't need stone anymore. Maybe it needs a witness."

"She is a *child*."

"She is a child the Maw is already hunting," Brannoc said quietly, from the dark beyond the fire. He hadn't been sleeping at all. None of them had. "And the Church wants her dead, and the ghosts want her marked, and every road but one leads to her dyin' scared and useless. At least this road, she walks it *choosin'*." The old pilot's one eye gleamed. "That's how you lot do things now, ain't it? Choice over blood. I've been listenin'."

Oscar looked around the fire—at Brannoc, at Petra, at Wren sitting straight-backed and solemn with her burning arm held in her lap like something precious and terrible. At Cassian. Looking, he realized, for someone to tell her she was wrong. Finding no one.

"The Ember Altar," she said finally, hoarsely. "Beneath Thornfell. My father's map points down, not out. If the third element can't come from a shrine..." She closed the journal. Her hand rested on its cover the way you'd rest a hand on a grave. "Then we go into the mountain. All the way down. And we find out what the first covenant-keepers knew, before there was anyone left to lie about it."

The wind came down off the peaks and stirred the pale ash of the destroyed shrine, lifting it in little ghosts that swirled through the firelight and scattered into the dark. Cassian watched them go and thought of prayers, rising, unanswered, and of doors, holding.

Behind them, far down the southern trail, a thin thread of torchlight moved through the trees—too distant to be sure, too deliberate to be chance.

Someone was following the same road home.
