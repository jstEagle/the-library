## 30. Locke Defects

The order came at dusk, written in the Grand Inquisitor's own hand and sealed with the sunburst of the Holy Office of Purity. Seraphine Locke read it three times by candlelight in her tent, and each time the words did not soften.

*By authority vested in this office: the settlement of Hollowmere is declared corrupted. All inhabitants bearing marks of god-touched affliction are to be cleansed by fire before moonrise. Inquisitor Locke will oversee the execution personally as penance for her earlier leniencies.*

Hollowmere was not a settlement. It was a hamlet—eleven houses, a goat pen, a well with a crooked roof over it—and its inhabitants were not heretics. They were children. Fourteen of them, the oldest perhaps ten years, gathered from across three provinces after their parents had died or abandoned them, all marked with small stigmata of the old gods: a birthmark shaped like a wave on one boy's shoulder, a girl whose tears smelled of rain on hot stone, twins who spoke in their sleep in a language no living tongue claimed. The Church had collected them the way it collected dangerous relics—in crates, in silence, in places where no one would ask questions.

Seraphine set the letter down. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself with a kind of clinical detachment that had served her for twenty years: her hands never shook when it mattered. They had not shaken when she burned the shrine at Marrow's Cross. They had not shaken when she pressed the hot iron to the smuggler's forearm until he gave up the name *map-witch*. They had not shaken in nineteen years of service, through forty-one burnings and nine hundred interrogations, because Seraphine Locke had believed—with the whole iron weight of her soul—that every flame she lit was a dam holding back a flood.

She had believed the world was a house with rotten floorboards, and that someone had to be willing to burn out the rot before it reached the load-bearing walls.

The letter lay on the table between her and the candle, and the wax seal gleamed like a wound.

*Penance for her earlier leniencies.* The phrase was precise. Someone in the hierarchy had been counting her mercies. She thought of Ilsa—the forger who had died on the Weeping Stair with a knife in her hand and defiance in her throat—and how Seraphine had knelt beside the body afterward, alone, while her soldiers looted the pass, and had found sewn into the dead woman's coat lining a scrap of vellum covered in cipher. She had deciphered it over four nights by her own lamp, telling no one. It had been a fragment of the old covenant seal—not the physical relic she carried from Marrow's Cross, but a *copy*, a working diagram, annotated in a cramped scholar's hand with marginalia that made her blood run cold even now.

*Note: the Sundering was not an invasion. The rites of binding preceded the breach. We opened the door ourselves.*

She had told herself it was forgery. Propaganda. The desperate scribbling of a dying heretic trying to poison the well behind her. She had told herself that for six days, and each day the telling required more effort than the last, like pushing a cart uphill through mud that deepened with every step.

And now this letter. Burn fourteen children. Before moonrise. As *penance*.

Seraphine rose, put on her coat, and buckled her sword with hands that still did not shake.

* * *

Hollowmere sat in a fold of the foothills where the land began its long climb toward Thornfell, and the evening light came down the valley in bars of copper and violet. The children had been kept in the tithe-barn—a structure built for grain and now holding small beds of straw, a single stove, and fourteen souls who had done nothing in their lives except be born wrong according to the arithmetic of the Holy Office.

Captain Vess met her at the barn door. He was a solid man, gray at the temples, devout in the way of men who had never been asked to test their faith against anything heavier than routine. He saluted. "Inquisitor. The pyre wood is stacked. Oil's been blessed. We can begin at moonrise as ordered."

"Have you looked at them?" Seraphine asked.

Vess blinked. "At the children, ma'am?"

"At them. Their faces. Have you looked?"

Something flickered behind his eyes—the discomfort of a man being asked a question he had trained himself never to ask. "They are vessels of corruption, Inquisitor. The doctrine is clear. What looks like innocence in a vessel only makes the corruption more dangerous."

"The doctrine is clear," Seraphine agreed. Her voice came out level, almost gentle, and she hated how gentle it sounded, because gentleness now felt like the most obscene thing in the world. "Walk with me."

They walked along the barn's outer wall, boots crunching on frost-stiffened grass. Through the gaps in the boards she could hear the children inside—one of them singing, tunelessly, a song about a fisherman. A small voice. An ordinary voice. The kind of voice that belonged to a child waiting for supper, not to a vessel of anything.

"I have served the Office for nineteen years," Seraphine said. "I have burned shrines and broken covens and put down things that were not human and deserved what they got. I want you to understand that when I say what I am about to say, I say it with full knowledge of what it costs."

Vess stopped walking. He had begun, very slightly, to sweat despite the cold. Men always knew. That was the terrible thing about soldiers—they could smell a fracture in command the way horses smelled wolves.

"I am not going to burn them," Seraphine said.

"Inquisitor—"

"Look at me, Captain." He looked. His face was doing arithmetic of its own: duty, fear, ambition, the memory of her reputation, the knowledge of what happened to officers who stood between Seraphine Locke and an order—or, tonight, between her and the refusal of one. "Fourteen children. The youngest is two years old. She has a mark on her heel shaped like a leaf. That is the sum total of her corruption. If you believe God speaks through the Office, then explain to me why His voice sounds like burning toddlers."

"The Grand Inquisitor—"

"The Grand Inquisitor has never held a child in his life. I have held eleven of these. They weigh almost nothing." She heard her own voice fraying at the edges and pulled it taut again. "You have a choice, Captain Vess, and I will make it honest for you. You can follow my order, which is that these children are loaded into the supply wagons tonight and taken north under guard. Or you can report me. One of those paths ends with you disobeying the Grand Inquisitor's direct command. The other ends with you standing in front of a fire you lit yourself, watching two-year-olds go into it, and then living with that for whatever remains of your life. Choose."

For a long moment there was only the wind coming down off the mountains and the sound, maddeningly small, of the child inside still singing about the fisherman.

Vess's jaw worked. "Where would we take them?" he said finally, and Seraphine understood that the battle was won—not because the man had found faith, but because he had found something sturdier: the simple animal refusal to be a monster before witnesses, including his own.

"North," she said. "There are mountain clans past the high passes who don't answer to the Office. And after that—" she paused, hearing herself say it aloud for the first time, feeling the words change shape in her mouth like a key turning, "—after that, I'll find somewhere."

* * *

They left Hollowmere an hour before moonrise, three wagons rolling north under a sky salted with stars. Seraphine rode at the rear. Behind them, far back down the valley, the lights of the main camp burned orderly and orange, and she knew that by morning—by midnight, likely—a rider would reach the camp carrying Captain Vess's report, or Vess himself would come in person to denounce her, weeping, begging the Office to understand that she had left him no choice. It didn't matter which. The outcome was the same.

She had done the accounting on the ride out of the camp, coldly, the way she did all accounting. Nineteen years of service, spent. Rank, spent. Reputation, the thing she had polished like armor for two decades, spent. What remained: her sword, her horse, the seal-fragment hidden in the false bottom of her map case, the cipher page from Ilsa's coat, and a name she had sworn she would never speak outside an interrogation chamber.

Dravenmoor.

The map-witch of Thornfell. Oscar Dravenmoor, if the smuggler's testimony could be trusted, and Seraphine had learned over nineteen years which testimonies to trust: the ones given in terror were usually true, because terror was a poor architect of lies.

Her superiors wanted the bloodline erased. That order had come down weeks ago, clean and absolute, and she had marched toward it without hesitation, because erasing the last heir of the Ember Covenant had seemed then like the final necessary amputation—the cutting away of the last infected limb so the body of the world could heal. But the cipher page had changed the arithmetic. If the Sundering had been self-inflicted—if the Church's own founding rites had torn the veil rather than sealed it—then the covenant-keepers were not the disease. They were the cure that had been murdered by the patients. And everything Seraphine had burned, everyone she had broken, every shrine and coven and god-touched wretch she had fed to the flames in the name of healing the world—

She reined the horse to a stop at the crest of a hill and looked back once at the distant campfires. Somewhere down there were the ledgers. She knew there were ledgers; there were always ledgers; an institution that burned people for a living kept meticulous records of the burning. Somewhere in those ledgers was a list of every rite performed since the Sundering, every binding renewed, every altar desecrated, and if she could get her hands on them—

No. Not yet. First the children to safety. Then the mountains. Then—

A horn sounded behind her. One note, then two, urgent, the signal for *unauthorized movement—pursue.*

So. Faster than expected. Vess had either grown a spine or lost his nerve, and either way the Office's riders were already moving.

"Drive!" Seraphine shouted, wheeling her horse alongside the lead wagon. "Wagons off the road, into the tree line—now! Move!"

The drivers whipped the horses. The wagons lurched off the packed earth and crashed into the birch scrub at the hill's shoulder, branches scraping the canvas covers, children crying out inside—small frightened sounds that went through her like needles. Behind them, torchlight bloomed on the road they had abandoned, a dozen riders fanning out with the drilled precision of the Office's pursuit protocols. Seraphine knew those protocols because she had written half of them. She knew they would split into three columns, sweep the tree line, and drive any fugitives toward the river ford where a second company would be waiting by dawn.

Unless the fugitives didn't run toward the river.

"The gorge," she called to the lead driver, a gaunt woman named Tally who had guarded Church prisoners for fifteen years and asked questions for none of them. "Not the ford. The old mining gorge, northwest. Can you handle the wagons on scree?"

Tally spat over the wagon's rail. "I can handle wagons anywhere you can handle a horse, Inquisitor."

"Then don't call me that anymore. The title's forfeit as of tonight." She drew her sword—not to fight, not yet, but because the familiar weight of it steadied her, and because tomorrow she might need to sell it for bread, and she wanted one last hour of being the person she had been. "Call me Seraphine."

* * *

They lost the riders in the gorge by moonset, thanks to a branch tunnel Tally remembered from her smuggling years—a detail Seraphine filed away without surprise; half the Office's best trackers had pasts the Office preferred not to examine—and by grey dawn they were climbing, fourteen children and five adults and three wagons reduced to one, up switchback trails toward the white shoulders of Thornfell.

The children had gone quiet in the way of children who have learned that quiet is safety. They rode huddled under sacking in the wagon bed, and as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the snowfields above them, turning the whole mountain range to rose gold, Seraphine saw the littlest one—the two-year-old with the leaf-shaped mark on her heel—peeking out at the peaks with her mouth open in naked wonder. No fear of the mountain. No sense that the world above the treeline was anything but enormous and beautiful and hers to look at.

Seraphine turned away and found Tally watching her with an expression she couldn't parse.

"You're going to her," Tally said. Not a question. "The witch. The map-witch the Office wants dead."

"I'm going to the person who might know how to end this," Seraphine said. "If she's what the ledgers say she is, she's the only person alive who can reforge what we broke. And if she isn't—" her hand rested on the map case at her hip, on the seal-fragment sleeping in its false bottom, warm as a coal, "—then I'm the most dangerous heretic in the history of the Office, and I've brought her my own head as an offering."

"And if she kills you for what you've done? For the shrines. The burnings." Tally nodded back down the mountain, toward everything below. "For people like Ilsa."

"Then I'll have died owing a debt," Seraphine said. "It's more than I'd have gotten otherwise."

Above them, the trail narrowed onto a shelf of bare granite, and the wind came down off the ice fields carrying the smell of stone and snow and something else—faint, impossible, unmistakable. Woodsmoke and juniper. The smell of a hearth. Of habitation, this high, this far into the wilds.

And beneath it, threading through the wind like a whisper through a shut door, something older. Something that made the seal-fragment press warm against her hip through the leather, and made the leaf-marked child laugh suddenly, delightedly, at nothing at all.

Seraphine Locke, heretic of the Holy Office, put her hand on her sword and climbed toward the smoke.
